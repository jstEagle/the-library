## 6. Terms of Debt

The border town was called Lastmark, which Vesper thought a fittingly honest name — the kind of place that had given up pretending to be anything but an ending. A single street of leaning houses, a chapel with a collapsed roof, and one tavern whose sign had been repainted so many times it read only *THE —* as though even the name had deserted it.

Inside, Ilsa Rennick was losing a fight with dignity.

Vesper watched from the doorway for exactly three breaths before deciding whether to intervene: one breath to assess, one to admire, one to regret. The ward-smith moved like someone who had once been trained by people who cared about form and had since decided that form was for people with something left to lose. She fought low, dirty, economical — elbow to the throat, boot behind the knee — and she was still losing, because there were three of them and they were sober and she very much was not.

"You're blocking the door," Vesper said.

"Am I," Ilsa said, and took a bottle across the shoulder rather than step aside.

Vesper sighed, set her ledger down on the nearest table where everyone could see it — a gesture that in certain circles meant *I am about to conduct business* and in others meant *I am about to conduct violence* — and stepped into the fray. It did not last long. Tavern brawls rarely do when one participant has spent ten years cataloguing the exact price of every bone in the human body. Vesper broke a wrist, dislocated a thumb, and put a man's face briefly into contact with the floor, all without spilling the ward-smith's abandoned drink, because she respected craftsmanship wherever she found it.

Isla picked herself up off the boards, spat blood, and looked at Vesper with eyes the grey of old iron.

"You fight like an accountant," she said.

"I am one."

"Then you can pay for the damages." She scooped up her cup, drained what remained, and grimaced at the emptiness inside it. "And buy me another. That's the toll for my door."

"It's not your door."

"Everything broken in this town is mine," Ilsa said, and there was something underneath the drunkenness then, something cold and precise, like a wire pulled taut through wet wool. "The wards went down on my watch. Every lock that doesn't hold from here to the sea is mine. So yes, accountant. My door."

* * *

They talked in the corner booth while the tavernkeeper pretended not to listen and the three men nursed their wounds and their grudges in equal measure. Vesper bought the drink. She also opened her ledger to a blank page, which made Ilsa snort.

"What, you're going to write me down? Put me in the book?"

"That's how the Gilded Thorn works. Everything gets an entry. Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. Second chances." Vesper turned the page toward her. "Ward-smiths."

"Ex-ward-smiths."

"Struck from the rolls, yes. I read your file on the road north." Vesper kept her voice flat, unhurried, the voice she used for inventory. "Embezzlement of ward-stone. Sabotage of the Merrow Gate. Eleven deaths when the flood-wards failed. You were stripped of rank, branded, and exiled from three cities. Impressive work, honestly. Most disgraced smiths manage one city at most."

Something moved behind Ilsa's face — rage first, then grief, then a tiredness so old it had worn grooves. "You came all this way to read me my sins?"

"I came all this way because you're the only person alive who understands what broke two nights ago." Vesper laid a hand flat on the table. "The border wards didn't fail, Rennick. They shattered. Like glass struck from the inside. And every impossible thing my guild owns started leaking power into the dark. I need someone who can tell me why."

For a long moment Ilsa said nothing. Then she reached out, turned Vesper's ledger back toward herself, and studied the Thorn's mark stamped into the cover — a thorned rose in gold leaf — with the expression of a woman reading a diagnosis.

"Inside out," she said quietly. "You said the wards broke from the inside?"

"They did."

"Then whoever built them built them hollow. Or—" and here her voice dropped, and the drunkard fell away entirely, leaving only the smith, "—or something was already living in them. Wards aren't walls, accountant. People think that, but they're wrong. A wall keeps things out. A ward is a *promise*. Something sworn to hold the line between here and everywhere else. And a promise—"

"—can be sold," Vesper finished.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Outside, snow was beginning to fall, and it fell strangely, drifting upward past the window in slow defiance of every law Vesper had ever trusted. Ilsa watched it go with the look of a woman watching her own funeral procession.

"The stars are gone," she said. "I've been telling myself I drank too much. But you know they're gone too, don't you. You wouldn't have come otherwise."

"I know," Vesper said. "And I know who can tell us why. But getting to him means crossing a country where winter falls upward, past whatever the Undercourt sends to collect, and down a road that hasn't existed in a thousand years. Which means I need the best ward-smith of her generation to build me a way in."

"And why would I help you?" Ilsa asked. "What's in it for a dead woman walking?"

"Your name." Vesper opened the ledger again and uncapped her pen. "Clear it. Fully. The Wardens' Guild answers to contracts the same as anyone, and the Thorn holds debts older than their charter. I'll spend whatever it costs. Your record restored. Your brand struck from the rolls. Your right to practice returned." She looked up. "In exchange, you rebuild the road into the mountain, and you stand between my people and everything that lives under it."

Ilsa stared at her. Then she laughed — a short, harsh sound with no joy in it whatsoever.

"You don't even know if I'm innocent."

"No," Vesper agreed. "I don't."

"And that doesn't matter to you?"

"It matters enormously. But not yet." Vesper met her eyes and let the accountant show through, the part of her that had spent a decade learning that truth was just another commodity with fluctuating value. "I've spent the last two days discovering that everyone I trusted has been lying to me by omission. So no, Rennick, I'm not offering to believe you. I'm offering to *hire* you. Belief can come later, if you earn it. Leverage comes now."

For the first time, Ilsa smiled. It was not a comforting smile.

"There she is," the smith said. "There's the woman who fights like an accountant." She extended her hand across the table, calloused palm scarred with old burn-marks in patterns Vesper almost recognized — ward-script, seared into skin. "Terms of debt, then. One road, one mountain, one name cleared. And accountant—"

"Ilsa."

"Ilsa." Vesper shook it. Her grip was iron and frostbite both. "If you find out I did it — the embezzlement, the gate, the eleven — you'll void the contract and leave me on the mountain."

"If you find out I did it," Vesper said, "you'll understand why I'd do the same to you."

"Fair," said Ilsa Rennick, and shook twice more to seal it, the way smiths sealed oaths, and somewhere in the empty sky above Lastmark, Vesper would have sworn she felt something shift — some vast attention turning over in its sleep.

* * *

Corvane Ash arrived at dawn, which meant he had ridden through the night, which meant he had known where she'd be, which meant — Vesper realized with a fresh and wearying bitterness — he had always known. He came into the tavern with frost in his silver-shot hair and his usual coat immaculate despite the road, because Corvane believed that a guildmaster's appearance was a form of accounting, and he never let his books fall into disorder.

He saw Ilsa at the corner booth, and stopped.

"Rennick," he said.

"Ash." She didn't rise. "Still wearing other people's gold, I see."

"Still drinking other people's patience." He slid onto the bench across from them, uninvited, and Vesper watched him take inventory of her new hire in a single glance — the brands on her wrists half-hidden under sleeves, the ward-scars on her palms, the sobriety she'd assembled overnight like armor. Whatever conclusion he reached, he filed it away without comment. "Vesper. A word."

"We're negotiating terms."

"We're renegotiating them," Corvane said, "because you've hired the one person in the world whose presence will make what's under the mountain *pay attention*, and you did it without consulting me."

"You gave me an order. Seal the vaults. You didn't give me permission to stop being your auditor." Vesper closed the ledger with a snap. "So audit me. Tell me what I'm walking into, and I'll tell you whether my terms were sound."

The silence stretched. Ilsa, wisely, ordered another drink and became very interested in the grain of the table.

"Vesper." Corvane's voice was gentle, and that was worse than anger; gentleness was what he used when the truth needed cushioning, and everything he'd said gently lately had been a lie. "Go home. Seal the vaults. Let winter come. In fifty years it will pass, and the Thorn will still be standing, because that is what I *built it to do*—"

"Fifty years." She said it flatly. "That's a strange number for a man to choose. Precise, I mean. Not 'it will pass,' not 'in time.' Fifty years. Like a term." She leaned forward. "Like interest."

She watched him hear it land. Watched the color leave his face the way heat leaves stone — slowly at first, then all at once.

"Three nights ago," Vesper continued, "the stars went out. Two days ago, I found a page in the vault ledgers written in my hand and dated three years from now, naming the Undercourt and describing a soul sold against collateral. Yesterday I rode north and hired a disgraced ward-smith who says our border wards weren't walls at all — they were promises. Promises that can be sold." She turned the ledger around and opened it to the impossible page, and pushed it across the table. "So. Partial confession, guildmaster. You owe the Undercourt. I know that much. Tell me what, or tell me nothing, but know that Ilsa and I are going either way, and every hour you keep silent, I price your silence higher."

Corvane looked at the page for a long time. His finger hovered above the ink — ink she hadn't written, in handwriting that was hers exactly — and Vesper saw something in his expression she had never seen there in ten years of service.

Not guilt. Recognition.

"Yes," he said finally, very quietly. "I owe the Undercourt. That much is true, and you may enter it in your books." He folded his hands, and for a moment the guildmaster vanished and something older sat in his place — a young man, tired, cornered, a century out of his depth. "But not what, Vesper. Not why. Because the terms of that debt forbid it. Speak its substance aloud, and the collection accelerates. Ask Bramble, if you doubt me — ask the oldest contract we hold. Debts to the court beneath the mountain are written in a language that punishes disclosure. Every word I give you buys winter a little closer."

"Then buy carefully," Vesper said, "and start spending."

He laughed — one dry note, no humor in it, the laugh of a man doing arithmetic he hates. He looked at her then for a long moment, and whatever he saw — the girl he'd taken in, the auditor he'd trained, the sworn blade of a silent god — it seemed to settle something in him.

"One thing," he said. "One entry, freely paid, and then you ride, and you don't ask again until the mountain forces the truth out of me." He drew a breath. "It wasn't payment. What I gave them. Understand that much, even if I can't say more. It was *collateral* — held against something larger, something that was coming due whether I signed or not. I didn't sell my soul to save the Thorn, Vesper. I pledged it to *buy time* for a bill I couldn't pay. And the stars going out means the deferral is over." His hands, she noticed, were shaking, and he let her see it, which from Corvane was its own confession. "Whatever you find under the mountain — however bad the ledger looks when you read it — remember that I did it with the door open. I could have run. I stayed and kept your wages paid for a hundred years."

Vesper wrote it down. *Collateral, not payment. Deferral ended.* Her pen moved steadily; her hands wanted to shake too, and she denied them.

"Terms accepted," she said. "Partial."

"Partial," he agreed, and stood, and buttoned his immaculate coat, and was for one moment the man who had fished a starving fifteen-year-old thief out of a canal and taught her that everything, even mercy, could be entered fairly in a book. "Rennick. Build her a good road."

"Build her a better guildmaster," Ilsa muttered, but she said it to her cup, and Corvane pretended not to hear, and left the way he'd come, into the upward-falling snow.

Vesper watched the door close behind him. Beside her, Ilsa drained her drink and set it down with a click.

"He knows what's coming for him," the smith observed. "He's known for years. And he sent you anyway."

"He didn't send me. I volunteered."

"Mm." Ilsa's mouth twisted. "My mentor used to say that about me, right before the tribunal. *No one sent her, she volunteered.*" She stood, rolling her shoulders like a woman taking up a burden she'd been avoiding for years. "Come on then, accountant. If we're riding into hell together, I want to see what you're carrying before I trust it to hold a ward-line."

Outside, the snow drifted upward past the eaves, and far to the east — where no star should have been, where no star had burned for three nights — something flickered. Once. Twice.

Green. Cold. Patient.

Ilsa saw it too. Her hand found Vesper's arm, grip iron-tight.

"That," she said quietly, "is a ward-light. Someone just lit a beacon on a dead network. Someone's calling the roads open." A pause, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "And there's only one person alive who knows how."

Behind them, in the empty booth, Vesper's open ledger stirred though no wind reached it — and on the impossible page dated three years hence, beneath the words in her own hand, a single new line surfaced in ink that gleamed wet and black:

*The road remembers. Descend.*
