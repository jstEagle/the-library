## 7. The Ghost Who Collects

Let me tell you something about being dead that the poets never get right: it is not the dying that wears on you. Dying is a moment, a door, a small wet sound and then nothing. It is the *paperwork* that goes on forever.

My name is Bramble. Was. Is. Grammar gets slippery after your third century. I was born under a bad moon in a good year, died of a fever at thirty-one owing seventeen crowns to a woman named Hetta Fenn who kept bees and grudges with equal devotion, and in the interval between my last breath and whatever comes after — I never found out what comes after; there is always more paperwork — I was bound to a contract. Not *a* contract. *The* contract. The oldest one in the ledger-house called the Gilded Thorn, inked in blood and tallow smoke before the mountain had its current name, before the Undercourt went beneath it, before anyone thought to ask whether eternity might be a bit much.

Three hundred and eleven years I have collected debts for the Thorn. Souls, secrets, shadows, the memory of a first kiss (which, incidentally, sells beautifully), the luck of a man who once won the same hand of cards four times running. I knock on doors that do not exist, in towns that have forgotten their own names, and I say the words: *Your account has come due.* And people weep, or bargain, or throw things — mostly things — and eventually they pay, because you cannot outrun a collector who does not tire, does not eat, does not bleed, and does not, under any circumstances, stop smiling. The smiling is contractual. Clause nine. I could show you the clause. I have shown many people the clause. Nobody enjoys it.

I mention all this so you understand my mood when, on a gray morning with snow falling in the wrong direction, I felt my binding *snap* like a wet cord pulled tight.

* * *

It happened in the Thorn's counting room, which is where I keep myself when I am not abroad collecting, hovering near the oldest ledger the way other men hover near a hearth. The book is a monster: calfskin gone the color of old teeth, clasps of black iron, pages that smell of myrrh and regret. My contract lives in it the way a tapeworm lives in a gut. Comfortable. Integrated. Permanent, I had assumed.

The snap felt like the time I dropped an anvil on my foot, except everywhere, except inside. I looked down at my hands — translucent, ink-stained, always ink-stained, even now, even dead, as if three centuries of ledgers had soaked through skin that no longer strictly existed — and watched the faint golden thread that ran from my chest into the book flicker, gutter, and dissolve.

"Huh," I said, to the empty counting room.

Then, louder, with considerably less composure: "*Huh.*"

Now. A word about bindings, since I am, professionally speaking, an authority. Bindings do not break. They are not twine; they are law, older and meaner than kings. In three hundred years I had seen exactly two bindings come undone: one when the debtor's entire bloodline ended in a fire that I did not start, despite the rumors, and one when a ward-smith named Rennick mispronounced her own anchor-rune and blew a hole in the Warden Guildhall that they're still sweeping up. Both times, the freed party had wept with joy.

I did not weep. I did arithmetic.

Bindings do not break — unless the *terms* of the binding become impossible to fulfill. And my terms were simple: I collect what the Thorn is owed, until the Thorn's oldest contract is discharged. Until the debt is paid, or forgiven, or rendered moot by circumstances too vast for mortal accounting.

Circumstances too vast for mortal accounting had arrived the previous night, riding on the back of every star in the sky going out like candles pinched between a god's fingers.

"Oh," I said quietly. "Oh, that is *not* good."

Because here is the thing nobody at the Thorn knew — nobody living — and here is the thing I have carried in my hollow chest for three hundred years like a stone I swallowed on purpose:

The Thorn's oldest contract was written by the Undercourt. The paper came from beneath the mountain. The ink was ground from starlight the court had hoarded before their exile. I know because I was there when it was signed, in a ballroom carved from a geode the size of a cathedral, watching a young man with hungry eyes trade away the only thing he owned outright. I know because I have delivered reminders to that court. I know the smell of the place — cold iron and crushed violets — the way you know the smell of a house you were hurt in.

And if the stars going out had voided the oldest contract in existence, then whatever was coming due beneath the mountain was coming due *now*, and it was coming due at a scale that made every collection I ever made look like picking up tabs at a tavern.

I did the only reasonable thing. I went to find Vesper Blackwood, and I attached myself to her like a burr to a dog.

* * *

She was in the vault corridor arguing with a locked door, which told me everything about how the night had gone. Behind that door, the Thorn's impossible inventory — bottled memories, borrowed luck, a stolen shadow in an iron cage that had begun, I noticed with alarm, to grow back — was leaking its power into the stones like wine through a cracked barrel. The wards were failing. Everything was failing. It was, I want to stress, a very busy morning, and I chose that moment to make my presence known by clearing my throat, which is a thing ghosts can do and which never stops being funny.

She did not scream. I want that on the record. Most people scream. Vesper Blackwood turned around with the flat expression of a woman who audited her own death and found it filed under *miscellaneous*, and said:

"Bramble. You're loose."

"Loose is such a strong word," I said. "I prefer *unmoored*. It has nautical dignity." I drifted down the corridor, hands folded over my waistcoat — I died in a good waistcoat, oxblood velvet, and I'll thank you not to picture me otherwise. "Also, hello, lovely morning, stars are gone, winter is coming in through the windows sideways, and my contract just evaporated. Thought you'd want to know."

"Evaporated." Her eyes narrowed. Vesper's eyes narrowing is an event with consequences; I've seen debtors confess to crimes they hadn't committed yet, just to get out from under that look. "Your binding was tied to the oldest contract. If it broke—"

"Then the contract is void. Voided. Unmade. Which means either someone paid it off — unlikely; I would have been present for the payment, I'm always present, it's clause eleven — or the party holding the note no longer exists in a condition to hold notes." I let that sit. She was quick; she was always quick; watching her work through a problem was like watching a cat dismantle a lock, all economy and quiet malice. "Or," I added, because I am constitutionally incapable of mercy, "the holder has decided the collateral is worth collecting personally."

"Corvane's soul."

"Corvane's soul," I agreed. "Which, fun fact — and I use *fun* in its technical sense — was never payment at all."

That got her. Her whole body changed, the way a drawn bow changes. "Explain."

"*Collateral*," I said. "A pledge held against a larger obligation. You put your house up against a loan, yes? The lender doesn't want your house. The house just guarantees you'll come to the table when called." I spread my hands. "Someone at the Undercourt is calling. And the house — forgive me, the *soul* — will be seized unless somebody negotiates terms."

"You've been there." It wasn't a question. "The Undercourt."

"Twice." I smiled, clause nine making it a real smile, which somehow made it worse. "Once to deliver a reminder. Once to attend a birthday party for a god who had stopped believing in birthdays and started believing exclusively in spite. I danced with a duchess made of frost. She stepped on my foot. I didn't feel it, but I respected it enormously."

"Why are you telling me this?"

And there it was. The real question, asked the Vesper way: stripped of courtesy, laid on the table like a knife. Why *was* I telling her? Let me think — no. Let me not think. Thinking is how I have survived three centuries, and some things must be said quickly or never.

"Because my binding is void," I said, "and that means I am free, and freedom, Miss Blackwood, turns out to be a coat that doesn't fit. I have worn servitude so long I've grown into its shape. I don't know how to be unbound. I know how to collect. I know contracts. I know the road under the mountain, the etiquette of entropy, which fork of the geode halls leads to the soul-vault and which leads to the tannery — don't ask about the tannery — and I know, better than anyone breathing, what the Undercourt does to people who arrive without a broker who understands the paperwork." I bowed, low, the full formal bow I had performed at ten thousand doors. "You are going after him. Don't insult us both by denying it. Take me with you."

"Why?" she said again. Stubborn woman.

"Because," I said, and here my voice did something embarrassing, something honest, "in three hundred and eleven years, no one has ever once asked me to come along anywhere."

* * *

We left two days later, and I will spare you the logistics except to record, for posterity, that Vesper haggled with our new ward-smith like a fishwife and lost, which delighted me beyond measure. Ilsa Rennick. Big hands, bigger scars, a mouth like a dock crane and eyes that had done time in the same prison I inhabited — the one called *useful*. She saw straight through me on sight, of course. Ward-smiths always can. First thing she said to me was, "You've got anchor-scars. Who held your chain?"

"Hetta Fenn," I said. "Beekeeper. Seventeen crowns."

"Seventeen crowns." She whistled. "Three hundred years for seventeen crowns?"

"The interest compounded," I said, with dignity. "Clause seven. Compound interest is the cruelest magic ever devised, and I say that having met gods."

Traveling with the living is strange. You forget, between collections, how loud they are. How warm. Ilsa swore continuously and inventively at the weather, which deserved it; the snow was falling upward now, drifting from the frozen ground back into a sky with no stars to hold it, and sometimes it fell in shapes — I saw a snowfall take the outline of a woman carrying a bucket, once, walking and walking across a field before dissolving. The dead remember winter, apparently, and winter remembers them back. I kept to the middle of the group where the warmth pooled, and pretended I was there for tactical reasons.

At night they made a fire, and I sat just outside its light, because sitting *in* the light felt like presumption, and Vesper — without looking at me, without comment — moved the fire itself. Dragged it a foot toward me with an iron poker, sparks scattering, and said, "You're letting the heat escape."

"I don't feel heat," I said.

"Then you've nothing to lose by sitting closer."

It is a terrible thing, being seen by competent people. They notice everything and mention none of it, and the noticing accumulates in you like snow on a roof.

On the fourth night, Bramble's melancholy got the better of his manners, which is the only excuse I can offer for what happened. Ilsa asked — casually, sharpening a knife, casual as a landslide — "So what's it actually like? Being bound that long."

And I opened my mouth to give the performance. I have a whole routine. The wit, the shrug, *it's not so bad once you stop wanting things.* Three hundred years of polish on that line.

What came out instead was: "Do you know what I remember most? Hetta Fenn's kitchen. The day I signed. There was bread proving by the window, and I remember thinking, when this is done, when the debt's settled, I'll have bread like that again. Warm. With butter. And then I died, and the contract caught me before I finished falling, and I have spent three hundred years thinking about bread." The fire popped. Nobody spoke. "That's what it's like. Not torment. Torment has dignity. It's just — the shelf. The bread on the shelf, forever, and your hand forever six inches short."

I laughed then, because clause nine demanded it and because laughing is cheaper than weeping and I have always been a thrifty man. "Anyway! The Undercourt. Shall I tell you about the doorman? He's a suit of armor animated entirely by obligation. Doesn't speak. Just stands there radiating the sense that you're late for something. Worst feeling in creation. Bring bribes."

They let me have the deflection. Competent people, kind people. But Vesper wrote something in her little ledger that night — I saw her, I see everything, occupational hazard — and I would have given seventeen crowns, give or take three hundred years of interest, to know what it said.

* * *

On the sixth day the mountain stopped being scenery and became destination, and I felt the change in my nonexistent bones the way sailors feel a storm in their teeth. The Undercourt wakes when you approach. It has to; it is a court, courts require guests, and guests are required. The air grew thick with crushed violets. Frost formed on my companions' breath in shapes — letters, almost, invitations almost legible. And along the road, standing in the upward-falling snow at perfectly regular intervals, we began to pass the sentries: courtiers of frost and courtesy, slender as candle flames, bowing as we went by.

"They know we're coming," Ilsa muttered.

"They've known since the stars went out," I said. "Possibly since Corvane signed. Possibly since before Corvane was born. The court plays long games. Their longest game, though—" and here I stopped walking, and the others stopped, because my voice had gone somewhere none of them had heard it go, down past the wit, down to the cellar where I keep the true things. "Their longest game is exile. They were thrown down here, do you understand? Gods, thrown down like tenants in arrears, and everything they have done since — the souls, the bargains, this endless winter — is a landlord's revenge. They don't want to be rich. They want to be *owed*. Owing is the closest thing to power an exile can hold."

Vesper looked at me across the violet-scented dark. "And Corvane's soul?"

"Is the deed to everything they intend to take," I said. "Come on. The doorman hates tardiness."

We crested the last rise as I spoke, and there it was: a gate carved into the mountain's root, tall as a cathedral spire, framed in iron worked into the shapes of hands — thousands of hands, palms out, fingers open. Waiting to receive.

And beside it, radiating obligation like a stove radiates heat, stood the doorman.

I smoothed my waistcoat. Adjusted my smile, clause nine engaging, three hundred years of practice settling over me like armor over an old wound.

"Ladies," I said, "try to look solvent."
