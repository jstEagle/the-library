## 8. Road Through the Long Winter

The snow began falling on our second day out of Hallowmere, and it fell the wrong way.

I noticed it first as a tickle at my collar — a cold fingertip against the back of my neck — and looked up expecting cloud. There was no cloud. There was no sky worth the name, just a low grey ceiling pressing down like a lid on a pot. The snow was rising off the ground in slow spirals, drifting upward past my shoulders, past the dead pines, up and up until it thinned into nothing somewhere above the treetops. It made no sound. That was the worst of it. Snow should whisper. This snow climbed in silence, like it was ashamed to be seen leaving.

"Winter falls upward here," Ilsa said, not slowing. She walked the way she did everything — like the ground owed her money and she'd come to collect. "Past the third wardline the world stops knowing which way is down. You get used to it."

"I doubt that."

"You will." She glanced back at me, wind-reddened, eyes the grey of old iron. "That's what getting used to things is. Doubting them until they bore you."

Bramble drifted along at the rear of our little procession, hands folded over his waistcoat, boots an inch above the crust without ever seeming to touch it. Ghosts don't leave footprints, but Bramble left something — a faint smell of ink and candle-wax, the residue of centuries spent hunched over other people's debts.

"In my day," he announced to no one in particular, "winter fell downward, out of respect for tradition. One knew where one stood with weather." He plucked a flake from the air beside his shoulder and examined it. "This one's got a face in it."

"Don't eat it," I said.

"I wasn't *going* to eat it. I was going to catalogue it. Everything's evidence, Vesper. Everything's a ledger entry waiting to happen." He let the flake go and watched it climb. Then, quieter, in the voice he used when he forgot he was performing: "Though I'll grant this is new even to me."

We were three days from Hallowmere and four days, if Ilsa's maps could be believed, from the mountain gate. Between us and it lay the Uplands — the country where the border wards had held longest and failed hardest, the land closest to whatever had woken beneath the mountain. Corvane had wanted to come. Corvane had been ordered, by me, by Ilsa, by three separate arguments delivered in escalating volume, to stay behind and keep the Thorn sealed and running, because someone had to hold the guild together while we went to steal his soul back from a court of exiled gods. He'd agreed in the end with the particular gracelessness of a man who knows he is a liability and hates it. He'd shaken my hand at the gate like we were concluding a business arrangement, and then he'd gripped it too hard and too long, and neither of us had said anything true.

The ledgers would have said something. Ledgers always know how.

Now there were three of us and one ghost walking north through a world coming apart politely at the seams, and the snow kept climbing, and I kept catching myself listening for a god who wasn't going to answer.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a waystation, a stone circle with a roof like a broken bowl. Ilsa built a fire from pine-knots that burned green and sullen. Bramble sat as close to it as a ghost can sit to a fire, which is to say he arranged himself near it and radiated longing.

"Does it warm you?" I asked him. I hadn't meant to ask; the question slipped out the way questions do when the silence gets long enough.

"Nothing warms me," Bramble said cheerfully. "But I remember warmth. Memory's nearly as good. Nearly." He turned his hands over the flames anyway, a habit older than his death. "You know what I collected, before? Before the binding?"

"You've never said."

"Luck. Borrowed luck, mostly. Little coins of it. A man gambles away more luck than he spends gold, over a lifetime, and somebody has to gather it up when he dies or it goes sour in the ground." He smiled with all his teeth and none of his heart. "I was very good at it. The Undercourt noticed. That's how I ended up bound to the oldest contract in your guild's vault. They lend people out, you know. Like tools. Like books."

"And now Vesper's quest voids your binding," Ilsa said from across the fire, flat as slate. "So you're helping us because helping us unhelps them."

"I'm helping you," Bramble said, "because after four hundred years I should like to see what I do when I'm free. Also because the Undercourt's wine cellars are divine and I never once got invited." He tipped an invisible hat. "Both things are true. Most true things are two things."

Ilsa snorted. It was, I would learn, the sound she made instead of laughing, and I filed it away the way I filed everything: carefully, under its proper heading.

I took first watch, because I always take first watch, because sleep and I have had an arrangement since I was fifteen years old and it does not involve much sleeping. I sat with my back to the fire and my face to the dark and I did the thing I do every night, the ritual ten years deep: I laid my hand flat on the ground and I spoke to Vael.

*Threshold-keeper. Door-opener. God of every between-place. If you hear me —*

Silence. Not even the ordinary silence of a god minding its own business. A silence with weight to it, a silence like a held breath, like a room where someone has just stopped speaking.

"If you're going to pray," Ilsa said, materializing at my shoulder with the soundless tread of a woman who'd spent twenty years sneaking up on wards, "you might do it facing something."

"He's a god of thresholds. The ground counts."

"The ground here doesn't count for anything." She lowered herself beside me, uninvited, and stared out into the dark. "Your god. Vael. The Thorn swears its blades to him, yes? All of you?"

"All of us who take the full oath."

"And he's been silent ten years."

"Ten years, two months, eleven days." I didn't look at her. "Give or take."

Something moved in the dark beyond the waystation wall — a shape, then not a shape. Ilsa's hand found my wrist and pressed once: *wait*. We both went still. Out in the snowfield, the upward-falling flakes had begun to gather, slowly, deliberately, into forms. A woman bent over a well. A soldier kneeling. A child running with arms out. They stood for a breath each, perfect and detailed — I could see the buttons on the soldier's coat, the ribbons in the child's hair — and then they came apart into flakes again and rose.

"The snow remembers," Ilsa said softly. "I told you. Past the third wardline, it holds the shapes of everyone who died out here. Every winter it shows them again." Her jaw tightened. "I maintained these wards for six years, Blackwood. I know what they were supposed to keep out. What I want to know is what they were keeping *in*."

"That makes two of us."

She looked at me then, firelight and starless dark trading places on her face, and for a moment neither of us was performing. Two proud women, sitting in the ruins of her life's work, watching the dead get remembered by weather. Something passed between us that I didn't have a ledger column for.

"Get some sleep," she said finally. "Tomorrow we cross the fourth line. Tomorrow it starts hunting us."

* * *

It started hunting us before noon.

I heard it as music first — a faint, sweet sound carried on wind that shouldn't have been able to carry anything, because the wind was busy hauling snow uphill. Violins, or something shaped like violins the way a mask is shaped like a face. Then the temperature dropped so fast my breath cracked in the air, and the light changed, going silver and formal, like the inside of a mirror.

"Down," Ilsa snapped. "Off the road. Now."

We dove for the cover of a fallen pine, and through the branches I saw them come: five figures walking abreast up the road as if the road were a ballroom floor. They were beautiful the way frost is beautiful, all filigree and precision, coats of white brocade that moved like smoke, faces smooth and smiling and utterly without pores or pores' imperfection — faces like porcelain painted by someone who had seen humans only in descriptions. Each wore a sash of pale blue. Each bowed, in perfect unison, toward the treeline. Toward *us*.

"Courtiers," Bramble breathed. His voice had lost all its comedy. "Undercourt scouts. Oh, hell's hinges, they've sent *couriers of the season*. Vesper, whatever happens, do not accept anything from them. No gift, no invitation, no compliment answered. Courtesy is their weapon. Every politeness is a hook."

"They're made of frost," I whispered.

"They're made of *manners*. The frost is just what manners look like when nothing living is wearing them."

The lead courtier stopped. Its smile widened by a precise half-inch. When it spoke, its voice was lovely and terrible, like glass learning to sing.

"*Travelers.* How good of you to be traveling. The Court extends its regards to guests upon the road, and its compliments to the lady of the wards, whom it remembers." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "*Ilsa Rennick. Your work was much admired. Before.*"

I felt Ilsa go rigid beside me. I put my hand on her arm. *Don't answer. Don't answer.*

But the courtier wasn't finished. Its head tilted, and its porcelain gaze slid past her, past Bramble, and settled on me with the attention of an appraiser finding a flaw in a gemstone — or a treasure.

"*And the sworn blade.* The god-touched one. The Regent has been so looking forward to meeting you." It extended one white-gloved hand, palm up, an invitation, a request, a trap with fingers. "*Come out, lady of the Thorn. The road is cold, and courtesy requires we offer you warmth.*"

Every word landed on me like a physical weight. I felt the pull of it — the genuine, awful pull of wanting to be polite, wanting to answer, wanting to step onto that road and be received. My foot shifted. An inch. Another.

A knife buried itself in the snow six inches from my boot.

I blinked. Ilsa had thrown it — thrown it at *me*, at the ground beside me, hard enough that it stood quivering, and her hand was already reaching for the second.

"It's not offering warmth," she snarled at the courtiers, loud enough to crack the courtesy-spell holding me. "It's offering a *doorway*. And I'll tell you what I told the Wardens' Guild when they expelled me: I don't build doors for things like you."

The lead courtier's smile did not falter, but the temperature dropped another ten degrees, and the five of them came apart — literally, beautifully, horribly — into spiraling shards of ice that reassembled mid-air as something faster, thinner, hungrier. Blades of frosted silver appeared in their gloved hands. The music turned discordant.

"Bramble!" I shouted.

"On it!" The ghost shot forward through the trees like a rumor, and wherever he passed, the courtiers *flinched* — four hundred years of debt-collector's presence, the aura of a thing that has audited gods, and even creatures made of etiquette recognized a process-server when they saw one. "You have outstanding obligations!" he cried with relish, drifting through the lead scout's chest. "Itemized! With interest!"

It bought us three seconds. In those three seconds, Ilsa moved like a woman settling an old grudge — ward-smith's sigils flaring on her gloves, carving a line of blue-white fire in the snow between us and them, shouting words in a grammar that hurt to hear. The line blazed up into a makeshift ward, crude, ugly, hers alone, no guild behind it, no license, just skill and fury.

And the courtiers hit it, and screamed, and came through anyway, because the wards were dying everywhere now and even a master-smith's quick work frayed at the edges of the Uplands. One of them lunged past the failing line, blade arcing for my throat —

— and Ilsa was there.

Not fast. *There.* As if she'd known where the blow would fall before the courtier did, she stepped inside its arc, caught its wrist with one gauntleted hand, and drove her short-hafted smith's hammer up under its chin. The porcelain face shattered. The whole creature burst — not into water, not into mist, but into a brief, glittering cloud of what looked like tiny letters, dissolving as they fell.

Then she turned and put herself bodily between me and the other four, hammer up, blood on her cheek from a cut I hadn't seen her take, and grinned at me with all the warmth of a drawn blade.

"Well?" she said. "Are you going to help, ledger-keeper, or are you going to write about it?"

I drew my sword. Ten years of silent gods, ten years of routine and loneliness and dry competence, and in that moment none of it mattered, because here — here was something I understood. A fight. A debt being paid in the only currency that never devalued.

"Together," I said.

"Together," she agreed, and we broke the ward-line ourselves and met them.

* * *

Afterward, we stood in the wreckage of the skirmish — three shattered courtiers, two fled back toward the mountain trailing discordant music, Bramble dusted in frost and insufferably pleased with himself — and Ilsa cleaned her hammer with the mechanical calm of someone who had done far worse and would again.

"You saved my life," I said.

"I threw a knife at your feet."

"You threw a knife at my feet and then you stood between me and four of them. I count that as saving." I held out my hand. She looked at it the way she looked at most things — suspiciously, like it might contain fine print. Then she took it. Her grip was calloused and crushing and honest.

"Terms of debt," she said. "You clear my name with the Wardens' Guild, and we're even."

"We were already even. This puts you ahead."

"Then work harder, Blackwood." But something in her face had shifted, some door unbarred a crack, and when Bramble floated over crowing about how he'd *threatened an agent of the Pale Regent with litigation*, she laughed — actually laughed, one short bark, quickly smothered, but real.

We walked on as the false afternoon dimmed. Behind us the snow kept rising, carrying its shapes of the dead up toward a sky with no stars to receive them. Ahead, far off, I thought I saw — or dreamed I saw — a single point of light where no light should be. Warm. Yellow. Steady.

An inn, impossibly, at the edge of the map.

"There's no town marked within forty miles," I said.

"No," Ilsa said quietly, staring at it. "But there's a story. And out here, that's the same thing."
