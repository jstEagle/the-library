## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

I have been dead for two hundred and forty years, give or take a decade I spent trapped in a strongbox in Marrow's Crossing, and in all that time I have learned exactly one thing worth passing on: the living are loud. They clatter through the world like coins in a tin cup, all jingle and no sense.

The Undercourt was quiet.

That should have been my first warning. Any place where gods go to sulk ought to howl — ought to shake the stone with grievance and thunder with old grudges. But when the mountain gate swallowed us whole, when the road of black glass tilted down into the dark like a tongue sliding into a throat, what I heard was *hush*. The particular hush of a great house at night, of servants moving on soft feet, of a master who must not be woken.

"Everyone stop breathing so loudly," I said. It is a joke. Dead men don't breathe. Nobody laughed, which in my experience is the correct response to most of my jokes, but Vesper's hand went to her knife anyway, which meant she'd understood me better than she let on.

The gate itself deserves a mention, if only because Ilsa Rennick stopped walking when she saw it and stood there with an expression I can only describe as a smith watching someone hammer nails with a masterpiece. It was a door without a doorframe — a slab of night standing upright in the rock, its surface crawling with ward-script older than any alphabet Ilsa knew, and she knew eleven of them, having complained about each one individually during our long winter march. The script wasn't written. That's the thing about it. It was *grown*, like frost on a window, and it moved when you weren't looking, rearranging itself the way a sleeping man rearranges his limbs.

"Those aren't wards," Ilsa said quietly. "Wards keep things out. These are... ledgers."

"Everything here is a ledger," I told her, and felt my binding pull tight around my ribs, that old contract-chain the Thorn's first page had knotted through my ghost-stuff two centuries ago. "Welcome to the family business."

* * *

Let me tell you how you get into the Undercourt, because nobody will explain it and everyone pretends they always knew.

You walk. The road goes down longer than any road has a right to — three days by my counting, though time down there is a suggestion rather than a law — and the walls of the passage change as you descend. First they're plain granite, honest and dull. Then veins of quartz appear, then garnets the size of fists, then things with no name above ground: crystals that held light from rooms that didn't exist, stones that were warm like skin, one patch of wall that whispered my name in my mother's voice, which was rude of it, since I never knew my mother.

Vesper walked like a blade being carried — sheathed but aware of it. Corvane walked like a man returning to a house he'd sold, cataloguing everything he'd left behind. Ilsa walked like a woman reading the walls, and occasionally touching them, and once — I saw this clearly, and I have excellent eyes for the dead — apologizing to a vein of crystal under her breath, as if it might report back to someone.

And Bramble? Bramble floated along at the rear, rattling his chains for effect, because a debt-collector ghost who doesn't rattle is just a draft with opinions.

"Question," I said, on the second day down, drifting alongside Vesper while she pretended not to be counting her heartbeats. "When we get there, and some courtier made of frost and courtesy asks you your business — what do we say?"

"We say what's true," Vesper said. "Mostly true. We're brokers from the Gilded Thorn, come to negotiate a debt settlement on behalf of a client." She glanced sideways at Corvane, who was pretending very hard to be somewhere else. "That's you, incidentally. The client."

"I'm the collateral," Corvane said. His voice was flat as slate. "Don't dress it up for these people. They'll smell the varnish."

"He's right," I said cheerfully. "They can smell varnish. They can smell intention. They can smell the last lie you told, actually, if they lean close enough — it lingers in the lungs. So don't lie. Mislead magnificently, but don't lie. There's a difference, and the Undercourt has spent ten thousand years learning exactly where the line runs, because the line is where they live."

"And what do you know about where they live?" Ilsa asked, not looking at me.

"Everything," I said. "I collected for them before I collected for the Thorn. Before I was dead, even." I let the chains sing a little, a small sad bell-song. "You think a ghost gets bound to a guild contract out of nowhere? Somebody has to die owing something first. I owed the Undercourt. Then the Thorn bought my debt, and when I died mid-collection — embarrassing story involving a bridge and a bet I shouldn't have taken — they kept me on. Dead collectors work cheaper. No wages. Just the binding."

Vesper looked at me then, properly, with those ledger-keeper's eyes that weighed everything and recorded more than they showed. "You've been inside the court."

"I've been *invited* to the court," I corrected. "Which is worse. Invitations imply you might come back."

* * *

The descent ended at a threshold that wasn't a threshold, and I want to be precise about this because precision matters below the mountain the way bread matters above it.

There was no line drawn on the ground. There was no gate, no arch, no guard. There was simply a point on the road where the air changed texture — where the dark stopped being absence and became *attention* — and every mortal instinct in every body behind me flinched at once, horses included, though we'd left the horses two days up with more sense than we had.

"That's the border," I said. "Of the court proper. Everything past this point belongs to them the way a word belongs to a sentence. Step carefully, speak politely, and whatever happens —" I looked at each of them in turn, and I'll admit my rattle went quiet, because this part mattered — "*do not thank anyone.*"

"Why?" Vesper asked.

"Because thanks creates debt," I said. "And debt, down here, is currency, and currency is blood, and blood is *power*, and they will hand you beautiful things all day long hoping you'll slip and say the word. A compliment. A kindness. A cup of wine pressed into your hands. Take nothing freely offered unless you can name its price, and thank no one for anything, ever, not even the floor for holding you up. Especially not the floor."

Corvane crossed first. Of course he did. He'd crossed before, a century ago, young and desperate and full of the terrible arithmetic of a man saving something he loved by selling the thing nobody could see. I watched him step over the attention-line and shudder, once, like a dog shaking off water, and then stand straighter, because shame makes some men taller.

Then Vesper. Then Ilsa. Then me, last, drifting across with my chains held silent in both hands, and the moment my ghost-feet touched court ground, the binding on my soul *sang*.

Not painfully. That would have been almost kind. It sang like a tuning fork finding its note, like a key turning in a lock it hadn't seen in centuries, and far away in the deep places of the mountain, something enormous rolled over in its sleep and noticed.

"They know we're here," I announced, unnecessarily. Ghosts announce things. It's most of what we do.

* * *

Now. The court itself.

You've heard stories, probably, if you've heard anything — of caverns vast as cathedrals, of geodes split open into ballrooms, of chandeliers made from frozen starlight. All true. All understated. Words are thin broth for a place like this; they fill the belly but feed nothing.

The first hall opened around us like the inside of a thought. Amethyst, cathedral-vast, its crystal walls faceted into ten thousand angled mirrors, so that wherever you looked you saw yourself multiplied, diminished, receding — a hundred small versions of you walking a hundred small halls, none of them quite reaching anywhere. Light came from nowhere in particular and pooled in certain corners like cats. And everywhere, everywhere, *couriers*: pale figures in clothes that moved like smoke given tailoring, gliding between conversations with trays of things that glittered and breathed.

And the courtiers. Ah, the courtiers.

I'd told the others about frost and courtesy, and they'd imagined monsters. What they got was worse: beauty. Tall, elegant figures whose skin held the blue-white shimmer of ice over deep water, whose faces were composed rather than born, whose smiles arrived precisely on schedule like tax collectors. They wore winter the way other beings wear jewelry — frost blooming along their collars, snowflakes orbiting their shoulders in slow devotion. When one of them turned to look at us, I watched Ilsa's hand drift toward her hammer and Vesper's drift toward her knife, and both women caught themselves, and both pretended they hadn't.

A courtier approached. Its face resolved into pleasantness the way fog resolves into shapes you half-recognize.

"Honored guests," it said, and its voice was the sound of a frozen lake settling. "The court bids you welcome. You are expected."

"Expected," Vesper repeated. Her broker-voice, level as a scale. "By whom?"

"The Regent holds audience at the turning of the deep hour." The courtier's smile widened by a measured fraction, calibrated to charm, achieving instead the exact temperature of a grave. "Until then, you are guests of the court. Rooms have been prepared. Refreshment is offered." It gestured, and a second figure drifted near bearing a tray, and on the tray sat five cups of wine the color of garnets, steaming faintly, smelling of summer — actual summer, cut grass and warm stone, a season none of us had smelled in months, and I watched hunger move across all their faces like weather.

"Don't," I murmured.

"It's just wine," Ilsa said.

"It's just *memory*," I said. "Pressed and bottled and poured. Drink it and you'll taste a summer you never had, and you'll love it, and you'll spend the rest of your life trying to buy that taste again, and the court sells it by the cup, and the price is always reasonable, and the price is never money." I drifted between them and the tray and spread my arms wide, chains swinging, playing the fool as I have played him for two hundred years because the fool is allowed to say true things. "First taste is free. That's how every debt begins. Ask me how I know. Go on. Ask."

The courtier regarded me with something that was almost amusement, if amusement could freeze solid and be displayed on a shelf.

"The ghost remembers us," it said.

"The ghost never forgot," I said pleasantly. "Forgetting is the one thing you don't sell. Pity."

* * *

They gave us rooms, because refusing would have been a statement and statements are expensive down here. The rooms were lovely in the way that a spider's web is lovely — engineered, patient, designed around an occupant. Vesper's chamber had a bed of black silk and a writing desk with paper and ink, and I saw her clock immediately that the ink was there so that anything she wrote could be read later by whoever read such things. Ilsa's had a forge, a small perfect forge, and I watched her face do something complicated, because a forge is an apology and a temptation in equal measure to a ward-smith in exile.

My room had a mirror. Just a mirror. The court has a sense of humor. Dead men and mirrors, ha ha.

We reconvened in the corridor outside Vesper's door, which was the only territory any of us trusted, and held council in whispers while courtiers glided past at intervals, each pretending not to see us, each seeing everything.

"Three hours until this 'deep hour,'" Vesper said. "Bramble. Walk us through it. What does an audience look like? What does the Regent want?"

"The Regent wants what the Regent always wants," I said, "which is the same thing every exiled god wants: to matter again. She was endings, once — not death, mind, endings, which is a subtler trade — and they put her down here when the sky reorganized itself, and she has spent ten thousand years building a court where she is still necessary. Souls are her treasury. Debts are her army. And your guildmaster—" I nodded at Corvane, who stood against the amethyst wall looking like a man at his own funeral, which, in fairness, "—is a coin in that treasury with a century's interest grown on him like mold."

"How much interest?" Corvane asked. Quietly.

I could have lied. I want that noted. I could have been gentle, and I chose not to be, because down here gentleness gets people killed and honesty merely wounds.

"A hundred years of compounding," I said, "on a principal of one immortal soul, in a court where time is currency and you've been paying in it since the day you signed. Guildmaster — you aren't a debt anymore. You're a fortune. And fortunes down here don't get collected." My chains shifted. "They get *displayed*."

He closed his eyes. Vesper stepped closer to him — half a step, barely anything, the smallest movement in the world — and I saw her hand hover near his sleeve and not land, because Vesper Blackwood touches people the way she writes in ledgers: rarely, deliberately, and only when the entry is final.

"Then we move before the audience," she said. "Tonight. While they think we're waiting politely—"

"No," I said.

She looked at me.

"You don't steal from a host *during* the feast," I said. "You especially don't steal when the host is a goddess of endings and the vault you want is guarded by entropy itself. We attend the audience. We listen. The Regent invited you, Vesper — do you understand what that means? Gods don't invite. Gods *summon*. An invitation means she wants something from you that requires your consent, or your belief that you're choosing. Which means there's a bargain on the table already, whether you can see it or not. Our job tonight is to find out what she thinks she's buying." I let my chains fall still. "And then we decide what she's actually paying."

Ilsa snorted softly. "You talk like a merchant."

"I *was* a merchant," I said. "Of the only commodity that matters. Everything else is inventory."

* * *

The ballroom, when the deep hour came and the courtiers came to collect us, was a geode the size of a fallen cathedral, split open and polished and hung with light. Crystals rose from the floor in slow columns, violet and white and one deep red like a wound, and between them the court moved in patterns too old to be called dances — processions, really, negotiations wearing music as a disguise. Somewhere a string instrument played itself, or was played by someone invisible, and the melody was beautiful and slightly wrong, the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes follow you.

And the souls. This is the part I need you to understand, the part that curdles wonder into horror the way milk curdles in a storm.

The powerful ones — the exiles, the fallen divinities, the things that had been worshipped before worship changed shape — wore their holdings. Not openly, not crudely. But when one of them laughed, I heard echoes in the laugh, dozens of voices, the harvested sounds of the people they owned. When another grew bored and turned away from a conversation, the candles nearest her dimmed, briefly, as if borrowing her indifference. And along the edges of the hall, set into niches in the crystal like votive lamps, burned small flames. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

Each one a person. Each one dimmer or brighter according to how much of themselves they'd had left when they were taken.

"There," I said quietly, to Vesper, and nodded toward a niche perhaps thirty feet along the wall, where a flame burned smaller than the others, guttering low, almost apologetic. Even from across the hall I knew it. Two hundred years of collecting teaches you to recognize a soul the way a shepherd knows sheep.

Corvane Ash's flame flickered when we looked at it. Just once. Like something noticing it was being watched, and flinching.

"Oh," Vesper said. It wasn't a word so much as a wound escaping. Her face did nothing — her face is a fortress, I'll grant her that — but her hand found her knife-grip and stayed there, and her breathing went shallow and counted, the way it does when she's holding the world together by arithmetic.

Across the hall, the crowd parted.

She came slowly, because endings are never hurried. Taller than the courtiers, paler than the frost they wore, dressed in grey that moved like ash after a fire — not falling, rising, forever rising. Her face was kind. That was the horror of it, the thing I'd tried to warn them about and failed: the Pale Regent was kind the way winter is kind to the exhausted, the way sleep is kind to the drowning.

Her gaze found Vesper first, and lingered, and I saw the Regent's expression shift by one precise degree into something that was not surprise, because nothing surprised her, but was adjacent to it. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite.

Then her gaze found me, and the binding around my soul pulled so tight I nearly rattled apart.

"*The collector,*" she said, and her voice reached every corner of the geode at once, gentle as snowfall. "Two hundred years in the Thorn's service. Tell me, little ghost — do you still remember the terms of your original contract?"

Every eye in the court turned to me. Every flame in every niche leaned, the way flowers lean at sun.

And I opened my mouth to answer, and discovered — with a cold that had nothing to do with the frost on anyone's shoulders — that I couldn't remember.
