## 12. An Audience with the Pale Regent

They came for us at the turn of the third hour, which in the Undercourt meant nothing at all, because there was no sun to divide the dark and the courtiers kept time by the slow breathing of the mountain instead.

The herald was made of frost. I want to write that plainly, because my memory keeps trying to soften it into something survivable: a tall figure of translucent ice wearing the shape of a woman, wearing courtesy the way other people wore clothes, and when she bowed, snow fell *upward* off her shoulders and dissolved before it reached the ceiling of the geode hall.

"The Pale Regent will see the brokers now," she said. Her voice was the sound of a lake freezing — a long, crystalline creak. "Please leave your weapons. Please leave your anger. Please leave everything you are not prepared to lose."

"We keep our weapons," Ilsa said flatly. Her hand rested on the haft of her hammer with the ease of long marriage.

"Of course." The frost-woman inclined her head, and I understood, with a small cold shock, that the request had been a test, and we had passed by refusing. "The Regent finds that guests who surrender their edges too easily are usually hiding none."

Bramble drifted at my shoulder, and though he had spent two days coaching us on court etiquette — never accept a gift without naming its price, never sit in a chair offered before it's earned, never, under any circumstance, say *thank you* — he had gone quiet as we descended. Now he murmured, so low only I could hear: "She'll be reasonable. That's the worst part. Remember I told you."

I remembered. I had thought he was being grim for effect. Bramble was many things, but he was rarely wrong about debts.

We walked. The hall to the throne chamber was carved through a single geode split lengthwise, its walls blazing with amethyst and something darker — a purple so deep it was nearly a sound, a low note struck on an instrument of bone. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the way light behaves underground, sourceless and accusing. Our shadows were cast in four directions at once, and one of them did not move when I did.

I did not look at that shadow again. There is a kind of fear that feeds on acknowledgment, and I have always starved mine on principle.

Vael's silence rode in my chest like a stone sewn there years ago, so old a weight I had stopped noticing until tonight, when every step toward the Regent made it heavier. Ten years of praying into a well with no bottom-sound. Ten years of keeping the oath anyway, because an oath kept in silence is worth ten spoken into answer — that was what I told myself, most nights, and some nights I even believed it.

At the threshold of the throne chamber, something brushed the edge of my senses. Faint. Faint as warmth from a door left open somewhere far away in a winter country.

I stopped.

"Vesper." Ilsa's voice, careful.

"I felt—" I closed my mouth. It had been nothing. A geode humming. The mountain's breath. Gods did not flicker back to life in the halls of their exiles.

We went in.

* * *

The Pale Regent received us seated, which Bramble had warned me was either a courtesy or an insult depending on how her hands were placed, and her hands were folded in her lap like a woman at prayer, so I elected to take it as courtesy and hope I was not about to die of etiquette.

She was beautiful the way endings are beautiful. Not a skeleton, not a shroud — a woman of middle years with a face you might pass at a market and forget, except that her eyes held the particular stillness of the last light in a house after everyone has gone to bed. Her hair was white without being aged, the white of birch bark, of salt, of paper waiting for a final line. She wore grey silk and no crown, and somehow the absence of the crown was more terrible than any circlet would have been.

Around her, the throne room opened like the inside of a geode the size of a cathedral. Courtiers lined the walls — frost-shaped, smoke-shaped, one or two wearing flesh so old it had gone translucent as parchment. They did not whisper. They *attended*, the way an audience attends a play they have seen a thousand times and still hope will end differently.

"Vesper Blackwood," the Regent said. Her voice was quiet and complete, like the last word of a sentence. "Sworn blade of Vael. Keeper of ledgers at the Gilded Thorn. You audit your guild's inventory every seventh night, you sleep four hours and dislike dreaming, and you have not truly prayed in six years — you have performed prayer, which is different." She unfolded her hands. "Sit."

There was a chair beside her dais that had not been there a moment before. Of course there hadn't. Bramble's rules turned over in my head: never sit in a chair offered before it's earned.

"No, thank you," I said, and heard Bramble make a sound like a man swallowing his own ghost — because I had said *thank you*, the forbidden word, and the whole court went still as held breath.

Then the Regent laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. That frightened me more than cruelty would have. It was warm, brief, genuinely amused — the laugh of someone who has been alone a very long time and has just been reminded what company sounds like.

"Oh, *well done*," she said. "You broke both rules in one breath. Refused the chair and thanked me for it. Do you know how long it has been since anyone in this court surprised me?" She looked at the assembled courtiers, and the look was almost fond. "This is why mortals were worth exiling ourselves for, once. You are all such magnificent mistakes."

The court resumed attending. I remained standing. Ilsa stood at my right shoulder, Bramble at my left, and behind us the doors sealed themselves with the soft finality of a ledger closing.

"You know why we've come," I said.

"I know why *you* think you've come. You believe you're here to steal back a soul your guildmaster sold carelessly, the way men sell things they don't understand they own." She tilted her head. "Corvane Ash. Sit down, Vesper Blackwood. This will take longer than your pride wants to stand for."

I sat. On my terms, in a chair I dragged across the flagstones myself, loudly. If the court wanted theater, I would give them a stubborn bookkeeper in steel-capped boots.

* * *

"Tell me what you found in your ledgers," the Regent said.

"A page in my handwriting dated three years from now. It names this court. It names Corvane's soul as collateral against a debt of standing." I kept my voice level, clerk-flat. "I assumed forgery. I assumed trap. I came anyway, because the alternative was letting the winter eat the borderlands while I audited my suspicions."

"And the wards?"

"Broke the night the stars went out. All along the frontier. Ilsa Rennick can tell you the mechanics better than I can—"

"I don't need the mechanics." For the first time, something moved behind the stillness of her face — a flicker, quickly mastered, that I would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It looked like grief. "I know exactly why the wards failed. I have known for a century. Sit with that a moment, keeper. I have been watching your borders unravel in my calculations for a hundred years, the way you watch a candle burn down and know precisely when the dark arrives, and being unable to stop it, because stopping it was never mine to do."

Ilsa shifted. Bramble had gone gray around the edges, which for a ghost is the equivalent of going pale.

"Explain the debt," I said.

"Corvane Ash was twenty-six years old," the Regent said, "and he had eleven people he loved and a guild with three coins to its name and a war coming up the valley like weather. You've read his early ledgers. You know the year I mean."

I did. The founding year. The entries got strange that spring — income from sources Vesper-the-auditor had never been able to trace, contracts with blank client lines, a payment recorded simply as *consideration rendered, account settled in full*. I'd spent three nights of my life puzzling over that entry, once, and gone to bed feeling like I'd been reading someone's diary through a keyhole.

"He came beneath the mountain," the Regent continued, "through roads that have since fallen, and he knelt where you are not kneeling, and he asked me for a century. One hundred years of prosperity for the Gilded Thorn. Contracts that found them. Luck that bent toward them. Survival — for eleven specific people whose names he recited, and I will tell you he recited them like a litany, like a prayer, and if your god had been listening in that moment, keeper, He would have recognized the grammar."

The stone in my chest turned over.

"The price," I said.

"His soul. Payable at the century's end." She said it the way I would read a line item aloud. No drama. Drama would have been mercy; it would have let me hate her cleanly. "That is the standard rate, and he agreed to it with his eyes open, because the alternative was burying those eleven people, and Corvane Ash has never in his life been able to bear burying someone he loves. Ask him sometime about the slavers' caravan he robbed to feed his first hires. Ask him about the grave he dug for the boy who didn't make it out."

"That doesn't explain the winter," Ilsa said, her voice rough. "A soul's a soul. One man's debt doesn't unmake border wards and put out stars."

"No," the Regent agreed. "Debt does that."

And then she explained interest.

I have kept ledgers since I could hold a pen. I understand compound calculation the way soldiers understand drill — in the hands, below thought. What she laid out for me was compounding of a kind I had never seen, because it compounded across *ontologies*, across kinds of owing. His soul was collateral. Collateral secures the loan. But the loan itself — the century of luck, of survival, of impossible inventory flowing through the Thorn's vaults like blood through a vein — every borrowed fortune drawn against a godless bank gathers its own weight, and that weight must be carried somewhere, and the Undercourt carries it the way mountains carry their own mass: downward. Into foundations. Into the deep structures of the world.

"The luck your guild traded," she said, "was mined. Every bottled memory, every second chance, every stolen shadow — drawn against accounts I opened, backed by holdings I hoarded, and the interest on a century of withdrawals comes due as *entropy*. As decay. As heat leaving the world." She spread one pale hand, and frost bloomed from her fingertips across the armrest, delicate as fern. "Your border wards were woven from a god's sacrifice, and they ran for generations on the assumption that the world owed itself balance. My collections unbalance it. The wards felt the imbalance before your instruments did. The stars felt it before the wards. Your guildmaster's soul is the anchor-stone of the whole architecture, keeper — and the collection notice is falling over your headquartners right now, one snowflake at a time."

Silence, of the kind that has texture. The courtiers attended. Somewhere far above, I knew, snow was falling upward over the mountain, remembering shapes.

"So collect the soul," Ilsa said. "Take it and go. Why hasn't the debt been called?"

"Because calling it cracks the wards permanently, and cracking the wards lets in the thing the wards were built to hold out, and even I —" she paused, and chose her next words with the care of a woman stepping onto ice — "even I do not particularly want what lives outside the sky to come inside it. Exile teaches patience. And perspective."

"Then what," I said slowly, "do you want?"

* * *

She rose, finally, and descending the three steps of her dais she became, briefly, less terrifying rather than more — motion humanized her, gave her hips and heels and the small labor of walking. She stopped an arm's length from me. Up close her eyes were the color of the last page of a book.

"I want to go home," she said.

The words landed in the throne room and the whole court flinched, all together, like grass under wind.

"We were not always beneath mountains, keeper. We were gods of small necessary things. Endings, yes — I was endings — but endings in the service of continuance. The harvest cut so the field feeds. The door closes so the house holds warmth. When the bright gods reorganized heaven — and they did reorganize it, whatever your temples tell you — we who dealt in termination were judged impolite. Unclean. We were invited to leave." Her smile was thin as a knife's edge seen end-on. "So we left. And we learned to survive on what mortals pay us, and survival curdles into appetite if you're not careful, and we were not always careful. I will own that. Some of my court became monsters because exile is a long lesson and not everyone learns it gently."

"But you," she went on, "you I find interesting. Sworn to a silent god. Keeper of a ledger of impossible things. A woman who breaks two sacred rules in one breath and drags her own chair." She crouched, deliberately, bringing her eyes level with mine, and I will not pretend my heart was steady. "I am going to offer you a bargain, Vesper Blackwood, and I want you to hear all of it before you refuse, because everyone refuses first and thinks later, and you are not everyone."

"Say it."

"Corvane's soul returns to him. The debt transfers." She let that settle. "Not to another soul — I have no interest in trading one mortal's damnation for another's; that is arithmetic, not resolution. The debt transfers to *you* as keeper. Not your soul. Your *name*. Struck from every record — birth rolls, guild registers, god-ledgers, memory. Unwritten. In exchange, the compounding stops. The entropy already loose in your world runs out its course and stills, the wards knit, the winter halts."

Behind me, Bramble made a sound I had never heard from him — something between a sob and a hymn.

"And the souls you hold," I said. My voice came out lower than I intended. "The vault of them. All the collateral you've hoarded for centuries."

"Released," she said. "Every flame returned to whoever's living body still walks. Those whose bodies are gone go onward, wherever onward goes — honestly released, freely, and you may audit the release yourself, keeper. I know you would insist."

It was too generous. That was the thing my gut kept snarling while my mind ran the numbers and found them, appallingly, *fair*. Bramble had told me she'd be reasonable. He had never said reasonable would feel like drowning in warm water — painless, enveloping, and impossible to fight, because there is no purchase on kindness from the thing you came to kill.

"Why your name?" Ilsa asked sharply. "What does a name buy that a soul doesn't?"

The Regent straightened. And here, at last, the mask moved — not slipping, *moving*, arranged deliberately into something older and wearier.

"Because names are how gods are known," she said. "And I have been a very long time without one that anyone alive would recognize. Strike Vesper Blackwood from the ledgers, and something must hold her place at the threshold of things — a gap, a vacancy, an unwritten line. Vacancy attracts." Her eyes rested on me, and they were terribly gentle. "You swore yourself to the god of thresholds, child. I am offering to make you one."

The geode hall hummed its low dark note. The frost courtiers leaned in, all of them, hunger and hope in equal measure, and I understood suddenly that I was not the first person to be offered this, but I might be the first to be offered it *honestly*.

"One condition," I heard myself say. "Before I answer anything. I want to see him."

The Regent's brows drew together, faintly. "Corvane?"

"No." I stood, and my knees were steady, and I did not know where the steadiness came from unless something had answered me somewhere far away, at the edge of hearing, like warmth from a distant open door. "My god. You said He would have recognized the grammar of Corvane's prayer. You've watched the wards fail for a hundred years. You've done your calculations." I met the last-light eyes. "You know where Vael is. And you know He isn't silent at all."

For the first time in a century, I suspect, the goddess of endings looked *caught*.

"Ah," she said softly. "So you felt Him. Down at my threshold, of all places." She glanced at her court, and something passed among them like a current, and when she turned back to me her expression had become, unmistakably, pity.

"Keeper," she said, "before you agree to anything I've offered — there is something you need to see. And I am afraid that once you see it, you may never forgive me for showing you."

She extended her hand, pale and waiting, and the doors of the throne room unsealed themselves behind us with a sound like the first page of a book turning.

"This way," said the Pale Regent. "Let me show you where your god has been."
