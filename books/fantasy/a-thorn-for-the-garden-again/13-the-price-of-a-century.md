## 13. The Price of a Century

There is a particular smell to a room where hope has just died. Corvane Ash had learned it young: tallow smoke gone sour, wet wool, the copper tang of coins counted and recounted and still not enough. It hung in the rented room above the fishmonger's stall in Marrowgate the night he decided to sell his soul.

He was twenty-six years old and he had eleven people to feed.

They were not a guild yet. They were a rumor with beds — a smuggler who could walk through locked doors if you paid her in songs, a forger with shaking hands and perfect penmanship, two sisters who could bottle a memory so faithfully you'd weep at the opening, and seven more like them, all of them hungry, all of them loyal to him for reasons he never understood and never dared question. Winter had come early that year, the old-fashioned kind, the kind that killed. The Thorn's coffers held forty-one crowns, an IOU written on a napkin, and a bottled thunderstorm nobody wanted to buy.

And Marta was sick. Little Marta, nine years old, the forger's daughter, coughing blood into a rag in the corner while her mother pretended not to see and everyone pretended not to see her pretending. A healer of the Sable Order could fix her. The Sable Order charged three hundred crowns and did not barter.

Corvane sat at the table with the ledger open before him — the first ledger, a butcher's account book stolen from a man who no longer needed it — and he did the arithmetic of despair. He added up everything they owned. He subtracted what winter would eat. The number that remained was not a number at all. It was a door.

He had heard the stories, of course. Everyone in the trade had. Beneath the mountain at the world's edge there was a court that bought and sold in currencies no mortal mint touched: names, years, first loves, souls. The exiled gods, they called themselves. The Undercourt. Traders' talk said their contracts were iron, their prices fair by their own lights, and their doors open only to those desperate enough to knock without knocking.

Corvane blew out the candle so the others wouldn't see his face, and he went down through the city in the dark, past the fishmonger's stall, past the last lit window, out past the border stones where the wards hummed their low eternal note like a held breath, and he walked toward the mountain until his feet bled, and when the mountain's foot rose up before him black against the blacker sky, he knelt in the snow and said, in the flat voice of a man reading terms aloud:

"I have something to sell."

The mountain opened its eye.

* * *

They say the Pale Regent receives petitioners in a hall of geode crystal, and this was true then as it was true a hundred years later, though Corvane would not know it until much later, standing in that same hall with different desperation wearing a better coat. That first night she was a shape of grey light at the far end of the chamber, and the courtiers — frost given posture, courtesy given teeth — arranged themselves around him like an honor guard around something already dead.

"Forty-one crowns," the Regent said, and her voice was the sound of the last page of a book turning. "An IOU on a napkin. A storm in a bottle. And one soul, unencumbered, sworn to nothing." A pause that felt like arithmetic. "You have done your own audit, mortal. You know what you have."

"I want three hundred crowns," Corvane said. His voice did not shake. He would be proud of that later, and ashamed of being proud. "Gold, tonight, delivered to a specific address. And I want it to look like luck. No healer asks questions; no mother refuses the money out of pride. I want the girl to live and I want nobody to know it cost anything at all."

"How elegant." Something in the grey light moved that might have been a smile. "Most come here howling. You come here with terms. What makes you think your soul is worth three hundred crowns?"

"It isn't," Corvane said. "It's worth whatever the interest says it's worth in a hundred years. I've read contracts. I know how this goes. So here are my conditions, Regent: the debt compounds quietly. No collectors touch my people. Whatever comes due comes due to me alone, at a time of your choosing after the hundredth year. And I keep my memories." He swallowed. "If I'm going to pay, I want to know what I'm paying for."

The silence that followed was long enough for him to count every mistake of his life, and he got to thirty-four before the Regent spoke again.

"A hundred-year deferral. No contact with your household. Collateral held in trust." The frost courtiers inclined their heads in unison, a field of pale wheat bending. "Accepted. Sign here."

The contract appeared in the air between them, or perhaps on the table that hadn't been there a moment before — ink already drying, already beautiful, in a hand that looked unsettlingly like his own but older, steadier, as if some future version of himself had written it and sent it back as warning. There was a line at the bottom where a name should go.

"Do not sign your true name," the Regent advised, and there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened him more than any threat could have. "A soul is attached to its name. Give me the name you answer to instead, and the leash sits lighter."

So Corvane Ash — born Corvan Askelund, in a village that no longer existed, son of a woman whose face he had already begun to lose to time — signed *Corvane Ash*, the name he had built out of driftwood and cinders to hide behind, and told himself it was cleverness rather than cowardice, and felt the contract close around his chest like a hand made of winter reaching down his throat.

When he came back up into the world, dawn was breaking over Marrowgate, which was impossible, because he had walked all night and dawn had been hours away. But there it was, gold and indifferent, and there was a purse of exactly three hundred crowns sitting on the windowsill of the room above the fishmonger's stall, wrapped in plain paper, tied with string, looking for all the world like luck.

Marta lived. She grew up tall and sharp-tongued and she never once asked where the money came from, and Corvane spent a hundred years being grateful for that mercy and knowing it wasn't mercy at all.

* * *

The Gilded Thorn did not survive despite the debt. It survived because of it.

That was the joke Corvane never told, the one he carried under his ribs like a stone he'd grown around. Every impossible thing the guild ever traded — the borrowed luck, the second chances, the shadows returned to their weeping owners — every one of it grew from that first seed of gold, and the gold grew from a soul sitting in a vault beneath a mountain gathering interest like snow on a roof. When the Thorn flourished, he flourished. When the Thorn hired, he hired with tainted coin. When Vesper Blackwood knelt in his office at fifteen, all angles and wariness, and asked him why he kept ledgers for things that couldn't be counted, he had almost laughed — because the whole guild *was* an entry in a ledger, one line, written in a hand that looked like his own but older.

*One soul, unencumbered, held in trust.*

He built the Thorn's rules the way a man builds walls around the thing that shames him. Never deal in souls, he told them. Never trade what can't be returned. We traffic in the recoverable, the revocable, the small mercies. And his people believed him — good people, honest people in their crooked way, Bramble with his melancholy jokes and Ilsa Rennick back when she still came to guild dinners, and Vesper most of all, Vesper who audited his books every night with those dry, unerring eyes, hunting for exactly the kind of lie he had buried deepest.

She found everything else. She found the forged entries, the double books, the smuggling routes disguised as pilgrimage records. She never found the page that mattered, because there was no page. His contract didn't live in the Thorn's ledgers. It lived in him.

Some nights — most nights — he stood at his office window and pressed a hand flat against his chest, feeling for the place the hand of winter had reached. The physicians called him sound. The god-priests he consulted in secret found nothing amiss. But Corvane knew what was missing the way you know a tooth has gone: by the hole it left, by the way his tongue kept returning to the absence. He loved his people. He was nearly certain he loved them. But somewhere along the hundred years, love had become something he performed rather than something he felt — a ritual conducted flawlessly in a language he no longer dreamed in.

*This is what it costs,* he would think, watching Vesper pray to her silent god across the office, head bowed over the ledger like a woman waiting for a letter. *Not fire. Not torment. Just subtraction.*

Vesper's faith was the other stone he carried. Ten years she had prayed to Vael, god of thresholds, and ten years the god had said nothing, and Corvane — who had sold himself to gods who spoke *plenty*, gods who negotiated and haggled and smiled — had never found the courage to tell her that silence from the divine might be the kindest sound she'd ever hear. He watched her kneel and he thought: *she still has hers. Whole. Unspent.* And then, in the same breath, the colder thought, the thought that had been growing in him for decades like frost in a wall: *for now.*

Because the hundredth year was coming. He could feel it the way sailors feel weather. The compounding had begun — he saw it in small things first. Luck curdling in the vaults. Bottled memories aging wrong, showing futures instead of pasts. The wards along the border singing a half-tone flat. And then, three nights ago, auditing the inventory of a stolen shadow, he had opened a ledger and found a page in Vesper's handwriting dated three years hence, describing a bargain with the Undercourt, and he had known — with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own death warrant and recognizing the seal — that the collection notice had gone out.

The stars began going dark the following evening. One by one, like candles pinched by a passing hand.

* * *

Now he stood in the vaults beneath the Gilded Thorn with a ring of keys in one hand and a lamp in the other, and he sealed the doors one by one.

It was work he knew intimately, work he had done a thousand times for ordinary reasons — inventory, rotation, the mundane housekeeping of miracles. But tonight each lock turned like a syllable in a confession. *Bottled memories: sealed. Borrowed luck, four hundred and eleven units: sealed. Second chances, expired and pending:* he hesitated over that drawer longer than he meant to, thumb tracing the grain of the wood. Somewhere in that drawer was a second chance with someone's name on it, maybe his own, filed by Vesper's exact hand, and he shut it gently, the way you close a coffin.

"You're doing it wrong."

He turned. Bramble hung in the doorway of the vault, translucent in the lamplight, arms crossed, expression caught between wry and wounded — his usual setting, worn smooth by centuries of use.

"The warding sequence," the ghost said. "Left hinge first, always left hinge first. You taught me that in—" he paused, calculating across decades— "well. A while ago. You're rattled, guildmaster."

"Bramble." Corvane set down the keys. There were things he could say to a ghost that he could never say to the living; ghosts had already lost everything and therefore could not be robbed. "Do you remember the oldest contract? The one you're bound to?"

Something flickered behind Bramble's eyes, some long-buried ledger of his own. "I remember everything about it except how it felt. Why?"

"Is it mine?"

The ghost was quiet for a moment, and the lamplight passed through him and laid his shadow — did ghosts even have shadows? apparently they did tonight — against the vault door like a signature.

"No," Bramble said slowly. "But it's adjacent to yours. Everything in this guild is adjacent to yours, Corvane. That's rather the problem." He tilted his head. "Where are you going?"

Corvane looked at the sealed vaults, at the neat rows of impossible things locked away safe from a winter that had only begun to fall, and he lied the way he had been lying for a hundred years: competently, completely, with love.

"Out. Business. Keep them inside, keep the fires lit, and don't let Vesper follow me." He picked up his coat. His chest was very quiet where the hole was. "She'll want to. She'll be good at it. Don't let her."

"She'll hate you for this, whatever this is."

"Yes," Corvane said, and found, to his surprise, that it was almost a comfort — hatred was a debt he could carry for her, one last entry transferred to his side of the ledger. "Better than the alternative."

He climbed the stairs, took the road north out of the city where the snow was falling upward now, wrong and wonderful and dreadful, and set his face toward the mountain. Behind him the Thorn glowed in the valley like an ember cupped in two hands, all his people inside it, alive, oblivious, owed nothing — that had been the point, the whole point, the only point, for a hundred years.

Ahead, the mountain opened its eye.

It remembered him. He could tell by the way the cold reached for his chest, patient and precise, a creditor's fingers finding the collateral exactly where it had been left.

"I've come to settle accounts," Corvane Ash told the dark, walking in. "Alone. Let's read the final figure."

And far below, in a hall of geode crystal, something ancient turned a page.
