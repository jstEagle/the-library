## 14. Ledger Entries, Forged and True

They gave her a cell with a view.

That was the first thing Vesper should have understood about the Undercourt: it did not need to imprison anyone, because imprisonment implied the possibility of escape, and the Pale Regent did not deal in possibilities. She dealt in certainties. So Vesper was housed in a guest chamber of black geode, its walls faceted like cut amethyst gone dark, its single window opening onto a corridor of the deep mountain where courtiers of frost drifted past in gowns of falling snow, and every surface — floor, ceiling, washbasin, the little desk with its little chair — hummed with a courtesy so complete it had teeth.

*You are welcome here,* the room said, in the creak of settling stone, in the soft sigh of the brazier's violet flame. *You are welcome. You are welcome.*

Vesper had spent three days being welcomed, and she had used all three the way she knew how: she read.

The Undercourt kept ledgers. Of course it did. Bramble had told her as much on the road, wringing his translucent hands — *everything here is debt, dear girl, everything is contract and collateral and interest accruing since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to fear the dark* — but knowing it and sitting inside it were different animals. The court's records lived in the walls themselves. When Vesper pressed her palm against a facet of geode and thought, clearly, of an account — *show me* — the crystal obliged, unspooling entries in script that glowed like veins of heat through ice. Souls held. Souls spent. Souls leased at ruinous rates to mortal kings who wanted harvests that outlived their heirs.

She copied what she could onto scraps of paper filched from the desk. It steadied her, the copying. Numbers did not lie, even here. Especially here, perhaps, where lying was done entirely in numbers.

Corvane was somewhere above her, in the guest wing, under the Regent's version of hospitality. Ilsa was with the vault-wards, having talked her way into an inspection she was absolutely not authorized for — Vesper had watched her go with something like admiration and something like grief, because Ilsa Rennick walked toward her estranged mentor the way soldiers walk toward the wall they're assigned to hold. And Bramble —

Bramble was late.

He was supposed to meet her two hours ago, after the changing of the frost guard, to go over the route into the Vault of Names. He had never once been late. Dead men, he liked to say, were punctual by profession; lateness was the one vice the living had that he refused to envy them.

Vesper sat at the little desk, turned back to the ledger-wall, and told herself the cold knot in her stomach was the mountain's chill and nothing else.

*Show me,* she thought, palm to crystal. *The Ash account.*

The geode brightened. Entries slid past: Corvane Ash, one soul, held in escrow, one hundred years, compounding annually at a rate that made her wince even now — she'd have charged half that and slept fine. Interest upon interest, a debt grown fat and patient. And then, near the bottom of the record, an annotation in a hand unlike any other in the ledger. Where the court's script flowed like frost-ferns, this was upright. Deliberate. Human.

It was *her* handwriting.

Vesper went very still.

She knew her own hand the way a sword knows its edge. Twenty years of ledgers — inventory columns, contract margins, the small cramped numerals she used for sums and the taller slanted letters she used for names. She had written that page. She had written it in the guildhouse at Thornhollow, at the long oak table, with the good iron-gall ink that bit slightly blue. She could smell it, almost, looking at the copy pressed into the court's record like a flower between pages.

Except she had never written it. Except it was dated three years from now, and she was four hundred leagues beneath the snow, and the page existed here, in the Regent's own archive, catalogued and cross-referenced like a deed.

*Show me the original,* she thought. *Show me when you took it.*

The crystal hesitated. That was the only word for it — a flicker, a dimming, the ledger equivalent of a clerk clearing his throat and glancing over his shoulder. And then, instead of refusing, it showed her something else entirely: not the page, but the *making* of it.

Not ink on paper. Ink on *snow*. A white expanse, and a hand — her hand, unmistakably hers, down to the scar across the second knuckle — writing in letters of frost that sank as fast as they formed. And behind the hand, holding it the way a puppeteer holds strings held from *inside* the glove, a presence vast and pale and patient, wearing Vesper's muscle-memory like a borrowed coat.

The vision closed. Vesper found she had stood up without deciding to, chair skidding, breath fogging though the room was warm.

*Oh,* she said, to no one. To the empty welcome of the geode. *Oh, you absolute — *

She didn't finish. The word that rose wasn't fit for a ledger.

* * *

She found Bramble an hour later, or he found her — hard to say, with ghosts. He materialized at her shoulder in a corridor of the guest wing just as she'd given up pacing, and she seized him by the collar despite knowing her fingers would pass through, because some instincts predate sense.

"You're late."

"I was detained," Bramble said, with dignity, drifting free of her grip. "Detaining a ghost requires considerable effort, incidentally. There are wards on the upper galleries keyed to my binding. Someone has been *studying* me." His hollow eyes, which could be comic or ancient depending on the light, were currently ancient. "Vesper. We should not speak here."

"Then we'll speak walking. Keep up." She set a pace that scattered a pair of frost-courtiers ahead of them, their courtesy parting around her like water around a hot blade. "The page. The future-dated page in my hand. It's in the Regent's private archive, Bramble. Not the general ledger. Her *own* collection. Why does the goddess of endings keep a forgery of my handwriting locked in her boudoir?"

Bramble didn't answer immediately. Frost-courtesy pressed in from both sides, whispering *welcome, welcome*, and Vesper realized the whispers had changed pitch since the vision. Warmer. More satisfied. Like a trap that had heard the click of its own spring.

"Because," Bramble said finally, very quietly, "it isn't a forgery."

* * *

They ended up in the inn-room the court maintained for mortal guests — the Undercourt found mortals easier to manage when given beds shaped like their own expectations — and Bramble sat on the hearthstone with his knees drawn up, translucent hands laced, looking for all the world like a man about to confess to a crime committed two centuries ago. Which, Vesper reflected, might be roughly accurate.

"When I was bound," he began, "the oldest contract in the Thorn's possession, the one that ties me to your guild's founding — do you know how such bindings are written? Not on paper. Paper burns. Not on skin; skin rots. A binding of true weight is written on a *soul*. The debtor's soul carries the terms, and the collector's soul carries the claim, and neither can be forged because souls don't lie. They can't. They're the one ledger that keeps itself."

"Get to the page."

"I'm getting there. You have no patience for craft, you auditors, it's why the dead find you exhausting." But there was no bite in it. He drew a breath he didn't need — habit, armor, the whole sad pantomime of a man who'd been dead too long to stop performing life. "Souls remember. Everything they've ever been party to. Yours, Vesper Blackwood, remembers every entry you've ever written, every oath you've ever sworn — and it remembers the night you swore yourself to Vael at fifteen, kneeling on a riverbank with slave-chains cooling in the reeds beside you."

Vesper's jaw tightened. Ten years of silence, and he spoke of it like a date circled in someone else's calendar. "What does that have to do with—"

"Everything. It has everything to do with everything, and I am sorry — genuinely, coldly sorry, in the way the dead are sorry — that I didn't see it sooner." Bramble looked up at her. "The Regent didn't forge your handwriting, girl. She didn't need to. A soul sworn to a god writes *ahead of itself*. Prophetic script — rare, mostly worthless, mostly nonsense. But in the right hands, with the right leverage, a soul that can write the future can also be made to *sign* it. That page dated three years hence isn't a prophecy. It's a draft. A contract awaiting execution. Written in your hand because your hand is the only pen that can sign away what you carry."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above, the mountain groaned under its new burden of winter.

"What do I carry?" Vesper asked. Her voice came out level. Ledger-flat. It was the voice she used when the numbers stopped making sense and the only honest thing left was to read them aloud anyway.

"You know," Bramble said gently. "You felt it at the gate. *Descend.* Ten years of silence and then a god answering in your bones like a struck bell. You thought it was hope." He shook his head slowly. "Vael didn't go silent, Vesper. Vael went *into* things. Into the wards, into the thresholds, into every sworn blade who ever knelt and offered up an oath the god was too busy dying to refuse. You aren't a mercenary whose god abandoned her. You're a mercenary whose god has been *sheltering inside her* for ten years, spending itself down to the wick to hold the border together. And the Regent—" he spread his hands, "—the Regent collects what holds worlds up. That page in her archive? It's not bait for a rescue mission. It's a bill of sale. Your soul, sworn-blade, vessel-of-the-last-threshold-god, delivered willingly into the Undercourt — and when you signed yourself in at that gate, in your own true name, you executed it."

Silence, then. The kind Vesper usually found restful, the silence of balanced books. This one rang.

"The stars," she said. "Going out. The wards failing. That wasn't the debt coming due. That was—"

"Collateral damage. Or bait. Both. The wards weaken, winter falls, the Thorn panics, and a loyal auditor walks into hell to save her guildmaster's soul. Every step of it foretold in a page written by your own soul while you slept." Bramble's mouth twisted — humor as armor, worn thin. "You came here thinking you were the rescuer. Dear girl. You're the *prize*. You always were. Corvane's soul is real, yes, and the Regent will cheerfully give it back — she values it at exactly nothing compared to what walked through her gate wearing your face."

Vesper turned away from him. Walked to the window. Below, the frost-court drifted through its endless courteous procession, and beyond them, faint as a vein of silver in dark quartz, she could feel the wards — the great border lattice strung taut across the world above, humming with a power she now recognized because it had been sleeping next to her heart for a decade. Thin. Fraying. Spending its last warmth keeping the sky nailed down.

*Ilsa,* she thought. *Ilsa is standing in front of the Vault of Names right now, planning to steal back a soul, and the entire heist is a gift-wrapped delivery. If we run the plan, we hand the Regent everything. If we flee, the wards fail on their own schedule and the Regent collects the wreckage.* There was no exit in the arithmetic. She checked twice. There was never a third column; that was the whole cruelty of good ledgers.

Except.

Her whole life, people had mistaken her competence for compliance. Guildmasters, clients, gods. Here's the work, Vesper; here's the danger, Vesper; kneel here, Vesper, and hold the door. And she had done it, gladly, dryly, because the work mattered and the family inside it mattered and someone had to keep the books true. But an auditor who only ever balanced the given numbers wasn't an auditor. She'd told apprentices a hundred times: *the ledger doesn't tell you what to do. It tells you what's been done. The difference is where you live.*

"Then we change what's been done," she said aloud.

"Vesper—"

"She wrote a contract in my hand and waited for me to deliver myself. Fine. Contracts require parties. They require *terms*. They require consideration flowing both ways, and witnesses, and — Bramble, you've been a debt-collector for two hundred years. Tell me the Regent's page is enforceable. Tell me it's clean. Look me in the eye and tell me a goddess of endings, working alone, forging prophetic script from another soul without consent or witness, produced a document that would survive five minutes in front of an honest arbiter."

Bramble blinked. Then, slowly, wonderfully, he began to smile — the first genuine smile she'd seen on him since the road.

"It wouldn't," he said. "It wouldn't survive thirty seconds. Voidable for coercion, defective consideration, no witness of record — oh, she's *powerful*, but power isn't validity, and underneath all that pale grandeur the Court runs on law, Vesper, it runs on law the way the mountain runs on heat. It's the one thing the exiled gods never dared corrupt, because it's the only thing keeping *them* from devouring each other." He was on his feet now, ghost-feet an inch off the stones, hands sketching columns in the air. "If you challenge the instrument — formally, before the Court, invoking your right as a named party — she has to convene arbitration. And arbitration means the vault opens for examination of holdings, which means—"

"—a legal door into the Vault of Names," Vesper finished. "While every eye in the Court is on the hearing."

"And if you lose the challenge—"

"Then she takes me openly, in front of her own laws, and the wards fall, and I lose everything honestly." Vesper looked out at the dark facets of the court, at the frost-procession, at the faint silver thread of her dying god stitched through the world above. Somewhere in this mountain, a small dim flame that had once been Corvane Ash sat in a vault waiting for a thief. Somewhere above, Ilsa Rennick was picking locks with her bare hands out of sheer stubborn principle. Somewhere everywhere, a god was spending its last coin to keep the stars lit, and had never once, in ten years, complained.

*You didn't abandon me,* Vesper thought, and the thought had edges. *You hired me. Without asking. Well. I'm renegotiating the terms.*

"Bramble," she said. "How quickly can you file a formal challenge with the Undercourt?"

His grin sharpened to something out of a debt-collector's worst nightmares. "Dear girl. I've been *itching* to serve papers on someone for two centuries."

He was gone before the echo settled. Vesper remained at the window a moment longer, watching the frost-court process below, feeling the Regent's attention settle over the mountain like fresh snowfall — patient, satisfied, certain of her prize.

*Come and audit me, then,* Vesper thought at her, at the whole buried glittering dark. *I keep excellent books.*

Far above, through a thousand feet of stone, through ward-lattice and starless sky, something answered — not in words, not yet, but in pressure, the way a threshold answers a footstep. The Regent knew. Or soon would.

And down in the guest wing, in the vault gallery, in the geode halls where courtesy had teeth, every light in the Undercourt flickered once — and began, deliberately, beautifully, to go out.
