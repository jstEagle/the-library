## 16. Stealing Back a Soul

The Vault of Names did not look like a vault. It looked like a library that had drowned and been pulled back up too late — shelves of black stone rising out of a floor of still water, every surface weeping a faint silver condensation that never dripped. The souls were kept in books. That was the cruelty of it, Vesper thought, standing in the antechamber while Ilsa traced the ward-lines on the doorframe and Bramble drifted through the wall to scout ahead. The Undercourt didn't keep its holdings in jars or cages or lanterns. It kept them *catalogued*. Bound. Indexed. Each soul pressed flat between covers like a flower someone had loved once and then decided was easier dead.

She had been inside the court for three days now, wearing the face of a broker from the Gilded Thorn, and she had learned its rhythms the way you learn a dangerous animal's: by watching where it put its teeth. The court slept in waves, not nights. When the Regent held her levees, the geode halls emptied of everything except the ambitious and the terrified, which in the Undercourt amounted to nearly everyone. The vault, then, would be attended only by its keeper.

*Only* by its keeper.

Ilsa finished her tracing and stepped back, flexing her fingers as if they'd touched something hot. "It's her work," she said quietly. "The outer lattice. Master Sable's hand is all over it — the triple-braided anchor points, the way she folds intention into geometry." A muscle worked in her jaw. "She always said my knots were sloppy. She wasn't wrong."

"Can you open it?"

"I can *argue* with it." Ilsa pulled a thin stylus of cold-iron wire from her sleeve. "Wards are conversations, Vesper. This one's going to be an argument with a woman who taught me everything I know and then told the whole guild I was a fraud. So. Give me time, and don't touch anything, and if the walls start singing, run."

"The walls sing?"

"Her best work does." Ilsa set the stylus against the doorframe's first anchor point, and her expression went somewhere else entirely — somewhere old and hurt and very focused. "Go on. You have a ledger to rob."

* * *

Bramble found her in the reading gallery outside the vault proper, hovering — there was no other word for it — over a lectern where the vault's master index lay chained open. He looked, as ever, like a man who had been dressed for his own funeral by someone who disliked him: grey coat, grey skin, the ghost-light of him guttering faintly at the edges. Three hundred years bound to the Thorn's oldest contract had given him the posture of a man perpetually waiting to be handed paperwork.

"Well?" Vesper whispered.

"Well," Bramble said, "I've read the index. Corvane Ash, one soul, lightly used, collateral status, entered under debt-instruments, century-term, compounding." He turned a page without touching it; the paper stirred for him the way water stirs for a fish. "Shelf forty-one. And here's the good news — the vault's filing system runs on acknowledgment. Every entry has to be *read aloud* to be moved. Which means—"

"Which means the vault hears me coming."

"Which means," Bramble said, with the small, dry pleasure he took in delivering terrible news, "the vault hears you coming. But." He raised one translucent finger. "It also means that anything already acknowledged — anything already read, logged, stamped, and shelved — can be *re-shelved* by anyone the system recognizes. And the system, dear girl, recognizes the Gilded Thorn."

Vesper felt it settle into place, the way a lock feels a key turn half-home. The Undercourt traded in souls the way her guild traded in bottled memories and borrowed luck. Contracts between the two houses would exist. They *had* to. And contracts meant cross-recognition — the polite machinery of commerce, built centuries ago when both institutions were younger and more trusting.

"You want me to walk in as a broker doing an audit," she said.

"I want you to walk in as a broker doing an audit, read Corvane Ash's entry aloud in your official capacity, sign the transfer chit with your real name—" Bramble's grin was ghastly and wonderful, "—because the one thing in all the world the Undercourt's ledgers cannot argue with is a name freely given — and carry him out under your arm like a returned parcel. Misdirection isn't hiding, Vesper. Misdirection is giving the truth a better story to wear."

"And if the story doesn't fit?"

"Then the walls will start singing," Bramble said serenely, "and you'll wish you'd listened to the ward-smith."

* * *

The vault's keeper was not what she expected. Vessel had braced for a monster — some exiled godling of dust and vigilance — and instead found an elderly man made almost entirely of patience, seated at a desk of black stone, mending a book with thread fine as hair. His eyes, when they lifted to her, were the pale grey of things long buried but not yet forgotten.

"Gilded Thorn," he said. Not a question. Her forged credentials — Bramble had produced them from somewhere in his own death, inked in a hand three centuries out of practice — sat on his desk before she remembered handing them over.

"Routine audit," Vesper said. Her voice came out steady, and she was grateful to ten years of mercenary work for that. "Cross-house reconciliation. Your Regent's recent... collection activity has generated discrepancies on our side of the mountain."

"Has it." The keeper threaded his needle. "And you've come to count our shelves."

"I've come to confirm that what you say you hold, you hold. Nothing leaves. Nothing is altered. I read, I sign, I go."

For a long moment the only sound was thread through paper, a whisper like snow settling. Then the keeper rose, took a ring of keys that made no sound at all, and gestured toward the stacks with a courtesy so complete it felt like contempt.

"Shelf forty-one," he said. "Debt-instruments. Mind the water. Some of them remember being people, and they get restless near the door."

That, Vesper thought, walking into the dark between the shelves, was the worst thing anyone had said to her since the stars went out.

* * *

The stacks went down farther than the room should have allowed. She counted her steps out of habit — thirty, sixty, ninety — and the water never rose above her ankles though it must have been deeper somewhere, because she could hear it moving below the floor, slow and tidal, like breathing. The books around her were quiet, mostly. Occasionally one would shiver as she passed, or exhale a smell that belonged to a life: woodsmoke, sea-salt, bread, rain on a specific street in a city she'd never seen. One slim volume, as she drew level with it, whispered a child's laugh and then stopped itself, embarrassed.

*Some of them remember being people.*

Shelf forty-one was marked in silver characters that rearranged themselves when she looked directly at them, as if shy. Debt-instruments. Century-term. Compounding.

Corvane Ash was the fourth book from the end.

He was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the thing that undid her, standing there in the drowned dark with her hand outstretched — she had expected something grand, because Corvane was grand, Corvane filled a room with his voice and his vanity and his terrible generosity. But the book was barely bigger than her palm, bound in grey leather gone soft with age, and when she lifted it, it weighed almost nothing.

A hundred years of interest paid down on a soul, and this was what remained. A pamphlet. A prayer card.

She opened it.

There were words inside, of course — contract language, dense and glittering, the Regent's signature like a scar of frost across the final page. But beneath the words, the way a face lives beneath a portrait's varnish, there was *him*. She felt it before she understood it: a warmth, faint as a coal remembered rather than seen. And then — unmistakably, horribly — the warmth flinched.

It knew her.

The little flame at the heart of the book pressed itself away from her touch, cowering against the binding like a dog that has learned what hands do. And Vesper stood in the flooded aisle of a vault full of the dead-and-living and felt her eyes burn, because she knew that flinch. She had worn that flinch herself, at fifteen, in the bottom of a slaver's cart. It was the gesture of something that had been owned so long it had forgotten it could ever be anything but owned.

"Oh, you stupid, brave old man," she whispered. "What did you think you were buying us with?"

The warmth trembled. It did not come closer. But it stopped retreating.

Behind her, unhurried footsteps on stone. The keeper had followed her down the stacks, or had always been following, or had simply *been*, the way certain things in the Undercourt simply were.

"That one's been restless lately," he said mildly. "Since the winter began. They feel the weather, the old ones. Feel the collection coming." He paused beside her, hands folded, and regarded the open book with something that in any other face she might have called grief. "You may proceed with your audit, broker. Read the entry. Sign the chit. That is the law of the vault, and the vault's law binds even the Regent — though she forgets it, most centuries."

"Why are you telling me this?"

The keeper's pale eyes met hers, and for one moment the patience in them cracked like ice over deep water.

"Because I have kept these shelves for nine hundred years," he said, "and in nine hundred years, no one has ever come to take one *back*. Do your work quickly, Gilded Thorn. The court wakes when the ledger argues."

* * *

She read the entry aloud.

Her voice — trained by ten years of reciting contract terms to merchants and mercenaries — carried clearly down the flooded aisles, each clause falling into the silence like a coin dropped into a well. *One soul, belonging to Corvane Ash, entered as collateral against debt-instrument seven-hundred-eleven... status: compounding... term: elapsed.* And the vault listened. She felt it listening, the way you feel a crowd go still. The water stopped moving. The whispering books hushed. Somewhere far above, in the geode halls where exiled gods drank light instead of wine, something turned its head.

Bramble materialized at her shoulder holding the transfer chit like a man presenting a bill he'd waited three centuries to deliver. "Name," he prompted softly. "Freely given. The one thing they can't forge."

Vesper took the pen. Her hand, she noted distantly, was shaking, and she let it, because a trembling signature was honest, and honesty was the whole architecture of the trick.

*Vesper Blackwood,* she wrote. Sworn blade of Vael. Keeper of the ledgers of the Gilded Thorn. Daughter of nobody, sister of everybody she'd chosen.

The ink sank into the page and the page *accepted* it — a soft sound, almost a sigh, the sound of a lock turning half-home and then, impossibly, all the way.

Shelf forty-one opened. There was no mechanism she could see; the books simply parted, the way a crowd parts, and the space where Corvane Ash had lived for a hundred years stood empty and waiting, and the little grey book in her hands grew warm for the first time, and pressed itself against her palms — not flinching now, but leaning, the way a tired man leans on a doorframe, the way trust begins when it has been starved too long to stand up straight.

"There you are," Vesper whispered to it. "Hold on. We're going home."

She slipped the book inside her coat, against her heart, where it fit as if it had always been meant to sit there.

They were four steps from the door when the alarms woke.

* * *

It was not a bell. It was the ledger itself, arguing — the great chained index in the reading gallery crying out in a voice like tearing silk, every entry in the vault shrieking its own name at once, a thousand souls remembering themselves in a single instant of panic. The water beneath the floor lurched. The lights along the shelves — pale, sourceless, patient — flushed the color of an open wound.

The keeper was already at his desk when they reached the gallery, and he did not look at them, and he was very carefully closing the master index as though shutting a mouth.

"Stairs," Ilsa gasped, appearing from the direction of the ward-line with soot on her sleeves and blood on her lip and an expression that said the argument with her mentor had gone exactly as well as expected. "Now. The lattice is down but it won't stay down, and Sable is *furious*, and behind her—"

The ceiling of the corridor beyond the vault door split open like a geode struck with a hammer, and the Regent's courtiers poured through — frost given courtesy, courtesy given hunger, their faces smooth white ovals with smiles cut into them after the fact.

"Run," said Bramble, with feeling. "Run *now*, and whatever happens, do not drop the parcel."

Vesper ran, one hand pressed flat over the small warm weight of a guilty, fragile, borrowed soul, through halls that were waking around them like a body remembering pain — and behind them, far above, she heard the Pale Regent's voice begin to speak, and it was not angry.

That was the worst part. It was not angry at all.
