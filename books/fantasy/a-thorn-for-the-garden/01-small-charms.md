## 1. Small Charms

The night market of Vell woke the way it always did: lamplight first, then voices, then the smell of frying onions and tallow smoke rolling down Copperlane like a slow brown river. Hazel Reyes had been awake for hours by then, as she was every evening, sitting cross-legged on the floor of her shop with her palms flat against the boards, listening to the city's bones.

Most people thought she sold trinkets. That was the point.

Her stall opened onto Lantern Row at dusk, a narrow slot between a cobbler who snored through his own working hours and a spice merchant whose turmeric had stained her doorframe gold. She hung her wares in careful rows—luck-knots braided from red thread and river-grass, fever-charms of dried willowbark wrapped in waxed linen, small clay beads fired with salt and ash that were supposed to keep nightmares honest. Three coppers apiece. Five for the knots, because luck cost more than sleep. She'd set the prices three years ago and never changed them, because prices that never changed looked like habit, and habit looked harmless.

"Two knots," said the fishwife, Maren, slapping coins on the counter with hands scaled like a pike's belly. "One for the boat, one for my fool son. He's courting the cooper's girl and needs all the gods' help he can get."

"The knot doesn't do the courting," Hazel said, wrapping them in brown paper. "It just keeps the boat right side up."

"And my son?"

"That's what mothers are for."

Maren laughed, a sound like a bucket of gravel poured out kindly, and took her change, and moved on into the crowd. Hazel watched her go and felt the familiar ache beneath her ribs—the ache of being liked and not known. Maren had bought charms from her for two years. Maren had told her about her dead husband, her bad knee, the price of herring. Maren had never once wondered why the knots seemed to work.

Because they did work. Not much. A little nudge here, a turned ankle avoided there, a fever breaking one night sooner than the physickers promised. Enough to build a reputation. Not enough to draw a second look. Hazel had spent three years learning exactly how much magic a person could use before the world noticed, and the answer was: less than you'd think, if you were careful, and none at all if you were smart.

She was not smart tonight. Tonight she was tired, and tired made her fingers itch.

* * *

The tremor came just past midnight, when the market had thinned to stragglers and the lamplighters were making their rounds with their long brass poles.

Hazel felt it in her teeth first—a vibration so deep it wasn't sound, more a pressure, like the whole city had been struck by a bell buried under its foundations. Then her hands knew it, flat against the floorboards, and then her gift rose in her like water answering a dropped stone, unbidden, eager, and she had to clamp down on it hard enough that sparks crawled behind her eyes.

*Not now. Not here.*

But the gate was trembling. She could feel it the way you feel a tooth going bad—localized, intimate, wrong. Beneath her shop, beneath Copperlane, beneath forty feet of clay and cellar stone and older things than either, the sealed gate shifted in its sleep. Or whatever passed for sleep in a thing that hungered.

She closed the shutters. Turned the sign. Locked a door that wouldn't have stopped anyone determined, but stopping people wasn't the point; looking like a woman with nothing worth stealing was.

Then she went down.

The cellar was legitimate—she kept pickled things and old stock down there, crates of unsold charms from her first year, jars of river-mud she actually used for the clay beads. Behind the shelving, behind a false wall of brick and lime mortar she'd built herself over six sweating nights, was the stair.

The stair was not hers. It was older than Vell's oldest quarter, cut from black stone that drank the lamplight instead of returning it, and it went down farther than any cellar had a right to. Her mentor had shown it to her the way he'd shown her everything: without apology, without comfort. *This is the work, girl. Everything else is decoration.*

Corwin Ashgrave had been dead two years now, and she still heard his voice in this stairwell more clearly than anywhere else on earth.

At the bottom was the antechamber, and in the antechamber was the gate.

No one who hadn't been trained would have seen anything at all—just a wall of that same black stone, seamless, marked with a spiral of worn characters that predated every alphabet taught in Vell's schools. But Hazel could see it the way a blind woman feels heat from a fire: the gate was a seam in the world, a held breath, a door locked from this side with chains woven out of pure intention. And behind it—

Behind it was hunger. Old, patient, vast beyond the reach of words she owned. It didn't rage. Raging was for things that feared. This thing simply waited, the way winter waits, certain that every door ever built would someday be left open.

Tonight, the chains were singing.

That was the word Corwin had used, and once she'd heard it she'd never found a better one. The binding hummed under strain, a high thin note below hearing, and where the note frayed she could see—or sense—threads coming loose. Not many. Never many. But each time, a few more than the time before.

Hazel knelt on the cold stone and pressed both palms to the wall and let herself grieve for exactly four seconds. She counted them. It was a discipline Corwin had taught her: grief on a schedule, so it couldn't take more than its share.

Then she worked.

Her magic came up through her like sap, warm and green and alive, and she hated how good it felt, how it lit her up from the inside like a paper lantern. She gathered it and pushed it into the frayed threads, weaving, mending, feeding strength into the ancient binding the way you'd feed a broth to a starving man—one careful spoonful at a time, because too much too fast and the gate would drink her dry and come back for seconds.

The threads knitted. The singing softened. The pressure behind the wall eased by a fraction—not gone, never gone, but settled, the way a storm settles when the wind turns.

And Hazel Reyes sat back on her heels in the dark, sweating, shaking faintly, feeling the particular emptiness that followed a mending—the hollowed-out, scraped-clean feeling of having given something away that she couldn't name and couldn't spare.

Three years of this. Twice a month at first, when the gate only murmured. Weekly, after the earthquake two winters ago. Now, some months, nightly. The thing below was waking by inches, or the binding was dying by inches, or both, and no one in Vell knew except a charm-seller on Lantern Row who charged three coppers for fever-charms and lied with every smile.

Corwin had warned her about this too. *Keepers die young,* he'd said, in his flat way, sharpening his knife by candlelight because he distrusted anything that worked without effort. *Every one of us. The gate takes what it takes. Your mother's mother held it thirty years and died at fifty looking eighty. I'll do better—I always say I'll do better—and I'll be wrong.* He'd been wrong. Forty-one years old, found in this very chamber, peaceful as a sleeping man, with white hair and empty hands.

Hazel was twenty-six. Some mornings she did the arithmetic and couldn't finish it.

She climbed the stairs slowly, legs heavy, and stood a moment in the dark of her cellar among the pickle jars and the ghosts, listening to the market above wind down toward dawn. Somewhere overhead a drunk was singing badly. Somewhere a baker was already firing his ovens. The city went on, enormous and oblivious, and she loved it so much it frightened her—all these thousands of lives stacked together like kindling, warm and careless, none of them knowing what they were stacked upon.

*Let them not know,* she thought, as she thought every night. *Let me be invisible. Let me be small. Let the knots and the fevers and the three-copper prices be all I am.*

It was a prayer, and like most prayers it was also a lie she told herself. Because invisibility had a cost, and she paid it daily, and the coin was loneliness of a very specific denomination: the loneliness of being needed by everyone and known by no one.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the shutters. Hazel washed, ate bread and honey standing up, and opened her shop as the last lamps died.

The morning brought the usual trade. A carter wanting a knot for a new horse. A grandmother buying fever-charms against the season, three for the price of two because Hazel liked her. A boy of maybe ten, quick-eyed and grubby, who lingered over the luck-knots and finally asked, in the blunt way children ask, "Do they really work?"

"No," Hazel said, smiling, and the lie sat in her mouth like a stone. "They're thread and grass. But believing works half the time, and that's cheap at five coppers."

He bought one anyway, with coins counted out one at a time, and she gave him the best knot in the row without letting him know it, and watched him run off clutching his luck like a bird.

She was restocking when the criers began.

It started as a single voice far off across the rooftops, then another, then another, the calls overlapping until the whole city rang with them. Hazel stepped out onto her stoop, wiping her hands on her apron, and listened.

*"By order of His Majesty the King—"*

She knew the proclamation before she heard it entire. She knew it the way animals know weather. The king's men had come to the border towns weeks ago; she'd told herself it would stop there, that Vell was too big, too old, too full of important people to trouble itself over hedge-witches and charm-sellers.

*"—let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of sorcery is forbidden on pain of the king's justice. Those who bear the gift shall surrender themselves to the crown's mercy. Those who conceal the gifted shall share their fate. Denounce your neighbors. Watch your streets. The King sees all."*

*The King sees all.*

Hazel stood very still on her stoop while the market erupted around her—outrage, laughter, nervous jokes, and underneath it all, already beginning, the sly sideways glances that would rip Vell apart seam by seam. She watched Maren pause halfway down the row and look back at her, just for a moment, just a flicker—and Hazel couldn't read it, couldn't tell if it was apology or appraisal, and that not-knowing was worse than anything the criers had said.

Her hands, hidden in her apron folds, were trembling. Not with fear for herself alone. With fear for the gate—for the mending she owed twice a week now, for the magic she could not stop using without letting the world crack open, for the impossible arithmetic of hiding a gift the crown had just made worth silver to betray.

Somewhere across the city, a bell began to ring. Not a market bell. A deeper one, from the citadel, tolling slow and formal, the sound of an edict becoming law.

Hazel went inside and closed her shutters, and stood in the dimness among her harmless charms, and understood—with the weary clarity of a woman who has done hard arithmetic all her life—that the years of being small were over.

Whatever came next, it would find her.
