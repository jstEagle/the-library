## 2. The King's Edict

The criers came at dawn, and they came in threes.

Hazel heard them before she saw them, the way she heard most things in Vell—through her floorboards, through the water pipes, through the old bones of a city that had been built twice and burned once. Sound traveled strangely above the gate. The deep places under her shop carried every footstep, every cartwheel, every whispered prayer up through the cellar stones like a throat clearing itself to speak.

*By order of His Majesty Aldric, third of his name, King of Vell and the Marchlands—*

She was grinding feverroot at her workbench when the first crier passed below her window, and she did not stop grinding. Her hands kept their rhythm. Her shoulders stayed loose. These were skills, too, though no one had ever paid her for them: the stillness, the smallness, the practiced boredom of a woman selling knots and dried herbs while the world rearranged itself outside.

*—that any soul found practicing the forbidden arts, bearing the gift of unlicensed magic, or concealing those who bear it, shall be taken into custody of the crown. Informants will be rewarded. Concealers will be judged as witches themselves.*

The words were read four times over, in the flat professional voice of a man who had read worse on other mornings in other cities. Then the bell in the crier's hand rang once, bright and small, and the procession moved on toward Tanner's Row.

Through the gap in her shutters Hazel watched them go. Three criers in royal blue. Six soldiers with halberds. And behind them, walking last, a man in grey leathers with a long coat and a face like a closed door—a witch-hunter, one of the new kind, the kind that had begun appearing in Vell only since winter. He did not look at her shop. He looked at *people*. At the faces in doorways, the hands clutching children, the eyes that flicked away too quickly or held too long. He was reading the street the way Hazel read her charms, looking for the flaw, the loose thread, the thing that did not sit right in its stitching.

He walked past her window without pausing, and Hazel exhaled for what felt like the first time since the bell had rung the night before.

Then she went downstairs and opened her shop, because closing it would be a confession.

* * *

By midmorning the line outside was shorter than usual, and the people in it were different.

They came anyway—the poor always came, because fever and bad luck did not pause for edicts—but they came differently now. They came with hoods up and eyes down. They bought their luck-knots without meeting her gaze. An old fishwife who had traded with Hazel for three years pressed a copper into her palm and whispered, not quite a question, not quite a warning: *"You're careful, girl. You hear things. Be careful."*

And there were new customers, too. That was the worst part. A woman Hazel had never seen before lingered by the shelf of household charms, picking each one up, turning it over, asking questions with an easy smile. *Does this one really work? My sister swears by your knots. Where do you learn a trade like this?* Hazel answered everything with shopkeeper's patter—family recipes, grandmother's tricks, nothing but knotwork and superstition—and watched the woman buy two fever-charms and leave, and marked the way she paused at the corner to speak briefly to a soldier leaning against the well-house.

An hour later the woman returned and bought a third charm, and asked about Hazel's family, and whether she lived alone, and Hazel lied beautifully, warmly, the way she had learned to lie from her mentor Maera, who had taught her that the best hiding place for a gift was inside a smaller, duller version of itself.

*Let them find a hedge-seller,* Maera used to say, her blind white eyes turned toward some middle distance only she could see. *A woman who ties knots and sells hope by the copper. No hunter in the realm burns a woman for that. Bury the true thing so deep even you forget where you put it.*

Hazel had never managed the forgetting. She felt the gate beneath her floor all day, the way she always did—a low pressure behind her sternum, a hum in her teeth, the vast patient weight of the binding she maintained with slivers of power siphoned off in secret, a thread at a time, invisible against the loom. It had trembled again in the night, harder than it had trembled in years. The seal was thinning. And now the crown was hunting every gifted soul in the realm, which meant the few keepers' arts that remained, scattered and secret, would soon be snuffed out entirely—or gathered up, she thought with a cold she couldn't explain, gathered rather than destroyed. Edicts like this one were nets. Someone wanted what the net caught.

She didn't let herself finish the thought. Finishing thoughts like that got people killed.

At noon, they took Tam Broadstairs.

* * *

It happened twenty feet from her door.

Tam was a cooper, broad and slow-moving and gentle, a man who bought a luck-knot every month for his daughter's cough and had once, drunk on festival wine three summers ago, grabbed Hazel's wrist across the counter and said, *You've got something real under all this, haven't you, missus? I felt it. Like standing near a storm.* And then he'd laughed, and bought his knot, and never mentioned it again. He'd been harmless. He'd been *kind*.

Now two soldiers had him by the arms in the middle of Copper Lane, and a third was holding his daughter, who could not have been more than eight, and Tam was saying—loudly, reasonably, in the voice of a man who believes the world still runs on sense—"I'm not gifted, I'm a *cooper*, I make barrels, ask anyone, ask my neighbors—"

"Your neighbors asked us," said the grey-coated hunter, stepping out of the crowd the way smoke steps out of a fire. He held up something small. From where Hazel stood frozen in her doorway, she could not see what it was. "Found in your workshop. Witch-token. You'll come quietly or you won't."

"I don't know whose that is—that's not mine, I've never seen—"

"Tam." It was his neighbor Wren, the tanner, pushing forward, hands raised, voice shaking. "Tam, tell them. Tell them about the light. In your shed, last month, we all saw the light in your shed—"

"There was no light, Wren, there was a *lamp*, you great coward, you know there was a lamp—"

Wren flinched back into the crowd. And the crowd—Hazel's neighbors, her customers, the fishwife, the widow Ostler who owed Hazel six coppers—closed around the moment like water, watching, saying nothing, doing the arithmetic everyone in Vell was suddenly doing: *not me, not today, not mine.*

They dragged him past her door. His boots scraped grooves in the mud. His daughter screamed his name until a soldier's gloved hand covered her mouth, and even muffled, the sound went through Hazel like a nail through green wood.

His eyes found hers. Just for a step, just for half a heartbeat, as they passed her threshold. There was no accusation in them. That was the unbearable part. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her across the counter a hundred times—warm, apologetic, a good man embarrassed to be making a scene—as if *he* were the one who should be sorry, for being dragged screaming past her shop, for disturbing her morning.

Then he was gone around the corner, and the crowd dissolved, and the lane was ordinary again, and Hazel stood in her doorway with a luck-knot in her hand—the one Tam had come to buy—and did nothing.

She had done nothing. She had stood in the exact place where a word from her, a flash of her true self, a single act of open power might have made the soldiers hesitate, made the crowd remember what fear of magic felt like from the other side—and she had done nothing, because Maera's voice was in her skull like a nail: *bury it deep, bury it deep*, because the gate beneath her feet needed a keeper more than Tam Broadstairs needed a miracle, because survival demanded complicity and complicity, she understood now, was simply the interest paid on a debt of staying hidden.

She closed the shop early. She sat in the dark behind the shutters with her head in her hands, and beneath her, faint and steady, the gate hummed on, indifferent to arithmetic.

That night she untied the luck-knot she'd made for Tam's daughter and retied it, tighter, and hung it on her own doorpost, where it did no one any good at all.

* * *

Dain Marrow had burned eleven houses in nine years of service, and he remembered every roofbeam.

Not the people—he'd learned early not to keep the people, the way a surgeon learns not to keep faces—but the houses. The way pitch caught in the thatch. The way a home collapsed inward, politely, almost shyly, as if apologizing for the trouble. His instructors at the Citadel had called this *professional detachment*. His instructors had also called the gift *the rot* and informants *the crown's finest instrument*, and Dain had believed all of it for nine years, the way a man believes the floor beneath him right up until it isn't there.

He stood now in the warrant room of the Vell garrison, reading Tam Broadstairs's file by lamplight, and the floor was not there.

The informant's statement was dated four days ago. The witness described witch-lights in the cooper's shed—green lights, rising lights, the classic manifestation. Standard. Except Dain himself had canvassed Copper Lane three weeks prior, following up on a theft report, and had spoken with this same witness, Wren the tanner, who had then told him—in writing, in his own hand, in a statement Dain still had in his saddlebag—that the shed contained nothing but a lamp and a drunk man singing hymns.

Two statements. Same witness. Same shed. Opposite worlds.

He pulled the next file. A weaver from the Dyers' Quarter, taken yesterday. Same pattern: an original statement of nothing, then a second statement of horrors, dated within days of the edict's announcement. And the next file. A midwife. A ferryman. Eleven files, and in nine of them the *evidence* had bloomed only after the criers began reading the new law aloud—bloomed like mushrooms after rain, fed by something.

By what?

Dain knew what informants were worth. He'd built his career on them. But this was different. This wasn't fear making neighbors careless; this was *curation*. Someone was choosing targets. Someone was feeding Wren the tanner his lines, and paying him, and Dain had checked—the reward ledger showed disbursements to Copper Lane informants signed by an office that technically did not exist: the Office of Provisional Inquiry, established by crown decree, reporting not to the Magistrate General but directly upward. Past the spymaster. Past the council.

To the king's own private secretary.

He thought of the arrests he'd made since winter. The ones where the accused had wept innocence. The ones where the accused hadn't—wept anything, gone quiet instead, gone *watchful*, the way animals go watchful, and one of them, a hedge-witch from the river district, had looked up at him from the wagon floor and said, not with hatred but with something far worse, pity:

*Captain. Ask yourself who taught you to hunt us. And ask yourself why the hunting never ends, no matter how many of us you catch.*

He had reported her for insolence. She had been transported north. He had not thought of her in a year, and now he could not stop thinking of her, sitting alone in the lamplight with eleven files spread out before him like a confession written in someone else's hand.

Somewhere below the city, faint as a pulse through stone, he felt—not heard, not sensed, nothing a hunter should ever feel—a low vibration, like a second heartbeat under the floor of the world. He told himself it was the garrison's forges.

He filed his doubts where he filed everything, in the locked drawer that made him good at his work. But the drawer, tonight, did not close cleanly. Something small and sharp had gotten into the mechanism.

In the morning, the Office of Provisional Inquiry posted its next list of names, and Dain Marrow read it, and recognized the fourth name down.

*Copper Lane. The charm-seller. Reyes, Hazel.*

Someone had finally sold him the witch he'd walked past twice and never seen.
