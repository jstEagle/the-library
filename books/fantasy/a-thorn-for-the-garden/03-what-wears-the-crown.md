## 3. What Wears the Crown

The rite of the King's Blessing came to Vell on the first cold morning of autumn, and the whole city turned out to be seen being loyal.

Hazel stood in the square before the Hall of Judgement, pressed between a cooper's wife and a boy selling roasted chestnuts, and told herself she had no choice but to be there. Every shopkeeper in the Lantern Ward had received a summons. Absence would be noted. Absence, in the three weeks since the edict, had begun to mean something.

She had closed her shop for the morning. She had left the luck-knots hanging, the fever-charms in their drawer, the little harmless stitchings of her trade sitting idle on their hooks—and she had walked to the square with her hands empty and her gift folded up so small and deep inside her that it was less than a whisper, less than a breath, a coal buried under ash under earth.

*Be nothing,* she told herself, the way she had told herself for three years. *Be the charm-seller. Be dull. Be forgotten.*

Then the bells began, and the king came out onto the balcony of the Hall of Judgement, and Hazel stopped being able to tell herself anything at all.

* * *

She had seen him before, of course. Everyone in Vell had seen Aldric IV at least at a distance—on coins, on proclamations nailed to doorposts, in the painted portraits that hung in the guildhalls with their careful, lifeless eyes. He had been king for six years. He had been a good king, people said, in the early years. He had ended the border war. He had lowered the grain tariffs. He had smiled from balconies exactly like this one.

But Hazel had never stood this close. Forty feet of cold morning air, no more. And she had never, in three years of hiding, been this afraid.

Because she could feel him.

Not his presence—she had braced herself for that, had expected the heavy pressure of a gifted man, the way a strong magician filled a room the way a fire fills a room, pushing warmth against your skin. She had prepared to shrink from that, to make herself smaller than small.

What she felt instead was a *hole*.

It was like standing at the edge of a well in the dark and realizing, too late, that the ground beneath your feet had been cut away. The king stood on the balcony in his crown and his crimson mantle, raising his hands to the crowd, and where a man should have been—a soul, a self, that warm animal weight of being—there was nothing. A hollow, shaped like a king. And into that hollow, something had been poured.

Magic. Stolen magic, she realized, with a lurch that turned her stomach. It moved in him the way water moves in a waterskin that has no bottom—pouring in and pouring through, never held, never *his*. She could see it now that she knew to look: fine bright threads stitched across the emptiness, thousands of them, tens of thousands, sewn over the hollow the way a miser patches a rotten coat. Each thread was a life. Each thread had a color, a texture, a *flavor*, and Hazel's gift, waking despite her, despite the ash and the earth she'd buried it under, read them the way her fingers read the knots of a charm.

There was a green thread that had once been someone who could coax orchards into early bloom. There was a thread the color of stormlight, frayed now, that had sung to rain. There were hundreds of dull grey threads, hedge-craft and hearth-craft, the small magics of small people—and threaded through them all, bright as a vein of ore, something older and vaster that made her gift recoil like a hand from a hot iron.

She knew that thread. She had felt its like only once before, on the worst night of her life, kneeling on cold stone with her palms pressed flat against a gate that hummed with hunger.

*No,* she thought. *No, that's not possible. That's not—*

The king began to speak, and his voice carried across the square, warm and measured and *wrong*, and every word confirmed what her gift was screaming.

"My people." The crowd hushed. "Three weeks ago, I set my hand to an edict, and some of you have wondered at its harshness. I hear your wondering. I feel your fear." A pause, perfectly judged, and Hazel watched the threads in him shift and settle, watched the stolen magic redistribute itself like weight in a sack. "But the gift is a corruption. It rots the soul that carries it. You have seen what the gifted do—how they twist luck, how they poison wells, how they whisper to things that should not be whispered to. I have not outlawed magic to punish you. I have outlawed it to *save* you."

He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes because there was nothing behind his eyes to contradict it.

"And to those who have already come forward—to those who have named their neighbors, their kin, their friends—I say: you have done a holy thing. You have cut the rot from the garden before it spreads. The crown remembers its friends."

Hazel's hands were shaking. She shoved them into her coat pockets and pressed her nails into her palms and thought, absurdly, of the fever-charm she'd sold the cooper's wife two winters ago, of the woman's sick daughter, of the small warm knot of craft she'd tied into the thread and the girl's fever breaking by morning. *Rot*, the king said. *Corruption.* That small warm knot had been the difference between a grave and a girl running in the garden by midsummer.

Below the balcony, at the foot of the Hall's steps, a line of prisoners stood in chains. Hazel counted them without wanting to: nine. A woman in a dyer's smock. Two men in the grey of the tanneries. A boy who couldn't have been more than fourteen, weeping without noise, the way you learn to weep when you've learned that noise makes it worse.

The captain of the witch-hunters stood beside them—Marlow, no, *Marrow*, she'd heard the name in the market, the young one they said was honest—and his face was stone, but his eyes, when they passed over the crowd, had the look of a man reading a ledger and finding the sums wrong.

The king raised his hands again.

"Bring them forward," he said, "and let the realm be cleansed."

* * *

What happened next, Hazel had been told about, in whispers, in the way people told such things—quickly, looking away, with a shrug that said *what can you do*. She had never seen it. She had made sure never to see it.

The witch-hunters brought the nine prisoners up the steps, and the king came down from the balcony to meet them, and the crowd was made to kneel, hundreds of people folding down onto the cold cobbles like wheat before a scythe. Hazel knelt with them. Through the gap between a fishmonger's shoulder and his son's head, she watched.

The king laid his hand on the dyer-woman's head. A blessing, it was called. *The King's Mercy.*

And Hazel, kneeling, felt it happen.

She felt the thread of the woman's magic—dull green, orchard-craft, the same flavor as the green thread she'd seen stitched into the hollow of him—*drawn out*. Pulled through the crown of her skull like a splinter drawn from flesh, and the woman gasped, and sagged, and the hunters caught her under the arms, and she was set aside, alive, emptied. And the thread went into the king. She watched it go. She felt the hollow drink it the way dead ground drinks rain, and the fine bright stitching over his nothingness grew by one thread, and his smile did not change at all.

One by one. The tanners. The boy, last, because the boy fought, because the boy was fourteen and had a storm-singer's gift and had not yet learned what Hazel had learned at twenty, which was how to be nothing. The hunters had to carry him. His magic came out of him in a bright blue-grey scream that only Hazel could hear, and she heard it all the way, and she knelt on the cobbles of her own city with her gift crushed flat beneath her fear and she *did nothing*.

The crowd, when it was over, cheered.

That was the worst of it. Not the hollow king, not the feeding, not the boy's silent screaming gift. The cheering. The cooper's wife beside her, clapping her chapped hands, tears in her eyes—*isn't it wonderful, isn't he merciful, to let them live*. The chestnut boy shouting with the rest. Nine people had been hollowed out in front of them, and the city of Vell had applauded, because the king had told them it was a cleansing, and people will applaud anything if they're told the blood is holy.

Hazel knelt among them and clapped her hands too, because not clapping was death, and she felt something in her chest crack along a line she hadn't known was there.

* * *

She didn't go back to the shop. Her feet took her the other way, down through the Lantern Ward, down the crooked stairs past the tanneries, down and down to the oldest part of Vell, where the streets were named for things that no longer existed—the Gatewright's Stair, the Underward, the Warden's Close—and where no one went after dark because everyone had forgotten why.

Hazel had never forgotten why.

She came to the door in the Warden's Close, the one that looked like a cellar door, and she unlocked it with three keys and a word, and she went down into the dark, down the worn steps, down to the corridor that ended in a wall of ancient black stone. And set into that wall, so old its carving had nearly worn smooth, was a gate.

It was humming.

It always hummed, faintly, the way a kettle hums before it boils, and for three years Hazel had come here every week to listen and to feed it what small magics she could spare and to tell herself: *not yet, not in my lifetime, not my problem, not my death.* Her mentor had held it forty years and died at fifty-three with white hair and empty hands, smiling, saying *you'll have longer, girl, the binding is strong.*

The binding was not strong.

Hazel pressed her palms to the black stone and felt the hunger on the other side, vast and patient and *familiar*, and she felt the thin bright threads of her own small magics woven into the seal, fraying, and she understood.

The gate was starving.

It had always been fed—she'd known that much from her mentor's books, though she'd never let herself understand it. The gate did not run on the keeper's gift alone. It ran on the gifted. For centuries, the keepers had drawn the stray magic of the world into the seal, willingly given, freely offered, hedge-witches leaving charms at the door, storm-singers singing the binding strong. The gate ate magic the way the city ate grain, steadily, generation after generation, and the devourer behind it slept.

And now the king was harvesting every gifted soul in the realm. Pulling the threads out one by one and stitching them into a hollow man on a throne in Vell, ten miles of stone and street away from this wall—and every thread he took was a thread the gate would never taste, a thread the seal would never be fed.

*It's not a hunt,* Hazel thought, and her breath came out of her in the dark like a sob. *It was never a hunt. It's a harvest. He's emptying the fields.*

The edict, the informers, the burnings, the Mercy—none of it was fear of magic. It was *appetite*. He was flushing the gifted out of every city and village in the realm and drinking them down, and the gate beneath her city was starving faster with every soul he took, and the thing behind the black stone was waking up hungry, and the man on the throne was stitched together with the same magic she had felt humming in this wall—

The same. She had felt it and refused to know it. The vast old thread, bright as ore, sewn through the hollow of the king.

*He's bound to it,* she thought. *Whatever he is, whatever he was, he's bound to the thing below, and he's feeding it the realm one soul at a time, and when the gate opens—*

The gate hummed under her palms, and far down beneath the stone, something that had slept for a thousand years turned over in its sleep, and Hazel Reyes, charm-seller, keeper, coward, knelt in the dark and finally understood the shape of the trap she had been hiding in for three years.

There was no hiding. There had never been any hiding. There was only the harvest, and the hunger, and how long the fields took to empty.

She stayed until her knees ached. Then she rose, and pressed her forehead once against the cold stone—*hold, hold, I'll find a way, hold*—and went up the stairs into the grey afternoon light, knowing three things now that she could never unknow.

The king was not a man. The edict was a harvest. And she could not hold this gate alone, and feed it, and hide, all at once—not anymore, not with the fields being stripped bare.

She needed help. The kind of help that got people burned.

Hazel locked the cellar door behind her with shaking hands and turned toward the river, toward the smugglers' docks, toward the only people in Vell who already lived outside the law—and began, at last, to run.
