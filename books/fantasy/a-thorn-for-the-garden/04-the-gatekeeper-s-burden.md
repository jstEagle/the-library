## 4. The Gatekeeper's Burden

Maren's house had been dead for three years, and the city had treated it the way Vell treated all its dead: politely, and by taking things.

Hazel found the door still locked—her key still fit, though the mechanism had rusted to a grinding complaint—and stepped into air thick with dust and old lavender and something else beneath it, a smell like wet iron that had no business in a herbalist's rooms. Her lamp guttered as she crossed the threshold. She steadied the flame with a thought, the smallest of her small sins, and told herself it was habit, nothing more.

The shop looked as if Maren had simply stepped out to buy bread. Bundles of feverfew still hung from the drying beams, browned to paper. The mortar on the counter held a ring of dried paste. A cup sat by the hearth with a tea leaf curled at its bottom like a drowned thing. Hazel had not come here since the funeral. She had told herself it was grief. Standing in the dark now, she understood it had been fear. She had known, in the wordless way she knew the weather through her bones, that this house was not empty.

The tremor came while she stood there—a slow roll through the floorboards, up through her boot soles, deep and patient as a heartbeat under the earth. She had felt it every night for three years from her own bed two streets away. Here, above the gate itself, it was not a whisper. It was a hand pressing up against the soles of her feet.

*Soon,* the pressure said. *Sooner than soon.*

"Not yet," Hazel said aloud, and hated how thin her voice sounded in the dark.

She set the lamp on the counter and began to search.

Maren had been many things to Vell's night market: herbwife, charm-seller, the old woman who could ease a birth or quiet a cough for a copper. To Hazel she had been the only mother she remembered, the one who had found an orphan girl with too much light in her hands and taught her to make it small. *Bury it deep, little thorn,* she used to say. *A garden survives by looking like nothing worth cutting.* Hazel had thought, for twenty years, that the burying was about witch-hunters. It was only in the last three years, holding shut what shivered beneath the city every midnight, that she'd wondered why Maren never once called the gift hers. *Your burden,* always. Never *your gift.*

There had to be records. There had to be more than the single page Maren had burned in front of her the week before she died—the page Hazel had glimpsed only in fragments: a diagram of concentric rings, a word repeated down the margin in a language that made her teeth ache, and one sentence in Maren's cramped hand: *It is not a door. It is a mouth.*

The floor under the hearthstone gave off that wet-iron smell strongest. Hazel knelt, worked her fingers into the seam where the stone met brick, and pulled. The stone came up grudgingly, trailing decades of ash, revealing a cavity lined with lead sheeting—and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a book.

Not a book, quite. A ledger bound in grey leather, its spine cracked white with use. Maren's name was not on it. No name was. Just a symbol stamped into the cover: seven concentric rings, and at their center a single point pressed so hard it had torn the leather.

Hazel carried it to the table, hands steady because she forced them to be, and opened it.

The first pages were in another hand entirely—one elegant and very old, ink gone the color of dried blood. She read standing up, then read sitting down when her legs decided standing was no longer among her talents.

*In the Year of the Two Winters, the city of Vell was raised not for harbor nor trade but for chains.* The words marched across the page in careful, scholarly lines. *That which the first kings named the Hunger Below—that which devoured three settlements entire before it learned to sleep—was not slain. It cannot be slain. It can only be fed slowly, or starved slowly, and starving it is the work of walls.*

*Seven rings were laid beneath the earth. Six are stone and oath. The seventh is living: a keeper of the gift, whose magic binds the mouth shut as a hand over a screaming man's lips. The keeper does not close the gate. The keeper IS the gate.*

Hazel turned the page. Diagrams followed—the same concentric rings she'd glimpsed in the burning paper—but drawn here in full, annotated in three dead scripts. And beside them, in the old hand, a passage she read four times because her mind kept sliding off it:

*Let no soul covet this office. The gift poured into the binding is not lent but spent. Each year the keeper diminishes; each year the Hunger gnaws what remains. The average of keepers' years is written elsewhere in this record. Do not look if you mean to serve. Look if you mean to refuse, for refusal is also recorded here, and it is worse.*

She looked anyway.

Maren had taken over the ledger in her own hand somewhere around its middle, and she had kept accounts the way she kept everything—precisely, without sentiment. Names. Ages at binding. Ages at death. Hazel ran her thumb down the column and watched the numbers shrink as the decades passed, as though the thing below grew stronger with each generation fed upon. Forty-one years. Thirty-six. Twenty-nine. Twenty-two. Near the end of the list, in ink slightly darker, as if written with a heavier heart:

*Maren Ostreva, bound at thirty-one.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, unfinished: *Expected span—*

Maren had died at fifty-three. Twenty-two years. Hazel did the arithmetic twice because the first sum felt like a blade between the ribs. Twenty-two years, and Maren had been stronger than Hazel would ever be. Maren, who had buried her gift so completely that Vell forgot she was anything but an herbwife. Who had taught Hazel to do the same.

*Oh,* Hazel thought, with a terrible calm. *You weren't hiding me from hunters.*

Three years ago, Maren dying, she had gripped Hazel's wrist and said only: *You know what to do. Every night. Don't stop until you find another way.* Hazel had assumed *another way* meant freedom. Now she read the last entries in the ledger and understood it might have meant survival—hers or everyone else's, she couldn't tell which anymore.

Because the final pages were different. The final pages were recent, the ink barely faded, written in a shaking hand during the last month of Maren's life. And they described something Hazel had never heard her mentor say aloud.

*The binding starves. This is the cruelty none of the old keepers foresaw: the edict, the burnings, the purges—every gifted soul the realm destroys is a river cut off upstream. Magic does not vanish when its vessel dies unspent. It goes DOWN. It pools below like rain finding the cellar. The Hunger drinks what we refuse it elsewhere, and grows fat on our terror, and the gate must hold back ever more with ever less.*

*I have measured the rings each solstice for forty years. The sixth ring wore through last spring. The fifth sings when I press my ear to the stones. I have bought Hazel eleven years by feeding it my own remainder, and I have almost nothing left to leave her.*

*If the harvest continues—if the crown keeps burning witches and the land keeps bleeding its magic downward—no keeper alive will hold the seventh ring past ten years. Not even her. She is stronger than I was. That is the tragedy of it. Strength only means the dying takes longer.*

Hazel closed the ledger.

She sat in Maren's dead shop with the lamp hissing and the wet-iron smell rising through the open hearthstone, and she let herself feel it, all of it, in one long cold wave: the shape of the trap she had lived in all her life without knowing its walls. Three years of nightly reinforcement, pouring her gift into the dark like water into a bottomless jar. She had thought herself a guard at a post. She was a candle burning at both ends in a room where the darkness drank light.

Every keeper dies young. Not murdered. Not hunted. Simply *spent*—devoured by inches by the thing they held shut, until one morning some niece or apprentice noticed the herbwife hadn't opened her shutters, and the city said *how sad, so young*, and a new keeper took up the ledger without knowing there was a ledger.

And now the arithmetic had changed again. Now the king himself—whatever wore the king's face—was tearing the gifted out of the realm root and branch, and every burning was a feast for the thing below. The gate wasn't failing the way a wall fails, slowly, stone by stone. It was failing the way a dam fails, faster each hour as the flood behind it swells.

Hazel opened her eyes. Beneath her, faint but unmistakable, the earth gave its long slow tremble, and this time she felt something new in it. Not just hunger. *Anticipation.* Something down there had tasted the harvest falling from above, and was waking to the flavor.

*It knows,* she thought. *All these centuries it waited blind, and now the sky itself rains food, and it knows.*

She took the ledger, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and pushed the hearthstone back into place. Then she stood in the middle of the shop and made herself say the next part out loud, because some truths needed air to become real.

"I can't hold it alone."

The words hung in the lavender-dark. Ten years, Maren had guessed, and Maren had guessed generously. Less, with the burnings accelerating. Less, with the edict criers on every corner calling magic crime and every arrested witch another meal delivered to the dark.

Which meant hiding was over. Not because the hunt had found her—though it might, any day, any knock at the shutter—but because there was nothing left to hide *for*. Survival demanded invisibility, and invisibility was now just a slower word for death. Hers, and then, when the seventh ring tore, everyone's.

She needed other hands. Other gifts. She needed, absurdly, impossibly, the very people the king was hunting—to gather them, to tell them the truth no one had told her at seventeen, and to ask them to stand with her over a mouth in the world.

Or she needed to go down and see the gate with her own eyes, before she decided anything at all. Maren's diagrams showed an access stair, sealed behind the sixth ring, somewhere beneath this district—in the cellars of the old salt exchange, if she read the annotations right. Three years of nightly work and she had never once seen what she was holding shut. Perhaps that had been Maren's last kindness. Kindnesses ended tonight.

Hazel banked the lamp low, tucked the ledger inside her coat against her heart, and let herself out into streets gone quiet under curfew. Above her, on the palace hill, the royal banners hung dead-still in the windless dark. Below her feet, half a mile of stone and clay and older things, something vast shifted in its sleep and dreamed of the taste of gifts.

Two days later, when she pried open the rusted hatch in the salt exchange cellar and descended the stair no keeper had walked in a generation, she found the sixth ring broken wide—and fresh footprints in the dust, leading down toward the fifth.
