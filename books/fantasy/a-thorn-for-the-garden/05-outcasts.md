## 5. Outcasts

Hazel left Vell through the corpse-gate at low tide, when the river dragged its filth seaward and the watchmen who should have searched her cart were drunk on cheap plum wine in the tollhouse. She lay among bolts of gray wool with a fever-charm pressed to her breastbone, the one charm she had ever made for herself, and it did its work too well: it cooled her fear until she felt almost nothing at all, just the sway of the cart and the wet stone grinding beneath its wheels.

Three years of invisibility, ended by one glimpse beneath a king's borrowed face. She had gone home from the rite, packed a single satchel, and burned her ledgers in the shop's cold hearth—her mentor's careful hand curling black into ash. Before she left she had knelt on the cellar floor, palms flat against the sealed gate's ancient stone, and poured everything she could spare into the binding without opening the hairline seam that would tell her how badly it was failing. It had shuddered under her hands like a sick animal. She had felt it *thinning*.

*Hold*, she had told it. *I will find something better than me.*

Then she had walked up into the gray morning and become smuggled goods.

The road south ran three days through drowned farmland where scarecrows wore the king's new edicts nailed to their chests. On the second night she paid a river-barge man in luck-knots—real ones, for once—and he carried her past the checkpoint at Weir Bridge folded into a coil of tarred rope, breathing through a reed while soldiers' boots crossed the deck above her head. The barge man never asked what she was. In the free lands beyond the crown's reach, not asking was its own currency.

Brask announced itself before she saw it: a smell of pitch, salt fish, and burnt sugar, rising off a huddle of islands stitched together with rope bridges and crooked piers. No walls. No gates. A city built on the certainty that no conqueror bothered burning a place with nothing worth taking—though Hazel knew, now, that this certainty was already rotting.

She came ashore at the Fishmouth stair with two coppers and a lie about looking for her uncle, and within an hour the lie was unnecessary, because a girl picked her pocket on the Rope Market bridge and stole the last thing of her old life she owned.

* * *

Sera counted the stolen purse's contents in the doorway of the Drowned Lantern, and stopped counting when she found the charm inside.

It was a small thing, a knot of braided hemp no longer than her thumb, warm as a held coin. She'd seen a hundred like it sold in Vell's night market by a round-faced woman with ink-stained fingers who never looked anyone in the eye. Sera had stolen from that woman once—a whole tray of fever-charms—and fenced them for enough to eat for a month. She remembered the woman's face when the theft was discovered: not rage. Something worse. A kind of weary recognition, as if the world had done this to her so many times it no longer surprised.

"Small world," Sera said to the purse, and laughed, and went to find the woman it belonged to.

Hazel was easy to locate; Brask was a village pretending to be a city, and strangers glowed like lanterns. She sat in the Drowned Lantern's corner nursing a cup of fish broth she couldn't afford, watching the door the way rabbits watch hedgerows. When Sera dropped the purse on her table, Hazel flinched as if struck.

"You," Hazel said.

"Me." Sera dropped into the opposite chair uninvited. Up close she was all sharp angles and sea-salt hair, twenty-something and built like a rumor. "You've got a good memory for thieves."

"You've got a bad habit of stealing from people who could turn your bones to glass."

"Could you?" Sera grinned, and it was a good grin, crooked and entirely without apology. "Here I thought you were just a charm-seller. Luck-knots and fever-wards. Harmless stuff." She leaned forward, voice dropping. "That's what everyone in this city used to be, you know. Harmless. Half of Brask has the gift in some small miserable way, and half of those are hiding from the edict, and now here comes a Vell witch smelling of gate-stone and grave-dirt, and everybody wants to know what she's really carrying."

The word hit Hazel like a hand on her chest. "How do you know about—"

"I don't. That's the point. I know you're hiding something bigger than charms, because I've spent my life learning what hiding looks like." Sera turned the purse over, pushed it across the table. "Keep your coppers. Keep your knot. But you should know the tide-talk says the crown's hunters took Weir Bridge two days ago. They're moving south. This city is the next name on their list, and most of the people in it are too busy pretending otherwise to make plans."

Hazel looked at the thief for a long moment—at the grin that didn't quite hide the calculation underneath, or the calculation that didn't quite hide the fear. Condemned people recognizing each other. That was all either of them was.

"Why warn me?" Hazel asked.

"Because you owe me a debt now," Sera said cheerfully, "and I collect in interesting times."

* * *

The hedge-witch was called Maren Osei, and she kept a garden on the roof of a collapsed customs house where she grew things that shouldn't grow this far north: feverfew with silver veins, monkshood that hummed when the wind touched it. Her magic was old and slow and patient—the kind that coaxed rather than commanded—and her hands were stained green to the wrist as if the garden had taken root in her.

"The gate," she repeated, when Hazel told her. Not the edict, not the false king—Maren had heard all that in fragments already. Only the gate. "You're the keeper's heir, then. Old Wren's girl."

"You knew my mentor?"

"I knew *of* her. We all did, back when there was still an 'all.'" Maren pressed her thumb into a sprig of monkshood and it quieted. "There were keepers once in every great city. Vell's was the last, and the hungriest binding. When Wren died, we thought the gift had passed out of the world entirely." Her dark eyes lifted. "Instead it passed to a girl selling luck-knots. Did she choose you?"

"She died before she finished choosing me. I got the burden and none of the teaching." Hazel wrapped her arms around herself. "The gate is failing faster. I felt it before I left. Something down there is waking up, and the king—" she stumbled on the word— "the thing wearing the king is feeding it gifted blood, and it still isn't enough, which means eventually it will come up here on its own. To Brask first. Then everywhere."

Maren studied her the way she studied plants, patiently, looking for rot. "And you came to warn us."

"I came to warn you and to beg for help. There are magicians here. Real ones. And the binding needs real strength to hold—or a way to remake it that doesn't kill every keeper who touches it."

"There's a child," Maren said slowly. "Came in off a wrecking ship two seasons back. Storm-touched. Calls lightning out of clear skies when she has nightmares. Sweet girl. Terrible dreams." She rose, brushing soil from her knees. "If you mean to gather the condemned and do battle with a devourer, Gatekeeper, you'd best start with someone whose power frightens even her."

* * *

The storm-child's name was Pip, and she lived in the bell cage of a sunken chapel, halfway up a rope ladder over black water, because up high nothing could sneak up on her and down low everything could.

Hazel climbed to her on the fourth evening, palms roped raw, and found a small figure of maybe ten years sitting cross-legged in the rusted cage with her hair standing on end in permanent static. Pip watched her come with eyes the gray-green of the sky before hail.

"Maren says you're magic," Pip said. "Real magic. Not garden magic."

"Garden magic is real magic," Hazel said, breathless on the cage's edge. "Mine is doors, mostly. Locks and gates and things that close."

Pip considered this. "Closing things is boring."

"It's the only reason the world hasn't been eaten yet. That's fairly exciting, if you think about it sideways."

Lightning cracked then—not from any cloud, but from the child herself, a white-blue snap that leapt from her shoulder to the cage bars and sang around the iron. Hazel's ears rang. Below, the black water lit up, showing shapes drifting beneath the surface. Pip had shrunk into herself, fists balled, breathing fast.

"Nightmare," she whispered. "Sorry."

"What did you dream?"

"That everyone I love turns into water." Pip wiped her nose with her sleeve. "That happens a lot lately. More than usual. Like something big is drinking the sky and all the storms want to run away from it."

Hazel felt the words settle in her stomach like stones. Even the weather, it seemed, sensed the thing beneath Vell stirring in its sleep.

"Pip," she said carefully, "if I promised you a family of people who wouldn't leave, would that help? Or would it just give your nightmares more material?"

The child's mouth twitched. "More material. But yes anyway."

* * *

They met in Maren's rooftop garden on the night the tide-talk confirmed it: riders sighted on the coast road, crown banners, a full company of witch-hunters with fire-wagons behind them. Two days, maybe three, and Brask would burn for the crime of sheltering the harmless.

Sera arrived last, late, and slightly bleeding from a job Hazel didn't ask about, and threw a rolled leather case onto the garden flagstones. "Stole these off a crown courier," she announced. "Don't thank me yet."

Hazel unrolled it. Maps. Palace maps—Vell's palace, every vault and subvault, including three floors beneath the oldest crypt marked in faded surveyor's ink: *gate approaches, disused*. Her heart slammed against her ribs.

"Where did a courier get these?"

"He didn't. His commander did. And his commander got them from the Office of Edicts, which apparently has known about your gate for years and has surveyors' notes on it going back decades." Sera's grin had gone thin. "Which means the crown always knew what sits under Vell, and outlawed magic anyway. You don't ban the thing you're afraid of, Gatekeeper. You ban it so you can harvest it quietly."

Maren's voice was very quiet. "Two days. Maybe three."

Around the garden, in the humming dark, four people stood looking at each other over a bed of silver-veined feverfew: a hedge-witch too old to run, a child who dreamed in lightning, a thief with stolen maps, and a gatekeeper with no training and a dying door beneath a dying city.

"We can't fight a company of hunters," Hazel said. "And we can't stay. So we take the tunnels on these maps, we go back to Vell, and we go under the palace—to the gate. If it fails, everything fails. If it holds, maybe I learn enough to save it." She looked around the circle, at the faces of the condemned, waiting for someone to laugh at her.

Pip reached out and took her hand. Small sparks jumped between their palms, painful and bright.

Nobody laughed. Sera just sighed, pulled her knife, and began cutting a slice of moonfruit with terrible elegance. "Interesting times," she said. "I did collect."

But it was Maren who saw it first—standing at the garden's edge, staring down toward the black water of the harbor, where lights were moving. Not ships' lights. Lanterns in a line, disciplined, advancing along the shore road through the night, hours early and moving fast.

"Fire-wagons," the hedge-witch whispered. "They're here tonight."
