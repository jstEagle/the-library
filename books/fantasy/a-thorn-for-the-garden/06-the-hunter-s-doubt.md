## 6. The Hunter's Doubt

The orders came at dusk, sealed in black wax, and Captain Dain Marrow read them three times before his hands stopped shaking.

*By the king's grace: the settlement called Brask harbors no fewer than forty practitioners of the forbidden art. Evidence attached. Burn it. Burn them. Leave nothing that casts a shadow.*

Forty. He had hunted sorcerers for eleven years, and he had never seen a writ naming forty in one place. Witches kept to ones and twos—a hedge-woman here, a hedge-man there, a child who talked to rain. Forty was not a coven. Forty was an army.

He unrolled the evidence with hands he forced to be steady.

There were confessions. Eleven of them, each sworn before a crown notary, each describing in careful detail the rituals practiced in Brask's cellars: blood drawn into bowls, names of saints burned backward, pacts signed beneath a moon the confessors swore had turned the color of rust. Dain read the first confession twice and felt something cold begin to crawl up his spine, because the handwriting was the same.

All eleven confessions, sworn by eleven different souls—a fishwife, a dockhand, a boy of perhaps nine—were written in the same clerk's hand. The same slant on the descenders. The same ink-blot where a nib had caught, repeated four times across four different names.

He sat very still in his tent and listened to the wind coming off the Saltreach marshes, carrying the smell of woodsmoke from Brask's chimneys, two miles distant. Supper smoke. Someone over there was frying onions.

Dain had burned people before. He wanted that truth sitting plainly in front of him, because he would need it for what came next. He had burned a woman in Corvane who had cursed a miller's ox dead—true confession, witnessed, the ox's carcass black as pitch. He had burned a man in Dunmoor Hollow who had opened a neighbor's veins with a word. He had knelt afterward, both times, and prayed, and told himself the fire was justice wearing its terrible face, and slept.

He had never once questioned whether the faces were true.

* * *

He rode into Brask himself under a parley flag, because he was not yet so far fallen that he would read names off paper without looking into eyes.

The smuggler city clung to the marsh like a barnacle to a hull—crooked streets, houses built on pilings over black water, bridges of rope and salt-bleached plank. Its people watched him pass with the flat, unblinking stares of creatures who had been hunted before. He counted doors as he went. Counted windows. Counted, without meaning to, heads.

Thirty-one. Perhaps thirty-five if you counted the children hiding behind their mothers' skirts, and he did count them, because someone had once counted him.

The woman who met him in the square was gray-haired and broad as a door, with burn scars up both forearms that he recognized—the old kind, from cooking fires, from lives spent near flame rather than wielding it. She introduced herself as Maren. She did not offer her hand. He had not expected her to.

"Captain." Her voice was flat as slate. "Come to count us for the pyre, or will you hear us first?"

"I'll hear you."

"Then hear this. There are thirty-four souls in Brask. Your writ says forty. Ask yourself where your crown found the other six." She let that sit. "Ask yourself how eleven of us confessed to crimes we've never heard of, when half those confessors died last winter of the marsh fever. I buried Ansel Cotter myself, captain. I put him in the ground with my own hands. Yet here is his name, sworn and living, testifying that I drink blood under the full moon."

She held out a paper. Dain took it. It was a copy of a confession—his own warrant's evidence, or its twin—and the name at the bottom was *Ansel Cotter*, and above it, in a strong careful clerk's hand, were words about bowls and blood and backward prayers.

"You keep copies," he said quietly.

"We keep everything." Her eyes were old and hard and unbearably tired. "We are smugglers, captain. Liars, thieves, runaways. Everything you say we are, we likely are, in some fashion. But we are not what your papers say. And I have wondered, these three years, why the crown keeps sending hunters to a swamp full of poor folk, when any real witch with sense has long since fled beyond the border." She leaned close, and her whisper carried the weight of a verdict. "I have wondered if it isn't the hunting you're sent for, captain. If it isn't the *finding*."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He sat in his tent with the evidence spread around him like the pieces of a dissection, and he cut the body of the lie open and looked at its organs.

The notary's seal on every confession belonged to Master Ellard Vay, Crown Notary of the Third Bench—a man Dain had met twice, whose office sat within a hundred feet of the king's own apartments. The ink on all eleven documents matched; he tested it against his field kit the way he'd been taught to test for forged warrants, and it matched down to the mineral bite of the lye. Dates were impossible—confessions dated days after their confessors' deaths, testimony describing Brask cellars by a woman who, per the parish rolls he'd demanded from the quartermaster, had lived her whole life in Vell and never traveled west.

And then there was the pattern. He laid out every writ he'd ever served, every hunt he'd ever led, eleven years of them, and saw what he had refused to see: they followed no logic of threat. The greatest concentrations of the gift—the academies that had once dotted the north, the great temple-healers, the battle-mages of the old wars—had scattered quietly years ago and were left alone. Instead the crown hunted fishermen's wives and fever-charmers and luck-knot sellers in night markets. Small game. Harmless game. Game that could not fight back, dragged screaming through the streets while crowds watched and learned to be afraid.

Not persecution. *Harvest.* The word arrived in his mind whole, the way truths sometimes do, fully formed and terrible, and once it had arrived he could not imagine how he had housed himself for eleven years without it. The crown was not afraid of magic. The crown was *collecting* it. Every hunter's pyre, every wagon of prisoners bound for the capital instead of the stake—bound for the capital *first*, always first, "for processing," a word he had never thought to question—every one was a delivery. He had been a courier. He had been a good dog, fetching things home.

Near dawn, his lieutenant, Brennick, lifted the tent flap and found him sitting among the papers like a man at his own trial.

"You've been here all night, captain."

"Brennick." Dain's voice came out strange to him, scraped hollow. "In Corvane. The woman with the ox. You remember."

"The witch? Aye."

"She confessed truly. That part was true. I've checked it a dozen times in my head; it was true." He looked up. "But she was the seventh, Brennick. Or maybe the ninth. I've lost which ones were real. I stood at eleven years of stakes and I cannot tell you anymore which fires were justice and which were just—*" His voice failed. He tried again. "Which were just deliveries."

Brennick was silent a long moment. He was a solid man, a farmer's son, and Dain watched him wrestle with it the way a man wrestles a gate he doesn't want to open. When he finally spoke, his voice was low. "The men are asking. Why Brask. They've heard about the confessions—some of them can read, captain. Some of them have noticed the handwriting too." He hesitated. "What do we tell them?"

Dain looked at the writ. At the black wax. At the king's seal, which he had kissed at every promotion, which he had pressed to his own forehead the day he earned his captaincy, believing it holy.

Justice cannot live inside a law that lies, he thought. Better a lawless world with clean hands in it than this.

"Tell them the truth," he said. "Tell them the evidence is forged. Tell them the crown sent us to murder thirty-four people to feed—" He stopped, because he did not yet have proof enough of *what*, only of *that*, and he was still a man who distinguished between the two. "To feed a lie. Then tell them Captain Marrow is done."

* * *

It should have ended with swords. He had prepared for it—drew his blade at sunrise and stood before his eighty men and told them everything, papers spread on a supply cart, voice raw. He had expected mutiny, arrest, a fight in the mud.

Instead, silence. Then a young crossbowman named Pell set his weapon down on the cart, deliberately, where everyone could see it. Then another man. Then six. Then forty.

Some did not. Twenty-nine rode east at midmorning, back toward the capital and whatever punishment awaited deserters, and Dain let them go, because they had chosen honestly and he could ask no more than that. Fifty-one remained, sitting their horses in the mist off the marsh road, looking at him the way sailors look at a captain after the storm has taken the mast.

"What now, captain?" Brennick asked. "We're dead men either way, come morning reports."

"Now," Dain said, "we learn what we were guarding."

Because there was one thing more. In his years at the palace barracks, he had walked corridors other men never saw, delivered prisoners down stairways that appeared on no plan, and once—only once—he had seen a map. An old surveyor, drunk and terrified, had shown him drawings of the palace deep vaults: levels beneath levels, older than the dynasty, and at the bottom of them a chamber marked with no room number but a single word in a dead language. The surveyor had been found in the canal three days later.

Dain had forgotten. He had made himself forget, the way soldiers make themselves forget most things. Now he drew it from memory on the back of the forged writ—the descending shafts, the guard rotations, the sealed iron door below the king's own apartments—and as he drew it, he understood that he had not been spared this knowledge by chance. Nothing about the crown was chance.

"Maren!" he called toward the marsh city, where the people of Brask had emerged to watch their pyre never come. "Your witches. Your runaways. Is there a woman among them—dark-haired, keeps a charm shop, goes by Reyes?"

Maren's brows rose. "Came through two nights past. She's asking after help of her own." A pause, sharp as a blade drawn. "Why, captain?"

Dain folded the map—the vaults, the iron door, the word in the dead language—and held it up where she could see it.

"Because I think we're all asking after the same door," he said. "And I think the thing behind it wears the crown."
