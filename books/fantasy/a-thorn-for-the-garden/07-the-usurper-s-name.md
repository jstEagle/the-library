## 7. The Usurper's Name

The archives beneath the palace of Vell had not been built by the king who now wore its crown. Hazel knew that the moment Dain's lantern light touched the first shelf. The stone was older, darker, veined with a faint silver that only showed itself to her gift—a lattice of binding-threads woven through the masonry like roots through soil. Whoever had raised this room had meant it to hold knowledge the way her gate held hunger: carefully, reverently, and with the understanding that some things must never be let out.

She trailed her fingers along a shelf of ledgers bound in grey leather, each one furred with dust thick as felt. Behind her, Sera moved through the stacks with the easy silence of a thief in a house she'd already decided to rob, and the hedge-witch Maren kept close to the lantern, lips moving in small protective charms she didn't bother to hide anymore.

"You said the deep vaults," Hazel murmured to Dain. "You said the crown keeps records no one reads."

"I said what my instructors said." Dain's voice was flat, careful. He still moved like a man walking on ice over deep water. Three days since Brask burned; three days since he'd torn the king's warrant in half and thrown it into the fire with everything else they'd saved. "I never thought to test it. Hunters aren't encouraged to be curious."

"Curiosity gets you promoted here or dead there," Sera said from the shadows. "Pick your poison."

Hazel wasn't listening. Her gift had caught on something—a wrongness in the air ahead, a thinness, like a room where a fire has just gone out. She turned down an aisle narrower than the rest, toward a door of black iron banded in silver, unmarked except for a single carved symbol at eye height: a gate, closed, with a thorn growing through its bars.

Her heart stopped and then started again, harder.

"That's the keeper's mark." Her voice came out strange. "My mentor wore it. Inked on his wrist. He told me it was just—old guild sigil. Nothing."

"Guilds love their sigils," Maren said softly. "They also love their secrets. Open it."

Hazel pressed her palm to the iron. It was warm. Not sun-warm—*blood*-warm, and through her gift she felt the answering pulse of something vast and patient far below, separated from her by forty feet of stone and three years of careful lies she'd told herself about being safe.

She pushed the door open.

* * *

Captain Dain Marrow had burned eleven houses in his service to the crown. He could name them. He made himself name them now, standing in the dark of the usurper's own archive while the witch Hazel Reyes held a lamp up to shelves of documents that would have sent him to the pyre for reading.

*Ashford. The miller's family, four souls. The girl in the river district whose crime was singing to storms.*

He had believed, once, that the edict was mercy. That magic was a rot and the crown the surgeon's knife, cutting clean before the infection spread. His father had died holding that belief like a shield, and Dain had inherited it along with his commission, and he had carried both without ever once looking underneath.

Now he looked.

Hazel had spread papers across a reading table older than the kingdom: census rolls, tithe records, hunter deployment orders going back six years. And the pattern was so obvious he wanted to be sick. Every purge. Every arrest. Every village where gifted blood had been found and taken—not executed, *taken*, carted to the capital in wagons with covered beds—and every one of them clustered along a line on the map. A line that ran, Dain realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the archive's damp, directly beneath the palace.

"They weren't executions," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "All those years. We were delivering them. Like—"

"Like grain to a mill." Hazel didn't look up. She was reading a folio with shaking hands, and her face in the lamplight was the color of tallow. "Like feed."

Sera leaned against the shelf beside him, arms crossed, watching him with a thief's flat appraisal. "You want to throw up, there's a corner back by the geography section. Everyone uses it."

"I want a name." Dain gripped the edge of the table until his knuckles went white. "Six years I served this thing. I'll not hunt it blind another day. Whose face is he wearing?"

Maren looked up from a genealogical roll she'd unspooled across half a table, weighted at the corners with candle stubs. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet, the way people speak in sickrooms.

"The queen's brother," she said. "Prince Aldric Vane. Queen Isolde's younger brother. He drowned crossing the Serpent's Reach twenty years ago—or the histories say he drowned." She traced a line of ink. "There's a marginal note here. Later hand. Different ink. It says: *'The body was never found. The body that came home was never his.'*"

The lantern flame bent, though there was no draft.

"Aldric," Hazel whispered. She had gone still in a way that made Dain's skin crawl—the stillness of someone listening to something enormous. "When I saw him at the rite. There was nothing inside him, nothing human, just borrowed power stitched over a hollow. But the stitching—" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and blazing at once. "The stitching was *gate-magic*. The same weave. The same hand. He didn't learn it. He *took* it, from the thing below, the way you'd take a coat off a corpse."

"And a man wearing a devourer's coat," Maren said, rolling up the genealogy with grim ceremony, "eventually starts thinking with the devourer's thoughts. He flushed the gifted out of hiding for six years because their blood feeds the binding that holds him together. And now that the harvest is nearly done—"

"Now he doesn't need to hide what he is," Hazel finished. "The coronation feast. He's going to stop pretending. He's going to *open himself*, and when he does—"

Dain pushed back from the table. "Then we have his name. What good is a name against a king?"

Hazel reached past him and drew out the last document, the one she'd been saving, the one wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the iron-bound chest. She unwrapped it with the tenderness of someone handling a wound. It was a single page, ancient, written in a script that hurt to look at directly—but beneath the script, in plain old Vellan, someone had added a translation, and above the translation sat the keeper's mark again: the gate, the bars, the thorn.

"It's the original binding," she said. "The words the first keepers used. My mentor's notes said the gate could be *opened* by any keeper's blood—but it could only be *remade* by the full rite. And the rite requires two things." She laid her finger on the page. "The devourer's own magic, freely given back. And a keeper willing to stand in the gate and speak the words until they're finished speaking her."

Silence settled over the archive like ash.

"He bound himself to it," Hazel said, and now her voice was steady, and Dain heard in it the sound of fear hardening into something else—something with edges. "Aldric bound himself to the devourer, which means the devourer's magic runs in him like sap in a tree. If we bring him to the gate—alive—if we give that stolen magic *back* to the thing below, the binding can be remade whole. Stronger than it's been in a thousand years. And he comes apart, because he is nothing but that magic wearing a dead man's face."

Sera whistled low. "So the plan is walk into a feast hall full of guards, drag the most powerful creature on the continent to a hole in the ground, and feed him to it. Simple. Clean. No chance anyone dies horribly."

"No," Hazel agreed. She rolled up the binding and tied it with cord, and her hands did not shake anymore. "But I've been dying slowly for three years anyway, holding a gate shut alone in the dark. I'd rather die *moving*."

Dain looked at her—at this woman who had hidden herself so long she'd nearly vanished, who had watched neighbors dragged away and hated herself for it, who carried a burden that should have broken a battalion—and he felt the last of his old faith crumble and something new set in its place, like bone knitting.

"I know the guard rotations," he said. "I know which nobles will run and which will fight, and I know the way from the feast hall to the old tunnels, because I helped seal them myself two years ago." He met her eyes. "I put hunters in those tunnels to make sure nothing came out of the dark. Let me use the same roads to put something back *in*."

Hazel studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and it felt like a vow.

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, and near dawn Hazel stood alone at the iron door, the binding rolled against her chest, listening.

Far below, the devourer stirred against its chains, and the tremor came up through the stone into her bones like a word trying to be spoken. And beneath it, thinner, higher, she heard the other thing—the hollow king above, pulling at the same root, drinking faster now, gorging himself for the day he meant to stop hiding.

Two mouths. One meal. And between them, her.

"We come for you tomorrow," she whispered to the dark, to the gate, to the mentor who had died keeping it and the strangers across centuries who had done the same. "Both of you. Tomorrow the garden gets its thorn."

Behind her, in the archive, Dain struck the lantern out, and the darkness took them all like a tide taking swimmers—and somewhere far above, the false king smiled in his sleep, and did not yet know why.
