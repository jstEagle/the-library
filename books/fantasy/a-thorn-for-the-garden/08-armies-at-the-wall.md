## 8. Armies at the Wall

The horns came at dawn, and Hazel knew before she opened her eyes that the sound was wrong.

Brask's harbor horns were deep, braying things, meant to call fishing boats home through fog. These horns were thin and silver and many, layered one atop another like knives laid edge to edge, and they came from the landward side, from the pass above the terraced fields where no army had ever come because no army had ever bothered. Brask was a smuggler city. Smuggler cities were useful to kings.

This king did not find them useful.

She stood at the window of the safehouse above the salt-merchant's yard and watched the pass fill with light. Not sunlight. Lantern-light, torch-light, the cold glitter of a thousand helments catching first sun, a river of steel pouring down the switchback road in perfect silence. No drums. No war-cries. The usurper's legions did not sing. They simply came, the way water comes into a hull, patient and certain and without malice, and that was somehow worse than any howling horde.

"They were supposed to take another week." Sera stood at her shoulder, arms crossed, her thief's stillness broken at last. Her knuckles were white. "The outriders said a week. I paid the outriders."

"Your outriders were paid twice," Hazel said quietly. "Once by us. Once by the crown."

Below them, Brask was waking to the same arithmetic. She saw it move through the streets like a tide going out—doors opening, faces tilting toward the pass, and then the doors closing again, and the market stalls that never closed, closing. A city of outlaws knew exactly what a legion at the pass meant. There would be no parley. There would be no trial. The edict had made the whole city guilty by existing, and the thing wearing the king's face did not leave guilty things alive to breed more guilt.

Nix was on the roof.

Hazel felt her before she saw her—that faint pressure in the air, the taste of a storm about to happen, ozone and iron. The storm-child sat cross-legged on the ridge tiles with her knees drawn up, seven years old and ancient-eyed, watching the legions come with the expression of someone reading a letter addressed to someone else. Static crawled in her dark hair. The weathervanes of Brask had all turned toward her in the night, every one of them, like iron filings finding north.

"You should come inside," Hazel called up.

"They're going to burn the boats first." Nix's voice was flat and certain. "That's what armies do. Burn the boats so nobody leaves."

She was right, of course. An hour later, when the vanguard reached the harbor road, the first thing the legion did was fire the shipyards. Hazel watched from the safehouse window as three seasons of smuggled hulls went up in a wall of black smoke, and the smoke bent—bent, against the wind—toward the sea, because Nix was standing on the roof with her arms raised and her small hands full of weather, dragging the smoke out over the water so the people below could breathe, could see, could run.

*She's seven years old,* Hazel thought, *and she's already spending herself like a woman with nothing left to lose.*

*So were you,* said the other voice, the mentor's voice, the one that lived in her chest now that Merrick was ash and his records were a bundle under her arm. *You spent three years spending yourself. The only difference is she never learned to hide.*

The hedge-witch, Maren, came up the stairs two at a time, gray braid flying, her satchel already strapped. "Tunnels?"

"Tunnels," Hazel said.

"You're sure they're still open? Three years, girl. Three years since you walked out of Vell, and roots don't care about your duty, and stone doesn't care about your—"

"They're open." She had checked. She had been checking in her sleep for weeks, her gift reaching down through Brask's foundations into the old keeper-ways that ran beneath the coast like veins beneath skin, the tunnels Merrick's maps had shown her, the ones that had let the first keepers travel between their gates unseen. The gate under Vell had a sister once. Every gate did. The network was dead, mostly—collapsed, drowned, forgotten—but dead things left bones, and bones left passages, and one of those passages ran from beneath the salt-yard to the cliffs four miles north of Brask.

Four miles of tunnel. Four miles ahead of an army that would find the entrance within the hour.

"Everyone out," Hazel said. "Now."

* * *

There were forty-one of them. She counted them into the dark the way Merrick had taught her to count charms onto a string—one, and the next, and the next, and never look back at the ones already threaded.

Maren went first with her lantern and her herb-satchel and two children not her own clinging to her skirts. Then the smugglers, the forger, the hedge-mage who could still the pain in burns, the old man who had once been a court musician until the edict learned his fingers could coax magic out of a fiddle. Sera went last of the adults, because Sera always went last, because Sera said a thief's job was to be the one nobody got behind.

Hazel stood at the tunnel mouth in the salt-yard's cellar, one hand pressed flat to the stone, holding the passage open. That was the truth of what she was doing, though she'd never told the others. The old ways did not stay open out of habit. They stayed open because a keeper held them, the way a hand holds a door against a wind that wants it shut. Her gift ran down her arm and into the rock and along four miles of dark, and she could feel every one of the forty-one as a small warmth moving through her, through the stone, like sparks down a fuse.

Above her, Brask began to burn in earnest.

She heard it through the stone—muffled, rhythmic, the sound of a city being unmade. The legions worked with terrible order: fire first in the harbor quarter, then the terraces, then the market where Hazel had bought fever-root and sold luck-knots for three years of hiding. Screams came down through the rock in fragments, and she could not tell which were human and which were gulls, and she hated herself for not being able to tell, and she hated the stone for being so thick, and she hated most of all that she kept counting. Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Because the counting was the duty, and the duty was the only shape her hands knew.

"Hazel." Sera's voice, low, at her shoulder. "The child."

"I know."

Nix had not come down from the roof.

They had told her. They had all told her, Maren with her arms out and Sera with her thief's promises and Hazel with the plain truth: *the tunnels, the cliffs, four miles, we go together.* And Nix had looked at them with those old eyes and said, "The smoke has to go out to sea, or they'll see where the boats didn't go. They'll follow the empty water." As if that settled it. As if a seven-year-old's arithmetic about smoke and search-parties were a door that closed behind her.

Hazel felt her now, above and behind, a knot of storm-light in her gift's reach. The child was dragging the wind itself across the burning city, bending the fire's breath, herding the smoke column north—*away* from the cliffs, away from the tunnel mouth, spending weather like a woman spending her last coin. The pressure of it made Hazel's teeth ache. Somewhere up there the weathervanes were shrieking in circles, and the sky over Brask had gone the color of a bruise, and a small girl was holding all of it the way Hazel held the gate: alone, and past the point where alone was survivable.

Thirty-one. Thirty-two.

The cellar door above them burst open.

Not the legion—worse, in a way. Neighbors. Brask's own, the ones who had decided, in the way of people everywhere when the soldiers come, that survival might still be bought with someone else's name. Three men with knives and torches, and behind them, in the doorway, the gray-and-crimson of a crown scout, and the scout's eyes went straight to the tunnel mouth, to the lantern-light spilling up out of the dark, to the line of the condemned filing into the earth.

"There," he said. "There's the nest."

Sera moved. Hazel had seen Sera move a hundred times—over rooftops, through locks, across a market stall with Hazel's own charm-purse in her hand—and it had always looked like grace. This was not grace. This was a knife that had been waiting three years for a reason. The scout went down before his sword cleared the sheath. The neighbors went down after him, or rather they went *away*, stumbling back into the smoke, because whatever was in Sera's face when she straightened was not a thing a neighbor wanted to buy survival from.

"Go," Sera said. "I'll hold the door."

"No." Hazel's voice came out of her like something torn. "We hold it *together*. Thirty-five. Thirty-six."

The last of them came down the stairs—Maren's stragglers, the fiddler carrying his broken instrument like a wounded child—and Hazel counted thirty-nine, forty, forty-one, and then the tunnel was empty behind her and full ahead of her and she let the door of stone begin to close.

That was when Nix let go of the sky.

Hazel felt it happen from four miles away, felt it the way you feel a bone break in your own hand. The storm-child had been holding back the lightning all morning—gathering it, banking it, the way Merrick had taught *Hazel* to bank power—and now she spent it all at once, every hoarded hour of it, down into the pass where the legions were funneled ten thousand strong between the cliffs. The thunder came through the stone as one long continuous roar. The tunnel dust jumped from the walls. Somewhere above, the sky over Brask turned white, and the pass turned white, and ten thousand men learned in the same instant what the edict had been hunting all along: not witches in towers, but a child on a roof, seven years old, who had decided that the price of forty-one strangers' lives was a price she could pay.

The stone door sealed.

Hazel stood in the dark with her palm against it, and through the last finger-width of open rock she felt the knot of storm-light flicker—flicker—like a lantern running out of oil—and go out.

Not fade. Not drift. *Go out*, the way a voice goes out, the way a name goes out of a house after the funeral.

"No," Hazel said, to the stone, to the dark, to the arithmetic. "No—"

"Hazel." Sera's hand on her arm, iron-tight. "The legions are still coming. The tunnel won't hold them long. *Walk.*"

Hazel walked. She put one foot in front of the other down four miles of Merrick's dead network, past the others huddled and stumbling and weeping, past Maren murmuring prayers to gods who had never once answered, and she did not cry, because the thing building in her chest was not grief. Grief was a soft thing. This had edges. This had a face—the king's face, the stolen face, the hollow thing behind it that had sent ten thousand men to burn a city and a child to hold the smoke.

At the tunnel's end the cliffs opened onto gray sea-light, and the forty-one came out into the wind, alive, and Hazel stood at the mouth of the dark with salt spray on her face and made herself a promise with edges on it.

The gate under Vell was failing. The usurper was coming to it, or she was coming to him—either way, the road ran through the deep vaults and the throne room and the thing below the earth, and she would walk every mile of it.

She turned to Sera, and Dain's maps were already open between them against the wind.

"Show me the way into the palace."
