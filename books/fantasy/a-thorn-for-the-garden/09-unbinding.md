## 9. Unbinding

The palace of Vell had never smelled of rot before.

Hazel noticed it the moment she passed beneath the outer gate in the stolen livery of a feast-servant—a sweetness gone wrong, like fruit left too long in a closed room, threaded through the incense and roasting meat and spilled wine. Three years of living above the sealed gate had taught her nose what hunger smelled like, and it was everywhere here, soaked into the stone, rising through the marble floors as though the whole palace were a lid set over something starving.

Which, she thought grimly, it was.

She carried a tray of honeyed pears and let the crowd of servants carry her. The coronation feast filled the great hall with light—ten hundred candles, chandeliers like frozen firefalls, nobles in silk the colors of bruised plums and old blood. Musicians played from a gallery above. Guards lined the walls in their black-and-gold, halberds gleaming, and at the far end of the hall, on the dais beneath the antlered crown of Vell, *he* sat.

The king who was not a king.

Hazel kept her eyes down and her gift folded small behind her ribs, a coal wrapped in ash. But even folded, even buried, she felt him—the way you feel a toothache through clenched teeth. Borrowed power radiated off the throne in slow waves, magic that did not belong to the man wearing it, stitched into his skin like embroidery into rotten cloth.

*Stolen,* she thought. *All of it stolen.*

The charm-buyer dragged screaming from the night market three months ago. The hedge-witch's brother, drowned in a cistern for a cantrip. The storm-child's mother, taken before they ever reached Brask. Every gifted soul the edict had flushed from hiding across three years—she could feel echoes of them now, faint signatures braided into the usurper's flesh, spent but not erased. He wore them the way he wore the dead king's face.

The tray trembled. She steadied it.

*Not yet.* Dain's voice in her memory, low and certain: *When the toast comes. Everything turns on the toast. Eyes up, cups raised, guards at parade rest. You'll have forty heartbeats, no more.*

Across the hall, near the musicians' stair, Sera moved through the crowd in a noblewoman's borrowed gown, laughing at some lord's joke, her fingers already inside his purse out of pure habit. Their eyes met for half a second. Sera touched her ear—the signal. *In position.*

Hazel breathed out and began to walk toward the dais with the pears.

* *

The toast came at the ninth bell.

"Rise," the chamberlain cried, "and drink to His Majesty, long may he reign!"

Ten hundred cups rose. Ten hundred voices thundered his name—and under cover of it, Hazel set down her tray, drew the thin knife from her sleeve, and walked straight through the parting crowd toward the throne.

Forty heartbeats. She counted them.

One. Two. The usurper lifted his own cup, and the candlelight slid across his face—the dead king's face, handsome still, still young, preserved the way things are preserved in amber. Only the eyes were wrong. They had been wrong at the public rite too, but here, this close, she could see why: there was nothing behind them looking out. No man. Just a shape holding its place, like water holding the memory of a hand.

Three. Four. A guard turned toward her. Five—

Sera stumbled into the man, all apologies and spilled wine, and the guard's attention broke.

Six through twenty, Hazel crossed open floor, and the hall's noise fell away from her like a cloak dropped. She felt the gate then—truly felt it, for the first time since leaving Vell's night market, since fleeing through its tunnels with smoke in her lungs and the storm-child's name caught in her throat like a bone. Beneath the palace, deeper than the vaults, deeper than the dead kings, the devourer stirred against its bindings, and every thread of stolen magic in the false king's body pulled toward it like iron toward a lodestone.

*It knows me,* she realized. *It has always known its keepers.*

Twenty-five. She reached the dais steps. Twenty-six.

The usurper lowered his cup and looked at her, and smiled with the dead king's mouth.

"Little keeper," he said, and only she heard it above the roar of the toast. "You came to me after all."

Thirty-one. She took the last two steps.

"No," Hazel said. "You're coming to *me*."

She drove the knife—not into his heart, there was no heart to find—but into the seam of him, the place below the sternum where the borrowed magic knotted tightest, and she *pulled*.

The hall went silent.

Not quiet—silent, as if the sound itself had been swallowed. The music died mid-note. The toast froze on ten hundred lips. And the face of the king began to come apart.

It peeled like bark off a rotten log. The handsome jaw sloughed; the young cheeks sagged into gray hollows; the dead king's face slid away in ribbons of light and showed what wore it beneath—a thing of stretched skin and shadow, tall as two men, its chest an open lattice through which glowed dozens upon dozens of tiny trapped lights. Faces flickered in each one. The charm-buyer. The hedge-witch's brother. Hundreds more, harvested and hoarded, burning in the dark of him.

The scream that came out of it was not one voice. It was a chorus of the taken, and it shattered every glass on the high table.

Then the hall exploded into motion.

* *

Dain had told her: *Forty heartbeats, and then everything I can give you, which won't be much and won't be long.*

He gave her more than that.

The captain of witch-hunters came over the high table like a landslide, black-and-gold coat torn open to show the plain gray shirt beneath—the shirt of a man with no crown left to serve. He put himself between the guards and the dais with a drawn sword and a voice that cracked across the hall like a whip: "This thing is not your king! Look at it! *Look at it!*"

Some looked. Some fled. Some—she would remember this forever—some threw down their weapons and stood aside. But enough charged. Enough always did. Dain met them at the base of the dais stairs and held, sword moving in the old honest patterns of the training yard, and when they cut him he bled like any man and did not stop.

Hazel seized the unbound thing by what remained of its wrist.

It was stronger than she was. Of course it was—it held the strength of hundreds. It hauled back against her grip, shrieking in a hundred stolen throats, and its free hand closed around her throat with fingers like cold iron, and the lights in its chest flared white with fury.

But it had made one mistake, the same mistake every thief makes.

It had fed on keepers too. Somewhere in that hoard of stolen fire burned the gift of a woman dead thirty years—her mentor, Maren, whose records Hazel had read by candlelight, whose warnings she had memorized, whose binding-craft ran in every knot Hazel had ever tied. And the devourer below knew its keeper's craft when it felt it, even secondhand, even worn by a thief.

The gate *pulled.*

Hazel felt it through the floor, through her bones: the ancient hunger straining upward, recognizing the magic knotted into the creature's flesh as food, as escape, as the door it had starved against for a thousand years. The usurper staggered. His stolen strength guttered like a lamp in wind.

"You can't," it rasped in one voice now, almost human, almost pleading. "The binding is sealed. You'd have to open—"

"Yes," Hazel said.

She dragged him down the dais steps. Past Dain, bleeding and grinning and shouting for her to *run*. Past nobles cowering behind overturned tables. Through the servants' door Sera held open, the thief's face pale and set, her hand steady on the latch. Down the stair that no map showed, the stair Dain had given them from the palace's deep vaults, into the dark where the air grew sweet-rotten and warm.

The thing fought her every step. It tore her sleeve, her skin, her hair. It offered her things in Maren's voice, in her mother's voice, in the storm-child's voice—*power, keeper, I'll give it back, all of it, more*—and she wept as she climbed down and did not stop dragging it.

At the bottom was the gate.

She had sealed it a thousand times in miniature, quietly, in the dark of her shop, and never once seen it whole. It was vast beyond sense: a circle of standing stones older than Vell, older than the kingdom, carved with knots that predated language, and between them hung not a door but a *thinness*—a place where the world wore through, and beyond it a darkness that was not absence but appetite.

The devourer pressed against it like a face against glass.

Hazel shoved the usurper through the stones.

The binding took him the way a wound takes a blade. The thinness parted; the stolen lights in his chest streamed out of him like sparks up a chimney, and the darkness beyond drank them, and drank *him*, and his screaming became a hundred voices became ten became one became silence. The gate shuddered. The knots blazed.

And the hunger, having tasted, pushed.

* *

Hazel stood before it with her hands empty and her gift roaring in her ears like the sea.

She knew what it wanted. It had always wanted this—not the usurper, not the harvest, but the *keeper*. The one thread strong enough to hold the weave, offered freely at the threshold. Every keeper before her had died pouring themselves into these knots, spending their gift to the last drop and dying of the spending.

She thought of the storm-child's laugh, lost in the smoke of Brask. Of Sera saying, *you don't have to be alone with it anymore.* Of Dain bleeding upstairs because he finally believed in something truer than his law. Of three years of harmless charms and weary vigilance, of luck-knots sold to strangers while she starved herself of her own nature.

*Ordinary,* she had promised herself once, in the worst hour of the night. *I'd give it all to be ordinary.*

The gate waited. The hunger waited. Somewhere above, boots on stone, and Sera's voice calling her name down the dark stair, frightened now, truly frightened.

Hazel spread her hands and began to untie the first knot.

"Not to spend," she said aloud, to the dark, to Maren, to herself. "To *give*. There's a difference. There was always a difference."

The first knot opened. Power poured out of her like light from a broken lamp—and instead of dying in her, it *moved*, flowing into the binding, weaving itself into the ancient knots, becoming part of the seal rather than fuel for the breach. The gate drank her gift and grew stronger. The hunger recoiled from its own threshold.

Knot by knot. Thread by thread. She gave it everything—the gift she had hidden, the gift she had feared, the gift that had made her hunted and alone. And as it emptied from her, something strange happened: she did not fall. She did not die. She simply grew quieter, the way a room grows quiet when a song ends.

The last knot closed over her final spark of magic, and sealed.

The thinness stilled. Became stone. Became merely a wall of old rocks in a dark cellar, and nothing behind it at all—or nothing that would ever again reach through.

Hazel Reyes sat down on the cold floor of a world without her magic in it, and found, to her astonishment, that she was still breathing.

Footsteps on the stair. Sera's voice, closer now, breaking: "*Hazel!*"

And beneath the words, so faint she might have imagined it, the smallest tremor—far away, deep down, something turning over in its sleep.

Testing a lock that should never open again.
