## 10. A Thorn for the Garden

The gate chamber had not changed in three years, and it had changed utterly.

Hazel knelt at its center where the seal still glowed—thinner now, threadbare as an old woman's shawl, the devourer's hunger pressing through like cold through a cracked window. Around her the old keeper-marks lay scorched into the stone, her mentor's work, his mentor's before that. Every one of them had died here or died young elsewhere, worn hollow by the holding.

She had come down through the tunnels with Sera's hand in hers and Dain's sword at their backs, past the bodies of the guard who had followed too faithfully, past the throne room's wreckage where the usurper had been dragged screaming out of his stolen face. The thing below had taken him the way a tide takes a sandcastle—slowly, completely, without malice. She could still hear him. Some nights she suspected she always would.

Now there was only this: the failing binding, and her gift, and the choice she had carried down these stairs like a lantern.

"You don't have to do it this way," Sera said. She stood at the chamber's edge, arms wrapped around herself, and for once the thief had nothing clever to steal or say. "The others—the records said they held it with what they had. You have more than any of them."

"That's exactly why I can't hold it," Hazel said gently. "A keeper who lives is a keeper the gate keeps calling. It would drink me down year by year until there was nothing left but the holding. My mentor lasted eleven years. I'd last twenty, maybe. And then some child would find my notes and inherit the trembling floor." She looked down at her hands, the hands that had tied ten thousand luck-knots, that had warmed fevers and found lost coins and never once been thanked for anything true. "But if I pour all of it in—all of it, at once—it isn't a keeper's trickle anymore. It's a river. The binding won't need tending. It will be *full*."

"And you'll be empty."

"Empty," Hazel agreed. "Ordinary." She smiled, and found to her surprise that it didn't hurt. "Do you know, I've been pretending to be ordinary for three years? It will be strange to finally mean it."

Dain said nothing from the stairway. He had said little since Brask burned, since the storm-child Wren had stayed behind in the smoke to turn the sky against the legions and had not walked out of it. But he unclasped the silver hunter's badge from his collar now, crossed the chamber, and set it on the stone beside her—a small bright thing, a king's promise he no longer believed in.

"For the knot," was all he said.

Hazel laughed, wet-eyed, and picked it up. Her fingers knew the work without magic; they always had. She wove the badge into a cord of red thread from her shop, tied the luck-knot she'd sold a thousand strangers, and hung it around her own neck. A charm with no power in it at all. The most honest thing she'd ever made.

Then she turned to the gate.

Up close, the seal was worse than it felt—she could see the dark moving beneath it, vast and patient, the way you can see water under ice. It had waited centuries. It would have waited centuries more, if keepers kept dying slowly enough to feed it their years. It did not know yet that she intended to give it everything at once and nothing after.

She laid both palms flat against the stone.

Her gift rose in her like dawn—not the small domestic warmth she'd rationed for three years, not charms and knots and whispered mending, but the whole of it, the deep aquifer she had spent her life standing on. She felt every spell she'd ever cast still living somewhere in the world: the fever broken in a fisherman's daughter, the luck she'd spun into a beggar's pocket, the gate-tremors she had quieted night after night alone. All of it answered her now, came home to her hands.

*Take it,* she told the dark. *All of it. And then be still.*

She poured.

It did not hurt, exactly. It was more like being unwritten—one thread pulled gently from the weave of her, then another, then all of them together, until the light coming off her skin was so bright that Sera cried out and covered her eyes and Dain stepped forward with his sword drawn at nothing, at the dark itself, as if steel could matter. The seal drank. The hunger below went quiet—not sated, never sated, but *bound*, truly bound, the way a river is bound by its banks, the way grief is bound by time. The scorched keeper-marks flared white and then settled into a soft, steady glow, the glow of something finished rather than something struggling.

And Hazel Reyes knelt in the silence afterward and reached for her gift, the way you reach for a tooth that is gone.

Nothing.

No warmth in her palms. No hum beneath the floor. Just stone, and cold air, and the ordinary weight of her own body—her heartbeat, her breath, the ache in her knees from kneeling. She searched herself the way you search a house after a theft, and found it bare, and found, beneath the bareness, something she had not expected.

Relief.

She started to laugh, and then to cry, and Sera was suddenly there, hauling her up off the stone, holding her hard enough to bruise.

"You absolute fool," Sera said into her hair. "You magnificent, infuriating fool. You're alive. Do you understand? Every keeper in those records is dust, and you're alive, and you're crying on me, which is very bad for my reputation."

"Your reputation survived Brask burning," Hazel managed. "It'll survive this."

* * *

They came out of the mountain into morning.

Vell had changed while they were underground. With the usurper dead and his harvest ended, the edict had collapsed like a puppet with cut strings—and the city had discovered, all at once, how many of its neighbors had been hiding gifts behind harmless charms. The witch-hunters' pyres stood cold in the squares. In the night market where Hazel had sold her luck-knots, a hedge-witch was openly brewing fever-tea, and no crier dared speak against her, because half the crowd could have answered with fire of their own.

Magic came back the way spring comes—messily, unevenly, everywhere at once. There were accidents. There were wonders. There were arguments in the streets about whether a storm-touched child should be taught or feared, and for the first time in memory, the argument was held aloud, in daylight, by people who were not afraid of being dragged away screaming.

The free cities sent envoys to Vell within the month. Dain Marrow stood before them in plain clothes, no badge, no captain's knot, and gave testimony that would have hanged him a season earlier: the fabricated evidence, the harvest-edicts, the hollow man on the throne. They made him offer him a title afterward. He declined it and took work training a city watch instead, teaching young people that justice and law were two different things that needed each other to survive. He visited the gate chamber sometimes, Hazel heard. He never spoke about it.

Hazel reopened her shop.

That was the part no one understood—not Sera, not the neighbors, not the hedge-witch who offered to teach her what she'd lost. She could have been a heroine. The free cities wanted heroines; they wanted the woman who fed the false king to the dark and sealed the gate forever, they wanted to put her face on coins and her name in songs. Instead she swept out the same little stall in Vell's night market, hung the same faded awning, and set out trays of luck-knots that were, at last, honestly just knots—red thread and a prayer-shaped pattern and nothing else.

People bought them anyway. More than ever. Perhaps because everyone in Vell now knew exactly what a charm was worth, and chose to buy hope regardless.

Some days she missed it. She would not pretend otherwise. On grey mornings she would reach for the warmth out of habit and find only her own hands, and the loss sat in her chest like a pebble in a shoe—not crippling, never crippling, just present. Other days she watched a storm roll in over the mountains and thought of Wren, who had turned weather like other children turned cartwheels, and who had bought them their future with a sky full of lightning, and Hazel thought: *this is why*. This is why the gate needed everything. So that no one else would have to stand a vigil alone until it ate them. So that magic could belong to everyone again—or no one—or anyone—instead of being a slow death sentence passed from keeper to keeper in secret.

She had traded her gift for the world's freedom. Most mornings, waking ordinary in a world that finally needed magic, she thought she'd gotten the better end of the bargain.

* * *

Sera found her at closing time on the first day of spring, three months after the gate, leaning in the shop doorway watching the market lights come on one by one.

"You're doing it again," Sera said.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting there looking like a woman deciding something." Sera dropped onto the step beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and produced from some thief's pocket a flask of plum brandy that Hazel was fairly certain belonged to neither of them. "Out with it. I've watched you tie four knots tonight and untie two. That's a decision in knot-language if I've ever seen one."

Hazel took the flask, drank, and looked out at the market—at the hedge-witch's stall, at children running between the tables chasing a conjured ribbon of light that some street performer had made for a copper, at a city learning to stop flinching.

"The gatekeepers' records," she said. "There were other gates. My mentor wrote about them. Seals in the far north, in the Sunken Isles, older even than ours—bindings that were never tended, never poured full. Starved things, sleeping badly." She turned the flask in her hands. "I can't feel them anymore. Can't sense a single one. But someone should go and look. Someone who knows what a failing seal looks like from the inside."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then she took the flask back and drank deeply, and when she lowered it she was smiling—that sharp, crooked smile Hazel had first seen across a market stall three years ago, attached to a hand lifting a purse.

"A journey," Sera said. "To distant lands. Full of dangerous ancient seals and probably smugglers and definitely terrible inns. And you're asking me—" she pressed a hand to her chest in mock wound, "—the reformed thief, the respectable fence, the pillar of Brask's rebuilding—"

"I'm asking the person who walked into hell with me twice."

"Well. When you say it like that." Sera stood, brushed off her coat, and offered her hand down to Hazel with a flourish. "Fair warning, Gatekeeper: I steal things. Habitually. It's practically a gift of its own."

Hazel took her hand and let herself be pulled to her feet—ordinary hands, ordinary strength, holding on anyway.

"Good," she said. "Between us, we might manage one honest miracle."

Above them the stars were coming out over Vell, and beneath the earth the old hunger slept behind a wall woven from everything Hazel had ever been, and in the morning they would buy horses, and neither of them slept that night for talking about the road north.
