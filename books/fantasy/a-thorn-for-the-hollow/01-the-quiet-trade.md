## 1. The Quiet Trade

The dead don't knock. That's the first thing you learn in my trade, and the last thing anyone believes until they've sat across from me long enough to feel a cold spot settle into their chair like it's paying rent.

They arrive however they like—a draft through a shuttered window, a name half-spoken by someone who isn't there, a smell of river water where there is no river—and I've learned to let them in the way you'd let in an old dog with muddy feet. You don't ask questions. You just get out the towels.

The morning Maren Colm brought me her daughter's bone, the dead were already crowded into my workshop like commuters waiting for a delayed coach. They do that when something's wrong. Most days they drift through Lowmark the way dust drifts—present, patient, unbothered—but that morning they had gathered at the edges of my hearing and pressed inward, all of them murmuring at once, low and urgent, like villagers whispering behind their hands about some scandal too large for gossip.

I didn't know yet what the scandal was. I only knew that when I opened my shutters, the light came in gray and reluctant, and the mountain at the end of the valley seemed closer than it had any right to be.

* * *

Maren Colm arrived before the frost had melted off my step, carrying her daughter wrapped in a wool blanket as though the cold could still reach her, which was kind of her, and futile, which was also kind of her. The girl had been pulled from the Merle three days ago. Eleven years old. Sela. A name like a bell rung once and left ringing.

"You're the carver," Maren said. Not a question. In Lowmark, everyone knows what I am, which is not the same as everyone knowing me. I have lived here my whole life—thirty-one years, most of them in this workshop with its smell of bone dust and linseed oil—and I could walk down the market row on festival day and count on one hand the people who'd greet me by name. They know what I do. They bring me their dead the way they bring a lame horse to the farrier: necessary, uncomfortable, best done quickly and without lingering.

"I'm the carver," I agreed.

"She drowned." Maren set the bundle on my workbench with the care of someone setting down something explosive. "They say the river took her quick. I want—I need—" Her hands made a shape in the air, some gesture that meant *peace*, or meant *proof*, or meant *I cannot bury her until I know she isn't still in there somewhere.*

"A memorial bone," I said. "Carved proper. So she can find her way."

"So she can rest."

"That too." I unwrapped the blanket. The bone beneath was small—the radius of a child's arm, pale as milk, still holding the river's chill. Bone keeps cold a long time. Bone remembers temperature the way it remembers everything else, if you know how to ask.

"Will it hurt her?" Maren asked, and I wanted to tell her that nothing I do hurts anyone, that carving is the gentlest work in the world, that a memorial bone is just a door left open with a lamp lit beside it. But grief doesn't want comfort phrased honestly; it wants comfort phrased kindly, so I said:

"It won't hurt her. It might tickle."

She looked at me like I was mad. Most of them do. I find it helps.

* * *

Here is how the trade works, since no one ever asks and everyone assumes.

Every soul leaves something behind in the body—a residue, an echo, a sediment of having been alive in that particular place. Most of it fades within days. But bone holds it longer, especially dense bone, especially if the dying was sudden. A carver who knows what he's doing can read that sediment the way a banker reads coin-weight: by feel, by grain, by the faint resistance the material offers under the blade.

And if the carver is like me—if he was born with his ears tuned wrong, tuned *inward*—he doesn't just read it. He hears it.

Not words, usually. Not at first. More like weather. A mood moving through the grain. Sudden deaths leave turbulence. Long illnesses leave smoothness, a worn-polish quality, souls that had time to make peace with leaving. Drownings are the worst and the best: worst because water scrambles everything, scatters the echo into ripples; best because drowning souls cling, they hold on to the last thing that held them, and if you can find the center of the ripple you can find them, calm as a stone at the bottom of a clear pool.

Sela Colm was at the bottom of herself, calm as anything. While Maren wept in the front room—Lowmark custom forbids the bereaved from watching the carving, though I suspect most couldn't bear to anyway—I laid the little radius on my bench, took up my finest blade, and began.

The first cuts are always orientation. Where was she strongest? What did she love? Bone grain follows use, and soul-grain follows love. I found her in the second pass: horses. She'd loved the colts on her father's hillside, had spent whole summers among them, and her bone remembered it as warmth along one edge, a galloping rhythm buried in the structure like fossil waves in cliffstone.

I carved her a horse. Of course I carved her a horse. Every carver has his own grammar, and mine runs toward the specific: not *a* horse but *that* horse, the dun mare with the white sock, the one whose name I lifted straight out of the bone's warm places. Bramble. The mare was called Bramble, and Sela had told her things she'd told no living person, and when I cut the mare's legs mid-stride, free and flying, I felt something in the bone loosen—a sigh, almost, the sound a knot makes when it finally slips.

That's the trade. That's the whole quiet trade. You find what they loved, you carve it true, and the soul has a road map home. To wherever home is. Nobody knows where the dead go. Even the dead aren't sure, most of them. But they go easier when they carry something familiar, and a memorial bone is a familiar thing made permanent: proof that they were loved specifically, by name, in detail.

It was going beautifully. It should have been a good day's work, two hours of clean cutting, and then Maren would take her daughter home lighter than she came, and I would sweep up the shavings and burn them at dusk the way the old customs require, and I would eat supper alone and sleep alone and wake alone, invisible again, the man Lowmark uses and does not see.

Except.

Except that halfway through the mare's mane, the dead outside my walls stopped murmuring and went silent—all of them, at once, the way birds go silent before a quake—and then every soul in Lowmark, every unburied echo drifting my streets, turned its attention toward the mountain and *listened*.

I put down my knife. My hands were steady. My hands are always steady; it's one of the few things I like about myself.

Something was moving beneath the Thornfells. Deep. Deeper than the smugglers' roads, deeper than the old mines, deeper than anything the living had dug or named. I couldn't hear it directly—it wasn't a soul, not exactly, more like a pressure that souls respond to, the way dogs hear a whistle that's only a feeling to us—but I could hear what it did to the dead. They were afraid. I had never heard the dead be afraid. They have nothing left to lose; fear is a living person's currency, spent at gravesides, hoarded in sickrooms. But that morning the dead of Lowmark huddled against my workshop walls like sheep before a storm, and beneath their huddling ran a sound I can only describe as a vast slow shifting, weight against stone, stone against weight, something turning over in its sleep down where the roots of the world go dark.

And then, threading through all of it, thin as wire:

My name.

Not shouted. Not spoken. *Remembered.* The way you might remember a word you hadn't used in twenty years—*oh yes, Jude Okafor, the carver, the boy with the wrong ears*—except the remembering was happening miles underground, in the dark, in a place no one had ever said my name aloud.

The dead pressed close. Dozens of them. Old Marta who froze in the winter of my childhood. The ferryman Hollis, drowned same as Sela, forty years earlier. Children whose names the town has forgotten, whose bones went into the paupers' field uncarved, unmourned, unguided—they clustered thickest, the unburied always do, and their small cold voices overlapped into something almost like speech:

*Under. Under. It knows your line. It knows your blood. Under, carver, under—*

Then it passed. Whatever moved below settled, or turned, or simply sank back past the depth where even I could hear it, and the dead of Lowmark dispersed like a crowd after a hanging, reluctantly, looking over their shoulders.

I stood at my bench a long while with a half-carved horse under my hands and a child's bone warming slowly toward room temperature, and I thought: *the wards*. Because that's the other thing everyone in Lowmark knows without saying—the border wards on the high passes, the ones the crown maintains, the ones that have kept the Thornfell borders sealed and starlit for a hundred years. Wards need feeding. Everyone knows that too. What nobody asks is what they eat.

The dead knew. The dead were telling me, in their scattered frightened way, that whatever fed those wards was stirring, hungry, waking—and that it had my family's name stitched somewhere in the lining of its hunger.

* * *

Maren Colm collected her daughter at dusk. I handed her the finished bone wrapped in linen, the dun mare frozen mid-leap along its length, Bramble's name hidden in the mane where only the dead would read it.

"She'll rest now?" Maren asked.

"She'll fly first," I said. "Then she'll rest."

Maren pressed coins into my hand—too many, the way the grieving do, buying absolution they don't owe—and paused at my door. For a moment I thought she was going to say something human, something that would crack the transactional shell we keep around each other in this town. She looked at me, this man who had spoken with her daughter's soul, who had given the girl back her horse.

"You'll burn the shavings proper?" was what she actually said.

"Proper," I promised.

She went down the darkening street, and I watched her go, and the street swallowed her, and I was alone with my shavings and my fire and the enormous listening silence of the mountain.

I burned the shavings at dusk. The sparks went up and up and did not come down—or rather, they came down somewhere far above, beyond the clouds, and I found myself staring at the sky thinking about stars, about how they're supposed to be fixed things, eternal things, the crown's own emblem of order, *as constant as the ward-stars over Thornfell*, the priests say every festival.

That night, for the first time in my life, I noticed the stars flickering.

Just at the edges. Just when I wasn't looking straight at them. Like candle flames guttering in a draft from a door I couldn't find.

Below my floorboards, beneath my cellar, beneath the bedrock, something that remembered my name rolled over in its sleep, and the dead of Lowmark whispered in the dark:

*Soon, carver. Soon.*

I did not sleep well. And in the morning, I decided, I would take the smuggling road up past the border wards myself, and see with my own eyes what my ears already feared.

Some trades you don't choose. Some trades choose you, and then, one gray morning, they send you a bill.
