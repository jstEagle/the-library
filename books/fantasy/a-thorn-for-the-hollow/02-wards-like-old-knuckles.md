## 2. Wards Like Old Knuckles

There are three rules to running the Thornfell passes in autumn, and I learned all of them the way you learn most things worth knowing: badly, once, at cost.

One: never run a route twice on the same moon. Two: trust the cairns more than the maps, because maps lie and stones only forget slowly. Three: if the wards hum, keep walking and don't listen too closely.

That last one had never been a problem before, because for as long as I'd been running routes through the borderlands, the wards had hummed the way old men snore—constant, dull, ignorable. You could set your watch by them, if Lowmark had watches worth setting. The wards ran along the ridge above Hollow Bridge like knuckles along a clenched fist, seven great standing stones sunk into the mountain's shoulder, each one wrapped in copper wire older than my grandmother's grandmother and singing the same low note they'd sung since the crown first decided the Thornfells needed holding shut.

I knew that note. Every smuggler did. It was the sound of *not yet*, of *turn back*, of *the crown is watching even when it isn't*. We timed our crossings by its gaps the way farmers timed planting by frost. The wards breathed; we slipped between breaths.

Except on the third morning of Frostwane, the wards didn't breathe.

They stuttered.

* * *

I'd come up the Goat's Ladder before dawn with a pack full of things I wasn't going to describe to you—warden seals, mostly, the good forged kind, and a bolt of southern silk that would sell in Lowmark for enough to keep me in bone and bread until midwinter. Standard run. Boring run. The kind of run where the danger is familiar enough to be almost company.

The cold came down off the peaks early that year. My breath hung around me like a small ghost that had decided to follow me instead of moving on, and I remember thinking—not thinking, exactly, but feeling in the place below thinking—that the dead were quiet that morning. Unusually quiet. Normally when I climbed toward the ridge I got the usual murmurs from the wayside dead: travelers who'd misjudged the weather fifty years ago, a smuggler or two whose luck had run out against mine hadn't yet, the occasional shepherd still fussing about his sheep. They didn't talk to me so much as mutter near me, the way strangers mutter in a crowded tavern. I'd built my whole life on the shape of that murmur. It was terrible company and it was the only company there was, and I had made my peace with that arithmetic a long time ago.

But that morning: silence. A silence with texture to it, the way a held breath has texture.

And then I crested the shoulder below the first ward-stone and heard the wards skip.

You've heard a millstone catch, maybe—a grinding note that hitches, stumbles, finds itself again. It was like that, except the thing stumbling was the sound of the border itself. The nearest stone stood forty feet up the slope, a black finger against the grey sky, and the copper around it flared—dimmed—flared again, like a lamp running out of oil being shaken by a desperate hand.

I stopped. Which, rule-wise, was stupid. Rule one of anything strange on a run is *keep moving*, because whatever it is, it isn't yours, and curiosity has buried more smugglers than wardens ever did.

But I'm a carver. Bone, mostly, sometimes antler, once—disastrously—slate. And when you spend your life reading the grain of things, learning where the strength runs and where it will split, you don't get to just walk past something that's showing you its grain. The wards were showing me their grain. And their grain was wrong.

I dropped my pack and worked closer, low against the scree, the way you move when you're not sure whether what you're approaching can see you. Up close the stone was worse than the sound. There were cracks in it—fine ones, hairline, spidering out from the base like frost-ferns—and from the cracks seeped something I didn't have a word for. Not light. Not smoke. A shimmer, maybe, the way air shimmers over a forge, except this shimmered *cold* and it moved uphill, toward the stone, feeding into it.

Feeding it. That was the part that sat wrong in my chest. Wards weren't fed, as far as anyone in Lowmark knew. Wards simply *were*, the way mountains were, put there by crown-wrights a century back and left to grind on forever. But this—whatever crawled out of those hairline cracks and up into the copper—it had the quality of sustenance. Of something being consumed. And beneath it, fainter than the dead's usual murmur, fainter than memory, I caught something else. A voice, or the ghost of one, saying words I couldn't parse in a language I half-knew the way you half-know a lullaby your mother sang before you could talk.

It said something that might have been *hollow*.

Then the flare guttered, and every hair on my arms stood up, because for one full heartbeat the ward went dark. No hum. No copper-glow. Nothing. Just a dead stone on a cold hillside and a hole in the world where a border used to be.

Through that hole, for that one heartbeat, I heard the mountain breathe in.

* * *

I want to be clear that I am not a brave man. I want to be very clear about that, because everything that happened next gets told afterward as though it required courage, and it didn't. It required bad judgment, which I have in abundance.

Instead of packing up and taking the long way around through Miller's Cut like any smuggler with functioning instincts, I climbed toward the second ward-stone. Because here's the thing about hearing the dead your whole life: you develop a professional interest in silences. And that silence under the mountain wasn't empty. Empty silence is just absence. This was *attentive* silence—the silence of something listening back.

The second stone was worse. Its cracks were wider, its shimmer thicker, and when I pressed my palm flat against the rock—cold enough to burn, cold enough to make my teeth ache—I felt the stutter directly, felt it in the bones of my hand the way I feel the grain of ivory. The ward wasn't failing the way a wall fails, crumbling from age. It was failing the way a body fails when it isn't being fed. There was a rhythm underneath the flicker, slow and enormous and labored, and I realized with a lurch that I recognized it, because it was a heartbeat. Something under the mountain had a heartbeat, and the wards were strapped to it like leeches to a vein, and the vein was running dry.

"Jude Okafor," said a voice behind me, "you are either the bravest man in the Thornfells or the stupidest, and I've arrested enough of both to know they look identical from behind."

* * *

I did not scream. I want that noted for posterity. What I did was make a noise like a kettle dying and go for my knife, which is what passes for dignity in the smuggling trade.

She was standing six feet away with her arms crossed and her breath steaming, and she had been there long enough to watch me fondle a ward-stone like a fortune-teller with a cracked crystal ball. Warden grey, warden boots, warden braid—copper-threaded, which meant rank, which meant trouble. Young for the braid. Mid-twenties, maybe, with a face built for disapproval and currently doing overtime. Her sword was sheathed, which I noted the way you note a trapdoor in the floor: it doesn't mean there's no drop, just that they're confident about the landing.

"Wren Ashvale," she supplied, when I didn't say anything. "Warden-Sergeant. You're standing on crown property touching a covenant ward with your bare hands, which is three separate crimes, and I'd cite the statutes but I suspect you carve them into pipes and sell them."

"That was one time," I said, "and it was a very good pipe."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been indigestion. Wardens aren't known for either.

"I know you," she said. "Lowmark. The bone-carver." She said *bone-carver* the way people say *rat-catcher*: a job that's necessary, faintly disgusting, and best not discussed at meals. "You run the Goat's Ladder route. Third of every month, give or take."

"You know my schedule." I kept my hand loose near the knife, mostly for form's sake. She'd have taken it off me before I finished reaching. "Should I be flattered or frightened?"

"Neither. Frightened men run, and then I have to chase them, and chasing people up the Ladder in Frostwane cold is beneath my pay grade." She uncrossed her arms and nodded at the stone. "How long has it been doing that?"

The question landed sideways, the way questions do when you've already answered them privately and someone asks aloud. "Doing what?"

"Don't." Flat, tired. "Don't do the smuggler shrug. I watched you touch it. I watched your face while you touched it. Whatever you felt, I've spent four months watching these wards do—" she gestured at the copper, which chose that exact moment to flare and gutter, obligingly, like a stage actor waiting for its cue, "—that. So. How long?"

Four months. She'd been watching for four months. Which meant command knew, which meant the flickering wasn't news, which meant everything I'd just felt against my palm was already filed and stamped somewhere in a warden garrison and no one had told the town sleeping in the mountain's shadow.

"A month," I lied. "Maybe two. It's worse at night."

"It's worse at new moon," she said, correcting me without heat, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't interrogating me at all. She was comparing notes. With a smuggler. On a mountainside, alone, off whatever patrol schedule she'd abandoned to stand here talking to a criminal. "You hear them, don't you. The wards."

"Not hear, exactly—"

"The dead," she said. "You hear the dead. Half of Lowmark says you're a fraud and the other half leaves offerings at your workshop door and won't admit it. My commander says you're a smuggler with theatrical instincts." A pause. "My commander also says these wards are fine."

"And you don't think they're fine."

She looked at the stone for a long moment. The shimmer crawled up through the cracks, cold and hungry, and the great black finger of it sang its broken note, and for a second—just a second—her composure slipped, and what was underneath looked a lot like fear wearing a warden's uniform.

"No," she said quietly. "I think they're starving. And I think everyone above me has decided not to know that, and I think a man who talks to the dead might know something about what feeds on the other side of a ward." She turned back to me, and the composure came down again like a visor. "So I'm going to do something inadvisable."

"Those seem to be going around."

"You're going to pick up your pack, and you're going to take the western fork past the third cairn, and I am going to stand here for a count of five hundred looking in the other direction. And when I file my report, it will say I patrolled the ridge and saw nothing but weather." Her jaw tightened. "In exchange, you're going to tell me one true thing."

"One true thing," I repeated. "About the dead?"

"About whatever's making the wards stutter. One true thing. That's the price."

Here's what I should have said: *I don't know anything, warden, I touched a rock and got cold.* Here's what I actually said, because apparently my mouth had decided to conduct its own foreign policy:

"They're not stuttering because they're breaking. They're stuttering because whatever feeds them is running out. It's not a machine, Sergeant Ashvale. It's alive. And it's starving."

The wind moved along the ridge. Somewhere below us, faint as a coin dropped down a well, the mountain breathed out.

She stared at me for a long time, and I braced for the arrest, the manacles, the long walk down to the garrison and the cell I'd always known waited at the end of my particular road. Instead she reached into her coat, pulled out something small, and tossed it to me. I caught it on reflex. A warden's whistle. Bone—actual bone, old bone, carved with the warden's knot—and it hummed faintly against my palm in a way that made my teeth itch.

"If the wards go out," she said, "blow that. Not to call wardens. To call *me*. I'll hear it anywhere inside the border." She was already turning away, already done with the conversation in the way soldiers end conversations, by leaving. "Count of five hundred starts now, Okafor. Don't be on my ridge when it ends."

"Why?" I asked her back. "Why let me go? Why warn me? What do you get?"

She stopped. Didn't turn around. When she answered, the wind nearly took it.

"Because everyone I work for thinks the wards are a wall," she said, "and I'm starting to think they're a wound. And wounds need someone who can hear what's underneath them."

Then she walked up the slope toward the third stone, shoulders straight, warden-grey fading into warden-grey, and I picked up my pack and took the western fork like an obedient criminal, counting under my breath, wondering the entire way down why my hands were shaking.

Not fear. Or not only fear. It was the other thing, the rarer thing, the thing I hadn't felt since I was a boy pressing my ear to my grandmother's memorial bone: the specific vertigo of being *heard*. Of someone asking me a question because they thought the answer mattered.

I blew no whistle. I sold the silk. I carved two memorial bones that week and slept badly, and every night I lay listening to the dead mutter their tavern-murmurs around my bed, and beneath them, deeper, slower, that vast labored heartbeat, counting down toward something.

On the seventh night, the stars began to dim.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. Just—one or two, at the sky's edge, gone out like candles pinched by a careful hand.

The dead stopped murmuring.

And every soul in Lowmark, living and otherwise, turned its face toward the mountain, and listened.
