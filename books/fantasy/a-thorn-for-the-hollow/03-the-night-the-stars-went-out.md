## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

I was carving a lamb when the sky went out.

Not a lamb. A ewe, technically — a memorial ewe for Widow Marsh, whose animal had died in the cold snap and whom she'd loved with more devotion than she'd ever shown her late husband. The dead of Lowmark were mostly animals this time of year, and I don't mean that as a complaint. A sheep's grief is simple. It wants its flock, it wants the warm side of the hill, and once you've given it those in bone it settles like a child sung to sleep. People are harder. People want apologies.

So there I was at my bench, past midnight, knife whispering through a knuckle of sheep femur, listening to the ewe's ghost hum contentedly somewhere near my left ear, when every dead thing in Thornfell screamed at once.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. It wasn't sound. Sound comes through ears, gets sorted by the brain, arrives politely labeled as *thunder* or *Widow Marsh's cat* or *your own pulse*. This came through everything — teeth, marrow, the water in the cup on my bench, the floorboards, the bones of the house itself. Every soul I had ever carved free of its flesh, every ghost I had ever quieted, every whisper I had spent thirty years learning to hear like other men learn to read — all of them opened their mouths at once.

The knife snapped in my hand.

I sat very still in the ringing dark and did not scream, which I count among my life's modest achievements. Outside, the ewe's ghost was gone. Everything was gone. For the first time since I was six years old, the world around me was silent in the way ordinary people find silence.

It was unbearable.

Then the second wave hit, and I understood that the first had merely been the inhale.

* * *

What the dead said had no words, exactly. But I have spent a lifetime translating the shapeless into the specific — that's the whole trade, bone-carving; you take a lump of death and you find the person inside it — so I will do now what I did then, which is translate.

They said: *the door is open.*

They said: *we are falling.*

And one voice — thin, old, familiar in a way that made my hands shake worse than the screaming had — said my name.

*Jude.*

Just that. My name, spoken the way my grandmother used to say it, back when she was alive and I was small enough to sit on her knee while she worked her carvings, back before I knew she'd been dead twenty years longer than anyone in Lowmark would admit.

The mountain spoke my name, and then the stars went out.

* * *

I made it to the window in time to see it happen, or rather to see it not happen. You cannot see a star vanish — you only see where it was and understand that nothing is there anymore. The sky over Thornfell had been clear and hard that night, winter-sharp, pricked with more stars than Lowmark ever bothered to name. I remember thinking, in the half-second before, that the sky looked like a bone before you carve it. Full of something waiting.

Then it went dark. Not cloudy-dark, not moonless-dark. Dark the way the inside of a closed mouth is dark. The whole bowl of heaven, wiped clean in a single heartbeat, and I felt that heartbeat in my own chest because apparently my heart and the sky had been keeping time together all these years without telling me.

Below my workshop, Lowmark woke up screaming.

Dogs first. Dogs always know before people do. Then the bell in the temple square, rung by somebody who presumably hadn't been told it was three in the morning, and then doors banging open down Crooked Lane, and voices — dozens, then hundreds, all asking the same question in different words: *what happened to the sky?*

I stood at my window with blood on my hand from the broken knife and watched my town come apart in the dark, and I thought, with the strange calm that has followed me my whole life: *they're going to blame someone for this. They always blame someone. And I am the man who talks to ghosts.*

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew, standing there, because I've had a long road since to wish I'd known more.

I knew the border wards had been flickering. Anyone who ran the smugglers' roads knew it — the wards along Thornfell's flank used to hum like a struck bell if you got too close, and lately they'd been stuttering, guttering like a lamp low on oil. I'd assumed rot. Wards are old work, older than the crown that claims them, and everything old rots eventually. I'd even felt a little smug about it, in the way of a man who notices things others don't. Smugness is a luxury I have since paid for many times over.

I knew the flickering wasn't rot. It was hunger. Something beneath the mountain had been eating, and the wards had been the meal, and tonight the plate was licked clean.

And I knew — in the place where the dead live in me, the quiet room behind my sternum that has never once been silent until tonight — that whatever had eaten them knew my name.

That was the part I didn't say out loud, then or for a long while after. The town could panic about the sky. Let them. The sky belonged to everyone. But a mountain speaking *Jude Okafor* in my grandmother's voice, through the mouths of ten thousand dead — that was mine alone, and I held it against my chest like a coal.

* * *

By dawn the wardens had sealed the passes.

I know because they hammered proclamation boards to every post in town, and because Tam Arless came pounding on my door an hour after sunrise — well, after what we were calling sunrise, the sun having risen into a sky like dirty slate, starless, as if the night had simply refused to leave.

"Have you seen it?" he hissed, pushing past me into the workshop without invitation, the way frightened men do. "Have you heard anything?"

Tam knows what I am. Half of Lowmark knows and doesn't say, which in Lowmark counts as not knowing. He's brought me bones to carve — his father, his wife, a daughter who never drew breath — and he pays honest coin and asks no questions about how I always seem to know just what the dead wanted written on them.

"Heard anything," I repeated. "Like what?"

"Like—" He glanced over his shoulder at the gray window, and lowered his voice as though the dark might lean in to listen. "Like the dead talking. That's your trade, isn't it. So talk. What happened out there?"

Here is what I should have said: *I don't know.* Here is what I wanted to say: *a door opened under the world and something called my name.* Here is what I actually said, because I am a coward in small ways if never in large ones:

"The wards fell."

"I know the wards fell, everyone knows—"

"They didn't fail, Tam." I picked up the broken knife, turned it in my fingers, set it down again. "They were *drunk*. Emptied. There's a difference between a wall that crumbles and a larder that's been raided, and the dead know the difference even if the wardens don't."

He stared at me. "Drunk by what?"

"That," I said, "is an excellent question, and I'd thank you not to ask it loudly enough for the wrong people to wonder why you're asking me."

But it was already too late for quiet, and we both knew it. Through the walls I could hear Lowmark rearranging itself around catastrophe — carts in the lane, children crying, the temple bell still going though nobody had rung it properly in years. And underneath all of it, fainter than the rest but growing, another sound: boots. Many boots, moving in formation. The wardens coming down from the pass garrisons into town.

"Proclamation," Tam muttered. "Captain's already posted it. No one leaves Lowmark. No one travels the mountain roads. Anyone found on them is presumed complicit in—" He faltered. "In whatever this was. Sabotage, they're saying. The Hollow Court, some of them. Old stories."

"Wardens sealing passes," I said slowly, "to keep people from fleeing. Or to keep people from reaching the mountain."

"Does it matter?"

"It might," I said, "to someone planning to travel the mountain roads."

He gave me a look I pretended not to understand, wished me well in the flat voice Lowmark uses for such wishes, and left. I stood in my workshop surrounded by listening bones — my grandmother's memorial among them, three generations old, hanging above the bench where it had hung my whole life — and I noticed, for the first time, that it was warm.

I'll come back to that. I have to come back to it, because everything after flows from it. But first there was the fire.

* * *

The wardens burned the old shrine at the crossroads that evening.

I watched from the crowd, hood up, face down, one anonymous shoulder among hundreds. It was a nothing shrine — a cairn really, a stack of river stones and a carved marker older than any living memory, the kind travelers touch for luck. Wardens said the words over it, the new sharp-edged words I hadn't heard since childhood, and Captain Harrow herself struck the flint, and the dry bones tucked inside it — offerings, generations of them, little carved tokens left by folk who'd lost someone and couldn't afford my prices — went up like autumn leaves.

Nobody in the crowd cheered. Nobody protested either. We stood in the light of a bonfire under a starless sky and let our own history burn, because fear makes people agreeable, and the captain knew it, and counted on it.

"—agents of the Hollow Court," Harrow was saying, her voice pitched to carry. She was tall the way a blade is tall, and her warden's brand showed silver at her throat. "The wards did not fall. The wards were *betrayed*. Someone fed them to the enemy. The crown will find them. The crown will make an example." She paused, and swept her eyes across the crowd, and I felt the look go through it like a fisherman's net through shallow water. "There are those in this town who traffic with the dead. Who claim to *hear* them."

My heart did something unhelpful.

"These are the doors the enemy uses," Harrow said. "Doors will be closed."

Beside me, an old woman crossed herself with two fingers, the old way, toward the burned shrine rather than the temple. "Fool woman," she whispered. "You don't close doors on the dead. You just stop being invited when they knock."

I could have kissed her. I kept my head down instead, and slipped away before the crowd broke, and walked home through streets gone strange with torchlight, and the whole way I could feel the silence behind my sternum like a missing tooth. Thirty years of company, gone. The dead of Thornfell had screamed and fallen silent, and I was alone in my own skull for the first time since boyhood, and I discovered that I did not want the silence. I wanted the whispering back. I wanted the dead of Thornfell alive in me again, murmuring their small grievances and longings, because they had been the only family I had, most of my life, and I missed them the way you miss a house that burned.

That was the night I learned the difference between solitude and loneliness, and it is: solitude is a craft, and loneliness is what's left when the craft burns down.

* * *

Back in the workshop, I lit no lamps. I didn't need them; carving is done by feel more than sight, always has been. I went to the shelf above the bench and took down my grandmother's memorial bone.

Every family in Lowmark keeps memorial bones, though most couldn't tell you why anymore. Tradition, they call it. Superstition, the temple calls it. But the Okafor line has always known what they are, because we made the tradition: a soul carved true into bone does not wholly leave. It rests. It waits. It listens, faintly, forever, the way a bell that has stopped ringing still remembers music.

My grandmother's bone was a wrist-bone, worn smooth by four generations of thumbs. My mother had held it. Her mother had held it. Tonight it was warm — not sun-warm, not hand-warm. Warm from inside, like a bird.

"Grandmother," I said aloud, feeling ridiculous, feeling desperate, feeling both at once, which I suspect is the natural state of anyone who talks to objects. "If you can hear me—"

The warmth pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat remembering how.

And in the terrible silence behind my sternum, in the empty room where the dead had lived in me all my life, something moved. Not a voice. Not yet. More like a hand pressed flat against the far side of a wall — there, patient, unmistakably *there*, waiting for me to notice.

The mountain had spoken my name. Now my grandmother's bone answered, warm in my palm, and I understood — with the clarity that only comes at the worst moments of a life — that these were the same event. The mountain calling *Jude*, and the bone going warm, and the wards falling hungry, and the stars snuffed out like candles pinched between wet fingers. One thread. And the thread ran through my family, through this bone, through me.

Through me.

I sat down on my stool, because my legs had opinions about standing. Outside, the wardens' patrols were making their rounds, boots ringing on frozen mud, and somewhere beyond the sealed passes the Hollow Court lay under the mountain with its door newly open, starving, and it knew my name, and I was going to have to go down there. I knew it the way you know a bone's true shape before the first cut — not with the eyes but with the hands, with something older than knowing.

"They'll hang me for leaving town," I told the warm bone.

The warmth pulsed. It felt, if I'm honest, amused.

"Yes," I agreed grimly, "that's what I thought you'd say."

I began, right then, packing for a journey no proclamation permitted: knife and whetstone, cord, dried fish, the good wool. And above the bench my grandmother's bone glowed faintly in the dark like the last star in a fallen sky, pointing down the mountain, toward the Court, toward whatever had eaten the wards and learned my name —

— and that was when I heard, quite clearly, from the direction of the market square, the sound of warden boots stopping outside my door.
