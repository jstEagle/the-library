## 4. What the Dead Remember

The wards died at moonrise, and by the time I'd laced my boots the whole garrison was awake and lying to itself.

"They'll come back," said Sergeant Hale, standing in the courtyard with his arms crossed like a man guarding a door that had already been kicked in. "Wards flicker. Weather turns. Give it an hour."

I gave it an hour. Then I gave it two more, because I was young enough in my post to believe that patience was a virtue rather than a way of hiding. The sky over Thornfell stayed black—not clouded, not overcast, but *empty*, the way a socket is empty. I kept catching myself looking up and then looking away too fast, like I'd walked in on something grieving.

By the third hour, Commander Vess had stopped pretending and started organizing. That was the thing about Vess: she was never wrong so much as she was early, and being early made her cruel.

"Ashvale," she said, when I reported to her office. She didn't look up from the map she was annotating. Her pen moved with the calm of someone who had already decided how the world would end. "You know the Thornfell section. You'll walk it."

"Walk it? It's forty miles of—"

"Of ward-line," she finished. "Which is currently a suggestion rather than a fact. I want eyes on every anchor-stone between here and Hollow Bridge before the smugglers realize the border is a rumor." She finally looked up. Her face was the color of good parchment and about as warm. "And Ashvale. If you find anything... irregular, you report it to me. Not to the chaplain, not to the records office. To me."

"Define *irregular*."

Her pen paused for half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat is a long time for Vess.

"You'll know it when you see it," she said.

I should have pressed. That's the sentence I return to on bad nights—I should have pressed, should have asked what she knew, should have noticed that the commander of a border garrison looked like a woman reading her own death warrant and taking notes. But I was thirty-one years old and my feet were cold and the sky was wrong, and when your superior offers you a job that means walking away from her voice, you take it.

"Yes, Commander," I said.

"Good. Take rations for five days." She went back to her map. "And Ashvale—walk fast."

* * *

The ward-road out of Lowmark was built by people who expected it to be beautiful forever. Stone flags laid by crown masons a century back, cairns at every mile, iron posts where the wards anchored to the earth. For as long as anyone living could remember, those posts had hummed. Not loudly—you felt it in your teeth more than your ears, a low constant note like a held breath. Children in the villages along the line were told the mountain was singing. Wardens were told it was working. Same sound, different hymn.

Walking the line now was like walking through a house where someone had just died. The silence wasn't absence. It was presence—the shape of a sound that used to be there, pressing against your ears from the inside.

I found the first dead anchor-stone at the six-mile cairn.

I say *dead*, but that's not honest. Dead implies it had stopped. This stone hadn't stopped; it had been *emptied*. I'd inspected anchors before—part of warden training, though nobody told you why until your second year, and even then they only said "resonance check" and moved on quickly, the way people step around certain topics at funerals. An anchor-stone should feel dense. Warm, faintly, the way a hearthstone holds heat after the fire goes down. There's a grain to it, if you press your palm flat—a slow pulse, once every ten breaths or so, patient as tide.

This one was light. Not cracked-light, not weathered-light. Light the way a purse is light after a cutpurse has been through it. I put both hands on it and felt nothing but cold stone and, underneath the cold, something worse: a hollowness with edges, a cavity shaped exactly like whatever had been inside it.

Someone had taken the *middle* out.

I knelt there longer than I meant to. The wind came down off the Thornfells carrying snow-smell and pine-rot, and I remember thinking, absurdly, of the smuggler on the pass road. Three weeks ago. The bone-carver with the quiet hands and the look of a man listening to music no one else could hear. I'd had him dead to rights—out past curfew on a closed route, contraband in his satchel, no permits, no story that would hold water in any court I answered to. And I'd looked at him, and he'd looked at me, and something in his face had been so *tired*—not afraid, tired, like he'd been caught so many times the fear had worn through—and I'd said, "Go home, Okafor," and let him walk into the dark.

I'd told myself it was pragmatism. A minor infraction, a crowded docket, better relations with the Lowmark traders whose goodwill kept our garrison fed in winter. All true. None of it true. The truth was that arresting him had felt like kicking a dog that was already starving, and I hadn't wanted to be the kind of warden who did that, and so instead I'd become the kind of warden who lied to herself about why she didn't.

Now the stars were gone and the wards were hollowed and my commander wanted reports, not confessions.

I wrote in my field book: *Anchor six-mile: voided. Residue absent. No sign of forced entry or physical tampering.* My handwriting was steady. I checked twice.

Then I stood, and turned south toward the next cairn, and did not let myself think about tired eyes in lantern-light.

I failed, mostly.

* * *

Every anchor between the six-mile and Hollow Bridge was the same. Twelve stones, twelve cavities, twelve absences shaped like souls. By the fourth one I'd stopped pretending it was vandalism. Vandals break things; these stones weren't broken, they were *harvested*. Whatever had been drawn out had been drawn out carefully, completely, by someone—or something—that knew precisely what it was taking.

At the ninth stone I found the carving.

It was small, tucked into the base where the anchor met its plinth, done in a fine hand with a burin: a spiral, open at the center, with a single dot beside it. I copied it into my field book and stared at it for a long time. It wasn't a smuggler's mark—smuggler marks are crude, territorial, meant to be read fast by other smugglers. This was delicate. Deliberate. Almost tender.

It looked like craft. Like the kind of thing a carver does without thinking, the way a singer hums.

*I am not going to think about that,* I told myself, and thought about it for four more miles.

Hollow Bridge came up on me at dusk, which is to say at nothing, since dusk requires a sun and the sun had risen and set behind a sky with no stars waiting above it. The bridge itself spans the gorge where the Thornfell river goes underground—a span of black stone older than the crown, older than the wards, older than anything with a name we still use. The wardens' records call it a chokepoint. Smugglers call it the front door. The old stories call it something I'm not paid to repeat.

The toll-house at the bridge's near end had been a warden station once. Now it was mine for the night, and I got a fire going, and ate hardtack and salt pork, and tried not to look at the dark through the window, and failed at that too.

Here is what I have not written anywhere, not in my field book, not in any report, because there is no column on a warden's form for it:

While the fire burned down, I heard whispering.

Not wind. Wind doesn't repeat itself. This was voices—many voices, thin as paper, layered like sediment, all saying fragments of the same thing. I couldn't make out words. I'm not sure I wanted to. But the *shape* of it was grief, the specific cadence of people mourning at a graveside, and it came from under the bridge, from under the ground, from the direction of the mountain that the wards had served for a hundred years.

I sat with my knife in my hand until dawn, feeling like the most foolish woman in the Thornfells, telling myself: exhaustion. Altitude. Bad pork. Grief does strange things to a body, and the whole province was grieving whether it knew it yet or not.

But part of me—the part that had let a tired man walk free on a closed road, the part that had always heard something under the ward-hum that the hymns never mentioned—knew that the whispers were real, and that they were getting closer to the surface, and that whatever had been holding them down for a century had just been *taken away*.

* * *

Vess met me at the pass station, two days later, which told me everything. Commanders don't meet returning scouts at stations unless they're afraid of what the scout will say in front of witnesses.

"Well?" she said.

"Twelve anchors voided between the six-mile and Hollow Bridge. All twelve identical—contents removed cleanly, no physical breach. One unknown mark at the ninth stone." I handed her the field book, open to my copy of the spiral. "Commander, those stones were fed by something. Whatever it was, it's gone, and whatever took it did it from the inside. This wasn't sabotage from the lowlands."

She barely glanced at the drawing. "Smugglers."

"With respect—smugglers can't hollow an anchor-stone. They don't have the craft, they don't have the access, and they don't have any reason to burn their own roads. Whoever did this understood the covenant architecture better than the masons who built it."

"Smugglers," Vess repeated, in the tone she used to end arguments she'd already won. "There's a family of them runs the Lowmark routes. Bone-carvers, tanners, the usual. The crown has files." Something moved behind her eyes—quick, swallowed. "One name in particular keeps surfacing. Okafor."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped stone.

"The carver?" I said, and was proud of how flat I kept it. "He moves memorial bones and unlicensed charms. He's a nuisance, not an architect."

"He's a bloodline." Vess closed the field book. "Ashvale, I'm going to tell you something because you walked the line and you've earned a straight answer, and because if you repeat it I will deny it and ruin you. The ward-covenant is anchored. Has been, from the founding. Not by machinery. By a soul. A given soul, bound into the stones, feeding the wards the way a heart feeds blood. That's what the humming was. That's what's missing now."

I stood very still. Somewhere behind her, soldiers were hauling crates, and the sky hung empty over all of it.

"Given," I said. "Given by whom?"

"That's above my rank and soon above yours. What's within your rank is this: a soul doesn't leave an anchor-stone on its own. Someone called it out, or something did. The hollowing follows the old bloodlines like water follows a channel, and the Okafor line has been trading bones on this border since before either of us was born." She handed the field book back. "You know him. You brought him in last month and released him—yes, I read the log, don't make that face. So you'll go back to Lowmark, and you'll find him, and you'll bring him to me. Alive, if it costs nothing. He'll answer questions about the ninth-stone mark, and about his grandmother, whom the files mention more than I like."

"His grandmother," I said. The word came out strange. "What about her?"

Vess was already turning away. Over her shoulder, almost gently, which from her was the most frightening register of all:

"That's the question you're going to ask him, Ashvale. I suggest you start there."

I stood in the pass station yard with twelve hollow stones behind me and a name in my pocket, and the whispering under the mountain seemed suddenly much nearer than the gorge.

Because here's the thing I haven't confessed yet, the thing I'd been circling for three weeks and would circle for three more: when I let Jude Okafor go, he'd thanked me. Quietly, without looking back, the way you thank someone for a kindness you didn't expect to survive. And I'd watched him go and felt *good* about it, clean, like I'd passed some private test.

Now I wondered what he'd been carrying in that satchel. Now I wondered what a bone-carver hears, alone on a mountain road, with his ear to the world.

And underneath all of it, in the place where duty lives and doubts are supposed to starve, a small cold voice that sounded unpleasantly like my own asked the question I'd be asking for a long time after:

If I arrest him—if I do my job perfectly, exactly as trained—what if the thing the wards are missing isn't hiding in Lowmark?

What if it's hiding in the files?

I saddled my horse. The mountain waited, whispering, and somewhere beneath it, a grandmother's name was starting to glow.
