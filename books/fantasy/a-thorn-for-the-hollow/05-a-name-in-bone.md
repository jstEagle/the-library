## 5. A Name in Bone

The dead don't knock. I've told them a hundred times—there's a door, there's a bell, there's a perfectly serviceable knocker shaped like a raven that my grandfather carved before the war—but they come through walls and floors and the inside of your own skull like they own the place.

Which, I suppose, on some nights, they do.

Three days after the stars went out, I was sitting in my workshop with every lamp lit, every candle stub burning down to brass, because dark had become a word with teeth in it. The town outside was doing what Lowmark does best: panicking loudly and blaming quietly. The wardens had sealed the passes by noon of the first day. By the second day, the rumor mill had produced four separate culprits—a smuggler cartel, a heretic cult, the Hollow Court itself (old stories die hard), and, my personal favorite, "a bone-witch in the low quarter who talks to corpses."

That last one was me, obviously. It's always me. When you spend thirty years being invisible, you learn to take it as a compliment when people finally look at you—even if what they're looking at is a noose.

So I kept my shutters closed and my hands busy, because hands are the only prayers I've ever trusted. On the bench before me lay the memorial bone for little Tam Wicker, drowned under the mill ice two winters back, finally paid for by an aunt who'd scraped together the coin one copper at a time. A good commission. A sad one. The kind I do best.

I was carving the last line of his name into the shinbone of a fallow deer—the grain there runs fine and true, takes letters like a lover takes promises—when the whispering started.

Now, the dead are always whispering. That's the gift and the curse of it, wrapped in the same wet cloth. But this wasn't the usual murmur. This was *urgent*, a tide coming up fast, and it carried a word I hadn't heard from the other side since I was a boy:

*Jude.*

They said it like a warning. They said it like a wound.

I set down my knife. "One at a time," I said, the way I always do, though they never listen.

They didn't listen this time either. What came through instead was a single voice, old and dry as winter reeds, and beneath it all the others went quiet, the way a crowd goes quiet when someone important walks into a room.

*Boy,* the voice said. *Look at the shelf.*

I looked at the shelf.

Every carver keeps a piece they don't sell. Mine sits on the top shelf behind the lamp-oil, wrapped in waxed linen, and it has sat there for as long as I've had this shop, because it isn't mine to carve and it isn't mine to sell. My grandmother's memorial bone. Carved before I was born by hands I never met, by a great-uncle or a grand-aunt—I was told the story once as a child and once is all you get in my family, apparently—and passed down like a debt nobody would name.

It had been warming.

Not metaphorically. Not the way old bone holds heat from a fire long after the fire dies. I took it down and unwrapped it and the thing was warm as a sleeping bird against my palms, warm through the waxed linen, and when I peeled the wrapping back the carved surface had gone soft at the edges—not melted, not damaged, but *listening*, if bone can be said to listen, the lines of my grandmother's name seeming to lean toward something below us.

Something far below us.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small for such a large room. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

*We do not joke,* said the dry old voice, which was not my grandmother's, though it spoke of her. *We remember.*

Here is what I knew about my grandmother, in total: her name was Sefa Okafor, she died before my father could walk, and the family did not speak of how. In a family of talkers—dead talkers, living talkers, talkers who wouldn't shut up even in the grave—that silence should have been the loudest thing in the house. I was a child. Children accept silences the way they accept weather.

I am not a child anymore. And the dead, bless their relentless hearts, do not accept silences at all.

"Tell me," I said to the workshop, to the bones, to the warm thing in my hands. "All of it. Don't soften it. You never do."

And they told me.

* * *

You have to understand how a soul-sale works, and the dead explained it the way they explain everything—in fragments, overlapping, each ghost contributing the piece it remembered best, so that the truth assembled itself in my skull like a mosaic made by a committee of drunks.

A hundred years ago, the border wards of Thornfell were failing. The mountain's roots run deep and hungry, and the wards that keep the Hollow Court contained—or the Court that keeps the wards fed; even the dead disagree about which way round it goes—needed anchoring. An anchor isn't a spell. An anchor is a soul, fixed into covenant-stone, giving and giving until nothing's left to give.

You can't use just any soul. It has to be willing. Or made willing. And it has to be bound by blood to the land, rooted in the soil it protects.

Sefa Okafor was twenty-six years old. She had a gift—our gift, the hearing—and the crown's wardens came to Lowmark with a contract written in words that meant one thing to the crown and another entirely to a young woman whose whole family had been dying poor for generations.

They told her she'd be honored. They told her the mountain needed her. Whether they told her she would never leave—that her death would be a formality performed decades later on a body already hollowed out—is the question the dead couldn't answer. Some of them said yes. Some of them said the contract was read to her in a language she didn't speak. One old ghost, a toll-collector who died in 940-something and remembers everything because he resented everyone equally, said he saw her walk up the smugglers' road herself, unbound, unwept, carrying her own lantern.

Carrying her own lantern.

My hands were shaking by then. The bone in them had gone from warm to hot—not painfully, but insistently, the way a hand goes hot when it's held too long over a flame you can't bring yourself to pull back from.

"She went willingly," I said. "Didn't she. That's the worst part. She *went*."

*Nobody walks into a mountain willingly, boy,* said the toll-collector. *They walk in believing a lie. It only looks the same from outside.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. Outside the shuttered window, Lowmark's bells were ringing the alarm again—third watch, fourth watch, the sound had become constant, a headache with a bronze tongue—and somewhere in that noise were men with warrants and fire-pots looking for whoever killed the stars.

Looking for me, most likely, or for someone shaped enough like me to hang.

But here's the thing about terror. It burns fast and clean, like lamp-oil, and underneath it, once it's burned away, there's something else. Something with better posture.

Because the dead weren't finished. They'd saved the last fragment for the end, the way you save the bitter rind of the fruit, and the toll-collector delivered it with what I swear was relish:

*And boy—the covenant doesn't end with her. Blood binds blood. The anchor-thread runs down Sefa Okafor's line like a vein. Every Okafor born since has been a knot in that thread. The Court knows your name. It has known your name since the day you were born.*

The mountain spoke my name. Three nights ago, when the wards fell and the sky went black, I'd thought I imagined it—thought the screaming of ten thousand dead had scrambled my ears. It hadn't been imagination. It had been recognition. Something beneath Thornfell had felt its tether go slack and reached down the bloodline to see where the thread led.

It led here. To a shuttered workshop in the low quarter of Lowmark, where a man who talks to corpses was holding his grandmother's warming bone and understanding, all at once, exactly why he'd spent his whole life feeling watched.

I set the bone down on the bench, gently, the way you set down something that might be listening. Which it was. Which everything was.

* * *

I want to tell you I wept. There's a version of this story where I sit down among my bones and cry for the grandmother I never met, sold like a sack of barley to keep the stars in the sky, and maybe I'll get to that crying later, on some night when the lamps are lit and the danger's past and grief has time to unpack its bags.

But that night, what I felt wasn't grief. Grief is soft. This had edges.

I looked around my workshop—at the finished commissions stacked in their linen, at the half-carved names waiting for families too poor or too proud to pay, at forty years of listening to the dead because somebody had to and nobody else could—and I understood something with terrible clarity:

They'd used her because of what I am. Because our blood hears. Because a listener makes the strongest anchor, the way a bell makes the best alarm. The crown had found a girl who could hear the dead and spent her like currency, and then let her descendants live on in the same town, generation after generation, close to the thread, close to the stone, in case the covenant ever needed *mending*.

In case it ever needed a spare.

The bells stopped. In the sudden quiet I heard boots in the street—not the scattered shuffle of frightened townsfolk but the coordinated tread of warden patrol, moving with purpose toward the low quarter. Toward the bone-carver's shop, if the rumors had done their work. If the whispers in the guardhouse had names attached.

I could run. Smugglers' roads, hidden ways, the grammar of the mountain I'd learned hauling contraband past flickering wards. I knew routes the wardens' maps had never heard of.

Or I could stay, and be arrested, and be hanged for a crime that amounted to inheriting my own blood.

Or—

I picked up the bone. It was cool now. Whatever it had wanted to say, it had said, and my grandmother's name rested quiet under my thumb, and I thought: *you waited a hundred years to be heard, and I'm the only one alive who can hear you properly.*

"Right," I told the empty workshop, and the dead rustled in the walls like wind through wheat, attentive, expectant. "Here's what happens now. Somebody sold my grandmother to a hole under a mountain. Somebody let the stars go out rather than admit the bargain was rotten. And tomorrow morning, some captain with a warrant is going to kick in my door and call me the criminal."

I wrapped the bone back in its linen and tucked it inside my coat, against my chest, where it sat warm as a second heartbeat.

"So the criminals might as well be interesting ones."

There was a scratching at my door. Not the dead—they don't scratch; they arrive. This was knuckles, hard and official, and a voice I recognized through the wood despite three days and a war's worth of distance between us: the warden from the pass, the one who'd let me go when letting me go had cost her something.

"Jude Okafor," Wren Ashvale called, and her voice had none of the pass's mercy left in it. "Open the door. I'm not alone this time."

I put my hand flat against the wood, and behind me, every bone in the workshop began, softly, to sing.
