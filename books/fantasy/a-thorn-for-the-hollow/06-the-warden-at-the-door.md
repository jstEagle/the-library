## 6. The Warden at the Door

### Wren

The warrant said *Jude Okafor, bone-carver, suspected of consorting with the fallen wards*, and it was signed in ink the color of dried blood by a captain who had not slept in four days. I carried it up Lowmark's crooked lanes like a stone I'd decided to keep in my mouth.

The town had changed since the stars went out. Shutters stayed closed past dawn. People walked in the middle of the road because the edges felt too much like cliff-faces now that the sky had turned to a lid. And everywhere there were wardens, gray-cloaked and jumpy as crows, asking questions nobody could answer: *Where were you when the lights died? Did you hear anything? Did you dream?*

Everyone had dreamed something. That was the part that unsettled my captain most.

I told myself this was simple work. A smuggler with a talent for slipping past the border wards — I'd caught him at it once and let him go, which made me either his best witness or his accomplice depending on how the tribunal wanted to read my report. So I would arrest him, deliver him to the citadel, and let other people decide what he was. That was the whole architecture of my life: carry the thing, hand it off, don't look inside.

His workshop sat at the end of Tanner's Row, where the street gave up pretending to be respectable and became a goat track. Smoke curled from the chimney. Ordinary smoke. I stood outside the door for longer than any warden should stand outside any door, telling myself the delay was tactics.

It wasn't. It was the memory of him looking back at me on the smugglers' road, three weeks before the sky broke, and the way I had let him go and then lied about it in my logbook. First lie of my career. There is always a first one; I just hadn't expected mine to have a face like his.

I knocked.

"Closed," said a voice inside. Tired. Rough.

"Warden Ashvale." I held the warrant up to the door, as if wood could read. "Open in the crown's name."

Silence. Long enough that I reached for the handle — unlocked, of course, because smugglers lock their goods and never their doors — and stepped into warmth and lamplight and a smell I can still conjure if I close my eyes: tallow, dust, and something older underneath, like a chapel that has been very well used.

The workshop was full of bones.

Not scraps, not trade stock. Bones hung from the rafters on braided cords, each one carved so finely the lamplight moved through the lines like water through channels. Ribs scored with spirals. A skull cap — sheep, I thought, then hoped it was sheep — covered in tiny marching figures. And along every shelf, rows of small carved tokens, each labeled in a careful hand: *Marta, weaver, d. 40th of Harvest. Ollin, foundling, no date.* Hundreds of them. The whole room was a congregation.

"Touch nothing," Jude said from the corner, where he sat sharpening a blade against leather. He didn't get up. He looked at me the way a man looks at weather he'd been expecting. "Though I suppose you're here to touch everything."

"Jude Okafor." I got the words out level. Mostly. "You're detained under suspicion of—"

"I know what I'm suspected of. Everyone's suspected of everything lately. It's very efficient." He set the strop down. "Warrant?"

I passed it over. He read it properly, all clauses, which annoyed me more than if he'd torn it up.

"You let me go," he said, not looking up. "On the ridge road. Why?"

"That's not your concern."

"It's about to be both our concerns, I think."

And then the dead spoke.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last discipline I have left. They did not speak in voices, exactly. It was more that the room *remembered aloud*. The nearest shelf of tokens hummed, and a word arrived inside my skull as if it had always lived there and had only just chosen to unpack: *cold*. Then another: *the river took me feet-first*. Then a child's voice, no older than my sister had been, saying, clear as a bell rung underwater: *is she the one who keeps the wall?*

My knees went. I caught the bench. Every bone in the rafters had begun, very softly, to sway.

"You can hear them," Jude said. He was standing now, and his expression had gone through surprise into something worse — recognition. "Stars above. You can actually hear them."

"What is this?" My voice came out wrong. Thin. "What are you?"

"A carver." He crossed the room slowly, hands open, the way you approach a spooked horse or a person holding a knife they haven't decided about. "The bones hold what the living give them. Names, mostly. Grief. I carve a place for it and the dead settle in like birds on a wire. It's a kindness. Or it was." His jaw tightened. "Lately they won't stop talking about the mountain."

"The mountain," I repeated, because repeating things bought time.

"Something came out of it. Or something stopped." He looked at me with those dark, patient eyes that had watched me across a checkpoint while I pretended not to decide anything. "You heard your captain say the wards were fed. Fed on what, Wren? What does a hundred-year-old wall eat?"

I didn't answer. I didn't know. But somewhere below us, under the town, under the rock itself, I felt — *felt*, in my chest, in my teeth — an attention turn toward this little room full of carved bone like a lighthouse beam swinging round.

That was when the second knock came. Not a knock. A boot. The door burst inward off its latch, and Captain Halvard Vey filled the frame with fire already climbing his forearm.

### Jude

I had seen wardens do many things, but I had never seen one burn a building with people in it, so when Captain Vey raised his hand and the doorframe caught alight, my honest first thought was *huh, so that's policy now.*

"Heresy!" Vey announced, to the room, to the street, to whatever god audits captains. "Necromantic craft! Ashvale — step away from the criminal."

Wren did not step away. She stepped *sideways*, placing herself between the captain and me with the crisp mechanical certainty of someone whose body has committed treason several seconds ahead of her mind. I filed that away. It seemed important.

"Captain," she said, "the warrant named detention, not destruction."

"The warrant predates what I've learned tonight." Vey's firelight made his face look carved by someone less kind than me. "The wards didn't fail, Ashvale. They were *fed* — fed by souls, fed by craft like this—" he gestured at the shelves, at the hundreds of names, and something in his voice cracked open to show the fear underneath, "—and this man's bloodline is in the covenant-stone. We burned the archives today. Do you know what was in the archives? *Okafor.* Four generations. This family sold the mountain its anchor and the bill has come due, and I will not let the debt collector walk around wearing a man's face."

He was right. That was the horrible part. Everything he said was true, or true enough — I had spent two days learning it from my grandmother's memorial bone, which had warmed in my hands like a living thing until the dead finally arranged themselves into sentences: *a soul was sold. Her soul. Before you were born. She holds the wall.*

So I understood why Vey wanted fire. Fire is what frightened men use instead of answers.

The problem was that fire, applied to a room full of listening bones, does exactly what you'd expect.

As the flames took the rafters, the dead began to scream.

Not the whisper-hum I knew, not the settled murmur of names kept safe. A scream — hundreds of stored griefs tearing loose at once, pouring out of the burning carvings and into the air, into the street, into *me*, and apparently into Wren Ashvale, who dropped to one knee with her hands over her ears though there was nothing to hear with anything but whatever she'd caught in my workshop like a fever.

"They're dying," she gasped. "They're — Jude, they're *fading*—"

Vey flinched at her tone. At her using my name. At all of it. "Ashvale, stand *down*—"

"No." She got up. She drew her warden's blade, but pointed it — I noted this with the part of my brain that survives emergencies by taking inventory — pointed it at her own captain, which is the single fastest way to stop being a warden forever. "No, sir. You're not burning them twice. Once alive, once again in here. I won't."

The workshop was well alight by then. My grandmother's bone was upstairs. Everything else — Marta the weaver, Ollin the foundling, nine years of the drowned and the grieved and the unburied — was turning to ash around us, and every name that went up went through me like a nail drawn hot from a fire.

So I did what I do. I worked.

There's a reason carvers survive. Our hands know things our heads haven't approved. I grabbed the emergency satchel — every carver has one, oilcloth lined with waxed wool — and I went down the shelves pulling tokens blind, fast, stuffing them in, dozens of them, hearing each faint voice flicker as it left its shelf and trusting the wool to muffle the passage. Wren covered me without being asked, backing toward the rear door, blade up, shouting things at Vey that I suspect will follow her into every tribunal from here to the capital.

"You'll hang for this," Vey said, and he sounded almost sorry.

"Probably," Wren agreed. "Move anyway, Okafor."

We went out the back into the goat-track dark with the roof coming down behind us, and the last thing I saw of my workshop was Vey standing in the doorway letting the fire take the rafters, and the last thing I heard — through the wool, through the flames, through everything — was my grandmother's voice from upstairs, calm as ever:

*Go, boy. Go down. Come find me.*

* * *

### Wren

We ran. I have been running since, in one sense or another, so the details blur: the goat track, the drainage culvert, the frozen streambed that leads out under the palisade where the wardens' own patrol schedule has a gap shaped exactly like twenty years of smuggler patience. Jude knew all of it. Of course he did.

What I remember cleanly is stopping, finally, in a hollow beneath a leaning pine, snow coming down soft and indifferent, and realizing three things at once.

First: I had drawn steel on my captain and there was no road back from that, only roads forward.

Second: my satchel was heavy, because at some point during the escape Jude had shoved half his rescued tokens into *my* pack without mentioning it, presumably so that if we were separated, the dead wouldn't all die twice.

Third: I could still hear them. Muffled, drowsy, murmuring through oiled wool against my spine like passengers asleep in a coach. *Cold. Cold. Is the river far? Whose hands are these? Are they kind?*

"They're asking if my hands are kind," I said stupidly, aloud.

Jude looked over at me across the small fire he'd coaxed from pine pitch, and for the first time since I'd known him, he smiled — tired, crooked, real. "Then tell them," he said. "That's all the craft is, in the end. Somebody answering."

So I opened the satchel, and I held the nearest token — Marta the weaver, d. 40th of Harvest — in both my gloved hands, and I answered her. I told her the river was behind us and the mountain ahead, that the man who made her resting place was alive, that I didn't yet know if my hands were kind but they were going to be busy.

She sighed, deep in the bone, and settled.

Above us, where stars should have been, the sky was a smooth black lid. Below us, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the mountain waited. And somewhere between the two, Captain Vey was gathering wardens and pitch and certainty, because a man who burns a workshop to solve a problem will always burn something bigger next.

Jude fed the fire another branch and studied me across it.

"So," he said. "You're either my prisoner or my accomplice now. Tribunal's going to have opinions."

"Let them stand in line." I pulled my cloak tighter and tried not to think about how natural the dead sounded against my back, like a heartbeat I'd somehow acquired. "Which way is down?"

He looked at the mountain, and the mountain — I swear this — seemed to look back.

"Straight through the smugglers' roads," he said. "Three days to the gate. Assuming the bridge doesn't eat us."

"The bridge *eats* people?"

"Not usually." He stood, shouldered his pack, and offered me the ghost of that crooked smile again. "Usually it just charges."
