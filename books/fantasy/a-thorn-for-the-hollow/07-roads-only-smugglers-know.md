## 7. Roads Only Smugglers Know

The first thing you learn on the smugglers' roads is that the mountain does not want you to find them. The second thing you learn is that the mountain doesn't much care, either, so long as you ask politely and don't litter.

I'd been running the Thornfells since I was fourteen, back when the wards still hummed like a hive of contented bees and the worst thing you had to fear was a customs man with a good nose and a bad temper. Ten years of cairns and cut paths and shrines so old the gods they honored had been forgotten twice over. I knew the mountain the way you know a difficult relative—its moods, its silences, the places where it liked to be touched and the places where it would bite.

Wren Ashvale knew none of this. She knew warrants, patrol rotations, and the proper angle at which to hold a lantern while interrogating a suspect. Which is to say, for the first day out of Lowmark, she knew nothing useful at all.

"That's not a path," she said, looking at the wall of scree I'd stopped in front of. Dawn was still an hour off and the world was the color of wet ash. Without stars, night didn't so much end as get embarrassed and shuffle away.

"It's a door," I said.

"It's rocks."

"Rocks arranged in a door shape." I wedged my shoulder against the slab that looked like every other slab and pushed where the mountain wanted pushing. It groaned, considered, and swung inward a hand's width. "The wardens sealed the passes, right? Official passes. This isn't one. This is the way wool goes south and tin goes north and nobody asks anybody's name."

She peered into the dark beyond it. "How far does it go?"

"Far enough that you'll want to stop talking and start breathing through your nose. There's bat lime in the first stretch. It's not dangerous. It's just humbling."

She gave me a look I was beginning to catalog. This one was *you are enjoying this*, and the answer was yes, a little, in the way a man enjoys anything when the alternative is remembering that his workshop had burned and his name was on a heresy writ and the stars had gone out the same night the mountain whispered *Okafor* into his skull.

I went through the door first. Partly it was courtesy. Mostly it was because if the mountain was going to bite somebody, I'd rather it be me.

* * *

By midmorning we were above the tree line, on a shelf of path the wardens' maps showed as blank paper. Wren had stopped asking if things were paths and started asking what things were *for*, which I counted as progress.

"That cairn," I said, nodding at the knee-high stack of stones beside the trail. It was older than Lowmark, its top stone worn smooth as a knuckle. "You don't build those. You add to them. One stone, no more. It's how you tell the mountain you've seen it and it's seen you."

"That's superstition."

"That's grammar." I crouched, chose a stone the size of a fist, and set it on the stack. "Every road has a language. The king's roads speak in milestones and tollgates. The smugglers' roads speak in cairns and echoes and shrines. You want to walk them, you learn to talk."

"And if you don't?"

"Then the mountain talks over you, and it's very loud, and it never repeats itself."

She was quiet for a dozen steps. Then, in the tone of a woman who hated the question even as she asked it: "Who taught you?"

"My grandmother." The word came out easier than I expected. That was the strange mercy of the last few days—grief hadn't dulled; it had sharpened, turned into something with an edge I could work with. "She ran this road when the crown still hanged smugglers in the market square. She said the mountain was the only honest landlord she ever had. Rent was respect, paid in small stones and remembered names."

"Your grandmother." Wren said it carefully, the way you'd handle a blade you'd been told was sharp. She knew. Of course she knew—I'd told her in the burning workshop, shouting over the crackle of her captain's fire, and she'd believed me, which was the only reason we were walking this road together instead of her marching me south in irons. "The one whose soul is under the mountain."

"Under the mountain, in a lantern, feeding the wards for sixty years while the crown called it *stability*." I kicked a pebble off the path with more force than the pebble deserved. "So yes. She taught me the grammar. I intend to use it to break into her prison."

Wren didn't flinch. I'd noticed that about her—she absorbed anger the way the mountain absorbed rain, without complaint and without giving any of it back. "And the warden who's helping you," she said, "does she get a stone on the cairn?"

I looked at her. She was watching the cairn, not me, which was its own kind of answer.

"You already put one on," I said. "Back there. While I was talking. I saw you."

"I did not."

"You absolutely did. Left hand, gray stone, very sneaky for a woman in warden boots."

The corner of her mouth moved. It was the first time it had done that in three days of flight, and I found I was stupidly pleased about it, which I decided not to examine. "The mountain can have one stone," she said. "As a down payment. The rest depends on your grandmother's rescue going well."

"Negotiating with a mountain. You'll make a smuggler yet."

"Don't insult me."

* * *

We made the old shrine at Hollow Nook by late afternoon—a hollow in the cliff face where somebody, a very long time ago, had set three standing stones around a basin of black rock that still held water no rain had put there. Smugglers left offerings. I left offerings. My grandmother had left offerings, and one of hers was still there: a small carved bone star, gone gray with age, tucked into a crack where only someone who knew to look would find it.

I took it down and held it a moment. Her mark was on the back—the same double-line she'd taught me, the carver's signature that meant *made by hand, made with care, made to last*. My chest did something complicated.

Wren watched me from the mouth of the shrine. "What is this place?"

"Waystation. For the living and the other kind." I set the bone star in my pocket and poured a measure of water from my flask into the basin, the way you did—giving the shrine the first sip, like a host. "The dead used the roads too, back when the wards were strong. Pilgrims, mostly. Folk whose bodies got lost but who still had somewhere to be. The shrines were inns. You leave water, they leave you passage. Everyone's polite, everyone moves on."

"And now?"

"Now the wards are down and the dead are—" I stopped, because I'd been avoiding noticing it all day, and the shrine had made it impossible to avoid any longer. "Wren. Don't move."

"What?"

"Just—don't move, and tell me what you feel."

She stood very still. Her hand drifted to her knife out of pure warden reflex. "Cold," she said. "Behind us. Like a door open onto winter."

"That's not the wind."

"I know it's not the wind. I've been feeling it since the Nook pass. I assumed—" She stopped. Turned, slowly, the way you turn when you already know what you'll see and are hoping the turning will prove you wrong.

There were eleven of them. Maybe fourteen; the light was bad and they didn't hold still in the ways living things hold still. They stood along the path behind us at a respectful distance, gray and half-there, like figures seen through frosted glass. Some had been soldiers once—warden-issue coats, gone translucent. Some had been nobody in particular. One was small, and I made myself not look directly at the small one, because looking at dead children does something to a man that no amount of trade-hardening fixes.

They weren't menacing. That was the thing that would have frightened a warden most, I think. They were *waiting*. Patient as herons.

"They've been following us since the workshop," I said. "Since I listened to them in the fire. I thought they'd scatter."

"Why would they scatter?"

"Because the dead don't follow the living. It's not—" I gestured, inadequately. "It's not the arrangement. They go where they're bound, or they go to the shrines, or they thin out into the ground. They don't *migrate*."

"Like birds," Wren said quietly.

"Like birds," I agreed, "except birds have somewhere to go."

The nearest ghost—old soldier, half his face gone soft as breath on glass—lifted one insubstantial hand. Not reaching. Pointing. South and up, toward the high shoulder of the mountain, the way we were already going.

"They know where you're headed," Wren said.

"Or they know where I'm headed, and they've decided I'm the nearest thing to a road they've got." I looked at the soldier a long moment. "Can you hear me?" I asked, not really expecting an answer.

He didn't answer. But the whole loose flock of them leaned, very slightly, the way wheat leans toward a fence gap in the wind. Toward me.

Wren exhaled slowly. "Your gift. The hearing. When the wards fell, when the covenant broke—"

"Everything got louder. I know."

"No, I mean—" She turned to face me fully, and there was something in her expression I hadn't seen before, something that wasn't warden and wasn't fugitive and wasn't fear. It took me a second to name it. Awe, maybe. Or the beginning of it. "The wards were an anchor. A soul, holding a hundred thousand dead in place, quiet, *ordered*. And it's gone. And they're not scattering, they're not screaming—" she nodded at the flock, "—they're following *you*. A bone-carver from Lowmark with a flask of water and a bag of knives."

"Chisels."

"Jude."

"I know what you're saying." I crouched at the basin, needing something to do with my hands. "I'm saying I don't want to be an anchor. Anchors get put in lanterns. Anchors get *kept*."

"Then don't be one." She crouched beside me, close enough that I could see the defector's brand at her collar, the raw-edged sigil the crown burned into its turncoats so no garrison would trust them. "Be a road. Roads don't hold anyone. They just show the way."

I looked at her. She looked at the basin, embarrassed, as if the sentence had escaped without a permit.

"That," I said, "is the single most smuggler thing a warden has ever said to me."

"Don't ruin it."

* * *

We sheltered that night in a cut I knew—a smuggler's cache, dry-walled and roofed with slabs, big enough for two if neither of us believed in personal space. We built a small fire, because the cold without stars had teeth in it now, and because Wren's hands had started shaking around dusk in a way she clearly hoped I hadn't noticed.

I noticed. I handed her the good blanket without comment. She took it without comment. Somewhere in that exchange, an entire negotiation was conducted and ratified.

"The soldier," she said, after a while. "The one who pointed. He was wearing a warden coat."

"Old pattern. Thirty years out of date, maybe more."

"My grandfather served the Thornfell garrison." She said it to the fire. "He died on patrol. They never recovered the body. My grandmother used to say the mountain kept him, and she said it like it was a comfort, and I never understood that until this week." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Do you think he's out there? In that—flock?"

"Could be. The wards held the dead near where they fell. If he died inside the border, he's been standing in the snow for thirty years waiting for someone to walk the road he can't."

"That's horrible."

"It's the covenant," I said. "That's what your crown bought with my grandmother. Quiet. Order. A hundred thousand souls standing in the dark, not haunting, not resting, just *held*, so the living could feel safe." I fed a stick to the fire. "The Court under the mountain was supposed to be a refuge for them, once. Before it got corrupted into a larder."

Wren was quiet a long time. "I arrested a smuggler once," she said. "Early on. Young. He was running medicine, actually, which the tribunal didn't care about. I reported him straight. Did my duty." The fire cracked. "They hanged him in the Lowmark square. I stood in the front rank, because that was also duty."

I knew. She'd told me in the workshop, in the smoke, and I hadn't forgiven her and hadn't said I wouldn't. I said nothing now, either. The fire talked for both of us.

"His name was Tam," I said at last. "Tam Ellery. He taught me the Nook pass. He used to say the mountain's grammar had one rule underneath all the others: *leave the road better than you found it, because somebody dead is walking behind you.*" I looked up at her. "I used to think that was a joke."

"It wasn't."

"No. It wasn't."

She nodded slowly. "I can't undo it."

"No."

"But I can—" She stopped, started again, and I watched a woman who had built her whole self out of duty take the materials apart in her hands and find she didn't know what she was building now. "I don't know what I can do. But I'll walk as far as the road goes. Farther, if the road's dishonest."

"That's the spirit."

"Shut up, Okafor."

"Yes, warden."

"*Former* warden."

"Former warden," I said, and found, to my surprise, that I was smiling, and that she was too, there in the firelight, and that for the first time since the stars went out the dark outside our little roof didn't feel like an ending.

* * *

The dead walked with us for three more days.

They kept their distance by day, a gray procession on the ridgelines, and at night they came closer to the fires—not to the flames, but to me, settling in a loose ring like travelers at an inn, and I began, without quite deciding to, to talk to them. Not the hearing—that was different, that was their voices in my skull—but talking, the way you talk at a shrine. Names, mostly. "Morning," I'd say. "Cold one." And the flock would lean, and once, the small one laughed—a sound like wind through a keyhole—and Wren, who had been pretending to sleep, opened one eye and said, "It *laughed*," in a voice of pure scandal, and I said, "They're people, Wren, people laugh," and she said, "I know, I know, it's just—" and didn't finish, and didn't need to.

On the fourth day we came up over the shoulder of the mountain and saw the road ahead: a spine of black rock running south toward a pass the maps called the Warden's Teeth, and beyond it, in a fold of the high country, the place every smuggler's song called the Hollow Bridge—a span of natural stone over a gorge so deep the bottom had never been mapped, where the air moved wrong and the echoes came back with opinions.

The flock had grown. Thirty now. Forty. Word, if dead had word, was getting around: there was a carver on the road who listened, a warden on the road who'd broken her leash, and a road going down under the mountain where the covenant was kept.

The old soldier walked at the front now. He'd stopped pointing south. He pointed at the bridge, and then—he did this three times before I let myself understand it—he pointed at *me*, and then at the bridge, and then he made a gesture I knew from a hundred graveside carvings: hands cupped, as if weighing something. As if paying.

"They want to cross," I said.

"Can they?"

"Everything crosses the Hollow Bridge. That's the problem." I looked down the long spine of rock, at the pass where the wind was already beginning to sound less like wind and more like a voice clearing its throat. "The bridge takes a toll. A memory from the living. And from the dead—" I turned the soldier's gesture over in my mind. Hands cupped. Weighing. "I don't know what it takes from the dead. But I think they're telling me we're about to find out."

Wren came and stood beside me, her brand catching the lightless gray of the sky. Below us, far down in the fold of the mountain, something glimmered—faint, wrong-colored, like a lantern seen through deep water.

"Jude," she said. "That glow. Is that where I think it is?"

I didn't answer right away. The flock stood around us on the ridge, forty souls in the open air, patient as herons, and every one of them was looking at the light.

"That's the mountain's roots," I said. "That's the way down to the Court." I shouldered my pack, and my grandmother's carved bone star pressed against my chest through my shirt like a small warm hand. "Come on, then. The bridge is a day's walk, and I have a feeling it already knows we're coming."
