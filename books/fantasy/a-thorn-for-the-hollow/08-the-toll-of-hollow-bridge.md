## 8. The Toll of Hollow Bridge

The bridge found us before we found it.

I want to say that up front, because Wren spent the whole morning insisting we were lost, and I would like it on the record that I was right in the way that matters most: alive, and pointed the correct direction. The smugglers' road through the Thornfells doesn't go anywhere so much as it *arrives*, the way a thought arrives — one moment you're walking through pine and scree with no reason to believe the mountain wants you anywhere in particular, and the next there's a gap in the trees and a gorge below it, and spanning that gorge, a bridge made of black stone that no mason ever cut.

It looked like knucklebones strung together by something with too many fingers and too little patience.

"That's not on any map," Wren said.

"No," I agreed. "That's generally how smugglers' roads work."

"I mean it's not on *any* map. I've seen the warden surveys. The deep ones. There's nothing here."

"There wouldn't be." I stopped at the tree line and let the dead tell me what they always told me at places like this. They were thick here, thicker than anywhere since Lowmark, pressing against my skull like moths against glass. *Toll, toll, toll,* they said, in the flat chorus they used when they'd been saying something for centuries. *Pay, pay, pay.* "Bridges like this don't get mapped because maps are for things that stay put. This one only exists when someone needs to cross who isn't supposed to be anywhere."

Wren looked at me the way she'd been looking at me for three days now — half suspicion, half the reluctant respect of a woman watching a fish do arithmetic. "You're telling me a haunted bridge is going to decide whether we're allowed across."

"I'm telling you it's going to decide what crossing costs us. Whether we're allowed is mostly our own problem."

* * *

We camped above the gorge rather than approach it tired. That was lesson one of the mountain's grammar: never meet anything hungry when you're also hungry. I built a small fire in a hollow of stone, and Wren sat across from it sharpening a knife she'd already sharpened twice, which was how she prayed, as far as I could tell.

"The dead are loud here," she said, after a while.

"You can hear them?"

"Not hear." She turned the blade. "It's more like... weather. Pressure. Like the air before lightning. It's been getting worse since the wards fell. Since *you*—" She caught herself, which was new for her. Wren Ashvale did not usually catch herself; she said the sharp thing and then stood very straight behind it.

"Since me," I finished for her. "Yes. Apparently when your grandmother's soul is the thing holding the sky together and somebody carves her free, the dead develop opinions about your travel itinerary." I fed a stick to the fire. "They've been following me since the pass. You've seen them."

"Migrating birds," she said. "You called them that. Birds don't follow people."

"These do. I'm apparently very interesting." I tried to make it a joke. It came out about sixty percent joke, which was my best ratio all week. The truth underneath it was less funny: they came to me because I could hear them, and they were frightened, and being the only listening ear in a valley of screaming ghosts is a loneliness I hadn't known how to describe until I was standing inside it.

"What do they say?" Wren asked. And then, quieter, like she was paying for the question in advance: "Do any of them sound like—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. Somewhere between the fallen wards and Hollow Bridge she had started carrying a weight she didn't name, and I'd learned not to poke at it, partly out of courtesy and partly because I knew exactly what an unnamed weight felt like from the inside.

"Some of them are angry," I said. "Some are just confused. Most of them, honestly, just want to know if anyone's coming. If anyone remembers." I poked the fire. "The dead aren't so different from the living, once you get past the whole transparency issue. They want to matter to somebody."

She was quiet a long time. The fire cracked. Far below, the gorge breathed its cold breath up at us.

"Get some sleep," she finally said. "Tomorrow we pay."

* * *

Morning came gray and thin, the kind of light that looks like it's already given up. The dead were gathered along both rims of the gorge now, dozens of them, standing in the mist like reeds, and they went silent — truly silent, the first real silence in my head in days — as we walked down to the bridgehead.

Up close, the bridge was worse. Better. Both. The stones weren't stones at all, or not only; they were bones fused into black glass, femurs and ribs and skulls melted together into an arch that spanned maybe forty feet of screaming white water. Frost crawled along its surface in patterns I recognized, because I'd carved them myself a hundred times — warding spirals, old ones, older than the crown's, older than Thornfell's wards. Someone had loved this bridge once. Someone had built it to keep the dead safe while they crossed, back when keeping the dead safe was a thing anyone thought worth doing.

At the center of the span stood the toll-keeper.

I'd heard of bridge-ghosts the way you hear of everything on the smugglers' roads — secondhand, exaggerated, discounted. The stories said a woman in gray who took memories instead of coin. The stories undersold it. She wasn't a woman in gray; she was a woman made of the space where gray should be, a silhouette with frost for hair and eyes like two holes punched through the world into somewhere colder. When she moved, the water below changed direction to watch her.

"Travelers," she said, and her voice was the sound of a door closing in an empty house. "Two souls. Two tolls."

"We're going down," I said. "To the Court."

"Everyone who crosses this bridge is going down." Something that might have been amusement flickered across the absence of her face. "And everyone who crosses has decided it's worth more than whatever I take from them. That's the tragedy of bridges. Come."

Wren's hand went to her knife. I put mine over hers, gently, and felt her tense under my grip like a drawn wire.

"Don't," I murmured. "You can't fight this. Whatever it takes, it takes, and then we walk."

"And if what it takes is—"

"It won't be that. It takes memory. Stories say so, and the dead confirm it." I glanced at the rim of the gorge, at the silent congregation. "They're not warning us off. That's the strange part. They're *watching*. Like it matters that we cross."

"Everything matters lately," Wren said grimly, and stepped onto the bridge.

The frost didn't crunch under her boots. That was the detail I'd remember later — no sound at all, our footsteps swallowed, the river's roar gone strangely distant, as though the bridge existed slightly outside the world and sound hadn't been invited.

Halfway across, the toll-keeper raised one hand, and we stopped, because stopping was simply what bodies did when she asked.

"Payment," she said. "One memory each. Not the worst. Not the best — I've no use for those, they're too heavy to carry and too bright to spend. A true one. A load-bearing one." Her hollow eyes fixed on me first. "Bone-carver. Son of a daughter of the covenant line. You've brought grief enough with you; choose what I take carefully, because I am not gentle, and memory comes out ragged."

My mouth went dry. I'd known this was coming — I'd walked toward it all morning with my eyes open — but knowing a price and paying it are different animals entirely.

"What happens to what you take?" I asked. "Do you keep it? Eat it?"

"I hold it," she said. "As the bridge holds travelers. Some things must be carried across, bone-carver, and cannot carry themselves." For a moment the frost-hair shifted, and beneath the silhouette I saw — or thought I saw — something exhausted. Ancient. A woman who had stood on this span taking payment since before Thornfell had walls, doing her one job in a world that kept forgetting why the job mattered. "Choose."

I thought of my mother.

Of course I did. The moment she said *load-bearing*, there she was, the keystone of every arch in me. Mama Okafor, who sang off-key and carved badly and laughed at her own bad carving. Who taught me the first spiral I ever cut into bone, guiding my hand with hers wrapped around it, saying *the pattern isn't decoration, Jude, it's how you talk to what's gone, it's how you listen*. Who died in the winter fever when I was fourteen, and whose memorial bone I carved myself, alone, in a workshop that suddenly echoed, and got it *wrong* — some flaw in the grain, some mistake in the spiral — so that her voice never quite came through the way other voices come through their bones. Fourteen years hearing every dead soul in Lowmark except the one I wanted most.

If she took that memory — the hand over mine, the song, the warmth — what was left? A man who carved bones for strangers' mothers and couldn't remember his own?

But the toll-keeper had said *not the best*. And here was the ugly truth I arrived at standing on a bridge of fused bones with a ghost waiting: the memory of learning wasn't the load-bearing one. The load-bearing one was the fever room. The last night. Holding her hand while she slipped, and hearing — with the gift I hadn't yet learned to hate — the exact moment her voice left her body and became something else. The moment I learned that death wasn't an ending but a change of address, and that I could follow the address, and that she still hadn't spoken to me.

That memory had done nothing but hurt me for fifteen years. It sat in me like a splinter against the bone. Maybe that was what load-bearing meant: the memory that held up the shape of your grief.

"If I give it," I said quietly, "will I still grieve her?"

"You will still love her," the toll-keeper said. "Grief is what love does when it has nowhere to put itself. Take away the splinter and the wound can close. It will scar. Scars are honest."

Wren was watching me. I could feel her attention like a hand between my shoulder blades.

"Jude," she said, low. "You don't have to explain yourself. But whatever you're weighing — the road gets worse than this. I've read the warden reports on the deep ways. If you're carrying ballast, this is the place to drop it."

Which was, in its flinty way, the kindest thing she'd said to me yet.

I looked down at the water. I looked at the dead lining the gorge rim, silent, attentive, like a congregation waiting for a reading. And I understood — sudden and total, the way the mountain's understandings always came — that they weren't watching out of morbid curiosity.

They were watching because they hoped. Because a man walking down to the Hollow Court to bargain for a soul, willing to pay even this, was proof of something they needed proven: that the living could love the dead enough to spend themselves on them. Every ghost on that rim had once been somebody's load-bearing memory. Nobody had paid for them. Nobody had carried them across. They'd just been left on the far side of their own deaths.

"Take it," I said to the toll-keeper. "The fever room. The last night. Take the splinter."

She reached out, and her hand passed into my chest without breaking skin, without cold, without anything except a sensation like a page being turned.

And it was gone.

Not the love. Not her name, not her face, not the off-key singing — those lived somewhere deeper than any single night, thank every star still standing. But the *night* itself, the sweat and the rattle and the terrible listening, the fifteen years of flinching away from it — gone, lifted out clean, and in its place a strange smooth emptiness, like the socket of a pulled tooth. I swayed. Wren caught my elbow. Her grip was iron and, unexpectedly, stayed there a moment longer than balance required.

"Oh," I said, because language had temporarily failed me. "Oh, that was—"

"Yes," the toll-keeper said. "Now you." Her hollow gaze swung to Wren. "Warden. Defector. Branded twice over — by the crown that owns you and the conscience that unmade you. What will you pay?"

Wren went very still. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd just done, faster and harder, a woman accustomed to triage. I also watched her try to lie to a creature made of absence, and fail, and know she'd failed.

"I don't—" she started.

"You're thinking of offering the day you reported the smuggler," the toll-keeper said, mild as milk. "The one the crown hanged. You think it's your worst memory, so surely it's fair payment. But I told the bone-carver and I'll tell you: not the worst. The worst is too useful to you. You polish it every night. It keeps your guilt sharp and your penance convenient." The frost-hair drifted. "No. Pay me something true and structural. Pay me what holds you up."

Wren's jaw worked. For a long moment I genuinely thought she'd turn around, oath-bound and proud, and we'd find another way or no way. Then she exhaled, long and controlled, the exhale of a woman stepping off a ledge on purpose.

"My oath," she said. "The day I swore in. The hall in the capital. The words. The feeling that I was finally *becoming* something." Her voice didn't shake, but I could hear the cost of keeping it level, the effort like rope creaking under load. "That's the load-bearing one. Everything I did wrong, I did because I believed that day made me righteous. Take it."

"Wren," I said — and didn't know what followed the name, so nothing did.

"Quiet, smuggler. Your turn to watch." She faced the toll-keeper square-on, chin up, like submitting to a search at a checkpoint. "Take it."

The hand went into her chest. Wren made no sound at all, which somehow was worse than any sound. When the toll-keeper withdrew, Wren stood differently — I couldn't have said how, except that a certain rigidness had gone out of her spine, a rigidity I'd mistaken for character and now recognized as architecture. She blinked at the gorge, at the bridge, at me, with the faint bewildered look of someone waking in a familiar room that's been rearranged overnight.

"I feel," she said slowly, "lighter. And worse. Both."

"That's the toll," said the toll-keeper. "Welcome to honesty." She stepped aside, and the bridge lay open before us, frost spirals glinting. "Cross, then. And travelers — the Court below is starving, and starving courts eat bargains. Whatever you intend to trade for, count your ribs before you enter. Count what's left."

She began to fade even as she spoke, dissolving into the bridge-stones, into the arch itself, and I understood with a small cold shock that the toll-keeper wasn't a ghost *on* the bridge. She was the bridge. Every memory she'd ever taken, packed into the span, holding it up over the void. Load-bearing. She'd meant it literally all along.

The dead on the gorge rim stirred as we crossed. As we reached the far side, one of them — a small figure, a child's outline, the drowned girl from Lowmark whose bone I'd carved in another life, weeks ago — lifted one translucent hand. Not waving. Pointing. Down the switchback trail that plunged into the mountain's roots, where a dull red glow seeped up through the rock like the mountain had a fever of its own.

"She's been following us since the pass," I said quietly to Wren. "Why?"

Wren looked at the child-ghost, and something crossed her face — the ghost of the guilt she no longer owned the memory of, haunting her anyway, the way scars haunt. "Because she thinks you're going to fix it," she said. "Whatever broke. All of them think that. That's why they're migrating after you, Jude. You're not interesting." She started down the trail, and her voice came back over her shoulder, dry and stripped and newly, terribly sincere. "You're *hopeful*. It's the most frightening thing about you."

Behind us, the bridge stood empty over its roaring water, holding up the sky's forgotten debts.

Ahead, the red glow brightened, and the dead began to descend.

I counted my ribs, like she'd told me. All of them still there.

I had the awful feeling I'd need every one.
