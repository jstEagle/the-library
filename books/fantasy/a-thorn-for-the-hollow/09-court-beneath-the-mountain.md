## 9. Court Beneath the Mountain

I had been a warden for eleven years, and in that time I had learned exactly three truths about the Thornfells: the passes close without warning, the cold finds the crack in every boot you own, and anything the smugglers' maps call a road is a rumor with ambitions.

The road we were on was all three at once.

Jude walked ahead of me with the confidence of a man who had never once considered falling off a cliff, which I found either inspiring or infuriating depending on how much sleep I'd gotten. The dead followed him now—not just whispering from their bones but actually following, drifting along behind us like migrating birds who had decided he was south. There were dozens of them. Maybe hundreds. Pale shapes without faces, keeping a respectful distance, and every time I looked directly at one it politely pretended to be fog.

"You're staring again," Jude said, not turning around.

"I'm assessing threats."

"They're dead, Wren."

"Dead things have historically been my problem."

He snorted and kept climbing. That was the other thing about the last six days of my life: somewhere between Hollow Bridge and the high shrine at Crag's Teeth, I had stopped being Warden Ashvale of the Crown's Third Wardens and become *Wren*—a woman who slept in smugglers' hollows, ate smoked goat she didn't ask questions about, and had apparently defected so completely that her body had stopped flinching when Jude touched her elbow to steady her on ice.

The brand on my forearm had not stopped flinching. It burned all day now, a slow coal pressed under the skin of the old defector's mark—the crown's little insurance policy for wardens it didn't trust, inked with ash and salt so that if we ever went traitor, everyone would know. It had been quiet for years. Since the stars died, it had been waking up angrier by the mile.

And today it was screaming.

We came over a shoulder of black stone in the late grey afternoon—there was no real daylight anymore, just degrees of dark—and the mountain opened below us like a throat. Not a valley. A wound. The rock fell away into a bowl a half-mile wide, ringed with cliffs, and at its center stood the gate.

I say "stood," because it did stand. Doors the height of ten men, set into the mountain's flank like it had grown there rather than been built, carved from a stone so black it swallowed the grey light whole. And through the seams of those doors, down through the roots of the mountain itself, ran light.

Not firelight. Not wardlight. Something older, redder and warmer, pulsing slow as breath, filling the cracks in the rock like blood in veins. The whole mountainside glowed faintly from within, a network of luminous threads converging on the sealed doors, and standing before it I felt the brand on my arm go from burning to *screaming*, and I heard—for the first time since Hollow Bridge took my oath away—a sound where the oath used to be.

It sounded like a door opening very far underground.

"Well," Jude said quietly. "There it is."

"There it is." My voice came out flat. I was gripping my own wrist above the brand hard enough to bruise. "You know what's strange? Eleven years of service. I arrested forty-one people. I wrote reports. I kept ledgers. And nowhere in any ledger did anyone write down *by the way, there is a glowing door under the mountain that our entire border defense may or may not be plugged into.*"

"That would have required the wardens to know."

"The wardens knew *something*. We knew the wards were fed. We called it the tithe. Nobody asked what the tithe was." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Do you know what we were told, when we asked? 'Old arrangements.' Captain Verel's favorite phrase. Old arrangements. Like the wards were an elderly aunt who needed looking after instead of—" I gestured at the glowing mountain, at the dead drifting patiently at Jude's back, at everything. "Instead of this."

Jude was looking at me with that expression he had, the one that made me feel like one of his bones laid out on the workbench—turned over, read, understood better than I wanted to be. "Your arm."

"It's fine."

"Wren."

"*Fine.*"

He crossed the space between us anyway, and before I could decide whether to let him he'd pushed back my sleeve. The defector's brand was raised and red against my skin, the crown-and-thorn sigil swollen like a fresh scar, and beneath it—I swear this—the skin had gone faintly luminous, veined with the same red-gold light as the mountain. The mountain knew I was here. The mountain had opinions about what I was.

"That's new," I admitted.

"When did it start?"

"This morning." I pulled my sleeve down. His hand lingered a moment longer than strictly necessary, warm despite the cold, and I chose not to comment on it because commenting would have required examining why I wanted it to stay. "Jude. Before we go down there, before whatever happens happens—there's something you should know. Something I should have told you days ago."

He waited. He was good at waiting. Carvers probably had to be.

"Hollow Bridge took my oath," I said. "My memory of swearing it. But it didn't take what I *did* with it. What I did before." I looked at the gate, because I could not look at him for this part. "Three years ago, there was a smuggler running the Thornfell roads. Good one. Better than you, if the stories are true—he moved cargo through passes that closed for everyone else. Fenn Marrow. Did you know him?"

The silence went on long enough that I made myself turn my head. Jude had gone still in the way he did—not frozen, but gathered, like water deciding whether to be a river or ice.

"Fenn," he said. "Fenn Marrow."

"He carried medicine once, during the fever winter, through the north pass when the wardens had sealed it. Saved maybe thirty lives in Lowmark. And the crown called it treason—unlicensed passage across a warded border—and put out a bounty." My mouth was dry. The brand pulsed with each word, eager, as if the mountain approved of my confession and wished me to continue. "I tracked him. I was young. Hungry for commendation. I found his camp in the high fells, and I reported it, and I told myself it wasn't personal, it was law, it was *duty*—"

"Wren."

"—and they hanged him in Lowmark square in the spring. Publicly. As an example." The words came out of me like something lanced, hot and unstoppable, three years of swallowing finally breaking open. "His mother threw herself on the scaffold. They had to drag her off. And I stood in the crowd in uniform and watched, and I felt *proud*, Jude, that's the worst of it, I felt proud that I'd done clean work—"

"Stop." His voice cracked across the wind like a whip. "Just—stop a moment."

I stopped. The dead drifted closer, I realized distantly, crowding soft and faceless at the edges of my vision, drawn perhaps by grief the way moths are drawn by flame. Even the mountain seemed to lean in, its vein-light pulsing faster.

Jude sat down on a boulder. Actually sat, heavily, the way a man sits when the ground has moved under him. He rubbed his face with both hands. When he spoke again his voice was careful, sanded smooth, the voice of someone holding himself together with craft.

"I buried Fenn," he said. "What was left. His mother wouldn't look at the body, so I did it. Carved his memorial bone myself. First one I ever made that mattered." He dropped his hands and looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and exhausted all at once. "You're telling me the woman who found him has been walking beside me for six days. Sleeping twenty feet from me. Teaching her the mountain's grammar. Telling her things about my grandmother that I've never told another living soul."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me now because?"

"Because we're about to walk into a court of the dead that feeds on souls, and your soul is the bargaining piece, and mine is apparently the strongest anchor left in the world according to a queen I haven't met yet." I laughed again, that awful dry sound. "Because if I'm going to be worth anything down there—if I'm going to stand in front of your grandmother's ghost and ask her to trust me—I can't do it carrying this. Because you deserved to know before the bridge took pieces of me, and I was a coward, and I'm done being a coward. Pick whichever reason hurts least."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The wind came down off the peaks and stirred the vein-light below us, making the whole mountain seem to breathe. One of the dead drifted nearer—close enough that I could almost make out a shape in the fog of it, broad-shouldered, tall—and Jude glanced at it and something crossed his face.

"Fenn's here?" I asked quietly. "Among them?"

"He's been following us since the shrine at Crag's Teeth." Jude's jaw worked. "They follow me when they want something, usually. Or when they've got something to say and no way to say it." He looked at the shape a long time. Then at me. "He wants me to hear something. I can't make it out. He's too—scattered. Dead past the bridge don't hold together well."

"I see." I wrapped my arms around myself, brand burning, and faced the thing I'd been circling. "So. Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"What you want to ask me. Whether I'm sorry. Whether I'd do it different. Whether you should leave me on this ridge right now and go down alone." My voice held, barely; I'd give myself that. "Whatever it is. Ask."

"I don't need to ask if you're sorry," Jude said slowly. "I've watched you apologize to rocks for tripping on them for six days. You're sorry. You've been sorry so long it's grown into you like the brand has." He stood, and came closer, and the fury in his face was settling into something more complicated and worse. "What I need to know is what you'll do next time. Next time the law says hang a man and your gut says the law is wrong. Next time duty points at something I love."

"I burned my duty at Hollow Bridge," I said. "It cost me a memory and I paid gladly."

"No." He shook his head. "Duty doesn't burn, Wren. Believe me—I know about obligations you inherit without asking. Mine's in a lantern under that mountain. Yours is wherever the crown points. Burning it just leaves a hole shaped like it, and holes fill up with whoever's shouting loudest." He tapped my branded arm, gently, right over the sigil. "So. What will you do?"

And there it was. Not forgiveness. A question. An honest one, harder than any accusation, and I understood suddenly that it was the only gift he could give me right now—that he was refusing to make it easy because easy would be a lie between us, and whatever this was, whatever we were building on these frozen roads, he wanted it built true.

I thought about the answer. Really thought, while the mountain breathed and the dead waited and the cold worked at my fingers.

"Next time," I said, "I'll be slow. I'll take the extra day. I'll knock on doors and ask ugly questions before I write the report. I'll be the warden who gets passed over for commendation because she couldn't be certain." I met his eyes. "Certainty is what hanged Fenn. Not cruelty. Certainty. I'll spend the rest of my life being uncertain, if that's the price."

Something in Jude's shoulders let go. Not everything—there would be no everything for a while yet, maybe ever—but something. He exhaled a long breath that fogged white between us.

"All right," he said.

"*All right?*"

"All right. I believe you. I don't forgive you yet—I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and you'd smell the pretense anyway. But I believe you, and I want you beside me down there, and Fenn—" he glanced at the pale shape, which had drifted closer still, near enough now that I fancied I could feel it watching, some attention ancient and patient resting on my face like a cold hand. "Fenn followed *you* onto this road, Wren. Think about that. The dead follow me everywhere. Him, he picked you."

"Why would he—"

"Maybe he wants to watch what you do next," Jude said. "Maybe that's the only justice the dead get anymore. Someone to witness." He turned toward the gate, toward the breathing red-gold light, and pulled his pack higher on his shoulders. "Come on. The mountain knows your name already—it's been shouting it through your arm all day. Rude to keep it waiting."

We descended the last slope together, and the dead came with us, dozens strong, silent as snowfall. Up close the gate was even worse and more beautiful than it had been from the ridge: the black stone carved all over with figures, thousands of them, generations deep—people dancing, mourning, working, sleeping, a whole civilization of the unburied rendered in relief and worn smooth in places by hands touching them over centuries. At the center of the doors, where they met, was carved a single figure holding a lantern, and the lantern's hollow had been filled with something that glowed.

The brand on my arm was nearly white-hot now. Beneath it, under the pain, I could feel the door-opening sound again, closer, and threaded through it—faint, unmistakable, impossible—

Singing. Many voices, thin and hungry and sweet, singing behind the stone.

Jude pressed his palm flat against the gate, the way he pressed his palms to bone. "Grandmother," he said softly. "I'm here. I've come to carve you free."

The lantern-figure in the stone lit bright as a struck match.

The gates began to open.

* * *

They opened inward without a sound, which frightened me more than grinding stone would have—as if the mountain were holding its breath. Beyond them lay a stair descending into the vein-lit dark, wide enough for ten abreast, walls carved with the same endless procession of figures. Warm air rose up to meet us, smelling of stone dust and, strangely, of woodsmoke and bread, like a house rather than a tomb.

At the top of the stair stood a figure.

She was small and slight, draped in robes the color of ash, and her face was kind and terrible in equal measure, the way winter is kind and terrible. She looked at Jude first, and something in her ancient face softened. Then she looked at me—at the brand, blazing now like a coal on my arm—and her kindness did not fade, which somehow made it worse.

"The warden," she said. Her voice filled the stairwell like water fills a cup. "We wondered which of you the mountain would send ahead." She stepped aside, gesturing down into the glowing dark, toward the distant sound of hungry singing. "Come. Your grandmother has been waiting a hundred years, carver. And I confess—" her gaze settled on me, gentle and merciless, "—I have been waiting a very long time to meet the woman whose guilt is loud enough to wake the roots of the world."

Behind me, among the drifting dead, I felt Fenn Marrow step forward and follow us down.
