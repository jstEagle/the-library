## 10. The Bargaining Hall

The gate did not open so much as admit us, the way a mouth admits food.

One moment Wren and I stood before a wall of black stone veined with that faint root-glow, and the next the stone had decided we were inside it, and the cold of the mountain closed over us like water over a dropped coin. I had time to think, absurdly, of my grandmother telling me that the dead don't drag you anywhere—they only leave the door open and wait to see if you're fool enough to walk through.

I was fool enough. I had been fool enough for weeks.

"Wren," I said, because saying her name was a way of checking she was still there.

"Still here." She had her hand on my shoulder—she'd taken to doing that in the dark stretches, a warden's habit of keeping contact with her charge, though somewhere on the road it had stopped feeling like custody and started feeling like something I didn't have a word for. Her brand, the defector's mark seared into her collarbone, had been burning since the gate. I could smell it faintly, like a candle guttering. "Jude. Whatever happens in there—"

"Don't make deals."

"You were going to say it."

"I was going to say it. Now you've said it, so I don't have to." I turned my head, and in the root-light her face was pale and set and closer than I'd realized. "We trade for my grandmother's soul. Nothing else. We don't offer. We don't volunteer. We don't fill silences."

"You've bargained before?"

"I've bargained with smugglers, tax collectors, and a woman in Lowmark who once tried to pay for her husband's memorial bone in live chickens." I flexed my fingers, felt the old familiar ache in the knuckles that meant I'd been carving too long and would keep doing it anyway. "The dead are easier. They only want one thing, and it's usually the same thing the living want. To be remembered right."

"And the Hollow Queen?"

I didn't have an answer for that. So I did what I always do when I don't have an answer, which is walk forward anyway and pretend the silence is a choice.

* * *

The road down was a road the way a throat is a road.

It wound through stone that grew more and more like bone the deeper we went—not metaphorically, I want to be clear, I have carved enough bone to know bone when I'm walking through its marrow. The walls had that dense, faintly warm grain to them, and here and there the mountain's roots had pushed through in pale branching fans, glowing like the veins of something sleeping. The dead walked with us. They'd been walking with me since the bridge—my flock of whispering followers, my congregation of the unburied—and down here they grew bolder, pressing close enough that I could feel them the way you feel a draft, a cold spot at the ear, a hand-shaped absence on the back of the neck.

"They're excited," I murmured.

"Excited," Wren repeated, in the flat tone she reserved for things she found either horrifying or suspicious, which down here was most things.

"They've been waiting a long time. All of them. The ones nobody buried, nobody named, nobody—" I stopped, because the dead were pressing against me like dogs at a gate, and what came through wasn't words yet, just a kind of pressure, a hunger shaped like hope. "Nobody came for them. That's what this place is, Wren. It's not a prison. It's a lost-and-found that nobody ever claimed from."

The road opened.

I have stood in the great hall at Thornfell Keep—once, from a smuggler's gallery, watching wardens drill beneath banners I'd have been hanged for touching. I have seen the cathedral at Cael Sarrow, or its bones, before the fire. Neither of them prepared me for the Bargaining Hall, because every hall I'd ever seen had been built to make the people inside it feel small, and this one was built to make them feel *seen*.

It was vast, and it was beautiful, and it was starving. All three at once, the way a face can be all three.

Pillars of bone rose into a dark so high it might have been sky. Between them hung lanterns—hundreds of them, thousands, each one carved, each one holding a soft gold light that was not fire. I know lanterns. My family has carved them for four generations, and I knew the work the moment I saw it: the fluted sides, the pierced stars, the little doors that open to let a soul breathe. Family work. My family's work, sold or stolen or given, over a century, until the Court's whole sky was a constellation of our craft.

And in that light moved the dead.

They were beautiful. That's what I hadn't been ready for. The stories say hollow-eyed horrors, and there were hollow eyes, but the horror of them was not monstrous—it was the horror of a face at a window, watching a table it will never sit at. They wore the clothes they'd died in, and some of those clothes were fine, and some were rags, and all of them were worn through at the edges like the world had been rubbing at them for a very long time. They turned to look at us as we came down the last of the road, hundreds of faces turning together, and the sound they made was not a moan.

It was a hush. The hush of a crowd when the music starts.

"Jude." Wren's voice had gone thin. Her hand had found mine without either of us deciding it. I didn't let go. "Are they—"

"Starving," I said. "Don't look at their hands."

She looked at their hands. I felt her understand. The dead of the Hollow Court were holding their hands the way the poor of Lowmark hold their hands in a bad winter—curled in, protective, ashamed of the emptiness in them.

* * *

She came to us across the hall the way winter comes across a field. No hurry. No malice. Just an inevitability so complete it made haste look like a lie.

The Hollow Queen was tall in the way that mountains are tall, which is to say you didn't see her whole at once, your eye kept refusing. She wore a crown of thorn-wood, black and living, and beneath it her face was—this is the part I keep turning over, the part I'll be turning over on my deathbed—her face was *kind*. Not beautiful the way the stories say. Kind, the way a hearth is, the way a mother's hands are, and it was the most frightening thing I had ever seen, because I could feel my own loneliness reaching toward it like a hand toward a flame.

The dead knelt as she passed. Or folded, rather, the way wheat folds before wind.

"Jude Okafor," she said. Her voice was my grandmother's voice, and my mother's, and the drowned child's, and every voice that had ever whispered to me in the dark of my workshop. "Bone-carver. Listener. The mountain has been saying your name for a hundred years, and here you are at last."

"You knew I was coming."

"I knew someone of your blood would come, eventually. The covenant always sends back what it takes." She smiled, and the smile was warm, and I hated how much I wanted to stand in it. "You want the old woman's soul. The one in the lantern with the fluted sides and the star pierced through the door. Your grandmother's hand helped carve that lantern, did you know? Before she was in it. There's a cruelty in the crown I could never improve upon."

My hands had gone cold. "You've seen it."

"I keep it." She turned, and the hall turned with her, and the dead turned with the hall, a slow wheeling of faces. "Come. Bargaining is done standing before what's bargained for. It's an old rule and I am very old."

* * *

She led us to the center of the hall, where a dais of black stone rose, and on the dais, on a cushion of woven root, sat a single lantern.

It was small. That's what broke me. A hundred years of horror, a century of wards fed and stars dimmed and a woman erased from her own family, and it came down to a lantern the size of two fists, carved in the old style, the fluting worn smooth where hands had held it. The light inside was dim. Not gold like the others. A tired amber, guttering, a candle in its last hour.

I knew the light. I had known it my whole life without knowing it. It was the warmth that had come off my grandmother's memorial bone when I held it as a child. It was the voice that had told me, when I was nine and crying over my first failed carving, that the wood wasn't wrong, I was just asking it the wrong question.

"Grandmother," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle like green wood.

The light stirred. And faintly, so faintly, through the pierced star in the lantern's door, came a sound I would know at the bottom of any grave, in any dark, in any life: my grandmother, humming.

The Queen watched me with her kind, terrible face. "You see the problem," she said gently. "She is nearly spent. A century as an anchor, poured out to feed the wards, to hold the border, to keep the crown's pretty lights burning over Thornfell. She was strong when they took her. A woman who could quiet a haunted house with a look. And they poured her out like wine." The Queen's voice did not rise, but the hall heard it anyway; the dead had gone still, all of them, a forest of faces listening. "I have kept her as long as I can. But the wards are fallen, the stars are dark, and the anchor is empty. The Court starves. She starves. Everything below is starving, bone-carver, and everything above is freezing, and the crown that did this is burning smugglers in the passes and calling it justice."

"Then give her to me," I said. "And we'll find another way to feed the Court. I'm a carver. I'll carve anything. I'll—"

"You misunderstand the arithmetic." The Queen turned from me to Wren, and I watched my whole plan die in the turning of her head. "The covenant requires an anchor. Not a carving. Not a payment. A *soul*, given over, held in the stone, poured out slowly across a lifetime. Your grandmother was the last. The wards fell when she ran dry. And the only soul left in this hall strong enough to hold the border—" the Queen's eyes, if she had eyes, or the places where eyes would be, fixed on Wren with something that was not quite hunger and not quite pity, "—is a warden's. Wardens are made, not born. Sworn, branded, bound. Her soul is a knot the crown tied itself. The strongest anchor left in the world."

"No," Wren said.

"It's an offer," the Queen said, "not a command. I am not the crown. I do not take." She smiled again, that hearth-warm smile, and I finally understood the horror of it, the true horror, which is that she meant it. She had been kind so long, and starved so long, that kindness and hunger had grown into the same tree. "Give me the warden, Jude Okafor, and I will put out your grandmother's lantern into your hands, whole and living, with a century of years still owed to her. She will see the stars come back. She will see *you*. The wards will rise, the border will hold, Lowmark will not freeze, and all it costs is one soul that was already sworn to the crown's service. A debt paid to the crown's own account." She leaned close, and her voice dropped to the register the dead use, the one that lives in the breastbone. "You have spent your whole life invisible, bone-carver. Unseen by the living, unclaimed by the dead. Here, I am offering you the one trade that makes you visible forever: the man who saved his grandmother. Everyone will know your name. Everyone below already does."

And God help me, the hall *breathed* with her. The dead leaned in. The lanterns swayed. Somewhere in the dark above, something like a star, something like an eye, waited to see what I would do.

Wren's hand was still in mine. Her brand had burned through her collar and I could smell the char of it, and she wasn't looking at the Queen. She was looking at the lantern. At my grandmother's guttering light. And her face had gone to the place it went on the bridge, the place of arithmetic, of a woman who had spent her whole life weighing lives against orders and had finally, terribly, found an equation she liked the answer to.

"It's a fair trade," Wren said quietly.

"It's not," I said.

"Jude—"

"*It's not.*" I turned on her, and the dead flinched back from the heat of it, and I didn't care. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand there with your burned-up oath and your guilt and decide you're the payment. I didn't carry you across the Thornfells to spend you. I didn't pay a bridge a memory of my mother so you could—" My voice broke. I hated it. I let it. "I have lost everyone I ever carved for. Everyone I ever loved is either dead or a light in a box. You are the first living thing that's walked beside me in years, and you don't get to volunteer to stop."

The hall was silent. The Queen watched us with her hearth-smile, patient as winter, and I understood, in that moment, that this was the bargain. Not Wren for my grandmother. The Queen was offering us each other, each of us as the price of the other, and whichever one of us broke first, she fed.

That was the horror under the seduction. That was the thorn in the hollow.

I turned back to the Queen, and I took my hand out of Wren's, and I made my voice as steady as a carving knife.

"Show me the lantern," I said. "Let me hold my grandmother. And then we'll talk about what I'm willing to carve for you—because I promise you, Majesty, there is a third arithmetic in this hall, and neither of us is the sum."

The Queen's smile widened by one degree, and the dead all around us drew breath they did not have, and somewhere high in the dark of the Court, something old and patient began, very softly, to laugh.

"Come," said the Hollow Queen, and gestured toward the dais, and the small worn lantern with its guttering amber star. "Hold her, then. And bone-carver—we shall see what your hands can do."
