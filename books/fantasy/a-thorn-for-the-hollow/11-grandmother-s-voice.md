## 11. Grandmother's Voice

The Queen's steward—she called it a *page*, which was like calling a landslide a footpath—led me down a corridor that had no business existing inside a mountain.

It was bone, but not the dead kind I worked with. This bone still remembered being alive. It had a warmth to it, a slow pulse underfoot like standing on the flank of something enormous and patient, and the walls were carved floor to ceiling with names in a script I didn't know but my hands did. My fingers kept drifting toward them as I walked. Tracing without touching. Reading without eyes.

*Family craft,* I thought, and my stomach turned over.

I've carved memorial bones since I was nine years old. My grandmother taught me—the real way, not the guild way. *The bone remembers what the flesh forgets, Jude. Your job isn't to make it pretty. Your job is to make it honest.* She'd been dead eleven years by then, or so I'd believed, and every stroke of my graver since had been a conversation with her absence. And here was an entire corridor of carving that moved the way mine moved, that breathed honesty the way she'd taught me, and it wasn't hers.

It was *ours*. Someone in my line had cut these walls. Maybe her. Maybe someone before her. The covenant thread didn't just run through my blood—it ran through my hands.

The page stopped at a door made of antler, branching and interlaced like frost given ambition, and gestured with a limb that had too many elbows to be polite about it.

"She is kept," it said, in a voice like wind through a winter field.

"Kept," I repeated. "That's a lovely word. You people have others? *Treasured*? *Stored*?"

The page tilted its head, which rotated further than heads should. "She dreams," it said, as if that answered anything, and dissolved into the wall.

Right. Fine. I stood in front of an antler door beneath a mountain full of starving ghosts, having walked into this whole mess on purpose for once in my life, and I was arguing semantics with the furniture.

I pushed the door open.

* * *

The room beyond was small. Domestic, almost—a low ceiling, a hearth with no fire that glowed anyway, a table with two chairs. Someone had furnished it the way you furnish a cell when you're lying to yourself about what it is.

And in the center, hanging from a hook of black iron, was a lantern.

Carved bone. Lattice-work sides, thorn-pattern border, a finial at the top shaped like a bird with folded wings. I knew the maker's mark on the base before I saw it clearly—I'd know that mark blind, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. A small crescent crossed by three lines. My family's sign. My grandmother's hand specifically; I'd seen it on a dozen pieces she made before I was born, the work from her middle years, when her cuts were surest and her patience longest.

Inside the lattice, where a candle should be, there was light. Not firelight. Not lamplight. It was the color of the moment just after sunset, that gold-violet that photographers chase and never catch, and it moved. It breathed. It pressed itself against the carved sides like a face against a window.

"Jude."

Her voice came through the bone the way voices come through my listening pieces—but warm, undimmed, *hers*, with that dry edge she used when she thought I was being an idiot. Which was often. Which was deserved, usually.

My legs stopped working somewhere around the third step. I sat down hard on the floor, in the doorway, because apparently I'd become the kind of man who collapses in thresholds now, and I said, very intelligently:

"You're supposed to be ash. Asham's Hollow. Eleven years ago. I held the funeral bone. I *carved* it."

"And a lovely job you made of it." The light shifted, gathered, and for a heartbeat I could almost see her—just the suggestion of a face in the glow, sharp-cheeked, one eyebrow permanently skeptical. "Though your linework's gotten sloppy at the joints. All that time alone and nobody to tell you when you rush."

Eleven years. Eleven years of talking to bones that carried echoes, impressions, the shape of the gone—and the actual woman, the voice I'd built half my life around missing, had been in a lantern under a mountain. "They took you. Before you died. The wardens came and—" I couldn't finish it. My mind kept snagging on the shape of it. My invisible grandmother, who went to market and haggled over turnips and told me my hair needed cutting.

"The night the first wards failed at Harrow Ford," she said. "Sixty-one years ago now, though they'll have fudged the records. Two crown men, very apologetic, carrying a piece of paper with the royal seal and a very old contract. Your grandfather signed it before you were born, love. Before your mother was born. He sold a soul of our line to the Court—not mine specifically, the contract says *a soul of the Okafor line, to be delivered when the anchor weakens.* They came to collect when the last one wore thin." A pause, and the light dimmed like a breath let out. "I was old. I was tired. I thought—I hoped they meant to kill me quickly. Instead they brought me here, and the Court drew off what it needed, a sip at a time, for sixty years. Sixty years of feeding the border so the crown wouldn't have to tell anyone what their wards eat."

I gripped the doorframe. The antler bit my palm. Everything I'd felt since the stars went out—terror, rage at the wardens, the awful recognition that the mountain knew my name—all of it reorganized itself around a new center, colder than any of it.

"They've been drinking my grandmother," I said. "For sixty years. To keep the lights on in Lowmark."

"To keep the lights on everywhere," she corrected gently. "The wards run the length of the Thornfells. Every pass, every crossing. And the stars, Jude. Don't leave out the stars."

"Why the stars?" I'd been turning that over since the night they vanished. Wards I understood—they were fences, basically, expensive ones. But the sky going dark in the same heartbeat meant whatever fed the wards fed something bigger.

She was quiet long enough that the light guttered. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—careful, the tone she used when she was about to hand me something heavy and wanted me to take it properly.

"Because of what the Court was. Before. Come sit down, love. My lantern-light gives out if I talk too long, and this part matters."

I sat in the second chair. Of course there were two chairs. Someone had set this room up for visits that never came, and I hated everyone responsible, including myself for taking eleven years.

"The Hollow Court is older than the kingdom," she said. "Older than the wards, older than Thornfell, older than any crown your history books flatter. It began as a refuge. Do you know what happens to the unburied, in your world? The drowned who are never found, the travelers lost in passes, the stillborn, the burned, all the dead who get no stone and no name spoken over them? They don't rest. They drift. Cold and nameless, fading by inches, and the fading hurts, Jude. It goes on and on."

"So someone built them a court," I said slowly. "Down here. Out of sight."

"A queen among the unburied took them in. Gathered them. Gave them halls and names and each other. That's all it was, at first—a shelter. And the living above never knew, except as stories, and mostly the stories were kind. Left offerings at hollow bridges. Spoke respectfully of the mountain." The light pulsed, warmer. "I remember it. Not firsthand—I was born long after—but the Court *remembers*. Memory is the only wealth we have down here, and the oldest souls remember everything. There were festivals. The dead danced, love. Did you know the dead can dance, when no one needs a piece of them to hold up a fence?"

I didn't answer. My throat had closed.

"And then the crowns came. Kingdoms rise and fall down here too—we watch them go by like weather. But one king, four hundred years back, had a problem: his wars made more unburied than his priests could bury, and restless dead don't stay politely underground. They walk into villages. They unsettle cattle. They appear at dinner tables asking why. So his wise men found the Court and proposed a covenant. Protection, they called it. The Court would take in the war-dead as always—but the crown would bind them. Bind them to the wards, to the borders, feed them into the fences as anchor-stuff. In exchange, the crown left the Court itself alone. Left the queen her halls."

"That's not protection," I said. "That's a tithe. That's—"

"It's what happens," she said, "when the strong offer shelter to the desperate and then discover the shelter can be farmed." The words came out flat and old. "The queen refused, at first. So they starved her court. Sealed the mountain roads. Waited. The unburied fade without... without *something*, Jude, I won't pretend I understand all of it, but the crown understood enough. Within a generation, the queen agreed to the binding—one soul, one anchor, renewed as needed, and her people fed from it in turn. She told herself it would be temporary. They all do."

The hearth-glow flickered. Her light was dimming; I could see it now, the way it took effort to hold shape, to hold voice, to be *her* for my benefit.

"Four hundred years of one soul at a time," I said. "Grandmother to grandmother. And the crown calls it warding."

"The crown calls it necessary," she said. "Which is worse. Nobody down here is a monster, Jude. That's what I need you to carry out of this room. The queen is cruel the way winter is cruel—because she is starving and vast and has forgotten any other season. The page who led you here was a midwife once; she can still tell you about every child she caught. The thing on Hollow Bridge that took your memory was a ferryman when the river had another name. They are *people*, worn down to their hungriest edges. And the crown made them this way, and then wrote stories about how they were always monsters, and charged your taxes to keep the fence up."

I looked at my hands. Carver's hands, scarred, steady. Hands that had spent twenty years believing the dead were things to be settled, quieted, put away—and learning, in one conversation, that the dead were a nation. A wronged one. And my family had been the tax collector.

"Grandfather sold us," I said. "One line of a contract, and every Okafor since was born already sold."

"He didn't think of it as selling," she said. "He thought of it as insurance—he was frightened, they were very persuasive, and the alternative was watching his children be taken instead. He was a coward in the ordinary human way. I've forgiven him, most days. You'll want to take longer about it. That's allowed." A pause, and when she spoke again the dry edge was back, gentler. "You came down here with the warden girl."

"Wren." My heart did something complicated. "You know about Wren."

"I'm a lantern, not a rock. The Court talks about nothing else—you brought a warden's soul into the deep places and half the hall thinks it's a feast day." The light shifted, and I swear the eyebrow arched. "And half thinks you've lost your mind. Both halves may be right. Listen to me, Jude. She offered herself yet?"

"How—"

"She has the look. Guilt sits on some people like a well-cut coat. Ask me how I know." The glow steadied, and for a moment her voice dropped all its armor. "Don't let her. Whatever bargain the queen offers—and she'll offer, she's starving, she'll dress it up as destiny—don't let that girl pay my ransom. I've been a captive soul for sixty years. I know exactly what we'd be doing to her. We would simply be becoming the crown."

"Then what do we do?" I leaned forward. "Tell me. You've had sixty years to think. The queen wants an anchor. The Court is starving. The wards are down, the stars are out, and above us the captain of the wardens is probably burning his way through the passes convinced he can fix the sky with fire. What's the move, Gran?"

Silence. The longest yet. Her light shrank to a coal, then steadied, and when she answered, she sounded very tired and very much like the woman who'd taught me to carve.

"I don't know," she said. "There. Now you've heard it. Sixty years in a lantern and I don't know. But I know what the queen won't tell you: the covenant is failing because it was always failing, slowly, the way any lie fails. One soul was never enough. It only ever looked like enough. And a lie this old, this big, with this many starving people leaning on it—" the light flared suddenly, urgent, "*Jude.*"

"What?"

"Someone's coming. Heavy boots, iron nails, and the page didn't stop them. Love, listen—the queen will offer you a trade, and when she does, look past the bargain to the *need underneath it*. The need is true even when the offer is a trap. And if the warden girl—"

The door burst inward.

Antler splintered across the floor, and in the doorway stood a warden in full plate I recognized the make of—Lowmark garrison, crown-issue, the kind they only send below ground when they intend to come back up without what they brought down. His visor was up. He looked past me, past the lantern, to the walls, and laughed once, short and joyless.

"Well," he said. "Found the heresy."

Behind him, up the corridor of carved and breathing bone, came the smell of pitch and the sound of torches being lit.
