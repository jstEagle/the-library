## 12. The Long Winter Falls

I learned to read the mountain from my mother, who learned it from a shepherd, which is to say I learned it wrong and had to unlearn half of it in the wardens' academy. But some lessons keep. One of them is this: snow that squeaks underfoot is safe snow. Snow that whispers is snow on the move. And a sky with no stars in it is not merely dark—it is *empty*, the way a room is empty after someone dies in it.

Above us, Lowmark was learning that lesson too.

We heard it in fragments, because news travels the smugglers' roads faster than it travels the king's, and faster still when it's bad. Refugees coming down the western passes brought it piece by piece like people carrying shards of a dropped jug: the frost line reaching Lowmark's wells in three days instead of thirty; the river at Harrow Ford frozen so deep the ferryman walked across it out of habit and found nothing to do; children born that winter who would never see a star except in the painted ceilings of chapels, where artists were suddenly very busy and very well paid.

Jude took it worse than I did. He heard the dead of Lowmark in his skull all day, every day, a tide going out and out, and he told me once—only once, at night, staring into our fire like it owed him money—that the dead were frightened. Not of dying. They were past that. Frightened of being forgotten, because without stars, he said, nobody remembered what the lights were *for*. The whole cosmology of grief was coming apart above us, and the living were too busy burning pews for warmth to notice.

Me, I took it the way I take most things: as a logistics problem with a body count.

"We need to move faster," I said.

"We're moving as fast as the mountain allows," Jude said. "Which, if you hadn't noticed, is grudging."

The Hollow Court lay two days below us still—two days by the smugglers' roads, which meant four by any honest map, and longer if the mountain decided it didn't like us. The roads had opinions. I'd stopped arguing with them around the time a switchback we'd used an hour earlier simply wasn't there anymore, replaced by a cliff face wearing the switchback's memory like a poorly fitted coat.

"Your captain," Jude said, carefully, the way you'd step onto ice you'd already fallen through once. "How long before she works out where we're going?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Tam Veylan doesn't work things out," I said. "She decides, and then the world arranges itself to confirm her. She decided weeks ago that destroying the Court restores the wards. Everything since has been her collecting evidence on behalf of her decision."

"And is she wrong?"

That was the question, wasn't it. The Queen had told us the Court was starving, sustained by a lie, my grandmother's stolen soul spinning in its lantern like a fly in amber. But Tam had spent twenty years believing the wards were walls between the living and everything hungry, and I'd spent eight of those years believing it with her, and beliefs like that don't die quietly. They die like wards fall: all at once, taking the sky with them.

"She's wrong about the cure," I said. "She might be right about the disease. And she's got three hundred wardens, fire-oil, and ward-breakers, and she does not require being right to be dangerous."

Jude nodded slowly. He'd gone thin since the bridge. We both had—the toll had taken a memory of my mother and Wren Ashvale's oath of office, and I kept reaching for the oath the way you reach for a stair that isn't there. My mother I could barely feel the absence of, which felt like a second theft. But the oath—I'd built myself out of that thing. Every morning for eight years I'd woken and it had been there, humming, telling me what I was. Now mornings started blank, and I had to decide who I was from scratch, every day, like a woman with no trade and no warrant standing on a frozen mountainside with a smuggler.

Most days I decided I was still deciding. It was honest, at least.

* * *

The refugees started passing us on the fourth day.

That shouldn't have been possible—we were above the tree line on a road that officially didn't exist—but the passes were sealed, the low roads choked with crown checkpoints, and desperate people find the ways smugglers make. They came up through the high notch in families, in strings, in silences. A woman carrying a loom shuttle and nothing else. An old man leading two goats and a boy who looked at Jude's pack of listening bones with the flat hunger of someone who'd stopped believing in anything that couldn't be eaten.

They didn't ask us for help, which somehow made it worse. They'd passed the stage where help seemed like a real category of experience. They just trudged past our fire, nodded the way you nod at strangers at a funeral, and kept climbing toward the southern passes and whatever rumor had promised them warmth there.

One girl, maybe ten, stopped. She looked at me—at the defector's brand on my wrist, which I'd stopped bothering to cover, because covering it required a sleeve and sleeves required caring—and said, "You're the ones the criers are talking about."

"Criers?" Jude asked.

"The wardens." She said it the way children say words that have grown teeth in their mouths. "They say a bone-witch and a broken warden broke the stars, and there's a price for turning us in, and my da says the price is a lie but my brother says prices are never lies."

"What do you say?" I asked.

She considered me with the terrible seriousness of the young. "I say if you broke the stars you wouldn't be sitting in the cold eating hardtack."

Then she ran to catch her father, and I sat there feeling seen by a ten-year-old, which is a particular humiliation.

"They're offering bounties," Jude said quietly, when the last of them had passed. "On us."

"On the idea of us. The actual us is a detail they'll discover when they collect." I turned the hardtack over. It had achieved a texture geologists would have respected. "Tam's smart enough to know a story needs villains more than a manhunt needs accuracy. Bone-witch and broken warden. It scans. People will sing it."

"You were her best tracker."

"I was." I let that sit a moment. "Which means she knows exactly how I think, and I know exactly how she does, and one of us is better rested and better provisioned and hasn't been paying tolls in memory. Guess which."

Jude, to his credit, didn't try to reassure me. That was one of the things I'd come to value about him—he understood that comfort freely given is sometimes just a way of asking you to stop being accurate.

Instead he said, "If she reaches the gate before us—"

"She'll burn it." I'd watched Tam breach a smuggling den in Kettle Row once, three years back. She hadn't breached it. She'd unmade it, with fire-oil and patience, and stood outside in the smoke with an expression I'd mistook then for grim duty and recognized now as something closer to worship. Tam Veylan believed the world was a wound and wardens were the stitching, and anything beneath the stitches was rot by definition. The Hollow Court, starving and strange and full of unburied dead, was not a patient to her.

It was an infection. And you don't bargain with infections.

* * *

We came down off the high shoulder two days later and saw the valley, and I made the mistake of looking north instead of south.

South was the mountain's root-gate, a seam of faint red light in the rock like a hearth behind a wall. Good. Destination. Progress.

North, past the ridgelines, past everything, was the sky. And the sky was doing something it had never done in my lifetime, which was nothing at all. No stars. No moon—I don't know where the moon went; nobody did; the Queen had gone quiet and strange when Jude asked, which from her was practically a confession. Just a black so total it looked solid, a lid, and beneath it the fires of the refugee columns strung along the passes like embers spilled from a grate.

Homesickness hit me then, sudden and physical, like a fist under the ribs. Not for Lowmark exactly—for the *idea* of it. For the town at dusk with lamps coming on and the wards humming their old knuckle-song overhead and everyone pretending the light was ordinary. For a sky with holes punched in it where the dead of the world hung shining, proof that love left a mark somewhere permanent. I'd spent eight years as a warden believing I was part of the machinery that kept that sky lit. Turns out the machinery ran on a grandmother's stolen soul and I'd been helping guard the theft.

"Hey." Jude touched my elbow. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting fires," I said. "There are too many of them and they're all in the wrong places."

He followed my gaze north. His face did the thing it did when the dead pressed close—that tightening around the eyes, the look of a man listening to several conversations at once and finding none of them polite.

"They're singing," he said. "The dead in the passes. The newly dead. There are—" He stopped. Started again. "There are more every hour, Wren. Cold takes them and they wake up confused and the others try to show them how to be dead and nobody knows the songs properly anymore because the Court's been hoarding them for a century."

"Can you help them?"

"With what? I'm a bone-carver, not a shepherd. I can hear them. Hearing isn't the same as knowing what to say." He rubbed his hands together, and I noticed again how the knuckles had split from cold and carving and wouldn't heal right. "My gran would've known. She always said the dead are just people who've lost their road home. You give them directions. You don't have to walk them there."

"Is that in the family craft, or is that just something kind?"

"Knowing my family? Probably both. Kindness was the Okafor curse. Runs worse than the hearing."

Almost, almost, I smiled. Then a sound rolled down the valley from the north, and neither of us smiled at anything for a while.

It was horn-call. Three notes, repeated. Wardens' signal, the old pattern, the one from before the academy standardized the codes—which meant Tam herself was blowing it, because Tam didn't trust anything younger than she was. Three rising notes. In the old tongue of the border service it meant: *found sign. Closing.*

"They're in the high valley," I said, my whole body going into the flat efficient calm I'd trained into myself, the calm that had once been an oath and was now just a habit with nowhere to live. "Half a day behind us. Maybe less if they've got a guide."

"A guide? Nobody knows these roads."

"Somebody sold them, or somebody's dead and talking, or somebody's following the refugees and letting the refugees do the dying part of mountaineering." I was already kicking snow over the fire. "Either way, we've lost our margin. We walk tonight."

"In the dark."

"There isn't any dark anymore," I said, before I could stop myself. "There's only less black."

Jude looked at me for a moment, and then he shouldered his pack, and we went down toward the red seam in the mountain with the horn calling at our backs like something patient.

* * *

We walked most of the night, and I'll tell you what I told no one: I was glad of it. Walking in starless cold is simple. You put your foot on the ground the ground tells you is there, and you keep doing it, and the part of you that remembers your mother's face or your own name gets to rest. Grief keeps poorly in motion.

It was near dawn, grey and grudging, that we found the shrine.

Every smugglers' road has them—little stone hollows where the runners leave offerings, candles and copper and once, memorably, an entire wheel of cheese. This one was older than smuggling. The stones were fitted without mortar, and the niche held not coins but bones, small ones, birds mostly, arranged in a spiral. Jude stopped as though he'd walked into a wall made of memory.

"This is Okafor work," he said. "Old Okafor work. The spiral-cut on the wing joints—my gran carved like this. Her mother before her." He reached out but didn't touch, hovering his fingers over the spiral like a man warming himself at a distance from a fire. "The roads knew my family. Before the covenant, maybe, the Okafors walked these roads. Carved for them."

"Then the mountain owes you hospitality," I said. "Let's collect."

But the horn sounded again, closer, and this time it was answered—from ahead of us, downslope, toward the gate. Two calls. Two parties. Tam had split her force, or she'd guessed the gate was our destination and sent a fast company around by the eastern shelf while the main body pushed our trail.

I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for two days. The main body was half a day behind. The eastern company was perhaps six hours from the seam of red light, and we were perhaps five, on legs that had eaten hardtack and guilt for a week. If we pushed, we'd arrive exhausted, minutes or hours ahead of ward-breakers and fire-oil, into a court of starving dead with a plan that consisted, in its entirety, of *talk to the Queen and hope*.

"Jude."

"I heard." He was pale, and not from cold. "If she fires the gate while we're inside—"

"The Court burns. Your grandmother burns. Every unburied soul in there goes wherever souls go when there's nowhere left." I looked at the little spiral of bird bones, at the shrine some dead-and-gone Okafor had built for travelers who were probably also dead and gone, and something in me set like mortar. "So we don't get fired upon. We get ahead. We get in, and we close the door, and we finish this before Tam finishes it for us."

He looked at me a long moment. "You know what she'll call you. When she takes us. What the charges will be."

"Treason, heresy, and consorting with the Hollow," I recited. "Standard set. There'll be paperwork." Then, quieter: "She'll be right about one of them. I did consort with the Hollow. I'd do it again."

"That's not the reassurance you think it is."

"It's not meant to be reassuring. It's meant to be true. True things are in short supply lately; I'm rationing mine."

And Jude laughed—a short, cracked, real laugh, the first in days—and the sound of it went up the frozen valley like a coin thrown in a well, and far above us, in the lid-black sky where stars should have been, I would have sworn something shifted. Like a sleeper turning over. Like a promise being remembered.

We ran for the gate.

Behind us the horns called, patient as winter, and ahead the mountain's roots glowed red as a hearth where someone was either keeping warm—

—or feeding the fire.
