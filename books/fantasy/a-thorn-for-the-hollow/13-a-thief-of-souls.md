## 13. A Thief of Souls

There is a rule among smugglers that my mother taught me before she taught me letters: *when the front door is guarded, the back door is a rumor.* The Hollow Court had exactly one front door—a gate of black stone carved with names nobody living could read—and Wren and I had come through it like invited guests, which was its own kind of trick. But courts keep their treasures close to their thrones, and thrones are always surrounded by more eyes than doors.

So I needed rumors.

I spent two days finding them. That sounds like a complaint; it wasn't. It was the first time since the stars went out that I felt like myself. Bone-carving is honest work but solitary, and smuggling, whatever else you say about it, is a *trade*—you learn to read people, to read rooms, to read the pause between two sentences where a lie lives. The Hollow Court turned out to be full of pauses.

The dead here were not like the dead at home. Lowmark's ghosts clung to places—a bridge, a well, the corner of a market where somebody had once laughed too hard. The Court's dead drifted through its bone-pale halls like courtiers without appointments, beautiful in the way frost on a window is beautiful, and just as fragile-looking. They didn't speak much. They *inclined*, the way grass inclines to wind, whenever the Queen passed, and after a while I noticed they inclined slightly, faintly, toward me too.

That was the rumor I needed: they could feel me. A living soul who hears the dead, walking through a court of the unburied—of course they leaned toward me. Of course they wanted to be near the one thing in this mountain that still had a pulse.

I used it shamelessly.

* * *

The lantern hung in the Vault of Names, three halls deep from the Bargaining Hall, past a corridor whose walls were set with niches like a dovecote, each niche holding a small object instead of an urn—a button, a ring, a child's wooden bird. Things the unburied had carried when they died, or wished they'd carried. I tried not to look at them. Looking made it harder to think like a thief and easier to think like a man about to do something unforgivable for very good reasons.

The vault itself had no lock. That worried me more than any lock would have. Instead it had a threshold of white bone laid into the floor, polished smooth by centuries of feet, and across the doorway hung what I can only describe as a curtain of hush. Sound went in and did not come out. When Wren stood at the entrance and said my name, her voice fell flat as a coin dropped on cloth.

"It knows breath," she said quietly, when we retreated down the corridor. "Warden training says the old vaults ward against the living. Anything with a heartbeat crossing that line rings like a dinner bell."

"Anything with a heartbeat," I repeated. "You say that like it's negotiable."

She gave me one of her looks—the flat, measuring look she'd aimed at me over the barrels of a smuggler's cart the first time we met, back when she was trying to arrest me and I was pretending to be a very boring man. She'd gotten better at hiding what was underneath it. Or maybe I'd gotten better at reading it. Underneath, these days, there was something that might have been fear for me, if I let myself flatter it.

"You're not seriously—" she started.

"I'm a smuggler, Wren. Heartbeats are negotiable. They're just expensive." I sat down on the cold floor with my back against a niche and pulled my kit out of my coat: needles, wax thread, a sliver of riverbone, and a twist of dried mothwing that smelled like my grandmother's cedar chest. "There's an art called the Still Trade. Dead men's roads, dead men's bridges—you cross them by being dead-adjacent. Slowing the blood. Not stopping it." I met her eyes. "Stopping it is not on the menu."

"How slow?"

"Slow enough that a threshold built to hear hearts hears something else instead. Something quiet. Like the Court itself." I threaded the needle. "The dead here lean on me because I'm the loudest quiet thing they've found. I'm going to make that literal."

"And how long does a man stay dead-adjacent before it stops being adjacent?"

I smiled at her, because smiling at Wren Ashvale when she was frightened for you was the only way I knew to keep her from doing something noble. "Long enough. Keep count for me. If I take too long, come in and make a lot of noise."

"That's your contingency? Noise?"

"Loudest quiet thing they've found," I said again, and began.

* * *

The Still Trade is mostly listening. People assume it's about the body—breath control, stillness, all that—but the body follows the ear the way water follows slope. I closed my eyes and listened to the Court the way I'd once listened to a warden patrol's campfire: not to the loud parts, but to the rhythm underneath. The mountain had a pulse. Slow, vast, patient—it was the Queen's attention moving through her halls, the way a spider's attention moves along a web, and it beat perhaps once every hundred heartbeats of mine.

I matched my blood to the spaces between.

It felt like drowning in reverse. My heartbeat stretched, and stretched, and the cold came up through the floor and into the marrow of me, and somewhere very far away Wren was counting under her breath, and somewhere even farther my grandmother's soul burned in its lantern of carved bone like a candle in a church I'd never been allowed inside.

I crossed the threshold of white bone and nothing rang.

Inside, the hush swallowed everything—even my own footsteps, even the sound of my own blood, which was nearly nothing anyway. The Vault of Names was smaller than the corridors had promised: a round chamber lit by no flame I could find, walls lined floor to ceiling with shelves, and on every shelf a vessel. Lanterns. Dozens of them. Some of glass gone green with age. Some of horn. Some of clay with holes punched in patterns—constellations, I realized, or what constellations used to be before the stars went dark.

And in the center, hanging from a chain of knucklebones, the lantern I had come for.

It was my family's work. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting: the grain of the bone chosen so the natural channel of it ran like a river through the carving, the border pattern of thorn and hollow that my grandmother had put on everything she ever touched, the little maker's mark—a bird, a bridge, a star—that meant *Okafor, and may it outlive you.* Her soul moved inside it. I couldn't see it clearly, but I could feel it, the way you feel a fire in the next room, warmth arriving ahead of light. Ninety years in a box, feeding wards she'd never seen, in a country that had forgotten her name, and she was still warm.

"Right," I whispered, and the hush ate the word.

Every smuggler's trick I knew arranged itself in my head like tools on a bench. Distraction first: I'd brought three of the Court's drifting dead to mind on the way in—I couldn't command them, but I could *call*, the way you call to a dog across a field, and they would drift toward wherever my attention pointed, and attention in this place was a currency. Then substitution: take the lantern, leave another in its place, because a guard notices a gap faster than a forgery. I had a horn lantern from my kit, plain, empty—but empty was wrong, emptiness would show, so I'd packed it with a pinch of grave-dirt from Lowmark and one of my own finger-bones' worth of shavings, carved off over the years for luck. Enough life in it to cast a shadow on whatever sense kept these shelves.

Then speed. Speed was the part I'd always been good at.

I lifted the chain off its hook with both hands, careful of the knucklebones—they clicked together with a sound like teeth—and the warmth of her rolled up my arms and into my chest, and for one heartbeat I stopped being a thief and became a grandson holding the last of his grandmother, and I nearly wept into the hush where nobody could hear it.

Then I set the false lantern on the shelf, adjusted it half a finger so its shadow lay the same way, and walked out of the Vault of Names at the pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be.

* * *

Two halls. That was all that stood between me and the side passage Wren had found—an old smuggler's road, actually, a crack in the mountain that predated the Court, which I found darkly funny: the crown's covenant and my grandmother's captivity, both built on top of the routes my people had always used to move unseen. Two halls of drifting dead, and then the crack, and then sky.

The dead let me pass. Better than let me—they *made room*. It was the leaning again, magnified now that I carried the warmest thing in the mountain against my chest. They curved away from me like iron filings from a magnet's wrong end, or toward me, I couldn't tell, opening a lane through the hall of niches as neatly as a crowd parts for a coffin.

I was twenty steps from the crack when the hall got brighter.

Not torchlight. There was no torchlight anywhere in the Court; everything glowed from some source the architecture refused to admit to. This was different. This was the light of attention, the spider feeling the web, and it came down the corridor behind me without hurry, without sound, without anything so crude as pursuit.

I ran anyway. Smugglers run; it's in the contract.

I got eleven steps.

Then the hall was no longer a hall. The niches, the bone-white walls, the crack in the mountain—all of it folded away like a map closing, and I was standing in the Bargaining Hall with the lantern still warm against my chest and my legs refusing, on very short notice, to be legs.

The Hollow Queen sat on no throne. She never did; thrones were apparently beneath her. She simply occupied the center of the hall the way winter occupies a valley, and her face was the most beautiful thing in the mountain and the least comforting, and she looked at the bundle in my arms with an expression I will spend the rest of my life trying not to remember.

"Well," she said. "A thief."

"Repossessor," I said. My voice worked. That was something. "The goods were stolen first."

"From whom?" she asked softly. "Stolen from *whom*, bone-carver? Tell me the crime as you understand it, and I will show you the ledger."

"The crown sold my grandmother," I said. "A century ago. Fed her into your covenant to light their precious wards, and everyone above forgot her, and you—" I hugged the lantern closer. "You kept her. You've been burning her. Slowly, all these years, like a lamp in a window. So yes. I'm taking her back."

The Queen tilted her head. Around us, the Court's dead had gathered—not crowding, never crowding, but present, a horizon of pale faces, and I noticed for the first time how many of them were small. How many came up to my elbow. How many had the proportions of children, rendered in that beautiful fading frost-light, and how none of them had made a sound since I'd entered this mountain.

"You believe this is a court of jailers," the Queen said. "Come and see the jail."

She turned, and the hall opened for her, and because I had no better option and because my legs had remembered their trade, I followed.

* * *

She took me down—not deep, the Court didn't seem to have depth so much as *inward*—to a long low room I would later fail to describe to anyone, because it looked like a barn, or a schoolhouse, or a hospital ward, depending on which second you looked at it. Rows of pallets that were not pallets. And on them, lying in attitudes I recognized from my work—from every memorial bone I had ever carved for a child taken too soon—lay the smallest of the unburied.

They were fading. I don't mean dimming; I mean *fading*, the way ink fades, the way a name fades from a stone when rain has years to work. Their edges had gone soft. One near the front—a girl with riverweed still braided in her hair, drowned, I thought, drowned like the boy I'd carved for in Lowmark a lifetime ago—turned her head when I came in, and where her cheek should have had definition there was only suggestion, watercolor, a face someone had started and the world had forgotten to finish.

"We were a refuge," the Queen said, and her voice had changed. Less winter in it. More weight. "Before the covenant. Do you understand what the Hollow Court *was*, Jude Okafor? Every soul that died unmourned, unburied, unnamed—the stillborn, the lost at sea, the ones the wars chewed up and spat into ditches. The living could not carry them, so we carried them. A court of the unburied, and a queen to keep the rolls. And the crown came to us with a bargain: give us one soul, a great soul, willingly given—or so they wrote in their ledgers—and the mountain's strength will hold your borders, and your borders will hold your dead, and everyone rests."

"And you took it," I said. My throat was tight. The girl with the riverweed was looking at the lantern in my arms. All the children were looking at the lantern. Like plants looking at the sun.

"She was willing," the Queen said. "That is the part you will hate most, so I will say it plainly and once: your grandmother came to us knowing. A carver of bones, a hearer of the dead, and she saw what would happen to this court without an anchor—the slow fade you are looking at right now—and she chose. The crown lied about the willingness afterward, papered it, made it a trade in their histories. But she chose. And her choice is the only reason there is a lantern to steal."

The lantern was very heavy suddenly. Or my arms were very weak.

"If you take her," the Queen said, "the wards stay dead, the stars stay dark, and this court begins to gutter like a lamp out of oil. These—" she gestured, and the gesture took in every small faded soul in that long low room— "will thin. Have you ever watched a ghost die, bone-carver? It is not dramatic. It is a draft through a window. One day there is a shape, and the next day the curtain moves and there isn't."

The girl with riverweed sat up. It cost her visibly, like an old woman rising in bad weather, and she crossed the room to me with steps that left less impression than they should have, and she looked up at the lantern the way children look at bread.

"Is that the lady?" she asked.

Her voice went through me like a hook. In all my weeks in this mountain I hadn't heard the Court's dead speak above a whisper, and here was a drowned girl asking after my grandmother in a voice worn thin as paper.

"She's my grandmother," I said.

"The lady tells us stories," the girl said. "Through the bone. When the mountain is quiet. She says hold on, she says the carver's coming, she says—" the girl's fading face creased, effortfully, into something like hope— "she said you'd have her hands."

I am going to write down what happened next because I promised myself I would not lie in this account, even where it shames me: I almost did it anyway. I stood in that long low room with theft in my arms and righteousness curdling in my stomach, and I thought, *they're already fading, they're already dying, one more loss won't change the sum*—and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, exactly how easy it is to become the crown. To do arithmetic with souls. To decide that the fading children of a monster are acceptable losses in the story where I am the hero.

My grandmother had held this whole court on her shoulders for ninety years, and her plan—for me, the plan she'd stitched through the bone of her lantern, *the carver's coming*—had apparently never been *steal me back and damn the rest.*

It had been *bring him his hands.*

The Queen watched me arrive at it. She had the patience of the very long dead, and something in her face that was not triumph. It looked, terribly, like relief.

"So," she said. "Now that you know the price of the door you picked—shall we go back to the bargaining hall, thief? Or would you rather hear the rest of the ledger?" Her gaze slid, deliberate and cold, past my shoulder, toward the corridor where a warden with a defector's brand was almost certainly already looking for me.

"There is," she said, "a matter of souls stronger than my anchor. And what I would ask of yours."
