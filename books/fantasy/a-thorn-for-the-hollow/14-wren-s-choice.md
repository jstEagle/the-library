## 14. Wren's Choice

I had decided sometime between the third and fourth hour of lying awake on cold stone, listening to the Court breathe.

That was the thing about the Hollow Court nobody warned you about in warden training—mostly because there was no training for this, because the wardens' manuals described the mountain as *inert geological feature, warded perimeter, no further interest*—it breathed. The whole cavern rose and fell on some slow tide I couldn't name. The veins of light in the walls pulsed like a pulse that had forgotten what it was counting. And underneath it all, everywhere, the dead. Not screaming the way the dead screamed above ground when the wards fell. Murmuring. Waiting. A congregation with nowhere to kneel.

So I lay there in the dark of our borrowed cell—borrowed being a generous word; *granted under sufferance* was closer—and did arithmetic.

One soul anchors the covenant. One soul has anchored it for a hundred years: Jude's grandmother, traded before he was born, kept in a lantern of her own family's craft. The Queen wanted a replacement. She had named her price with the serenity of a merchant who knows you cannot walk out of her market: the warden. Me. My soul, "dense with oath," she'd called it, the way you'd praise a cut of meat.

And I had spent four hours doing the only sum that made sense. Grandmother Okafor had been sold by people who loved her, or at least by people who needed something badly enough to dress the sale up as necessity. I owed the dead a smuggler's life—I'd reported Tomas Vey myself, watched them hang him from the Lowmark gate, filed the paperwork after. I owed the wards a century of stolen warmth. I owed Jude a debt so large it didn't have a shape anymore, just weight.

One Wren Ashvale against all of that. It wasn't even a bargain. It was rounding down.

The trouble was that Jude was going to be insufferable about it.

* * *

He found me at dawn—or whatever passed for dawn down here, the veins brightening from ember to blood-orange while somewhere far above a real sun came up over a starving town. He came into the gallery where I was sitting with my back against the wall, sharpening a knife I had no intention of using on anything but my own stupidity, and he took one look at my face and said:

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've been sitting there for an hour looking like someone rehearsing a speech. You do your jaw thing." He pointed at my chin with a bone stylus, because of course he carried one everywhere now, like a priest carries his god. "You're going to offer yourself."

The dead in the walls rustled, interested. Traitors, every one of them.

"Jude."

"Don't." He sat down across from me, cross-legged, deliberate, the way he did everything—as if the floor were a workshop bench and he was arranging his tools. "Don't give the speech. I've heard the speech. Everyone who ever traded away somebody they loved gave that speech. *It's necessary. It's the only way. I'm the right one for it.*" His voice cracked on the last word, which he covered badly, which meant he wasn't covering it at all. He never covered anything well. That was the whole problem with him.

"It is necessary," I said quietly. "You know it is."

"What I know," he said, "is that you've spent three days down here not sleeping, doing sums. I can hear you doing them. Your lips move."

"That's—"

"And I know the answer you keep getting is wrong, because you're bad at arithmetic. You always were. You counted my smuggling runs wrong twice."

"Once. And that was an honest error."

"It was flattery," he said. "You were trying to get me to talk. It worked. Doesn't make you good with numbers."

The gallery we were in had once been, I think, a place of honor—the ceiling ribbed with carved stone like the inside of a throat, the walls hung with niches where the unburied dead had been given names instead of graves. Now half the names were worn smooth. Half the niches were empty, their occupants faded past the point where even the Court could hold them. Starving children, the Queen had called them, and shown us, and I still couldn't scrape the image off the inside of my eyelids.

"You're not trading yourself for my grandmother," Jude said. "That's the part you keep leaving out of the sums. You're trading yourself to make yourself feel less like a warden. There's a difference, and it matters."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "Most true things aren't."

I stood up because staying seated felt too much like losing, and I said, "Your grandmother has been in a lantern for sixty years. Sixty years, Jude. I've read the reports—I wrote reports about the wards, I audited the covenant-stone's draw rates, I knew to the decimal how much soul it consumed per season and I signed my name under the numbers and thought *efficient.* She's been the fuel line for every warm hearth in Lowmark since before you were born, and you want to leave her there because—because what? Because you'd miss me?"

"Yes," he said simply, and it landed harder than any argument could have. "Because I'd miss you. Because she would be furious. Because—" He stopped, and pressed his knuckles to his mouth, and I watched him decide whether to say the next thing. He decided. He always did. "Because I already lost everyone else to other people's necessities. My mother to the river. Tomas to your report. Her to theirs. I am not giving you to mine."

The dead murmured. Somewhere above us, faint as a memory of weather, the Queen was listening—I could feel her attention the way you feel frost forming on a window.

"So what's the plan?" I asked. "Steal her? You tried. You got caught. She showed you the fading ones and you came back gray as ash and didn't speak for a day."

"I'll try again."

"She'll catch you again."

"Then I'll try better."

"That's not a plan, that's a temper with a schedule."

He laughed—one short, sharp note with no humor in it—and then his face set, and I realized we were standing two feet apart in a dead queen's gallery, having the fight we'd been circling since the bridge, maybe since the door of his workshop, maybe since the night on the border road when she let him go and couldn't explain why even to herself.

"Say it plainly," he said. "Tell me exactly what you're proposing."

"I propose to take her place." Plainly. Cleanly. Like a report. "My soul for hers. The Queen said warden souls run dense. Mine's been soaked in oath for fifteen years—it should anchor the covenant for another century at least. Your grandmother goes free. You go home. Lowmark gets its stars back eventually, or enough of them, and nobody else pays."

"And you?"

"I pay," I said. "That's the job. That's what I should have been paying all along. I hung Tomas Vey, Jude. I helped feed the machine that ate your grandmother. I put the brand on my own arm when I defected, and the brand burns every hour down here because the mountain knows what I am. This isn't sacrifice. It's accounting."

"Accounting," he repeated softly. "Gods. You sound like the crown."

That one went in deep, and I let him see that it had, because if we were going to wound each other we might as well be accurate.

"You don't get to refuse for me," I said. "That's the whole point of offering. It's mine to give."

"And it's mine to refuse," he said. "You're not a tithe, Wren. You're not a line item. You're—" He stopped again, jaw working.

"Say it," I whispered, though I don't know why I wanted him to, except that if he said it then it existed outside both of us, and things that exist can be lost, and I needed very much to lose something specific rather than everything at once. Duty makes terrible geometry out of a person.

"You're the first living person who's heard the dead with me," he said instead, "and listened like it mattered, and not like it was a symptom. Do you know what that's worth? No. Of course you don't. You think you're worth a fuel line."

"I think I'm worth exactly what I cost."

"Well," said the Queen.

We both spun. She hadn't been there. I would have sworn on my mother's grave—which was an actual grave, one of the few decent ones—that the gallery had been empty. But there she was at the far end, seated on nothing, wearing her court like a cloak: the unburied dead arranged around her in ranks, translucent and patient and hungry. She looked like a woman carved out of the moment just before crying, if the carver had been very old and very kind and had then waited a thousand years to see how it turned out.

She looked amused. That was the worst part. Amused, and tired, the way you get tired at the end of a long performance you no longer believe in.

"The accounting metaphor," she said, "is very popular among the living. They come down here with ledgers in their eyes. Debts and payments, balances and costs." She drifted closer; the dead parted for her like water remembering it was once a river. "Do you know what I was, before the crown taught me arithmetic?"

"A refuge," Jude said. "For the unburied."

"For the unburied," she agreed, and her gaze settled on me, and it was like being read by winter. "And do you know what a refuge requires, warden? Not a ledger. An ear. Someone who hears who has arrived, and remembers them, and says their name back to the dark until the dark learns to say it too." She turned her head toward Jude, slow as a door. "His grandmother told me about him, you know. In the early years, when she still spoke. *My grandson hears the dead,* she said. *He thinks it's a curse. He hides it in bone.*"

Jude had gone very still. Stillness was his weapon and his wound; he'd learned it young, invisibility as armor. "You talked to her?"

"I keep her. Keeping and talking are different trades." The Queen lifted one hand, and the nearest niche—a worn one, its name scraped smooth—seemed to lean toward her. "Here is what your grandmother said, warden, when the crown's men came with their covenant and their lantern: *Someone must listen to them, or they will forget themselves, and forgetting is the only death the dead truly fear.* The crown heard *someone must be kept.* I heard *listen.* We were both wrong, and we were both right, and the result was a hundred years of one woman's soul stretched thin over a lie."

"Then tell us the third option," I said, because I could feel it coming, the way you feel a bridge groan before it gives. "There's a third option. You've been holding it back like a card."

"There is always a third option," the Queen said. "The first two are only ever offered to people who haven't looked sideways yet. The covenant wants a soul that stands between the living and the dead. A soul dense with oath. A soul that *hears.*" Her smile was small and terrible. "Not a captive, warden. Your oaths are secondhand—you inherited them from men who inherited them from liars. But him."

The gallery went quiet in a way that wasn't quiet at all. Every dead thing in the walls leaned in. I felt them like a change in pressure, like the moment before a storm breaks over the Thornfells.

"He offered once already," the Queen said softly. "On the border road, when the wards fell. Did you know that, warden? The mountain called his name and he answered *take me instead of them.* The dead heard. They've been following him ever since—not hunting. Following. Migrating birds know a good roost when they find one." She spread her hands, and the veins of light in the rock pulsed brighter, once, like a heart deciding. "A living listener. Not drained. Anchored. Not imprisoned—rooted. The difference between a lantern and a tree. He stays. He hears. He gives the dead what your crown stole from them, which is witness, and the covenant feeds not on his soul but on the hearing itself. It is a poorer meal than a captive spirit." Her tired amusement deepened into something almost gentle. "But it is an honest one, and honesty, I am told, is fashionable again."

"No," I said.

"Wren," Jude started.

"*No.*" I turned on him and the fear in me came out as anger, because that's what fear does when it's been trained by the crown. "You don't get to volunteer either! You just spent an hour telling me I'm not allowed to trade myself, and now you'll—what? Root yourself into this mountain like a fence post? Hear the dead for a hundred years? You told me you wanted quiet. You told me the gift was a curse, that you carved bones to keep it leashed. And she's offering you a lifetime leash and calling it a tree."

"A tree isn't a leash."

"It is if you're nailed to it!"

We were shouting. Down in the galleries below, the dead had begun to gather—not menacingly, worse than that: attentively, faces tilted up like an audience at a play they'd waited a century to see. The Queen watched us with her chin on her hand, and I swear to every star currently missing from the sky, she was enjoying herself.

"You would stay here alone," I said, lower now, because the shouting had burned off and left the truth exposed underneath, ugly and bare. "Alone, Jude. In the dark. With them. Forever. That's not a refuge, that's a sentence with better furniture."

"I wouldn't be alone." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and certain, and he said the thing, the real thing, the one I'd been dodging since the bridge: "I'd be waiting. There's a difference. Ask any smuggler—ask any warden. You spend your whole career learning the routes. Come learn this one."

"That is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me," I said, "and it's also insane, and I hate you."

"I know," he said. "I hate you too. You tried to arrest me twice."

"Three times. You forgot the ferry."

"The ferry doesn't count. You bought me a drink afterward."

"To loosen your tongue! It's called interrogation!"

"It's called *buying me a drink,* Wren."

Above us, the Queen began, very quietly, to laugh. It wasn't a cruel sound. It was the laugh of someone who has watched a great many tragedies and finally, after centuries, been handed a comedy—and I realized with a jolt that the tragedy and the comedy were the same story, and the only thing that had changed was whether the two idiots in the gallery kept choosing duty over each other.

"Oh, stop," she said, wiping at an eye that may not have had a tear duct in a thousand years. "Both of you. Before you argue yourselves into the old covenant out of sheer stubbornness. Yes—he anchors. No—he does not do it alone, and no, he does not do it forever, and no, warden, you do not get to buy your guilt with your soul, it's counterfeit currency and I've seen it devalue empires." She rose, and the whole Court rose with her, the dead straightening in their niches, and for the first time since we'd descended I saw her look not like a queen but like a woman with a plan she'd been assembling for a hundred years, laying out the final pieces. "The captain is coming. Fire in the roots, ward-breakers on the stair. I can feel her torches the way you feel a toothache. Whatever we build, we build tonight, and we build it fast, and we build it out of what we actually have."

"Which is?" I asked.

"A thief," said the Queen, nodding at Jude, "who owes me one more theft. A warden," nodding at me, "who is going to stand at his back and mean it this time. And a listener," and here her voice dropped into something older than the mountain, "who must carve a covenant that has never been carved before, from materials that do not exist yet, before the fire arrives."

Jude's hand went to the stylus at his belt. I watched his thumb move over it the way a man touches a rosary, or a blade.

"What materials?" he asked.

The Queen smiled, and behind her, faint as breath on glass, a scatter of light moved through the dark—small, silver, familiar, wrong, impossible, *his.*

"Come and see," she said. "Bring your grandmother's pieces. Bring your blood. And bring her—" a tilt of the ancient head toward me, "—because whatever you carve, someone is going to need to hold the line while you hold the knife."

Far above us, muffled through a mile of living rock, came the first dull boom of the captain's fire.

The Court exhaled. And Jude Okafor rolled up his sleeves.
