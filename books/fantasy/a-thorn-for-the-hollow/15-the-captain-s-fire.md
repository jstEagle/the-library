## 15. The Captain's Fire

### WREN

I smelled the smoke before I heard the horns.

That was wrong, and I knew it was wrong, because there is nothing in the Hollow Court that burns. No torches, no hearths, no lamps that need oil. The halls beneath the mountain are lit by the dead themselves, a soft grey-green glow like moonlight drowned in deep water. So when smoke curled along the ceiling of the Bargaining Hall—real smoke, tar-and-tallow smoke, the smell of the crown's campaign rations—I understood two things at once.

First: Halvard Dane had found us.

Second: he had not come to negotiate.

"Jude." I said it quietly, the way you say a warning to a man who has spent his whole life being quiet himself. He was crouched by the dais where the Queen held court, his grandmother's lantern cradled against his chest like something newborn. He looked up, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I had just done, watched him arrive at the same ugly answer.

"The wardens," he said.

"My captain."

"Your *former* captain."

"He doesn't know that yet," I said. "He's about to find out in the worst possible way."

The Queen rose from her throne of woven bone, and for the first time since we'd descended into her court I saw something in her face that was not amusement, not hunger, not the vast patient boredom of a creature who has outlived every promise made to her. It was fear. Small, controlled, the size of a coin—but fear nonetheless, on a face that had worn nothing but masks for what she claimed was four hundred years.

"They brought ward-breakers," she said. "I can taste them. Iron and salt and scripture. They mean to bring down the mountain's roots."

"Can you stop them?" Jude asked.

"I am the Court," she said, and the way she said it told me the answer before she did. "The Court is a covenant, and a covenant is only as strong as its anchor. And my anchor, bone-carver—" her gaze flicked to the lantern in his arms, and then, unbearably, to me "—is currently deciding what it wants to be."

I didn't have time to feel guilty about that. The far doors of the Bargaining Hall—the great ones, the ones carved with the faces of the unburied dead, each one named, each one waiting—shuddered once, twice, and burst inward in a bloom of white fire that cast no heat and left no mark on the stone, only on everything alive within it.

Ward-fire. I'd carried it myself for six years. It doesn't burn wood. It burns *belonging*.

* * *

### JUDE

Here is a thing nobody tells you about fire: the dead hate it more than the living do.

Not because it hurts them—they're past hurting, mostly—but because fire unnames things. A burned body can't be recognized. A burned memorial bone can't be read. Every carving I've ever made, every name I've ever given back to some grieving family, lives under the same sentence: *one spark and it never happened.*

So when the doors blew open and the white flame came through, the Court screamed. Not the Queen. Not Wren. The *dead*—hundreds of them, thousands, the unburied of four centuries crowding the galleries and alcoves and hanging from the vaulted ceiling like pale fruit—and they all screamed at once, and my skull became a bell, and I went down on one knee with the lantern hugged to my chest and vomited sound into the silence between heartbeats.

Through the ringing, through the noise, I heard boots. Dozens of them. Wardens, in their grey lacquered armor, spreading through the breach in pairs, each pair carrying a ward-breaker between them—a thing like a harp crossed with a battering ram, strung with wire made of consecrated iron, humming the particular note that unravels covenants.

And at their center, walking like a man entering his own church, came Captain Halvard Dane.

I'd never seen him. I knew him anyway. Wren talked about him the way you talk about a scar—you don't look at it directly, but you know exactly where it is. Broad, silver-templed, a face like a cliff that has decided to be patient about falling. He stopped three paces inside the hall, surveyed the starving dead, the smoking doors, the Queen on her throne, and me on the floor clutching a lantern like the world's most desperate thief.

"Okafor," he said. My name, flat as a ledger entry. "Ashvale. You are both under arrest for high treason against the Crown of Thornfell, for conspiracy with hostile entities, and for the crime of soul-theft." His eyes moved to the lantern. "Surrender the vessel."

"You don't understand what this is," I managed. My voice came out wrecked, scraped thin by all those screaming dead. "This is a person. This is my *grandmother*. She's been down here a hundred years feeding your precious wards, and you want to—"

"I want," Dane said, "to restore the stars. The wards fell. Winter is killing Lowmark. Refugees are freezing in the passes by the score, and the Crown's diviners tell me the cause is *here*." He raised one gauntleted hand, and the ward-breakers around him hummed up a half-tone, and the whole hall shivered like a struck glass. "Whatever bargain you thought you were striking, carver, it ends now. Break the Court, break the curse. Simple as surgery."

"It's not a curse," Wren said. She'd come to stand beside me without my noticing—that warden training, the moving-like-you're-not-there of it—and her hand was on her sword, but pointed down, which from a defector facing her own captain is practically a love letter. "Captain, I've seen what's down here. They're not monsters. They're *refugees*. The crown built its wards on a stolen soul and called it peace—"

"Ashvale." He said her name the way you'd pick something up off the floor with tongs. "You were the best I had. That makes this worse for both of us. Stand aside or be stood over."

And here is what I remember most clearly, later, when I try to put the night in order: she stood aside.

Not toward him. Aside—one step to the left, out of the direct line, sword still down, still pointed away. Because she wasn't going to fight him. Because some part of her, the part that had reported a smuggler to her superiors and gotten Tam Voss executed for a sack of contraband charms, could not raise steel against the man who'd taught her how to hold it. I saw it happen. I saw her choose guilt over violence, duty-over-duty, and I wanted to scream at her that there was no clean place to stand in what was coming, but I didn't have time, because Dane gave the order.

"Breakers. On my count. Bring down the root-vault."

* * *

### WREN

I have spent a long time since that night trying to decide whether standing aside was cowardice or conscience, and I still don't know. What I know is what happened next.

The ward-breakers began to sing.

It's an ugly sound, if you've never heard it—not loud, but *wrong*, a note that slides just below hearing and stays there, in the teeth, behind the eyes. Covenants are woven things, threads of oath and anchor and consent, and the breakers pluck those threads until they snap. I'd felt it a hundred times from the outside, collapsing smugglers' wards, collapsing a shrine or two that needed collapsing. From the inside, it was different. From the inside it felt like someone pulling stitches out of my chest.

The Court convulsed. The grey-green light guttered. Along the walls, the carved faces of the unburied began to *move*, mouths opening on names that no one alive had spoken in centuries, and the dead themselves dimmed like coals going out one by one.

"No!" The Queen's voice cracked across the hall like a whip of ice. For the first time she abandoned her throne entirely, swept forward in her ragged-crowned majesty, and threw herself—not at the wardens, at the *walls*, pressing her hands against the stone as though she could hold the mountain's veins closed by will alone. "You fools, you beautiful blind fools, the wards were never the covenant—the *anchor* is the covenant—break me and you break the last thread holding your little town's sky together—"

"Diviners say otherwise," Dane said, and there it was, finally, the crack in him—not in his voice, which stayed level as a frozen lake, but in his eyes, which moved to the refugees' faces in the gallery, the fading children, and flinched away. He'd been told the Court was a monster. Monsters can be killed cleanly. He was finding out that the story had seams, and he was choosing not to look, because looking would cost more than he had to spend tonight. I knew the feeling intimately. I'd lived in that exact refusal for six years.

"Captain," I said, and stepped fully between him and the dais, and this time my sword came up. "Listen to them. Just—listen. One minute. You have wardens with ears; use them."

"Ears won't feed Lowmark, Ashvale."

"*Souls* fed Lowmark! For a hundred years! One soul, chained down here in the dark so everyone above could pretend the sky was honest!" My voice broke on it, and I hated that, hated that he heard it, hated that some part of him registered the break as confirmation that his defector had gone soft instead of gone true. "You're not restoring anything. You're burning the evidence."

Something moved behind his eyes. I want to believe, even now, that it was doubt.

Then the counterattack came from the other side—from the dead themselves, the braver ones, the ones who remembered fighting. Pale shapes poured off the galleries, unarmed, unstoppable in the way grief is unstoppable, hurling themselves at the warden lines not to kill but simply to *be seen*, to crowd the wardens' sight with faces until the men faltered. Some did falter. One young warden dropped his breaker entirely and stood staring at a dead woman who was holding her empty arms out to him in the unmistakable shape of someone who had died waiting for a child to come home.

Dane drew steel. "Hold formation! Breakers on the root-vault, now!"

And in the chaos—in the screaming light and the singing iron and the dead pressing in like a tide made of names—Jude Okafor did the bravest stupidest thing I have ever witnessed, and I include my entire career in the comparison.

* * *

### JUDE

I ran for the root-vault. Not away from the fight—into the middle of it, lantern clutched to my ribs, because I'd finally understood what the Queen had been trying to tell us and what Dane refused to hear: the Court wasn't the fortress. The Court was the *wound*. And the lantern—

The lantern was the thread.

My grandmother's soul sat in carved bone that my family's hands had shaped generations ago, before the trade, before the betrayal, back when an Okafor still trusted whatever crown came asking. If the ward-breakers snapped the covenant while her soul was still bound inside it, she wouldn't be freed. She'd be *spent*. Burned like fuel to power one last flicker of the old lie, and then gone—scattered past any carving, any hearing, any grief I could ever offer her again.

Unless I got her out first. Unless I pulled the thread loose with my own hands, here, now, in the middle of a battle, with no idea what unspooling a century-old binding would do to me or to her or to the mountain overhead.

Smuggler's arithmetic. Move fast, hit soft places, apologize never.

A dead woman cleared my path—literally, hurling herself into the path of two wardens, buying me three strides. A ward-breaker's note caught me mid-stride and I felt something tear, somewhere deeper than muscle, some thread of *me*, and I kept running anyway because that's what the road teaches you. Wren appeared at my shoulder the way she does, impossible and punctual, her sword finally up and finally pointed at the people she used to call brothers.

"The dais!" I shouted. "There's an old binding-stone under the throne—the lantern locks into it—I can feel it pulling!"

"You can feel it?"

"I can feel *everything* down here, Wren, it's like standing inside a bell!"

We reached the dais together. I knelt, set the lantern on the binding-stone, and pressed my palms flat against the carved bone, and did the thing I have done over ten thousand memorial bones in my life: I listened for the lock.

Every craft has a grammar. Carvers know it in their wrists. The lantern was made by my bloodline, and my bloodline leaves signatures—little mercy-details, escape-hatches, the habit of a family that has always known the difference between keeping a thing safe and keeping a thing *caged*. My grandmother's jailers hadn't built the lantern. My ancestors had. Which meant somewhere in the weave of it, almost certainly invisible, almost certainly forbidden, there was a hinge.

I found it on the third verse of listening. A seam, thin as breath, running under the base.

"Jude." Wren's voice, tight, above me. "Captain's coming."

"Not yet." I dug my thumbnail into the seam. The bone was warm. Warmer than bone should be. Warmer than it had ever been, and I suddenly understood that she could feel me too, that she had known I was coming for years, decades, that every warm moment the memorial bone on my shelf had ever given me was *this*, anticipated—her grandson's thumb finding the seam at last. "Almost—"

"HOLD!" Dane's roar, close now, too close. White fire bloomed at the edge of my vision. "BREAKERS—FULL RESONANCE—"

The note changed. Deeper. Truer. The whole mountain groaned like a ship in weather, and dust fell from the vaulted dark in curtains, and the binding-stone under my hands split from crown to base with a sound like a tooth breaking.

But the seam opened first.

I felt her leave the lantern the way you feel a bird leave your open palm—not weight departing but *decision*, wings catching air, a rush of presence so vast and so gentle that I laughed out loud, one broken syllable of joy, in the middle of the end of the world.

Then the resonance hit the lantern itself.

Later, Wren told me the sequence: the full-resonance wave, the shattered binding-stone, the lantern thrown from my hands by the shock of it, arcing through the smoky air in its long slow terrible turn. I don't remember the arc. I remember the landing. I remember the sound bone makes when it breaks on stone—not a snap, a *grief*, a sound I have never once heard from my own work and heard now from my family's oldest piece, my grandmother's prison and my grandmother's gift, cracking into three pieces on the steps of the Queen's dais.

Her soul didn't go back in. Souls aren't water; they don't pool. Hers scattered—outward, upward, everywhere at once, a burst of warmth and light and *voice*, a single syllable of my name in her own accent, her own living throat, the first time I'd ever heard it—

"—*Jude*—"

—and then thinning, fraying, pouring out through the cracks in the Court like light through a shattered window, streaming toward the root-vault, toward the surface, toward the sky, dissolving as it went into strands I couldn't follow, couldn't gather, couldn't carve back together with any craft I knew.

"No," I said. I was on my knees in the broken pieces. I picked them up. All three. As if that mattered. As if hands could fix this. "No, no, *no*—"

Around me the battle was ending or beginning—I genuinely could not tell you which. Dane shouting orders. Wren screaming something, my name or his. The Queen on her knees among us, hands pressed to the floor where the light had spilled, and her face—the mask finally, finally gone—was the face of a woman watching the last of her people die.

The light thinned. Frayed. Went out.

Everything the plan depended on, gone, in the time it takes a lantern to fall.

And then—because the universe is cruel but not tidy, because mountains keep secrets even from themselves—the dust settled, and the silence underneath the silence spoke.

It came from the root-vault. From the deep veins of the mountain, where the glow had always pulsed like a heartbeat. The pulse was changing. Quickening. And threading through it, faint as a whisper through a wall, familiar as my own hands:

my grandmother's voice, scattered, strand by strand, singing the road-song Okafor women sing over their carvings.

She wasn't gone. She was *everywhere*—and I had no idea whether that was salvation, or the beginning of something none of us would survive.

Above us, through a hundred fathoms of splitting stone, the first crack of dawn-light showed where the ceiling of the world was coming down.
