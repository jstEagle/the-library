## 16. What the Stars Are For

The fire had eaten the hall down to its ribs.

I stood in the wreckage of the Bargaining Hall with soot in my teeth and my grandmother's soul scattered across the floor like a dropped handful of river stones, and I thought, absurdly, of my workshop in Lowmark. Of how I'd spent ten years keeping that room swept. How I'd have given anything, once, for one loud morning where nothing whispered.

Be careful what you grieve for. The dead are generous listeners.

The lantern lay in three pieces near my feet. Bone, my family's craft, carved by hands two generations gone—carved, I understood now, to hold what should never have been held. The soul it had carried was not inside anymore. Captain Vess's ward-breakers had done their work well. When the lantern cracked, my grandmother's soul had come apart like breath on cold glass, and now she was everywhere and nowhere, thin as smoke, too scattered even to whisper.

I knelt in the ash and gathered fragments anyway. Because that is what Okafor men do. We carve, we keep, we hold pieces of the dead in our hands and refuse to put them down.

"Jude." Wren's voice, hoarse from smoke. She limped toward me through the ruin, her warden's coat scorched at the hem, her defector's brand still glowing dull red on her wrist like an ember that refused to die. Behind her, the Court's dead huddled in the shadows of collapsed arches—the unburied, the starving, fading even now without their anchor. A child-shape flickered like a candle guttering. "Jude, we have to go. Vess will regroup. She'll bring more fire."

"I'm not leaving her."

"She's not *here* anymore."

"That," I said, picking up a sliver of bone no bigger than my thumbnail, "has never once stopped me talking to someone."

Wren didn't laugh. She'd stopped laughing somewhere around the moment the ceiling came down, which I respected. There was ash in her hair and blood on her lip and she looked, in that moment, less like the woman who'd once tried to arrest me and more like someone standing at the edge of something enormous with no map.

Good, I thought. Welcome to my whole life.

Behind us, from beneath a fallen pillar of black stone, came a sound I hadn't heard in all my time beneath the mountain. Not a voice. Not a scream. A laugh—thin, cracked, but unmistakably a laugh.

The Hollow Queen was dying, and she found something funny about it.

* * *

We dug her out together. It took both of us, Wren hauling stone while I braced the pillar, and when we finally uncovered her I nearly wished we hadn't. Queens should not look like this. Whatever majesty the Hollow Queen had worn in the Bargaining Hall—that terrible beauty, that patience like a spider's—had burned away with her court. What remained was small. Grey-skinned. Old, in a way that made me think she hadn't been old in centuries, because being old requires time, and time requires a future, and the Court had been living on borrowed futures for a hundred years.

Her crown had melted into her hair. Her eyes were the color of starlight through fog.

"Well met, bone-carver," she said, and coughed, and the cough sounded like gravel sliding down a well. "You'll forgive me if I don't rise."

"Don't talk," Wren said. "Save your strength."

"For what?" The Queen smiled. It was not a cruel smile, which unsettled me more than any cruelty could have. "Child, I am a lie that has been eating itself for a century. There is no strength left to save. Only time. And time—" her gaze drifted up, through the broken ceiling, to the black sky above the mountain, "—time is all anyone ever wanted from me."

I crouched beside her. Up close she smelled like cold iron and old paper, like the inside of a sealed room. "You knew this would happen," I said. "Vess. The fire. You let us walk into it."

"I let you walk into everything." Her eyes found mine. "That is what courts do. But no, carver—I did not know the captain would burn the hall. I hoped. Hoping is different. Hoping is what the starving do instead of eating."

"The lantern," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated that it cracked. "My grandmother. Was there any way to free her that didn't end like this?"

The Queen was quiet a long moment. Around us, the unburied dead crept closer—not threatening, just close, drawn to their dying sovereign like moths who'd forgotten moths fly toward light rather than away from it. The child-shape sat down an arm's length away and began, very softly, to hum a song with no melody.

"You want a villain," the Queen said at last. "It would be kinder, wouldn't it? A monster under the mountain, a wicked queen, a clean sword-stroke to fix it all. Your captain believes in that story. She is killing us for it right now."

"Then tell me the true one."

She reached out—her hand weighed nothing, less than the bone fragments in my pocket—and touched the ash between us.

"A long time ago," she said, "before your crown had a name, this court was a refuge. Do you hear me? A refuge. The unburied came here—the drowned with no river to claim them, the lost with no road home, the ones whose names were eaten by war or distance or simple bad luck. No one loved them enough to bury them. So they came beneath the mountain, and we kept them. That was all. That was everything. We kept them, and we were warm, and we waited for someone above to remember them."

"And then?"

"And then the crown built Thornfell's wards." Her voice went flat, the flatness of a story told too many times. "Wards need feeding, carver. Everyone knows wards need feeding—but no one wants to say what they eat. So the crown made a covenant. One soul, sold below, to anchor the wards above. A prisoner, traded generation after generation. And the Court—" her hand closed slowly around a fistful of ash, "—the Court learned to be hungry. Because a prison must justify itself. Because the crown needed the Court monstrous, so no one would ask what the monster was *for*. So we became the thing they wrote in their wardens' primers. And every year the anchor soul grew thinner, and every year the stars—"

She stopped. Her breath caught. For a moment I thought she was dying right there, mid-sentence, and some part of me—a part I didn't examine—panicked.

But she wasn't dying. She was crying. Grey tears, cutting pale lines down her soot-dark face.

"Ask me," she whispered. "Ask me what the stars are."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked back at me, and for the first time since I'd known her, the warden had no answer ready. No protocol. No oath. Just wide eyes and ash and the same question burning behind her face that burned behind mine.

So I asked.

"What are the stars?"

* * *

The Queen told us, and I will carry it until whatever carries me.

"Stars," she said, "are the dead who were loved well."

The words fell into the ruined hall like stones into deep water. Even the humming child went silent.

"Not the powerful dead. Not the buried-with-honors dead, though some of those qualify. Loved *well*, carver. Grieved properly. Remembered rightly. Someone above spoke their name with warmth until the warmth outlasted the speaker. That is what a star is. Every light in your night sky is a person who was held so tightly in memory that the holding turned to shining. The dead do not need thrones or temples. They need to be *kept*—freely, warmly, by name—and when they are kept that way, they rise."

I thought of my workshop. Of the memorial bones stacked in careful rows, each one carved while I listened to the dead tell me who they'd been. I thought of how, in Lowmark, people paid me in bread and silence and looked away when they passed my door—and how the dead themselves had never once made me feel invisible.

"And the covenant dimmed them," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Because—"

"Because a hoarded soul cannot shine." The Queen's voice gentled, the way you speak to someone standing at a grave. "Your grandmother was loved well, carver. Oh, she was. Her family kept her name for three generations before she ever died—you know this, you grew up hearing it. She should have risen. She should have been a light bright enough to steer ships by. Instead the crown bought her, chained her beneath the mountain, and used her warmth to power their walls. A hundred years of stolen starlight, poured into stone. And the sky above Thornfell has been going dark ever since—one star at a time, one loved-and-imprisoned soul at a time—as the covenant took more and gave back nothing."

The black sky through the broken roof. The wards falling. The dead screaming through my skull the night it happened—all of it reassembling itself in my head into a shape I should have seen months ago.

"It was never a bargain," I said. "It was theft. The crown stole its own dead and called it order."

"Now you understand why I could not simply give her back." The Queen laughed again, that cracked dry sound. "Give her back to what, carver? To a sky that had already been robbed of the means to receive her? The covenant was a wound with walls built around it. Breaking the walls does not heal the wound. Something had to change in the shape of the keeping itself."

Wren sank down onto the rubble beside the Queen, all pretense of warden posture gone. "The anchor," she said quietly. "The new covenant needs an anchor. That's why you wanted me. A warden's soul—duty-bound, oath-carved—it's dense. It holds."

"It holds," the Queen agreed. "And it dims, in time. As yours would have. As hers did." She nodded at the shattered lantern. "Anchors are prisons, child. Beautiful ones. Necessary-seeming ones. But every prison dims its prisoner, and every dimmed soul takes a little more light out of the world. Can you not see it? A century of your crown's arithmetic: one soul for the wards, one soul for the borders, and the sky pays the difference."

The humming child had crept closer. It was watching me with eyes like holes punched in fog. And I realized—suddenly, completely, the way ice realizes it is water—that the Queen had answered a question I'd been carrying since Lowmark. Since before Lowmark. Since I was a boy pressing my ear to my grandmother's memorial bone and hearing, faint as a heartbeat, her hum.

"Why me?" I asked. My voice came out strange. "Not Wren. Not her oath, not her guilt. Why did you want *me* at the bargaining table? You had a warden's soul offered freely. Dense. Holds. Everything your arithmetic required. And you watched us fight over it like it was entertainment."

The Queen's grey eyes fixed on mine, and for a moment—just a moment—the old majesty flickered through the ruin of her, the thing she'd been before the crown taught her to be hungry.

"Because I was tired," she said simply. "Tired of anchors. Tired of prisons that work." She lifted her weightless hand and pointed—at me, at my hands, at the bone-dust worked so deep into the creases of my fingers it would never wash out. "You listen, carver. Not command. Not bind. Not trade. You sit with the dead and you hear them, and you carve what they tell you, and you *keep* them. Freely. Warmly. By name." Her smile returned, small and terrible and kind. "Do you know what you've been doing all these years, alone in your little shop in Lowmark, while the town looked through you?"

I couldn't answer. My throat had closed.

"You've been making stars," she said. "Small ones. Quiet ones. Every memorial bone you ever carved true was a soul remembered rightly—and every soul remembered rightly rises a little easier. You thought your gift was a curse that made you invisible." Her hand fell to the ash. "It is a lamp. It was always a lamp. And the covenant is dying, and the sky is empty, and the Court is starving, and the only thing that has ever refilled a dark sky is a listener who keeps the dead for love and not for use."

* * *

For a long time nobody said anything. The fire crackled somewhere far off, in another wing of the Court Vess hadn't reached yet. The unburied pressed closer, and their closeness didn't frighten me anymore. It felt like standing in a crowded market, almost. Like Lowmark on a feast day, if Lowmark had ever let me stand in its crowds.

Wren broke the silence first, because of course she did. Wardens break silences; it's in the primer.

"So the anchor," she said slowly. "The new covenant. You're saying it can't be a soul in a lantern. It can't be a prisoner at all."

"A prisoner dims," the Queen said. "A listener keeps the light lit."

"But then what *is* the anchor? What holds the wards, or replaces them, or—whatever comes next? If it's not a captive soul, what is it?"

And there it was. The shape of it, rising in me like water finding its level. I had felt it building since the bridge, since the toll, since I'd paid my mother's memory to a ghost and walked on lighter and emptier and somehow more myself. I had felt it in my hands every time I picked up a fragment of my grandmother's lantern and felt not silence but *scatteredness*—a soul not destroyed, only unpinned, waiting for someone to gather it the way you gather seed.

I pulled the fragments from my pocket. All of them. Every sliver and shard of my grandmother's lantern-bone that I'd gathered from the wrecked bargaining floor, and more besides, scraped from the ash where they'd fallen. They sat in my cupped palms, grey and lightless.

"She's not gone," I said. "Scattered isn't gone. I've heard scattered before—drowned sailors, bodies lost downstream. The dead don't stop speaking when they come apart. They stop being *listened to*."

"Jude." Wren's voice was careful, the way you're careful near a ledge. "What are you thinking?"

"What the Queen said. About lamps." I looked down at the fragments, and my hands were steady, steadier than they'd been since the stars went out. "A lantern holds a soul so it can't leave. That's the old craft. The crown's craft. But there's another way to work bone. You don't seal it—you tune it. You carve it so it resonates, so it carries, so that everyone touching it hears the same thing at once. A shared listening." I looked up at the Queen. "You said the anchor must not be a prisoner. Then it's a listener. A living one. Someone who sits beneath the mountain and *hears* the dead—keeps them by name, freely, every day, forever—and the keeping itself becomes the covenant. Not one soul fed into a wall. A thousand souls kept in the open, and the wall comes down."

The Queen exhaled—a long, slow sound, like a glacier deciding to become a river.

"Yes," she whispered. "Yes, carver. That is the third option. That is the door I could see but never open, because I was made of the old craft. A queen is a hoard. A queen cannot listen—only rule. But a carver..." Her eyes were wet again. "A carver might."

"And the cost?" Wren asked, sharp. Always sharp, my warden, always doing the arithmetic the rest of us flinch from. "There's always a cost. What does it cost him?"

The Queen looked at me. Really looked, the way the dead look—straight through skin and story to the thing underneath.

"He gives up the quiet," she said. "Forever. He hears every unburied soul beneath this mountain, every night, for the rest of his life. No silence. No sleep that is fully sleep. The dead are not gentle company, even loved ones. Especially loved ones." She paused. "And he gives up the road. An anchor stays. That is the shape of it. He chose where he belongs the day he walked down here—or he will, when he picks up his knife."

I looked at my hands. Carver's hands. Scarred, stained, steady. Hands that had spent thirty years learning the one skill the whole broken system had been missing: how to keep the dead without caging them.

I thought of Lowmark, which had never seen me. Of the passes, which I'd crossed a hundred times unseen. Of the loneliness so old I'd mistaken it for my skeleton.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a heartbeat, I heard it—the hum. My grandmother's hum, coming not from a lantern, not from a single point in the dark, but from everywhere. From the ash. From the air. From the fragments in my palm, faint and scattered and *waiting*.

Not gone. Unpinned. Waiting for a listener.

"Then I need my knife," I said, "and I need every piece of her that's left, and I need everyone in this Court to stand still and tell me their names."

The Queen began, very softly, to laugh—and this time the laugh didn't crack. It rang, thin and clear as a struck bell, and around us the unburied dead lifted their faces toward the sound like flowers turning toward a sun that hadn't risen yet.

Above us, through the broken roof, the sky stayed black.

But somewhere in the ash of my grandmother's scattered soul, something shifted. Turned. Listened back.

And far above the mountain—so far I might have imagined it—one point of darkness blinked.

Like an eye, remembering how to open.
