## 17. The Carving

The wreckage of the bargaining hall was still settling around us, dust sifting down like gray snow, and somewhere in it lay the pieces of my grandmother.

I knelt among the rubble and gathered them the way I'd gathered bones my whole life: gently, with both hands, as if the dead could feel impatience. Which they can. Which they had told me, more than once, in voices that sounded disapprovingly like my grandmother's.

Her lantern had shattered in the captain's assault—shattered and scattered, fragments of carved bone flung across forty feet of broken flagstone. Each fragment still held a sliver of her. I could feel them the way you feel a fire through a wall: warmth without shape, warmth that wanted to be a person again.

"Jude." Wren's voice, low, from the doorway she was watching. "We don't have long. She'll regroup."

"I know."

"You've said 'I know' four times in the last hour and done nothing but pick up pieces."

"I'm a bone-carver," I said. "Picking up pieces is the entire profession."

She didn't laugh. She almost never laughed at the right moments, which I was beginning to suspect was a deliberate policy on her part, like a soldier who refuses to enjoy the view because the view might kill her.

The Queen lay against the far wall of the hall, her court gathered around her in a half-circle of the unburied dead. She was fading—I could see the flagstone through her now, the way you can see the moon through torn cloud. The captain's ward-breakers had done their work. The old covenant was dying, and with it, apparently, everything it had been pretending to hold up.

Including the stars. Including, if I didn't move fast, my grandmother.

"Tell me again," I said, to the Queen, because my hands needed something to do besides shake. "Tell me how it's supposed to work."

"A listener." Her voice was thin as winter ice over a stream. "Not a prisoner. A prisoner holds the door shut from inside. A listener *hears* the door, and answers it, and the door remembers it was ever a door at all." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen all week, and I had seen a lot. "Your grandmother could have done it, once. That was the crown's crime, Jude. They took a woman who could have *chosen* and made her a lock."

I looked down at the fragments in my hands. A knuckle of carved bone with a spiral on it—her spiral, the one she'd put on everything she ever made, the one I'd been copying since I was nine years old without knowing why. A curve of jawbone. A piece of rib, hollowed, whistling faintly when I breathed on it.

"You could still run," Wren said behind me. Not accusing. Just saying it, the way you'd say *the bridge is out* or *it's going to snow*. "Take the fragments. Get out. Find another way."

"There is no other way."

"There's always another way. Smugglers' rule."

"Smugglers' rule number two," I said, "is that there's always another way. Rule number one is that everything costs. I'm about to pay rule number one with rule number two."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "What do you need?"

"Blood. Time. And for your captain to stay dead for another twenty minutes."

"Two out of three I can promise."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about carving: it is not a skill. It is a conversation.

You don't impose a shape on bone. You listen for the shape that's already in there—the grain of it, the memory of the animal or the person it belonged to—and you clear away everything that isn't that. My grandmother taught me that when I was small, her hands over mine on the little knife, saying: *the bone knows what it wants to be, Jude. Your job is just to stop arguing with it.*

I had spent my whole life carving memorial bones for the dead of Lowmark. Little spirals and names and the things their families remembered. I had never carved a covenant-stone. I had never carved an anchor for a court of the unburied dead.

But the bone knows what it wants to be.

I cleared a space on the broken floor, swept the dust into a circle with my sleeve, and laid the fragments of my grandmother out in the pattern the dead had been whispering to me since I entered the mountain—a pattern I hadn't understood until now, a spiral, of course, always a spiral, her spiral, the spiral that was apparently the oldest shape in the world and the shape a soul makes when it refuses to be a straight line.

Then I cut my palm open on my carving knife and let the blood fall.

The dead came at once. Not the screaming dead of the night the stars went out—those had been dead in *panic*, dead who had felt their anchor ripped away. These were the Court's dead, the unburied, the ones the covenant had been hoarding like a miser hoards coins he never spends. They crowded in around me, and for the first time since I'd learned to hear them, they weren't asking for anything.

They were helping.

A dead smuggler whose name I'd never learned pointed—insisted, wordlessly—at a fragment I'd placed wrong. A child who had died before the crown was born hummed a note that turned out to be exactly the pitch I needed to hold in my head while I carved. The Queen watched from her wall and said nothing, but her fading was slower now, as if proximity to the work itself was a kind of food.

I carved. I carved the way I had never carved in my life—not with skill, but with everything I had, every trick, every hour at the bench, every night I'd sat alone in my workshop listening to the dead because the living had never wanted to talk to me. The fragments knit together under my knife and my blood, bone growing into bone, the spiral deepening, widening, becoming not a memorial for one woman but a *doorway* for thousands.

And through it all, my grandmother was there. Not whole—not yet, maybe not ever again the way she was—but present, the way warmth is present in a stone that held the sun.

*Jude,* she said, in the voice I'd spent thirty years forgetting and three weeks remembering. *You're holding the knife wrong.*

"I'm holding it fine."

*You're strangling it. Loosen your grip. The bone knows.*

"The bone is my dead grandmother's soul and the anchor of a starving court, Gran. Forgive me if my grip is a little tight."

*Loosen it,* she said, and there was laughter in her, real laughter, the kind I remembered from before she died, from the one summer I'd known her alive. *You were always so afraid of dropping things. You never once dropped anything, love. Not because your grip was good. Because you never picked up anything you weren't willing to carry.*

I loosened my grip.

The knife moved like it was moving itself. The blood ran into the grooves and the grooves drank it, and where it drank, the bone glowed—not the sick amber of the old covenant, but a clean white, star-white, and I understood suddenly what the Queen had meant. *Stars are the dead who were loved well.* I was carving my grandmother with love, and the Court's dead were crowding in to be carved with her, and every one of them was a small light waiting for a sky.

* * *

The captain came at the worst possible moment, which is the only moment captains ever come.

I heard her boots before I heard her voice—that deliberate, parade-ground tread, the tread of a woman who wanted the whole world to know she was arriving. Wren heard it too. I saw her shoulders change, saw her turn from the doorway and put herself between me and the sound.

"Keep carving," she said.

"Wren—"

"Keep. Carving." She drew her warden's blade—the one she'd tried to arrest me with, in another life, in a town that had stars. "How long?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less if nothing interrupts me."

"Then I need eleven."

The captain came through the broken gate with six wardens and a ward-breaker's ram, and the light of the hall went red with the fire she carried. She saw Wren and stopped, and for a moment the two of them just looked at each other across the wreckage—teacher and student, or whatever they had been, the woman who had taught Wren to hold a sword and the woman now holding one against her.

"Ashvale," the captain said. "Step away from the heretic."

"No, Captain."

"You've seen what he's doing. You've seen what it costs. The wards can be rebuilt the old way—"

"The old way is a woman in a lantern," Wren said. "The old way is a hundred years of a woman in a lantern, and everyone above her calling it peace." Her voice didn't shake. I was the one shaking, ten feet away, knife in a wound in my hand, and her voice didn't shake at all. "I reported a smuggler once. He was executed. I told myself it was the law. I'm done telling myself things, Captain."

The captain's face did something complicated and then did something simple, which was decide to kill us.

The fight, from where I stood, was mostly noise and light and my own heartbeat in my ears. I carved. That was the whole of my world: the knife, the blood, the spiral, the fragments becoming whole. Behind me, steel rang on steel. Someone screamed. The ward-breaker's ram hit stone somewhere and the floor jumped under my knees, and a fragment of my grandmother skittered an inch out of place, and a dead child—quick as a bird—pushed it back with fingers that had no weight at all.

*Jude,* my grandmother said, calm as a kitchen. *The center. Finish the center. The rest will hold.*

The center was the smallest fragment. A piece of her knuckle, the one with the spiral. I set it in the heart of the stone and pressed my bleeding palm against it, and I said the only words I had, which were not a spell and not a prayer, but the words I said over every memorial bone I'd ever made:

"You were here. You were loved. You are not lost."

The stone took my blood and took my words and took the dead—all of them, the whole starving court, thousands of souls pouring into a shared anchor, no cage, no lock, no single prisoner, just a door that heard them and answered—and it *lit*.

White light, star-light, up through the mountain's roots, up through the broken ceiling, up toward a sky that had been dark for a season and was about to remember its job.

The captain stopped fighting. I saw it happen—the ram lowered, the sword arm sagging, her face turned up toward the light coming through the cracks in the world. Wren stood over her, blade shaking now for the first time, blood on her sleeve—I never found out whose—and neither of them moved.

The stone hummed under my hand. Warm. Alive. Full.

*There,* my grandmother said. Not in my skull anymore. In the stone. In the air. In the faint white glow that was beginning, very faintly, very far above, to be answered. *There. Now loosen your grip, love.*

I looked at my palm. The wound was closing—not healed, but closing, the way wounds close when something has decided you're not done yet.

And then the Queen, who had been fading against her wall all this time, stood up. All of her. Solid as sunrise.

"Jude Okafor," she said, and her voice was no longer thin. It was vast, and glad, and it made the dust dance. "Look up."

Through the shattered roof of the Hollow Court, through the mountain's throat, through the dark—

One star. Then another. Then a third, low on the horizon, climbing.

The dead were going home.
