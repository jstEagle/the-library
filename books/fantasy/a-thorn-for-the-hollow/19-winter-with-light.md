## 19. Winter With Light

The mountain spat us out at dawn, or what passes for dawn when the sky has been a black lid for a season. I felt it before I saw it—that pressure behind my ears lifting, the way it does when a door closes on a noisy room. Except the room in this case was the Hollow Court, and the noise was a few thousand unburied dead learning to sing.

Wren went first up the last stretch of smugglers' stair. She always goes first now. I think she believes if something is going to bite her, it should have the courtesy to do it before it bites me, which is either devotion or a very specific guilt-management strategy. With Wren it is usually both.

"Jude." Her voice came back down the stair, strange and thin. "You need to see this."

I climbed. The gate—the old sealed one, the one that had taken us three days and two bribed ghosts to open—stood wide, frost furred along its carvings like a cat's whiskers. And beyond it: snow. A whole field of it, running down from the mountain's shoulder to the tree line, blue-grey and untouched except for our own footprints going in weeks ago, half-filled, softened, forgiven by the weather.

And above the snow—stars.

I don't mean that figuratively, though I am a novelist's son and could be accused of it. I mean there were stars, hundreds of them, scattered across the vault of the sky like someone had finally remembered the ceiling was supposed to be painted. Not the flat, dead pinpricks of my childhood, not the dim embers the covenant had been hoarding for a century. These burned. They had color to them—amber, white, one low on the horizon with a greenish edge like a bruise healing. The kind of stars you see in stories and assume the storyteller is lying.

I sat down right there on the threshold stone, because my legs had opinions about holding me up, and looked until my eyes watered.

"That one," Wren said quietly, crouching beside me. She pointed east-southeast, above where Lowmark would be, if the world were kind enough to be laid out flat beneath us. "The bright one."

"I know which one," I said, and found my voice had gone gravelly. "She always liked being early. First one out at festivals, complaining everyone else was slow."

My grandmother's voice was among them.

I want to be clear about what that means, because it doesn't mean what you'd think, and it took me a long moment on that cold stone to understand it myself. It isn't that I heard her speaking down at me, all wisdom and warmth, dispensing posthumous advice. It's that I *felt* her, the way you feel a fire in another room—not the words, but the presence. The particular shape her listening took. All my life, hearing the dead has been like standing in a crowd with my ear pressed against every mouth at once. Now it was like standing in a crowd where everyone had finally stopped shouting and settled into conversation, and one voice near the front was humming a tune I knew from childhood kitchens.

"She's louder than the others," I said. "Still bossy."

"Of course she is." Wren pulled off a glove and pressed her bare hand into the snow, testing, then did something I didn't expect: she laughed. A short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose. "It's warm. Jude, the snow is *warm*."

It wasn't, exactly. But compared to the Court's airless dark, compared to the long winter without stars, the cold coming off that field tasted clean. It tasted like weather instead of punishment.

We walked down into it.

* * *

There is a thing that happens when you've spent your whole life as a bone-carver who hears the dead, and then suddenly the dead stop screaming. You keep waiting for the scream. You flinch at silences that used to be full.

By midmorning we'd made the tree line, following the smugglers' road down through stands of thorn-trees heavy with rime, and the quiet followed us like a well-trained dog. The dead were still there—I could feel them, faint as heat through a wall—but they'd stopped pressing. That was the change. For thirty years they had pressed against me, hungry for a listener, clawing to be known. Now they simply... were. Neighbors behind their walls. Some of them nodded, in whatever way the dead nod, and moved along.

One did stop, though, near the old way-shrine where the road forks toward Hollow Bridge. An old man's voice, unhurried, amused.

"You're the Okafor boy."

"Not a boy," I said.

"You're somebody's boy forever, that's how it works." He chuckled, a dry leaf of a sound. "Heard about what you did down there. Whole choir's talking about it. New anchor, they're calling you. Listener-in-chief." He said it kindly, but I felt the weight of it settle onto my shoulders anyway, familiar as an old pack.

"It wasn't just me," I said, glancing at Wren, who was pretending very hard to study the shrine. "And it wasn't anchoring. That's the point. Nobody's tied to anything anymore. They can leave whenever they like."

"Mm." The old dead man considered that. "Some of us like staying. You'll find that too. Being free to go makes staying mean something." A pause, and then, gentler: "Tell the warden girl the bridge-ghost says her oath arrived safely. It's been using it as a bookmark."

Then he was gone, and I stood there in the falling light feeling like I'd been handed a gift I hadn't unwrapped yet.

"What?" Wren asked, catching my expression.

"The bridge-ghost sends its regards. Your oath is apparently good reading material."

Color rose in her cheeks—the first color I'd seen on her since the captain's fire. "That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Jude."

"You gave it your *oath*, Wren. Your solemn warden's vow, sworn before crown and covenant. It's holding pages apart with it." I grinned, and she fought the smile and lost, which is becoming a pattern between us that I intend to encourage.

But underneath the banter, something else was turning over. *Being free to go makes staying mean something.* I turned the words over like a bone in my hands, looking for the grain of them.

Because here is the thing I haven't said aloud, maybe haven't even thought clearly: when the Queen dissolved, when she led her court up into the sky and the stars came home one by one like lamps being lit in windows, part of me braced for emptiness. My gift had been built on hunger—the dead's hunger, funneling through me. Take away the hunger and what was left? I'd assumed: nothing. A quiet so total it would feel like deafness.

Instead, walking down that mountain, I kept catching fragments. The thorn-trees hummed with the dead of every bird that had ever roosted in them, a drowsy chorus. The snow itself remembered avalanches fondly. And threaded through it all, steady as a pulse, my grandmother—not speaking, not instructing, just *there*, the way she'd been there my whole childhood, doing her carving across the table while I did mine, two people comfortable enough in each other's company to say nothing for hours.

Mourning braided with joy. That's the only phrase I've got for it. Grief didn't leave. It just stopped being the only thread in the rope.

* * *

We camped that night at the high shepherd's bothy, the one with the collapsed roof and the excellent view. Wren got a fire going while I unpacked the food—smoked goat and hard bread and the last of the honey, which we'd been rationing with monastic discipline and which I decided, sitting under actual stars for the first time in a season, we had earned.

"To the honey," I said, raising the jar.

"To the honey," Wren agreed solemnly, and drank from it directly, which would have scandalized my grandmother and therefore delighted me.

We ate in the firelight. Above us the sky did its slow wheel, and every hour or so one of us would look up and go quiet again, because it turns out you don't get used to it. You don't get used to the stars coming back. I hope I never do.

"Do you know what I keep thinking?" Wren said eventually. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, firelight catching the defector's brand on her wrist—faded now, almost gone, the way brands do when the thing they branded you for stops existing. "In the Court, when the captain came through with the ward-breakers. When everything was burning and the lantern shattered and I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought, *this is what my life adds up to*. Fire and failure. And then you knelt down in all that wreckage and started picking up the pieces of her soul-bone like it was the most obvious work in the world. Like of course that's what you do when everything breaks. You carve."

"My grandmother taught me that," I said. "Never waste a breakage. The cracks tell you where the grain wants to go."

She looked at me across the fire for a long moment. There was something in her face I couldn't read—or could, but wasn't ready to. "What happens to us now?" she asked. "When we get back. To Lowmark."

I poked the fire. "They'll want to arrest you. Officially, I mean. Defection charges, desertion, consorting with heretics—" I gestured at myself. "That's me. Consorting with heretics."

"And you?"

"They'll want to study me, probably. Or burn me. Depends which officer draws the short straw." I shrugged, aiming for casual and knowing I missed. "The wards are gone, though. Really gone. Whatever replaces them will have to be built different, and whoever builds it will need..." I tapped my own ear. "A listener."

"You're volunteering."

"I'm observing that the labor market has shifted in my favor."

That got the laugh out of her again, easier this time. Then she reached over and took the honey jar from beside me—purely, I'm sure, for practical reasons—and our fingers overlapped on the glass, and neither of us moved for what was probably three seconds and felt like a season.

"We should sleep," she said.

"We should," I agreed, not sleeping.

* * *

The dead visited me in dreams that night, but gently, the way family visits. My grandmother stood in the dream-workshop we'd shared when I was nine, her sleeves rolled, her hands steady on the carving knife, and she didn't say anything at all. She just worked, and I worked beside her, and the bones we carved weren't memorials—they were maps. Roads only smugglers know, drawn in marrow. And at the end of every road, a small star.

I woke before first light with the taste of honey still in my mouth and my eyes wet, which is the best kind of crying there is, if you have to do it.

Wren was already up, standing outside the bothy with her breath smoking, looking east. I joined her. The horizon had gone the pale silver-green of new ice, and above it the stars were fading out one by one—but not all of them. A handful held on past sunrise, stubborn, early risers.

"She was never late in her life," I said. "Why start now."

Wren's shoulder came to rest against mine. Deliberately, I noted. Weight given, not fallen. There's a difference, and after a lifetime of reading bodies in grief I have learned to know it the way you know rain coming.

"Lowmark today?" she asked.

"Two days, if the pass holds. One and a half if we take the drover's cut." I hesitated. "Wren. When we get there. They're going to put you in front of some tribunal, and they're going to ask you to choose between the crown and everything you saw under that mountain, and either answer costs you something you can't carve back."

"I know."

"And I wanted to say—before any of it—whatever you choose, however it goes—" I stopped. Swallowed. Tried again, because some things deserve better than eloquence. "You let me go on that road, months ago, when you had every reason not to. Before you'd heard anything, seen anything. Before you had proof. You just... let me go. Everything since has been building on that. So whatever they decide you are—a traitor, a hero, a witness, a corpse—I know what you are. I've known since the wards."

She didn't answer right away. Out east, one last star hung on past all reason, burning low and gold above the pass to Lowmark.

"Grandmother's watching," I said softly. "Making sure I behave."

"Is it working?"

"No comment."

Her hand found mine in the space between us, cold fingers lacing warm, and together we watched the morning come up over a world with its ceiling painted again. Somewhere below, past the tree line, past the drover's cut, Lowmark was waking to its first true dawn in a season—refugees on the roads, wardens with questions, a town about to learn what its peace had cost and who had paid it. None of that was finished. None of it would be easy.

But the stars were out. And for the first time in my loud, haunted, invisible life, the silence around me wasn't empty.

It was company.

Down the mountain, the road waited—and so did everyone in Lowmark who had ever lost someone, all of them about to discover that their dead had things to say, and that a bone-carver was coming home who could help them listen.
