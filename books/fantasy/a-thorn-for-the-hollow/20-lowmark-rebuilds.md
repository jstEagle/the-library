## 20. Lowmark Rebuilds

**JUDE**

Lowmark smelled the same when we came down out of the passes—woodsmoke and wet wool and the river's cold iron—but it looked at me differently.

I'd spent thirty years being invisible in this town. The carver nobody watched. The man you handed a memorial bone to through a doorway because looking too long at the death-trade felt unlucky. I had cultivated that invisibility like a garden, and I had hated it like a garden too: something I tended because the alternative was bare ground.

Now every eye in Lowmark found me before I found it.

We came in on the smugglers' road at dusk, past the old toll-stone where Wren had once nearly arrested me, past the shrine to no god in particular where I'd left offerings for years without knowing who took them. The wards along the border were gone—not broken, not flickering, just *gone*, the way scaffolding is gone from a finished wall—and the mountain behind us stood open-mouthed and quiet under a sky full of returned stars.

Wren walked half a step ahead, the way she did, as if she could arrest the road itself for obstruction. Her defector's brand was still visible at her collar. She hadn't covered it since the mountain.

"Everyone's staring," she said.

"They're staring at you."

"They're staring at *you*. I'm the traitor. You're the miracle."

"I'm the bone-carver," I said. "The miracle is mostly my grandmother's doing."

A woman stepped out of the chandler's shop as we passed—Marta Voss, whose son I had carved a memorial for eleven years ago, drowned in the millrace. She looked at me the way people look at a door they'd always assumed was painted on.

"Okafor," she said. "Is it true? What they're saying. That you went under the mountain."

"It's true."

"And the stars—"

"My grandmother's up there," I said. "Along with rather a lot of other people the crown forgot to mention."

Marta Voss put her hand over her mouth. Then, slowly, she took it away and said the sentence that would change my life more thoroughly than anything the Hollow Queen had offered:

"My mother died in the winter of the fever. No one carved her. Could you—" She stopped, embarrassed. "Could you tell me if she's all right?"

I had spent my whole career carving bones for the dead so the living would have something to hold. It had never once occurred to anyone—including me—that the living might want something back.

"She's in the sky," I said. "Third row from the river-star, if I've got my bearings. She looks contented. A little impatient. She wants you to fix the shutter on your north window; it's been rattling for two years and it's driving her mad."

Marta laughed and cried in the same breath, which is, I have come to believe, the most honest sound a human being can make.

Word travels fast in a town of four hundred souls. By the time Wren and I reached my workshop, there were nine people waiting outside it, and by morning there were forty, and I understood that my invisibility had not ended. It had simply inverted. I was now the most visible thing in Lowmark, and I discovered that visibility, like most blessings, arrives with teeth.

**WREN**

The summons came on crown paper, which meant someone in the provincial office still believed the crown had authority here, which told me everything about how badly news traveled.

*Warden-Captain Ashvale. You will present yourself to the magistrate's court in Harrowgate to answer charges of desertion, consorting with heretics, and destruction of crown property.*

Destruction of crown property. The covenant-stone. As if my grandmother's soul—or Jude's—had been a fence post.

Jude read it over my shoulder while I sat at his workbench, which had become our workbench somewhere in the last month without either of us remarking on it. His knife kept moving through a piece of riverbone even as he read, small sure strokes, the way other men drum their fingers.

"You don't have to go," he said.

"Yes, I do."

"They'll hang you."

"They'll *try*," I said. "But if I run, they hang someone else in my place, and they keep the covenant's paperwork clean. Someone has to stand up in front of the magistrates and say out loud what the wardens fed to that mountain for a hundred years. Names. Dates. The ledgers exist, Jude. I've seen them. Every warden-captain for four generations signed off on a delivery of one soul per decade, and called it 'maintenance.'"

He set the knife down. "I'll come with you."

"No."

"That wasn't a request."

"It wasn't an invitation." I turned to face him. He had this way of looking at me—not challenging, exactly. Patient. Like stone waiting for weather. "If you walk into that courtroom, they won't try you, they'll just make you disappear quietly, and then the testimony dies with both of us. You stay here. You have forty people outside your door who need their dead heard. That's testimony too. Better than mine, maybe. Mine gets a man hanged. Yours might get the whole covenant pulled apart in daylight."

He didn't like it. I could see him not liking it, turning it over, looking for the flaw. There wasn't one, and he knew it.

"How long?" he said finally.

"The trial? Days. The verdict—" I shrugged. "Depends how brave the magistrates feel. Harrowgate's a long way from the capital. Out here, the crown is mostly a stamp on paper and a tax collector. And the tax collectors haven't been paid since the stars went out, so honestly, morale is low."

Something twitched at the corner of his mouth. "Try not to get hanged. I've grown accustomed to your face."

"That," I said, "is the least romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and I intend to treasure it."

**JUDE**

Harrowgate was three days' ride, and Wren rode out on a Tuesday with a satchel of copied ledger pages and a face like a drawn blade. I stood in the road and watched her until the fog took her, and the dead around me—there were always some now, drifting through Lowmark like weather—made a sound I can only describe as respectful silence.

Then I went inside and got to work, because grief is a luxury and I had forty families in a queue.

Here is what I learned in those weeks, working dawn to dark with my hands in bone and my ears full of the departed:

The dead do not want much. This surprised me. I had expected—after the Court, after the starving children of the unburied—I had expected need, hunger, the terrible gravitational pull of souls that had been hoarded. But the dead of Lowmark were different. They had been loved. Imperfectly, distractedly, in the ordinary human way, but loved. And love, it turns out, is a kind of provisioning. Marta Voss's mother wanted her shutter fixed and her good shawl given to the church. The drowned miller's boy wanted his father to know he hadn't gone in on a dare—he'd slipped, and he didn't want his father carrying guilt alongside the grief, one burden was enough. Old Tam Hedley, dead forty years, wanted someone to finally admit his cider had been terrible.

"He knows?" Tam's granddaughter asked me, horrified.

"He's known for forty years," I said. "He finds it very funny now."

The workshop changed shape around the work. People brought chairs. Someone—Marta, I suspected—put a kettle on a brazier by the door. The queue outside stopped being a queue and started being a kind of gathering, neighbors talking to neighbors while they waited, comparing notes on what their dead had asked for, laughing sometimes. I had spent my life alone in this room with my knives and my whispering skulls, and now there were children in it, chasing each other between the shelving while their parents listened to grandmothers speak through my mouth.

One afternoon a warden came. Not a patrol—a single young warden, barely shaved, standing in my doorway with his hand nowhere near his sword, looking like a boy who had walked into the wrong story.

"You're Okafor," he said. "The one Warden-Captain Ashvale—"

"Defected with," I supplied. "Yes."

He shifted his weight. Behind him, the gathering had gone quiet in the particular way crowds go quiet when they're listening harder than ever.

"They told us at the garrison the wards fell because of smugglers," he said. "They told us the mountain was punished by the gods for our sins. They told us—" He stopped. Started again. "She testified yesterday. In Harrowgate. My cousin was there. She read the ledgers out loud. All of them. The deliveries." His voice cracked on the word. "My grandfather was a warden. Was he—did he know?"

I thought about lying to him, and rejected it. These people deserved better than comfort shaped like fiction; I'd spent enough time among things that survived on lies to last several lifetimes.

"He knew," I said gently. "They all knew. It was in the oath they made him swear—the part they don't teach recruits. 'To keep the border whole, at whatever maintenance the crown requires.' That's what 'maintenance' means, son. It always has."

The boy stood there a long moment, absorbing it, the way you absorb a blow—staggering slightly, staying upright. Then he asked the question I would hear, in one form or another, for the rest of my life:

"What do we do now?"

And I, who had never belonged anywhere, who had learned my gift in hiding and practiced it in shame, heard myself answer with the voice of a man giving directions to a neighbor:

"Now you bury them properly. All of them. You dig real graves, you carve real names, you say the words out loud instead of paying someone else to carry them silently under a mountain. It's slower than a ward-stone. It doesn't last a century without tending. You have to *keep doing it*." I picked up the bone I was working—a small one, a child's memorial, a star shape for the sky she'd never gotten to see. "That's the price. Turns out it always was. We just paid it wrong for a hundred years."

The young warden nodded once, hard, like a man accepting a wound, and left. And the gathering behind him breathed again, and the kettle whistled, and Tam Hedley's ghost made a comment about the tea that I generously did not repeat.

**WREN**

The verdict, when it came, was neither the hanging nor the pardon I'd braced for. The magistrates of Harrowgate—three elderly men and one formidable woman who had clearly done her own reading of the ledgers—declared that the matter exceeded their jurisdiction, and referred it to the crown, and everyone in that courtroom understood that the crown, in its current state of starlit chaos, would take years to respond. Which was, I suspect, exactly what they intended. Justice by bureaucracy: slow enough to be survivable.

I walked out free, technically. Practically, I walked out stripped of rank, pay, and garrison rights—a warden in name only, belonging to no post, no patrol, no chain of command.

I stood on the courthouse steps in the grey Harrowgate drizzle and realized I had no idea where I belonged, and then realized, with a feeling like a knot untying, that I did know. I'd known for weeks. I'd just been waiting for permission from an institution that had never given me any.

It took me four days to walk home, and I use that word deliberately. Home. Lowmark. The workshop with the kettle by the door and the children underfoot and the bone-carver who had told me, once, on a frozen bridge with a memory of his mother freshly torn out of him, that he'd grown accustomed to my face.

I came through the door at dusk, mud to the knees, and Jude looked up from his bench, and the whole crowded room went quiet, and he said nothing at all—just cleared a space on the bench beside him, which in the language we had built between us on the mountain was a speech.

I sat down. The room exhaled. Somewhere in the rafters, I would swear, the dead applauded.

"Well?" he said quietly.

"Referred to the crown. Which means forgotten. Which means alive." I pulled the crumpled summons from my coat and laid it on the bench between us. "Also, I'm unemployed."

"Mm." He picked up the paper, considered it, and did something I will think about for the rest of my life: he reached for his knife and began cutting the crown's seal off the summons in careful curls, feeding the scraps to the brazier. "Terrible material, government paper," he said. "Doesn't burn clean."

"What are you doing?"

"Making room," he said, "on the wall above the bench. For something worth hanging."

I looked at the wall. Bare pegs, old tool-shadows, decades of his solitary life written in dust outlines. Room for anything.

"Jude," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing, "what exactly am I to you?"

He kept carving. Of course he kept carving; the man thought with his hands. But he answered, and his answer was so plainly, laboriously honest that it undid me more than any pretty thing could have:

"I don't have a word yet. I've been trying to carve one." A pause, the knife moving. "Give me time. I'm better at bone than vocabulary."

**JUDE**

Winter deepened, but it was a different winter now—one with stars in it, and therefore with light, thin and silver and enough. Lowmark rebuilt itself the way towns do: badly, loudly, with arguments about the granary and a festival nobody had budgeted for. They held a naming-day for the returned dead in the square, hundreds of names read aloud into the starlight, and I stood at the edge of the crowd and listened to the sky listen.

That should have been the end of the story. Belonging, at last; the emotional beat delivered; curtain.

But I am a bone-carver, and I know that stories, like bones, have marrow beneath the surface, and one night in late winter the marrow spoke.

I was closing the workshop when the dead went silent.

Not quiet—silent. Total. The way a forest goes silent before something moves through it. Forty years of whispers in my skull, months of company, and then a hush so complete I could hear my own heart misbehave.

Then, from the direction of the mountain, faint as breath on a winter window, a sound I had hoped never to hear again: singing. Thin, many-voiced, rising out of the sealed gate like smoke through a crack.

And threaded through the singing, unmistakable, a voice I knew—old and dry and fond, my grandmother's voice, saying words that dropped through me like stones down a well:

*"Little carver. Come see what's growing at the gate."*

I stood in my doorway with my hand on the latch, the warm workshop at my back, the whole bright town behind me, everything I had finally chosen and finally been chosen by—and the mountain called my name.

Wren would be back from the garrison archives within the hour. I put another log on her fire, set out two cups out of stubborn hope or cowardice, and picked up my coat.

Some thorns, it seemed, were not done with us yet.
