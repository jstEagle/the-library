## 22. A Thorn for the Hollow

The sapling came up out of the ground like it had opinions about being moved.

I'd dug the hole at dawn, when the frost was still in the soil and my breath hung around me like a small ghost that hadn't decided where to go. The thorn-tree's roots were a knotted fist, black-barked and stubborn, and they did not want to leave the pot Wren had made from an old warden's shield. I suppose I understood. It's hard to leave a place you've grown into, even a bad one.

"Come on," I told it, working my fingers under the root ball. "You're going somewhere better."

It didn't believe me. Trees rarely do.

A year ago to the day—or near enough; the dead are imprecise with calendars and so am I—I had walked out of the Hollow Court with dirt in my blood and starlight in my eyes, carrying nothing but a bone knife and a woman who'd once tried to arrest me. A year ago the mountain behind me had been a wound, its gate sealed with ward-stone and shame, its halls emptied of the dead who had finally found their way home to the sky. And now here I was, kneeling in the snow at the mountain's mouth, planting a tree in the scar.

Not to seal it. That was the old thinking—the crown's thinking, the covenant's thinking: bury it, lock it, feed it something quiet and hope it stays fed. No. The tree was for watching. A thorn-tree grows slow and mean and lives longer than any covenant, and its roots go down deep and honest, and if anything ever tried to grow beneath it—anything hungry, anything sold, anything traded away in the dark—it would come up through those roots first, and I would hear it.

That was the whole design. Not a lock. A listening post.

"You're sure about the placement?" Wren stood at the edge of the dig site, arms crossed against the cold, her defector's brand catching the early light. She still wore her warden's coat, though she hadn't been a warden in over a year. Some clothes you keep because they fit. Some you keep because taking them off would mean admitting what you were when you put them on. "Old Man Corrin says the east face gets more sun."

"Old Man Corrin also thinks the stars are holes in a leather sky," I said. "I'm placing it where the roots will find the old covenant-stone channel. If anything wakes down there, this tree feels it before anyone else does."

"And you'll feel it through the tree."

"I'll feel it through everything." I tamped the soil down around the base of the sapling, packed it firm with the heel of my hand. "That's rather the point of me now."

She didn't argue. She'd stopped arguing with that particular truth somewhere around midwinter, when I'd sat bolt upright in bed at three in the morning and said, "There's a fox dying under the ice on the millpond," and she'd gone out with an axe in the dark and come back soaked and triumphant with a very annoyed animal wrapped in her coat. After that she stopped asking how I knew things and started asking what we were going to do about them.

That, I think, is the definition of love. Or partnership. Or whatever it is when someone stops requiring proof.

* * *

The dead pass me on the roads now like neighbors.

I want to be clear about what that means, because I spent thirty years believing that hearing the dead was a curse—a skull full of whispers, a life spent flinching at voices no one else could hear. And it was that, for a long time. But the new covenant changed the weather of it. When the Queen dissolved and led her court up into the dark, when the stars came back one by one like lamps being lit in windows down a long street, something in the architecture of death itself got repaired. The dead aren't trapped anymore. They're not hoarded, not sold, not anchored against their will. They move. They finish their business. They go where they're going, and some of them take their time about it, and some of them, on their way, stop to say hello.

An old woman passed me on the Lowmark road last month, walking the opposite direction with a basket on her arm, and she nodded at me the way you nod at a man you've seen at market a hundred times but never learned the name of. I knew her by the shape of her absence—she'd been dead eleven years, drowned in the weir, and her family had never held a proper burial because the body never came up. She stopped. She looked at me with eyes like fogged glass.

"You're the carver," she said.

"I'm the carver."

"My granddaughter still sets out honey-cakes on Allnight. Tell her I never liked honey-cakes. Tell her I liked the plum ones."

"I'll tell her."

"She won't believe you."

"No," I agreed. "But she'll cry, and then she'll buy plums, and that's how it goes."

She smiled—or the memory of a smile moved across her face the way light moves across water—and went on down the road toward whatever comes next, and I stood there in the mud feeling something I didn't have a word for yet. It wasn't grief. It wasn't joy. It was closer to what you feel when a stranger returns something you lost years ago and doesn't make a fuss about it.

That's most days now. The dead nod as they pass. Some ask after their families. One soldier comes by every few weeks to check whether the war memorial in Lowmark square has his name spelled right (it does; it always did; he just likes hearing it). A child who died in the fever winter follows the shepherd's road above town, chasing a dog that isn't there anymore, or maybe is—I've stopped being certain about the boundaries of things. The living don't see them. Most of the living can't. But the roads are crowded in a way no census has ever caught, and I am the only man in Thornfell who knows it, and instead of that being a torment it has become—there's no other word for it—a neighborhood.

My workshop reflects the change. It used to be a place where I worked alone in the blue hour before dawn, carving memorials for people who paid me and left quickly, uneasy around a man who talked to bones. Now there's a bench by the window where folk sit while I work, and they bring bread, and they tell me about their dead—not to have bones carved, necessarily, but because telling me is a kind of burial in itself. Old Marta brings her husband's pipe and asks me to hold it and tell her if he's cross about the roof. He isn't. He thinks the roof is funny. The miller's boy comes to ask if his mother saw him win the wrestling match the week before she died, and I get to tell him yes, she saw, she was louder than the whole crowd, and the boy walks home two inches taller.

And twice a season, someone comes who needs more than talk. Someone whose dead are stuck—unfinished, unburied, tangled in something they couldn't let go. For those, I carve. Not prison-bones like the old covenant used, not lanterns to keep a soul in. Release-work. I carve the shape of what the dead person loved, or failed to do, or needed to say, and I give it to the family, and the family speaks to it, and the dead hear, and then—usually within a moon's turn—they move on. Upward. Toward the lights.

Wren calls my shop "the ferry office." She thinks she's joking. She is not entirely joking.

* * *

The sapling took root by afternoon. I felt it happen—a small, stubborn grip in the soil, like a hand closing. Thorns don't ask permission. They decide the ground is theirs and act accordingly, which is why I chose them. A rose would have been prettier. A rose would have been wrong.

Wren crouched beside me and pressed her palm flat to the earth over the roots. She does this now, sometimes. She says she can't feel anything, that her gift—if she ever had one—died with her oath on Hollow Bridge, paid out like a toll. But she presses her hand to the ground anyway, the way you touch a sleeping animal, the way you test a wall for warmth.

"Anything?" she asked.

"Give it ten years."

"Ten years." She snorted. "You realize we'll be old."

"We're already old. We're smugglers with a legitimate business. That's the oldest thing a person can be."

She laughed—that short, surprised laugh she still seems startled by, like laughter is a language she's relearning—and knocked her shoulder against mine, and we sat there in the snow like a pair of fools watching a stick pretend to be a tree.

Below us, under forty feet of stone and a sealed gate and a year of careful silence, the old covenant-hall lay empty. I could feel it the way you feel a tooth that's finally stopped aching—the absence of pain as its own sensation. Nothing down there now but dark and root and the faint mineral patience of the mountain. The Queen's court had gone up. Every soul. Even the smallest ones, even the fading children I'd nearly stolen a lantern to save, even the ones too broken to walk who had to be carried. The Queen herself had gone last, and the look she gave me at the gate—before she came apart into light like breath off a winter pond—was not gratitude exactly. It was more like recognition. One keeper of hard truths acknowledging another.

*You were never invisible,* she'd said. *Everyone below always knew your name.*

I'd thought it was a kindness. A parting gift. It took me most of a year to understand it was neither. It was a correction.

Because here is the thing I got wrong for thirty years: I thought I was hidden. I thought the gift made me a ghost among the living—invisible, unheard, unwelcome, a man who walked through Lowmark like smoke through fingers. I built my whole loneliness on that foundation. And it turns out the loneliness was real, but the invisibility was a lie I told myself to make the loneliness bearable. Because the dead had always seen me. From the moment I was born—before, probably, from the moment the gift rooted in me—the dead of Thornfell had watched me walk through their world like a bright thread through grey cloth. They had known my name. They had spoken it to each other in the dark places, in the hollows and the wells and the quiet rooms where the dead wait. *The carver. The listener. Okafor's grandson.* They had known me the way a village knows its strange child—with pity, with curiosity, with a kind of fierce protective tenderness I had never once suspected.

When the wards fell and the mountain spoke my name, it wasn't calling me. It was greeting me. It had been trying to greet me my whole life, and I had spent that whole life covering my ears.

The thorn-tree swayed in a wind I couldn't feel at ground level. Its branches were bare and black and utterly without promise, and I loved it completely.

"Wren," I said.

"Hm?"

"When it blooms—thorn-trees bloom white, did you know that? Vicious little tree, flowers like snow—we should have a festival. Lowmark hasn't had a festival since before the wards fell. Something ridiculous. Honey-cakes. Plum ones."

She looked at me sideways, and the corner of her mouth did the thing it does when she's deciding whether to mock me or kiss me, and I've learned to enjoy the suspense. "A festival for a tree."

"A festival for watching. For keeping watch. For everyone who ever got sold or buried wrong or forgotten. For—" I gestured at the sky, where the first stars were beginning to show in the deepening blue, each one a soul that had been loved well, each one a neighbor of mine now, "—for them. So nobody forgets what the lights actually are."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and took my hand, hers rough with cold and rope-callus, and pressed it flat against the frozen ground next to her own.

"They'd come," she said. "You know that. Half of Lowmark would come. Marta would bring honey-cakes out of spite."

"The good kind?"

"There's only one kind of spite-cake, Jude. The best kind."

Above us, the stars thickened. Below us, the roots tightened their grip. And between the two—between the dead who had risen and the earth that held the watching tree—sat the two of us, alive and ordinary and thoroughly visible, waiting for spring.

I stayed until full dark. Wren went back for the mules and I told her I'd follow, and I meant it, but I lingered, the way you linger at a graveside, except this was the opposite of a grave. This was a sentry post. This was a promise planted in dirt.

The dead passed me on the road below, heading wherever the newly dead head, and one of them—an old man with a smuggler's pack still on his shoulders, a friend I had carved a release-bone for last autumn—stopped and looked up at me on the hillside, and raised two fingers off his brow in the old road-salute.

"Evening, Jude," he said.

"Evening, Tam."

"Tree'll take," he said. "Ground's good here. Always was."

Then he went on, whistling, and I stood alone on the hillside under the returned stars, and I laughed until my breath smoked, because he was right. The ground was good. It always had been. It was only ever the bargains laid on top of it that were rotten.

I was never invisible. Everyone below had always known my name.
