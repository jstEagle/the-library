## 1. The Charm That Didn't Work

The luck-charm cost me four hours of my life and sold for six coppers, which tells you most of what you need to know about the Charmwright Guild of Vell.

I want to be fair to it. I made it myself, in the guildhall's back room, over a brazier that smoked like a dying man's last words. I'd followed the old formula — ash of rowan, a drop of tallow blessed by nobody in particular because blessing requires magic and we hadn't had much of that since my grandmother was young. I'd whispered the binding-words into its heart, the ones Corvane taught me with his hands shaking too badly to demonstrate. And when I held it up to the lamplight, the little copper hawk at its center had *glowed*, just barely, just enough to make me think: maybe this one. Maybe tonight the guild remembers what it is.

Six coppers. A fishwife named Marda bought it for her son, who was shipping out on the *Salt Widow* come morning.

"Guaranteed?" she said, squinting at it in the lantern-light. She had hands like knotted rope and the flat, unfooled stare of a woman who'd haggled with better liars than me.

"Charmwright's guarantee," I said. "Guild-sealed." Which was true, technically. The seal was mine. So was the guild. All three of us — me, Corvane, and old Sefa who mostly slept — fit inside it with room left over for the ledgers.

Marda paid. I watched her walk away through the night market with the charm strung around her neck, and I felt the particular shame of a craftsman who knows exactly how good her work isn't. Because here is the truth about our charms: they worked once. Not my grandmother's generation — further back, when the guildhall had forty benches instead of three, when the king himself wore a charmwright's seal on state occasions. The old charms are still around. You see them sometimes on rich wrists in the high city, little things of silver and ember-glass that actually do what they promise. They hold their charge like the fire inside them is still alive.

Ours don't. Ours flicker for a week and gutter out, and the buyer comes back with a face like Marda's going to have tomorrow, and we give back the coppers and apologize, and the whole transaction has the flavor of a funeral conducted by people who can't remember the deceased.

* * *

You could set a clock by the Vell night market, if you hated clocks. It opened at dusk in the shadow of the Unbinding Wall, where the masons had carved the old guild sigils off the stones two generations ago but never managed to sand away the grooves. Stalls of eelskin and secondhand boots. A woman who told fortunes with cards so faded you had to take her word for which suit was which. The pie man, who was honest about everything except the meat.

I'd been selling there since I was fifteen, ten years now, long enough to know every crack in the flagstones and every face that meant trouble. So when I saw the tall man in gray stop at the mouth of Copper Lane and just — stand there, not buying, not browsing, hood back in the cold like he wanted to be seen from a distance — I started packing before he took his first step.

Wardens didn't raid often. Twice a year, maybe, on average. But they were thorough when they came, and they had a way of arriving exactly when your stock was laid out prettiest, and the law was simple enough: possession of charmed objects without writ was a flogging the first time and a hanging the third, and while the guild technically held a writ older than anyone alive, writs had a way of meaning less each year, like everything else we owned.

"Trouble?" said the pie man, watching me throw canvas over my tray.

"Warden."

He sighed and began covering his pies, which told you something about the quality of his meat.

The tall man had stopped being alone. Two more grays had appeared behind him, and then a fourth, moving down the lane with the unhurried confidence of people whose wages never depended on making a sale. The market did what markets do — nothing, loudly. Everyone kept selling, kept haggling, kept their eyes down, and underneath the noise ran that low animal hum of a crowd deciding whether tonight would be someone else's problem.

I got my tray into its satchel. Most of it, anyway. Luck-charms, fever-knots, a half-dozen sleep-sigils that worked about as well as counting goats. My whole inventory weighed less than a sack of flour, which was either a mercy or an indictment depending on how I felt that evening, and that evening I felt it was an indictment.

They started at the far end of the lane. I heard the fortune-teller's table go over — wood cracking, cards everywhere, her voice climbing into that register women save for fires and bailiffs. Then the pie man's cry of protest, cut short by the particular silence of a man being made to kneel.

I went out the back way, between the tanner's fence and the wall itself, where the carved-off sigils still left their ghost-grooves under my fingers. There was a gap there, a drainage culvert barely wide enough for me and definitely not wide enough for the satchel, so I did what my mother taught me before she vanished, which was: shove first, apologize to your ribs later.

I got through. The satchel got through. My dignity stayed on the other side with the Wardens, which seemed fair, since I hadn't been using it.

Behind me the market noise changed key — the hum becoming shouts, a stall collapsing, someone running. They'd found a real charm somewhere, maybe, or they'd decided the pie man looked magical. Either way it wasn't my problem anymore, and I told myself that all the way home through streets gone quiet as held breath, past the Unbinding Wall with its blank, gouged face, past the notice-board where a new proclamation was going up in fresh paste:

*By order of His Majesty: any person harboring practitioners of the forbidden arts will be considered complicit. Vigilance is loyalty.*

His Majesty. Thirty years on the throne and still putting up fresh notices, like a man reminding everyone of a joke only he gets. I read proclamations the way other people read weather. This one felt like snow coming.

* * *

The guildhall sat at the end of Ropewalk, wedged between a chandler's and a building that had been condemned so long the rats had opinions about it. Once it had been a hall — great doors, a courtyard where apprentices practiced the first forms until the air smelled like ozone. Now it was a house with delusions, and the great doors had been sold panel by panel until what remained was a regular door with a very expensive frame.

Corvane was awake, which was unusual, sitting in the common room with the ledger open and a candle burning down to a puddle. He looked up when I came in, and his eyes did the thing they always did — went first to the satchel, then to me, checking which of us had survived the night more intact.

"Raid," I said.

"Casualties?"

"My pride. The pie man, probably."

"The pie man sells cat." He turned back to the ledger, and I stood there dripping river-smell from the culvert, and neither of us said anything for a while, because that was the shape of us: three people keeping a fire that had gone out years ago, feeding it paper so we wouldn't have to admit it.

"Sold anything?" he asked finally.

"One luck-charm. Six coppers."

"To whom?"

"A fishwife. For her son." I hung the satchel on its peg. "It'll fail by Thursday."

Corvane's pen stopped. He didn't look up, but he stopped, and in him that was a whole speech. "Then refund her."

"I know."

"And Lena—" He did look up then, and the candlelight did him no kindness; it showed every year since the ban, every apprentice who'd walked out and every master who'd died or fled or simply stopped coming down to the benches. "Lena. Why do we keep doing this?"

It was the closest he ever came to asking the real question, which was why *I* kept doing it, since he'd asked himself the other version nightly for thirty years and gotten nowhere. I gave him the answer I always gave, the true one, the useless one:

"Because somebody has to remember how."

He nodded slowly, like I'd handed him something heavy to put down somewhere, and went back to his ledger. I climbed the stairs to my room under the eaves, where the roof leaked in exactly one spot I'd learned to sleep around, and I lay there listening to Vell settle into itself — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the distant bell of the Watch marking the hour — and I thought about Marda's son on the *Salt Widow*, and Thursday, and the six coppers I'd have to press back into a stranger's palm with both hands and an apology.

That was the inheritance, right there. Not the guildhall. Not the name. The apology. Ten generations of charmwrights distilled down to a girl who could make a copper hawk glow for a week and say *sorry* like she meant it.

I slept. Of course I slept. You learn to.

And if I'd known what was coming up those stairs toward me even then — riding through the dark with its skin burned off and my mother's word dying in its throat — I don't know that I'd have slept worse. Some mercies you only recognize afterward. That night I dreamed about the market, ordinary and whole, the luck-charm glowing steady in Marda's fist, and nobody anywhere asking me to guarantee a thing.
