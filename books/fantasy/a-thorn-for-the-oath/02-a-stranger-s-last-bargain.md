## 2. A Stranger's Last Bargain

The guildhall's back room smelled of mildew and old ambition.

I'd spent the better part of an hour cataloguing the night's wreckage: three cracked luck-knots, a love-charm with the structural integrity of wet bread, and a box of "warding sigils" that were, in truth, painted river stones I'd bought off a barge-man at cost. The ledger called this *inventory*. The truth was closer to *evidence*. Someday, someone was going to arrest us for fraud, and my only defense would be that the fraud barely worked.

Corvane had gone to bed an hour after the raid, which for Corvane meant he'd shuffled to his cot muttering about the good old days when Charmwrights were burned at the stake instead of merely fined. He considered progress a personal insult. He was, at least, consistent.

So it was just me, the candle, and the inventory, when the door came open like it had been kicked by God.

He fell through it — a man-shaped ruin in courier's leathers, one shoulder blackened down to something that wasn't quite cloth and wasn't quite skin. He smelled of char and ozone and something sweeter underneath, the way magic smells right before it goes wrong. He got one hand on the floorboards and dragged himself two feet toward me, leaving a smear that I would later scrub out with lye and would never entirely forgive myself for resenting.

"Door," he rasped. "Bar it."

I should have run. Every sensible instinct I owned — and I own several, they're just badly maintained — screamed at me to go out the window and let whatever was chasing this man find an empty room. Instead I crossed to the door and dropped the bar, because apparently my instincts were on strike.

"Who—" I started.

"No time." He caught my wrist. His grip was furnace-hot even through my sleeve, and his eyes had that glassy shine you see on people who have already left their body behind and are just waiting for the rest of them to catch up. "You're guild-blood. I can smell it. Kavanagh."

My name in a stranger's mouth. My mother's name, actually, before she traded it for a courier's road and then for nothing at all. Ten years since anyone had said it like it belonged to me.

"You've mistaken me for someone important," I said. It's a good line. I've used it on tax collectors and one very persistent marriage broker.

"I don't make mistakes." He fumbled at his breastplate — courier's rig, scorched nearly through — and pulled free a leather scroll-case fused half to its strap. He pressed it into my hands and folded my fingers over it with both of his, which trembled like leaves deciding whether to fall. "Emberhold."

The word went through me like a fishhook.

I hadn't heard it in ten years. Not since the night my mother kissed my forehead, told me she had one last delivery, and walked out of Vell into a story I'd stopped believing had an ending. She used to say it as a bedtime thing, half-lullaby: *the Emberhold keeps the fire, little spark.* I'd assumed she invented it, the way mothers invent things — dragons under beds, kings worth serving, reasons to come home.

"Take it," he said. "Not to the Wardens. Not to the crown. To no one who wears a badge or a crown or asks politely. You understand? No one who *asks*."

"That's most people," I said. My voice came out steadier than my hands deserved.

"Good." Something that might have been a smile moved under the burns. "She said you'd argue. She said you get that from her."

The candle guttered. Not from any draft — the windows were shuttered, the door barred. It leaned sideways like a man listening at a wall, and then stood straight again.

Outside, faint but getting less so, boots. Many boots. The particular rhythm of Wardens marching, which is not like other marching; it has a smugness built into the cadence.

"They followed me," he whispered. "Three days. Through the pass, over the ice, down the— " A cough took him, wet and terrible. When he surfaced, there was red at the corner of his mouth, and his eyes had gone from glassy to distant. "Listen. The binding is old now. Thirty years old. Old bindings get hungry. It needs fire, and the fire is going out, and when it does—"

"When it does what?"

But he shook his head, or maybe the shaking was already happening whether he willed it or not. "Tell no one. Trust the disgraced." His gaze drifted past my shoulder, fixing on something in the middle distance that I couldn't see and didn't want to. "Trust... the disgraced..."

The boots stopped outside.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is otherwise scarce in my life: in that moment, with the bar across the door and a dying stranger's blood cooling on my floor, my first feeling was not nobility. It was the small, animal wish to be somewhere else — anywhere else, ideally a life in which I sold working charms and my biggest problem was the moisture content of painted stones.

Then he did the bravest thing I have ever watched a person do, which was to die slowly and loudly enough to buy me time.

He hauled himself up against the wall, put his back to it, and began to *sing*. That's the only word for it. It wasn't melody; it was more like a key turning in a lock the size of a city. Every charm in the room woke at once — my cracked luck-knots rattling on their hooks, the love-charm humming like a struck wineglass, even the painted river stones glowing a faint, apologetic blue, as if embarrassed to finally be doing something. The whole room lit with borrowed power, screaming *guild-magic here, guild-magic here*, loud enough to drown out one dying heartbeat among many.

A diversion. A real one. Made from the last coin of a life.

"Go," he said, without looking at me. "Back stairs. Don't let them see your face."

"I can't just leave you—"

"You can," he said, "and you will, and you'll hate yourself for it, which means you'll remember it, which means I win." Now he looked at me, and his eyes were clear as mountain water. "Kavanagh's daughter carries it. Say it back."

"Kavanagh's daughter carries it."

"Good girl." He closed his eyes. The singing continued. It did not need him awake to continue, which frightened me more than anything else that night. "When they break the door — and they will — tell them nothing. Grief is allowed. Answers are not."

* * *

I went out the back stairwell with the scroll-case strapped under my coat and my heart doing something arrhythmic and undignified. The back stairs of the guildhall are a disgrace of carpentry — each step announces itself like a town crier — so I took them in a crouch, weight on the edges, the way my mother taught me when I was nine and she was teaching me things she claimed were games.

The alley behind the hall stank of cabbage-water and rain. Two streets over, orange light bobbed: Warden lanterns, sweeping. I heard the front door of the guildhall splinter — the bar lasted four seconds, which for our carpentry was heroic — and then voices, and then the singing stopped mid-phrase.

The silence afterward was worse than the song.

I made it six rooftops away — six, because grief makes you stupid and stupid makes you fast, and because some animal part of me needed to be far before I let myself feel what I felt. On the sixth roof I sat down behind a chimney pot that smelled of pigeon, and I opened the scroll-case with hands that would not stop shaking.

Inside: a map, scorched at every edge as though it had been carried through a fire rather than merely near one. Mountain ranges I didn't recognize. A route inked in a fine, impatient hand, running east-northeast out of Vell, up through the passes, into territory marked with a single word in old script:

*Emberhold.*

And in the margins, tiny annotations — distances, hazards, a warning beside one river crossing (*ferryman lies, pay double*) — in handwriting that made my chest hurt in a way I still can't describe properly. I knew that hand. I'd watched it write shopping lists and birthday notes and one letter of resignation from a life, left on a kitchen table under a cold teacup.

I am not a woman who cries easily. I cried then, briefly, furiously, hating myself for it, wiping my face with a sleeve that smelled of pigeon.

Because if the handwriting was hers — and it was, it was, no one else crosses their sevens like that, like they're stabbing the page — then my mother hadn't vanished. She'd been *sent*. Or she'd sent something. Or she'd spent her last ten years walking toward a mountain with a burning map, and tonight a man had died on my floor to finish the delivery.

*I cannot unknow this.* The thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do. Whatever was on this map — whatever waited at the end of those inked lines — I was carrying it now. You can give back a package. You cannot give back knowledge. The Wardens could take the case, burn the map, burn me, but they couldn't reach inside my skull and unlearn the word *Emberhold* out of me.

Which meant I had exactly two choices. Give it to someone who asked politely, or find out why everyone was willing to kill for it.

Below me, the lanterns were converging on the guildhall. I saw them pause at our door, saw a sergeant — tall, gray-cloaked, methodical — step inside where the blood was.

In the morning, Corvane would find the stain and ask questions. In the morning, I'd have to decide whether to lie to the last master of a dead guild, or worse, tell him the truth.

Tonight, I climbed down the far side of the roof, turned my collar up against the rain, and started walking — not east yet, not yet, first to the one person alive who might know what my mother had stepped into.

Old men who burn ledger pages always know something. It's practically the job description.

Behind me, Vell slept under its false king's peace, and in my coat, against my ribs, the scorched map was warm as a coal that refused to die.
