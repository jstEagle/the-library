## 3. What the Guild Remembers

The guildhall of Vell had once been a palace of glass and lantern-light. I knew this because Corvane told me so roughly four hundred times a year, usually while sweeping the same corner he'd swept the day before, as if repetition could conjure the chandeliers back out of the dust.

What it actually was: three rooms above a shuttered dyer's shop on Candlewick Row, one working hearth, forty-one years of unpaid rent owed to a landlady who'd stopped asking, and a ledger so old its spine had gone soft as bread. The great seal of the Charmwright Guild still hung over the door — a hand cupping a flame — except the flame had been painted over during the ban, because even symbols could get you hanged in this city, and Corvane hadn't gotten around to repainting it after the Wardens got bored of checking.

I sat at the worktable with the scorched map flattened under a jar of dead beetles we used as a paperweight, and I waited for dawn the way you wait for a tooth to finish aching.

I hadn't slept. The courier's blood was still under my fingernails no matter how I scrubbed, and the word she'd pressed into my ear along with the map — *Emberhold* — kept repeating itself in the dark like a cough in an empty church. The map itself didn't help. It was drawn on vellum that had been half-burned, the western edge eaten away to a black lacework, but what remained showed the mountain roads north of Vell, and somewhere past the last marked town, in the blank space where honest maps stopped being honest, a symbol inked in red-gold: a hand cupping a flame.

Our seal. Guild-work, or something older than guild-work. On a map leading into mountains where, per every law and every fireside story for thirty years, nothing existed at all.

Corvane came down the stairs at his usual hour — which is to say whenever his knees decided — and stopped halfway across the room, sniffing the way he did when something was wrong. He was a small man made smaller by time, white-haired, with hands so spotted and knotted they looked like tree roots wearing rings. Once, they said, those hands had lit a hall of candles with a snap of his fingers. Now they mostly held brooms and teacups and, occasionally, grudges.

"You've found something," he said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Boy's got no tea going, map on the table, face like a wet funeral." He lowered himself into his chair by degrees, like a building settling. "Out with it."

So I did. I laid it out as plainly as I could — the market, the failed charm, the raid, coming home through the back ways, and the burned man — no. The burned woman. I still hadn't let myself say that part properly, even inside my own skull. A courier, half-consumed by Warden-fire, dying in our back room, pressing this into my hands and spending her last breath on a word she must have known would ruin my life.

Corvane listened without moving. When I finished, he reached out one root-hand, turned the map ninety degrees, studied it, then turned it back as though the direction mattered and he disapproved of it either way.

"Where's the body?" he asked.

"In the cellar. Under the sacking."

"Buried?"

"Not yet."

"Good." He rose, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and went to the ledger shelf.

That was wrong. Corvane did not move quickly, and he especially did not move *purposefully*, and there was something in the set of his shoulders now that I had seen maybe three times in ten years — all three times just before he lied to a Warden.

He took down the ledger. Not the current one, which was mostly a record of charms sold, charms failed, and apologies issued; the *old* one, the fat one bound in grey leather that predated the ban and that we were legally required to keep but never, ever open. He set it on the table beside the map, and my heart did something complicated.

"Master Corvane—"

"Hush." He licked his thumb, turned pages. The paper whispered. And I saw — briefly, only briefly — columns of names in faded iron-gall ink, and among them, in the margin of the page he stopped at, a sketch. Mountains. A gate. That same cupped-flame seal.

Then he struck a taper from the hearth and held it to the page.

"Wait—" I was up and grabbing his wrist before I understood I'd moved. "What are you *doing*?"

"What has to be done." His voice was terrible in its mildness. "Sit down, Lena."

Flame walked up the corner of the page. Old vellum burns fast and bright, and he had chosen the corner well — the sketch, the marginalia, a whole column of names curling brown, then black, then gone. I wrenched his wrist aside but I was too late; the page was half ash, and the ash was getting everywhere, settling on the map like grey snow.

"She said *Emberhold*," I told him. My voice came out low and strange. "You know that word. You flinched like I'd thrown it at you."

"I'm old. Everything makes me flinch."

"The seal is on the map. Our seal. In mountains that don't officially exist. A woman died in our back room to bring it here, to *me*, and you're burning the only book that might explain why."

"Yes," said Corvane.

Just that. *Yes.* No deflection, no grumble, no story about the good old days. It frightened me more than shouting would have.

"Ten years ago my mother walked out of this hall to run a delivery and never came back," I said. "You told me the roads took her. You told me not to go looking. And now a burned courier brings me a guild-sealed map and dies saying a word nobody's spoken aloud since before the ban, and you want me to sweep the ashes into the grate and make tea."

"Lena—"

"*Is it her?*"

The question cracked out of me louder than I meant. Corvane closed his eyes. For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the cooling stove and, faintly, from the street below, a milk cart that didn't exist anymore rattling through my memory.

"No," he said at last, gently, which is how I knew it might be yes. "It can't be her. Sit down, child. There are things better buried, and this guild has spent thirty years burying them, and I will not watch the last of us dig them up to die in the dark over them."

"The last of us," I repeated. "Three charmwrights selling luck-charms that don't work. That's what's left. We're already dying in the dark, Master. Slowly. Of dust and rent and shame." I picked the map off the table, ash and all, and folded it along its old creases. "She chose me. Out of everyone in Vell, she ran here. That means something."

"It means she was dying and we were close," Corvane said flatly. But his hands, on the table, were trembling, and old men's hands only tremble like that when they're holding themselves very hard together.

He forbade me to leave. Formally, standing, in the old words — *by charter and by spark* — which neither of us had any legal right to invoke anymore, and which hit me anyway like a slap from a dead man. I was to stay in the hall. I was to surrender the map. I was to let him handle it, whatever *it* was, and he would explain everything eventually, he promised, when it was safe.

"When it's safe," I said. "Master, nothing about tonight was safe. Safety left this city before I was born."

We stood there glaring at each other across the smell of burnt names, two stubborn people who loved each other in the way family does — inefficiently, and with raised voices — and I understood, distantly, that whichever of us won, we both lost something in the room that night.

* * *

I pretended to sleep.

This was easy; I've slept badly for ten years, and Corvane long ago learned not to check on me more than once a night. I heard him climb the stairs at midnight, slow as erosion. Heard his door. Heard — this was the part that nearly broke me — him begin to pray, in the old guild cadence, the words barely audible through the floor: *keep the spark, keep the circle, keep each other.*

Then I got up and committed my first real crime.

Not theft of the map — that was already mine, given freely by a dying woman, whatever the law said. No. The crime was the cabinet at the back of the storeroom, behind the stock of trinkets that didn't work, behind the false panels and the decoy junk we kept for exactly the raids that had come through the night market. The lock on that cabinet was guild-made, keyed to blood, which sounds impressive until you remember whose blood built it.

I cut my thumb on the little silver knife we used for trimming wick-cords and pressed it to the plate, and the lock thought about it, and then — with a sensation like a sigh, like something waking stiff from a long sleep — it opened.

Inside, on a bed of rotted blue velvet, lay the guild's one working charm.

It doesn't look like much. A lodestone the size of a plum, cased in pierced silver, warm to the touch the way a cat is warm. We called it the Wayfinder, though that was aspirational; what it actually did, when it deigned to do anything, was pull. Toward safety, toward sanctuary, toward whatever its maker had loved enough to anchor it with. Corvane swore it had once guided three lost guildwrights out of a collapsing mine. He also swore it hadn't worked in twenty years, and that anyone caught with it would be hanged twice, if the Wardens could manage it.

It was warm in my palm. Warmer than metal should be, sitting unused in the dark for two decades. And when I lifted it, it leaned — gently, insistently — north.

Toward the mountains. Toward the blank space on the map. Toward Emberhold.

"You knew," I whispered to it, and felt ridiculous, and kept whispering anyway. "You've known all along where you wanted to go."

I took cord, cold-weather oil, the good knife, and the food that wouldn't be missed. I wrote Corvane a note and then, at the last moment, didn't leave it — because anything written could be found by Wardens, and because I didn't know what to say that wasn't either an apology or an accusation, and I wasn't ready to find out which. Instead I banked the hearth so the hall wouldn't chill, straightened the ash-smeared ledger out of some stupid reflex of tidiness, and paused at the bottom of the stairs.

His door was shut. The prayer had stopped. From under the door: lamplight, and silence, and I chose — I will always wonder if wrongly — not to knock.

"Keep the circle," I murmured at the floorboards, in the old cadence, and went out into the dark.

The city at that hour is honest in a way it never manages by daylight: just cold stone, guttering lamps, your breath and your nerve. Two wardens-at-walk passed me by the tannery and didn't look twice, because a girl with a peddler's pack is furniture in Vell. I walked north through streets I had memorized for escape routes I never thought I'd use, and the Wayfinder pulled at my palm like a sleeping animal turning toward a window.

At the Northgate, where the road climbed out of the city and the walls fell away and the mountains stood up black against the stars — mountains that were supposed to hold nothing, nothing at all — I stopped and looked back once at Vell, all its thousand chimneys smoking its thousand small surrendered lives into the sky.

Then I looked at the lodestone in my hand, and it pulled, and I followed.

Behind me, faint as a memory of light, the city's bells began to ring the alarm hour — and ahead, high on the mountain road, something answered. A glow. Brief. Orange. Like a signal fire catching.

Like someone burning a message to say: *I see you coming.*
