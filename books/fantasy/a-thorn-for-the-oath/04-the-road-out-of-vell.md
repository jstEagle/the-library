## 4. The Road Out of Vell

The map said north, and north, it turned out, was uphill the entire way.

I'd been walking since before dawn, when I'd slipped out of the guildhall with Corvane snoring in his chair and the ledger's burned page still smelling of tallow and regret. My stolen prize sat in my pocket: a lodestone compass-charm no bigger than a thumbnail, its brass casing worn smooth by three generations of Kavanagh thumbs. It was supposed to point toward whatever you needed most. It had never once pointed at anything except a tavern.

Now, on the mountain road with Vell shrinking behind me into its bowl of chimney smoke, it trembled against my palm and pulled — actually pulled — toward the pass ahead. First time in my life. I chose not to find that comforting.

"Good morning," I told it anyway. "Nice of you to start working now, while I'm carrying stolen guild property through the one province where they hang us."

It spun smugly. Charms have no manners. That's the first thing they should teach apprentices, if anyone ever taught apprentices again.

The road out of Vell was a scar of old imperial paving, cracked and stitched with moss, climbing through terraced orchards gone feral. By midday I'd passed three shrines to the Unseen King — the real one, presumably, though nobody in Vell had ever seen him do anything more miraculous than appear on coins — and each shrine had been defaced with the king's new face, freshly painted, watching me with those eyes that never quite blinked in portraits. Someone had painted him over an older carving, and under the cheap pigment you could just make out scales.

I tried not to think about that.

I thought about Corvane instead, which was worse. The way he'd held the burning ledger page like it was a dying bird. *Some doors are locked for your protection, girl.* Well. I'd spent ten years being protected by a luck-charm business that didn't work and a master who drank himself brave every night. Protection, it turned out, looked a lot like neglect from the inside.

By evening I reached the switchbacks above the Graywater valley, where the road thinned to a ledge and the drop beside it went down long enough to reconsider your life choices. The charm was pulling hard now, steady as a heartbeat, and I was tired enough to talk to it again.

"Almost there, or leading me somewhere scenic to die?"

The answer came from behind me, in a voice like a drawn blade.

"That depends entirely on how sensible you decide to be."

I turned with my hands up, which was the only weapon I owned. A woman stood ten paces back on the ledge road, grey-cloaked, grey-eyed, with the silver badge of the Unbinding Wardens pinned at her collar. She had the stillness of someone who had done this a hundred times and never once enjoyed it. Her crossbow wasn't raised, which was either courtesy or confidence, and her boots said she could close those ten paces before I finished deciding to run.

"Lena Kavanagh," she said. "Charmwright Guild. Third rank, lapsed." She pronounced my guild name the way you'd pronounce a diagnosis. "Sergeant Ilse. You're not in trouble. You're just carrying something that is."

"And what's that?"

"You know what it is. A dead courier gave you a map, and instead of burning it, you're standing on it." She tilted her head. "I've read the reports. The courier in the back room. You were there."

"I sell luck-charms," I said. "Bad ones. That's the whole of my criminal career."

"Then walk back to Vell and keep selling them." She said it almost gently. Almost. "There's a life in that. Small, but whole. I've watched better people than you follow a piece of scorched paper off a cliff, and none of them thanked me for letting them."

Something in her voice snagged. Not threat. Experience.

"The man who died in our back room," I said. "Do you know who he was?"

Ilse was quiet a moment too long. "Yes," she said. "That's why I'm here alone, instead of with a squad. He was—" She stopped herself. "He was known to the Wardens. Walk back, Kavanagh. Please."

Nobody had said please to me in years, and it nearly worked. Then the charm in my pocket gave a hard, urgent pull, like a dog scenting home, and I remembered the word he'd breathed into my ear with his last breath. *Emberhold.* Ten years I'd carried nothing but ash where my mother used to be. I was not giving up the first warm thing I'd found since.

"I'm sorry," I said, and stepped backward off the road.

I want it noted that I'm not brave. What I am is lucky — occasionally, ironically, in the manner of a joke the universe has been telling for twenty-three years. There happened to be a goat path two feet below the ledge, invisible until you were falling onto it. I landed badly, slid thirty feet on gravel and dignity, and came apart from the road in a clatter of stones.

"KAVANAGH!" Ilse's voice, furious, echoing off the valley walls. Boots on the ledge above. But the goat path dropped into a ravine thick with pine shadow, and I ran, because running downhill is the one athletic skill my trade requires. Behind me, fainter: whistles, two notes, sharp and repeating. Calling something in.

I was maybe half a mile down when the whistles stopped meaning anything, because the ravine filled with light.

Not firelight. Not lantern-light. Light with a color to it, gold going to amber going to something older, pouring around the bend ahead like spilled honey. I skidded to a stop, and there in the middle of the path stood a cart — a tinker's cart, hung with pots and pans and wire cages of glass baubles — and beside the cart stood a broad man in his fifties with a grey beard trimmed like he'd given up arguing with it, holding up one hand, from which all that impossible light was streaming.

"Hold still," he said mildly, as if we were discussing weather. "They're about to be very confused."

Above us, on the ledge road, voices began shouting. Someone screamed about the sun. Somebody else started praying.

"What did you do?" I whispered.

"Misdirection," said the tinker. "Old guild-work. Very old." His hand closed; the light guttered out, leaving afterimage ghosts across my vision. In the sudden dark he looked at me — really looked, at my face, then lower, at my hands, at the burn-scar on my wrist shaped like a stylus that every guild apprentice got at their oath-taking. His expression did something complicated.

"Kavanagh blood," he said quietly. "Gods help us all. Get in the cart."

* * *

From the field journal of Warden-Sergeant Ilse:

The Kavanagh girl went off the ledge road at the second switchback. Alive — I heard her land, gods forgive me for laughing even inwardly — and vanished into the Graywater ravine. I called for lanterns and we followed the goat paths down.

At dusk we found what the men are still calling a miracle and I am calling a professional embarrassment. Light, vast and sourceless, filling the ravine mouth — the kind of phenomenon the old texts call *guild-luminance*, third-rank work at minimum, though no third-rank guild has produced it in thirty years. Two of my wardens fell to their knees. One recited the Coronation Oath, incorrectly, twice.

By the time the light died, there was nothing in the ravine but a cold campfire, wheel ruts heading east toward the high pass, and this, wedged under a stone where I was clearly meant to find it: a copper coin from the reign of Aldric the Third. Who has been dead thirty years. Whose face on the coin does not match the face on the throne.

I have written in my report that the luminance was a natural phenomenon, refraction off the valley mists. My lieutenant believes me, because he wants to. The men believe the miracle, because they want to more.

I have also written down, in this private book, where the wheel ruts go, so that I will remember why I chose to follow them slowly, alone, and without filing the route.

Thirty years ago I swore the same oath everyone in the Wardens swears. I have begun to wonder what exactly it was we swore it to.

— I.

* * *

The cart smelled of solder, lamp oil, and forty years of roads. We rattled east through the dark with the lamps hooded, the horse plodding with the untroubled patience of an animal that had seen everything twice, and the tinker drove with his elbows and studied me sidelong like a man doing sums he didn't like.

"You're going to ask who I am," he said finally.

"You're going to tell me anyway. You've got the look."

"The look?"

"Of a man rehearsing a confession." I hugged my knees on the pile of sacking among the pans. "I grew up above a shop. I know a customer working up to bad news."

That surprised a laugh out of him — short, rusty, like a hinge. "Fair. Dain," he said. "Formerly Master Dain, Charmwrights' Guild, formerly of Vell, formerly a great many things." He glanced at my scarred wrist again. "I took the oath before your mother did. Before your master Corvane, even, gods preserve his pickled liver."

My stomach dropped somewhere near the axle. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew of her. Everyone did, at the end." The wheels clattered over a rut; he let the silence ride it out. "And I know where that map in your coat leads, girl, because I helped make the lock on its door."

"You—" The pieces rearranged themselves ugly. "You're the guildmaster they struck off. Before I was born. The one who went over to the palace works."

"I built the siphon cradles," he said flatly. "Twelve of them, down in the deep vaults, to hold a fire that shouldn't be held. I told myself it was engineering. Fire in a cage, power for the city, progress. I was very good at telling myself things." He flicked the reins needlessly. "Then one day I looked at what the fire was actually feeding, up in the bright rooms of the palace, and I left. Swore off the craft on the palace steps. Every charm I owned, I buried in a riverbed — save the one I used tonight, which I swore on my own name I'd never use again, because it only answers to a guildmaster's oath and I am not supposed to be one anymore."

"Why break it? Tonight, for me?"

Ahead of us the pass opened between black shoulders of mountain, and far up it, faint as a coal under ash, something glowed. The charm in my pocket pulled toward it so hard it hurt.

Dain saw where I was looking. His jaw tightened.

"Because thirty years ago I chained a dragon's fire to a lie," he said, "and tonight a Kavanagh walked past me carrying the key. Some debts you pay when they knock." He clicked his tongue at the horse and the cart swung onto the pass road. "Sleep if you can, Lena Kavanagh. Tomorrow we reach the border towns, and after that the Emberhold — and once we're inside the wards, nothing that wears a crown in Vell will be able to pretend it doesn't know we're coming."

Behind us, far down the valley, a single Warden lantern moved along the road — unhurried, alone, following our ruts at exactly the pace of someone pretending to lose the trail.

I pretended not to see it. Dain pretended not to see me pretending.

We rode north into the mountains, three liars in a cart full of small bells, and every time the wheels turned, the bells rang softly, all the way up into the dark.
