## 5. The Hollow Court

I had a rule about confessions: never make them anywhere with good acoustics. Bad acoustics give you an excuse to mumble, and mumbling is the coward's mercy. But Saltmarch was a border town built entirely of slate and river-stone, and every word I said in that inn bounced off the walls like a guilty conscience looking for somewhere to hide.

Lena Kavanagh sat across from me with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and she asked the question I'd been dreading since the mountain road.

"You knew," she said. "On the road. When Ilse showed you the Warden sigil, you weren't surprised. You were *recognized*."

"I'm a memorable face."

"Dain."

There it was. My name in her mouth, flat as a dropped coin. Ten years I'd been nobody — Tinker Vess, Tinker Aldous, once memorably Tinker Old Man Who Sells Broken Spoons And Should Be Pitied — and this girl with her mother's stubborn jaw said my name like she was pulling a splinter out.

I looked at my tea. It was terrible. Everything in Saltmarch was terrible except the acoustics.

"The king of Vell is dead," I said.

The inn went quiet around us in the particular way rooms do when they're pretending not to listen. A fishwife two tables over kept shelling peas with great dedication. Lena didn't blink.

"He rode past my stall last Wintersday," she said. "Waved from the balcony."

"A man waved from the balcony." I turned my cup a quarter-turn, a habit I'd picked up from watching liars. "Thirty years ago, King Aldric Vell went up to the Emberhold to negotiate a treaty with the last dragon. He took forty men and his court charmwright — a young man with excellent references and no sense whatsoever."

"Your references."

"My sense was considered excellent at the time. That should tell you something about references." I set the cup down before I could keep turning it. "We came back. Thirty-nine of us came back. His Majesty did not. What came back wore his face, sat his throne, signed his decrees, and has done so for thirty years without aging so much as a wrinkle. The court calls it long life and good breeding. I call it what it is."

"And what is it?"

"Hollow." The word tasted like ash, which was appropriate, because ash is where it started. "A shell shaped like a king, sustained by fire that isn't its own. Dragon-fire. Siphoned out of the Emberhold through conduits we — " I stopped. Started again, because she deserved the whole ugly thing or none of it. "Through conduits *I* helped design. I was young. The Guildmaster said it would be a mercy, that the dragon was dying anyway, that its fire could warm the kingdom for a century instead of guttering alone in a mountain. They didn't tell me what the fire was *for*. By the time I understood, the thing on the throne had already learned to smile."

Lena's hands had gone very still around the cup. "And the real king?"

"Alive." I watched her flinch. "Bound beneath the Emberhold, dreaming, while his fire feeds the impostor's crown. The binding needs a feeder. The siphon needs a keeper. The Wardens were created to guard both and call it law."

"That's why they hunt guild-charms."

"That's why they hunt anything with a spark in it. A true charm works by the same principles as the siphon — draw, hold, give. Every luck-charm your guild sells is a tiny heresy against the throne's monopoly on borrowed fire. They can't have the people remembering that magic can be *given* rather than taken." I smiled, and it felt like a wound reopening. "So they burn the guildhalls one by one and call it public safety. Three of you left. I counted more than three hundred, when I was your age."

The fishwife had stopped shelling peas. I pretended not to notice. In border towns, pretending not to notice things is a survival skill, right after running and slightly ahead of lying.

"Why tell me now?" Lena asked. "You've had ten days. You've had every night's campfire."

"Because the map leads to the Emberhold, and the Emberhold leads to the siphon, and if you walk into that vault thinking you're going to find treasure, you'll die ignorant. If you walk in knowing what I know, you'll die informed. It's the best inheritance I have to offer."

"That's a horrible inheritance."

"I never claimed to be a good father figure. Or mentor. Or person, some weeks."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died there. Then: "The courier. The one who gave me the map. She was burned — Wardens' work?"

"Or siphon-work. The conduits leak. Anyone who travels too close to the Emberhold's veins comes back scorched, if they come back at all." I hesitated. There was one more thing, and I'd been carrying it like a stone in my boot for ten days, and boots are meant for walking away. "Lena. Your mother didn't vanish. She ran. From the Guild, from the Wardens, from something she'd learned. Corvane knows what. He burned that page of the ledger because he's spent thirty years keeping people alive by keeping them stupid, and I can't even hate him for it."

"You could have led with any of this."

"I'm leading with all of it now. Consider the acoustics. Everyone in this room knows I'm a traitor by now; it seemed rude to leave you out."

That was when the front door opened, and the room went quiet in the other way — the way rooms go quiet when pretending stops being an option.

Three Wardens came in out of the rain. Not Sergeant Ilse's patrol; these were strangers, grey-cloaked, unhurried, which was worse than hurrying. Unhurried Wardens are Wardens who already know how the evening ends. The one in front carried an Unbinding rod, and the rod was already lit, a thread of stolen gold light crawling along its length like a worm made of morning.

"Tinker," he said, to me, pleasantly. "Vess, isn't it? We've been chasing your cart tracks since Harrow Ford."

"Forks," I said. "Terrible town. The mud there is mostly mud."

"Stand up slowly."

Now, here is a thing about guilt: it doesn't paralyze you. Guilt is a fuel, same as fear or love or spite. For ten years I'd been rationing mine, hoarding it, saving it for an emergency, the way misers save coins they'll never spend. And the rod came up, and Lena's hand went into her coat for the charm she stole off my own shelf — the one working charm in the whole rotten guild, and I'd let her take it because I couldn't bear to watch her leave empty-handed —

— and I thought: *enough*. Enough rationing. Enough being a tinker.

I stood up slowly, exactly as instructed, and then I did the thing I'd sworn on a dead woman's memory I would never do again. I reached down into the place where my spark lived, the little banked coal of guild-blood that I'd been starving on purpose for a decade, and I opened the conduit.

Not at the Wardens. Never at people; that road only goes downhill. At the inn itself. At the hearth-fire, the lanterns, the rain on the roof, the river under the floorboards — everything warm and moving and *giving*. A charmwright's oldest trick, older than guilds: redirect. Draw, hold, give. I pulled the light of every lamp in Saltmarch into myself and threw it sideways, out the windows, down the street, a false flare of guild-magic blooming in the market square like a second sunrise.

Every head in the inn turned toward it. Including, fatally, the Wardens'.

"*Run*," I told Lena, and we ran.

* * *

Saltmarch burns beautifully, which I mean as a technical observation and not a compliment. Slate doesn't catch, but the tarred boats do, and the fish-oil stores, and the hay barns along the quay, and by the time we reached the treeline the whole waterfront was a wall of orange reflected twice over in the river. Behind it, horns. The Wardens hadn't bought the decoy forever. Forever is not a thing Wardens purchase; they rent it by the hour.

"They'll burn the town because of us," Lena said. She was breathing hard beside me in the dark, and there was ash in her hair, and she was watching the fires the way you watch a debt collector count your spoons.

"No. They'll burn the town because of *me*, specifically, and they'd have burned it anyway once they confirmed I'd passed through. I'm not a fugitive to them, Lena. I'm evidence." I kept walking, because if I stopped walking I'd have to look at the fire, and if I looked at the fire I'd see a different fire, thirty years old, in a corridor of the Emberhold where a young man with excellent references had just realized what he'd built. "They don't leave witnesses near evidence."

"You could have told me that part before the confession."

"I'm telling you now. Pace yourself. I've got decades of material."

She laughed — one short, cracked note, gone immediately — and I counted it a victory anyway. In my experience you measure a night by its small survivals.

We climbed. The trees closed behind us, black on blacker, and the horns fell back toward the burning town where the fishwife and the pea-sheller and the innkeeper with the terrible tea would be standing in the square watching their livelihood go up, and hating two names: the tinker's, and whatever name the Wardens gave the girl.

"Lena." I stopped at the ridgeline. Below and ahead, far off under the clouds, the mountains stood up against the sky like closed doors. "There's one more thing you should know before we get there, and I want you to hear it while the town's still burning, because I don't think I'll be able to say it when it's quiet."

She waited. She was good at waiting. Her mother had been too.

"The siphon I helped build — it doesn't run on machinery. Conduits, yes, channels, valves of worked stone and bound silver, but the heart of it, the thing that keeps the fire flowing, is a *person*. Someone with guild-blood, bound into the mechanism, giving forever. That was my design." My voice came out level, which I resented. I'd earned the right to shake. "They used someone else. But the terms of the binding — who can bind, who can unbind, what it costs — those are mine. Whatever you find in the Emberhold, whatever choice the vault puts in front of you, know that I wrote half the rules."

The wind came up the ridge and carried smoke with it, and Lena looked at me for a long moment with her mother's eyes, which is to say with eyes that saw straight through a man to the boy he'd been and refused to look away.

"Then you'll be there," she said, "to help me break them."

Below us, Saltmarch burned. Ahead of us, the mountain waited with its stolen king and its dying dragon and its one-way doors. And in my chest, the spark I'd starved for ten years sat up like an old dog hearing its name, warm and traitorous and awake.

I followed her down toward the dark.
