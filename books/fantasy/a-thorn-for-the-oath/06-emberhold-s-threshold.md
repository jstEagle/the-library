## 6. Emberhold's Threshold

The map stopped making sense on the third day, which was when it started making sense.

I'd spent the whole climb arguing with it. The courier's scorch marks had eaten the lower corner where a proper map would say *you are here*, and what remained was a tangle of ink lines that looked less like geography and more like someone had dropped a spider in a candle and let it run. Dain squinted at it over my shoulder every few hours and offered opinions I hadn't asked for.

"That ridge," he said, pointing with a stub of charcoal he'd been chewing. "The line doubles back on itself. That's not a path, that's a switchback."

"It's a scar from the burn."

"It's a switchback."

It was a switchback. I hated that about him — the way he'd be wrong for an hour and then right all at once, without apology, as if being right were just another chore he'd gotten stuck with. We climbed through pine that gave way to bare grey stone, through stone to snow, through snow to a wind that seemed personally offended by our presence. By dusk we'd left the treeline behind and the world up there was nothing but rock and silence and the enormous indifferent shoulder of the mountain, which the old maps called Karrow's Tooth and the map in my hands called something else entirely.

Because near the top, in handwriting I still couldn't place — small, cramped letters pressed hard into the paper like the writer had been afraid the words would escape — someone had written: *the door remembers blood.*

"Cheerful," Dain said when I read it aloud.

"You could have stayed in Vell."

"I could have," he agreed, "and then who would you have to be annoyed at?"

We made camp in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel. Dain built a fire with the efficiency of a man who'd done it ten thousand times and enjoyed none of them. I sat with my back against the stone and took out the charm.

I'd carried it since the guildhall. It was Corvane's best work, or had been, once — a flat disc of tarnished silver on a leather cord, worked with a spiral pattern so worn you needed faith and good light to see it. Guild-charms weren't supposed to do much anymore. Everyone knew that. The fire that fed them had thinned to almost nothing, and what the three of us sold in Vell was mostly hope hammered into cheap metal. But this one had always been different. When I held it, sometimes, it felt less like an object and more like a held breath.

"What is it?" Dain asked. He was watching me over the fire with those hooded eyes of his, the ones that had seen too much and confessed to none of it.

"A luck-charm." I turned it over. In the firelight the spiral caught and held the flame's reflection, and for a moment it looked like the spiral was turning. "Corvane said it came down from before the ban. He said it was older than the guild."

"May I?"

I hesitated. Everything I owned fit in one satchel, and most of that was stock. But Dain had misdirected two Wardens on a mountain road with a charm he swore he'd never use again, and he hadn't asked me for anything except my share of the bread. I passed it across the fire.

He held it the way you'd hold a sleeping animal. His thumb traced the spiral once, twice — and stopped.

"Lena." His voice had gone very quiet. "Where did Corvane get this?"

"He never said. He said it chose the guildhall, if you can believe that. That it was here before any of us."

Dain didn't laugh. That frightened me more than anything he could have said. He passed it back and wouldn't meet my eyes for the rest of the evening.

* * *

We reached the gate at midmorning, and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it properly, so let me fail honestly.

The mountain simply ended its argument with itself and became a wall — a sheer face of black stone rising out of the scree like the prow of a drowned ship. And set into that face, half-swallowed by centuries of lichen and frost-tilted rock, was a door.

Not a door like doors are. It was a single slab of some darker stone, thirty feet high if it was one, and carved across its whole surface with the same spiral I wore around my neck — except here the spirals were alive with age, deep-cut, deliberate, radiating outward from a central seal the size of a wagon wheel. No hinges. No handle. No seam for a key. Just that vast carved face, patient as a closed eye.

There was no inscription. There didn't need to be. Every Charmwright learns the spiral before they learn their letters; it's the first thing we draw, the first thing we're taught means *making* — the old sign, the maker's mark, the shape magic makes when it passes through willing hands. Seeing it thirty feet tall, cut into a mountain, was like hearing your own name shouted by a crowd.

"The Emberhold," Dain said. He sounded like a man reading his own eulogy. "I only ever saw the siphon-end. I never knew this part survived."

"Siphon-end of what?" I'd asked him versions of that question for six days and gotten six evasions. This time he looked at me — really looked, the way you look at someone when you've decided the truth will hurt less than the lie.

"When we reach whatever's behind this door," he said, "you'll want to sit down first."

I turned to the door instead. Because the map's margin note was doing something to my thoughts I didn't like, and because the charm against my sternum had gone — there was no other word for it — *eager.*

I lifted the cord over my head and held the disc up to the carved seal.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. It was a trinket, a pretty failure, a piece of the guild's long humiliation—

The spiral turned.

Not the reflection. Not a trick of light. The engraved lines of the disc moved, slow and grinding, like a lock accepting a key, and light came up out of the metal the color of coals breathed upon — orange, gold, a red so deep it was nearly living — and the same light answered from the door. The great carved spirals woke one after another, ringing outward from the center like ripples in stone, and the mountain *hummed*. I felt it in my teeth. In my ribs. Somewhere under my feet, vast and slow, something that had been asleep drew a breath.

And the charm in my hand flared true.

Understand — I had spent my whole life around charms that barely worked. I knew their flickers and stutters, their apologetic warmth, the way they'd glow for a buyer and die by morning. This was not that. This was a bonfire where I'd known candle-ends. Light poured off the little disc until I had to squint to hold it, and heat rolled up my arm, not burning but *recognizing*, the way a hand finds a hand in the dark — and underneath the light I felt the other thing, the impossible thing, the feeling of being *known*. The mountain knew me. Not my name. Deeper than names. It knew whatever runs in guild-blood, whatever thin thread we'd been selling scraps of in Vell's night market without ever once understanding what we were selling.

"Lena." Dain's voice, hoarse. "Lena, put it in the seal."

My hand shook. I pressed the disc flat against the wagon-wheel sigil.

The door opened the way dawn opens — gradually, then all at once. Black stone swung inward on darkness that wasn't empty but *deep*, a corridor receding farther than the mountain should have allowed, its walls veined faintly with the same coal-colored light, pulsing in a rhythm I realized with a jolt was not mechanical at all.

It was a heartbeat.

Warm air breathed out past us, smelling of hot metal and something older — cedar, maybe, or memory. The charm went quiet against my palm, banked now, satisfied, a dog that has finally come home.

"Well," Dain said unsteadily. "That answers whether the blood holds."

"Dain." I couldn't look away from the dark. "What did we just open?"

"Ask me inside," he said. "Where it's harder to leave."

* * *

The corridor swallowed sound. Our footsteps went in and didn't echo — the walls drank them, the way velvet drinks light. The vein-light brightened as we descended, or perhaps the dark simply got closer to its source, and after a hundred paces I noticed the rhythm of it had synced with my own heart, and after two hundred I couldn't tell which was following which.

Dain walked ahead of me with his shoulders squared and his hands slightly raised, and I understood that he wasn't admiring the architecture. He was waiting for something. He'd been waiting since the gate.

I was still working out how to ask him what when the light ahead changed.

It dimmed — no. It *flinched*. All along the corridor the veins guttered like candles in a draft, and the heartbeat-rhythm stumbled, skipped, caught again faster. Dain stopped so abruptly I walked into his back.

"Wardens," he breathed. "They followed the gate-light. Gods' teeth, of course they did — you could see that flare from the valley—"

"How far?"

"Close enough that we don't discuss it."

He shoved me toward an alcove — a shallow recess in the wall where the stone curved inward like a cupped palm — and then he did the thing I'll carry longer than the wound he took there. He stepped back out into the corridor, alone, between me and the dark we'd come from, and he reached into his coat and took out a charm I'd never seen him touch.

It was ugly. Old iron, unpolished, worked with a spiral so crude it might have been scratched by a child, and I remembered — I finally remembered — what he'd told me on the road, drunk on exhaustion rather than wine: *I swore I'd never use it again.* He'd built things, once, before the disgrace. Big things. Things with consequences.

"Lena," he said, without turning around. "Whatever happens next, you go deeper. You don't stop, you don't look back, and you find whatever this mountain is keeping. That's the whole job now. That's your mother's job, if the map says what I think it says."

"My mother's—" 

Boots. Many boots, ringing up the corridor from the direction we'd entered — no, wait, that was wrong, we'd heard nothing coming, the walls drank sound, which meant—

"They've been behind us since the gate," Dain said calmly, horribly. "The corridor carries their steps forward. It wants us warned. Old courtesy." He raised the iron charm. "Go, Lena."

The first Wardens rounded the bend even as he spoke — four of them in grey lacquered armor, Unbinding-staves already humming that dead-felt hum I'd learned to fear in Vell's night market, and their sergeant's voice rang flat off the stone: *"By writ of the crown, stand and be bound."*

Dain laughed. It was not a nice laugh.

"I stood once," he said. "Never again."

The iron charm went up like a struck bell. Light — real light, guild-light, furious and ancient — burst down the corridor in both directions, and the mountain itself seemed to take his side: the veins flared white, the heartbeat-rhythm roared into a drumbeat, and the Wardens staggered as the floor tilted beneath them, gently, impossibly, the corridor remembering that it was a throat and choosing to swallow. Stone groaned. Dust sheeted down. One Warden went to her knees; another's staff cracked like ice on a pond.

But there were more behind the four, and Unbinding-light speared through the glare and took Dain high in the side, and I heard him make a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

"*GO!*"

I went.

I ran deeper while the corridor shuddered and the light warred behind me, past alcoves, past carvings whose shapes I'd remember later and grieve later, my stolen charm blazing against my chest like a second heart, guiding me downward, inward, toward the source of the pulse — toward whatever the mountain had woken for, whatever my blood was for, whatever my mother had died pressing a scorched map into a stranger's hands to reach.

Behind me, Dain was still holding the passage.

Ahead of me, the dark opened like a held breath about to end — and somewhere far below, something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
