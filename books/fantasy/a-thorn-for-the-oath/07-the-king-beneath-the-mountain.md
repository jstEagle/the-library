## 7. The King Beneath the Mountain

I had imagined a lot of things in the vaults beneath the Emberhold. Treasure, mostly. That's what maps like mine were supposed to lead to — chests of guild-silver, charms that actually worked, maybe a grimoire with my name already inscribed in it by some optimistic ancestor.

What I got instead was a king on a slab of stone, dreaming.

The passage Dain held open behind me — at cost I didn't want to think about yet, because thinking about it would stop my feet from moving — let out into a chamber so vast that my little stolen charm's glow couldn't find the far wall of it. The light just went out into the dark and gave up, like everything else in Vell. Underfoot, the floor was smooth black stone veined with something that wasn't quite gold and wasn't quite ember, and it was warm. Warm like a sleeping animal. Warm like a hand you'd forgotten holding yours.

And there, in the middle of all that dark warmth, lay a man.

He was older than any portrait of Aldric IV I'd ever seen on a Warden's wanted-poster or a tax-office wall. Gray had taken most of his hair. His face had lines carved deep enough to lose a coin in. He wore no crown, only a plain circlet of iron, the kind kings wear when they mean it, and chains of the same black stone as the floor ran from his wrists and ankles and throat down into the rock as if the mountain had simply grown over him while he slept.

His chest rose. Fell. Rose again.

"He's alive," I said out loud, because the alternative was screaming, and I've always had rules about where I do that.

* * *

The dragon was harder to see, which sounds impossible until you understand that she was dying, and dying things make themselves small.

She lay coiled along the eastern curve of the chamber, and at first I took her for part of the architecture — another ridge of the mountain, another vein in the floor. Then one of the veins moved. Then an eye the size of a cart wheel opened, amber going gray at the rim like an egg gone bad, and looked at me with an expression I recognized from every night market stall I'd ever packed up in defeat: the look of something old, tired, and profoundly unimpressed.

"You're late," she said. Her voice came not through the air but through the floor, through my boots, through the bones of my feet, the way thunder comes through walls.

"I— sorry?" My voice cracked on the second syllable. Very heroic. Corvane would have wept.

"Thirty years, girl. Thirty years the fire has fed two mouths instead of none, and the courier promised me guild-blood before the last of me goes out." Her nostril flared — a wind hot enough to curl the hair off my forearms — and her eye narrowed. "You are guild-blood."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because apparently when a dragon asks if you're real, you confirm. "Lena Kavanagh. Charmwright's Guild. Third rank." I hesitated. "Of three members."

Something that might have been a laugh shuddered through her coils, and dust sifted down from a ceiling I still hadn't found. "Three. When I made my bargain there were four hundred. When the courier came to me, ninety. Your kind burns down to embers and calls it a guild still." The amber eye drifted closed, then open again, slower each time. "Come closer, Kavanagh. Looking at you costs me nothing. Everything else does."

I went closer. I want it noted that this was a decision, not an accident. My legs shook the whole way, but they carried me, and I have decided to be proud of them.

Up close she smelled of hot iron and rain-on-stone and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of the guildhall's back room where old charms went to be unmade — the smell of magic that has been somewhere and come back changed. Her scales near her breastbone had gone dull as old pewter, and between two of them, set into her flesh like a coin pressed into wax, burned a sliver of light. Red-gold. Restless. The same color, I realized with a lurch, as the glow in the black veins under my feet, the same color as the warmth coming off the king's chains, the same color as —

"The fire," I said. "The thing wearing his crown in Vell. It eats this."

"Everything eats this." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "The prison holds him because I agreed to hold the prison. The impostor wears his face because the prison needed a warden who would never wonder why the door only opens inward. And I am the hearth for all of it." A plume of smoke curled from her jaw, thin and gray. "I was young once, Kavanagh. Young dragons make bargains the way young charmwrights make luck-charms: sincerely, and badly."

* * *

She showed me. That was the worst part — she didn't tell me, she *showed* me, pressing the memory into my mind the way you'd press a seal into soft wax, and for a few terrible seconds I was thirty years younger and enormous and full of fire.

I saw the true king, Aldric, come down into the mountain alone — because he was a man who did things alone, that was his whole tragedy — carrying a charter and a question: *the Wardens say magic must be bound out of Vell; will you bind yourself too, old friend, so no man can ever again raise a dragon against the city?*

And I saw her, flattered and frightened and certain she was being noble, say yes. Saw her wind the binding herself — her own fire braided into the terms, because a binding is only as strong as what feeds it, and nothing feeds a binding like a willing heart. Saw her write the terms in fire on the inside of the mountain:

*A binding freely given may only be freely ended.*

*Only guild-blood may speak the ending.*

*And the blood must surrender its own spark — wholly, gladly, without taking anything back.*

Then the years compressed. I saw her realize, too late, that a binding sustained on her fire would gutter when she did — and that when it guttered, the impostor-warden would not gently release the throne, it would devour the last of the fire and the last of the king both, and go looking for more. I saw the couriers she'd sent out over decades, each one carrying a scrap of map, hunting for guild-blood that still remembered how to give instead of sell. I saw the last one — a woman with gray in her hair and a scar on her left hand that I have seen a thousand times across a kitchen table in a house that stopped existing ten years ago —

I came back to myself on my knees, with my palms flat on warm stone and my breath coming in pieces.

"That courier," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, dragged over gravel. "The one who died in the guildhall. She had —"

"I know whose daughter you are." The dragon's eye had gone very soft, for a cartwheel of amber. "She told me about you. The girl who mended broken charms instead of scrapping them. She said the guild had failed you in every way except the one that mattered." Smoke curled. "I begin to see what she meant. You're still here. Most who see my memory run."

"I've run plenty," I said. "Just never downhill."

* * *

I sat against the king's slab for a while, because my legs had filed a formal complaint, and because sitting next to thirty years of chained sleep felt less lonely than standing. His breathing was slow and even, the breathing of a man deep in a dream he can't wake from, and up close I could see that the black chains weren't touching his skin — they hovered a hair's breadth away, held off by the last stubborn heat rising off him. The fire kept him alive. The fire kept him prisoner. Same flame, both jobs, and the flame was running out.

I could see that now with plain eyes. Where the veins in the floor branched toward the impostor's siphon-channel — a great dark throat bored westward, toward Vell — the glow had gone thin, watery, a candle seen through fog. And where the veins fed the king's chains, it was worse. One of them had already gone dark. The chain it fed had settled half an inch closer to his throat.

"How long?" I asked the dragon.

"Ask me something easier. How long is a song? How long is forgiveness?" But her eye tracked to the darkened vein, and stayed there. "Weeks. Perhaps days. The impostor feels me failing — it grows greedy, draws harder. Greed hastens what it fears. They usually do."

"And if I do nothing, it takes the fire, kills him, and comes out of the mountain hungry."

"Yes."

"And if I do the — the other thing." My hands had closed into fists without consulting me. "I give up my spark. All of it. Wholly, gladly, nothing taken back." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Here's my problem. I don't know that I have a spark. Our charms barely work. I sold a luck-charm two weeks ago that did the exact opposite of luck. Whatever the guild-blood in me is, it's pretty diluted."

The dragon was quiet long enough that I thought she might have slipped into sleep, or worse. Then, through the floor: "Show me your charm."

I fished it out — the one working charm in the entire guildhall, the one I'd stolen off Corvane's shelf on my way out the door, a small iron compass-needle on a cord that was supposed to point toward whatever you needed most. It had pointed at the mountain gate. It had flared like a festival-lantern when my blood touched the stone. It had been the single most reliable object of my entire life, for approximately four days.

I held it up. It glowed steadily, pointing — I checked — directly at the dying dragon.

"There," she rumbled. "Do you know what that means?"

"That I need you? I could've guessed."

"It means your spark is not small, girl. It means it has been *starving*. A spark fed on trinkets and lies forgets its own size. Yours has spent ten years waiting in the dark like the man on that stone." The amber eye fixed on me, and I felt weighed, measured, and — horribly — found worth the trouble. "You could unmake this binding tonight. Or tomorrow. You could walk away and live a small safe life and let Vell burn in a month. Those are your terms. I wrote them fairly, and I have regretted their fairness every day since."

I looked at the king. At the chain drifting nearer to his throat. At the compass needle glowing in my hand, steady as a heartbeat, pointing at the oldest, tiredest creature I had ever met.

Wholly. Gladly. Nothing taken back.

I thought about the guildhall. About Corvane burning ledger pages to keep secrets instead of people. About a market full of strangers buying luck they didn't believe in. About my mother, walking into fire with a scrap of map and her daughter's name folded into the margin, and I understood, finally, what she'd been carrying all those years she was gone.

Not a mission. An inheritance. The kind you don't get — the kind you *give*.

"All right," I said, and stood up, and my legs only shook a little. "But I'm going to need to know exactly how this works, because the last time I improvised a ritual I set fire to Corvane's beard, and he's still bitter about it."

The dragon's laugh moved through the whole mountain this time.

And then, from the passage behind me — the passage Dain was holding — came a sound that turned my blood to well-water.

A Warden's horn. Three notes. *Found them.*
