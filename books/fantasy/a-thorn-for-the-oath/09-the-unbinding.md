## 9. The Unbinding

The dragon's fire had gone the color of old embers in a grate at dawn — that particular orange that means *soon* — and I stood before the binding with my hands shaking and a joke dying on my tongue.

I want to say I was brave. What I actually said, out loud, to a bound king and a dying dragon and no one else who mattered, was: "Well. This is either going to work or it's going to be very embarrassing for both of us."

The dragon's eye, vast as a barn door and dimming by the minute, did not laugh. Dragons, I've decided, have no sense of humor. Or perhaps they simply don't find me funny. Fair enough. Most people don't.

The binding itself wasn't what I'd expected. I'd imagined chains, wards, something you could see and hate. Instead it was a knot of light wound through the king's chest like thread through cloth, silver-white and patient, fed by a hairline vein of fire that ran up out of the vault, through the mountain's spine, all the way to Vell — where something wearing a dead man's crown drank from it every day and called itself a king.

Thirty years of stolen fire. Ten years of my guild selling trinkets that barely worked because whatever true magic remained had been siphoned off to keep a hollow thing breathing.

And the terms, which the dragon had shown me without ceremony, without apology, the way you'd show someone the fine print on a contract after they'd already signed:

*Only guild-blood may unmake it. Only freely surrendered.*

Not taken. Not tricked. Given.

"Corvane knew," I said. My voice sounded small in a space built for thunder. "He burned the page because he couldn't bear to be the one who told someone."

The dragon's breath moved through the vault, warm and smelling of iron and rain. It didn't answer. It was saving what it had left. So was I, if I'm honest — though what I had left wasn't much more than a spark, the little ember every Charmwright carried, the thing that made charms *almost* work instead of never working. The thing I'd spent ten years pretending was an inheritance instead of an insult.

Somewhere above us, muffled through a hundred yards of mountain, steel rang against steel.

Dain.

I closed my eyes for exactly one second — I counted it; I've always been good at counting when I'm terrified — and then I stepped forward.

* * *

You should understand what surrendering your spark feels like, because nobody had explained it to me and I spent a great deal of time in the following weeks wishing someone had.

It doesn't hurt.

That's the cruel part. I braced for pain the way you brace for a Warden's binding-iron, teeth set, shoulders up around my ears, and instead it felt like setting down something heavy that I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten it was heavy. Like taking off wet boots. Like the first breath after a fever breaks.

I pressed my palm flat against the knot of light in the king's chest and I gave it everything.

My spark went out of me like water finding a crack in a dam. I felt it leave — felt it as a warmth under my breastbone thinning, thinning, thinning to nothing — and I felt the binding drink it, and I heard, distantly, the way you hear a bell through several walls, the sound the mountain made when thirty years of wrong began to come undone.

The knot loosened. Not all at once. It came apart the way Corvane's hands used to come apart a badly-made charm: one thread at a time, with terrible patience, muttering about shortcuts.

And here is the other thing nobody tells you. As the spark drained out of me, I saw things. Flashes. The courier's burned face resolving into features I'd spent ten years refusing to remember — my mother's jaw, my mother's crooked smile, gone before I could finish recognizing her. A younger Dain, standing in a circle of chalk with his sleeves rolled up, drawing the first line of the siphon while men in crown-livery watched and nodded. Corvane, decades ago, arguing in this same vault, arms wide, voice cracking: *you cannot feed a lie forever.* And Ilse — Ilse at nineteen, swearing her Warden oath with her eyes fixed somewhere over the false king's shoulder, because she already couldn't look at him directly.

I hadn't known any of that. But the fire knew. The fire remembered everyone who'd touched it, and now, briefly, so did I.

Then it was gone, and I was just a woman kneeling in the dark next to a stranger, holding nothing.

The last thread of the binding snapped.

* * *

The king woke the way mountains wake — slowly, and then all at once.

His eyes opened. They were ordinary eyes, gray, bloodshot, confused. Thirty years of dreaming looked out of them and then, gradually, a man looked out instead.

"You're not the dragon," he said. His voice was rust.

"No," I managed. "Sorry to disappoint."

He tried to sit up. Failed. Tried again. I put a hand behind his shoulders and helped, and he weighed almost nothing, which frightened me more than anything else in that vault — a grown man hollowed down to sticks by three decades of someone else living his life.

"The crown," he whispered. "Is it still—"

"It's cracking," I said, because I could feel it, even then, even magicless — a tremor running up out of the mountain's bones, a note too deep to hear but impossible not to feel, like standing near a bridge as it takes a blow. In Vell, right now, something hollow was coming apart. "Right now, probably."

He laughed. It turned into coughing. He gripped my wrist with fingers that had once signed laws and would again, apparently, and he said, "What did it cost you?"

"Nothing I was using," I said.

Which was a lie, and he knew it, and he was kind enough not to say so.

Above us, the ringing stopped.

* * *

I ran. I know kings are supposed to be rescued at a stately walk, but I ran, up the passage Dain had held, past scorch-marks on the walls that were still smoking, past a Warden lying unconscious with his own irons locked around his wrists — Ilse's work, neat as a ledger entry — and into the antechamber where the ambush had been.

Ilse stood in the middle of it with a sword in one hand and a broken binding-iron in the other, and there were four Wardens down around her, two groaning, two very still. She was bleeding from her scalp, and her sergeant's insignia had been torn off, and she looked, for the first time since I'd met her on the mountain road, like she knew exactly who she was.

"Dain," she said, when she saw me. Just that. Her face did something complicated.

I found him against the far wall, sitting propped like a man resting after a long day's market, which we both knew was a lie.

There was a wound in his side that I didn't need magic to read. Old Charmwrights' trick, actually — you don't need a spark to know when a body has stopped bothering to argue.

"Hey, girl," he said. "Did it take?"

"He's awake."

"Ha." Dain laughed, and winced, and laughed anyway, because Dain had never once let a bad moment go unembellished. "Thirty years I carried that. Did I ever tell you? First line of the siphon. My hand. I was twenty-six and they told me it was for the city's protection, and I believed them, because believing is easier than asking." His eyes found mine. "You asked. At the market, with the luck-charm that wouldn't work — you asked why. That's the whole guild, right there. That's the whole damn thing."

"Dain—"

"Ilse," he said, louder, and she came over and knelt on his other side, and the two of them looked at each other across him like former colleagues at a funeral, which, I suppose, is exactly what they were. "Sergeant. You turned."

"Took me long enough," Ilse said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't.

"Everyone takes as long as they take." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a charm — the misdirection-charm, the one he'd sworn never to use again, cracked down the middle now, spent twice over. He pressed it into my hand. His grip was still strong. Guildmaster's grip. "Sell this one. It works."

"It's broken."

"Everything worth having is a little broken." He smiled at me, and it was Corvane's smile, and it was my mother's smile, and it was every Charmwright who'd ever sold a trinket with crossed fingers and a straight face. "Lena Kavanagh. Third of the guild of Vell. Listen to me now, because I'm only saying this once and I intend to be insufferable about it later."

"You're going to be fine," I said.

"Don't interrupt a guildmaster. The guild isn't the magic. It was never the magic. It's the *asking*. Why doesn't it work. Who broke it. Who pays. You keep asking those questions and the guild lives, whether there's one of you or a hundred." His breath rattled. "Tell Corvane he was right and I'm sorry and also he's still a pompous old—"

He didn't finish the sentence. I think he meant to. I think he'd have enjoyed finishing it.

Ilse bowed her head. I didn't cry, mostly because I was fairly sure that if I started, the mountain would hear about it, and the mountain had enough problems.

Through the stone beneath us, faint and final, came a sound like a great door closing — and then silence where thirty years of fire had been.

The siphon was shut. The crown, wherever its pieces were falling in Vell, was finished.

And the ember of the dragon's last breath drifted down through the dark of the vault like a slow golden snowfall, and one small piece of it — no bigger than a walnut, no heavier than a promise — settled into the open palm of the waking king.

"Well," he said, looking at it, and then at me, and then at Ilse, who was trying to figure out whether she was a prisoner, a deserter, or a hero, and settling visibly on *none of the above*. "I appear to owe a debt to a guild of three. One dead, one disgraced, and one—" he paused, delicately, the way kings are trained to pause "—no longer carrying what she carried."

"That's the whole guild," I said. "We're between apprentices."

The corner of his mouth moved. It was, I would learn later, the first expression his face had made in thirty years that belonged entirely to him.

"Then we'd better get back to Vell," said the King of Vell, in a voice like a man remembering how to use his own name, "before anyone notices I've changed."

Behind us, deep in the mountain, something that might have been the dragon — or might have been the Emberhold itself, exhaling — sighed once, softly, like a fire finally allowed to go out.

And I reached into my coat for the scorched map, my mother's map, with her handwriting in the margins and one last instruction I hadn't yet dared to read —

and the paper, in the dark, was glowing.
