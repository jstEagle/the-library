## 10. A Guild of Three, Rebuilt

The guildhall door still sticks. I've oiled the hinges twice, planed the frame once, and threatened it in three languages, and it still insists on groaning like an old man asked to dance. Some part of me suspects that's intentional. Buildings remember things, Dain told me once — not magic exactly, just habit worn into stone and timber the way a path wears into grass. This hall remembers every Charmwright who ever leaned against that door with their arms full and their hopes low, and it greets me each morning as one of them.

I let it. It's the only thing left that does.

Months now since the mountain. Since the vault where a king slept thirty years inside his own name, since a dragon's last breath went out of the world and into my keeping, since I gave up the spark I'd spent my whole life pretending was bigger than it was. My hands are the same hands. That's the strange part. You'd think surrendering your magic would leave some mark — a scar, a paleness, something the fortune-tellers could point at. But no. The morning after the unbinding, Ilse brought me water and I drank it, and my hands shook from exhaustion and grief and nothing else, and they have been nothing else ever since.

Empty. And lighter than I expected. Lighter than anything has been in years.

The bell over the door — also sticky; this building is held together by stubbornness — rattles, and I look up from the ledger to find my first apprentice of the day standing in the doorway holding a dead luck-charm like a confession.

"You said these were guaranteed," Tam says. He's fourteen, all elbows and indignation, the kind of boy who argues with weather. "Guaranteed, Lena. I carried it to the fish market for two weeks and I dropped a whole crate of eels."

"Was the crate insured?"

"Lena."

"I'm teasing." I hold out my hand and he gives up the charm — a little brass thing on a cord, one of the last my father ever made, the kind we used to sell at the night market with our fingers crossed behind our backs. It doesn't work. It never worked, not really. A faint warmth, a suggestion of fortune, the way a candle suggests the sun. "Here's your lesson for today, then. Lesson one: most charms don't work, and the ones that do mostly work sideways."

"That's a terrible lesson."

"It's the foundation of the entire craft," I say solemnly. "Sit down."

He sits. They all sit, eventually — the four of them now, which is four more than the guild has had apprentices in twenty years. Tam, who wants magic the way other boys want knighthood. Marta, who is quiet and watches everything and asks questions like she's already decided the answers are lies. Old Fen, sixty if he's a day, who came in off the street the week the ban lifted and said he'd always wanted to know how the trinkets worked, and stayed because nobody told him to leave. And Sera, who doesn't talk about where she came from, but whose hands already know things her mouth won't say — the way she handles silver thread makes me certain she learned it somewhere the Wardens used to raid.

Four apprentices. A guild of five, counting me. Counting the ghost of everyone who came before, if you're feeling generous, and I'm trying to be generous lately. It turns out generosity is easier when you're not starving.

* * *

The king's proclamation arrived in the third week of autumn, read aloud in the market square by a herald with a voice like a landslide and a face like he'd rather be anywhere else. *By order of Aldric, trueborn King of Vell: the ban on guild-craft is lifted. Let the Charmwrights practice openly, under law, their workings registered and their harms accounted.*

Under law. Registered. Accounted. I stood in the crowd listening to the fine print pile up and thought: thirty years of outlawry, and the best apology we get is paperwork.

But then the king himself came out onto the balcony, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds go quiet around fire.

Aldric looks wrong, still. Not hollow — the impostor was hollow, a shape wearing a crown like a glove wears a hand — but wrong in a smaller way. He moves like a man who slept badly for thirty years, which is precisely what he did. His beard is gray at the temples though his face isn't old enough for it. Time got into him somehow while he dreamed, or maybe time got out of him, leaked away into the siphon along with everything else the Emberhold fed upward. He stands at railings like he doesn't trust them. When he speaks, his voice carries the particular weight of someone who has rehearsed being alive.

"The Charmwrights kept a light burning in Vell when my court would not," he said that day. "Three of them, starving, selling broken luck to people who needed it anyway. If that is what survives a ban, imagine what survives a blessing."

Imagine what survives a blessing. I wrote it down that night in the ledger's margin, because it sounded like something worth keeping, and because — I'll admit it — a king said it about us. About the guild. About my father's broken luck-charms and Corvane's stubbornness and a girl who couldn't sell a working charm to save her life, because there weren't any.

Corvane heard the proclamation from his chair by the guildhall hearth. He didn't cheer. He took off his cap, which for Corvane is roughly equivalent to weeping, and held it in his lap for a long moment before putting it back on.

"Took them long enough," he said.

"You helped build the thing that stole the throne," I reminded him gently.

"I helped build a great many things, girl, and regretted most of them. That's called having a career." He looked at the burned page in the ledger — the gap where the truth used to be, before he'd fed it to the fire rather than let the Wardens find it. "We'll write new pages. That's what ledgers are for. The old ones only tell you what you lost."

Dain would have argued with him about that. Dain argued with everyone about everything, right up until the end. We buried him on the mountainside below Emberhold's gate, in sight of the passage he held open so that a girl he'd known for six weeks could walk through it and change the world. Ilse carved the marker herself — Warden discipline, steady hands even shaking. *Dain Merrow. Guildmaster. He swore he'd never use it again. He lied.* I think he'd have liked that. I think he'd have complained about the word count, too, and then secretly been pleased.

* * *

The ember sits on my shelf above the workbench, in a dish of black glass that Sera made without knowing why she made it — she woke up one morning saying she'd dreamed a shape and couldn't rest until it existed. It's the size of a robin's egg and the color of a banked coal, deep red going to gold at its heart, and it is warm. Always warm. Never hot, never cooling, never flickering. The dragon's last breath didn't die with the dragon; it condensed, the way breath condenses on cold glass, into this small patient thing, and when the king's men came asking what remained of the Emberhold, this is what I showed them instead of the map, which I burned. Some inheritances aren't for registers and accounts.

It isn't mine. That's the important thing, the thing I had to learn. For years I thought inheritance meant blood — guild-blood, the mountain knowing me, my mother's handwriting in a map's margins telling me I was chosen, chosen, chosen. And then I stood in the vault with my spark open in my cupped hands like a coal of my own, ready to give it up, and I understood that the choosing was the whole point. The dragon needed someone who would give freely. Blood just made the door easier to find. The walking through it — that was always mine.

So the ember sits warm on the shelf, not mine, belonging to whoever comes next, and every apprentice in this hall eventually works up the nerve to ask about it.

"What is it?" Tam asks today, mid-lesson, staring at the shelf instead of the soldering iron he's supposed to be learning.

"A gift from someone who ran out of time to give it properly."

"Is it magic?"

"It's warmer than it should be. Make of that what you will."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agree. "Most of the good ones aren't." I turn his attention back to the iron. "Lesson two: heat follows intent. If you want the solder to flow, stop gripping the iron like you're afraid of it and decide where the metal goes. The craft obeys decisions, not wishes."

Marta looks up at that, sharp-eyed, and I know she's filing it away next to whatever else she's collected. She'll ask me someday what I gave up and whether it was worth it, and I'll tell her the truth: that I can't feel the currents anymore, can't sense a charm's hum across a room, can't do the one thing my mother could do and her mother before her. And that I don't miss it. That what I feel instead is this hall full of people, the smell of flux and hot metal, Fen laughing at Tam's failures, Sera humming while she works. Belonging, it turns out, was never in the blood. It was always in the room.

My mother knew that. Her marginalia said so, in the end — I found it on the map's final fold, the crease nobody thinks to check: *If you're reading this, L., then you walked through. Don't look back for me. Look forward for the guild.* Ten years I'd wondered why she left. Ten years I'd assumed abandonment. It was commission. She was the courier because someone had to carry the map out, and she chose to be the one who couldn't come home, because home was the thing she was sending me toward.

I keep the map's ashes in a jar next to the ember. Warm and gone, side by side. That's about the size of a life.

* * *

Winter comes early to Vell, and with it the first real test of the rebuilt guild: the city wants charms. Real ones, registered and accounted, but wanted — luck for the harvest festival, warmth-wards for the poor quarter, a hundred small workings that haven't been legal in a generation and that nobody living actually knows how to make well. The knowledge died with the purges, mostly. What's left is me, Corvane's failing memory, Dain's scattered notes, and whatever the mountain saw fit to leave in a dying woman's margins.

"We'll fail at first," I tell the apprentices around the workbench, the five of us shoulder to shoulder in lamplight, the ember glowing above us like a small red conscience. "Our charms will be crooked and half-alive and they'll work sideways if they work at all. People will buy them anyway. That's not cynicism — that's history. Vell bought broken luck from a dying guild for thirty years because wanting magic is a kind of hope, and hope doesn't check the craftsmanship."

"And when we get good?" Sera asks quietly.

"Then we teach more people. That's the whole trick. A guild isn't three survivors hoarding secrets in a cellar. It's whoever shows up, and what they choose to carry forward." I tap the bench. "Lesson three. There is no lesson three yet. You'll write it."

Outside, the first snow is starting, sticking to the glass in the dark. Somewhere down the street, a Warden patrol passes — Ilse's people now, reformed into something closer to guards than hunters, and Ilse herself due tomorrow with the winter registration papers and, if her letters are any indication, a complaint about the quality of our paperwork that will take an hour to resolve and that she'll stretch into an afternoon, because Ilse visits the way soldiers hold ground.

Tam blows out his solder joint. Fen laughs. The door sticks when Marta opens it to shake out the rag, and groans like an old man asked to dance, and I find myself smiling at it, at all of it, at the crooked warm ordinary machinery of a life that finally fits.

Then the knock comes. Three raps, unhurried, deliberate — not the knock of a customer or a Warden or anyone expecting trouble. Just someone at the door, at nightfall, in the snow.

Through the frosted pane I can see a figure wrapped against the cold, tall, hooded, waiting. And on the shelf above the workbench, without any wind, without any reason, the ember brightens — just slightly, just briefly — the way a coal breathes when a door opens somewhere in the house.

I put down my tools and go to answer it.
