## 1. The Last Charm on Cordwainer Street

The widow Maren Tull came down Cordwainer Street at the gray hour, when the lamps were guttering and the fish carts hadn't yet begun to stink, and Edmund Underhill saw her coming the way he'd learned to see everything in ten years of outlawed living: sideways, without looking.

She was wearing her good shawl. That was how he knew.

He kept his hands busy at his stall — a folding table, a bolt of canvas, a tray of ordinary things. Bone buttons. A spool of waxed thread. Three fishing flies tied so badly they'd only catch a fish that wanted to be caught, which, if you knew anything about fish and less about luck, was exactly the point. He was a button-seller. He had been a button-seller for six years, and before that a mender of nets, and before that nothing anyone in Saltmere could name without risking a fine.

Maren stopped at the table. She didn't touch anything. Widows who wore their good shawls to buy buttons never touched anything first; touching meant wanting, and wanting meant admitting you'd come for something else.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning." He turned a button over with his thumb. River shell, dyed brown. Perfectly dull.

"My boy's going out on the *Kestrel* tomorrow," she said. "Deep water. Three weeks."

"Good boat, the *Kestrel*."

"Her last two trips came back light. One man drowned off the net-boards in the spring." She said it flatly, the way people in Saltmere said everything about the sea, because grief you said loudly would follow you home. "I'm not asking for anything."

"No," Edmund agreed. "You're not."

"I want buttons."

"Brown ones?"

"Whatever you have."

He had whatever she needed, but he wasn't going to say that on an open street with the wardens' morning patrol due any minute. Instead he crouched below the table as if sorting stock, and his fingers found the false bottom of the tray, and beneath the false bottom lay a small cloth roll tied with red string, and inside the roll were eleven charms.

Eleven. The guild had once moved four hundred through this city in a season. Luck-knots and fever-knots, salt-blessings and quiet-word amulets, charms against drowning and against despair, which were often the same charm with different knots. Now there were eleven left in Edmund's roll, three members left in the Guild of Small Mercies, and one charter struck from the city rolls so thoroughly that even the paper it had been written on had been confiscated and burned by men who believed, sincerely, that they were cleaning something.

He chose the luck-knot. It was the size of a walnut, woven from grey cord and one strand of something that had once been golden, knotted in the old pattern — nine crossings, no two alike, the ninth hidden where only the wearer's pulse would find it. It smelled faintly of the sea, though it had never been near one. Charms made properly remembered what they were for.

"Here," he said, surfacing. "These are my best. Brown, brown, and — hm. This one." He set the luck-knot among the buttons like it was nothing, like it had always been a button, round and grey and unremarkable.

Maren looked at it. Her breath did something small.

"That one," she said.

"It's the same as the others."

"It isn't."

"It's a better knot," Edmund admitted. "Tighter work. Costs a copper more."

"How much?"

"For you? Two silver."

Her face went still. Two silver was half a week of fish money. They both knew it. She looked at the knot, and then out toward the harbor mouth, where the *Kestrel* would clear the breakwater tomorrow morning and become a speck and then a rumor and then, if the sea was kind, a shape on the horizon three weeks from now with her boy standing on it.

"Two silver," she said.

"And I'll throw in the thread." He pushed the spool across. "Waxed. Good for patching oilskins."

She paid him. Their hands didn't touch; he slid the coins off the board with one finger and into his palm, and dropped them into a pocket that held no guild mark, no license, nothing but lint and a bent fishhook. Then she took the luck-knot, and here was the part that never stopped mattering, the part the law couldn't reach: she closed her fist around it, and Edmund watched the fear go out of her shoulders by a degree. Not all of it. The sea kept most of it. But a degree.

"You're a strange button-seller," she said.

"I'm told that."

"My husband bought from a man like you. Before." Before the ban. Before the wardens. Before the word *guild* became a word you said quietly or not at all. "He said the knots held."

"They hold," Edmund said. "That's the whole trade."

She went up the street with her good shawl and her waxed thread and her fist closed around a walnut-sized piece of mercy, and Edmund watched her go the way he watched everyone go, which was fully, and without expecting to see them again.

The bell above the chandlery rang the quarter hour. Somewhere behind him, the city woke: shutters banging back, a dog complaining, the first fish cart already rounding from Tanner's Row with its smell arriving ahead of it like a herald. Ordinary sounds. Saltmere was very good at ordinary. Saltmere had been practicing ordinary for ten years, ever since the Crown's reformers had stood on the customs-house steps and declared that magic sold for coin was magic corrupted, that mercy should be free or nothing, and that any soul caught trafficking in charms would pay fines that no charm-seller could afford — which was, everyone understood, the entire design.

Edmund began packing his tray. Buttons first, always buttons first. The false bottom went in over the empty space where the luck-knot had been. Ten charms now. He did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way you count your own heartbeats: ten charms, three guild members, one city that had forgotten it used to be protected.

He was lifting the table to fold it when he felt it.

Not heard — felt, in the teeth, in the old scar along his forearm where Mother Aldsey had once pressed a hot needle making a point about precision. A wrongness, thin as wire, running under the morning. Like a note played just below hearing. Like the moment before a bell is struck, stretched out and refusing to resolve.

He looked up. Cordwainer Street went on being Cordwainer Street. Fish smell, dog, shutters. But at the far end, where the street bent toward the customs house, the air had a color to it. Not quite a shimmer. More like the memory of a shimmer, the way a wall remembers a picture taken down years ago.

And under his sleeve, against his wrist, his guild mark — the small blue knotwork scar every member bore, inked in salt and silver when they took their vows — went cold.

It had never done that. In ten years of hiding, of lying low, of selling buttons and saying nothing, the mark had been exactly what the reformers promised it would be: nothing. Dead ink. A scar with a history.

Now it was cold, and the air at the end of the street was remembering how to shine, and somewhere far off — so far off it might have been the mountains east of the city, or might have been under the world — something made a sound like a bell rung underwater.

Edmund stood very still, holding a folding table full of buttons, and understood with the calm certainty of a man watching weather come in that the long decade of hiding was ending, and not the way he'd have chosen.

Down the street, three wardens turned the corner by the customs house in their grey coats, moving fast, faster than a morning patrol moved, and the tallest one was pointing — not at Edmund, not yet — at the sky.

Edmund looked up.

Over Saltmere, over the harbor, over the whole visible curve of the world, the stars were going out.
