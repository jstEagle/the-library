## 2. Ledger of Small Mercies

The guildhouse smelled of tallow and old paper and the particular sweetness of dried feverfew that Quillon hung from every beam because he believed — against all evidence — that it kept moths and magistrates out with equal efficiency.

Edmund came in through the back door, shaking rain off his coat, and found the old man already at the long table with the ledger open before him and a pot of barley tea going cold at his elbow.

"You're late," Quillon said, without looking up.

"I sold a luck-knot to Marta Cade."

"Marta Cade." Quillon's pen paused. "Widow of the fisherman?"

"Widow of the fisherman."

"Did you charge her?"

"Three coppers."

"Edmund."

"It was three coppers *and* she fed me mackerel," Edmund said, hanging his coat on the hook by the door. "I consider myself paid handsomely."

Quillon made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been indigestion; after forty years in the man's company, Edmund had learned they were acoustically identical. He pulled a chair to the table and sat, and for a while there was only the sound of rain on the roof and Quillon's pen scratching its slow way down columns of figures.

This was the ritual. Every quarter-end, the Guild of Small Mercies closed its books. Once, when the guild had been legal, when it had had a charter and a hall and eleven masters and forty-odd journeymen scattered through Saltmere selling charms the way bakers sold bread, the audit had taken three days and required two clerks. Now it took an evening and required one old man who refused to admit his eyes were failing and one younger man who refused to say so aloud.

"Read me the column," Quillon said, pushing the ledger across. "My eyes are tired."

They were not tired. But Edmund read anyway, because the ritual mattered more than the truth of it. "Luck-knots, nine sold, twenty-seven coppers. Fever-charms, four sold, two returned — the Hollis girl again, her mother says the charm sweats—"

"Charms do sweat. It means they're working."

"—two returned, so net ten coppers. Warding-salts, none. Memory-tinctures—" Edmund stopped.

"What?"

"The memory-tinctures." He turned the ledger around so Quillon could see. "Look at this entry. Dated the ninth. *Sold, one memory-tincture, to whom it may concern.* Paid in full."

"So?"

"So we don't sell memory-tinctures anymore. We haven't since the Proscription. And look at the payment line. It doesn't say coppers. It doesn't say silver. It just says *paid*."

Quillon leaned over the page. The lamplight caught the deep seams of his face, the liver-spotted scalp beneath the thin white hair, the small round spectacles he wore pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyes waiting for their turn. For a long moment he said nothing at all, which from Quillon was more unusual than shouting.

"That's odd," he said finally.

"It's more than odd. Look further back." Edmund began turning pages, faster now, the paper whispering under his fingers like something trying to get away. "Here. Third month. *One dream-ease draught, paid.* Fifth month. *Two luck-knots, paid.* Seventh month — Master, half these entries are marked paid in nothing. No coin listed. No barter. Just *paid*, in a hand I don't recognize."

It wasn't Quillon's hand. That was the thing that sat wrongest in Edmund's stomach. Every other entry in the ledger, going back decades, was in one of two scripts: Quillon's cramped, upright bookkeeping, or old Mother Aldsey's looping, generous scrawl, all flourishes, as if even her arithmetic couldn't resist decorating itself. But these entries — maybe thirty of them, scattered across the last two years like stones dropped into a stream — were written in a hand that was neat without being human about it. Perfectly spaced. Perfectly level. As though the pen had been held by something that had never once been hurried or careless or drunk.

"Somebody's been writing in our ledger," Edmund said.

"No one's been in the guildhouse but us three."

"Then somebody's been writing in it from somewhere else, which is worse."

Quillon took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on. All of it very deliberate. Edmund had seen him do exactly this sequence exactly twice before: once when the Proclamation came down outlawing the guild, and once when Aldsey didn't come home.

"Give me the book," Quillon said.

Edmund gave him the book. Quillon went through it slowly, page by page, his lips moving, and Edmund watched him and drank the cold barley tea and tried to name the feeling sitting in his chest. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was the feeling you got walking home at night when you realized the footsteps behind you had matched yours for three streets — not danger yet, just the *shape* of danger, arriving ahead of schedule.

"Where's the balance page?" Edmund asked. "Quarter-end, we total it. Where's the total?"

Quillon's finger stopped on a page near the middle of the book. He didn't answer.

"Master?"

"There's a page missing," Quillon said.

Edmund took the ledger back and found it himself: the stub of a torn page, ragged at the gutter, the ghost of ink pressed through from the reverse side. Someone had torn it out carefully — not ripped, not cut, but eased out along the binding, the way you'd extract a splinter. Whoever did it hadn't wanted to damage the book. They'd wanted the book to keep looking whole.

"When?" Edmund said. "When did this happen? We audited at midsummer. There was no torn page at midsummer. I'd have seen it. You'd have seen it."

"Yes," Quillon said. "We would have."

"So it happened between midsummer and now, inside a locked house, in a book that lives in a locked drawer, and neither of us saw it done." Edmund set the ledger down flat between them, and his voice came out lower than he intended. "Master Quillon. What is going on?"

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down Cordwainer Street a dog started up and thought better of it.

"We'll count what we have," Quillon said. "That's all an audit is. You count what you have and you make your peace with what you don't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got tonight." The old man rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and went to the shelf where the candles lived. His back was to Edmund. "Count with me, boy. It'll go faster."

* * *

Quillon had kept books for fifty-one years.

He had learned it from his own master, back when the guild still had a name people said above a whisper, and the first lesson had never left him: *the ledger is the guild. Charters burn, halls fall, masters die. The ledger is the guild.* Every mercy given, every debt owed, every kindness sold for a copper or forgiven for free — if it was written down, it endured. If it wasn't, it had never happened.

He had taught Edmund the same lesson. The boy had thought it was about accounting.

Quillon lit the second candle and carried it back to the table, and his hands were steady, because he had spent fifty-one years teaching them steadiness, and they obeyed him now the way a well-trained dog obeys — faithfully, and without any say in the matter.

He knew the hand in the ledger. Of course he knew it. You did not forget a hand like that, level and patient and utterly without warmth, the way you did not forget the smell of the room where you'd signed your worst decision. He had seen it once before, thirty-one years ago, on a single sheet of vellum that he had burned in the guildhouse stove and then, in the weeks after, wished a hundred times he had kept, because burning it had changed nothing except to leave him alone with the memory.

*Paid,* the entries said. And that was even the word they'd used, back then. Not bought, not traded. *Paid.* As though the transaction were a courtesy being done to *him*. As though the court beneath the mountain were settling an account rather than acquiring goods.

He looked across the table at Edmund, who had his head bent over the columns, lips moving, young face furrowed in concentration, and felt the old dread rise in him like water into a cellar — slow, silent, finding every crack.

Because here was the arithmetic Quillon had never written down anywhere:

A guild does not survive thirty years of proscription on twenty-seven coppers a quarter. A guild does not keep its roof patched and its stove fed and its three remaining members in bread and lamp-oil on the earnings of luck-knots sold to fishermen's widows. Something else had been paying the guild's way for a long time. Quillon had told himself, over the years, a great many comforting things about what that something might be — that Aldsey had private savings, that the guild had endowments he'd never been shown, that the money simply *was*, the way weather was.

But he had known. In the place where a man keeps the knowledge he has decided not to have, he had known. And tonight the ledger had finally said it out loud, in a hand too neat to be human: the debts were marked paid, and the currency was not coin.

He thought of the torn page. He thought of what would have been written on it. He had not seen the page itself — whatever power moved through the book had spared him that — but he knew the shape of the hole the way you know the shape of a tooth you've lost. Quarter-end totals. A running balance. And, almost certainly, one entry older than all the rest, carried forward year after year like a coal that wouldn't die:

*One soul, sold to order. Debt discharged.*

He had signed that entry himself, thirty-one years ago, in his own cramped upright hand. He had told himself it was the only way. The guild was dying; the creditors were at the door; and the court's agent had come in his grey coat with his patient smile and said, *the mountain pays its debts in advance, master, and asks so little in return.* So little. One name off the guild rolls. One soul from among the guild's own, chosen by lot, and the guild would never want again.

And the lot had fallen — had it fallen? had it been *made* to fall? — on Mother Aldsey.

She had gone quietly. That was the worst of it, the part that had let him live with himself all these years: she had gone *quietly*, packing her one bag, kissing Edmund's forehead — he'd been nineteen, asleep, she'd done it while he slept — and telling Quillon at the door, in her looping generous voice, *keep the books honest, Quill. Whatever else you do. Keep the books honest.*

He had kept the books honest. He had simply never told them everything the books were holding.

"Master." Edmund's voice pulled him back. "This entry here. Twelfth month, last year. *One soul-lamp wick, replaced, paid.* Soul-lamp. We don't deal in soul-lamps. Nobody deals in soul-lamps. That's court-work. That's—" The boy stopped. Looked up. And Quillon watched, across the width of the table, across the width of thirty-one years, as the arithmetic finished itself behind Edmund's eyes. "Why does our ledger know about soul-lamps?"

"Boy—"

"And why is the guild's rent paid? Why are we never hungry? Who has been paying for us, Master? Because I've counted it now, I've counted it three times, and we don't earn enough to be alive. We should have starved years ago. Somebody has been keeping this guild alive, and they've been writing it down in our own book like they own it, and there's a page torn out, and you—" Edmund's voice cracked, not with anger yet, with the fear underneath the anger, "—you looked at that hand and you weren't surprised. I watched your face. You weren't surprised."

The rain came harder. Down the street, shutters knocked in the wind. Quillon looked at the boy he had raised, in the hall his master had built, under the roof that something beneath the mountain had been quietly paying to keep over both their heads, and he opened his mouth to lie — fifty-one years of practice, and the lie was right there, ready, *it's nothing, boy, some clerk's error, some collector's joke* —

And what came out instead, small and dry as ash, was:

"Sit down, Edmund."

Edmund sat.

"There are things in this ledger older than you," Quillon said. "There is a debt in this ledger that was paid before you ever held a charm-cord in your hand. And I am going to tell you about it, because I am an old man, and I can hear the mountain clearing its throat."

Edmund stared at him. "What mountain? What are you talking about? What debt?"

Quillon reached across the table and laid his hand flat on the open ledger, on the neat inhuman script, on the word *paid*.

"Hers," he said. "Aldsey's."

And outside, far off toward the border hills, so faint it might have been thunder and might not, something rang — one low note, like a bell struck underwater.

Neither of them moved for a long time.

"Tomorrow," Quillon said quietly. "I'll tell you tomorrow. Tonight, bank the fire and check the wards on the door. All of them. Even the ones we stopped believing in."

Edmund checked them. All of them. And when he climbed to the roof loft much later, unable to sleep, he lay looking up through the skylight at the stars over Saltmere — all of them still there, still burning, ordinary and bright —

— and did not see the one, far in the north, that flickered once, like a lamp running low, and went out.
