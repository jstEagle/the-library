## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Edmund knew something was wrong before he knew it, the way you know a knock at the door is bad news by its rhythm alone.

He was up on the guildhouse roof because he couldn't sleep, which was itself a small betrayal of routine. For ten years the routine had been simple: lock the shop on Cordwainer Street, walk the four streets to the guildhouse with his collar up and his mark hidden under his shirt, eat whatever Quillon had left in the pot, audit a ledger or mend a charm or do nothing at all, sleep. A life folded so many times it fit in a pocket. He had come to mistake the folding for peace.

Tonight the pot was empty and Quillon was asleep and the ledger wouldn't stop nagging at him — that torn page, that column of debts paid in some currency neither of them could name — so instead of sleeping he climbed the outside stair to the roof with a cup of barley tea gone cold in his hand, because it was the only place in Saltmere where a man could look up without a roofline cutting the sky into rations.

The stars were out. That much was ordinary. Saltmere's sky was famously generous; the fishermen swore by it, the farmers set their calendars by it, and the border wards — the great old wards strung along the salt marshes like beads on an invisible wire — drank starlight all night long, every night, and had done for as long as anyone remembered. You didn't think about wards. That was the point of them. They were the silence between the bells.

Edmund sat on the low parapet with his back against the chimney and did what he always did when he couldn't sleep: he named stars. Not their true names — nobody living had those — but the names his teacher had taught him, back when teaching him anything hadn't been against the law. The Cart Ox. The Drowned Girl. The Long Needle that pointed not north but *home*, whatever home meant to the person looking. Mother Aldsey had said the naming mattered more than the accuracy. *You keep a thing alive by calling it, Edmund. Even from far away. Especially from far away.*

He was halfway through the winter constellations when the sound came.

It was not loud. That was the horror of it, later, when he tried to describe it to people who hadn't heard — that the end of everything solid should arrive so quietly. It sounded like a bell rung underwater: one deep round note, muffled, patient, as if the whole sea had been holding a bell in its mouth for a century and had finally decided to let it go. The tea shivered in his cup. His teeth felt it. Somewhere below, every dog in Cordwainer Street began to howl at once and then, worse, stopped at once.

Then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. They went out the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick — all of them, everywhere, in the same instant, the whole spilled handful of light simply taken back. The sky above Saltmere became a flat black nothing, so total that Edmund lost the horizon, lost the rooftops, lost his own hands. The city below vanished into its own darkness. He heard a scream from somewhere near the harbor, thin and far, and another answering it, and then the particular roar of a whole city waking up afraid.

Edmund stayed exactly where he was.

Later he would understand this about himself: that when the world broke, his instinct was not to run but to watch, to stand very still at the edge of the wound and take notes, because a man who sold luck for coppers had learned long ago that panic was just information arriving too fast. So he sat with his cold tea on a dark roof under a starless sky, heart slamming, and watched.

There was still light down there — hearth-glows spilling from doors thrown open, lanterns raised on poles, the harbor beacons flaring as the night-watch panicked them alight. Saltmere lit itself candle by candle like a city trying to remember what fire was. And against all that small desperate light, Edmund saw something fall.

It came out of the black sky itself. Not fast, the way meteors go — no streak, no burn. It fell slowly, unhurriedly, the way a leaf falls if the leaf were made of pale silver and refusal. It drifted past where the stars should have been. It grew larger. It resolved, impossibly, into a shape with arms and legs.

A child. Falling out of the dark onto the guildhouse roof.

Edmund did not decide to move; his body decided for him, tea cup abandoned, boots skidding on tiles, arms out. But the child did not land hard. The child landed the way snow lands — a soft settling, weightless, upright on the ridgeline of the roof as though roofs were where children belonged. Unharmed. Barefoot, in a shift too thin for any season, seven or eight years old perhaps, hair drifting around her face as if she were still underwater.

And she glowed. Faintly — not the harsh flare of a torch but the deep steady shine of a coal an hour after the fire dies, a light coming from underneath her skin, from her eyes, from the spaces between her fingers. In all that ruined darkness she was the brightest thing in Saltmere.

They looked at each other.

"Hello," Edmund said, because ten years of selling charms to frightened strangers had taught him that you start with hello, always, even when nothing else makes sense.

The child considered him with an expression far too old for her face — not fear, not wonder, but the tired recognition of someone checking an address against a memory. Then she said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole:

"You kept calling."

"I'm sorry?"

"The names." She tilted her head back toward the empty sky. "The Cart Ox. The Drowned Girl. The Long Needle." She said them perfectly, better than he'd said them, and each one landed on him like a stone dropped down a well. "Somebody kept calling. It got very quiet, and then somebody called, and I followed the calling down."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "That was me. I was — those are just names. Old names. My teacher's names."

"She'd know them too, then." The child smiled, and the smile lit the rooftop, literally, shadows swinging across the tiles. "Tell her the wick is pinched. Tell her the lamps are all down in the mountain and the tally is wrong." Her face crumpled suddenly, terribly, into an ordinary child's confusion. "I don't know why I said mountain. I've never seen a mountain. Have I? I can't remember having feet before tonight."

Below them, the city screamed and prayed and lit things. Above them, nothing. Edmund stood on his own roof in the ruins of his ordinary folded-up life and understood, in the slow cold way understanding arrives, that the world had just gotten much larger and much worse.

Because he knew what drank starlight in this country. Everyone knew, the way you know your own heartbeat — the border wards, the quiet old wards, fed nightly by the sky. And the wards had failed with a bell-note the moment the stars died, which meant they hadn't been broken, hadn't been attacked. Broken wards snapped. These had *gone out* — pinched, like wicks — and a child who remembered being bright had fallen out of the space where the stars used to be, talking about lamps and mountains and tallies.

Tallies. Debts paid in a currency neither of them could name.

"Quillon," Edmund said aloud, and the word came out cracked.

"Is that a name?" the child asked. "Are you calling me?"

"No," Edmund said. "No, that's — he's the master of this house. He keeps the books." He crouched down slowly, the way you'd crouch to a spooked animal, though nothing about her seemed spooked; she seemed *patient*, which frightened him more. "What's your name?"

She thought about it. Actually thought, lips moving, searching.

"Wren," she said at last, surprised, as if she'd found it in her own pocket. "I was very small and very bright and there were thousands of us and now I'm this size." She held out her hands, examined them, turned them over. "I would like to come inside now. It's very large here and the ground keeps being far away."

"Yes," Edmund said faintly. "Yes, inside is — yes."

He held out his hand. She took it, and her grip was warm — warmer than warm, a low pleasant heat like a stone left by the hearth — and she stepped down off the ridgeline onto the tiles beside him with the easy certainty of someone for whom falling had never once been dangerous.

That was when the fear arrived properly. Not before — during. Wonder curdling as it cooled, the way milk curdles, into something he recognized as the specific dread of a man who has just realized that the law will be here soon, and the law does not ask children where they came from. Wardens had passed him on Cordwainer Street that very afternoon, boots ringing, and he had hidden his guild mark the way he always did, and he had told himself, as he always did, *small mercies stay small and nobody gets hurt.* There was nothing small about the creature holding his hand. Whatever she was, whoever had put her away and let her out, the wardens of Saltmere would not kneel at the sight of her. They would reach for chains, or worse.

"We need to get you downstairs," he said. "Quickly, and quietly, and then we need to decide—"

The roof door banged open.

Edmund spun, pulling Wren behind him, one hand already inside his coat on the only charm he carried that the law would hang him for — and there in the doorway stood a woman in the grey-and-silver of the Border Wardens, sword drawn, face lit from below by the impossible glow of the child.

She wasn't looking at Edmund. She was looking past him, up, at the naked black sky, and her expression was not triumph. It was grief. It was the face of a woman watching a funeral she'd been dreading for years.

Then her gaze dropped, found Wren, found the light pouring off the girl's small hands, and changed. Grief hardened into something colder and more terrible: recognition.

"There you are," the warden said softly. "One of them got out."

She leveled the sword.

* * *

Edmund put himself between them — all five foot nine of him, unarmed except for forbidden charms sewn into his lining, facing a warden of the border in full kit — and heard himself say, in the reasonable tone he used on customers who wanted refunds, "I don't think you want to do that on a roof, in this wind, with the whole city watching the skies."

"The whole city is watching the skies," the warden agreed. "That's rather the problem. Move, charm-man. I know what you are; I can smell the guildhouse. The child comes with me to the citadel and maybe the rest of you never existed."

"She's a child."

"She's *property*," the warden said, and the word came out of her flat and practiced and hollow, like a lesson recited too many times, and Edmund saw — sharply, suddenly — that she hated the word, that saying it cost her something, that her sword arm was not as steady as her voice.

Behind him, Wren peeked around his coat and asked, conversationally, "Why is the sky empty? Did they spend all of us?"

The warden flinched as if struck.

And Edmund, standing in the dark with a sword pointed at his chest and a fallen star gripping the hem of his coat, understood that the next chapter of his life had already begun whether he consented to it or not — and that it would begin, like everything else the guild had ever touched, with a debt someone else had signed for.

"Quillon," he said again, louder this time, toward the roof door, toward the stairs, toward the man asleep below with a ledger full of answers. "*Quillon!*"

Footsteps, muffled, beginning below.

The warden's sword did not waver. But she did not advance either — and in the space she left open, in that held breath between the drawn blade and the descending steps, the whole war ahead of them stood waiting to be born.
