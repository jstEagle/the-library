## 4. What the Warden Knew

They came for the child at dawn, and Edmund knew it before the knock, because the gulls went quiet.

Gulls never went quiet. Gulls were the city's alarm bells, its gossips, its oldest creditors — they screamed at everything: fish carts, funerals, the tide itself. But when the ward-wardens crossed Cordwainer Street in their grey-and-salt cloaks, the birds lifted off the rooftops all at once and held their silence like a debt called due. Ten years outlawed had taught Edmund to read that silence the way other men read weather.

He was downstairs with a kettle on and Wren asleep in the guild's back room, wrapped in Quillon's spare coat, glowing faintly through the wool like a coal under ash. Harrow sat at the long table turning an unlit pipe over in his fingers, because even a defected debt-collector apparently needed something to do with his hands when the walls started listening.

"Three of them," Edmund said, looking out the shutter crack. "No. Four."

"Five," Harrow said without looking up. "One's on the roof across the way. They always put one high." He set the pipe down. "That's not a collection detail. That's a retrieval."

"You can tell that from—"

"I wrote retrieval orders for eleven years, Underhill. I know the shape of them." He stood, and there was something in his face Edmund hadn't seen before — not guilt exactly, but its older brother. "Grey-and-salt means mountain jurisdiction. The court doesn't send five retrievals for a runaway lamp. It sends five for something that matters."

Edmund's stomach did what it had been doing since the stars went out: dropped, and kept dropping.

The knock came. Three blows, evenly spaced, the kind of knocking that expected the door to apologize.

Quillon was already moving, slow as ever, unhurried as a tide. He opened the door himself, which was either courage or senility, and Edmund had spent thirty years failing to determine which.

"Wardens," Quillon said pleasantly. "We're closed."

"We're not here to buy charms." The woman in front was tall, grey-cloaked, her warden's badge scraped half-blank — he could see the gouges from across the room, deliberate ones, someone who had tried to scratch out their own name and been stopped by law before finishing. Her eyes went past Quillon immediately, sweeping the room with a professional's economy: exits, hearth, stairs, the two men, and then, briefly, the door to the back room, where the light under the door was not entirely firelight.

"Sera Voss," she said. "Formerly senior ward-warden of the Saltmere line. You'll have heard of me."

Everyone had heard of Sera Voss. The warden who'd reported her own border stations falsified. The warden who'd stood at her own tribunal and told the assembled magistrates that the wards of Saltmere were being fed something no one would name, and been stripped of rank for slander against the crown's accounting. The warden whose disgrace had been so complete and so public that Edmund had assumed, along with everyone else, that she was either dead or bought.

"I heard you were mad," Edmund said.

"Convenient word." She stepped inside without invitation, and her two visible wardens flanked the doorway behind her. The third stayed in the street. The one on the roof, presumably, stayed on the roof. "Mad, drunk, ambitious, bitter. I've collected the whole set. Where is the child?"

"There's no child here," Edmund said.

Voss looked at him. It was not a hostile look. That was the worst part — it was tired, and precise, and it said plainly: *I have done this arithmetic too many times to enjoy it.* "Charm-seller," she said, "I watched your roof last night. Everyone on this street watched your roof. There was a hole in the sky and something fell out of it onto the tiles of a banned guildhouse, and this morning the light under your back-room door is the wrong color." She held up one hand, forestalling him. "I'm not going to pretend I don't know. Pretending is how we got here. I want to see her, and then I want to ask her three questions, and then I will tell you things about those wards that will keep you awake for the rest of however long you live. Which may be short."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then my wardens do their duty, badly, on purpose, because I've already told them what I saw in the vault records and half of them haven't slept since." She rubbed her eyes. "Please. I've been chasing this for six years. Six years of filing reports that get sealed, requesting audits that get denied, asking *what is the fuel* — and three nights ago every star over the Saltmere line went dark at once, and I felt the answer go past me like a freight train made of cold. Something fell. Something that remembers. And I am done being the only person in this kingdom who finds that suspicious."

Harrow made a small sound. Edmund glanced at him; the debt-collector had gone very still, the way he did around ledgers.

"The fuel," Harrow said quietly. "You asked what fuel fed the wards."

"Eleven years of requisitions," Voss said. "Ward-stone doesn't burn. Salt-wards run on binding, and binding runs on—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "See her first. Then talk."

Edmund looked at Quillon. Quillon looked at the floor, which for Quillon was a whole speech, and not a comforting one. Then he looked at Harrow, who gave the smallest nod, and Edmund understood that they had both done their own arithmetic and arrived at the same sum: a woman who had destroyed herself telling the truth was worth more than any lie they could build in ten minutes.

He opened the back-room door.

Wren was awake. Of course she was awake — she'd probably felt them coming, the way she seemed to feel everything now, the city's hums and hungers arriving in her like weather. She sat on the cot with the coat around her shoulders, and when the grey morning light from the window touched her face, it bent slightly, the way heat bends air over a forge.

Voss stopped in the doorway.

Edmund had seen hardened men weep at executions and magistrates faint at audits, but he had never seen anyone look at another living thing the way Sera Voss looked at Wren. It was the look of a woman finding the one document she'd been told didn't exist — and finding it alive, breathing, eight years old, frightened.

"Hello," Wren said.

"What are you?" Voss whispered. And then, catching herself, kneeling — actually kneeling, grey cloak pooling on the floorboards, a disgraced warden kneeling in an outlawed guildhouse — "I'm sorry. That's a rude question. My name is Sera. I keep the— I *kept* the wards. The big ones, at the border. Did you... do you know them?"

Wren tilted her head. "I know the sound they made," she said. "When they stopped. Like a bell underwater. I heard it from very far away. From *inside*, mostly." Her small brow furrowed. "It sounded like being let out of a mouth."

Voss's breath left her in a controlled, careful exhale, the exhale of someone managing themselves on purpose.

"Three questions," she said. "I promised three questions. First: do you remember the mountain?"

"Yes."

"Do you remember a hall with lamps?"

Wren's glow flickered — Edmund had learned in two days that this was what fear looked like on her, a stutter in the light. "Lamps hang from chains," she said slowly. "There are so many. They hum when the Tallyman walks. Some of them used to be people. I don't remember if I was one yet or just watching. It's like remembering a story you were told before you could talk."

"Last question." Voss's voice had gone rough. "The wards at the border. The ones that failed. Do you know why they failed?"

And Wren — sweet, strange, star-fallen Wren, who spoke in fragments of light and remembered being bright — said the sentence that broke the morning open:

"They weren't broken. They were *finished*. Like a meal." She looked up at Voss with terrible, gentle clarity. "You can't fix a wall that got eaten. Someone ate for a hundred years."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, the gulls still hadn't come back.

Voss rose. She turned to her wardens in the doorway. "Wait downstairs," she said. "All of you. Off the street. If anyone asks, we found nothing and we're embarrassed about it."

They went. Whatever she'd shown them in the vault records, they obeyed her the way soldiers obey a wound.

Then Sera Voss turned back to the four of them — charm-seller, old master, defector, child — and pulled a folded sheaf of papers from inside her cloak, worn soft at the creases, handled thousands of times.

"Now," she said, "the part that will keep you awake."

* * *

She spread the papers on the long table, and Edmund recognized the format instantly, because it was the same format as his own guild ledger: columns, dates, entries in a clerk's disciplined hand. But these were requisitions. Ward-requisitions, crown-sealed, spanning — he counted the dates, counted again — ninety-eight years.

"Every ward-station on the Saltmere line draws from a central reservoir," Voss said. "That's by design. Central binding, distributed release. The reservoir is beneath the mountain, in the deep vaults, administered by the Office of Accounts. Every quarter, a transfer comes up the salt road. Every year, the reservoir level drops and a replenishment order goes down." She tapped the column. "Look at what's ordered."

Edmund looked. His lips moved through the archaic clerk-shorthand, the kind they taught no more, and Quillon leaned over his shoulder, and Edmund heard the old man's breathing change.

"*Units,*" Edmund read. "Replenishment in units. One hundred units. One hundred and forty. Two hundred after the war years..." He looked up. "Units of what?"

"That," Voss said, "is the question I filed fourteen times. Here's answer number one." She slid a paper across: an audit response, crown-sealed, dated nine years back. *Reservoir composition is a matter of state security. The Office of Accounts certifies sufficiency. Further inquiry is not productive.*

"'Not productive,'" Harrow repeated softly. He had picked up one of the requisitions and was holding it like a man holds a snake he's decided not to fear. "I've seen this hand." He tapped the clerk's signature. "This is a Tallyman's second-tier notation. I trained under a man who trained under this hand." His voice had gone flat and careful. "In collections, we're taught the court keeps reserves. Collateral pools. Old contracts, defaulted souls held in trust against future debts." He looked up at Voss. "Nobody ever says what the collateral *is for*."

"It's for the walls," Wren said, from the doorway, coat clutched around her. "The walls are hungry and nobody tells them no."

Nobody moved. Outside, far off, the gulls began returning one at a time, screaming, as if resuming an argument.

"Ninety-eight years," Voss said. "Do you understand what I thought, at first? I thought it was ore. Soulstone, maybe, mined somewhere ugly. I wanted it to be ore. Ore you can stand." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Then I got into the deep annex two years ago. Before they caught me. Before the tribunal." She drew out the last paper — not a requisition but a manifest, older than the others, the ink browned with age. "This is the founding entry. The first replenishment ever ordered. Look at the description column."

Edmund read it aloud, because his eyes refused to do it alone.

"*Received into reservoir: one hundred souls, purchased per contract, voluntary, debts forgiven in consideration.*"

"Purchased," Quillon said.

"Voluntary," Edmund said.

"Debts forgiven," Harrow said, and now his voice cracked clean through the flatness. "That's the language. That's *my* language. That's a standard settlement clause — I've written it, I've *collected* on it. Voluntary conveyance of self against outstanding obligation." He sat down heavily, the chair complaining. "A hundred souls. The first hundred. And the requisitions go up every year, and the population records go up every year, and the poor-years — look—" his finger stabbed down the columns, collector's precision overriding horror, "—every famine, every flood, every bad harvest, the replenishment *doubles*. Because that's when people sell. That's when 'voluntary' gets easy."

"They've been eating the desperate for a century," Edmund said. The words came out strangely calm, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "To keep the lights on at the border. To keep the wards fed. And three nights ago the meal ran out, the wards died, and the sky went dark — because the sky was never dark, the sky was *paid for*—"

"No," Voss said sharply. "Get that right or you'll get everything else wrong. The stars are real. Stars are always real. But the Saltmere line didn't just hold back raiders and wild magic. The old kings built it as a *veil-work* too — a softening, a dimming of what's beyond, so the far dark stays far. When it ran, the night sky over the marches sat quiet and tame. When it stopped—" she gestured at the ceiling, at the black that had hung over them for three nights, "—everything the veil was holding back is now leaning on a door with no lock. And the court's answer, I guarantee you, is not to confess. Their answer will be to find more fuel." Her eyes went to Wren. "Something fell out of the sky with enough binding in it to run a border station for fifty years. What do you think five retrievals in grey-and-salt are for?"

Wren's glow guttered. Edmund moved without deciding to, putting himself between the child and the door, an absurd gesture, a body against an army, and knew he'd do it again.

"So defect," he said to Voss. "You already did — your badge says so. Why come here? Why warn us? You could be halfway to the free coast."

"Because I spent six years being told I was insane," Sera Voss said, and the bitterness in it was a live coal, banked but burning. "Six years of tribunals and sealed reports and colleagues looking at me with pity while the numbers sat in front of everyone's faces. I don't want escape. I want to be *right in public*. And that—" she gathered her papers, folding them along their thousand-folded lines, "—that means the wards need to be rebuilt on honest terms, which means the reservoir needs to be emptied, which means going beneath the mountain, which means—" she looked around the table, at each of them in turn, ending on the glowing child, "—I think the five of us may be the only people in this kingdom with all the pieces. A charm-seller who knows binding law better than the courts. An archivist who's been keeping a ledger with a page torn out. A collector who knows the contracts from the inside. A warden who knows the vaults. And whatever she is, who has *been* the fuel and walked away from it."

"Four of us," Edmund said automatically. "Plus the child."

"Five plus the child," Voss corrected, with the ghost of a smile that had forgotten how to finish itself. "I count her double. She's the only one of us who's been inside the reservoir."

Harrow was staring at the requisitions, and when he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere private and awful. "The bad years. The famine years. My father sold our harvest-debt down in the southern parishes the winter my brother was born." He swallowed. "Settlement clause. Voluntary conveyance. I used to think the family story was that he ran off." His hands had gone white on the table's edge. "Underhill. How many entries does your ledger have marked paid in a currency you don't recognize?"

"One page's worth," Edmund said. "Torn out."

"Find that page," Harrow said. "Because I think your guild and the mountain have been doing business longer than anyone wants written down. And I think the torn page is a receipt."

Quillon said nothing at all. But Edmund saw the old master's hand drift toward his sleeve — toward the place where, Edmund realized with a slow cold turning, Quillon had kept the ledger's loose papers for as long as Edmund had known him.

Outside, the gulls screamed. Beneath the mountain, something waited with a pen.

And Wren tugged Edmund's sleeve and pointed, not at the door, but at Quillon, and said, in the tone children use for stating the obvious:

"He's already crying."
