## 5. The Child Who Remains Bright

The child did not sleep that first night, and neither did Edmund.

She sat cross-legged on the guildhouse floor where Quillon had laid a blanket, in the corner beneath the shelf of unsold charms — the luck-knots, the fever-binds, the little stitched hearts meant for grieving pockets. She glowed. That was the thing Edmund could not stop looking at. Not brightly, not the way a lantern glows, but the way snow remembers light after dark: a faint silver suffusion under her skin, strongest at her wrists and throat, pulsing very slowly, like breathing.

Like a tide.

"Her name," Sera Voss said from the doorway. She had not left since she'd carried the child down from the roof, and she stood with her back to the frame the way wardens stand when they've decided a room is a post they can no longer hold. "She told me on the roof. Before you came up. She said her name was Wren."

"Did she say anything else?"

Sera's jaw worked. In the lamplight her face was all angles and old shame. "She asked me why it was so dark. I told her the stars went out. And she said—" Sera stopped. Started again, quieter, the way people speak when they're handling something breakable. "She said, *No. They were taken. I was there.*"

Edmund looked at the child. The child looked back.

She was perhaps seven years old by every measure his eyes offered him — small, thin, dark-haired, dressed in a nightgown of a cut he didn't recognize, fine-woven and decades out of fashion. But her eyes were not seven years old. Her eyes were patient in a way children's eyes never are, the patience of things that have waited in high cold places.

"Hello, Wren," Edmund said.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. Her voice was soft, unhurried, faintly amused, as though she found the world a clever trick someone had played on her. "You smell like thread and salt."

"That's accurate." He lowered himself to the floor across from her, joints complaining, and set the ledger between them without quite knowing why. Habit, maybe. When he didn't understand a thing, he brought it to the ledger. "I'm Edmund. That's Master Quillon, snoring in the chair. The woman at the door is Voss."

"I know what she is." Wren tilted her head. "Warden. Ward-warden. She used to be bright too, a long time ago, before they dimmed her."

"I beg your pardon," Sera said sharply.

But Wren had already turned away from her, attention sliding like water, and fixed on the ledger. On the gap in it. Edmund watched her look at the place where page forty-one should have been — the stub of it, ragged, torn close to the binding — and watched her whole small body go still, glow flaring once, twice, like a heartbeat stumbling.

"There," she whispered. "That's mine."

* * *

Quillon woke, of course. Quillon always woke when the ledger was touched; he'd slept beside it for fifty years the way other men slept beside wives. He came over stiffly, rubbing his eyes, took one look at the glowing child and the open book and said, "Tea," as if tea were a solution to anything, and put the kettle on because in the Guild of Small Mercies it usually was.

They sat around the worktable while it heated. Four of them now, plus whatever Wren was. Edmund turned the ledger so the torn stub faced her.

"Tell me what you see," he said.

"Numbers," Wren said. "A hundred and six numbers. Names under some of them. Not under most." She frowned, and the frown made her look suddenly, terribly young. "It's hard to read here. It's brighter below."

"Below where?"

"Where I was put away." She wrapped her small hands around the cup Quillon gave her, though she didn't drink; she just held it, absorbing its warmth the way stone absorbs sun. "There's a mountain with a door in its root. There are halls under it full of lamps. Thousands. More than thousands. Each lamp is a person who isn't using their person anymore." She said this with the mild precision of a child reciting a lesson, and it landed in the room like an anvil through a ceiling.

"Lamps," Sera repeated flatly.

"Hung up in rows. They burn slow. The ones in charge feed them to the wards a little at a time, so the borders stay strong and the taxes stay paid and nobody asks questions." Wren looked up, and her glow reflected in her own eyes until it seemed her eyes were doing the shining. "That's what stars are, you know. Or some of them. Some stars are just stars. But some of us got sold."

The kettle shrieked. Nobody moved to it except Quillon, whose hands, Edmund noticed, were shaking badly enough that he poured with both of them.

"Sold," Edmund said carefully. "To whom? By whom?"

"To the court. By everybody." Wren shrugged, a child's shrug, terrible in its simplicity. "Debts get paid. Debts have to get paid. Somebody has to pay them." Her gaze slid again to the ledger, to the torn edge, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Somebody paid with me. A hundred and six. I was number sixty-one."

Edmund felt the floor tilt gently, the way it does on a dock when a big boat passes far away.

Because he knew the ledger. He had audited it with Quillon two nights before the sky went black — the ledger of the Guild of Small Mercies, three hundred years of small debts forgiven and small kindnesses rendered, entries in Quillon's careful hand going back generations. And he knew the page that was missing. Page forty-one. He'd noticed the tear the way you notice a tooth gone from a comb: a small wrongness, easy to overlook, nagging.

And he remembered what had been written around the gap. The entries before it, in an older hand. Entries marked *paid* in a currency notation none of them recognized — a symbol like a star drawn inside a circle, repeated down the column. Quillon had seen it and gone quiet in a way Edmund had never seen him quiet, and said only, *Old business. Leave it be.*

Sixty-one. If the numbering ran true, entry sixty-one would have fallen squarely on page forty-one.

"Wren," Edmund said, and his voice came out strange to him. "When were you put away? Do you remember a date? A season? Anything about—about the people involved?"

Wren's brow creased. The glow along her wrists flickered, steadied.

"It was winter," she said. "Cold inside and cold outside. There was a room with green paint peeling off the walls, and a man with ink-stained fingers counting coins, and a woman standing behind me with her hand on my shoulder. She was crying. She kept saying sorry. She said—" Wren's face worked, and for the first time she looked like a child trying to carry something far too heavy for her arms. "She said, *the guild will keep your light safe, my love. The guild keeps everything safe.*"

The words fell into silence.

Edmund looked at Quillon. Quillon was staring at the ledger as if it had opened a vein.

"The guildhouse had green paint," Edmund said quietly. "In the east parlor. It was repainted before my time, but the ledgers mention it — maintenance records, back pages." He turned the ledger around, flipped past the torn stub into the older sections, running his finger down columns. "And these entries here, thirty-eight through forty — they're marked *paid*, in that currency we couldn't read. Star-in-circle. Paid in a lump sum. Three entries, then the page goes, then nothing for eleven years until the hand changes." He looked up. "Master Quillon. What happened in the winter of the year these were written? What did we sell?"

"I don't know," Quillon said.

It was, Edmund thought later, the speed of the answer that damned it. Not a pause, not a search of memory — just the instant, reflexive denial of a man who had rehearsed a lie for so long it had worn smooth as a river stone.

"Quillon."

"I don't *know*, boy." The old man's voice cracked on it. He gripped the table. "I know what you think I know, and I am telling you that I cannot— that it is not—"

"*Paid in a lump sum,*" Edmund said. "Three entries, one page torn out, and the guild survived a famine year that should have ended it. I can read an audit, Master. You taught me to. Whatever was on page forty-one, it was worth more than everything else in this book combined." He gentled despite himself, despite the heat climbing his throat. "What did we sell?"

Quillon looked at the child. At her glow. At the place where the page had been.

And then, horribly, he began to cry — silently, the way old men do, tears simply arriving on his cheeks as if they'd been waiting in line for decades.

"I'll tell you," he said. "God forgive me, I'll tell you everything. But not tonight. Tonight I need—" His voice failed. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I need you to let an old coward have one more night of being the man you thought I was."

* * *

Sera Voss took the second watch, which meant she sat by the window with her sword across her knees and pretended not to listen.

Edmund sat with Wren by the banked fire, because the child wouldn't go near the blanket in the corner. "Too dark down there," she explained. "Everything's darker near the ground. Light pools upward. Didn't you know?" He hadn't known. He suspected he would learn a great many things he hadn't known.

He fetched his charm-kit instead — the battered tin box, outlawed twice over — and laid out his tools between them: threads, needles, bone buttons, a spool of wire fine as hair. Not to sell. Just because hands that are busy make better company than hands that aren't.

"What's that one?" Wren asked, pointing.

"Fever-bind. You tie it over a sickroom door and it takes some of the burning into the knots. Doesn't cure anything. Just borrows the worst of it for a while, so the sick person can rest."

"Who holds the fever while they rest?"

Edmund paused. "The knot holds it."

"And then?"

"Then eventually it frays, and the fever goes back to where fevers go." He tied as he talked, showing her the loop and turn. "It's not a grand magic. It's a small mercy. That was the whole idea of the guild. The big magics went to the towers and the courts and the wars. We did the small stuff. Luck-knots for fishermen. Kindnesses stitched into pockets."

Wren watched his fingers with an intensity that had nothing childish in it. "I remember pockets," she said. "I remember being carried in one. Someone sewed me a pocket with a charm in it, so I wouldn't be afraid of the dark. But I was never afraid of the dark." She smiled, faint and distant. "I *was* the dark's favorite."

"What were you, Wren? Before?"

The glow pulsed. Once. Twice. Like thinking.

"Bright," she said finally. "I don't have a word for the shape of it. Words fit wrong, like shoes. I was bright, and I was part of a bigger bright, and we hung over the salt marshes and watched the boats come home, and there was a woman who used to talk to us. To me. She'd sit on the roof of a house with green paint and tell us about her day. About her students. About a boy who was bad at knots." Wren glanced sideways at him, sly and sudden. "That was you."

The needle stopped in Edmund's hand.

"She talked about me? To the—the stars?"

"To me. I liked you best. You were the one who couldn't stop asking why." Wren hugged her knees. "Then one winter the talking stopped. Then the taking started. Men with ledgers came to the roof of the world, and there was a weighing, and I was small enough to take." She rested her chin on her knees. "Do you know what the worst part was? It wasn't the dark. It was that they wrote it down. Everything gets written down. Somewhere there's a line with me in it, and the line says I was *paid*, like I was flour or nails."

Edmund found, to his surprise, that his eyes were wet.

He was a grown man, a hardened seller of outlawed charms, a survivor of ten years of hiding, and he sat on the floor of the ruined guildhouse with wet eyes because a star in a child's body had told him she minded being entered in a ledger.

"They shouldn't have written you down," he said. "Or if they wrote you down, it should have been right. It should have said what you actually were." He cleared his throat, picked the needle back up, and held out his palm — on it, a half-finished knot, white thread shot through with a single strand of silver wire. "Here. It's not done yet. But it's a luck-knot. Small mercy, grade one, guaranteed against nothing much." He hesitated. "I'd like to finish it for you. If you want it. It doesn't fix anything. It's just—so somewhere, there's a line with you in it, and the line says somebody made you something. Freely. With no debt attached."

Wren looked at the knot for a long time.

Then she reached out and touched it, and where her finger met the thread, the silver strand lit — genuinely lit, a thread of dawn caught in wool — and the knot bloomed into a pattern Edmund had never tied and could not have tied, loops folding through loops like a constellation learning to be a flower.

"Oh," Edmund said.

"Sorry," Wren said, pulling her hand back. "Sometimes I improve things by accident."

"Don't apologize. That's the finest luck-knot I've ever seen. I'd have been hanged for selling it." He laughed — really laughed, for the first time in longer than he wanted to count — and Wren laughed too, a bright startling sound, and for a moment the room was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fire.

From the window, Sera Voss said quietly, "Edmund."

He followed her gaze. Through the frost-feathered glass, down Cordwainer Street, the unnatural snow was still falling — the first snow Saltmere had seen since the stars went out, falling straight down, silent, in flakes that glittered even without any moon to glitter in. And at the far end of the street, where the lane bent toward the fishmarket, stood a figure with a lantern.

Not a warden. Wardens walked in pairs and carried staves. This man stood alone, still as a posted letter, and his lantern burned with a flame the color of ink.

"He's been there since midnight," Sera said. "Watching the door."

Quillon rose from his chair, went pale, and said, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant:

"That's the court's collector. That's Harrow."
