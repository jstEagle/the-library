## 6. A Debt Collector Changes Sides

He came at the hour when Cordwainer Street went the color of old pewter, when the fish carts had gone and the lamps hadn't yet been lit, and he came without hurry, which was the worst thing about him.

Edmund saw him first from the guildhouse window — a broad man in a coat the gray of slate, walking the middle of the street as if the street belonged to him, which, in a way Edmund was only beginning to understand, it did. He carried no weapon that Edmund could see. He carried a book.

"Someone's coming," Edmund said, and heard how his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading a charm's fine print.

Sera Voss was at the table with Wren, teaching the child to hold a warder's knot between finger and thumb — not to make it, just to hold it, the way you'd teach anyone to hold anything that could burn. Sera looked up, and Edmund watched ten years of disgrace rearrange themselves into something colder and more useful.

"Gray coat," she said. "No escort. That's Harrow."

"You know him."

"Everyone in the wardens knew *of* him. The court's collector. He comes when something is owed." She rose, and her hand went to her hip where a warder's baton would have hung, in the life she'd had before. It found nothing. Her jaw tightened at the absence. "If he's here for the child, we can't fight him. I want that understood before he's through the door. We can't. Whatever you're thinking — whatever the guild taught you about clever exits — put it down. Men like Harrow don't chase. They wait, and the debt does the chasing for them."

Wren looked up at the mention of her name, or at the shape of what they were saying around her. She was seven, or she had the shape of seven, or she had chosen seven the way a star might choose a window to look through. Her eyes still held that faint wrong brightness that no lamp could account for.

"Is he the one who put me away?" she asked.

"No," Edmund said. "But I think he's the one who fetches."

Quillon was in the back room with the ledger, as he had been every waking hour for three days, running his thumb down columns of names as though the answer to everything were hiding in the ink. Edmund went to him first. Not to warn him — Quillon had already heard, or Quillon always heard, the way old houses heard weather — but because there was a moment, standing in the doorway of that small warm room that smelled of tallow and dried rue, when Edmund understood that whatever happened next, it would happen to all of them together or not at all.

"Gray coat's at the corner," Edmund said. "Sera says it's the collector."

Quillon didn't look up. "Then he'll want to see the ledger," he said. "They always want to see the ledger."

* * *

Harrow knocked. That was the thing Edmund remembered afterward, the thing that unsettled him more than anything else that night: the court beneath the mountain, the court that had swallowed a century of souls and put out the stars to do it, sent a man who *knocked*.

Edmund opened the door. Harrow stood on the step with his book under his arm and his hands visible, and he had the kind of face that had been arranged long ago into patience and had never since been rearranged. Not cruel. Not kind. *Settled*, the way silt is settled at the bottom of a slow river.

"Edmund Underhill," Harrow said. Not a question. "Charm-seller. Last of the Guild of Small Mercies, by the court's count."

"By the court's count," Edmund agreed. "Which is a count I never agreed to."

"Nobody agrees to a count. That's what makes it a count." Harrow's gaze moved past Edmund's shoulder, unhurried, cataloguing the room the way a tide catalogs a beach. "I'm here for the one called Wren. The court holds a claim on her. I have the instrument of that claim." He touched the book under his arm, lightly, the way a man touches a key he's carried so long he's forgotten it opens anything. "I'd rather not take her by force. Force is expensive. It costs the taker as much as the taken, in the long arithmetic. So I'm asking you to open the door the rest of the way, and let the ledger do its work."

"The ledger," Edmund said. "You keep saying that word like it means the same thing in your mouth as in mine."

"Doesn't it?"

"Come in," Edmund said, "and we'll find out."

He heard Sera shift behind him — a warning in the creak of a floorboard — and he ignored it, or rather he made a decision inside the ignoring, the way you step off a roof and call it a choice. Because here was the thing, the thing he'd been turning over since the night the stars went out: the court had sent its collector, and its collector had come *alone*, and men do not come alone to take children from houses unless they are very sure, or unless they are not sure at all.

Harrow stepped inside. He ducked under the lintel — the guildhouse was old and built for smaller, poorer men — and stood in the front room with his hands still visible, and Sera put herself between him and Wren with the smooth economy of a woman who had once done this for a living, and Harrow looked at her and said, without heat:

"Voss. They struck you off the rolls. I have the paperwork. It's in the book."

"Then you know I've got nothing left to lose," Sera said.

"Nobody has nothing left to lose. That's a warder's fantasy." He said it mildly, almost kindly, and then his eyes found Wren, half-hidden behind Sera's shoulder, and something crossed his settled face — not a crack, nothing so dramatic as a crack. A weather change. "There she is," he said quietly. "They said she'd be bright. They undersold it."

"I remember you," Wren said.

Edmund felt the room go still.

"You came to the place where they put me," Wren said. "A long time ago. You stood outside the door and you didn't come in, and afterward you walked down the corridor and your boots made a sound like *tok, tok, tok*, and I counted it. I counted for a hundred years and your boots were the loudest thing."

Harrow did not move. But Edmund, watching him — watching the way you watch a wall for signs of shifting — saw the man's hands close, slowly, into fists, and saw that the fists were not for them.

"That was my first collection," Harrow said. His voice had lost none of its evenness, which was how Edmund knew how much it had cost him. "I stood outside the door because the instrument said the claim was valid, and the instrument is never wrong, and I have spent thirty years being a man for whom the instrument is never wrong." He looked down at his own hands as though they belonged to the court, which, in fairness, they might. "I'm not here to argue with a child about what she counted."

"Then why are you here?" Sera asked.

"Because the ledger is unbalanced," Harrow said, "and I am the man whose whole life is the balance, and for three nights the stars have been out, and every account I hold has gone quiet, and I cannot sleep, and I have come to the only house in Saltmere that might know why." He turned to Edmund, and for the first time the settled face asked for something. "Show me your ledger."

* * *

They put it on the table between them — the old ledger of the Guild of Small Mercies, its spine cracked, its corners furred with a century of handling, the torn page leaving its ragged scar two-thirds through. Quillon sat across from Harrow with his hands folded, wearing the face of a man watching a surgeon open a body. Sera stood by the window with her back to the light. Wren sat on the floor at Edmund's feet, cross-legged, her head tipped back, watching the collector the way children watch thunder.

Harrow read the way he did everything: without hurry. His finger went down the columns. Names, debts, payments, the small mercies of the guild's old trade — luck-knots and fever-charms and grief-wards, sold at prices the poor could almost pay. Then his finger reached the scar of the torn page and stopped.

"This page," he said. "When was it taken out?"

"Before my time," Edmund said. "Before Quillon's, he says."

"It was not before mine," Quillon said quietly, and said nothing more.

Harrow's finger went on, past the scar, into the last decades of entries, and Edmund watched him read and understood, with a slow cold settling, what the man was actually looking for. Not Wren's claim. Not the court's instrument. He was reading the guild's ledger the way a drowning man reads the shore.

Then the finger stopped.

Edmund saw it stop. Saw the stillness come into the broad shoulders, the stillness of a man who has walked into a door he did not know was there. Harrow turned back one page. Turned forward again. Went back to a line two-thirds down, in faded brown ink, in a hand Edmund did not recognize — one of the old masters, dead before Edmund was born.

Harrow read the name. Edmund watched his lips move around it, though no sound came out.

Then Harrow closed the ledger, gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom, and stood there with his hand flat on the cover, and for a long moment the only sound in the guildhouse was the sea, far off, doing what the sea does.

"Read it to me," he said.

"Harrow—" Sera began.

"Read it to me," he said again, and his voice did not rise, and that was worse. "The entry. All of it."

Edmund opened the book to the line. It was an old entry, in the guild's old format, before the trade had been outlawed and the format had died with it: a name, a debt, a payment, a note.

"*Tobias Harrow,*" Edmund read, and heard the collector's breath go out of him like a struck bell going quiet. "*Debt to the Guild of Small Mercies: one fever-charm, one winter's coal, one burial. Paid in full to the court beneath the mountain, per arrangement of the third Master Quillon. Considered settled. Considered* —" Edmund's voice caught on the last word, and he made himself finish it — "*considered collected.*"

The room was very quiet.

"He owed you three small things," Harrow said. His hand was still flat on the ledger. It had begun, very slightly, to tremble. "A charm against fever. Coal for one winter. And the burying of our mother, who died in the salt fever year, because the guild took pity on a family that couldn't pay and — and the guild took pity, that's what it *did*, that's what it was *for* —" He stopped. He straightened. Edmund watched him assemble himself out of the wreckage of the moment, plank by plank, the way a man rebuilds a wall around a broken window. "And the payment for that pity was my brother."

"The court bought souls," Sera said. It wasn't a question. She had known for three days; she had been the one to say *spent, not broken* on the roof with the stars going out. But she said it now the way you say a thing in front of a witness, to make it real. "For a century. The wards were fed on them. The court paid the guild's debts in — in that currency, and the guild let it."

"I didn't know," Quillon said, from across the table, and his voice was the voice of an old man standing at the edge of something, and Edmund looked at his old master and thought: *that is not the voice of a man who didn't know.* But there was no time for it, then. There would be time. There was going to be so much time.

"I didn't know," Quillon said again.

Harrow was not listening to Quillon. Harrow was looking at the ledger, at the entry, at the three small mercies that had cost a man his brother, and Edmund saw the guilt arrive — saw it land on the settled face like a bird landing on a branch, and stay.

"I collected her," Harrow said. "The child. I stood outside her door a hundred years ago and I didn't come in, and I told myself the not-coming-in was mercy. I told myself that for thirty years. That I was the gentle one, that I knocked, that I gave them the chance to open the door themselves, that a collector who knocks is a kinder thing than a collector who breaks." He laughed — one dry note, with no floor under it. "And all the while my brother's name was sitting in a guild ledger in a house on Cordwainer Street, marked *collected*, and I was the collection."

"Harrow," Edmund said.

"The court sends me when something is owed." Harrow picked the book up — Edmund's ledger, the guild's ledger — and held it in both hands, and looked at it the way a man looks at a mirror he has avoided for thirty years. "I have spent my whole life being the instrument. I have told myself the instrument is never wrong. But an instrument that reads a brother's name and calls it a balance—" He set the book down. Carefully. Precisely. The way he did everything. "The instrument is wrong. The instrument has always been wrong. And I am the only man in Saltmere who knows the way down."

* * *

They talked until the lamps burned low and the sea turned black beyond the windows.

Harrow drew the way down in charcoal on the back of an old charm-flyer: the salt road out of the city, the withered ford, the mine mouths in the mountain's southern flank, and beneath them — his charcoal moving slower now, more reluctant — the long descents, the tally-halls, the vault of lamps. He drew the wards that guarded each gate and told Sera which ones still answered to a warder's seal and which ones had gone feral in the dark. He drew the Tallyman's hall as an empty rectangle and would not put anything inside it.

"You can't draw him," Sera said.

"You can't draw him," Harrow agreed.

"And the child's claim." Edmund turned the charcoal sketch toward himself. "You came with an instrument. The court holds a claim on Wren. If you walk back down without her—"

"I won't walk back down without her," Harrow said. "That's rather the point."

"The point," Sera said, "is that you'd be defecting. There's no coming up from that. The court doesn't strike men off its rolls the way the wardens did me. The court *collects* them."

"I know what the court does." Harrow looked at her, and then at Wren, who had fallen asleep against Edmund's side somewhere in the third hour, her faint brightness dimmed to an ember, breathing the small even breaths of the truly safe. "I have spent thirty years telling myself I was the gentle one because I knocked. I have a brother in a vault under a mountain, hung up like a lamp, and a child in front of me who counted my boots for a century, and a ledger in my hands that says the guild I'm robbing was robbed first." He closed his eyes, opened them. "Guilt is a currency too. I intend to spend all of mine in one place."

Edmund looked at the sketch. The salt road. The ford. The mountain, drawn as a triangle with a small square at its heart, and the small square labeled in Harrow's careful hand: *the lamps.*

"When do we go?" Edmund asked.

"Tomorrow," Harrow said. "Before the court sends someone who doesn't knock." He stood, and gathered his coat about him, and at the door he paused, and looked back at the ledger on the table — at the guild's whole history of small mercies, and the scar of the torn page, and the entry that had unmade him.

"Master Underhill," he said. "When we're down there. When we find them. My brother first. I'll help you with whoever you came for — I give you my word on it, and my word is the only instrument I have left that isn't broken. But my brother first."

"Your brother first," Edmund said.

Harrow nodded once, and went out into the dark, and his boots on the cobbles made a sound like *tok, tok, tok*, and this time no one counted it.

Edmund stood in the doorway a while, watching the gray coat diminish up Cordwainer Street. Behind him, Quillon began gathering the ledger's scattered pages with hands that were not quite steady, and Edmund turned, and looked at his old master, and saw that Quillon was not looking at the pages at all. He was looking at the scar of the torn page. He had been looking at it, Edmund realized, for three days. Perhaps for thirty years.

"Quillon," Edmund said. "The third Master Quillon. The arrangement."

The old man's hands went still on the paper.

"There's something you have to tell me," Edmund said. "Before we go down that mountain. Before I walk into a vault of lamps looking for her. There's something you have to tell me about who it was that sold."

Quillon closed his eyes. Outside, the first flakes of the unnatural snow had begun to fall, soft as ash, past the window, past the lamp, past the face of the old man who had kept the guild alive at a price he had carried alone through every warm domestic year of Edmund's childhood — and Edmund knew, in the same breath that he asked the question, that he did not want to hear the answer, and that he was going to hear it.

"Tell me it wasn't her," Edmund said. "Tell me it wasn't Aldsey."

Quillon opened the ledger to the scar of the torn page, and could not speak.
