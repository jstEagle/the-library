## 7. Quillon's Confession

The guildhouse smelled of tallow and old paper, the way it had every night of Edmund's remembered life, but tonight the smell sat wrong in his nose — sour, like meat left out. He had lit every lamp they owned. It was a childish thing to do. He knew it was a childish thing to do. He did it anyway.

Wren slept in the back room, curled on Quillon's good blanket, glowing faintly through her sleep like a coal breathing. Sera Voss stood at the window with her back to the room, watching the street for wardens who had not yet come. Harrow sat at the long table with the ledger open before him, reading it again from the first page, as if the answer to his brother might be hiding somewhere he'd already looked.

And Quillon sat by the cold hearth with his hands folded in his lap, an old man made suddenly very old, watching Edmund light the last lamp on the mantel.

"You're going to ask me," Quillon said.

Edmund set the lamp down. His hand was steady. He noticed that about himself sometimes, in the worst moments — the steadiness. It felt less like courage than like a door closing somewhere inside him, a room going dark so another could stay lit.

"Ask you what?" he said.

"Edmund."

"The currency." Edmund turned around. "You looked at that page three weeks ago and went white as salt. You knew what paid those debts. You've known since before the stars went out, since before Wren fell onto my roof, since before any of it. And I have stood in this kitchen and eaten your bread and mended your coat and called you *Master* while you kept it behind your teeth." He heard his own voice climb and forced it down. "So no. I'm not going to ask. I'm going to stand here until you tell me, because I am done being managed by old men who love me."

Sera didn't turn from the window. Harrow closed the ledger softly, a small courtesy, like drawing a sheet over a face.

Quillon nodded slowly, as if Edmund had confirmed something he'd been counting on. He rose — it took him two tries — and went to the ledger shelf, the one Edmund had dusted since he was nine years old, and pressed the false back the way only guildmasters knew. The panel swung open on a shallow cavity, and from it he took a second ledger.

Thinner. Older. Bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth at the corners. Edmund had never seen it in his life, and yet he knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a dream — because it was a guild ledger, marked with the seal of Small Mercies, and it was older than the ban, older than the law, older than him.

"What is that?"

"Volume four," Quillon said. "There were six volumes once. The court bought three when the Guildfall came. This one they never found." He set it on the table beside Harrow's elbow. "Because I hid it. Because of what's written in it."

Harrow looked at the book the way a man looks at a snake he has decided not to fear. "Then let's read what's written."

Quillon didn't touch it. "Page forty," he said. "Read page forty aloud, Harrow. I'd rather hear it in a stranger's voice. Mine would crack."

Harrow opened it. Turned pages with a collector's delicacy. Stopped.

He read: "*Sold to the Undermount Court, one soul entire, freely surrendered by its holder for discharge of debt outstanding. Name of debtor: the Guild of Small Mercies. Name of soul—"*

Harrow stopped.

"Say it," Edmund said.

"*Mother Aldsey,*" Harrow read. "*Teacher of the craft. Entered the ledger of the living forty-one years past. Removed from it this day.*"

The lamps did not go out. That was the strange part, Edmund thought later. The room stayed warm and yellow and ordinary, the kettle still ticked on the hob, and Mother Aldsey had been sold forty-one years ago and nobody had told him.

He sat down. Not gracefully. His knees simply ended their argument with gravity.

"She died," he said. "When I was fourteen. She got the fever, and she died, and we buried her in the Salt Yard under the rowan, and I put a luck-knot on the stone every year on her day. Twenty years of luck-knots on an empty grave."

"There is no grave," Quillon said quietly. "There never was. There was a fever, yes. And a body that breathed and did not wake, because the greater part of her had been carried down the mountain. We buried what remained. I have hated myself with great skill and dedication ever since, if that's worth anything to you. It isn't to me."

* * *

Edmund did not remember standing up. He was across the room with his fist cocked and Quillon hadn't moved to stop him, hadn't flinched, had tilted his chin up slightly like a man offering his throat to a blade he thought he deserved — and that, more than anything, stopped Edmund's hand. You could hit a man who defended himself. Hitting this would be kicking a dog that wanted it.

"Tell me why," Edmund said, and his voice came out low and terrible, a stranger's voice. "Tell me why, or I swear by every charm I ever sold I will walk out of this house and never—"

"Sit down," Quillon said, "and I'll tell you why, and you will wish to your gods I hadn't."

Edmund sat. His hands shook now. Now that it was over, now that the blow had landed or failed to land, now they shook like a drunkard's.

"Forty-one years ago the guild was dying," Quillon said. "Not outlawed yet — that came later — but dying. The plague years. Do you remember the plague years? Of course you don't, you weren't born. Half our clients dead, the rest too poor to pay in coin, and coin was what we needed, because the crown had sent assessors. The Guild of Small Mercies held charters in eleven cities, boy. Eleven. Saltmere was the last one left after the fever took Cordwainer Street end to end. The assessors were coming to strike our charter, seize the house, burn the ledgers, and scatter what was left of us into the gutters. And I was master of a guild with debts it could not pay and members it could not feed."

He turned the old ledger toward himself, though he didn't look at it.

"A man came. From beneath the mountain. Before the Tallyman there were others — clerks, factors, men with soft voices and hard arithmetic. He offered to buy. Not charms. Not services. Souls. He said the wards of the border were hungry, that the crown fed them in secret, that the court beneath paid better than any patron above ground, and that the transaction was — his word — *merciful*. The seller feels nothing, he said. They sleep. They dream lightly. They are not in pain."

"And you believed him."

"I *wanted* to believe him," Quillon said, with sudden ferocity, and for a moment he was forty years younger and master of something. "That's worse, isn't it? Yes. I believed him because believing him meant the guild lived. Eleven charters down to one, and I was the man who let the last one die? No. No, I could not be that man. So I went through the rolls looking for a debt large enough to matter, and there was one name on nearly every page, because Aldsey had spent thirty years buying medicine for other people's children against her own share, and her account was the deepest hole we had."

"She'd have refused."

"She *did* refuse. Twice. So I waited until she had the fever, when she was past refusing, and I signed for her." Quillon's voice did not break. Edmund wished it would break. A breaking voice would have been a crack in the wall; this was the wall itself, mortared and load-bearing. "I told myself she'd have consented if she'd been awake. I told myself the guild was a thousand mercies and one sin was a fair price. I told myself every lie a coward tells himself at night, and then I took the money, and the assessors came, and saw our accounts whole, and went away, and the guild lived."

He finally looked up. His eyes were wet but steady, and Edmund hated him, and loved him, and could not find the seam between the two.

"Every charm you ever learned," Quillon said, "every knot and word and small mercy you can work — you learned it in a house that was purchased with her. I taught you the craft your mother-figure sold away to keep alive. There is not a blessing this guild has given in forty years that isn't built on her bones. That is my confession. Write it where you like."

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke. The kettle ticked. Somewhere outside, far off, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

It was Sera who broke the silence, without turning from the window. "The wards," she said. "The border wards eat souls. The court's been feeding them for a century. Your Aldsey has been burning in the wards for forty-one years." A pause. "Or in whatever they keep instead."

"In the lamps," Edmund said hollowly. "Harrow said. They hang them like lamps."

"Then she's been lighting their halls," Sera said, "with the light they stole from her."

Edmund put his head in his hands. He thought of her hands — big, cracked, quick, smelling of hemp and honey — tying a knot over his nine-year-old fingers and saying, *the mercy goes in last, always last, or it's just rope.* He thought of twenty luck-knots laid on a stone that covered nothing. He thought of the torn page in the current ledger, the debts paid in a currency neither he nor anyone recognized, and understood at last that the guild had been paying installments all along, quietly, for decades, and that the currency was the same as it had ever been.

"We're three people," he said into his hands. "We were three people. And we've been a lie the whole time."

"No," Quillon said.

Edmund looked up.

"The guild is not the house, or the charter, or the ledgers, or me." The old man leaned forward, and there was something in his face Edmund had never seen there — not guilt, exactly; something older and harder, worn smooth by decades of carrying it. "The guild is the mercy. That's all it ever was. Everything else was packaging. I sold her soul to save the packaging, and that was my sin — I mistook the shell for the thing. But the thing itself, the mercy — that was hers. She put it in you, knot by knot, and no assessor or Tallyman or foolish old man can buy that back off you." His voice roughened at last, finally, usefully. "I don't ask forgiveness. I'm telling you where the fault line runs so you don't build on it blind."

"I should hate you."

"You should. It's cheaper than what I'm asking, which is that you use me. I'm old, I know the mountain's older ledgers, I remember the factor's face and the words of the contract — there were *words*, Edmund, terms, conditions, a clause of return. Contracts beneath the mountain are contracts. Even kings of arithmetic keep their own rules. If there is a way to un-sell her, it runs through the paperwork, and I am the only man alive who has read it."

Harrow stirred. For the first time since opening the book, the debt-collector looked almost gentle, which on Harrow's face was more frightening than anger. "Clause of return," he repeated. "Everything the court buys carries one. Redemption at par, plus interest. It's how they bind the sellers — everyone who sells believes, someday, they'll buy back." He tapped the old ledger. "If your guildmaster here kept the original contract—"

"I kept everything," Quillon said. "Guilt is an excellent filing system."

Despite himself — despite the fury still sitting in his chest like swallowed coals — Edmund laughed. One short, broken sound. Quillon smiled, and it was the saddest thing Edmund had ever seen on a human face.

"I don't forgive you," Edmund said. "Not tonight. Maybe not—"

"I know."

"But tomorrow we go down the mountain, and you come with us, and you spend every year of your guilt getting her back. That's not forgiveness. That's a payment plan."

Quillon bowed his head. "The guild," he said, "always did offer terms."

* * *

Later — much later, when Sera had taken the watch and Harrow slept sitting up with the old ledger under his hand like a man guarding a wound — Edmund climbed to the roof.

The sky was still wrong. Empty. A black ceiling with no nails holding it up. He found himself unable to look at it for long, and looked instead at the city: Cordwainer Street, the harbor, the dark hump of the mountain far south, wearing snow that had no business falling in this season.

Wren was there. Of course she was there; he was learning that the child appeared on rooftops the way cats appear in warm kitchens. She sat cross-legged at the ridge, faintly luminous, watching the blank sky with the patience of someone waiting for friends to arrive.

"Quillon sold my teacher," Edmund said, sitting beside her. He didn't know why he told her. Perhaps because she was the only creature in the world to whom a soul was a simple subject. "Before I met her. He sold her, and she's hanging in a vault under the mountain, and I tied knots on her empty grave for twenty years."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a judge.

"When stars fall," she said, "they don't die. They get *put away*. Put-away isn't done." She frowned, searching for the words, her small hands turning over each other. "Done is... undone. You undo it. Like a knot."

Edmund stared at her. "Like a knot."

She nodded firmly, satisfied, and pointed one glowing finger at the southern mountain. "Somebody put her away up there. So somebody goes up there and unties her."

The wind came off the harbor, cold and tasting of the unnatural snow. Below, in the guildhouse, an old man slept badly among his sins, and a debt-collector dreamed of his brother, and a warden watched the street for soldiers.

Edmund looked at the mountain a long time.

"All right," he said quietly. "Tomorrow we learn the contract. Tomorrow we go and untie her."

And far to the south, deep beneath the snow, in a cathedral of hanging lights, one lamp among thousands flickered — just once — as if something inside it had heard its name spoken after forty-one years, and was trying, with all its dimming strength, to answer.
