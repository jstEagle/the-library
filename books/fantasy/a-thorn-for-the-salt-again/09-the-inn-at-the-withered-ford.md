## 9. The Inn at the Withered Ford

The inn announced itself before they saw it — a smell of woodsmoke and old bread riding the wind across the frozen ford, and beneath it something stranger, a stillness, like the air inside a sealed room. The Withered Ford had been a crossing point when Saltmere's salt road was a vein of empire. Now the river ran gray under ice, the bridge was down to its ribs, and the inn stood alone on the far bank with its windows lit against a sky that hadn't shown a star in six days.

"I know this place," Harrow said, and stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into his back.

"You would," Sera said. "Debt-collectors know every road out of every city."

"Not as a collector." He didn't turn around. "As a boy. My father brought me through here once, heading south. There was an innkeeper with white in her beard." He frowned. "That was thirty years ago."

Edmund looked up at the sign swinging over the door — a fish split lengthwise, painted and repainted so many times it had become more weather than picture — and felt the particular cold that wasn't cold at all, the one he'd learned to read in a ledger that wouldn't balance.

"Then we should be polite," Edmund said, "and ask no questions about anyone's age."

Quillon coughed into his scarf. "Or we ask all of them."

"Master Quillon."

"He's earned one question," Quillon said. "The rest can wait for breakfast."

Wren had gone quiet, which was itself an answer. She'd been quieter since the salt road, since the feral wards had parted around her like dogs recognizing their master's daughter. Now she stood looking at the inn with her head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear.

"There are people in the walls," she said.

Everyone stopped moving at once.

"Inside the walls?" Sera's hand went to her knife out of pure warden reflex, and she caught herself, embarrassed. "How many?"

Wren counted with her fingers, lips moving. Her counting was strange to watch — she'd start at ten and work down, or skip numbers entirely, because numbers were a human habit and she was only wearing humanity like a coat she hadn't decided to keep.

"Eleven," she said. "Maybe twelve. One of them is very small." She looked at Edmund, and her eyes did the thing they sometimes did, catching light that wasn't there, holding it. "They're not hiding from us. They're hiding from him." A nod at Harrow. "From what he is."

Harrow stood very still. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he let it.

* * *

The common room of the Withered Ford was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fire, which burned low and orange and never seemed to need feeding. The innkeeper came out wiping her hands on an apron gone soft as cloth can only go after decades of the same washing, and she had white in her beard, exactly as Harrow remembered, not one hair more.

She looked at the five of them — the disgraced warden favoring her side, the old man leaning on his stick, the charm-seller with a child made of starlight asleep against his shoulder — and her face did something complicated and settled on hospitality.

"Five beds is two too many," she said. "I've got three rooms and a loft. Stew's on. Bread's yesterday's but it keeps well here."

"How long does it keep?" Quillon asked, mild as milk.

"As long as you're eating it," she said, and met his eyes, and neither of them blinked. "Name's Maren. You'll be wanting to pay?"

"We'll want to pay," Edmund said carefully.

"Mm." She took down bowls. "Coin spends here. It just doesn't leave." She ladled stew without asking what anyone wanted, which was the kind of confidence Edmund associated with women who had survived things by deciding in advance how they'd behave. "There's a rule in my house. Nobody asks anybody how long they've been staying. Some of my guests checked in during the last king's reign and I'll thank you to treat them with the courtesy due any paying customer."

"And if someone asks anyway?" Sera asked.

"Then they eat outside." Maren set the bowls down hard enough to make a point. "Which reminds me. There's a corner table by the back wall. The draft comes through it somethin' awful. I'd keep clear of it, if I were guests who mind drafts."

She turned back to her kitchen, and Edmund understood, slowly, the way you understand a charm's working — not all at once but in layers, each one worse.

The draft came through the back wall. And there were eleven people hiding in the walls.

He carried his bowl to the far end of the room, sat where he could watch that corner, and waited. Beside him, Harrow ate nothing. He sat with his collector's ledger — the real one, the black one with the court's seal pressed into the leather — closed under his hand, staring at the wall as though he could see through plaster and lath into the dark spaces between.

"My father stayed here two nights," he said quietly. "I remember the stew. I remember being seven years old and thinking this was the finest place in the world." His jaw worked. "I don't remember the innkeeper having a beard then either."

"She did," said a voice from the corner table.

* * *

She stepped out of the wall the way smoke steps back into a chimney — one moment absent, the next present, a woman in a patched coat with dust in her hair and a face so ordinary it took a moment to understand why Edmund's chest seized. It was the ordinariness. She looked like anyone. Like the widow he'd sold a luck-knot to. Like a person, whole and unlit, walking around free.

Except her shadow was wrong. It lay behind her a half-second delayed, catching up to her movements like something following at a respectful distance.

"You're one of them," Sera breathed. "A lamp-soul. Out of the mountain."

"Out of the vaults, technically." The woman sat down at their table without invitation, which Edmund suspected was a habit of people who'd spent years not being allowed to sit anywhere. "Myra Tull. I was a midwife in Duncastle, sixty years ago. Sold to settle my village's grain debt. I hung in the dark for—" she waved a hand— "a while. Then the wards got hungry, the way they do, and stretched thin, and I walked out between two heartbeats of the alarm bell. Been walking south ever since. Or north. Direction stops mattering."

"Why are you in the walls?" Edmund asked.

Myra smiled without much humor. "Because word travels, even underground. Word says the court sent a collector up the salt road after a runaway star-child." Her eyes went to Harrow and stayed there, flat and unafraid in the way of someone who had already survived the worst day of her life and found it survivable. "And word says collectors don't travel alone, don't change sides, and don't stop hunting. So we hid. Eleven of us. We've been coming to this inn for years — Maren doesn't age, the ford doesn't freeze all the way through, and the court's ledgers have a hole where this valley should be. It's the one place a soul can stand still."

"The walls," Harrow said. His voice sounded scraped. "You've been living in the walls of an inn while I slept in the rooms."

"We heard you snoring, if it helps."

"It doesn't."

"No," Myra agreed. "I imagine it doesn't." She looked around the table, taking inventory the way collectors take inventory, and something in her expression shifted toward pity. "You're going down the mountain. All five of you. To steal lamps."

"To steal one lamp," Edmund said.

"They always say that." Myra leaned forward. "Charm-seller, I hung next to a man who went in to retrieve his daughter and came out with forty. Once you see them hanging there, all that light, all those lives in jars — you don't take one. You take what your arms can carry and you curse yourself for the rest." She tapped the table. "But that's not why I'm here. I'm here because of him." A chin-jerk at Harrow. "Because the eleven in the walls voted, and the vote was close, and what it came down to is: do we run tonight, or do we trust the collector? And I drew the short straw to come ask him to his face."

* * *

The kitchen was warmer, and smaller, and Maren had retreated to the far end of it with a cloth she kept wringing and rewringing, pretending very badly not to listen. Wren sat on the flour barrel with her legs dangling, watching Harrow with the frank attention of a child watching weather decide what it will become.

The escaped souls had crowded into the doorway — Myra first, then an old soldier whose shadow lagged a full second behind him, then a girl of maybe fifteen with a lamp-glow still faintly visible beneath the skin of her throat, pulsing slow as sleep. Twelve, counting the smallest, a boy of four who peered out from behind the soldier's leg with eyes like banked coals.

Harrow stood facing them, and Edmund watched him arrive at the place he must have known was waiting for him somewhere down the salt road: the spot where everything he'd been became a thing he had to answer for.

"I collected for nineteen years," Harrow said. "I found names. That was my gift, they called it. I could find a debtor's name anywhere — hidden in a marriage, buried in a charity, folded into another man's signature. The court gave me lists and I brought back souls." His voice held steady, which Edmund recognized as effort. "Some of you may be on those lists. Some of you may be there because of me specifically. I won't insult you by saying I didn't know what the lamps were. I knew. I told myself the court kept them safe. Kept them *somewhere*. I told myself a great many sentences that began with *the court*."

"That's very pretty," the soldier said. "Does it bring back the years I hung in the dark?"

"No."

"Does it feed us? Clothe us? Get us past Cade's wardens, who I'm told are a day behind us and angry?"

"No," Harrow said again. "But I can tell you what's behind the third stone in the vault corridor, and which of the lamp-wards answers to a warden's cadence rather than a clerk's, and where the Tallyman keeps his second key, because the first one is a decoy and has been for sixty years. I can tell you these things whether or not you forgive me, and I'll spend them on you regardless, because—" he stopped, started again, and Edmund heard the sentence rebuild itself brick by brick— "because a contract is a promise you make to someone else, and mine's ended, and I find I still need to keep promises. It's all I know how to do."

Silence, except the fire, which crackled in a way that seemed almost deliberate, almost encouraging.

It was the little boy who broke it. He detached from the soldier's leg, crossed the floor with the solemnity of the very young, and stood in front of Harrow. He reached up and took hold of the collector's coat lapel in one small fist, not pulling, just holding, the way a child tests whether a thing is solid.

"You smell like paper," the boy said.

"Yes."

"Paper smells sad."

Harrow crouched down. This was the man who had come to Saltmere to collect a star-child and deliver her to a cathedral of lamps, crouching on a kitchen floor to be eye-level with a four-year-old whose shadow couldn't keep up with him.

"It does," Harrow said. "I'm working on that."

The boy studied him with coal-bright eyes, then patted the lapel twice, some verdict rendered in a language older than ledgers, and went back to the soldier. And Edmund watched Harrow stay crouched a moment longer than he needed to, head bowed, collecting himself the way other men collect coins.

"Well," said Myra into the quiet. "The vote stands at eleven to nothing now. The little one votes twice; house rules." She looked around at all of them. "We're coming with you as far as the mines. Not down — I'm done with the mountain, and I'll say that plainly. But there's a smuggler's track over the ridge that wardens don't watch, and we know it, and you don't. Consider it the first payment on a very long account."

Maren finally abandoned the pretense of her cloth. "You'll want the loft," she said gruffly. "All of you. And nobody — *nobody* — asks how long I've been keeping this inn, because the answer involves a tax assessor I haven't seen off my doorstep since the Old King's time, and he's still sitting there."

* * *

Later, when the loft had settled into breathing and the fire below had burned down to its patient orange, Edmund found Wren at the loft window, knees pulled up, watching the sky.

No stars. Six days, and the dark above the mountains was total, a blackness with texture to it, like felt. Somewhere beyond that darkness the mountain stood with its halls of lamps, and somewhere in those halls hung a light that had taught him knots and mercy and how to haggle with the dead.

"Wren," he said softly. "What did you mean, the first night — when you fell out of the sky? When you said you were put away."

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, in her fragmentary way, picking words like stones for a wall:

"He was gentle. The tally-man. First one. Before the lamps, before the ledgers. He was a shepherd and the sky was thin and leaking and somebody had to count what fell through." Her fingers curled around her knees. "He counted me. And then he couldn't stop counting. That's what happens to shepherds who lose the flock. They build a fold." She turned her coal-dark eyes on Edmund. "You're going to meet him. And he's going to offer you something fair. And you'll want to say yes, because fair is the most beautiful lie there is."

"What do I say instead?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of someone remembering several centuries.

"You say what my mother said, when the sky asked her to give me up." She smiled, small and strange and bright at the edges. "You say: *no. She's mine.*"

Below them, muffled through the floorboards, the front door of the inn opened and closed. Edmund went to the window in time to see a figure standing in the snow at the edge of the firelight — tall, armored, travel-worn, with a lantern raised against the dark.

Sera was already on the stairs, knife drawn, face gone gray. "Cade," she said. "He's alone. He wants to talk."
