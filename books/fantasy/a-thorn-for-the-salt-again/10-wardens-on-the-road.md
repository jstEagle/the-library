## 10. Wardens on the Road

The wardens caught them at the second milestone past the Withered Ford, where the salt road narrowed between two shoulders of black rock and the old waystones leaned together like conspirators.

Edmund heard them before he saw them — the particular rhythm of boots that belonged to no one in their party. Five sets, maybe six, moving at a march. He turned, and there they were: six city wardens in salt-grey cloaks, crossbows slung but hands resting near them, and at their head a broad-shouldered man with a face like a door someone had once tried to kick in and mostly failed.

Sera stopped walking so abruptly that Harrow nearly collided with her.

"Cade," she said.

"Sera." The warden's voice was flat as a ledger line. "You're under arrest for desertion of post, theft of court property, and—" his eyes moved down the line, cataloguing them the way he'd once catalogued breaches in the wards, "—consorting with persons unknown. Hand over the child and the rest is paperwork."

Wren had gone very still against Edmund's side. Not frightened — he'd learned the difference by now. Frightened, she shrank. This was something else. She had drawn herself up to her full height, which was not much, and her eyes had gone faintly luminous, like coins at the bottom of a well.

"She's not property," Edmund said.

Cade looked at him then, properly, for the first time. "And you are?"

"Nobody," Edmund said. It came out too easily. He'd been nobody for ten years; it should have fit better than it did.

* * *

They had talked about this, in the way people talk about storms — knowing it might come, hoping to be elsewhere when it did. Around the inn's dead hearth two nights ago, Sera had laid it out with a soldier's bluntness: *If Cade catches us, don't run. Running gives him reason. And whatever happens, keep Wren behind you.* She hadn't said what she would do, because she didn't have to. Sera Voss had spent eleven years learning every way a body could be persuaded to stop, and she carried all of that knowledge in her hands like a debt she'd never asked for.

Now she stepped forward, out of the line, and put herself between Cade and the rest of them, and Edmund understood that the conversation was already over.

"You read the vault records," she said quietly. "I know you did. I saw you reading them the night I left. You know what the wards were fed on. You know what's hanging in those halls under the mountain like laundry."

"I know my orders," Cade said.

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Cade agreed. "It isn't." And there — just for a heartbeat — something moved behind his face, some old argument he'd been having with himself since long before the stars went out. Then he closed it away, the way he closed everything away, and lifted two fingers.

The wardens' crossbows came up.

Edmund had one moment — one long, stretched, syrup-slow moment — to take in the geometry of it. Six bolts, five of them angled toward Sera because she was the trained threat, one toward Harrow because Harrow had made the mistake of reaching inside his coat. Wren behind him. Quillon behind Wren, too slow to move, too proud to show it. The waystones leaning over them. The sky above, still starless, still wrong, three weeks now since the night the dark came down like a lid.

He had sworn, ten years ago, on a guildhouse hearth with Mother Aldsey's hand on his shoulder, that he would never do it where anyone could see. The small magics were outlawed; the deep ones were something worse than outlawed. They were *remembered*. There were men in Saltmere who could describe the last charm-worker who'd used open craft in a public street, and what the wardens had left of him, and how long the story kept children honest.

Aldsey's voice, in memory, dry as chalk: *Visibility is the first expense, Edmund. Everything else is paid after.*

Six bolts. One moment.

He paid.

* * *

What he did had no name in the guild ledger, because the guild had never been permitted to write it down. It was old craft, road-craft, the kind Aldsey said had been worked openly back when the salt road was young and the city was a fishing village that hadn't yet learned to be afraid. He didn't gesture. He didn't speak. He simply reached — the way you reach for a knot you've tied a thousand times — into the space between the wardens and their arrows, and he *untied* it.

The world between intention and action came loose in his hands like wet rope.

The crossbows fired. That part was real, and loud, and the sound went off down the valley and came back twice. But the bolts left the strings and entered the loosened place, and the loosened place didn't know what it was supposed to hold, so it held nothing. Six bolts simply ceased to be arrows — became, briefly, a confusion of wood and feather and intent, drifting like ash, falling like snow.

Every soul on that stretch of road watched them fall.

Then the cost arrived, because it always arrives, and it always knows the way.

It started in his teeth. A coldness, metallic, like biting a coin. Then it dropped through him — through the chest, through the belly, down into the ground itself — and Edmund felt the road feel him back. That was the thing about the deep craft that no warning had ever made bearable: it wasn't a wall you broke, it was a *door* you opened, and doors open both ways. For one terrible instant he stood wide open to the whole grey landscape, and the landscape poured in. Every feral ward in a hundred miles turned its blind attention toward him like a field of flowers finding the sun. The waystones sang one low note. Somewhere far off, under the mountain, something vast and patient made an entry in a book.

He staggered. Harrow caught his arm without looking, the way you catch a man who has done this before and will do it again.

"Underhill," Harrow breathed. "You absolute fool. Do you know what they'll call you now?"

"Alive," Edmund managed. "Call me alive."

* * *

The wardens stood frozen, staring at the ash-fall of their own arrows, and in that frozen moment Sera moved.

She didn't waste it on speeches. She closed the distance to Cade in four strides, and what happened next was fast and ugly and nothing like the duels in ballads — Cade got a forearm up and took her off balance, and she went down hard on the scree and rolled, and he drew his short sword because Cade had always believed in ending things properly, and Edmund, still half-blind with the aftershock of the working, heard himself shouting her name.

But Sera Voss had spent eleven years learning every way a body could stop, and she knew the one Cade never guarded, because it was the one partners never used on each other. As he came down toward her she swept his ankle across the rock — the same trick, Edmund realized later, the same exact trick, that she must have taught him herself, years ago, on a training floor in the warden barracks, laughing about how no one ever sees it coming from family.

Cade went down. Her knife was at his throat before his sword hit stone.

"Tell me to surrender," she said, ragged. "Go on. Tell me how it ends."

"It doesn't end," Cade said. His voice cracked, finally, the door in his face broken off its hinges. "Sera. It doesn't *end*. You think I wanted this? They gave me your warrant and a file of your sins and told me the child goes to the Tallyman or the city burns, and I believed them, because believing them is the only thing I've got left that works." His eyes found hers, and they were wet, and furious about being wet. "You were my partner. You don't get to be right about the mountain. Being right about the mountain means everything we did was—"

"Worse than you think," Sera said. "I've seen the ledgers, Cade. It's worse than either of us thinks."

And that was when the bolt took her.

Not from Cade — from the fifth warden, the young one at the rear who had reloaded while everyone else was busy being human. The shot was wild, panicked, fired at the movement rather than the person, and it caught Sera high in the shoulder as she knelt over Cade, spinning her sideways into the rocks.

Everything after that happened quickly and also, to Edmund, slowly, in the way of things you will remember for the rest of your life.

Harrow stepped forward and did something with his voice alone — some collector's cadence, some official register that lived in his blood — and shouted the wardens into a paralysis of procedure, *names and numbers, whose shot, whose authority*, and it bought thirty seconds because bureaucracy is the deepest magic of all. Quillon was moving, faster than a man his age had any right to, kneeling in the scree with his coat already off and folded into a pad. Wren walked — *walked*, calm as Sunday — out from behind Edmund and stood over Sera, and the light bled up out of her skin until the shadows of the rocks leaned away from her, and the young warden who had fired sat down abruptly on the road and began, very quietly, to cry.

And Cade, with a knife-mark on his neck and his partner bleeding into the stones beside him, looked up at the starless sky and said, in a voice Edmund was not meant to hear:

"Oh. Oh, hell. It's true. It was all *true*."

* * *

They bound the wound with Quillon's coat-pad and a length of luck-knot cord Edmund cut from his own satchel, which was the closest thing to prayer the Underhill family had practiced in four generations. The bolt had gone through the meat below the collarbone — bad, not mortal, the kind of wound that slows a march and haunts a sleep. Sera stayed conscious through all of it, jaw set, watching the wardens.

Because the wardens had not fled. That was the strange thing. Their officer lay disarmed, their comrade wept, their quarry glowed like a lantern with a soul in it, and the five of them stood in a rough half-circle holding empty crossbows, and no one ran, because running would mean going home to explain, and there was no explanation that survived daylight.

"We can't take them with us," Harrow said quietly, at Edmund's shoulder. "We can't leave them to report accurately. And we can't kill six men for doing their jobs badly." He rubbed his face. "This is why I defected. The arithmetic. There's never a column that comes out clean."

"There's one more option," Edmund said. "It's just expensive."

He walked — legs unsteady, the cold of the working still pooled in him like meltwater — to where Cade sat in the scree. He crouched, wincing, so their eyes were level.

"You saw the bolts come apart," Edmund said.

"I saw *something*."

"You saw craft. Open craft, road-craft, the kind that gets a man burned in the market square if the right warden writes it down." He held out his hand, palm up, and let Cade look at it — an ordinary hand, ink-stained, rope-callused, trembling slightly with the aftermath. "There are six witnesses standing behind you. Whatever report reaches Saltmere, you're the one who decides what it says. So here's what I'm asking, and I'm asking because she loved you once and I can see it's mutual and inconvenient."

Cade's jaw worked. "Ask."

"Report a charm-seller. Report a hedge-witch and a stolen lamp and a runaway warden — report the *old* lies, the comfortable ones, the ones the city already believes. Say the child died on the road if you have to. Say anything except the truth about the mountain." Edmund paused. "Or arrest us all, and in twenty years, when the last lamp under the mountain gutters out and the winter doesn't end, explain to whoever's left why you had the truth in your hands and filed it under *orders*."

For a long moment the only sounds were the wind off the ford and the young warden sniffling and Sera breathing in careful, counted breaths.

"The stars," Cade said finally. "Three weeks, no stars. My daughter asked me where they went, and I told her weather, and she *knew* I was lying, and she's seven." He looked at his own hands. "Seven years old, and she already knows what I am."

"What are you?" Edmund asked gently.

Cade didn't answer. But he stood up, and he collected his wardens with a jerk of his head, and he ordered them back down the road toward the Ford, and the report he would write was already visible in the set of his shoulders — a man carrying a lie like a wounded bird, deciding whether to nurse it or crush it.

At the bend of the road he stopped. Didn't turn around.

"The Tallyman keeps a hall of lamps," he said. "My grandmother used to say the mountain ate travelers. We thought it was a story to keep children off the passes." A pause. "If you go down there — and I can see that you're going down there — then whatever you find, come back up and tell it true. Somebody has to start telling things true."

Then the wardens were gone around the black shoulder of rock, and the salt road was quiet again, and Edmund discovered that his knees had opinions about standing.

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a leaning waystone, because Sera couldn't ride and wouldn't say so and Quillon had overruled her with the full authority of a man who had set bones since before she was born. She lay propped against the stone, grey-faced, letting Wren tuck a blanket around her with enormous solemnity, and if her hand shook as it passed over the child's hair, no one was rude enough to notice.

Edmund sat apart, feeding the fire stick by stick, and Harrow came and sat beside him the way men sit beside other men when neither wants to talk and both need company.

"Ten years," Harrow said. "You really went ten years without anyone seeing what you are?"

"Two people saw. Quillon. And—" He stopped. Started again. "And her. Aldsey. She taught me the rule. Visibility is the first expense. You stay small, you stay dull, you stay *unrecorded*, and you live long enough to help people quietly." He watched the flame. "Today I lit myself up like a signal fire in front of six wardens and a feral ward-network, and something under the mountain felt me do it. I felt it *write me down*, Harrow. Somewhere in those ledger-halls there's a fresh entry with my name in it, and the Tallyman reads his mail."

"So the cost," Harrow said. "That's what it was. Becoming visible."

"That's part of it." Edmund pulled his cloak tighter. The cold of the working hadn't fully left; he suspected part of it never would. "But it's not the worst of it. The worst is—" He gestured vaguely at the camp: Sera pale against the stone, Wren curled close to her like a cat, Quillon keeping watch with a crossbow he barely knew how to load, Harrow himself, deserter, brother-thief, friend. "The worst is that I can't unsee them seeing me. Ten years I was safe because I was nobody. Now I'm somebody, and somebodies get hunted, and hunted men make mistakes, and my mistakes aren't mine alone anymore." He laughed, short and bitter. "Aldsey warned me. She always said the day I used the deep craft openly, I'd better be doing it for something worth being caught for."

"And was it?" Harrow asked. "Worth it?"

Edmund looked at Sera, alive because he'd spent his invisibility. At Wren, safe because he'd spent it. At the road ahead, running down and down toward the mountain and its cathedral of lamps, where a soul hung in the dark that the law said belonged to the crown and his heart said belonged to none of them and all of them.

"Yeah," he said. "And the terrifying part is, I'd pay again tomorrow."

Across the fire, Wren stirred in her blankets and looked at him — not at his face, but at his hands, the ones that had untied the world. Her eyes were still faintly bright. Star-bright.

"You rang," she said simply. "When you did the untying. All the way down the mountain, everything that still remembers the old light — it heard you." She pulled the blanket to her chin. "The Tallyman heard you too. He knows somebody's coming who can work like *that*."

The fire popped. Far off, beneath their feet, so deep in the rock that it might have been imagination, something tolled — once, soft, like a bell underwater, counting.

"He's setting another place at the table," Wren said, and closed her eyes.
