## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The salt road ended at a door made of nothing.

That was how Edmund thought of it, afterward — though at the time it was simply a seam in the mountainside, so old and so ordinary that he'd walked past its like a hundred times on the cliffs above Saltmere without ever once wondering what was behind it. Harrow put his palm flat against the rock and said a word that was less a word than a subtraction, and the mountain opened like a ledger being unclasped.

Cold came out. Not winter cold — cellar cold, mine cold, the patient chill of stone that has never once been warm. It smelled of salt and old paper, which was a combination Edmund's body understood before his mind did: his whole life had been spent in rooms that smelled of one or the other, and here they were braided together at last.

"Rules," Harrow said, turning to face them in the grey light spilling from the opening. He'd changed since the inn. Or maybe Edmund was only now seeing him clearly. "The court doesn't patrol. It doesn't need to. Everything down there is counted, and everything counted is known. So we are not five people sneaking. We are four people who belong here, escorting one person who is property."

Wren looked up at him. "I'm the property."

"You're the parcel," Harrow said, and Edmund heard what it cost him to say it. "And parcels get carried where they're meant to go. Nobody questions a delivery." He held out a hand. In it lay a thin brass token, stamped with the Tallyman's own seal — a circle bisected by a line, like an eye half closed. "This is my collector's writ. It says I'm returning an escaped asset for assessment. Sera is my guard. Quillon—" he hesitated over the old man, and Edmund watched him hesitate, and understood that Harrow had already done arithmetic about Quillon that he didn't want to share, "—Quillon is a retired clerk come home to die near the books. They allow that. Some of them even want it."

"And me?" Edmund asked.

Harrow looked at him for a long moment.

"You," he said finally, "are my porter."

"A porter."

"Every collector has one. Someone to carry the lamp, the ink, the ledger-box. Porters are furniture. Furniture is invisible." A ghost of something crossed his face that might have been apology. "It's the best lie I can give you. The truth would be worse."

"What's the truth?"

"The truth is that you look at things," Harrow said. "I've watched you do it since Cordwainer Street. You look at things like you're memorizing them, and down there, everything worth memorizing is worth killing to keep unseen." He pressed the brass token into Wren's small hand instead of his own pocket, which told Edmund something too: Harrow trusted the child more than himself. "Keep your eyes down, charm-seller. Count your steps if you have to count anything. But don't look at the lights."

* * *

They went down.

The mines were older than Saltmere, older than the kingdom, maybe older than the idea of kingdoms — shafts cut in long spirals, each ring of tunnel wider than the last, as if the mountain were a shell and they were descending through the coils of something that had once been alive. Salt glittered in the walls where lamplight caught it, whole seams of it, veins of buried brightness running through the dark like frozen lightning. Sera kept her hand on her sword hilt the entire way and never once drew it, which Edmund recognized from experience as the posture of a woman fighting a battle entirely internal.

Quillon walked beside him and breathed wrong.

Edmund noticed it in the third ring of tunnels. The old man wasn't winded — he was listening. Each breath drawn through the nose, slow, deliberate, the way a man smells a stew he hasn't eaten since childhood. And with every spiral lower, his breathing got quieter and his face got stranger, until somewhere around the fifth ring he stopped walking, put one palm against the salt-crusted wall, and stood there trembling.

"Master?" Edmund kept his voice at porter-pitch, flat and servile, because two of them could play the game better than one.

"Sixty years," Quillon whispered. "Sixty years I told myself it was a warehouse. Paper and coin and strongboxes. That's what they tell the clerks. That's what I signed on believing." His hand slid down the wall, leaving a streak through the salt dust. "But you can smell it, can't you, boy? From this deep. Even without the talent."

Edmund could. He'd been refusing to name it for two rings of tunnel now, the way you refuse to name a sickness until the physician forces you. Under the salt and the paper there was something else in the air, faint as a memory of a smell: woodsmoke. Sea-smoke. Rain on hot cobblestones. His mother's kitchen. Aldsey's workroom, with its kettle and its bundles of dried yarrow hanging from the beams. Every good small thing he had ever known, distilled to almost nothing and hanging in the dark like a scent trapped in amber.

Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All of them breathing out the shape of the lives they'd been taken from, and all of it pooling in this stairwell because there was nowhere else for it to go.

"They can still feel it," Quillon said. "Even after all this time. That's what I couldn't let myself know." He straightened up, wiped his hand on his coat, and became again the retired clerk going home to the books. But his eyes stayed wet, and he didn't wipe those.

* * *

The seventh ring opened all at once, the way a cathedral opens when you've been crawling through its crypts.

Edmund had prepared himself for a vault. He had prepared himself for a prison, a treasury, a mine, even — in his worst imaginings — a slaughterhouse. What he had not prepared himself for was beauty, and beauty was what hit him first, beauty like a blow, and he hated himself for the gasp that escaped before he could stop it.

The cavern was vast beyond measuring, ribbed with columns of white salt that some ancient patience had carved into pillars, arches, flying buttresses of stone that had never known weather. And filling that vastness — hanging in the air, tier upon tier upon rising tier, ascending into darkness the eye couldn't follow —

Lamps.

Not lamps. He knew that even as his mind reached for the word, because lamps don't breathe. But each one hung from a fine chain that rose into the black overhead, and each one glowed, and none of them glowed alike. Some burned steady and gold, the color of hearth-fires. Some flickered blue-white, quick, nervous. One near the floor pulsed a deep, contented red, like an ember that had decided to be comfortable. There were thousands. There were more than thousands. The light they cast together filled the whole cathedral-soft space with a glow like late afternoon, and beneath it, at long desks arranged in concentric rings on the polished floor, sat clerks in grey, writing.

That was the horror. Not the lamps — the desks. The clerks. The soft scratch of ten thousand pens moving in perfect silence, each one recording, each one maintaining, each one doing bookkeeping on the souls of the dead-and-not-dead with the unhurried diligence of men copying out shipping manifests. Nobody screamed down here. Nobody wept. The worst room in the world was quiet as a library, and orderly as a garden, and lit like the last hour of a summer day, and Edmund stood at its threshold with his heart slamming and understood why no one had ever torn this place down.

You couldn't hate it. That was the design. You were meant to stand where he was standing and think: *this is reverent. This is careful. This is not cruelty; this is stewardship.* And then you would believe anything they told you about necessity.

"Don't stop," Harrow murmured, barely moving his lips. "Walk like you've seen it every day of your life."

Edmund walked. Sera fell in a half-step behind Wren, close enough to catch her, far enough to look like guarding rather than mothering. Quillon stared at the floor with the discipline of a man staring at the floor. And Edmund fixed his eyes on the back of Harrow's collar and counted his steps and did not look at the lights.

He lasted ninety steps.

He didn't mean to look. It happened the way it happens to everyone — a flicker at the edge of vision, a color his body knew before he did, green-gold, the exact green-gold of a charm-candle burning yarrow and salt on a winter night. He looked up, and across three tiers of hanging lights, above the heads of the writing clerks, past a dozen souls whose colors he refused to read—

There she was.

He knew her the way you know a voice calling from another room. The lamp hung low enough to see clearly, swinging very slightly on its chain as if stirred by a draft that didn't exist, and its light was not gold or blue or red but that particular green-gold that he had seen every evening of his childhood, that he had mixed himself a thousand times, that no one else in the world had ever quite managed because she'd never written the proportions down. *A trade secret,* she used to say, tapping the side of her nose. *A guild secret. When you've earned it.*

She had earned nothing like this. She had been sold like a bolt of cloth, entered in a ledger, hung on a hook, and her light — her actual light, the warmth she'd poured into forty years of luck-knots and fever-charms and small mercies for people who couldn't pay — it was down here lighting the desk-work of the thing that owned her.

Edmund's foot was moving before his mind caught it. One step off the path, toward the central aisle, his hand rising—

Sera's elbow found his ribs. Hard, precise, professional.

"*Porter,*" she said, loud enough for a passing clerk to hear, with contempt perfectly pitched. "The box. You've dropped the strap."

There was no strap. There was no drop. But his hands were empty and shaking and everyone within thirty feet had glanced over and away again, because porters dropping straps was not interesting, and Sera's glare gave the moment exactly the dullness it needed. Edmund bent, gripped the ledger-box's handle, and stood back up with his eyes wet and his lungs full of that faint green smell of yarrow that wasn't really in the air, that couldn't be, that was coming out of a lamp sixty feet away and forty years ago all at once.

"Steady," Sera breathed, only for him. "We take her out the front door or we take nothing. Break cover now and you hang on a chain by morning."

"I know."

"You nearly—"

"*I know.*"

Something moved behind his breastbone that wasn't quite forgiveness and wasn't quite rage and would need, eventually, to become one or the other. He filed it away with the rest of the debts he was keeping. The list was getting long.

* * *

The Tallyman's antechamber announced itself by sound: a single bell, deep and sweet, tolling once as they approached, though no bell was visible anywhere in the vaulted hall. Harrow stopped at a desk of white salt where a clerk with spectacles and no expression checked their token against a book, ran one finger down a column, and nodded.

"Collector Harrow. Asset return, one. Assessment pending." The clerk's eyes flicked up, took in Wren, and lingered — not with suspicion but with something worse, something like recognition. "Unusual provenance," the clerk said. "The Tallyman will want to see this one personally."

"That's why I brought it personally," Harrow said.

"Mm." The clerk stamped the page. "Wait here."

While they waited, Edmund allowed himself, carefully, sidelong, the thing he'd sworn not to do: counting. Tier by tier, ring by ring, he counted lamps until the numbers got too large and the counting turned into a different kind of arithmetic entirely — years divided by souls, debts divided by decades, the guild's century of quiet survival laid against this forest of stolen fire. Every ward around Saltmere. Every border-stone. Every safe night anyone in the lowlands had slept since his great-grandfather's time. Paid for. Paid for with *this*, and the bills had been sent, quietly, generation after generation, to people like Mother Aldsey, who had died owing money she'd never borrowed to buyers she'd never met.

"Boy." Quillon's voice, very low. "When we get our chance — whatever happens — I need you to know the page I tore out wasn't hers."

Edmund looked at him.

"It was mine," Quillon said. "My debt. My sale. She just — she covered it. That was her mercy. She always paid other people's accounts." His hands were folded like a man at a funeral. "So whatever you decide I'm owed, remember that I'm the one who put her on the hook. I just let her take the weight."

Before Edmund could answer — before he knew whether the thing rising in his throat was a curse or absolution — the bell tolled twice, and every clerk in the great hall set down their pen at the same instant, and every lamp in the cathedral of souls swung gently on its chains, all together, all in the same direction, as if the whole cavern had turned its head.

Toward them.

From between the far pillars, unhurried, came a figure in grey robes carrying no light of its own, because everywhere it walked was already bright enough. And beside Edmund, Wren — who had been silent and small and properly parcel-like for six rings of tunnels — lifted her chin, and her eyes went star-white, and she said in a clear voice that carried to every desk in the hall:

"He remembers me. He just wishes he didn't."

The figure stopped.

And the Tallyman smiled.
