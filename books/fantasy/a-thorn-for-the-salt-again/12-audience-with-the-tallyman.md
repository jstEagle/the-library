## 12. Audience with the Tallyman

The hall of ledgers had no ceiling Edmund could find.

He looked up twice and gave it up. The dark overhead was not darkness exactly — it was *depth*, a black that went on the way a well goes on, and somewhere far up in it hung lamps. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one burned with a light too small to cast shadows, each one swaying on a chain so fine he couldn't see the chain at all, as if they floated of their own accord, patient as moths.

Souls, Sera had said. Hung like lamps.

He made himself stop counting. Counting them was how you went mad down here; Quillon had told him that much before the salt road took him off his feet and left him coughing blood into a scarf two days behind them. *Don't count the lights, boy. A man who counts the lights starts looking for one.*

Edmund had already found his. He'd found it an hour ago, in the antechamber where the party had been made to wait — Aldsey's lamp, third row from the door, burning a soft amber-gold that no other light in the cathedral matched. He knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark. Ten years of her voice lived in that color.

He had nearly broken cover reaching for it. Sera had caught his wrist with a grip like a manacle and held him until his knees stopped wanting to fold, and by then the doors had opened on their own, and there had been nothing to do but walk in.

Now they stood in the nave of the tally-hall, and the Tallyman came down the aisle between the writing-desks to meet them.

Edmund had expected a monster. Everyone who grows up on outlawed stories expects a monster. What came instead was a tall, spare figure in a clerk's grey robe, ink-stained at the cuffs, with a face like a well-kept cemetery — orderly, calm, faintly sad, tended daily by someone who cared about appearances. He walked without hurry. He carried no weapon. In his hands he carried a single ledger, closed, and he held it the way a priest holds scripture or a gambler holds his last coin.

"You are smaller than your reputation," the Tallyman said. His voice was dry paper, unhurried, almost kind. "All of you. I had imagined more."

"You imagined a guild," Sera said flatly. She stood with her ward-warden's stance even here, boots planted, hand nowhere near the sword she wasn't allowed to carry. "We're what's left of one."

"Yes." The Tallyman regarded her without malice. "Warden Voss. You spent nine years keeping my wards fed and never once asked what they ate. That is not a condemnation. It is simply rare, how long a good conscience can go unfed itself." He turned to Wren. Bowed — actually bowed, a small precise inclination of the head. "Little light. You've grown."

"I don't know you," Wren said. Her voice was thin in the vastness, but she didn't step back. Edmund put his hand on her shoulder anyway, because she let him, because it helped *him* more than her.

"No," the Tallyman agreed. "I did the putting away. The ones who do the putting away are rarely remembered fondly." He lifted the ledger slightly. "Master Underhill. Or should I say — the last charm-seller of the Guild of Small Mercies?"

"Edmund's fine."

"Edmund, then. You have come a long way down to commit a theft. I admire that. Most thieves steal from strangers. You have walked four hundred miles to steal from me specifically, knowing I would be waiting." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile, because it was genuinely warm. "Shall we conduct this like accountants, or like animals? I confess a preference for the former. Animals make such a mess of the margins."

* * *

They sat at a desk the size of a dining table. The Tallyman poured tea from a pot that had been waiting, Edmund noticed, already steaming — waiting since before they arrived, perhaps since before they left Saltmere. He drank his. It tasted of nothing, which frightened him more than any flavor could have.

"The situation is simple," the Tallyman said, opening the ledger. "I will not pretend otherwise, because pretending is for men who fear their own arithmetic, and I have done mine. One thousand, one hundred and forty-one souls are held in this mountain. They were acquired lawfully. Every acquisition is documented, consented to, witnessed, and paid for. Your guild sold its debts into my care across eleven decades. Your crown charmed my operation, taxed it, and looked away. The wards above failed because the last payments came due and no one above remembered they were making them." He folded his hands. "The stars went out because the sky was mortgaged, Master Underhill. Not stolen. *Mortgaged.* There is a difference, and the difference has kept me lawful for three hundred years."

"And if I say the difference is horseshit?" Sera said.

"Then I will show you the documents, and you will find them impeccable, and you will hate me for it, which is your privilege." He said it without heat. "But you did not come four hundred miles to call me a liar. You came for one lamp."

"Aldsey," Edmund said. His voice cracked on the name and he hated himself for it. "Mother Aldsey. Sold in the year the guildhouse flooded. Sold to pay—" He stopped.

"To pay the roof, the coal, the fines, and Master Quillon's apothecary bills," the Tallyman finished gently. "Yes. I keep meticulous records." He turned pages. Edmund watched his own guild's history scroll past under that ink-stained finger — entries in a hand he half recognized, entries in hands he didn't, dates and sums and the cold little word *acquired* over and over. "Which brings us to business."

"Name it," Harrow said. He hadn't sat. He stood behind Edmund's chair with his arms crossed and his face doing something complicated that Edmund had learned, on the road, to read as grief being held very still.

"The bargain is old law, older than your guild, older than your crown. Freely given balances freely taken. One soul returned for one soul offered — offered *knowingly*, *willingly*, and in sound mind. No coercion, no trickery, no debt behind it. If such a soul stands before me and gives itself, I release Mother Aldsey's lamp within the hour, unharmed, whole, restored to the world." The Tallyman's eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who has watched centuries of paperwork — settled on Edmund. "That is the offer. It does not expire tonight. It does not expire this year. I am very patient. It is my only vice."

Silence. Somewhere above them, a lamp swung, or seemed to.

"And the catch," Edmund said.

"There is no catch. That is what no one ever believes." The Tallyman spread his hands. "There is only the price, and the price is plain. Whoever gives themselves does not die. They hang. They wait. They burn small and slow and they think, all the while, about what they bought with the trade, until the thinking wears smooth. Some of my lamps have hung three hundred years and are quite serene now. Serenity comes eventually to everyone. It is the one mercy built into the system." He paused. "Though I notice, Master Underhill, that you keep glancing upward. Third row from the door. Amber-gold."

Edmund's blood went cold. "You knew I'd seen it."

"I knew you'd seen it before you saw it. Why do you think it hangs where it hangs?" The Tallyman closed the ledger softly. "I did not move it there. It has hung there for thirty years. But I confess I have wondered, some nights, whether lamps choose their places the way people choose their windows — hoping someone will look up."

* * *

It was Wren who broke the silence that followed, and she broke it by standing on her chair, which nobody had thought to forbid, and looking straight up into the deep.

"They're tired," she said.

The Tallyman went still. It was a small thing, his stillness, but Edmund had spent ten years reading customers who wanted to hide what a price was doing to them, and he saw it land.

"They're not asleep," Wren went on, in that fragmentary way she had, words arriving like weather. "Asleep is different. Asleep lets go. They can't let go. They hold on to — to being somebody — and holding on burns fuel, and the fuel is *them*, and that's why the light gets smaller." She looked down at him, and her eyes were full of starlight and something older than pity. "You shouldn't trade anyone for me. I got out. That was the miracle. Don't spend a person on a miracle that already happened."

"Wren—" Edmund started.

"He'll say yes," she told the Tallyman, matter-of-fact, devastating. "That's what he does. He sells luck-knots to widows for coppers and tells them they're free. He'd hang up there in a minute. So don't let him. Make it cost something real."

The Tallyman studied her for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — not warmer, but *careful*, the way a man walks carefully near ice he knows is thin.

"You remember more than I estimated."

"I remember everything. That's the problem with being a star." She hopped down off the chair and took Edmund's hand. "Come on. We're leaving. Nobody's buying anything today."

"That," said the Tallyman, rising also, "is where you are mistaken, little light. Nothing in my hall leaves unpaid. But—" and here he did something Edmund would turn over for nights afterward, something that did not fit any story he'd been told about the court beneath the mountain — "the accounts may remain open. Debts are my trade, after all. Open debts are how I stay in business." He inclined his head to each of them in turn. "You may leave. All of you. The child included, for now. Consider it... a courtesy extended to negotiating parties."

"Why?" Sera asked bluntly.

"Because," the Tallyman said, "in three hundred years, no one has ever walked out of my audience refusing the bargain and *meaning* to come back. You mean to come back. I can smell intent the way you smell rain. And when you return — and you will return, Master Underhill, because that lamp will not let you sleep — you will bring me either a freely given soul, or something better." The pale eyes gleamed. "Something no one has brought me yet. But I get ahead of my ledger. Go. Sleep. The guest quarters are through the west arch, and yes, the beds are real, and no, they are not traps. Traps are inefficient. Ask anyone."

* * *

The west arch led to cells that had been monks' quarters once, maybe, or miners' bunks — stone, spare, clean, each with a candle stub and a view of nothing. Sera posted herself in the corridor out of pure habit. Harrow vanished without explanation, which was becoming his signature move. Wren fell asleep in thirty seconds, curled against Edmund's side like she'd been doing it all her life, glowing faintly even in dreams, and he sat in the dark of the cell listening to her breathe and hating the mountain and loving her and doing the arithmetic he'd sworn not to do.

One freely given. It could be him. It should be him — he was the one who'd come for Aldsey, he was the guild's last living member in anything but name, he was the one whose whole miserable career was built on paying other people's prices. And yet Wren's small furious voice rang in him: *he'd hang up there in a minute. So don't let him.*

And beneath that, colder, the Tallyman's parting line, coiling: *something no one has brought me yet.*

He must have drifted, because when the whisper came he couldn't swear he'd been awake for all of it.

A scrape at the cell door. Then Harrow's face, grey in the candleless dark, close enough that Edmund could smell road dust and old ink on him.

"Get up," Harrow breathed. "Quietly. Bring nothing you can't run with."

"What—"

"I've been walking these halls since before you were born, Underhill. Collecting. I know every corridor the ledgers don't map." His eyes were terrible and certain. "And I know which door the Tallyman doesn't lock, because the Tallyman doesn't believe anyone would dare. There's a keyring in the tally-vault antechamber. Vault keys. Lamp-vault keys."

Edmund sat up slowly, Wren stirring against his arm. "Harrow. That's theft from *him*. From the court. That's—"

"Yes," Harrow said. "It is." And for the first time since the inn, since the road, since Edmund had known him at all, the debt-collector smiled — a small, crooked, ruinous smile. "My brother's lamp is number six hundred and twelve. I've been telling myself for twenty years that collecting was honest work. Tonight I'm going to collect properly."

Somewhere far above them, deep in the hanging dark, a thousand small lights swayed — and one of them, Edmund would have sworn, swayed toward the door.
