## 13. Harrow Pays His Brother's Debt

The Tallyman's court kept no clocks, but Harrow had learned to read time in the lamps. They dimmed and brightened in slow tides, a hundred souls breathing light in the ledger-halls above, and when the tide ran low — when the whole cathedral of accounts seemed to hold its breath — that was the deep of night, if night still meant anything under the mountain.

He lay on a pallet of salt-pressed wool in the guest alcove the Tallyman had granted them, courtesy of a collector's standing, and listened to Edmund Underhill breathe three alcoves away. Sera Voss slept like a soldier, flat and silent. The child didn't sleep at all; she sat by the door with her knees drawn up, glowing faintly, watching the corridor with the patience of something that had once watched the whole sky.

Harrow watched her back and did his arithmetic.

He had been doing it for two days, ever since they'd come down through the mines and he'd seen the lamps. He was good at arithmetic. It was, in the end, the whole of what he was. The court had made him from numbers: a boy with a head for sums, taken into service, given a ledger and a hook and a title, and told that everything in the world was either owed or owned. He had believed it for thirty years. He had believed it the way you believe the floor.

Then he had read a charm-seller's shabby guild ledger by candlelight and found his brother's name in it — *Tammas Harrow, sold to the court, year of the Long Frost, debt assumed by the crown* — and the floor had opened under him, and he was still falling.

Tammas. Who had been dead to him for forty years, sold the same winter their mother died, and Harrow had grown up telling himself the court had taken him because Tammas was weak, because Tammas had gambled, because Tammas had deserved it. That was the first lesson of collection: the debt is always just. You cannot hook a man's soul out of his chest at dawn unless you believe the ledger is holy.

He sat up. Wren's glow turned toward him, question-shaped.

"I need to see him," Harrow said quietly. "The Tallyman."

"You shouldn't go alone," she said. Her voice was still strange to him, that layered quality, a child's voice with weather behind it. "He'll want something."

"He always wants something. That's all he is. Wanting, with excellent bookkeeping." Harrow stood, straightened his collector's coat out of habit, and made himself stop touching the collar. "Keep the others here. If I'm not back by the low tide—"

"I'll know where you went," Wren said. "Everyone here goes to him eventually. It's the shape of the place." She tilted her head. "You're going to offer yourself. For your brother."

Harrow went still. "How—"

"I was a lamp for a hundred years." She said it the way other children said *I was asleep*. "I heard the bargains. They all sound the same. *Take me instead.*" Her glow dimmed. "It never works. But you should say it anyway. Some things need to be said out loud even when the answer's no. That's what mercy is, mostly. Saying it anyway."

Harrow looked at her for a long moment. Then he bowed — an old habit, a collector's bow, half a degree deeper than required — and went out into the breathing dark of the halls.

* * *

The Tallyman received him in the counting room, a chamber at the mountain's heart where the walls were not stone but stacked paper, centuries of it, compressed into strata like shale. A single lamp hung at the center — not a soul, Harrow realized, but something older, burning with a light that cast no shadows and made the numbers on every page legible at any distance.

The Tallyman sat behind a desk of black salt. He looked, as always, like a patient clerk who had outlived his own kingdom: grey, neat, unremarkable, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had the flat reflective quality of a scale at rest.

"Collector Harrow." He did not look up from the page before him. "You've come at low tide. Good. Accounts are clearest at low tide."

"I want to amend a file."

"Files are not amended. They are appended." The Tallyman turned the page. "But proceed."

Harrow had rehearsed this in the dark for two days, and now that the moment came, all the rehearsal burned away and left only the raw thing underneath. He knelt — fully knelt, collector to king, forehead nearly to the salt floor — and said the words.

"My brother. Tammas Harrow. Lamp nine hundred and six, third vault, western gallery. I've read the original instrument of sale. The debt was my family's, and I was a child when it was made, but I'm not a child now, and the family's not ended. I'm the last of it. The debt is mine by succession." His voice held. He was proud of that, later. "So I'm here to pay it. Take me instead. A soul for a soul, freely given, which you yourself said was the only currency you honor. Take mine, and let him go."

The Tallyman set down his pen.

For a moment the only sound was the lamp at the center of the room, burning its shadowless light, and Harrow's own heart, which suddenly seemed very loud, very mortal, very much the thing he was offering.

"Stand up," the Tallyman said. "Kneeling is for debtors, and you are not one. That is precisely the problem."

Harrow rose.

"You've made a category error," the Tallyman said, and there was almost gentleness in it, which was worse than cruelty. "You think you are offering something. But a collector's soul is not his own to give. It was never his own. Read your own contract sometime — you signed it at fourteen, which the law of this court holds as competent age, more's the pity. Every collector is bonded. Your soul is *collateral*, Harrow. It secures the ledgers you keep. If you default — if you steal, if you defect, if you die outside my service — the court collects you automatically. There is no transaction required. There is no freedom in it to give." He folded his hands. "You cannot pay a debt with money that is already pledged. You of all people should know that. It's the first rule of the first page."

The words went into Harrow like a hook going in. Not because they were cruel. Because they were *correct*. He checked them against the contract in his memory — he had signed at fourteen, he had never read past the fourth page, no one reads past the fourth page — and every word of the Tallyman's ruling was true, and true things were the only things that had ever held weight in his life.

"So my brother stays a lamp," Harrow said.

"Your brother stays an asset. As do you. As does everything, until the accounts close." The Tallyman picked up his pen again. "I am not unkind, Collector. You may visit lamp nine hundred and six. Supervised. Once. Consider it — a courtesy between colleagues."

"I'm not your colleague."

"No," the Tallyman agreed, and for the first time he looked up, and his eyes were the eyes of a man reading a figure that had come out wrong. "You're not. You've been not-my-colleague since you walked in with a charm-seller and a stolen warden. I've been wondering when you'd notice it yourself." He returned to his page. "The visit is at your convenience. Good tide, Collector."

Harrow walked out of the counting room with his spine straight and his hands steady, because a collector's training holds even when everything behind it has collapsed into rubble. He did not let himself shake until he was alone in the corridor, in the dark between lamps, and even then only for the length of one long breath.

Then he did the arithmetic again. The real arithmetic. The kind the court had never taught him, because it was the kind that unmade courts.

*You cannot pay a debt with money that is already pledged.*

True. For payment. But he had spent thirty years learning every other thing a pledged thing could do. Collateral could be *moved*. Collateral could be *misfiled*. Collateral could be — and here his heart began to hammer with something that was not fear, or not only fear — collateral could be *released by the loss of the debt it secured*. And the debts were kept in a vault. And the vault had a key. And the key hung, as it had hung for a century, on the Tallyman's own belt, because the Tallyman trusted no one, which meant the Tallyman had never once had to imagine betrayal from the only people close enough to commit it.

The first rule of the first page. Harrow had never read past the fourth page. But he had *written* the enforcement clauses. He knew, better than any man alive, exactly how the court watched its own — and exactly where it didn't.

* * *

The supervised visit was that evening, if evening was the word.

A silent attendant walked him down the western gallery of the third vault, where the lamps hung in ranks that receded into dark, each one a soft gold point of light with a small brass tag beneath it. Nine hundred and six was in the fourth row, at shoulder height, and Harrow stopped before it and could not move.

The tag read *TAMMAS HARROW — ACQ. LONG FROST — BAL. UNPAID.*

"Talk to it, if you like," the attendant said, bored. "They don't answer. They're not *in* there, exactly. Just the bright part of them. The rest went where it goes."

"He'd be seventy-eight now," Harrow said. "If he'd been allowed to be anything."

The attendant shrugged. Harrow waited until the man drifted back down the gallery, and then he stood close to the lamp, close enough that the light touched his face, and spoke in a voice too low for the dark to carry.

"I was eleven when they took you. I told myself you deserved it for thirty years because the other thing — that they took you because I was good with numbers and you weren't, because the court wanted one son from our street and I was the one it wanted, so it took the other one to make room — that thing would have killed me." The lamp glowed. It glowed the way all lamps glowed. It did not answer. "I found your name in a charm-seller's ledger, of all the filthy little books in the world. A guild of small mercies. They wrote down everyone they couldn't save. Nobody writes us down, Tammas. Nobody's ever written us down."

He reached out and touched the brass tag. Just that. The metal was warm.

"I can't buy you," he said. "I tried. He's right — you can't pay a debt with pledged coin. But I'm the one who wrote the collection clauses, little brother. And I'm going to write us both out of the book."

Behind him, soft as a footfall, the attendant had returned to the end of the gallery and was watching. Harrow noted it, catalogued it, and did not turn around. Let the court see a collector grieving at a lamp. Grief was permitted. Grief was even efficient — it kept collectors compliant, kept them hungry for the courtesy of visits.

What the court had never learned to read was a collector doing sums.

* * *

The key hung at the Tallyman's belt at all hours, and the Tallyman slept — this was the one human fact about him — four hours at low tide, in a chair in the counting room, pen still in hand, because he did not trust the mountain to keep the accounts without him even while he dozed.

Harrow had put him to sleep there a thousand times. That was the horror of it, and the opening: the court's security was the Tallyman's own vigilance, and the Tallyman's vigilance had a seam in it four hours wide, and Harrow had sewn that seam himself, years ago, with a tincture of salt-ash and poppy that was standard issue for collectors who needed witnesses calm. He had dosed kings of the salt roads and matriarchs of the mining families and once, memorably, an entire assize of crown judges. He had never dosed the Tallyman, because the Tallyman was the court, and the court was the floor you stood on.

The floor had already opened once. He was getting used to the fall.

He waited for the low tide. He told the others nothing — not Edmund, who would have argued, not Sera, who would have planned, not the child, who would have *known*, because she always knew. He told them nothing because a theft with accomplices is a conspiracy, and a theft with one man is a mistake, and mistakes are survivable in a way conspiracies are not. If it went wrong, the court would collect one defaulting collector, and the charm-seller's bargain would go on. He had run the numbers. This was the configuration with the most survivors. He was, in the end, still good at arithmetic.

At low tide he walked into the counting room carrying a tray of the Tallyman's evening salt-tea, which he had carried a thousand times, and set it down at the elbow of a king who never looked up from his pages, and the tincture went into the cup the way thirty years of trust goes into a blade: quietly, from behind.

"Your tea, Tallyman."

"Mm." The pen scratched. "The Underhill file. Curious. His guild's ledger shows a payment in the old currency — the pre-charter coinage. Nobody's paid in that for a hundred years. It's as if the debt was never meant to be *collected*, only carried." The Tallyman's eyes flickered, heavy already, the poppy finding the tired places a century of bookkeeping had worn into him. "Carried debts accrue, Harrow. Remember that."

"I remember everything," Harrow said. "You made sure of it."

The Tallyman almost smiled. His pen slowed. His head bowed, not in sleep at first — in thought, in that patient clerk's way, reviewing a column — and then the thought went on without him, and the pen rolled from his ink-stained fingers, and the Tallyman of the court beneath the mountain slept in his chair with the lamp burning shadowless above him and his kingdom of paper breathing quietly around him.

Harrow stood very still and counted to two hundred.

Then he unhooked the key from the sleeping man's belt.

It was smaller than he'd expected — a plain iron thing, unornamented, the kind of key that opens a door whose lock is the ornament. It was warm from the Tallyman's body. It was the warmest thing in the mountain. Harrow held it in his palm and felt thirty years of service run out of him through his skin, and what replaced it was not freedom, exactly. Collateral could not be free. But it could be *spent*. And he knew now what he wanted to buy.

He turned to go, and the Tallyman spoke.

Harrow froze. But the voice was thick, submerged, a man talking from the bottom of the poppy dark, and what he said was not an alarm. It was barely words at all.

"...carried debts accrue," the Tallyman murmured. "Everything accrues. Even mercy. *Especially* mercy. It was the first entry..." His hand twitched toward the desk, toward a page Harrow could not see. "...she forgave me once. Before the lamps. Before the arithmetic. She said the accounts would close..." A breath, long and slow. "...she's down there still. In the first lamp. I never had the heart to..."

The voice trailed into sleep.

Harrow stood in the shadowless light with the key in his fist and his heart going like a hammer on a door, and he looked down at the sleeping king and understood two things at once, the way the best entries balance: that the Tallyman had been a man once, before the court, and that someone had forgiven him, and that he had carried the forgiveness like a carried debt for a hundred years, accruing.

And the second thing: *the first lamp*. The oldest soul in the vault. The one the child would kneel to, though Harrow didn't know that yet, couldn't know it — only that the words landed in him with the weight of a figure that comes out true.

He slipped the key inside his coat, against his chest, where the contract-signature scar sat over his heart like a brand, and he walked out of the counting room past lamp nine hundred and six, and he did not stop, and he did not look back, because looking back is how collectors get collected.

In the guest alcove, Wren was awake, of course. Her glow turned to him, and her eyes went straight to his coat, to the small iron shape of it pressing against his heart, and she smiled — a child's smile, with weather behind it.

"You told them nothing," she said.

"There was nothing to tell."

"There's always something to tell." She stood, and the light in her steadied, sharpened, the way a star steadies before it's needed. "The bells will know by morning. They always know. So whatever you're going to do with that—" her eyes flicked to his chest, "—you should do it fast, and you should do it with all of us. Because I've been in a vault for a hundred years, collector. And I know exactly which lamp you're going to lift first."

Harrow looked at her. Behind her door, Edmund Underhill was stirring, and Sera Voss was already awake — she had been awake since the tea tray left, because soldiers count cups — and somewhere above them the lamps were dimming toward the low tide's turning, and the mountain, patient as paper, was beginning to breathe in.

"Then tomorrow," Harrow said, "we go to the third vault. And we take everyone."

Wren's glow flared, brief and bright, like a star deciding.

"No," she said. "Not everyone. *Hers.* We take hers first. The rest will follow. They always follow the brightest one out."

And far above them, in the western gallery, lamp nine hundred and six burned on in the dark, waiting, its little brass tag catching the light — *BAL. UNPAID* — as if the mountain itself had written the promise and was only waiting to see who would be foolish enough, and loyal enough, and free enough in the only way that mattered, to come and collect.
