## 14. The Vault of Lamps

The key Harrow stole was small and ugly — a sliver of black iron no longer than a finger, stamped with a tally-mark that looked like a scar. It weighed nothing in Edmund's palm and everything in his chest.

"That's it?" Sera whispered. "A century of stolen souls, and the lock is *that*?"

"The lock isn't the iron," Harrow said. He crouched at the mouth of the service gallery, his back against the warm salt wall, and for once he didn't look like a man built out of ledgers. He looked like a man who had done something unforgivable and was glad of it. "The lock is the vault itself. The lamps are warded from the inside. You don't break in. You have to be let out."

"Then we're not stealing," Edmund said. "We're breaking out."

"You're finally understanding," Harrow said. "I told you. Nothing leaves this mountain that the Tallyman hasn't finished counting. So we make sure he never finishes counting us."

Above them, through forty feet of salt and stone, the cathedral of ledger-halls went on with its terrible industry. Edmund could hear it even here — the whisper of quills, the soft chime of beads moved along counting-wires, the endless patient arithmetic of the court beneath the mountain. Somewhere up there hung ten thousand lamps, and in one of them, dim as a coal wrapped in ash, burned everything left of Mother Aldsey.

He had seen her light once, from the visitors' gallery, and it had nearly killed him. He had lunged for the rail and Harrow had caught his collar and Wren had put her small cold hand flat on his wrist, and the starlight in her skin had said, without words: *not yet. Not like this.*

Not yet was over.

* * *

They came to the vault by the collectors' stairs, the ones Harrow had walked for nineteen years, the ones whose every seventh step sang a note only wardens heard. Harrow knew which steps to skip. Sera walked behind him with her hand on her knife and her eyes on the walls, reading the wards the way Edmund read a ledger — fast, hungry, looking for the lie in the numbers.

"It's wrong," she breathed at the third landing. "Do you feel that? The wards down here aren't holding souls *in*. They're holding them *down*. There's a difference. A holding-in ward breaks when you pull. A holding-down ward—"

"Pushes back," Harrow said.

"Heavier than you think," Sera finished. She turned and looked at Edmund, and for the first time since the roof of the guildhouse she smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent eleven years being called a fool for saying the wards of Saltmere were being eaten from inside, and who had been right, and who intended to be right all the way down to the bottom of the world. "Good. I'm tired of gentle work."

Wren had not spoken since they entered the mines. She walked in the middle of the party with her hood back, letting her light show — faint, silver, the glow of a lamp behind paper — because down here, Harrow had explained, starlight was furniture. Starlight was expected. The court had spent a century putting lights like her into boxes; a child-shaped glow on the collectors' stair would draw nothing but boredom.

But now, at the last door, Wren stopped walking.

Edmund felt it too. The air beyond the door was thick, the way air gets before summer lightning. Through the seam under the door came a light that was not silver. It was gold, and amber, and one shade of blue he had only ever seen in Aldsey's charmwork, the blue she used for charms against drowning.

"Ten thousand," Sera murmured. "You can *feel* them."

"They can feel you," Wren said. Her voice was very quiet. "They're waking up. They do that when someone comes close who isn't a collector." She looked up at Edmund, and her eyes were the dark between stars. "They think you're coming to take one of them away."

"I am," Edmund said.

"No." Wren shook her head slowly. "That's what they think. That's why they're waking up. They think someone finally came, and it's going to hurt, and they're going to hope anyway, because that's what souls do." She took his hand. Her fingers were cool and steady. "Don't make them hope unless you mean it."

"I mean it," Edmund said, and found that his voice did not shake, though everything else in him did. "On my name."

And then he remembered he had no name anymore — he had signed it away at the last gate, erased himself from the guild ledger to open their road — and the words landed strangely, like a coin dropped into a well that never rang. But Wren squeezed his hand as if she'd heard it anyway.

*On my name,* he thought. *Whatever's left of it.*

Harrow set the black iron key in the door's keyplate. It did not turn so much as *agree*, sinking in like a knife into tallow, and the door opened inward on a darkness full of light.

* * *

The Vault of Lamps was not a room. It was a wound in the mountain, hollowed and hung, and the lamps were not lamps.

They were jars. Some glass, some clay, some things that might have been bone or coral or the cured husks of sea-things Edmund had no names for. Each one held a light, and each light was shaped — not like flames, but like people. He saw a fisherman bending over nets that weren't there. A woman with her arms around a shape that might have been a child, rocking, rocking. A soldier standing eternally at attention. A scholar hunched over an invisible book. Ten thousand small figures made of light, each doing forever the thing they had loved best or feared most or simply been doing when the court bought them, and none of them knowing anything at all.

Except that they knew someone had come. As Edmund stepped onto the first gantry, the nearest lamps brightened. Then the next. Then the next, a ripple running outward through the dark like wind through wheat, ten thousand lights turning toward him the way flowers turn toward a sun that has not risen in a hundred years.

"Keep moving," Harrow said hoarsely. "Don't look at any one of them too long. They read intent. If you stare at your teacher's jar and your intent is loud enough, the vault will hear it too."

"Then how do we take her?"

"Quietly," Sera said. "Like thieves. Which is what we are tonight, whatever else we are after." She was already working, her hands moving over the nearest ward-anchor — a pillar of salt glass fused with wire and beadwork, the court's answer to the guild knots Edmund had grown up tying. "Ugly work. No grace in it at all. They build locks like men who've never had to pick one." She pulled a bead, twisted a wire, and somewhere far above them something sighed. "First ring's down. They'll notice in—" she cocked her head, listening to the mountain the way Quillon listened to weather, "—four minutes. Maybe five. The Tallyman counts everything, but even he can't count *this* fast."

"Four minutes," Edmund said. "Where's her jar?"

Harrow unrolled a scrap of vellum — the vault manifest, stolen off the Tallyman's own desk, and Edmund did not ask how — and ran his finger down columns of tally-marks. Row upon row upon row. Names reduced to marks. He stopped near the bottom of the third panel.

"Aldsey, M. Acquired winter of the old count. Shelf nine, tier four, alcove of the drowned." His voice went strange on the last word. "They shelve them by trade, sometimes. By what they died of. Sometimes just by weight."

"Show me," Edmund said.

They moved along the gantry, quick and quiet, and Wren walked last with her hands open at her sides, and where she passed, the waking lamps dimmed again — not sleeping, but *hushed*, the way a room hushes when a mother enters. Whatever she was, however much of her the court had managed to bottle, the souls recognized it. Edmund heard it in the silence she left behind her: not peace exactly, but attention. Ten thousand lights waiting to see what the little piece of sky would do.

Shelf nine rose above them like the ribs of a drowned ship. Tier four was at the top of a narrow stair of pegged salt, and Edmund climbed it with his heart going like a hammer on wet wood, and at the top, in an alcove marked with a faded sigil of water and rope—

There she was.

The jar was ordinary. That was the worst of it. After the cathedral halls, after the gold and the horror, Mother Aldsey's soul sat in a jar like pickle brine, like a grocer's ware, a fist-sized globe of thick glass stoppered with wax and wire. And inside it, curled small, was a woman of light with her hands folded in her lap, in the exact posture she used to sit in on the guildhouse steps of an evening, mending net-charms, humming.

She wasn't humming now. But her head lifted as Edmund reached the alcove, and the light of her turned toward him, and though there were no eyes in it that could see, though there was no mind left in her that could know him, some residue, some habit of the soul, some *Aldsey-ness* that a century of arithmetic had failed to tax away — that turned toward him like a plant toward the last window in a dark house.

"Oh," Edmund said. Just that. Just *oh*, the way you say it at a grave, or a birth. "Oh, *Mother*."

"Don't," Sera snapped from below, sharp as a slap. "Don't speak to it. Don't touch the glass yet. Edmund. *Edmund.* Look at me."

He looked. Her face was pale in the rising light of the waking vault.

"The jar is warded to its contents," she said. "It knows what's inside. It knows if the thing inside is wanted." She climbed the stair to stand beside him, close, her voice dropping to almost nothing. "If you touch that jar while your whole soul is screaming *mine, mine, mine*, the vault will hear it, and every bell in this mountain rings at once, and we die here among the shelves. You have to want it quietly. Can you do that? Can you want something more than you've ever wanted anything in your life, *quietly*?"

Edmund looked at Aldsey. At the folded hands. At the small patient light.

"She taught me knotwork," he said softly. "She said a charm is just a want, tied down so it can't run wild. She said the trick isn't wanting less. It's tying the want tight." He rolled up his sleeves. On the inside of his left forearm, the old guild mark still showed, faint as a scar — the knot of small mercies, inked into him at fourteen, outlawed at twenty, hidden for ten years. He had signed away his name in the ledger, but no ledger had ever owned this. "She taught me how."

Sera stared at him a moment. Then she laughed, low and fierce, and stepped back to give him room.

"Well," she said. "Tie it tight, then, charm-seller."

* * *

He worked the way she had taught him, thirty years ago, on Cordwainer Street, with her hand over his hand and the smell of salt and tallow in the room.

Want is the thread, she had said. Fear is the knot that holds it. Tie fear around want, and the want holds. Tie want around fear, and the fear eats everything.

His want was enormous. It filled the alcove, the shelf, the whole humming dark — *give her back, give her back, give her back.* So he tied it. Loop over loop, breath over breath, the way you tie a luck-knot for a widow who has nothing left but luck. He bound the want in the memory of her voice. He bound it in the weight of the ledger page torn out before he was born. He bound it in Wren's cool hand and Harrow's stolen key and Sera's bitter vindication and Quillon's confession, that terrible evening, the old man's face like wet paper: *I sold her, boy. I sold her to keep three of us alive instead of two, and I have counted every day since, and the count has never once come out even.*

He tied the want down until it was silent. Until it was a stone at the bottom of a deep clear pool, invisible, immovable, shaping everything.

Then he reached out and lifted the jar.

Nothing rang. Nothing flared. The wax seal crumbled at his thumb like old bread, and the wire fell away, and the stopper came free with a sound like a sigh, and Mother Aldsey — what was left of Mother Aldsey — poured into his cupped hands, warm as a fresh egg, light as smoke.

For one moment, one impossible moment, the small figure of light stood in his palms and *looked up at him*, and though she had no face he knew, though a century had worn her down to a gesture and a warmth, he felt — he would swear it to his dying day — he felt her recognize the knotwork. Felt her see her own teaching alive in his hands, the want tied tight, the mercy held.

Her light flickered. Once. Twice.

Like a wink.

"Go," Sera breathed. "*Go now—*"

Too late.

Somewhere above them, in the dark of the mountain's throat, a bell woke.

It was not a loud bell. That was the horror of it. It tolled once, soft and clear, a sound like a finger tapped on crystal, and every lamp in the vault answered — ten thousand lights flaring at once, a sun born underground — and then the second bell came, deeper, and the third, and then the mountain itself seemed to take up the tolling, bell answering bell answering bell, down through the salt and up through the halls, until the whole world beneath the world was one vast bronze heartbeat and Edmund was running down the pegged stair with a soul in his hands.

"The manifest!" Harrow shouted over the bells. "The Tallyman reads the manifest — he'll know within minutes which jar is empty — he'll seal the gates—"

"Then we're past caring about quiet," Sera said, and drew her knife, and cut her own palm, and slapped the bleeding hand against the nearest ward-anchor. "Get Wren! Get to the gate! I'll bring this whole rotten shelving down on anyone who follows!"

"Wren—" Edmund turned, and stopped.

The child stood in the middle of the gantry, in the middle of the blazing vault, with her hood thrown back and her light no longer hidden, no longer a lamp behind paper, but *open* — silver pouring off her like water off a weir, and the ten thousand souls of the court beneath the mountain were reaching toward her, straining against their jars, and she raised both her small hands as if lifting something vast.

"Hold them," Edmund said. It wasn't a question.

"They held me," Wren said, without turning her head. Her voice was calm in the way of deep water. "Longer than I can say. Long enough to forget my own burning." The silver light swelled. Every jar it touched went quiet, hushed, *held* — held safe this time, held the way you hold a frightened bird. "Now I hold them. Go get her home, Edmund Underhill. I'll keep the dark off your back."

*Underhill.* His name, spoken aloud in a place that ate names, and it didn't burn, didn't vanish — it landed in him like a keel laid down, something to build on.

He ran.

Behind him the bells went mad, and Sera's knife sang, and Wren blazed like a fallen star refusing to go out, and in his two cupped hands, tucked against his chest like the last coal of a fire he had carried across a country, Mother Aldsey burned on — small, and stubborn, and *waking*.

The bells of the vault followed them all the way to the gates.

And beneath the bells, faint but unmistakable, from everywhere and nowhere in the salt-dark, came a new sound: slow, unhurried footsteps, and the dry scratch of a quill, and a voice like an accountant closing a long day's book:

"*One lamp short.*"
