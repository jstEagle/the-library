## 15. Winter Falls All at Once

The bells stopped.

That was the first wrong thing. For an hour they had rung through the mountain like the mountain's own heartbeat, every corridor and salt gallery answering in kind, and then — between one toll and the next — silence. Not peace. Silence the way a held breath is silent.

Edmund stopped running so fast he nearly lost his footing on the wet steps. Ahead of him Sera Voss turned, lamp raised, her face cut into planes of shadow.

"No," she said. "No, that's worse. That's much worse."

"What's worse?" Wren's hand was small in Edmund's. Her grip had gone tight as a burr since the vault. She glowed faintly even through his cloak-wrapped arm, a light that leaked at the seams of him, and he had not let go of her once since the alarm began and did not intend to now.

"When a warden stops ringing the hour," Sera said, "it's because the hour has stopped mattering." She lifted her chin, listening with her whole body. "When a Tallyman stops ringing an alarm, it's because he's done counting what the alarm was for."

Behind them, deep in the ledger-halls, something vast shifted its weight.

Harrow came up the stairs two at a time, the vault key still on its iron ring in his fist, his breath ragged. He had been a debt-collector for thirty years and Edmund had learned in one night that Harrow ran like a man who had spent those years running toward things rather than away, and it showed — no grace to it, all stubbornness and shoulder.

"Gate's this way," Harrow gasped. "The Salt Gate. It opens onto the road —"

"The road's a hundred miles of open country," Sera said.

"The road's a hundred miles of *away*," Harrow said. "I'll take it."

Then the winter came down.

Edmund felt it before he understood it: a cold that came not from any direction but from *inside*, rising out of the stone itself, out of the mountain's old bones, as if some furnace at the world's root had been banked by a single deliberate hand. The damp on the walls went to frost in the space of a heartbeat. The frost went to ice. His breath tore out of him in a white flag.

Above them — far above, past a hundred feet and a hundred years of mined-out dark — came a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

"Snow," Wren said.

She said it the way other children said *thunder*. She pulled free of Edmund's grip just enough to press both palms against the frozen wall, and her starlight flared, and Edmund saw her face and wished he hadn't. She wasn't afraid. She was grieving.

"It's early," she whispered. "The winter was supposed to wait. He kept it in jars. I remember the jars. Rows and rows of them, cold as coins, and he used to say —" her voice did something terrible then, went old and small at once — "*a winter saved is a winter owed.*"

Sera swore with the fluency of a woman who'd spent fifteen years among wardens and had nothing left to lose by it. "He's releasing it. All of it. Every hoarded year."

"Why?" Edmund asked, though some part of him already knew, the way you know the shape of a knife by the wound.

"Because we're running," Sera said, "and he can't chase five people through his own halls without leaving the vaults unguarded. So he isn't chasing us." She looked up, toward the sound of the sky. "He's closing the door behind us. On the whole mountain. On the whole *road*."

*On the whole world,* Edmund thought, and did not say it, because Aldsey's lamp was swinging against his chest inside its sling of guild-cloth, warm as a sleeping bird, and he could not afford the thought. Not yet. Maybe not ever. One thing carried at a time; that was the first rule of flight and he had learned it young, carrying his master's kit through back streets while wardens whistled at corners.

They ran.

*

The halls of the court beneath the mountain had been built to be walked slowly. That was the horror of them, Edmund realized as they pounded along corridors meant for processions — colonnades of black salt-salt marble, ceilings high enough to hold weather, floors polished so that a century of accountants could see themselves coming and going. Nothing here had ever been designed for haste. Haste was for debtors. The court moved at the speed of interest accruing.

Now the haste was theirs, and the architecture punished it. Stairs that spiraled the wrong way. Corridors that met at angles no mason would choose. And everywhere the lamps — the soul-lamps, thousands of them, hung in their niches like fruit too heavy for the bough — swung in a wind that had no business being underground, throwing light that was not light across walls that drank it.

Wren flinched at each one they passed.

"They know me," she said once, small. "The ones that are awake. They're calling."

"Don't listen," Sera said.

"I'm *listening*," Wren said, with a flash of the steel that had surprised Edmund more than once since the roof, "that's how I keep them safe. If I don't listen, I might leave one behind."

"We are leaving all of them behind," Harrow said, harsher than Edmund had ever heard him, and then, immediately, quieter, ruined: "I know. I know, little star. Keep walking."

Edmund carried the lamp and did not look at the others. Aldsey's light pressed against his sternum with every stride, patient, banked, the way a fire waits under ash. Somewhere behind them the vault stood open and stripped of one lamp, and somewhere ahead of them a gate stood shut, and between those two facts lay everything he had left.

The mountain shuddered. Plaster fell from seams that had held for centuries. A whole section of colonnade groaned like a ship.

"It's sealing," Sera shouted over the noise. "He's collapsing the galleries — cutting off sections! We have to get above the tally-halls, there's an old pilgrim stair, from before the court bought the mountain —"

"How do you know about it?"

"Because I audited the wards on it when I still had a badge!" She was already veering left, and they followed her, because there was no one else to follow and because she ran like a woman who had rehearsed her own betrayal a thousand times in her head and finally gotten to perform it.

The cold deepened. Frost climbed the lamps' niches and the souls within them dimmed — not dying, Edmund told himself, *not dying*, hoarded things didn't die, that was the whole obscene point of hoarding — but dimming, drawing in, like animals before an earthquake. And overhead, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the snow began to land.

They heard it as a whisper first. Then as a hiss. Then as weight — an enormous, patient, accumulating weight, the sound of a sky emptying itself all at once upon the salt road and the withered ford and the city of Saltmere and every soul between here and the sea.

"The long night," Edmund said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"Yeah," Sera said. "The one the histories mention. The one the court's been saving."

"For what?"

"For whoever made them give something back."

*

The pilgrim stair was exactly what Sera promised: old, narrow, human. After the court's cathedral spaces it felt almost indecent — steps worn hollow by bare feet, walls close enough to touch both sides of, carved here and there with marks Edmund knew in his bones. Guild marks. Charm-marks. Little mercies scratched by travelers three hundred years dead, asking safe passage, easy childbirth, a good death far from home.

He touched one as he passed. Habit. Prayer without permission.

Quillon would have known this stair. The thought arrived without warning and hit like a fist. Quillon, who had walked these mountains as a young man, who had sold the guild's heart to keep its body breathing, who was at this moment — wherever he was, however the Tallyman's bargain sat on him — waiting for them or not waiting for them, alive or not alive, and Edmund had left without an answer and would not —

*One thing at a time.*

The stair shook. Dust came down in ropes. Below them, distantly, they heard the tally-halls go: a long exhalation of stone, a bloom of white dust rising up the shaft after them like a tide coming to fetch them home.

"Harrow!" Sera screamed. "Where's Harrow!"

"I'm here." His voice, from below, ragged. "The key — the gate mechanism runs through the tally-halls, I'm checking —"

"You're what?" Edmund turned. The stair below was filling with dust, and Harrow was *descending*, back down into it, the vault key raised like a torch.

"There's a fail-safe," Harrow called up. "Court-built gates seal with the mountain unless they're counter-sealed. Someone has to stand in the tally-hall and hold the counter-mark while the rest go through, or the gate closes on the road side and you're all buried in the pass —"

"Then we all stay," Edmund said. "We find another way."

"There is no other way! There's the gate or there's the mountain's grave!" Harrow's face appeared through the dust, gray with it, eyes bright. "Thirty years I collected for this place. Thirty years I knocked on doors and took what the ledger said was owed, and last night I found my brother's name in your book and understood I'd spent my whole life on the wrong side of a door." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Edmund had ever seen, because it was genuine. "Let me be on the right side of one. Go."

"No," said Wren.

Everyone stopped.

The child stepped down two steps, into the falling dust, until she stood above Harrow, looking down at him with her impossible eyes, and the starlight in her skin steadied itself the way a flame steadies in a draftless room.

"You don't get to trade yourself," she said. "That's *his* trick. Trading. Counting. One thing for another thing." She pointed up the stair, toward the gate, toward the snow. "Nobody gets traded tonight. Nobody pays. That's the whole point of what we stole."

"Wren —" Harrow started.

"I'm a star," she said simply. "Stars don't haggle. Come *up*."

And Harrow — thirty years a collector, a man whose whole soul had been mortgaged to an accountant-king — looked up at a child made of light telling him debts were cancelled, and climbed.

Edmund caught his arm at the top and hauled him over the lip of the landing and held on a moment longer than he needed to. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing in either of their ledgers for moments like that, which was maybe why they mattered.

*

The Salt Gate was a slab of green-black stone forty feet high, older than the court, older probably than the city, set into the mountain's flank where the mines broke surface onto the old salt road. Beyond it, visible through a seam no wider than a coin, was white. Not daylight-white. Snow-white, night-snow, the long night beginning, the hoarded winters of a century falling all at once upon the world.

Sera put her hands against the seam and the wards woke under her palms — court-work, but old court-work, work built by people who had never imagined a disgraced warden with a stolen key.

"Key," she said, and Harrow gave it up, and she fitted it into a slot that shouldn't have existed and did, and the gate groaned awake like something surfacing from a very long sleep.

"It'll take a minute to unseal," she said. "Maybe two. The mechanism's fighting the mountain's collapse — they're both trying to decide if the gate exists anymore."

Above them the ceiling cracked. A fissure opened along the length of the gate-chamber, and through it poured not dust but cold — true cold, outside cold, the first taste of the night they were running into.

"The road will be gone behind us in minutes," Sera said, working the key, sweat freezing on her brow. "Once we're through, the pass fills, and there's no coming back for anyone. No rescue. No second trip. Whatever we carry out is everything that leaves this mountain forever."

Edmund's hand found the lamp at his chest.

Aldsey. Mother Aldsey, who had taught him knots and kindnesses and which lies were merciful; whose soul had been sold before he ever knew her name was for sale; whom he had seen hanging in the vault-halls like a lantern in a shop window, and had reached for, and been dragged back from, and had stolen anyway, at last, at cost.

Everything that leaves this mountain forever.

"Good," he said.

Sera looked at him. Something passed between them — warden and outlaw, hunter and hunted, two people who had both spent years being the wrong thing and were, at last, in this collapsing chamber, briefly and improbably the right ones.

"Two minutes," she said, and went back to the key.

*

They came through the gate as the mountain sealed.

Edmund went last, because someone always goes last, and because the lamp was his to carry and he would not hand it forward into a crowd. Behind him the gate-chamber folded — he heard it, a sound like a book closing, like an account settled — and the green-black slab swung to meet the cliff face, and the counter-marks died, and the Salt Gate became, in the space of one heartbeat, simply stone again. A wall. A fact.

Before him lay the salt road, and the night, and the snow.

It was falling the way nothing should fall: all at once, everywhere, thick as wool, silent as judgment. It had already buried the lower switchbacks. It rose past his knees within twenty paces of the gate. There was no wind, which was the worst part — snow should have wind, snow should *howl*, and this fell straight down with the patience of something that had waited a hundred years in jars and intended to enjoy itself now that it was out.

Sera stood hip-deep and stared back at the mountain. The peak was already disappearing, swallowed from the top down, the long night eating the horizon course by course.

"No way back," she said. She didn't sound broken. She sounded like a woman reading her own sentence aloud and finding it fair. "You understand that. The inn, the ford, the city — days apart, if the road even exists under this. We walk into that and we don't stop, because stopping is dying, and there is no one coming after us."

Edmund looked at his companions. Harrow, dust-gray, key-less now, free in the way of men with nothing left to guard. Sera, bleeding quietly from somewhere under her coat and pretending otherwise. Wren, glowing like a coal in all that white, the only warmth in a mile of cold.

And the lamp against his chest, warm as a sleeping bird, holding the only piece of his life that had ever truly been his.

"No one coming after us," he agreed. He turned his face into the snow and found, absurdly, that he was almost smiling. "Then it's just us. The way it was always going to be. Five people and a guild that doesn't exist anymore."

"Six," said Wren.

He looked down. The child was gazing back at the vanished mountain, and her light was steady, and her voice, when it came, was the oldest thing he had ever heard come out of anyone.

"Six," she said again. "There are six of us walking out. You're just carrying one."

Edmund's hand closed over the lamp.

Somewhere beneath a hundred feet of new-fallen winter, the mountain finished closing its mouth. Above them, the long night went on falling, and would fall, he understood suddenly, for years — but the road was under their feet, and the road ran north, and north was the sea, and the sea remembered being sky.

"Walk," Edmund Underhill said, having no name left to say it with, and needing none. "While the snow's still deciding what it's allowed to bury."

They walked.
