## 17. Aldsey Wakes

The snow did not fall. It arrived.

One moment Edmund was running through the last stretch of mine tunnel with a lamp held against his chest like a second heart, and the next the mountain's mouth spat them out into a whiteness so total it seemed the world had been erased and redrawn in a single color. The cold hit him like a wall of hands. It pressed him back a step, and Sera's shoulder caught him, and then they were all stumbling together down the slope of the salt road — Harrow hauling Wren under one arm, the stolen vault key still clenched in his fist, Sera bleeding through a bandage she'd tied one-handed without slowing, Edmund clutching the lamp.

Behind them, the mountain was closing.

He could hear it. Not an explosion, not a roar — a sound like a ledger being shut, page by page by page, the gates of the deep court sealing themselves against the theft of one small light. The tunnels they'd climbed through were gone. The mine shafts were filling. Somewhere below, in a cathedral of hanging lamps, the Tallyman was counting what remained to him and finding it one soul short, and the mountain itself was doing his arithmetic.

"Keep moving," Sera said. Her voice was rough as rope. "If it buries the road, it buries us with it."

"The road's already gone." Harrow turned, and Edmund turned with him, and they both looked back at the way they'd come.

There was no way they'd come. There was a slope of white rising behind them, smooth and unmarked, as if five people had never walked out of the earth at all. The salt road — the old road, the one that had carried salt and silver and, for a century, souls in sealed carts — had vanished under snow that fell without wind, without clouds, without any sky to fall from. Edmund looked up and saw nothing. Not clouds. Not stars. The stars were still in the lamps. The stars were still in the vault, and in the court, and in the Tallyman's careful hands, and the night above them was a ceiling of black cloth.

Wren lifted her head from Harrow's shoulder.

"She's crying," Harrow said, and there was wonder in it, and fear. "Why is she crying? We got her out. We got *out*."

"She's not crying for us." Edmund's arms were shaking. The lamp was heavier than it had any right to be — a glass vessel no bigger than a loaf, filled with a light that was not fire and had never been fire, a light that pulsed slow and patient, like something breathing in its sleep. "She's crying because she remembers what the sky's supposed to look like."

They made it as far as a shrine.

It was nothing — a waystone and a leaning post where travelers had once tied ribbons for luck, back when luck could be sold honestly on Cordwainer Street. The snow had spared it, or the snow had chosen to spare it; Edmund no longer trusted the difference. Sera sat down against the stone with a sound she didn't let become a groan, and Harrow set Wren on her feet, and Wren stood in the white with her face turned up toward the starless dark and her small hands opening and closing, opening and closing, like a child learning to catch something that wasn't there.

"Here," Edmund said. "Here. Now."

His voice cracked on the second word. He knelt in the snow and set the lamp down between his knees, and the others gathered, and for a moment none of them breathed.

"How does it open?" Sera asked.

"It doesn't. It *unseals*." Edmund turned the lamp in his hands. The glass was warm. It had been warm since he lifted it from its hook in the vault, warm in a way that had nothing to do with temperature, warm the way a hand is warm. "There's a seal on the neck. Guild work — old guild work. The court didn't make these vessels. They *bought* them." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "They bought them from us. From the Guild of Small Mercies, three hundred years ago, when we still thought we were selling lamps."

"You're stalling," Harrow said gently.

"I'm memorizing." Edmund looked up at him. "I'm memorizing what her light looks like. In case this doesn't work. In case I open it and there's nothing inside but a hundred years of someone else's keeping, and whatever was Mother Aldsey is — is *accounted for* somewhere I can't follow."

Wren crouched beside him. Up close, in the lamp's glow, her eyes were not eyes. They were apertures. Something vast looked out of them with a child's patience, and when she spoke, her voice was the voice she'd had since she fell out of the dark onto his roof — fragments, starlight, a grammar of elsewhere.

"You knew her," Wren said.

"I was her apprentice. Twelve years old, selling luck-knots on the corner where the tanners' runoff met the street. She taught me every charm I know and half the ones I don't." His thumb found the seal on the lamp's neck. "She used to say a mercy isn't a mercy unless it costs the giver something. I thought that was a lesson. I think now it might have been a warning."

"She'd have hated the lamp," Sera said.

"She'd have hated the *whole ledger*." Edmund's jaw tightened. "She'd have burned the court down with her bare hands and apologized to the flames for the smoke. And instead she's been hanging in their vault for sixty years, and the man who put her there—" He stopped. Quillon's face rose in his memory: the old man's hands shaking over the torn ledger page, the confession that had come out of him like a tooth coming out of a jaw, slow and rotten and necessary. *I sold her to keep the rest of us alive. I told myself it was one soul against thirty. I have told myself that every night for sixty years.* "Quillon's dying," Edmund said instead. "He stayed behind in Saltmere because he can't make the walk, and he's dying, and the last thing he asked me — the *only* thing he asked me — was to tell her he's sorry. Not to ask her forgiveness. Just to say it."

"Then open it," Wren said, "and say it."

Edmund broke the seal.

It did not shatter. It *unmade* itself, the way frost unmakes on a warm pane — the guild-mark worked into the wax lifting away thread by thread, the old signs of the Guild of Small Mercies dissolving into nothing, into the snow, into the air. And the light inside the lamp stood up.

That was the only word for it. The light had hung like a held breath for a century, and now it stood, and it stepped, and it came out of the glass the way a woman comes out of a house she has been locked in too long — blinking, unsteady, furious at the door and the lock and the years. The snow around the lamp melted in a ring three feet wide. The dark above them flinched.

And then the light folded itself down, and down, and down, into the shape of a person, and Mother Aldsey stood in the snow at the foot of a luck-shrine with her bare feet in the meltwater, and she was *small*.

That was the first thing. Not her face, not her voice — her size. Edmund remembered her as a mountain. He remembered being twelve and looking up at her the way you look up at a cliff face, her gray braid like a rope thrown down a wall, her hands like things that could mend a broken charm or break a broken man. The woman standing in the snow now was bent, and thin, and her hair was white without a single thread of the gray he remembered, and her hands — her hands were just hands. Old ones. Human ones. Starless.

The light had been taken out of her, and what was left was only a woman, and she was *shivering*.

"Aldsey," Edmund said.

She looked at him.

For a long moment there was nothing in her face at all — no recognition, no confusion, just the vast blank of someone surfacing from water too deep to dream in. The snow fell on her shoulders and did not melt. Whatever warmth had been hers was in the lamp, and the lamp was empty, and she stood in the cold like anyone. Like anyone at all.

Then her eyes narrowed. Not at his face. At his hands.

"You're holding your charms wrong," she said. Her voice was rust and river-stones, thinner than he remembered, but *hers*, and Edmund's whole body shook with the sound of it. "Thumb on the knot. You always put your thumb on the knot. I told you a thousand times, boy, the thumb is where the luck leaks."

And Edmund Underhill, last charm-seller of the outlawed Guild of Small Mercies, who had walked through a feral ward-line and a collapsing mountain and the vault of the dead without weeping, sat down in the snow and wept like the twelve-year-old she still thought he was.

* * *

They got her to the shrine. It took all of them — Harrow on one side, Sera on the other, Wren walking ahead with her hands out as if she were parting something invisible, and maybe she was, because the snow seemed to fall more gently where the child passed. Aldsey sat against the waystone and let them fuss, and Edmund saw that she was letting them, that she was cataloguing them the way she had once catalogued everything — the disgraced warden with the blood on her sleeve, the debt-collector with the stolen key, the child who was not a child. He saw her decide, one by one, that they were worth her attention.

"Edmund Underhill," he managed, when he could speak. "You knew me as — I mean, I'm — my name is—"

"I know your name." She said it the way she'd once corrected his knot-work: not unkindly, but as a fact, settled. "Edmund Underhill. You were a skinny thing with ink on his fingers and a habit of giving charms away to people who couldn't pay. I told you the guild couldn't eat generosity." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly a wound. "I see you didn't listen."

"The guild's dead," Edmund said. "The charter's struck. The ledger's closed. Quillon—"

He couldn't finish. She watched him not finish, and sixty years of hanging in the dark passed behind her eyes, and then she reached out and took his wrist — her grip still strong, her hand still cold as the snow — and said, "Tell me."

So he told her. He told her about the torn ledger page and the debts paid in a currency no one recognized. He told her about the night the stars went out and the child who fell onto his roof. He told her about Sera Voss, who had spent her career enforcing the wards and chose, in one night, to burn that career to the ground rather than hand back a glowing girl; about Harrow, who had come to collect a debt and instead read his own brother's name among the sold. He told her about the court beneath the mountain, the cathedral of lamps, the Tallyman with his patient voice and his fair, fair offers. He told her about the vault, and the key, and the gate.

He did not tell her about the price. Not yet. He got as far as *the gate sealed behind us* and stopped, and she looked at him with those human, starless eyes, and she said, "And?"

"And we walked out," Edmund said.

"Edmund." She had never needed more than his name in that tone. It had made him confess to broken windows at twelve. It made him confess now. "The vault key worked for a guild member in good standing. Quillon told me that much before I was taken — the old law, the founding law. The lamps answer to the guild, or they answer to nothing." Her grip tightened on his wrist. "Who signed?"

The snow fell. Somewhere behind them, far below the white, the mountain finished its counting and was silent.

"Me," Edmund said. "I signed. I signed my name in the ledger to open the gate, and signing erased me. I'm not in the guild anymore, Aldsey. There *is* no guild anymore. I signed it out of existence so its last mercy could walk out of that mountain free." He heard his own voice go ragged. "You spent twelve years making me a charm-seller. I spent one minute unmaking it. And I'd do it again. I need you to know that. I'd sign it again a hundred times, and I don't know who I am without the ledger, and I don't care, because you're *here*, you're *out*, you're—"

"Hush." She pulled him forward by the wrist, and he went, and she held him the way she had held him once when he was small and the city had taken his father's boat and his mother's luck in the same winter — one hand on the back of his head, his cheek against her shoulder, her chin resting on his hair. She smelled of nothing. That was the worst of it. She had always smelled of cedar and salt and the inside of the guildhouse, and now she smelled of nothing at all, of a century of glass and dark.

"I remember you," she said into his hair. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? A hundred years in that vault, and the lamps don't dream, they only *keep* — but I kept you. You were the thing I held onto. Every soul in that cathedral was holding onto something, boy. That's how we stayed ourselves. The Tallyman thinks a soul is a sum. He never learned that a soul is what it refuses to let go of." She pushed him back to arm's length and looked at him, and her eyes were wet, and human, and enough. "You gave up your name to carry mine out of the dark. So we'll make you a new one. That's guild work, that is. First thing they teach and last thing they learn: a name is just a knot. You can tie another."

"There's nothing left to tie it to," Edmund said. "The guild is—"

"The guild," said Mother Aldsey, "is whoever is sitting around this shrine in the snow. Count them. Five. That's a quorum." She looked at each of them in turn, and Edmund saw the old authority come back into her like blood into a numb limb — diminished, yes, starless, yes, human and mortal and cold, but *hers*. "A disgraced warden who chose right. A debt-collector who chose better. A child who fell out of the sky and landed, of all the roofs in all the world, on ours. And a boy who still holds his charms wrong." Her voice caught, just once. "The Guild of Small Mercies is not dead. It's *unregistered*. There's a difference, and it's the most important difference there is."

Wren had been standing at the edge of the shrine-light, hands still opening and closing on the absent sky. Now she came closer, and Aldsey looked up at her, and something passed between them that Edmund could not follow — two kinds of light, one stolen and one returned, recognizing each other.

"You were in the vault," Wren said.

"I was in the vault."

"I remember the lamps. I remember all of them. I remember *you*." Wren knelt in the snow, and took Aldsey's cold hand in both of her small ones, and her aperture-eyes were wet. "You used to talk in your light. When the Tallyman wasn't counting. You used to say a name over and over, very quiet, like a charm. I thought it was a spell."

Aldsey went very still. "What name?"

"Edmund," Wren said. "You said *Edmund*. For sixty years. I kept it for you. I kept it the whole way down."

The snow fell on them, gentle as a held breath, and Mother Aldsey bowed her head over the child's hands and wept without any sound at all.

* * *

Later — how much later none of them could say; the snow kept its own time — they built a fire of shrine-ribbons and driftwood the storm had carried impossibly far inland, and Sera slept, and Harrow sat turning the vault key over and over as if it might confess to him, and Wren slept with her head in Aldsey's lap, and Aldsey watched the fire with eyes that would never hold starlight again and seemed, somehow, to need none.

Edmund sat beside her. He had a charm half-tied in his fingers — habit, pure habit, a luck-knot for no one, worked from a thread off his own cuff — and she watched his hands without comment, and he realized she was waiting for him to speak, the way she had always waited, patient as tide.

"Quillon's dying," he said at last. "He stayed in Saltmere. His heart, his lungs — sixty years of guilt does what it does. He asked me to tell you he's sorry. He made me promise not to ask you for forgiveness. He said he hadn't earned the asking."

Aldsey was quiet for a long time. The fire cracked. The snow fell.

"He sold me," she said. "I know. The lamps remember what was done to them, Edmund. I have known, in the way you know a thing in the dark, since the night they sealed me in. He signed the paper himself. I felt his name go onto it like a stone dropped into me." She looked into the fire. "And I have had sixty years to hate him, and what I feel instead is so tired and so sad and so *like him* that I think it must be forgiveness, or the shape forgiveness leaves when it's too worn to wear." She turned to Edmund. "Tell him I said the debt's paid. He'll know what I mean. He'll argue. Let him argue — arguing was the only prayer he ever had."

"He'll want to see you."

"He'll see me." She said it simply, and Edmund understood that she meant *alive*, and that she meant it as a mercy, and that it might be the largest mercy anyone had ever offered the old man, and that it would probably kill Quillon faster than his heart would have. Some mercies were like that. She'd taught him that too.

The fire burned low. And then Wren, asleep in Aldsey's lap, sat up all at once — not waking, not sleeping, somewhere between, her aperture-eyes wide and reflecting a light that was not the fire's.

"What is it?" Sera was on her feet with a knife she'd slept holding. Harrow's key stopped turning.

Wren tilted her head, listening to something none of them could hear, and her voice came out in her fragment-grammar, but clear, and certain, and cold:

"He's coming up. The one who counts. He's coming to ask for it back." She looked at Edmund, and then at Aldsey, and then at the starless sky. "And he's bringing the winter with him. All of it. Every year he ever saved."

Above them, at the edge of the world, the dark began, very faintly, to glow — a line of pale light rising in the east like the wrong kind of dawn, the kind that comes from below the horizon, the kind that is walking toward you.
