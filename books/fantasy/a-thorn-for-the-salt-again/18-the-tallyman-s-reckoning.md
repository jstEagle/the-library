## 18. The Tallyman's Reckoning

The snow stopped falling.

That was the first wrong thing. Not a slowing — a stopping, mid-air, every flake between the gate and the buried road hanging like a held breath. Edmund stood with Aldsey's lamp cradled against his chest and watched the white world go still around them, and understood that the mountain had not finished with them after all.

"He's coming," Wren said.

She was standing at the front of their ragged line, small and barefoot in the drifts, her glow guttered down to almost nothing since the vault. She looked like any child half-frozen on a winter road. She did not look cold.

"How do you know?" Sera asked. Her arm was bound against her ribs where Cade's blade had found her, and her voice had the flat calm of a woman saving everything she had left for one more fight.

"I remember the sound he makes." Wren tilted her head, listening to something beneath the silence. "Before I was put away. Before the lamps. He came to my house too, once. He knocked very politely."

Edmund's arms tightened around the lamp. Inside the glass, the light that had been Mother Aldsey for forty years turned slowly, like something dreaming. They had carried it up through the collapsing halls while the mountain sealed itself stone by stone behind them, and they had won out under the last gate by the width of Edmund's erased name, and he had thought — fool, fool — that the worst part was over.

The snowfield ahead of them dimpled. Then it sank. Then it opened, without violence, the way a ledger opens when a careful hand lays it flat, and the Tallyman rose out of the white like a figure rising out of paper.

He was not what Edmund had expected. In the cathedral of ledger-halls, seen at a distance across the hanging lamps, he had seemed vast. Here, standing in the snow no taller than Quillon, he seemed almost gentle: a lean man in a grey coat, ink-stained fingers, a face like a page someone had written on so many times the words had worn smooth. His eyes were the only terrible thing about him. They were patient the way winter is patient.

"Underhill," he said. His voice was quiet enough that Edmund had to strain for it, and yet it reached all of them at once, the way a bell reaches everyone in a town regardless of which way they're facing. "You have taken property of the crown. One lamp, four hundred and eleven years old this spring. And you have damaged a key." A pause, mild as an accountant noting an error. "And a great deal else."

"The guild's debts are paid," Edmund said. His voice cracked on *paid*, and he hated himself for it. "You wrote it yourself. My name cleared the ledger."

"Your name cleared *your* entry." The Tallyman folded his hands. "The lamp remains mine. Souls are not given back, Underhill. They are only moved from one column to another. You have simply made yourself into a column I must now audit."

Harrow stepped forward. Edmund had never seen the debt-collector look like this — he had seen him afraid in the vault, guilty at the inn, resolute on the salt road, but never this: a man standing in front of his own grave and squaring his shoulders to fit it better. "Take me instead. You said it yourself in the halls. A collector's soul is already collateral. It's practically yours. Sign it over, close the account, let the woman go."

"No," Edmund said.

"It's arithmetic, charm-seller—"

"It's *not*—" Edmund's voice tore loose before he could stop it. "It's not arithmetic. That's the whole point. That's what none of you ever—" He stopped. Breathed. The lamp was warm against his chest, warmer than it should be, warm as a hand pressed flat between shoulder blades. "No. We're done trading people."

Sera had gone very still beside him. "Tallyman," she said, in her warden's voice, the one that had once commanded the border wards. "I'm invoking review. The wards were fed unlawfully. The purchases were never consented to by the souls themselves. By the old statutes—"

"The old statutes were written by me," the Tallyman said, not unkindly. "Warden Voss. You audited those wards for eleven years and signed your name to their health twice a year. You are not separate from the ledger. None of you are. That is rather the tragedy of ledgers." He turned his smooth face toward Edmund, and Edmund felt the weight of those patient eyes like a hand on his sternum. "Give me the lamp. I will not even take payment for the key. Consider it mercy, if you like. I am told I am capable of it, though I confess I've never had occasion to test."

And Edmund — God help him — Edmund almost did it. Because the Tallyman's voice was reasonable, and reasonableness is the most dangerous sound in the world when you are tired and bleeding and holding something precious, and because some exhausted clerk-part of Edmund's soul whispered: *he's right, it's just moving things between columns, and columns can be fought over later, alive, another day.*

Then the lamp grew warmer.

Not brighter. Warmer. And through the glass, faint as breath on a window, Edmund saw the light inside gather itself — press itself against the inner curve, the way a cat presses against a door it knows opens outward.

Aldsey was pushing.

"She's in there listening," Edmund said. His eyes stung. "Forty years in your dark and she's *listening* to us negotiate over her body like it's a fishmonger's dispute." He lifted his head. "No. Whatever you do to us, we don't give her back. She spent a century keeping your borders lit and nobody asked her either. So no."

The Tallyman sighed. It was a small sound, and somehow it was worse than thunder. "Then I will take it," he said, "and I will take the child as well, who has been owed to me longest of all, and I will take the collector who stole my key, and the warden who broke her oath, and the old man dying in the inn if he's still alive, and I will write it all down neatly, and in a hundred years no one will remember there was ever anything here but entries." He unfolded his hands. "I am sorry. Truly. Fairness is not the same as kindness, and I have only ever been required to be fair."

He took one step forward, and the snow began to fall again — but upward now, drawn off the ground in slow spirals, and Edmund felt the pull of it in his own chest, felt his namelessness straining toward the Tallyman like iron filings toward a magnet, because even an erased name is still a thing, and things can be collected—

"*Stop.*"

Wren's voice was not loud. But the snow stopped again, harder this time, and the Tallyman stopped too, one foot raised, like a man who has heard his own name spoken in a room he thought empty.

The child walked past Edmund. Past Harrow, past Sera with her bound arm and her drawn knife. Her glow, guttered to nothing since the vault, kindled again — not the wild starlight of the night she fell onto Cordwainer Street, but something steadier, older, a banked coal of it, the light of a thing that had burned before there were lamps to hold fire.

"Wren," Edmund started, and then couldn't finish, because he didn't know what he'd say. *Come back*? She had never been going anywhere. She was walking toward the one thing in the world she remembered from before.

The Tallyman looked down at her.

And Edmund watched something happen to that smooth written-over face that he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe. It was not fear. It was recognition — the specific, unbearable recognition of a man opening a ledger to its first page and finding his own handwriting there, younger, different, belonging to someone he no longer was.

"You," the Tallyman said.

"You know me," Wren said.

"I—" The word failed him. The most patient creature under the mountain, the accountant-king whose voice never strained and never hurried, could not get a second word out. His ink-stained hands hung at his sides, and they were trembling.

"I was the first," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She said it the way she said everything, in fragments, but these fragments fit together like she'd been carrying them a long time, waiting for the shelf. "Before the court. Before the lamps. When you were just a man with a debt no one else would collect. You came to my house." She smiled, small and crooked, and Edmund's heart broke clean in two, because it was such a human smile, such a *child's* smile. "You knocked very politely. I remember. I remember everything. That's what being a star is. You don't forget. You just get put away."

"I was collecting for the crown," the Tallyman said. His voice had gone thin. "There was a famine. The wards were failing. Someone had to—"

"Yes," Wren said gently. "Someone always had to. That's what you told yourself. I heard you tell yourself. I was bright enough then that I could hear everything." She took another step closer, and the Tallyman — the Tallyman *stepped back*. Edmund would remember that until he died: the accountant-king of the mountain court, retreating from a barefoot child through his own summoned snow. "And then you kept having to. And then it was easier. And then it was just what you did. And then you forgot you'd ever been a man who knocked politely on doors."

"I did not forget," the Tallyman said, and for the first time his voice had edges on it, grief ground sharp. "I *filed* it. There is a difference. There has to be a difference, or—"

"Or what?" Wren asked. "Or you'd have to stop?"

The silence went on so long that Edmund could hear the mountain groaning somewhere below them, settling into its long burial. Snow drifted down, downward now, ordinary snow, ordinary night. Somewhere above the clouds, though no one could see them, the stars were still gone.

"I cannot undo it," the Tallyman said at last, and it sounded like a confession pried out of a locked drawer. "Child. Bright one. First one. I cannot un-take what was taken. The ledger does not work backward. If it worked backward, nothing would mean anything. Debt is the only law that held when everything else fell. I built a century on it."

"I'm not asking you to undo it," Wren said.

The Tallyman went still.

"I'm asking you to be forgiven anyway."

Edmund heard Harrow's breath catch. Beside him, Sera lowered her knife — not sheathed it, just lowered it, the way a soldier lowers a weapon when the battle turns into something weapons are no use for.

"That is not how accounts work," the Tallyman whispered.

"No," Wren agreed. "This isn't an account."

She reached up. She was so small, and he was not a large man, but he seemed to Edmund suddenly enormous, towering, a whole architecture of patience and paper and a hundred years of polite knocking — and she reached up and took his ink-stained hand in both of hers, the way a child takes a parent's hand crossing a street.

"I forgive you," she said. "For me. For knocking politely. For putting me away in the dark and calling it keeping. I forgive you." Her glow brightened with each word, and Edmund realized with a lurch that forgiveness, in her, was not a feeling. It was a *force*. It was the oldest kind of light there was. "And because I forgive you, you can't keep me anymore. And because you can't keep me, you can't keep anyone. That's how it works. You knew that. You've known it for four hundred years. That's why you never came to see me. You were afraid I'd say exactly this."

The Tallyman made a sound. It was not words. It was the sound a building makes when it decides, after centuries, to sit down.

He knelt.

The accountant-king of the mountain court knelt in the snow before a child, and his coat spread out around him grey as ash, and his head bowed, and Edmund saw — clearly, unmistakably — tears cutting tracks through whatever a face like that used instead of skin. "I only wanted," the Tallyman said, and his voice was a young man's voice now, a collector's voice, a man-with-a-debt-no-one-else-would-collect voice, "for something to *hold*. Everything lets go. Everything dies or drifts or forgets. Debts stay. Debts are the only staying there is."

"I know," Wren said. She put her small glowing hand on his bowed head, the way you'd bless someone, the way you'd absolve them, the way you'd pat a dog that finally came home. "But holding isn't the same as keeping. You can hold someone and let them go. That's what love is. I think you knew that too. I think that's why you cried the night you took me. I heard that as well."

Above them, impossibly, a single point of light showed through the cloud.

Then another.

Edmund didn't understand it at first — the stars weren't due back until Wren gave the hoarded light to the sky, and she hadn't done it yet, the lamps were still in their arms, the vault-light still unspent. Then he looked at the Tallyman kneeling in the snow, and at the tears freezing on his smooth face, and understood.

The Tallyman was letting go.

Whatever he had held of his own — whatever fragment of the first soul he'd ever taken he'd kept closest, the way a miser keeps one coin from his first purse — he was releasing it. Giving it back. Un-writing the first entry in the ledger with his own hand, and the sky was taking it, and the sky was saying *thank you* in the only language it had.

"There," Wren said softly. "See? Nothing ends. It just changes columns."

* * *

Afterward — Edmund could never say how long afterward; time had gone strange and soft as new snow — the Tallyman rose. He was smaller than he had been. Or perhaps he had always been this size, and the rest had been the weight of the ledger wearing him like a coat.

"The vault is yours," he said. His voice was steady again, but it was a different steadiness, the steadiness of a man who has set down something heavy and doesn't yet know what to do with his arms. "All of it. The lamps, the records, the keys. I'll sign nothing, because I no longer sign. I'll claim nothing, because—" he glanced at Wren, and something almost like humor crossed the worn-smooth face, "—apparently I no longer can."

"And the court?" Sera asked. "The city above? The wards?"

"The wards are dead and should stay dead," the Tallyman said. "They were built on theft. Let Saltmere learn to stand in the dark like everyone else. As for the court—" He looked back at the mountain, at the sealed gates, the buried halls, the cathedral of hanging lights now dark and ownerless. "Someone will have to carry the lamps home. All of them. To wherever home is, now, for each of them. It will take years." A pause. "I find that I would like to begin immediately."

"You?" Harrow said. Suspicion was stitched so deep into the debt-collector that it would probably survive his own death. "You want to *help*?"

"I am extremely good at keeping track of things," the Tallyman said. "Where each soul came from. Who it belonged to. What it loved. It's all in my books. Every name, every purchase, every polite knock." He flexed his ink-stained fingers, and Edmund saw that the ink was fading, the stains lifting out of the skin like frost off glass. "Let me spend it on returns, for once. Instead of collections."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Wren took his hand again — casually this time, the way children do, as if it were already settled — and the Tallyman looked down at their joined hands with the expression of a man reading a language he'd forgotten he spoke.

Edmund shifted the lamp in his arms. The light inside was turning faster now, pressing at the glass, insistent, and warmth was spreading through his ribs and down his arms, and he thought: *soon. Soon, Aldsey. Just get us clear of the mountain. Just get us somewhere the snow is only snow.*

They turned toward the pass — toward the long walk back down the salt road, through the feral wards, past the inn at the Withered Ford where escaped souls waited in the walls to hear whether they were free. Behind them the mountain sat silent under its burial, and above them the stars were coming back one at a time, shyly, like names being read aloud after a long silence.

Wren walked beside the Tallyman, chattering at him in fragments, and he listened the way no one had ever listened to him before: completely.

Edmund watched them go and felt the lamp pulse against his chest, once, twice — impatient, hopeful, alive.

"Hold on," he murmured to it. "Almost home."

The light flared — and through the glass, for half a heartbeat, Edmund saw a shape. A hand, pressed flat against the inside, fingers spread, the way you press your palm to a window when someone you love is leaving on a train.

Then the snow closed over the pass behind them, and the mountain let them go.
