## 19. Stars Return

The snow stopped falling.

Not slowly — not the way snow ends, thinning and drifting and forgetting itself. It stopped all at once, mid-flake, as if the whole sky had been caught holding its breath. Edmund stood in the white blindness with Aldsey's lamp cradled against his chest and watched a single snowflake hang in the air an arm's length from his face, perfect and six-pointed and utterly still.

"Wren," he said. His voice came out cracked. "Wren, what did you do?"

She was standing at the edge of the buried gate, small against all that whiteness, her head tipped back. The starlight that lived under her skin had come up through her like water finding its level — he could see it now plainly, veins of silver at her throat, at her wrists, pooling in the hollows of her eyes until she looked less like a child and more like a window someone had cut into the night.

"I'm letting go," she said.

Behind him, Sera made a sound that might have been a prayer or a curse. Harrow said nothing at all. He had gone very still, the way he went still before collecting a debt, except there was nothing left to collect here, and Edmund understood after a moment that the stillness was awe.

"Letting go of what?" Edmund asked, though he already knew. He had known since the vault, since the lamps, since he'd lifted Aldsey's light into his arms and felt it lean toward Wren like a child toward its mother.

"The sky." She turned to look at him, and her smile was the smile of someone much older wearing a face much younger. "It's been dark so long. It forgot what it was for."

* * *

They had come out of the mountain into a world erased. The salt road was gone beneath ten feet of new snow. The cliffs on either side had softened into suggestion. Even the mountain itself — the court, the tally-halls, the vault where a thousand lamps still hung waiting — had been folded away behind them, sealed by the Tallyman's winter, buried so deep that Edmund could almost believe they'd dreamed it.

Almost. Except for the lamp in his arms. Except for the way it pulsed now, faint and warm, like a heart remembering how.

Aldsey's soul. Mother Aldsey, who had taught him his first knot-charm with a fisherman's twine and infinite patience, who had sold luck to people who couldn't afford hope, who had been taken apart and hung in the dark for sixty years because Quillon couldn't bear to let the guild die.

He had carried her out. He had signed himself out of existence to do it. And now she sat propped against a boulder with Sera's cloak around her shoulders, breathing, blinking, human — but dim. Starless. The lamp had given back only what a body could hold, and a body, it turned out, held very little.

"Edmund," she had said, when they opened it. Just his name. Not his guild name — he didn't have one anymore; the ledger had eaten it — just *Edmund*, plain as bread, and he had wept like a boy while the snow came down around them.

Now she watched Wren with the expression of a teacher seeing a student surpass every lesson.

"What is she doing?" Sera asked quietly. The ward-warden's arm was bound where Cade's blade had found her on the road, and she stood with her weight off it, but her eyes were sharp as ever. "I've read every text on souls ever written. There's no rite for this. There's no *word* for this."

"She isn't doing a rite," Aldsey said. Her voice was rough from disuse, sixty years of silence worn into it. "She's doing what she was before anyone taught her otherwise. Look at her hands."

Edmund looked. Wren had raised both hands to the hanging snow, palms open, and the flakes nearest her were beginning to glow. Not reflecting light — making it. Each one kindled from inside, a tiny cold fire, and then another, and another, a field of sparks suspended in the stillness.

"They're not snowflakes," Harrow said. His voice was strange. "Or they are. But they're also—" He stopped, and Edmund watched the debt-collector struggle with something that had no column in any ledger he'd ever kept. "They're also stars. Small ones. Seeds of them."

"Every flake is a little bit of sky," Wren said simply. "Sky falls all the time. People just forget to look down."

And then she closed her fists, and opened them, and let go.

* * *

The light went up.

There was no other word for it. It rose out of the snowfield like breath rising out of a sleeper, out of every flake at once, ten thousand points of silver streaming upward past their faces — Edmund felt one pass through his chest and it was warm, impossibly warm, warm as a hand on his shoulder — and up, and up, into the black.

For a moment nothing. The dark above swallowed them all.

Then, directly overhead: a single point of light.

It hung there, trembling, as if unsure. As if knocking on a door it hadn't opened in a hundred years.

And the sky answered.

The second star kindled a hand's width away. Then a third, further east. Then a scatter of them, then a spill, then a flood — stars returning not all at once but one by one, each with its own small birth, each flaring awake like a lamp relit by a careful hand, and Edmund heard Sera Voss — who had spent thirty years keeping wards and reading omens and never once, he would have bet anything, cried at anything — make a sound low in her throat and press her good hand over her mouth.

"It's coming back," she whispered. "The old sky. The *real* sky. The one the charts were drawn from."

"Not the same," Aldsey murmured. "Nothing comes back the same. But close enough to steer by."

Wren stood beneath it all with her arms spread wide, and the light poured through her and out of her and she laughed — a child's laugh, bright and unburdened, the first true laugh Edmund had heard from her since the night she fell onto his roof — and the stars came faster, answering her, filling the heavens the way water fills a dry channel, and somewhere far below them, deep in the sealed dark of the mountain, Edmund knew there must be empty hooks in a vault full of lamps, a thousand little absences, each one a soul going home.

Except the ones that weren't.

* * *

He noticed them first as movement at the edge of vision — figures standing in the snow some distance down the slope, where the road had been. A dozen of them. Then more. They hadn't been there a moment ago. They hadn't walked there. They had simply arrived, the way the inn's hidden souls had moved through walls, except these had come further, and they stood blinking in the returned starlight like people stepping out of a house after a long illness.

"The ones from the inn," Sera said. "And others. Souls that escaped the vault over the years. They followed us."

Harrow had gone forward without seeming to decide to. Edmund watched him walk among them — the collector moving through a crowd of debtors who owed him nothing anymore — and saw him stop before a woman with grey in her hair and a miner's burn-scar on one cheek.

"You," Harrow said. "You were in the third gallery. You went missing forty years ago. There's a page in the ledger with your name on it and a note in my father's hand: *unrecoverable.*"

"I was recoverable," the woman said. Her voice was rusty. "Just not worth the trouble."

Harrow flinched as if struck. Edmund had seen the man take a crossbow bolt on the road without changing expression, and this undid him more thoroughly than any wound.

"They can stay?" Edmund asked. He came up beside Harrow, and the escaped souls turned to look at him — at the man carrying the empty lamp, at the guild mark burned from his skin but somehow still legible in the set of his shoulders. "Out here. In bodies. In the world."

"They're already staying," Aldsey said. She had risen, leaning on Sera's arm, and she surveyed the crowd with the frank, unsentimental kindness Edmund remembered from every hard day of his apprenticeship. "Look at them. They've chosen. That's the whole of it. A soul in a body that chose it needs no ward, no warrant, no law. The Tallyman's claim ran on consent, and theirs ended the moment they gave themselves to the ground instead of the ledger."

One by one, as if her words had given them permission, the escaped souls began to move — not away, not scattering, but *inward*, toward each other, forming something that wasn't quite a crowd and wasn't quite a congregation. A village, maybe. Or the beginning of one. A woman took a child's hand — a child Edmund realized with a start had also stepped out of nowhere, also glowed faintly, also remembered being bright. Families that had been separated by a century of accounting, finding each other again in the snow.

An old man approached Edmund. His hands were scarred the way quarrymen's hands are scarred, and his eyes were wet.

"You're the charm-seller," he said. "From Cordwainer Street. I used to buy from your guild, before. Before the ban. A knot against drowning, for my son. It held. Forty years on the water and it held." He gripped Edmund's forearm, hard. "I never knew what you paid for that. None of us did. I'm sorry."

"We didn't know either," Edmund said. And found, saying it aloud, that the grief and the gratitude could share the same sentence without breaking it.

* * *

They built fires as the night deepened — real fires, ordinary ones, fed from the wreck of a salt-wagon the snow had spared — and the settlement of the returned arranged itself around the flames with the instinctive competence of people who had once lived lives. Someone produced a kettle. Someone else knew where a spring ran unfrozen beneath the snow. Sera, who had spent her career dismantling unlawful gatherings, sat in the middle of one and taught three children a warding song, and did not report a single one of them.

Edmund found Wren sitting apart, on a rise, looking up.

He sat beside her. For a while neither spoke. Above them the sky was nearly whole — not the sky of the old charts, not entirely, but a sky dense with stars, constellations bent into new shapes, familiar figures wearing unfamiliar clothes. Somewhere up there, he thought, were the lights that had hung in the vault. Somewhere up there was whatever portion of Wren she had released, and whatever portion she had kept.

"Does it hurt?" he asked. "Letting go?"

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything.

"It hurts like taking off boots after walking all day," she said. "It hurts because it fits better after."

"And what's left? You gave the sky back its light. What's left in you?"

Wren held up her hand. In her palm, small and steady, burned a single point of silver — one star's worth, kept back, tucked away like a coin sewn into a hem.

"One," she said. "Everyone should get to keep one thing. That's what you taught me. On Cordwainer Street. The last charm on Cordwainer Street — you told the widow it wasn't magic that mattered, it was the keeping."

Edmund's throat tightened. "I don't remember saying that."

"That's all right." She closed her fist over the little light, and it shone between her fingers. "I remember enough for both of us."

Down the slope, Aldsey was laughing at something Harrow had said — actually laughing, the sound rusty and real — and Sera was arguing ward-theory with a soul who had helped build the mountain's defenses a century ago and had opinions, strong ones, about everything Sera had ever been taught. The fires crackled. The stars wheeled overhead, new-made and ancient at once.

Restoration, Edmund thought. But not forgetting. The mountain was still there under the snow. The ledger was still written. Every name in it had walked out tonight in a body, and every body carried its years in the dark like a stone in the shoe — and would carry them always. That was the price, and the proof, and nobody here wanted it otherwise.

"Wren," he said. "When the sun comes up. When we go home. What happens to you?"

She looked at him, and in the firelight and starlight together her face was simply a child's face, tired and hopeful and lit from within by exactly one star's worth of kept light.

"I'm coming with you," she said. "Obviously. Someone has to teach you what the knots are really for."

He laughed — and then stopped, because down the slope, at the edge of the firelight, the snow had begun to move.

Not falling. *Parting.*

Something was walking up through it, unhurried, patient as interest accruing, and the fires bowed toward it without being touched, and every soul in the camp went silent, and Edmund rose to his feet with his empty hands open, because there was only one creature in the world that walked like that.

The Tallyman came up the slope into the starlight, brushing snow from his coat, and stopped before the boy-girl-thing who had unmade his kingdom with a single act of forgiveness.

"I have one more entry," he said, "and no ledger to put it in."
