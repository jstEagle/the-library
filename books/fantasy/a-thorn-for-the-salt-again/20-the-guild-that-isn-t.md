## 20. The Guild That Isn't

The clerk's pen scratched like a beetle in a dry wall.

"Charter of the Guild of Small Mercies," he read aloud, in the flat voice of a man reading something already dead, "struck from the rolls of Saltmere by order of the Lord Provost. Entered into record this day, the ninth of Thawmonth." He blotted the line, turned the ledger around so Edmund could see it, and tapped the entry with one ink-stained finger. "You'll want to witness it. It doesn't take effect otherwise."

Edmund looked at the page for a long moment. One hundred and forty years of luck-knots, fever-charms, grief-tokens, small kindnesses sold at honest prices to people who couldn't afford large ones — reduced to a single line of clerical shorthand and a blot of ink shaped vaguely like a fish.

He picked up the proffered quill.

"You don't have to look so solemn," the clerk said. "Half the guilds in this city have been struck since the wards fell. The Chandlers' Guild. The Worshipful Company of Bell-Hangers. The city can't protect what it can't feed."

"That's not why I'm solemn."

"No?"

"I'm trying to decide if this is a funeral or an acquittal."

The clerk considered that with the patience of a man paid by the hour. "In my experience it's usually both. Sign there."

Edmund signed. The nib caught on the paper — old paper, cheap paper, the kind the city bought in bulk for its erasures — and left a small ragged hook at the end of his name, like a thorn.

"There," the clerk said, satisfied. "The Guild of Small Mercies no longer exists. Congratulations."

* * *

Quillon laughed until he had to sit down.

It was the first time Edmund had heard him laugh since before the mountain, and it came out rusty, with a cough attached, but it was unmistakably laughter. The old man sat on the guildhouse steps — the guildhouse steps that were now just somebody's steps, legally speaking, belonging to nobody and therefore, in the way of such things, to everybody — and wheezed at the sky.

"Struck from the rolls," he managed. "One hundred and forty years, struck in an afternoon. By a boy with a bad pen."

"You could show some respect. That boy with the bad pen unmade you."

"He did me a kindness." Quillon wiped his eyes. "Do you know what we were, Edmund, these last ten years? Outlaws pretending to be a guild. Meeting in secret. Hiding our marks. Whispering prices. A guild is a body, lad — a body with a charter and a roll and a right to stand in the light. We'd been a ghost haunting our own house for a decade. Now the city has done us the courtesy of burying the corpse properly." He patted the step beside him. "What's left is just us. Which is all it ever should have been."

Edmund sat. The street smelled of thaw and woodsmoke and, faintly, of salt — the sea had come back to the air after the long winter, as though it had only been waiting politely for permission.

Inside the guildhouse, through the open door, Sera Voss was sweeping.

She swept the way she'd once patrolled the border wards: methodically, relentlessly, treating dust as a personal enemy. She had been doing it for six days. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had asked her anything, really, since they'd come down out of the snow; she had simply arrived at the guildhouse the morning after they returned, found a broom, and begun imposing order on a building that no longer had any legal reason to be orderly.

"The hearth-charm needs re-laying," she called out, without turning. "It's been patched wrong twice. And your stock cupboard smells like a cat died in it."

"A charm-seller's stock cupboard is supposed to smell interesting," Edmund called back.

"It smells like *failure*."

Harrow's voice drifted down from upstairs, where he had claimed the old records room. "Leave him alone. He's grieving. Men are never more useless than when they've just lost their own funeral."

* * *

They fell into it without deciding to.

That was the thing Edmund would remember afterward — how little ceremony there was. No vote, no oath, no re-founding. The charter was struck on the ninth of Thawmonth, and on the tenth of Thawmonth a fisherman's widow came up Cordwainer Street looking for someone who could ease her son's nightmares, because the wardens' licensed physickers charged three silvers and she had four coppers and a smoked herring, and she knocked on the guildhouse door out of pure habit, because it had always been there.

Sera answered it. Sera, who had spent twenty years hunting charm-sellers, stood in the doorway of an outlawed guildhouse holding a broom, and looked at the widow for a long moment.

"What do you need?" she said.

And that was it. That was the whole founding.

By the end of the month they had a rhythm none of them named. Harrow kept the books — of course Harrow kept the books; he had been a debt-collector for thirty years and the man could no sooner stop accounting than stop breathing — but the books were different now. No debts carried forward. No collateral. If a customer couldn't pay, the entry read *given*, and nothing else ever followed it. He wrote *given* with the same grim precision he'd once used to write *seized*, and if his hand shook slightly on those entries, no one mentioned it.

Wren sat in the window.

That was her post, self-appointed. She sat in the front window of the guildhouse with her feet tucked under her, watching Cordwainer Street, and people came to her the way sailors once came to lighthouses. She was nine years old and had been a star for longer than the city had existed, and she remembered being bright, and there was something in her face that made the grieving stop halfway up the steps and simply stand there, breathing.

She couldn't fix anyone. She said so herself, in her fragmentary way — *"I'm not a lamp anymore. Only a wick."* But she could sit with them, and things quieted near her the way water quiets near a keelstone, and half the healing they did that spring was just Wren being present while Sera made tea and Harrow pretended not to listen.

Aldsey taught.

Of course Aldsey taught. She came back diminished — starless, human, sixty years owed to a court beneath a mountain and returned at last with interest unpaid — but she came back *herself*, sharp and warm and utterly without reverence, and within a week she had commandeered the back room and begun taking students. There weren't many. Charm-work was still outlawed; the guild's craft had never been pardoned, only its practitioners forgotten. But there were three of them at first, then five — a dockhand with steady hands and a talent for fever-knots, a girl who could make a lamp burn on almost nothing, a widow who wanted to learn the grief-tokens so she could give them away properly.

"Nameless and unregistered," Aldsey told them, on their first evening. "That's not a shame anymore. That's a curriculum. Everything the guild ever built its pride on — the roll, the seal, the standing — is gone, and good riddance. What's left is the work. Learn the work."

Edmund stood in the doorway and listened to his teacher teach and thought about a torn ledger page and a soul hanging in a vault like a lamp, and had to go outside and look at the ordinary stars for a while.

They were still there. All of them. Wren had given the hoarded light back weeks ago and the sky had taken it greedily, star by star over the buried mountain, and now the constellations hung over Saltmere exactly as they had before — except that Edmund knew, now, which of them had names, and which of them had been *people*, and he could no longer look up without saying hello.

* * *

The wardens came in the second week of Thawmonth.

Edmund saw them from the roof, where he still went sometimes — the roof where Wren had fallen out of a darkened sky into his life, the tiles still bearing the faint scorch of starlight. Two of them, walking up Cordwainer Street with the unhurried gait of men who expected doors to open. City gray cloaks, warden staves, the new provost's badge.

His hands went cold. Ten years of habit does not strike from the rolls as easily as a guild does.

But they stopped at the door, and one of them knocked, and Sera opened it, and Edmund heard — leaning over the roof edge, heart hammering — the younger of the two say, in a voice carefully stripped of expression:

"Warden-Commander Voss. Ma'am. The Provost's office sends its compliments."

"My what?"

"Compliments, ma'am." A pause. "Regarding the... establishment."

"This isn't an establishment. This is a private residence."

"Yes, ma'am." Another pause, longer. Edmund could picture the young warden staring at his boots, rehearsing lines that had clearly been written by someone senior and cowardly. "The Provost wishes it known that the city takes no official notice of any activity at this address. No raids are planned. No inspections are scheduled. Should complaints arrive, they will be filed and misplaced in the usual manner." He swallowed. "There's a widow on Tanner's Row whose daughter had the croup all winter. The physickers wouldn't come. Someone here did, apparently, for nothing." The young man's voice went very slightly off-script, very slightly human. "My mother was sick like that once. Before. When the guild was still—" He stopped himself. Straightened. "The Provost sends his compliments, ma'am. That's all."

Sera stood in the doorway for a long moment. Edmund, flat against the roof, held his breath.

"Tell the Provost," she said at last, "that no establishment exists at this address, and that whatever happens here is none of the city's business, and that if he ever sends boys to say so again I will have him audited."

"Ma'am."

They left. Sera watched them go, then looked straight up — she always knew where Edmund was, it was unnerving — and said, "Your funeral has excellent attendance."

* * *

On the last night of Thawmonth, they closed the shutters early and ate together in the old common room, the five of them and Aldsey, at the table where the guild had eaten for a hundred and forty years.

It was not a celebration. Nobody proposed a toast. But Quillon brought up a bottle of plum wine he'd been hiding since before the outlawry — "for the day the guild ended," he said, "though I confess I imagined a different ending, with more fire and fewer clerks" — and they drank it out of mismatched cups, and the talk wandered the way family talk wanders.

Harrow told a story about his brother, who had woken in the inn at the Withered Ford with forty years missing and a temper intact, and who now repaired nets in the harbor and refused, absolutely refused, to speak of the mountain except to call it "the office." Sera described, deadpan, the growing rumor mill of Saltmere — how the city had decided that the charms appearing on Cordwainer Street were made by no one, sold by no one, originating nowhere, and how the wardens had opened three separate inquiries into a ghost. Wren listened with her chin on her fists and contributed, apropos of nothing, *"The harbor smells like the sky used to,"* and everyone agreed that this was either nonsense or poetry, possibly both.

Then Quillon set down his cup and looked at Edmund across the table, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet around old men about to be serious.

"Do you understand what you did?" the old master asked. "Not at the gate. Not the name. I mean the whole shape of it. A century ago this guild began selling its mercies under a charter, and the charter became the point, and the point became the ledger, and the ledger became the kind of thing that could be balanced with souls. Every institution rots the same direction, lad — toward its own records." He tapped the table. "You didn't save the guild. You let it die cleanly, in front of witnesses, and kept the mercy. Do you know how rare that is? Most things can't tell the difference between themselves and their paperwork. They die together rather than lose the file."

"I mostly just missed her," Edmund said.

"Yes," Quillon said. "Well. That too."

Aldsey reached over and put her hand briefly over his — his hand, the hand that had lifted her lamp out of the vault, the hand that had signed itself out of existence to carry it through the gate — and said nothing, which from Aldsey was a sermon.

Later, when the others had gone up to bed, Edmund sat alone in the common room with the last of the plum wine and looked at the wall above the hearth, where for a hundred and forty years the guild charter had hung. There was a pale rectangle there now, cleaner than the surrounding plaster, the ghost of a frame. The charter itself was ash; he had burned it himself, quietly, the night they got home, and felt nothing break and everything settle.

The guild was dead. Long live whatever this was.

He took out the old ledger — the true one, the original, rescued from the mountain and carried home wrapped in oilcloth — and set it on the table, and looked at it for a while without opening it. He wasn't sure why he didn't open it. Some instinct, maybe. Some sense that the book was not finished saying what it had to say.

Outside, Cordwainer Street lay quiet under the returning stars. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere the sea turned over in its sleep.

Edmund put his hand flat on the ledger's cover, said goodnight to no one in particular, and went to bed.

In the morning, the final page would be waiting for him — rewritten overnight in handwriting he no longer owned, in a name he no longer had — but that was a discovery for daylight, and the night, for once, asked nothing of anyone at all.

* * *

Spring came late that year and came anyway.

On the first warm morning, Edmund hung out the sign. Not the old one — the old sign had borne the guild mark, the knot-and-candle, and was contraband now along with everything else. He'd made a new one himself, plain pine, no sigil, just words burned into the grain in his own careful hand:

*MERCIES. SMALL ONES. INQUIRE WITHIN.*

Sera looked at it, snorted, and helped him hang it straight.

"Subtle," she said.

"We're a dead guild. Dead things shouldn't advertise loudly."

Below them on Cordwainer Street, Wren was teaching the dockhand's apprentice to skip stones, and losing gracefully on purpose, and the stones skipped anyway, further than physics strictly allowed, glittering faintly as they went. Aldsey's voice floated out the open back-room window, correcting a student's knotwork with affectionate brutality. From upstairs, Harrow's pen scratched — *given, given, given* — keeping the only accounts that mattered.

Quillon dozed in the sun on the steps, the charter's absence behind him and the whole unlawful morning ahead.

Edmund stood under the crooked new sign and thought about thorns and salt and seas, and about how the best things he'd ever owned were the things no record could prove he had.

"Inquire within," Sera read again. "People will think you're a brothel."

"People will think whatever they like. We're not registered to correct them." He squinted down the street, where the first customers of the unofficial resurrection were already climbing the hill — the net-mender's boy, the chandler's widow, a stranger with a limp and a look of someone carrying a story too heavy to hold alone. "Come on, Commander. Mercy doesn't keep office hours."

They went in together, out of the light, into the work.
