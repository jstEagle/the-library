## 1. Charms for the Godless

The lamps of the Gullet Market were lit with tallow and prayer, in that order, because Saltmere had learned the hard way which of the two it could trust.

Orsen Halloran hung the last of them at dusk—a stubby thing that guttered even before the wind found it—and watched its light spill across their stall: three planks of driftwood on two barrels, draped in indigo cloth gone grey from salt. On it they arranged the night's wares with the care of a surgeon and the conscience of a fraud. Knot-charms woven from fishing line, each one blessed by nothing but patience. Dried gull-feathers tied with red thread to keep nightmares off. Little clay beads fired in a kiln like any other kiln, stamped with spirals that meant *protection* in a language nobody living could read, because meaning sold better than honesty.

None of it was magic. That was the whole point.

"Charms," Orsen called anyway, soft-voiced, because the market had rules about volume the way it had rules about everything—keep it small, keep it dim, keep it moving. "Charms for the godless. Guaranteed to do exactly nothing."

A dockhand laughed as he passed, coins already out. "Then what am I paying for?"

"The feeling of paying." Orsen pressed a knot-charm into his palm and took his copper. "Cheaper than most feelings in this city."

He grinned and moved on, into the smoke and the smell of fried eel and tar, past the fortune-teller who read tea leaves because reading anything else was a hanging offense, past the Inquisitor's notice-board where the paper notices fluttered like trapped moths: *MAGIC IS FORBIDDEN. THE OLD GODS ARE DEAD. REPORT WHISPERS, SIGNS, AND ANSWERED PRAYERS TO YOUR LOCAL WARDEN.*

*Answered prayers.* Ten years ago, that phrase would have been a joke. Prayers went up and nothing came down; Saltmere had made a civic religion of the silence. The Inquisitors had burned the temples, drowned the god-touched in the harbor pool, sealed every gate and well and standing stone until the city hummed with absence. Orsen knew better than anyone how thorough the sealing had been. They'd helped.

They kept that knowledge the way they kept everything else—buried deep, under the floorboards of a cellar no customer ever saw, beneath a shop that smelled of beeswax and dried kelp, in a body that answered to a name that wasn't quite true.

Because here was the thing about Orsen Halloran, charm-seller, night-market fixture, unremarkable in all ways the law cared about: half a mile under their feet, older than the city's foundations, there was a door. And doors, however well sealed, hummed. And people who grew up with a door humming under their bones learned to sleep through it, the way sailors sleep through weather.

Tonight, for the first time in ten years, the pitch of the hum had changed.

Orsen noticed it between customers, the way you notice a clock stopping. Not louder—*looser*. Like a knot giving its first sliver of slack. They set down the clay beads they were stringing and pressed their bare palm flat against the driftwood counter, letting themselves feel it properly for one dangerous heartbeat.

The gate beneath the shop breathed out.

Not much. Not enough for anyone else to feel, or so they told themselves. Just a long, slow exhale of something patient and vast turning over in its sleep, the way the sea turns over before a storm.

Orsen snatched their hand back and smiled at the next customer like a person who had never felt anything at all.

* * *

The child came an hour after full dark, when the market was at its thickest and the lamplight made gold coins of the puddles.

She was maybe six, wrapped in her mother's shawl too big for her, dragging a woman whose face had the grey, stretched look of someone who hadn't slept since the fever started. Orsen saw them coming and did the arithmetic automatically—sick sibling or sick father, too poor for the physicker on Cord Street, come to the charm-seller because the charm-seller was cheaper than hopelessness.

"For the fever," the woman said. She didn't ask if Orsen sold cures. Nobody asked that anymore. Asking implied belief, and belief got you a visit from the Wardens. "Whatever you have. A knot. A bead. Something to hang over his bed."

Orsen had a whole tray of somethings. They chose the smallest clay spiral, cool and ordinary, fired that week. "Hang it high, out of his reach. Keep the window cracked. Boil your water." They named a price the woman could pay, and when she counted it out coin by coin with shaking fingers, Orsen slid two of the coins back. "Market's slow tonight. Call it a discount."

The woman's mouth trembled around something that might have been thanks or might have been prayer, and Orsen looked away quickly, because that was the other rule: never watch a customer believe. It was bad for business and worse for the soul.

But the child—the child looked up at Orsen with the terrible directness of the very young, and said, clear as a bell across the noise of the market:

*"Please let my brother live."*

Not to Orsen. To the air. To whoever was listening. Children still prayed in Saltmere, quietly, the way they picked their noses and believed in monsters—things adults pretended not to see.

And the air listened back.

Orsen felt it happen the way you feel lightning decide on a tree. The hum beneath the shop—loose, loose, too loose—*leaned*. Something old turned its attention upward through half a mile of stone and clay and the packed dead weight of Saltmere's history, and it looked at the child, and it said, in a voice that was not a voice but arrived in Orsen's marrow all the same:

***Yes.***

Just that. One word, vast and unhurried, spoken the way a king grants a pardon.

The child blinked. Smiled—sudden, sunlit, certain—and tugged her mother away into the crowd, already chattering about her brother getting better, and the woman glanced back once at Orsen with wet eyes as if the charm-seller had performed a miracle, and Orsen stood behind their stall with their hands gripping the driftwood edge hard enough to splinter it, watching them go, hearing the word echo down and down and down.

*Yes.*

Old gods did not answer prayers. Old gods were dead, or sealed, or mythologized into harmlessness—that was the founding promise of the Inquisition, written in ash over thirty years. If something under the city had just said *yes* to a six-year-old, then either the seal was failing—

—or it had already failed, and everyone simply hadn't noticed yet.

Around Orsen, the market went on. Eel-smoke, laughter, the fortune-teller's patter. No one else had heard. No one else had felt the ground lean. Orsen sold two more knot-charms with steady hands and a voice pitched perfectly between friendly and forgettable, and all the while the word sat in their chest like a swallowed coal.

At midnight, when the lamps were stubbed and the stalls folded, Orsen went home to find out how bad it was.

* * *

The cellar smelled of cold stone and old wax. Orsen lit a single candle, carried it down the stairs they'd descended ten thousand times without ceremony, and held it up to the far wall.

There were nine ward-stones set into that wall, each the size of a fist, each carved with a Halloran seal that predated writing. They weren't wards in the common sense—they didn't keep things out. They kept things *quiet*. Anchors for the great silence below, tuned by generations of Hallorans who had served as the gate's custodians back when custodianship was an honor instead of a death sentence. When the Inquisition came, the Hallorans had done the unthinkable: they'd helped. Sealed their own birthright, buried their own power, scattered the family name like ashes. Orsen's grandmother had called it mercy. Orsen's mother had called it survival. Ten-year-old Orsen, kneeling in this same cellar with bloodied palms pressed to these same stones, had called it nothing at all, because they'd been too busy screaming.

For ten years the stones had held. Through inspections, through informers, through the long gray decade of being nobody.

Tonight, the fourth stone from the left was cracked straight through.

Not chipped. Cracked—clean as a struck bell, a hairline of darkness running top to bottom, and from the crack seeped the faintest thread of light, gold-white, pulsing gently in time with a rhythm Orsen recognized and wished to every dead saint they didn't.

It was breathing. The light was breathing. Slow and vast and *awake*, and beneath it the hum had risen now, just slightly, just enough that Orsen knew—with the sick certainty of a physician reading a death mask—that anyone with the gift, anyone god-touched, anyone who knew what listening felt like, could stand in the street above and hear it.

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"Stop it," Orsen whispered, and hated how young their voice sounded. "You're shut. You've been shut for ten years. I *shut* you."

The light pulsed once—affectionate, almost, the way a sleeping animal twitches toward a familiar hand—and the whisper came again, closer now, threading up through the stones and into the roots of Orsen's teeth:

***Orrrrrrin.***

The name landed like a knife finding the gap between ribs.

No one had spoken that name in ten years. Orsen had buried it in this cellar alongside the gate—had stood right here, sixteen years old and terrified, and fed their true name into the seal along with everything else they were, because the Inquisition's pyres ate names first. *Orsen* was the shell that remained, plain and sellable, the knot-charm version of a person. *Orrin* was the key, and keys got you burned.

And the gate had just said it. Said it *fondly*. Said it the way you say the name of someone you've been dreaming about.

"No," Orsen said aloud, to the stone, to the light, to the dark half-mile below. "Don't you dare know me. I'm not yours. I stopped being yours."

The crack in the fourth stone widened with a sound like ice on a warming lake—a soft, ticking *tick*—and the fifth stone shivered in its socket.

Orsen stood very still in the candlelight, doing sums they didn't want to finish. A gate that whispered names. A god that answered children. Ward-stones failing one by one, each failure louder than the last. How long before the hum reached the surface? Before some god-touched beggar in the Warrens lifted their head mid-prayer and *heard*? Before the Wardens' instruments—those ugly brass things that tasted the air for magic—began to tick?

Days. Maybe less.

Ten years of invisibility, ending not with a bang but with a breath, and Orsen Halloran—charm-seller, liar, last of a line of traitors—stood alone in their cellar and understood, with perfect clarity, that the hiding was over.

Above them, muffled by floorboards, the shop bell rang.

Orsen froze. Past midnight. The market closed hours ago. They blew out the candle in one motion and climbed the stairs silent as smoke, and through the dark window of the shopfront they saw a figure waiting outside in the lamplit fog—tall, cloaked, holding a lantern that threw its light upward onto a face Orsen didn't know.

Except that wasn't quite true either. Because the face was young, and sharp, and wore the unmistakable cropped hair of an Inquisitor's household, and its eyes were fixed not on the shop but on the cobblestones beneath it, as if it were listening.

As if it had *heard*.
