## 2. The Prayer That Answered Back

The cellar stairs were the worst part. They always had been.

Twelve steps down into the cold, each one worn smooth by Orsen's own feet over ten years of nightly descent, each one a small ritual of pretending. *Just checking the preserves. Just airing the storeroom.* Lies told to an empty house, because the truth was that Orsen Halloran went down those stairs every night the way a physician visits a sleeping patient—quietly, with dread, listening for the breath to change.

Tonight the breath had changed.

The ward-stones sat in their old wooden crate beneath a shelf of pickled quince and dusty lamp oil, seven of them arranged in the pattern their grandmother had drilled into them before Orsen was old enough to question it. River-stone, salt-bleached, each one carved with a sigil that no Inquisitor's primer would recognize and no god-touched zealot would survive touching. For ten years they had hummed. Not loudly. Not musically. Just a low, constant note beneath hearing, like the memory of a bell, and Orsen had learned to sleep through it the way one sleeps through rain.

Now three of the seven were cracked clean through, and the hum had become a whisper.

Orsen crouched beside the crate, lamp held low, and did not touch the stones. Touching them was the first mistake everyone made. Their grandmother had been very clear about that, in the last year of her life, when her hands shook too badly to work the sigils and she'd begun talking to Orsen as though to an heir instead of a child. *The wards don't break, little gate. They thin. And thin things tear when you press them.*

The cracks weren't breaks. They were thin places, hairline seams of darkness running through the stone like veins of night, and from each seam came a sound.

Not sound, exactly. Orsen had spent a decade learning the vocabulary for things that had no honest words. It was a pressure against the ear, a suggestion of voice, the way a dream leaves a phrase behind that you can almost repeat on waking. But underneath the suggestion, unmistakable, was rhythm. Cadence. Something down there beneath the floor of Saltmere, beneath the cellar, beneath the ward-stones and the old foundations and the drowned streets the city had been built over and forgotten—

Something was speaking.

Orsen sat down hard on the cold flagstones and made themself breathe.

*This is what you trained for. This is the whole shape of your hidden life. You are the keeper of a door, and doors are checked, and doors are mended.*

Except they had never been taught mending. Their grandmother had died in the spring of the year Orsen turned sixteen, and the seal had been finished that same autumn, and there had been no one left to teach the part that came after. Only the part that came before: how to bind, how to close, how to pour a bloodline into stone and salt and silence. The maintenance of a miracle had been left to a teenager with cracked hands and a talent for pretending, and Orsen had done what they could—ward-stones renewed each season, sigils traced in lampblack and salt, the nightly descent, the listening.

It had been enough. It had been enough for ten years.

They reached into the crate, slow as honey, and lifted the first cracked stone into the lamplight.

The whisper sharpened.

*—osen—*

The lamp nearly went into the crate. Orsen caught it with a fumbling hand, heart slamming, and set it down on the floor where the light pooled and shook with their trembling.

No. No, that was nothing. That was the sea in the stones—Saltmere was built on salt and drowned things, and the tide made noises in old masonry that could sound like anything, like a name, like—

*—orsen—*

Not the sea. The sea did not know their name. The sea knew the name they sold charms under, the name painted on the little sign above the shop—*O. Marlow, Charms & Small Mercies*—the name on the tax rolls and the night market ledger, a name as thin and safe as paper.

This was the other name. The buried one. The one their grandmother had spoken over them at the sealing, the one that had gone into the gate along with the blood and the sigils and the last of their childhood magic, the one Orsen had not said aloud, had not written, had not let themselves *think* in its fullness for ten years because a name spoken is a name alive, and a name alive is a hook, and they had spent a decade making themself unhookeable.

The gate knew it anyway. Of course the gate knew it. The gate had *taken* it. It was woven into the seal the way a weaver's name is woven into a tapestry's hem—invisible from the front, structural from behind.

And the seal was failing, and the name was coming loose, and the gate was saying it into the dark like a child calling for the parent who had shut it in.

Orsen pressed the cracked stone against their sternum, the way their grandmother had once pressed her hand there, and closed their eyes, and listened properly this time, the way they'd been trained to listen before they'd been trained to hide.

The whisper resolved. Not one voice. Many, layered, patient, the way a congregation is one sound made of hundreds. And beneath the voices, beneath the name, was the thing Orsen had been dreading since they'd seen the first crack three nights ago and told themself it was settlement, old houses settle, old stones crack:

The rhythm was wrong.

The seal had a pulse. Their grandmother had shown them how to feel it—two fingers to the ward-stone, and wait, and wait, the slow deep beat of a door held shut by a willing bloodline, steady as a sleeping heart. Orsen counted it now against their own pulse, and their own won the race twice for every one beat of the stone.

*It's slowing,* they thought, and the thought went through them like a fishhook. *It's slowing because it's not being held. It's slowing because the hand on the other side of the door is letting go.*

Because the hand was theirs. Had always been theirs. The seal wasn't a wall, wasn't a lock, wasn't any of the things the Inquisitors' doctrine said magic was—something to be crushed, outlawed, burned out of the world. It was a *held thing*. A door kept shut by grip, and grip required a keeper, and the keeper had spent ten years pouring everything of themselves into being nobody. O. Marlow. Charm-seller. Small mercies only. Harmless as candle smoke.

The seal had been starving on that. On smallness. On the deliberate amputation of everything that made a Halloran a Halloran.

Orsen opened their eyes and looked at the cracked stones, all three of them, and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding.

Three cracks in one season. One crack in ten years before that. The curve was not gentle. Whatever had answered the child's prayer in the night market two nights ago—whatever old thing had leaned against the world hard enough to make a beggar's cup fill and a dying woman's fever break—it had leaned through *their* door. Through their thinning seal. And every prayer it answered pried the seams a little wider, because the old gods had never learned to knock. They only knew how to push.

"Stop," Orsen said aloud, to the stones, to the gate, to the congregation of patient voices. Their voice cracked in the cold air and they hated how young it sounded. "I know what you're doing. I know what you want. You don't get it by saying my name."

The whisper paused.

The whole cellar paused—the lamp flame standing straight, the drip of the cistern hanging mid-fall, the dark itself holding its breath—and in that pause Orsen felt, with terrible clarity, that they had been *heard*. Not the name. Not the bloodline. *Them.* The person. The one who came down twelve steps every night for ten years and never once failed to come.

Then the moment passed, and the drip fell, and the flame leaned, and the whisper resumed—softer now, almost gentle, almost fond, and that was worse.

*—come home, orsen—*

"No," Orsen said, and put the stone back in the crate, and stood, and made their legs work.

* * *

They didn't sleep. They sat in the shop's back room with all the lamps lit, which they never did—light was for people with nothing to hide—and laid out the tools of their other inheritance on the workbench: the salt, the lampblack, the bone stylus their grandmother had carried across three borders and one war. Renewing the ward-stones was a season's work done properly. Orsen had perhaps days.

They were on the second sigil, hand aching, when the first prayer came through.

Orsen felt it the way you feel a door open in a far room—a change in pressure, a thread of cold. Somewhere in Saltmere, someone was asking the dark for something. And somewhere beneath Saltmere, something old and starving and *grateful* was reaching up through Orsen's failing seal to give it.

The ward-stone in their hand warmed. Then warmed further. Then sang—one clear note, the first honest sound it had made in ten years, and Orsen dropped it and watched it roll across the floorboards and come to rest pointing, unmistakably, toward the harbor district.

Toward the night market. Toward the shop of a god-touched beggar named Pell, whom Orsen had sold a luck-charm to three days ago and whose fortunes had turned with a speed that had nothing to do with Orsen's honest, hollow work.

"Oh no," Orsen said quietly. "Oh, *no.*"

Because they understood it now, all of it, the whole terrible mechanism. The gate was answering prayers through the cracks. Every answered prayer was a miracle, and every miracle in Saltmere was a beacon, and there were two kinds of people in this city who watched for beacons: zealots who wanted the old gods back, and Inquisitors who burned anyone who helped them come. And Orsen's name—both names—was written into the door both kinds of people were now pushing against.

They looked down at the sigil half-finished on the workbench, at the stylus trembling in their grip, at the salt scattered like fallen snow across the wood.

Ten years of invisibility. Ten years of smallness, of the safe name, of the nightly descent and the listening and the pretending. And it was ending—not because they had been careless, not because they had been caught, but because the door they kept could no longer be kept by a ghost. The seal needed a Halloran. All of one. And every day Orsen spent being O. Marlow, the cracks widened, the whisper grew louder, the prayers got answered, and the whole hidden architecture of their life came apart one miracle at a time.

Somewhere out there, Pell the beggar was telling people about his luck. Somewhere out there, reports were being written in a neat Inquisitorial hand. Somewhere out there, a woman with light in her eyes was following the same thread Orsen had just traced, backward through every answered prayer, toward its source.

Toward the gate. Toward the bloodline. Toward *them*.

Orsen blew out all but one lamp, and took up the stylus again, and worked until their fingers bled, and when the sky behind the shutters went grey with false dawn they finally admitted what the whisper had known all along, what the cracked stones had been telling them in their patient stone language:

The decade of being no one was over. The only question left—the question that had followed them down twelve stairs every night for ten years and now stood waiting in the grey light like a creditor—was who would reach them first.

The ones who wanted the gate open.

Or the ones who wanted the gatekeeper burned.
