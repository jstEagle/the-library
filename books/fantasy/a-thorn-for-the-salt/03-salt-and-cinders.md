## 3. Salt and Cinders

The reports arrived the way ash arrives after a fire: quietly, in drifts, from every quarter of the city at once.

Sefa had been cataloguing them for eleven days. Her father's office on the third floor of the Inquisitorium kept a strict taxonomy—*unverified, investigated, dismissed, condemned*—and she had inherited the unverified pile because she was the Inquisitor's daughter and therefore trustworthy with paper. The pile had been a drawer. Then a shelf. Then two crates stacked beside her desk, and this morning a third, and the clerks had begun to look at her the way sailors look at a rising tide.

She read them all anyway. It was easier than admitting what they meant.

*A woman on Tanner's Row prayed over her son's fever and the fever broke before dawn, and the boy woke speaking a word no one taught him, a word like water going backward.*

*A fisherman lost his nets in the deep channel and found them returned, mended, with a silver coin knotted into each mesh. He would not say who he'd thanked.*

*A child in the Saltward district drew a door in chalk on a wall and the chalk would not wash off, and now people leave offerings in front of it, and the chalk has begun to look less like a door and more like a mouth.*

Sefa copied each one into the ledger in her small, disciplined hand. She was good at this work—good at the ordering of chaos into columns, at the soothing bureaucratic fiction that a thing written down was a thing understood. Her father had taught her that a report was a weapon you handed to the truth. He had not taught her what to do when the reports began to agree with each other.

Because they did. That was the rot in the heart of it. Eleven days, forty-three reports, and every single one described the same shape of miracle: small, personal, answered *specifically*. Not the vague providence priests used to peddle before the Purging, when the old gods still walked and the world had burned for it. These were answers with the caller's name in their mouth. Prayers were being heard, and something on the other side was listening *carefully*.

The Inquisition's founding doctrine fit in a single sentence, and Sefa had known it since before she could write: *The gods gave mortals war; mortals gave the gods their walking-back.* Her grandfather had marched in the Purging. Her father had built his career on its ashes. Magic was outlawed, charms were confiscable, and prayer itself was permitted only in the sanctioned form—addressed upward, to nothing, to the safe and silent sky.

And yet the tide was coming in.

* * *

She found the beggar on the steps of the Ash Chapel on a gray morning when the harbor smell lay thick over the Saltward district—brine and coal smoke and the sweet rot of the fish market. He was old, or old the way the sea is old, with a face like weathered rope, and he sat with his tin cup arranged before him and a line of small stones beside it, each one placed with the precision of a man setting a table for guests.

Sefa knew him. Everyone in the district did. They called him the Stone Man, and he'd been on those steps for years, harmless, murmuring. The Inquisition left beggars alone as a rule; poverty was not yet a heresy.

But this morning there were three of them on the steps, and two were not beggars. They were zealots of the kind the Inquisition called *god-touched*—the ones who'd crept out of the woodwork in the last season, whispering that the old gods were stirring, that the Purging had been a wound the world meant to heal. This pair wore their faith openly: ash smeared on their brows, a bell hanging silent from one man's belt. The woman held the Stone Man's cup and was pouring its contents into his lap.

"—and you will tell us what you heard," she was saying, in the gentle voice people use for the very old and the very mad. "The stones. You arranged them for someone. Who came to you?"

"I arrange them for the tide," the Stone Man said. "The tide comes. The tide goes."

"Someone *answered* you. The whole street heard you weeping on Tuesday night. What did you ask for? What did it *say*?"

Sefa should have walked past. That was the doctrine too: the god-touched were the Inquisition's problem, and she was a cataloguer, a daughter, a keeper of paper. She had her father's gray coat on, the one with the ash-and-anchor sigil at the collar, and anyone who saw it saw the Inquisition and looked away.

The old man's hands were shaking. That was what decided her, she told herself later. Not the woman's voice, not the silent bell, not the way the crowd had begun to gather with the particular stillness of people watching something they were afraid to stop. Just the old man's hands, shaking like a child's.

"Is there a problem here?"

The god-touched woman turned, saw the sigil, and her gentleness did not crack so much as *rearrange* itself. "Inquisitor. This man has been in contact. He's been—"

"This man is under Inquisitorial observation," Sefa said, and was surprised at how easily the lie came, how smoothly it fit her mouth like a coin she'd been carrying for years. "His case is open. His statements are to be taken by the office, not by the public. You're interfering with an active file."

The bell at the man's belt swung once, though he hadn't moved. Sefa did not look at it. She had the sudden, animal certainty that if she looked at it, it would ring.

The zealots withdrew—not chastened, but *satisfied*, which was worse, as though she'd confirmed something for them. The woman smiled at her on the way down the steps. "The door is opening, Inquisitor. Your father can't close what's already opening."

Sefa crouched by the Stone Man. Up close he smelled of salt and something older, like the inside of a cave that had never seen sun. "Are you hurt?"

"They want to know who I pray to." He smiled, gap-toothed, guileless. "I pray to the tide, girl. The tide doesn't answer. The tide just *keeps* things."

"What things?"

"Whatever falls in." His eyes, milky at the edges, fixed on her with sudden terrible clarity. "You've got cinders in your coat, daughter of ash. You're carrying somebody's burning."

She stood up too fast. Around them the crowd was dispersing, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning, and Sefa walked back toward the Inquisitorium with the Stone Man's words lodged in her chest like a fishhook.

*Somebody's burning.* She didn't know what it meant. She only knew that she had lied for the first time in her life with her father's sigil on her collar, and that the lie had felt less like a fall and more like a *door*.

* * *

That night she did not go home to the Inquisitorium's family wing. She went to the reports.

She laid them out on her desk floor in the pattern her father used for conspiracy cases—a web, strings of red thread between pins—and forced herself to see what the threads were drawing. Forty-three answered prayers in eleven days. She mapped them by district, by date, by the *direction* the prayers seemed to have been heard from, which was a thing no report asked and which she found herself adding in the margins, because the answers had a geography. They were not everywhere. They were *centered* somewhere.

She worked backward from the anomalies. The chalk door in the Saltward: three streets from the harbor, facing east. The fevered boy on Tanner's Row: his mother said the answer had come "from below, like a bell rung underwater." The fisherman's mended nets: found in the deep channel, but the coin knotted in them was old Saltmere mint, a coin from before the Purging, from before the gates were sealed.

Below, said one report. Underwater, said another. The old mint, said a third. Sefa stood over her web of thread and felt the answer assemble itself with the cold click of a lock turning.

The night market. The low quarter along the old canal, where the licensed charm-sellers hung their harmless trinkets—knots for luck, salt sachets, copper rings that did nothing and were taxed for doing it. The one district in Saltmere where the Inquisition permitted magic's *shadow*, because outlawing the shadow had proved impossible and taxing it was profitable. Her father called it the city's abscess. He'd raided it twice a year for a decade, always finding nothing, always finding *exactly* nothing, which Sefa had never until this moment thought to question.

And beneath the night market, if the old harbor charts in the Inquisitorium's cellar were to be believed—and she had checked, three nights ago, alone, with a lamp and her heart in her throat—there were vaults. Pre-Purging vaults. Sealed since the war. Sealed since the *gates* were sealed.

Every thread in her web pointed down, and under the night market was the deepest down there was.

She should take it to her father. That was the motion her whole life had been built around: find the truth, carry the truth, hand the truth to the man who turned truth into verdicts. She could see it already—the raid, the pyres, her father's grim satisfaction, the drawer of unverified reports stamped *condemned* and closed forever. She could see the charm-sellers in their stalls, and the beggars, and the Stone Man with his stones arranged for the tide, and the fevered boy who'd woken speaking water going backward, all of it fed into the same fire that had burned her grandfather's world clean.

*Your father can't close what's already opening.*

Sefa sat down on the floor among her threads and put her face in her hands, and for a long time the only sound in the office was the harbor wind worrying the shutters.

When she looked up, her eyes had dried, and something in her had finished a long, quiet argument and arrived at a verdict of its own. She did not know yet what she believed about the old gods. She did not know if the thing under the night market was a wound or a door or a god. But she knew she could not hand it to her father unopened, unexamined, un-*understood*—not when every report read like a prayer answered kindly, and the Inquisition's whole doctrine rested on the claim that the gods answered only with war.

She would go herself. Tonight. To the night market, to the abscess, to the place where the threads converged. She would see it with her own eyes before she let anyone—her father, the zealots, the silent bell—decide what it was.

She took off the gray coat with the ash-and-anchor sigil and did not put it back on. Underneath she wore a plain wool cloak, the kind any clerk or dockhand might own. She copied the three strangest reports into a notebook, tucked it inside her shirt, and blew out the lamp.

At the door of the office she paused. On her desk, the red-threaded web lay spread across the floor like a wound in the room, all its threads running down and inward, down and inward, to a single point beneath the city.

The night market opened at dusk. The charm-sellers would be lighting their lamps.

And somewhere beneath them, Sefa was almost certain, something old had just heard her name for the first time.
