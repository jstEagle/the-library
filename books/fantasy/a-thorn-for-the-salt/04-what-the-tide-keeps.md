## 4. What the Tide Keeps

The knock came at the hour when the shop should have been empty—three past midnight, when even the gulls went quiet and the tide below the cliffs pulled back like a held breath.

Orsen knew before they opened the door. The wards in the cellar had been singing all night, a thin silver note that no one else could hear, and now that note bent toward the threshold like a compass needle finding north. Someone was standing on the other side of the door who carried law the way other people carried knives.

They opened it anyway.

The woman on the step wore plain wool over an Inquisitor's gray undercoat, the collar turned down to hide the rank-stitching. She had her father's jaw and nothing of his certainty. Rain beaded in her dark hair. In her gloved hand she held a folded paper, and Orsen's eyes went to it the way a man's eyes go to the blade.

"Sefa Askelan," she said. "You sold my mother a charm for her knees last spring. It worked."

"It usually does." Orsen leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, wearing the lazy smile they'd spent ten years sanding smooth. "Though if you've come about a refund—"

"You know why I've come." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled, like ice over moving water. "Eleven answered prayers in this district in six days. All within two streets of this shop. I traced every report. And when I mapped them—" she unfolded the paper, and Orsen saw the ink lines radiating outward from a single point, "—every line pointed here."

Orsen looked at the map for exactly as long as an innocent person would look at a map: curious, brief, faintly bored. "Charms draw pilgrims. Pilgrims pray. Prayers get answered sometimes—it's weather, not witchcraft."

"The prayers are being answered *correctly*," Sefa said. "A fisherman asked for calm water and got calm water—not a drowned boat and a lesson. A widow asked to see her husband once more and dreamed of him so truly she woke weeping with his smell still on her pillow. That isn't weather, Halloran." She said the surname softly, and it landed like a stone dropped down a well; Orsen heard it falling for a long time. "That is something *listening*. Something old."

The cellar hummed. Orsen felt it through the soles of their boots—a pulse, a lean, the gate beneath the floor straining toward the woman on the doorstep like a dog toward an open hand. *Of course,* Orsen thought grimly. *It likes her. It would.*

"Come inside," Orsen said. "You're letting the cold in."

* * *

Sefa had rehearsed the arrest eleven times on the walk over.

She'd rehearsed it in her father's voice, too—*hands where I can see them, you're coming to the Bastion, you'll hang by week's end*—and each rehearsal had sounded more hollow than the last. Because she had stood that morning in the evidence room holding the beggar-woman's confession, the god-touched crone who'd been dragged in for praying aloud, and Sefa had burned it rather than file it. Burned it in the lamp like a girl burning a love letter. Her hands still smelled of smoke.

So instead of reading the warrant she didn't have, she sat on a stool in a charm-seller's kitchen, surrounded by jars of salt and dried rowan and little knotted threads promising safe childbirth and honest dreams, and watched the shopkeeper pour tea with hands that were very slightly unsteady.

"You're afraid," she said.

"I'm cautious. There's a difference. Fear is for people with something to lose." Orsen set the cup down in front of her. Their fingers were long, ink-stained, deliberate. "You didn't bring soldiers."

"I didn't bring soldiers," she agreed.

"Why?"

It was the question Sefa had been turning over all night like a coin worn smooth. She gave it the only honest answer she had. "Because my father burns people who pray wrong. And I've started noticing that the people he burns are mostly poor, and mostly desperate, and none of them have ever actually hurt anyone. And because eleven miracles happened on your street and the worst thing anyone reported was a dream that made a widow cry." She wrapped both hands around the cup. "I'm supposed to write that up as a contagion of superstition. I can't make the words sit straight on the page anymore."

Orsen studied her across the steam. Whatever they saw in her face seemed to decide something. "You want to see what's answering."

"Yes."

"And then what? You see it, you run back to the Bastion, and I burn?"

"Then I bury my report," Sefa said. "All of it. The map, the names, the trace. It never existed." She met their eyes and let them weigh her, and did not flinch, though flinching sat right there behind her ribs. "My father taught me that an oath given carelessly is worthless. So hear this one carefully: I swear on my mother's grave that whatever is under your shop stays between us until I decide otherwise. That's the best bargain I have."

"That's a terrible bargain," Orsen said. "You just admitted you might decide otherwise."

"Yes."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through Orsen's face without settling anywhere. They looked at her a long moment—the Inquisitor's daughter, rain-damp, sworn on a grave, drinking tea in a criminal's kitchen—and then stood and took a ring of iron keys from beneath their shirt.

"Finish your tea," they said. "You'll want warm bones where we're going."

* * *

The way down was older than Saltmere.

Sefa had known the city kept cellars, cisterns, smugglers' runs—every coastal city did—but this was different. The stairs behind the false wall in Orsen's cellar descended through masonry that changed as they went: brick, then fitted granite, then stone no quarry had cut, black and faintly warm, veined with something that glimmered when her lantern crossed it. Salt crust bloomed on the walls in patterns too regular to be accident. Spirals. Eyes. Hands, open.

"These are pre-Banishment carvings," she breathed, tracing one without touching it. "These are *forbidden* pre-Banishment carvings. My father has a whole wing of the Bastion dedicated to scraping these off buildings."

"My family built half of what your father scrapes," Orsen said, not looking back. Their voice had changed down here—flatter, older, stripped of the market-day warmth. "Keep walking."

"How far down does it go?"

"As deep as the tide. Deeper, some nights." A pause, and then, quieter, almost unwilling: "It goes as deep as the gate."

Sefa stopped on the stair. "Gate."

Orsen turned. Lantern light carved their face into planes and shadow, and for the first time since she'd walked into their shop, they looked young—and exhausted, and terrified, and carrying something enormous behind the eyes.

"You swore on your mother's grave," they said. "Let me show you what you swore to keep."

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was not a room. It was a wound in the world that someone had tried very hard to dress.

Sefa's lantern seemed ashamed of itself there, its small flame bowing constantly toward the center of the cavern as if in prayer. The floor was salt, white and fine as sugar, and across it ran cracks—hundreds of them, branching like frost, like lightning frozen mid-fall. And from those cracks bled light. Not firelight, not lamplight. A color she had no name for, gold shot through with green, pulsing slow and patient as a heartbeat, pooling in the low places and sinking into the salt without staining it.

And behind it all, filling the far wall from floor to unseen ceiling, stood the gate.

It was not locked. That was her first insane thought—that everything she'd been raised to believe said the gods were shut out, sealed away, banished beyond a bolted door, and here was a door standing open by a hand's width, held by nothing but a lattice of light stretched across its frame like strings across a harp. The strings sang. She could feel them in her teeth. Every crack in the salt floor was a place where a string had frayed.

"What am I looking at," she whispered.

"The reason magic is outlawed." Orsen walked out onto the salt, barefoot—they'd removed their boots at the top of the stairs, she realized, with the ease of long habit—and the light rose gently around their ankles like tide around a wading bird. "Not because it's wicked. Because of *this*. Nine hundred years ago my ancestors closed this door on things that fed on worship the way leeches feed on blood. The seal needs a keeper of the bloodline. One keeper. Always one." They looked back at her, and their voice cracked on the next words. "There's supposed to be a Halloran standing here keeping it taut. There hasn't been one for ten years. I ran. I buried the name, I buried the craft, I opened a charm shop directly on top of the thing because I thought proximity would let me watch it fail slowly instead of suddenly."

"And now?"

"Now it's not failing slowly." They knelt and pressed their palm flat against the salt. Beneath it, visible even through the white grains, a vein of light crawled like something alive. "Now it's reaching. Those answered prayers? That's not the gods getting free. That's the gate *whispering through the gaps*, listening, answering, reminding the city it exists. It's gathering believers. It's gathering—" they hesitated, and Sefa watched them choose honesty over safety, "—it's gathering *me*. Last night it spoke my true name. The one I drowned ten years ago. It remembers me better than I remember myself."

Sefa stepped onto the salt before she decided to. The light touched her boots and did not burn; it warmed, the way sun warms a windowsill, and the singing strings shifted pitch—softened—as if the gate had noticed her too and found her interesting.

She understood, suddenly and completely, why Orsen hadn't run again. You could not report this. You could not burn this. You could only kneel beside it, the way she was kneeling now, close enough to see that each strand of the luminous lattice was woven from thousands of smaller threads, and that some of those threads were snapping, one by one, with sounds like distant bells.

"If this breaks," she said slowly, "the gods come back."

"The old ones come back. The hungry ones. The ones who took worship and gave war." Orsen's hand hovered over a snapped thread, not touching it. "I don't have enough left in me to hold this alone. Maybe I never did. Maybe that's the part nobody wrote down."

The gate pulsed. Both of them felt it pass through them, gentle and vast, like a whale surfacing beneath a swimmer. And in the silence after, Sefa heard it—not with her ears, but lower, in the sternum—a voice like deep water saying a word she couldn't parse, over and over, patient as erosion:

*two. two. two.*

She looked at Orsen. Orsen was staring at the gate with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life: the look of a person realizing the prison they'd guarded was a door, and doors are meant to be held, not hidden—and that it was asking for something neither of them had planned to give.

Above them, muffled through nine hundred years of stone, a bell began to ring in the night market district.

Neither of them had hung a bell there.

Orsen went white. "That's not the Inquisition's pattern," they whispered. "That's a calling bell. That's *believers*."

The light in the cracks brightened, like something below leaning closer to listen.
