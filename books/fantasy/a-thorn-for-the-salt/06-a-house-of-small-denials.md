## 6. A House of Small Denials

The house Sefa grew up in smelled of lamp oil and iron, and it had never once smelled of forgiveness.

She knew before she crossed the threshold. The lamps in the front parlor were lit—both of them, even the one her father called *the wastrel* because it burned more oil than light—and Inquisitor Dain Voss did not light wastrels for ordinary evenings. He lit them when he wanted a witness to his own patience.

Her boots were still damp from the undercity stairs. She had scrubbed the gate-grime from her hands at a public fountain, but there was salt in the creases of her knuckles that no amount of water would lift, as if the sea below Saltmere had signed its name to her skin.

Her father sat in the good chair with a folder across his knees. Not a file. A folder—the heavy grey kind, bound in cord, stamped with the double-key sigil of the Office of Inquiry. She had seen perhaps a dozen of them in her life. They were what became of reports that stopped being paperwork and started being cases.

"Sit," he said, without looking up.

Sefa sat. Across the room, above the cold hearth, hung the portrait of her mother, painted two years before the fever took her: a woman with Sefa's dark eyes and none of Sefa's doubts, holding a sprig of ash-leaf because the painter had run out of ideas. *Be brave,* her mother had said once, meaning something small—a tooth pulled, a thunderstorm. Sefa had spent ten years mishearing it.

"You filed nothing today," her father said.

"I was cataloguing."

"You were cataloguing." He looked up then. He had her grandfather's face, all planes and verdicts, softened only by the grey at his temples that Sefa had always privately considered an act of mercy on the part of time. "The Merrow Street prayer. The baker's daughter who spoke to a drowned thing in her flour bin. The beggar you let walk out of the Holding House with a blessing in his mouth and no entry made in any ledger." He turned a page with one finger, unhurried, a man turning pages he had already memorized. "Three omissions, Sefa. Three. Omissions are how houses fall. I've seen it. I've *made* it happen."

"Those anomalies are noise," she said. "Prayer-hysteria. You taught me yourself that nine of every ten answered prayers are coincidence wearing a costume."

"And the tenth?"

"The tenth is why we have the Office."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Worse. Approval, of a kind, arriving late. "Yes," he said softly. "It is." He rose, went to the window, and stood with his back to her, silhouetted against the harbor lights. "There's a tenth now. A real one. The Archonate has reports from four districts. Ward-stones fracturing in cellars. A child on Rope Lane who prayed for rain and got a flood that drowned nothing but her father's debts—every ledger in the house washed clean of ink, every debt, gone. That's not hysteria. That is something old leaning against the door of this world." He turned. "And my own daughter has been standing between me and it for a month."

The fire in the grate had burned down to a red eye. Sefa watched it rather than meet him.

"There's a man in the night market," her father said. "A charm-seller. Harmless-looking. Half the city swears by their luck-charms and the other half by their fever-salves, and neither half knows they're buying from a Halloran."

The name went through her like a hook through cloth.

"We've known for years, of course," he continued, conversational now, which was how she knew they had left safety far behind. "We let them be. A gatekeeper who sells trinkets and bothers no one is worth more than a gatekeeper driven underground where zealots can find them. It was policy. Containment by comfort." He came closer, and his shadow fell over her lap like something laid across her wrists. "But the seal is failing, Sefa. And three days ago someone marked that charm-seller with god-touched sign so bright my own officers could see it from the street. Every believer in this city knows there's a Halloran in Saltmere now. Every zealot. And the Office has exactly one person who knows where they hide."

"I don't—"

"Don't." His voice didn't rise. It never needed to. "You walked into the undercity on Tuesday. You came back at dawn smelling of salt-water that doesn't exist above ground. You have gate-light in your eyes—I saw it in the hall mirror before you saw yourself. Do not insult both of us."

Silence. The wastrel lamp guttered and steadied.

He crouched beside her chair then, and for a moment he was not the Inquisitor, not the crusade, just an aging man whose hands were scarred from pyres he had lit himself. "I fought in the Godfall," he said quietly. "I was younger than you. I watched a priest call something up out of the harbor and I watched that something wear forty people like coats. We burned the magic out of this city to keep that from ever happening again, and I have spent my whole life making sure the burning was *finished*. If the gate opens—if the old ones come back—it will be war, Sefa. Not metaphor. War. And the first pyre we build will need fuel."

"Then find your fuel somewhere else."

"That's the thing about fuel." He stood, and his gentleness closed like a door. "It's usually someone who loved the wrong person." He picked up the folder. "You have until the bell of First Watch tomorrow. Deliver the gatekeeper to the Office—alive, cooperative, and sealed—and your omissions become clerical errors. Clerical errors can be survived." At the doorway he paused, and did not look back. "Or burn with them. Those are the only two entries left in your file. Choose which one gets written."

His footsteps faded down the corridor toward his study, where, she knew, he would pour one measure of arrack and drink it standing, and sleep without dreaming, because that was the price he had paid and paid gladly.

Sefa sat alone in the parlor a long while, watching her mother watch her from above the dead hearth.

*Be brave.*

She thought about her father's face when he'd said *war*, and understood, finally, that he wasn't lying to her. He believed every word. That was the horror of it—that duty, taken far enough, looks identical to love from the inside. He would burn Orsen Halloran and grieve sincerely at the funeral of everything else.

She thought about salt in her knuckles. About a voice under the city saying *you brought the law; good, I was owed one.* About the way Orsen had flinched when the gate-light touched them, as if being seen were the same as being struck.

She stood up.

* * *

The archive of the Office of Inquiry kept its files in the cellar, in rows of iron cabinets that breathed cold air and old paper. Sefa had a key. She had always had a key; daughters of Inquisitors inherit keys along with their fathers' faces.

She let herself in at midnight, carrying a storm-lantern unlit, and walked the aisles to drawer nineteen, sub-row C: *Anomalies — Prayer Reports — Unresolved.* Her own handwriting looked back at her from thirty folders. Neat, careful, dutiful handwriting. A month of small denials, each one a brick in a wall she'd built between her father's world and the man beneath the shop.

She took them all.

In the courtyard behind the archive there was an iron brazier where the junior officers burned confiscated charms on feast days, a little ceremony of sparks and righteousness. Sefa lit it alone, with a taper meant for candles, and fed the folders in one at a time.

They burned beautifully. That was the obscene thing about paper—how eagerly it surrendered. Names she had protected went up in orange blossom: the baker's daughter, the child of Rope Lane, the god-touched beggar whose real name she had learned and never written down. Her month of treason, curling to black lace on the wind.

She burned other things too. The copy of the undercity survey with the shop circled. Her field notes, six pages, describing the taste of the air below Saltmere (*like the moment before a wave decides*). Last of all, folded small, a page she had told herself was inventory: a sketch she'd made without meaning to, of a charm-seller behind a market stall, laughing at something a customer said, lamplight caught in their hair.

The smoke stung her eyes. She told herself it was the smoke.

When it was done she stood in the ash-smell until the brazier ticked and cooled, and she felt lighter and emptier in equal measure, the way a house feels after the furniture is sold. She was no longer the Inquisitor's daughter with a promising career and a father's ear. She was a woman in a courtyard with soot on her cuffs and nowhere in the world she was permitted to be.

It was the freest she had ever felt.

* * *

She reached the shop on Merrow Lane as the sky went the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. The charm-stall was shuttered. The ward-lamp above the door—the one Orsen had charmed to look like a moth trap—was dark.

For one terrible minute she thought she was too late.

Then the door opened a hand's width, and one grey-green eye found her through the gap. "You shouldn't be here," Orsen said. "Your father's people have been walking this street since dusk. Two of them. Bad at pretending to buy chestnuts."

"I know." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "One of them is going to arrest you at First Watch unless I deliver you first. So." She pushed the door wide and stepped inside out of the rain beginning to spit, into the smell of dried lavender and hot copper. "I'm delivering myself instead. Consider it a package deal."

Orsen stared at her. Behind them, the floorboards of the back room gave off a faint, patient glow, like coals remembering fire. "What did you do?"

"I burned my files. All of them. Twenty years of my life fit in one brazier and it barely smoked." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges, and she let it. "My father gave me until the bell. Deliver you or share the pyre. And I stood in my mother's parlor and realized I've been choosing all month—I chose you on Tuesday, in the undercity, I chose you every time I wrote *no anomaly found* in my neat little hand. Tonight I just made it official."

"Sefa." Orsen reached out and took her hands, and turned them over, and frowned at the soot in the creases. Their thumb moved across her knuckles, slow, reading her like script. "This isn't a thing you can take back. Family. Rank. A roof that isn't hunted."

"I know what it costs. I've seen the ledgers." She lifted her head. "I'm done paying in denials."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the house around them seemed to hold its breath—then the glow beneath the floorboards pulsed, soft and unmistakable, twice, like a heartbeat that had been waiting to be acknowledged. Orsen huffed something that was almost a laugh, almost grief. "Even the gate approves. That's either very sweet or very bad."

Outside, across the city, a bell began to toll.

Not First Watch. First Watch rang seven strokes, even and dull as bread.

This bell rang twelve, and then kept ringing, wild and cracked and joyful, and beneath it Sefa heard—rising from the streets, from a hundred throats at once—a sound she had only ever read about in confiscated hymnals: voices raised in the old cadence, singing the names of things that had been outlawed before her birth.

Orsen went white. "That's Veyra's congregation. That's—" They turned toward the back room, where the floor-glow was no longer faint at all, but climbing the walls in veins of gold. "They're not waiting for First Watch. They're marching on the gate *now*."

Sefa grabbed the go-bag she'd packed in the archive cellar, back when burning files had still been a plan rather than an obituary. "Then we're done hiding under floors," she said. "Tell me how to get us below before they tear it open—or tell me how to stand in front of it."

Orsen was already hauling up the trapdoor, light pouring through the seams like dawn arriving from underneath the world.

"Together," they said. "Either way—together."

They went down into the shining dark as the bells rang on overhead, counting down a city's oldest sin come home to roost.
