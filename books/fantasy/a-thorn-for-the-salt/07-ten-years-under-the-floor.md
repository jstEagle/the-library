## 7. Ten Years Under the Floor

The undercity smelled of drowned candles and old salt—the breath of a city holding its secrets underwater.

Orsen ran with Sefa's hand locked in theirs, boots splashing through channels that had once been streets, before Saltmere's founders stacked the new city on the bones of the old like a liar stacking a second story over a confession. Behind them, above them, muffled by forty feet of stone and brick and compacted centuries, the bells of the Inquisition were still ringing.

Not the alarm pattern. The *hunt* pattern.

"They'll seal the stairwells," Sefa gasped, her lantern swinging wild shadows across the flooded arches. "My father knows these tunnels better than anyone alive. He trained in them."

"Then we don't take tunnels." Orsen pulled her left at a junction where a shrine to some forgotten saint had been bricked over, its niche now home to a colony of pale crabs that scattered from their light. "We take the places he'd never fit."

They squeezed through a gap behind the shrine—Sefa hissing as the stone scraped her shoulder—and came out into a hall so vast the lantern couldn't find its ceiling. Pillars rose out of black water, each one carved with faces worn smooth as river stones. Faces with too many eyes. Faces smiling.

"The first city," Sefa whispered. "I thought it was a story they told recruits to scare them."

"It was a city." Orsen kept moving along a submerged causeway barely wider than their shoulders. "Before the gods were outlawed, before the gates were sealed, this was where people came to *talk* to them. Not worship, not at first. Just talk. Like neighbors over a fence."

"And then?"

Orsen didn't answer. They had reached the middle of the hall, where the water deepened and the pillars clustered close, and here—here was the reason they had run this way instead of any other. A pillar stood apart from the rest, and on it, at the height of a person's heart, someone had carved a spiral. And inside the spiral, someone had carved a name.

*HALLORAN.*

Old carving. Old salt-crusted letters. Ten years old exactly.

Sefa raised the lantern. "That's your name."

"That's my name," Orsen said. "The one I buried."

* * *

They made camp in the hollow beneath the pillar, a dry pocket of stone where generations of smugglers had scratched their marks and left their leavings—a rusted brazier, a blanket gone stiff with mildew, half a candle stub that still took flame. Orsen lit it because their hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Sefa sat across from them, still in her Inquisitor grays, though she'd torn off the rank-braid hours ago when her father's men spotted them at the fish market. Without it she looked younger. Without it she looked like someone deciding something.

"You've been quiet since the market," she said. "Quieter than scared-quiet. This is thinking-quiet."

"You're learning my silences. That's either very good or very bad for both of us."

"Orsen." She leaned forward into the candlelight. "Veyra marked you. Half the god-touched in the city can feel where you are. My father has every stairwell watched. We are sitting in a hole under the sea, and you brought us *here*, to a pillar with your family name on it, which means this place matters, which means whatever you're not saying is the thing that decides whether we live through tomorrow. So tell me. Please."

The candle guttered. Somewhere far above, faint as a memory, the hunt-bells rang again.

Orsen looked at the carved spiral. It was easier than looking at her.

"You know what I told you," they said. "That I inherited the gate. That the Hallorans were keepers going back nine generations, and when the Inquisition came, my mother hid me in plain sight and died keeping the secret. That's true. Every word of it is true."

"But?"

"But it's not the whole shape of it." Orsen drew a breath that hurt on the way in. "I didn't just inherit the seal, Sefa. I helped make it."

The words came out of them like something that had been swallowed wrong ten years ago and had been lodged in the throat ever since.

"I was sixteen. There were four of us who knew what the gate was—my mother; my aunt Ilse, who kept the northern wards; old Master Corvin, who wasn't Halloran blood but knew the rites; and me. The Inquisition had already burned the temple districts. Already drowned the prayer-houses—you've seen the flooded shrines upstairs, that wasn't the sea's doing, that was *policy*. And the gate under my shop was the last major one in Saltmere. The last door the old gods could reach through.

"My mother wanted to hold it open. Just a crack. Just enough for the small gods—the hearth-gods, the tide-mothers, the ones who never asked for blood—to keep touching the world. She thought if magic went fully dark, people would forget what it felt like, and forgetting turns to fear, and fear turns to pyres forever. She wanted to leave a lamp burning in the window.

"Aunt Ilse wanted to shut it completely. Seal it with no key, no hinge, nothing. Cut the gods off clean. She'd lost sons to the zealot wars. She said anything less than total silence was an invitation.

"And Corvin—" Orsen laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "Corvin said the truth nobody wanted: that the gate couldn't be held by force alone. It was built balanced. Open-but-ordered. One keeper of the bloodline to speak for the gods' side, one keeper of the law to speak for the mortal side. Two hands on the same door, always. That was the original design. That's why the old kingdoms never fell to the gods and never starved of wonder—because the door was never one person's to hold.

"But there was no keeper of the law anymore. The law had declared war on the very idea. So Corvin proposed a desperate thing. A seal built from one side only. Bloodline magic, poured in deep enough to fake the balance. It would hold—but it would cost. It would take everything the giver had. Every drop of gate-sense, every charm in the blood, burned as fuel. The giver would live. But they would live *empty*. Mundane. Invisible to everything divine and everything arcane, forever. A ghost to the world they saved."

Sefa had gone very still. "Orsen. You were sixteen."

"I volunteered before my mother could stop me." The candle flame bent, though there was no draft. "Aunt Ilse argued. Mother argued. But I was the strongest of the line in three generations—I'd known it since I was small, I could hear the gate humming through the floorboards like other children heard rain. And I was arrogant. Sixteen and arrogant. I thought emptiness was a fair price. I thought I was trading a fortune I hadn't earned yet.

"So we did it. Four of us, down here, at this pillar—that's why my name is carved here, it was part of the rite, the seal needed a witness-stone. I knelt in the water and I gave it all away. Everything. And do you know what the strangest part was?" Orsen's voice cracked and they let it. "It didn't hurt. It felt like being *unpacked*. Like someone taking furniture out of a room until the room was bare and echoing, and then closing the door on the empty room, and telling me: this is yours now. This bareness is yours. Live in it.

"For ten years I lived in it. Sold charms that were only wood and wire and wishful thinking. Told myself the numbness was peace. Told myself I'd done the bravest thing anyone ever did." They finally looked up at her. "And then the wards started cracking. And the gate started whispering my true name. Because a one-sided seal doesn't fail all at once, Sefa. It fails the way a lie fails. Slowly, and then all at once, and then everyone remembers everything."

The silence between them filled with water-sound—the drip and hush of the drowned hall, patient as breathing.

"When I touched the seal," Orsen said quietly, "it didn't feel like a lock. It felt like a wound I'd cauterized instead of cleaned. And it's been festering for ten years, and Veyra is picking at it, and my father's hunters—" Sefa flinched at the word *father* but didn't correct them. "—and everyone is fighting over whether to burn the door or bar it, and the whole time, the answer was written on a pillar in the first city. *Two hands.* The gate was never meant to be mine alone. It was never meant to be shut alone. I sealed it wrong. I sealed it *alone*, because I was a child who thought loneliness was the same thing as strength."

"Orsen—"

"Don't." They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Don't comfort me yet. Let me finish being ashamed. I've had ten years of practice at hiding it; give me five minutes of not."

But Sefa moved anyway. She crossed the space between them and knelt in the damp stone and took their wrists, gently, and lowered their hands from their face. Her eyes in the candlelight were steady as a harbor.

"You were sixteen," she said. "You gave up everything you were to save a city that would have burned you for it. That's not shame. That's the most Inquisitor thing I've ever heard, and I mean that as a compliment, which will horrify us both." A breath. "You didn't seal it wrong because you were weak. You sealed it wrong because the world left you no partner. That's not your failure. That's ours. My father's crusade's. Mine, for carrying his files for twenty years without reading what they meant."

"Sefa—"

"I'm not finished." But her voice softened. "You said the gate needs two keepers. One of the bloodline. One of the law." She lifted her chin, and Orsen saw where this was going and their heart stopped like a struck bell. "I have spent my whole life being the law, Orsen. Even tonight—even burning my own name to run with you—I am still the law, the part of it that remembers what it was *for*. If the gate needs two hands—"

"No." Orsen grabbed her shoulders. "No. Do you understand what binding means? What it *cost* me? You'd be giving up—"

"I've seen what it costs to hold nothing." Her voice didn't waver. "I grew up in a house full of rank and fire and certainty, and it was the emptiest house I've ever stood in. Whatever the gate takes from me, it can't take more than my father already did." Her hand came up to Orsen's cheek, salt-damp and warm. "Ten years under the floor, Orsen. You've been down here long enough. Both of us have."

Orsen kissed her—or she kissed them; in the dark of the drowned hall, under the pillar with the carved name, it was impossible to say who moved first, and for the first time in ten years, Orsen found they didn't need to know. Some doors don't require a keeper. Some just require two people willing to stand on either side.

When they broke apart, foreheads touching, the candle had burned down to almost nothing.

"We should sleep," Sefa murmured. "Move before dawn, before the patrols rotate."

Orsen nodded. But as they settled back against the pillar, drowsing in the dark with Sefa's heartbeat against their shoulder, they felt it—faint, rising through the stone, through the water, through the bare room inside them where their magic used to live.

The hum. The gate's hum.

Except it wasn't humming anymore.

It was *ringing*. High and clear and urgent, like a bell struck under the sea, like a door being tested from the other side by hands that had waited ten years and were done waiting.

Orsen opened their eyes in the dark.

"Sefa," they whispered. "Wake up. It's starting."
