## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The counting house of the Vellum Guild kept its own hours, and none of them belonged to the sun. August Delacroix had worked the night ledgers for six years now, and in that time she had learned that midnight arithmetic was a different discipline entirely from the day sort. By day, the guild traded in memories — bottled, sealed, and sold to the wealthy who wanted first kisses they hadn't earned or griefs they could wear like borrowed furs. But by night, the trades came home to roost, and someone had to make the numbers confess.

Tonight that someone was August, alone at the long cedar desk beneath the green glass lamps, with three ledgers open before her and a fourth locked in the drawer she was not permitted to open.

She dipped her pen and wrote, in her small exact hand: *Received, one memory, 40 years quality — harvest festival, first love, riverbank. Sold to Lady Vessimore, aged seventy-one. Price: remission of debt, one estate lien.* Then, beneath it: *Source: debtor's son, aged twelve. Consent: implied by guardianship.*

*Implied by guardianship.* August let the phrase sit on the page a moment, ink drying glossy as an oil slick. The guild loved phrases like that. They were how you sold a child's summer and called it bookkeeping.

Her rules were simple, and she recited them to herself the way other people said prayers. *Balance every column. Question nothing aloud. Take nothing that is written down.* The third rule was the important one, because the guild did not fear thieves; it feared irregularities. A stolen vial was inventory. An unrecorded kindness was a wound in the ledger, and wounds in ledgers got people erased — not killed, exactly. Erased. Their debts sold, their names struck, their memories harvested down to the studs until what walked out of the extraction room was a person-shaped agreement to start over.

August had seen it happen twice. She had balanced both accounts afterward, because someone had to.

* * *

The trouble with working nights among impossible things was that some of them leaked.

Memories were stored in vials of smoked glass, racked in the iron cabinet behind her like dark honey, and every so often one would sweat. Not physically — the seals were perfect, Master Corvane saw to that — but a strong memory bled through glass the way cold bled through a wall. Sit near enough a good one for long enough and you'd catch whiffs of it: rain on a wedding morning, the smell of a grandmother's kitchen, the particular terror-joy of a first childbirth. The guild called it *bleed* and docked the wages of anyone who mentioned it, on the theory that savoring the merchandise was a form of theft.

August had gotten expert at not mentioning things.

At half past one, the door opened and Master Corvane came in with the night's intake, which meant he came in smiling, which meant somebody somewhere had lost something precious tonight. He was a narrow man, Corvane, gray as ledger dust, with the habit of standing too close while you wrote so he could read upside down.

"Three fresh," he said, setting vials on the tray. "Log them before the courier returns for his receipt."

"Three," August repeated, and reached for the first.

A soldier's memory: a hillside, a trumpet, the moment before a charge. Clean work, honestly taken — the man had sold it himself, soldiers always did, to forget what came after the trumpet. She logged it without feeling anything except the usual faint envy of people who could choose what to lose.

The second was a midwife's: forty years of deliveries compressed into a single distilled draught, sold by her daughter to pay for a coffin. August logged it. Her pen did not shake. She had trained it not to.

The third vial was smaller than the others and warm, though the room was cold. When August lifted it, the bleed came through so strong she nearly set it down again. Rain. Wet wool. Someone laughing, low, the way you laugh when you've just been told something wonderful and can't say it yet. And under all of it, threaded through it like wire through wax: *a woman's voice saying a name.*

Not her name. That was the first thing August checked, and she hated herself a little for checking. Just a name. Some mother's voice, saying some child's name, in the last golden moment before whatever happened happened. The label read: *Voluntary surrender. Debtor: Ilse Varrow. Balance outstanding after collection: zero.*

Zero. Nobody's balance came out to zero unless they'd given everything, and nobody gave everything voluntarily unless the alternative was worse.

"Problem, Delacroix?" Corvane had not gone to the door. He was watching her with his lamp-gray eyes, hands folded behind his back like a man admiring a garden.

"The seal's sweating," August said. "Heavy bleed. Recommend restricted shelving."

"Mm." He took it from her hand, held it up to the green light, and for one long moment the whole night seemed to hang on what his face did next. Then he shrugged, that economical shrug she'd learned to read as *nothing here concerns you.* "Mothers," he said, as if that explained it, and shelved it himself — not in the general rack but in the low drawer, the one at knee height, the one whose lock had been replaced last month with a mechanism August had spent two quiet evenings studying from memory alone.

Then he left, and the door clicked, and August sat alone with four ledgers and a heart that was beating wrong.

* * *

Here is what August Delacroix knew about loneliness, having studied it professionally for thirty-one years: it was not the absence of people. The counting house had people pass through it all night — couriers, debt-takers, the occasional weeping seller being escorted gently toward the extraction room. Loneliness was the absence of witness. It was knowing that if you vanished between one entry and the next, the only thing that would change was the handwriting.

She finished the columns. She ruled the lines. She initialed the margins in the tiny cramped monogram — *A.D.* — that the guild required, proof that a specific accountable person existed, which was, she sometimes thought, the closest thing to being witnessed the Vellum Guild offered.

And then, because she was who she was, she broke rule three.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the point of it. August's rebellions were engineered to be invisible, the way a locksmith's rebellion is a filed cog: everything still turns, nothing looks touched. Over six years she had committed perhaps nine small crimes, one for roughly every eight months, each one chosen the same way. She never took anything recorded as valuable. She never took anything that would be missed within a fiscal quarter. And she never — never — took anything whose loss would fall on the desperate. She stole from the guild the way a beggar steals bread from a banquet: only what would rot anyway.

What she took were discards. Memories the guild deemed worthless — blurred, faded, duplicates, fragments too slight to price. There was a discard bin in the sorting alcove, unlabeled because everyone knew what it was, and once a month or so a vial would sit in it long enough for August to pocket it, carry it home, and drink it down in her narrow room above the chandler's, so that for a few minutes her life would have someone else's warmth in it. A dog's whole devotion, once, from the dog's side. A boy's memory of learning to whistle. An old man's single afternoon of fishing, taken whole, water and light and the patient tug of the line. Small things. Unmissable things. She told herself she was giving them a burial, which was a lie, and that she wasn't profiting, which was also a lie, since she profited the way the starving profit from scraps — barely, privately, enough to keep standing.

Tonight, the discard bin held one vial.

It was the Varrow vial. Corvane had moved it — no, he hadn't; he'd shelved a decoy, she realized, going cold. The real one was here, in the bin, mislabeled *discard* in Corvane's own precise hand, and there was exactly one reason a man like Corvane discarded a full-quality memory for free: he wanted it drunk by someone deniable. Or he was testing the bins. Or—

Or she was tired, and lonely, and the bleed was still in her fingertips, rain and wet wool and a mother's voice saying a name like it was the answer to a question.

August looked at the vial for a long time. Then she did what she always did: she checked the ledger first. *Varrow, Ilse. Voluntary surrender. Zero balance.* No resale entry, no destruction order, nothing in any column anywhere that claimed this memory belonged to anyone's account. On paper, it did not exist. On paper, drinking it was not theft; it was mercy for an orphaned thing.

On paper, she thought, I am the most honest woman in this city.

She wrapped the vial in her scarf, tucked it against her ribs where the ledger-warmth of her body would hide its chill, and went back to ruling lines. Her hand was perfectly steady. Six years of practice had bought her that much: the ability to commit a small treason with the face of a woman adding numbers.

She was sealing the fourth ledger when the bell at the loading door rang — wrong hour, wrong rhythm, three urgent pulls instead of two courteous ones — and Master Corvane's voice came through the speaking tube, thin with something she had never heard in it before.

"Delacroix. Front room. Now. We have a courier bleeding on the steps and he keeps asking for the ledger-keeper by name."

August set down her pen.

Against her ribs, wrapped in wool, a dead woman's memory lay cold as a coin, and somewhere below, a dying stranger was saying her name — hers, the one no one outside this building knew — and the night, which had felt long, turned suddenly into something else entirely.

She picked up the fifth ledger, because a keeper always brought a ledger, and went to see what impossible thing had come home to roost.
