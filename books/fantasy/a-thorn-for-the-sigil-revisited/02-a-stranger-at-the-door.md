## 2. A Stranger at the Door

August Delacroix counted other people's memories the way other people counted sheep: out of necessity, and never quite to the end of them.

The vial she had pocketed from the counting house sat against her ribs now, tucked into the lining of her coat where a less careful woman might have kept a knife. It was warm. Memory always ran warm, like a body recently left. She could feel it through the wool with every step up her stairs, a small stolen heartbeat pressed to her own, and she hated how much comfort she took from it.

Her rooms sat above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, three narrow flights up, behind a door with two locks that she had installed herself and a third that existed only in the mind of anyone who asked. The stairwell smelled of tallow and rain. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog was dreaming aloud, paws thumping against the boards.

She had just lit her lamp when someone knocked.

Not knocked — struck. A fist, then a weight falling against the wood, then a sound she knew from the ledgers' margins, from the guild's quieter columns: the sound a body makes when it stops pretending to hold itself up.

August did not move. This was rule one, learned before she could write: *when trouble knocks, count your exits first*. There was the window over the roof of the chandler's shop. There was the coal hatch. There was the door she was not going to open.

The knock came again, weaker. Then a voice, slurred thin as watered wine.

"Delacroix. They said — Vellum Guild said you'd be — " A cough. Wet, wrong. "Please."

Every sensible instinct she owned told her to stand very still until whoever it was bled out on the landing and went to whatever ledger received such souls. The guild kept no column for kindness. Kindness was an unbalanced entry, and unbalanced entries got audited, and audits in the Vellum Guild ended with names written in the losses column.

But the voice knew her name. And under the wet cough there was something worse than pain: urgency, still burning, the way a candle end burns brightest right before it takes your fingers.

She opened the third lock, the one that didn't exist, because it only worked if you believed in it, and pulled the door wide.

He fell across her threshold like a delivered parcel.

Young. Guild courier's grey sash, soaked dark at the hem where he'd used it as a bandage. His doublet beneath carried three holes she could see and one she couldn't, that one low and bad, welling with each breath. He had a courier's build, all wire and endurance, and a courier's face — forgettable by design. But his eyes were still his own, still frightened, still *working*, which meant there was time, briefly, to be worth something.

"Door," August said, already kneeling, already stripping her scarf for a tourniquet she knew would insult the wound with its optimism. "Help me get you — "

"No." His hand clamped her wrist. Courier-strong. Dying-strong; the two are closer than people think. He dragged something from inside his sash — oilcloth wrapped around stiff card, sealed with wax the color of dried blood — and pressed it into her palms and folded her fingers shut over it with both of his shaking ones, as if in prayer.

"Thirteen turns," he whispered.

"What?"

"Thirteen turns. When the last moon finishes its circle. Don't let them — don't let it finish." His eyes rolled toward her lamp, hunting focus. "You balance things. I heard them say it. You're the one who balances things. So *balance this*."

"I'm a bookkeeper," August said, absurdly. "I keep accounts of — "

"Of impossible things." He smiled. It was a terrible smile, blood-flecked, but it had craft in it, the craft of a man who had decided how to die some streets ago and had stuck to the decision. "Then you know what this is worth. You know what they'll pay to bury it. Don't sell. Whatever they offer. Don't *sell*."

The map was heavy in her hands. Not physically — physically it weighed nothing, a few folded sheets. But her fingers, trained across ten thousand entries of traded lives, read its weight the way a moneylender's thumb reads clipped coin. Something in that wax seal was *wrong*, the way a sum is wrong by one that changes everything after it.

"Who cut you?" she asked. "Tell me who — "

"Listen." He pulled her close enough that his blood was on her lips, copper and lamplight. "It shows the thirteenth moon. Nobody's supposed to know it exists. The king doesn't even — " A shudder took him. He fought it down like a man swallowing glass. "Turns start at the new moon. Count them. If you're clever, and they said you were clever, thirteen nights is enough."

"Enough for what?"

His answer was a long exhale that didn't stop being an inhale.

For a moment the room was very quiet. The lamp flame leaned. Below, the chandler's dog had stopped dreaming.

August knelt beside a dead stranger on her own threshold, holding a sealed map worth more than the street she stood on, and felt her old life begin the slow arithmetic of its ending. She checked his pulse anyway — twice, professionally, hating herself for the professionalism — and closed his eyes with two fingers, gently, because no one else in this city was going to.

Then she heard boots on the stairs.

* * *

Three sets. Heavy, unhurried, spaced like men who owned the building and were letting it know. Guild enforcers walked exactly that way; she had watched Sable train a dozen of them in that cadence, *you're not chasing, you're collecting, make them wait for you.*

Sable. Her stomach turned over. If Sable was on those stairs —

No time. Rule one: count your exits. Rule two, learned the same hard night: *never be found with the evidence in your hands*.

The oilcloth map went into her coat where the vial lived, pressed against her heart like a second, colder heartbeat. The dead courier she could do nothing for except not add him to the debt she owed without knowing how.

The window over the chandler's shop opened onto a ledge, and the ledge onto the roofline of Candlewick Row, which ran ragged from the chandler's to the cooper's to the tall thin house that leaned drunkenly against the old grain tower. August had run these roofs for years — not from anyone, in the beginning, just to be above the city, above the ledgers, somewhere the columns couldn't reach her. She knew every gap and gutter the way she knew her own accounts.

She went out backward, pulling the window shut behind her, and had reached the ledge when the door below gave way.

She flattened into shadow. Through the glass, lamplight spilled: two enforcers, guild-grey, masks pushed up. And between them, filling the doorframe, a figure she knew better than her own handwriting.

Not Sable. Worse luck for her heart, and worse luck for the dead man. Master Corvane's chamber-steward, Vey, a small neat man with small neat hands and eyes like ledger lines, straight and close together and impossible to escape between.

Vey crossed the room, stepped over the courier's body without looking down — actually without looking down, that was the horror of him, the way you step over a dropped glove — and stopped at the window. His breath fogged the pane. August held still against cold slate and did not breathe at all.

After a moment that lasted a season, Vey drew the curtain.

"She's gone out," his voice said, muffled, bored. "Roof route, westward bias — the break on the sill is fresh. Loose the others. Bring her to the counting house alive. Corvane wants the map and wants her memory of tonight *intact* for extraction." A pause. "The courier's vial, too. She pocketed something Tuesday. She always pockets something."

August moved.

Westward bias, he'd said, so she went east, over the cooper's yard, dropping eight feet onto tar paper that stank of summer even in the rain, rolling, rising, running. Behind her, whistles bloomed along Candlewick Row like ugly flowers.

The roofs of the capital at night, in the rain, are a country unto themselves: chimneys for hills, clotheslines for borders, gutters running silver under a sky the color of dirty pewter. August ran it with her lungs on fire and her mind doing what her mind always did under pressure — *balancing*. Assets: one map of unknown value, one stolen memory vial, ten years of rooftop habit, and a body that had never been strong but had always been *exact*. Liabilities: the entire Vellum Guild, whose reach was the whole city's forgetting. Whose enforcers, tonight, wanted her alive — which bought her hours, not safety, and only if the map stayed hidden.

And one more liability, arriving on a delay, the way grief does: *the courier knew my name. He knew what I do. Who sent him?*

She vaulted the gap to the grain tower, hung by her fingers from the old hoist beam, swung, dropped. Rain in her eyes. Somewhere to the west a whistle died and another started, closer, patient. They weren't trying to head her off. They were herding her, funnels narrowing, and she realized with cold clarity that they knew her routes because Sable had taught her all of them, personally, patiently, on nights when the lesson had felt like love.

*Every exit I know is an entrance they know.*

So she did the thing that wasn't in any lesson. She went *down*.

Down the grain tower's inner shaft, hand over hand along the hoist chain, into the dark where rats moved like rumors, out through the loading arch into the alley of the tanners — a street no one ran to voluntarily, where the stink peeled paint and pursuers hesitated, because even guild enforcers have noses. She came up reeking, hood up, walking fast, a tired bookkeeper going home in the rain, and the whistles passed overhead and away like geese.

Only in the tanners' alley, leaning against a wall that wept chemicals, did she permit her hands to shake.

They shook, and then they stopped, because she made them, the way she balanced a column that wouldn't come right: firmly, line by line, until the figures obeyed.

She took out the map.

The wax seal was blood-colored and bore no sigil she recognized — or rather, it bore one she almost recognized, a curve of marks like moon phases, except that any honest astronomer would tell you there were twelve moons over the capital and here were thirteen, the last drawn slightly larger, slightly darker, as if it had been inked twice. As if it were coming.

Under the seal, when she picked it open with a thumbnail that wanted very much not to, the first fold showed a corner of cartography so fine it had to be guild work, palace-work, better — and along the margin, in a cramped hand she did not look at yet, deliberately, the way you don't look at a sum you're afraid won't balance:

*thirteen turns. the vault keeps the count.*

August refolded it. Put it away. Put her back against the weeping wall and breathed the chemical air and looked up through the rain at a sky where twelve moons should have been wheeling their slow procession and felt, for the first time in her ordered life, that the books were lying to her.

A dead man had chosen her. Out of every lockpick and knife and rebel in this city, a dying courier had run to a bookkeeper and put a map in her hands and said *balance this.* That meant either she was already part of the account — entered long ago, in ink she hadn't seen — or she was about to be.

Both possibilities terrified her equally.

She thought of her rooms above the chandler's shop. Her two real locks and one imaginary one. Her kettle. The narrow bed with the blanket worn soft. All of it burned now, not with flame but with the colder fire of association: by morning Vey would have the building watched, and by tomorrow the rooms would be a trap dressed as a home, and she could never sleep there again. Ten years of careful, lonely, exact living — spent, just now, on her own threshold, in the time it took a stranger to die holding her wrists.

She could go to the guild. Confess. Hand over the map, plead mishandling, take whatever column Corvane wrote her into. She had seen what happened to people who tried that. She had *entered* what happened to people who tried that, in neat sums, in her own hand.

Or she could follow the map.

*Thirteen turns,* the dead man had said. *Don't let it finish.*

Below the tanners' alley, the city sprawled under its twelve visible moons, ignorant and lit and loud. Somewhere out there was whoever had annotated a forbidden chart. Somewhere was the reason a courier bled through three streets to find a bookkeeper. Somewhere, at the bottom of all the columns, was a thirteenth moon turning in the dark, patient as interest accruing.

August pulled her hood lower, chose a direction at random and then, out of long habit, chose a better one, and walked out of the tanners' district into the rain-washed night carrying two stolen things against her heart — a memory that was warm, and a map that was cold — and understood, with the terrible clarity of a column finally balancing, that whatever waited at the end of thirteen turns, she was already in the ledger.

The only question left was what she'd be written down as having cost.

Behind her, faint under the rain, the chandler's dog began to howl.
