## 4. The Debt Collector

The summons came at the hour of tallow, when the counting house lamps burned low and honest guildsmen were asleep.

Sable did not sleep. She sat in her rooms above the enforcement wing with a whetstone and her short sword across her knees, drawing the edge along the stone in long, patient strokes that made no sound worth mentioning. It was an old habit from her soldiering days — the blade never truly needed the stone anymore, not since she'd had it folded by a smith in Corvane who owed the guild three favors and a daughter — but the ritual was the point. Ten strokes a side. Then she could think.

She was four strokes into the second side when the knock came: two beats, pause, one beat. The Master's rhythm.

"Enter."

The messenger was new — a boy of perhaps fifteen, collar high against the cold, eyes fixed on the floor tiles as if they might open and swallow him before he had to speak. They never opened for anyone. Sable had worked for the Vellum Guild eleven years and the floors had never once swallowed anybody, though she'd personally fed six people to the river under them.

"The Master requests your presence," the boy recited. "Immediately. Regarding the Delacroix matter."

Something behind Sable's ribs went very still, the way a hare goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"The Delacroix matter," she repeated.

"Yes, Warden." He still hadn't looked up. "He said you would know which keys to bring."

*Which keys.* There were keys and there were keys. The brass ones on her belt opened doors — evidence rooms, holding cells, the drawer where the guild kept its poisons labeled as medicines and its medicines labeled as poisons. But *which keys* meant the others. The flat black iron ones that opened nothing in the building at all, because what they opened was a person: the sealed cabinet where August Kavanagh's contract lived, along with everything the guild had ever learned about her, bought or bled or stolen.

"I know which keys," Sable said.

The boy left at speed. Sable finished her ten strokes, put the whetstone away, and did not look at the blade again, because looking at it felt suddenly like looking at something that had already decided.

---

The Master received her in the map room, because of course he did. He was a man who understood theater down to his bones, and the map room was his best stage: a round chamber lit by a single hanging lantern, its walls papered floor to ceiling with charts of every city the guild had ever picked apart, pins glinting like stars over streets where someone had once sold a wedding, a childhood, a face. In the center stood the table, and on the table lay a courier's satchel, cut open, empty.

"He was found at the Shambles stairs," the Master said, without turning around. "Throat opened. Professional work, but hurried. Whoever killed him wanted him quiet more than they wanted him presentable." He lifted the ruined satchel, let it dangle. "The seal was broken before our people reached him."

"The map?"

"Gone." Now he turned, and the lantern light caught his face the way it always did, leaving half of it gold and half of it dark, which Sable had privately suspected for years was not an accident of architecture. "Taken, we believe, by the person he was carrying it to."

"You believe it was being carried *to* someone." Not to the guild.

"I believe," the Master said, "that a dying man runs toward whatever he trusts. And the trail ends at a door only one person alive could have picked without leaving a mark." He slid a sheet of paper across the table. A likeness, freshly inked: August Kavanagh, twenty-six years old, sharp-boned and sharper-eyed, her hair escaping its braid in the manner the artist had chosen to suggest flight rather than portrait. It was a good likeness. It made her look frightened. August, when genuinely frightened, never looked it; she looked *interested*, which was far worse for whoever had interested her.

"She didn't kill him," Sable said.

"You can't know that."

"I trained her." Sable kept her voice level. "She couldn't cut a man's throat even now. I tried for nine years to teach her the throat-cut, and she'd do everything right, the angle, the grip, the silence, and then at the last moment her hand would shake. Not from weakness. From *accounting*. She'd stand there doing sums about the person. What they had for breakfast. Whether their mother was alive." She stopped, hearing how much she was saying, and said the rest anyway, because it was true and true things had weight. "She didn't kill your courier. Someone else followed him and she ran."

"Someone else," the Master repeated softly. "Yes. That is the interesting question, isn't it, Warden? Who else wants a map to the Moonvault badly enough to kill for it?" He folded his hands. "But that question is mine. Yours is simpler."

He told her what her task was, in the plain terms he reserved for orders he expected to be obeyed without argument. Retrieve the map. Retrieve, if possible, the reason the courier died. And erase August Kavanagh — erase meaning all of it: the memory of the map, the memory of possessing it, and since the possession had already touched her, the memory of the guild entire. A full extraction. Nine years of training, of ledgers balanced, of small rebellions tolerated and larger ones punished, all of it drawn out of her skull like thread from a spool, and what remained sold south to pay the costs of the operation.

"There will be costs," the Master observed, "but her extraction value is considerable. A talent like hers, untrained and blank — the southern houses pay beautifully for raw material. She'll have a better life, in a sense." His mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling. "Cleaner."

"And her consent?"

"Was given eleven years ago, when she signed." He tapped the table. "You've never troubled yourself with consent before, Warden. Why begin with her?"

Because, Sable thought, and did not say, *because she was a child with frostbite and I carried her in from the snow myself.* Because August had been eight years old and had signed a contract she could not read, in letters she copied out wrong twice, and Sable had stood witness with her hand on the girl's shoulder feeling the shivering slow as the fire in the counting room finally reached her, and had thought: *this is rescue.* Eleven years later she was less certain what it had been.

"I'll need the file," she said aloud. "Everything. Habit patterns, bolt-holes, the rooftop routes she favored at fourteen."

"You know her habits better than any ledger."

"That's why the ledger comes with me anyway." She held out her hand. "You taught me never to trust my own memory of a target. Memory edits itself. Paper doesn't."

The Master studied her for a long moment — long enough that she felt the study like weather against her skin — and then passed her the folder, thin as a prayer book. "Three days," he said. "After that I send Quill and the Ferret twins, and I promise you, Warden, they are not sentimental men. Whatever you find of her, find it quickly."

---

* * *

Sable spent the first day doing what she was paid for, and hated how good she was at it.

August's bolt-holes fell in order like dominoes. The loft above the chandler's on Wick Lane — abandoned, straw still warm-shaped where she'd slept. The coffin-rental cellar behind the Glasshouse — abandoned, but with three vials of soup hoarded and a blanket folded with military precision, because Sable had drilled fold-precision into her at twelve and some things outlive everything else. The widow Maren's attic, where August sometimes traded healing-memories for shelter — the widow tearful, certain August was dead or worse, and Sable standing in the doorway with her enforcer's badge visible, watching the old woman's fear and thinking: *this is what we are to them. This is the shape we make in other people's doorways.*

By dusk she had a picture, and the picture pointed downriver, toward the Shambles, toward smuggler territory, and she stood on Wick Lane in the failing light with the folder under her arm and realized she was moving at the pace of a woman who did not want to arrive.

She made herself run.

* * *

The girl had come to the guild half-dead, and Sable had been the one sent to collect her, though *collect* was the wrong word and always had been. Debt collection, the guild called it, and the ledgers were real, and little August Delacroix's mother had sold two years of the child's memories to cover a debt of eleven crowns — a bad winter's rent and a funeral. Two years of a childhood, eleven crowns. Sable had done the arithmetic a hundred times since and it never came out kinder.

But when she'd pried open the cellar door off Cooper's Row, the child hadn't been crying. She'd been sitting in the dark with her knees drawn up, and beside her, arranged with terrible care, was everything in the room she could carry: a bent spoon, a candle stub, a book with water-swollen pages. Inventory. The girl had been taking inventory, because somewhere in her eight years of life she had already learned that when powerful people come to take things away, the only defense is knowing exactly what you have.

"What's your name?" Sable had asked.

"August." Then, with the flatness of a child who has learned transactions early: "How much of me do you want?"

Sable had knelt down on the wet stone, in full view of her colleagues waiting above, and said the first true thing that came into her head: "Not today, little ledger. Today I'm just the person who carries you somewhere warm."

She had lied, technically. The extraction happened the next week. But she'd fought for the terms — argued the Master down from five years of memories to two, from the mother-years to the mother-less ones, and she'd won, and winning had felt like virtue for nearly a decade afterward, right up until tonight, standing in a dead courier's wake with a warrant for the rest.

Nine years she'd trained the girl. Escaping, climbing, reading a room, reading a lock, reading a lie. And underneath the tradecraft, quieter lessons she'd never named: that competence was armor, that rules were scaffolding you built so you wouldn't have to decide anything while afraid, that loneliness was survivable if you dressed it well. She'd taught August everything she knew about surviving the guild, and never once asked whether the girl should be in it at all.

And now the ledger had come due, and Sable was the debt collector, and the debt was everything.

* * *

She found the blood on the second night.

Not the courier's — that scene had been scrubbed by the tide hours before the guild arrived. This blood was fresher, a smear on the coping of a Shambles rooftop, low on the parapet where someone had caught themselves after a jump gone slightly wrong, and beside it, snagged on the rough stone, a single copper-brown hair. Sable crouched in the dark with the hair between finger and thumb and did what she had trained herself to do, which was read a scene the way August read a ledger.

One person here. Jump from the adjacent roof — see the scuff on the gutter opposite. Caught the parapet, scraped herself, kept moving north along the ridge line. North meant the river quarter. North meant the smuggler cellars under Tanner's Walk, if August still remembered the route Sable had shown her during the Fermentation Riots, when she was fourteen and bleeding from a knife-wound in her shoulder and laughing, actually *laughing*, as they ran the rooftops with the whole city watch baying behind them.

*You run like this,* Sable had told her that night, hauling her over a gap by the wrist, *and someday you'll run from me.*

"Prophecy," Sable said aloud to the empty rooftop, and heard how it cracked.

She should signal Quill. That was protocol; she had the whistle in her pocket; two notes and the Ferret twins would come over the chimney-tops like weather. Three hunters instead of one. The task done by morning, the map recovered, the loose end trimmed clean, and Sable's conscience filed away in the cabinet where she kept the other eleven years of things she chose not to look at directly.

Her hand didn't move toward the pocket.

Instead she sat down on the cold lead of the rooftop like an old woman at a graveside, and took out the folder, and read it by the moonlight, which was a mistake, because the folder contained a page she had written herself, seven years ago, in her own careful blocky hand:

*Subject displays anomalous retention. Extracted memories leave structural residue — subject remembers the shape of what was taken, if not the content. Notes she keeps lists of absences. Recommend monitoring. Recommend against further extraction until method improves.*

Recommend against further extraction. She had written it. She had known, seven years ago, what the guild's work did to the girl — left holes shaped like people — and she had filed the knowledge exactly where such knowledge belonged, and continued collecting debts for six more years.

Somewhere below, in the drowned dark of the river quarter, August was running with a dead man's map in her coat. Running alone, hunted by the only family she had ever been permitted, carrying holes in her memory shaped like a mother. And Sable had a whistle in one pocket and a blade on her hip and orders in her mouth like ash.

*Erase her.* The Master's voice, smooth as sealing wax. *Cleaner.*

There was a version of Sable Kestrel who did it. She knew that woman intimately; had shared a body with her for eleven years; had watched her feed six people to the river and call it accounting. That woman would find August by dawn. That woman would do it gently, if such a thing existed — a hand on the shoulder, the way it had been in the snow, and then the needle, and then the spool running out.

But the other thing the Master had said kept circling back, the thing he had almost certainly intended her to miss: *a dying man runs toward whatever he trusts.* Which meant the courier had trusted August. Which meant the map had been meant for her hands. And the Master, who understood theater down to his bones, had staged this whole night — the empty satchel, the thin folder, the three-day deadline — like a man laying a trap he needed his best hunter to walk into willingly.

Someone else wanted that map delivered. The Master wanted to know who, and wanted it known by someone whose methods were subtlety itself. Or the Master wanted August erased for reasons that had nothing to do with maps at all. Sable had served him long enough to hold both possibilities at once, the way she'd been trained to hold two truths in each hand and let the target pick which one killed them.

She put the folder away. She stood, and rolled her shoulders, and drew the short sword just enough to check the edge — ten strokes' worth of edge, honed for a task she now could not name.

Then she went north along the rooftops, alone, following a smear of blood and a single hair, moving at the pace of a woman who had not yet decided whether she was hunting or warning.

Behind her, in the counting house, the thirteenth lamp on the Master's table had begun, very quietly, to burn the wrong color — but Sable did not see it, and would not, until the night the moon turned and everything she had not yet decided came due all at once.

Below her, past the tanner's smoke, a cellar door stood open on Tanner's Walk, and lamplight spilled up the steps like an invitation, and from inside came voices — August's, unmistakable even at forty feet, and beneath it a stranger's laugh, and the deep single bark of an enormous dog.

Sable stopped at the edge of the roofline, hunter's stillness settling over her like an old coat, and listened to the sound of her student building a life she had no contract for.

Three days, the Master had said.

Sable counted her strokes, and began to climb down.
