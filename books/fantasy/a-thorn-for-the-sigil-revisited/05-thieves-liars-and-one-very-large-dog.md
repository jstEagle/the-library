## 5. Thieves, Liars, and One Very Large Dog

The cellar door had no handle. That was the first thing August noticed, standing in the rain-slick alley behind the Drowned Lantern with the map burning a hole against her ribs and two days of flight in her bones.

She knocked anyway — three quick raps, then one slow, then three again, the sequence Bramble had taught her back when she'd still been allowed to know people like him. For a long moment nothing happened except the rain finding every gap in her cloak. Then she heard the scrape of something heavy being dragged aside, and the door swung inward on a man shaped like a question mark, all stooped shoulders and wild grey hair, holding a crowbar as if it were an extension of his arm.

"Bramble," she said.

His eyes went wide, then narrow, then wide again, cycling through recognition and disbelief like a lock testing keys. "August Delacroix. In my doorway. Rain-wet and wanted, if the broadsheets are to be believed." He stepped aside just enough for her to pass. "Which they usually are."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know you're mad to come here at all." He barred the door behind her — three locks, a chain, and something that clanked in a way that suggested machinery rather than iron. "They posted your face in every counting house from here to the Salt Gate. Five hundred crowns."

"Five hundred?" She peeled back her hood and wrung rain from her hair. "I'm insulted. I balanced the Vellum Guild's ledgers for six years. I was worth at least eight hundred."

Something cracked at the corner of Bramble's mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. The cellar beyond him was a cathedral of locks: hundreds of them hung from pegs and shelves and nails driven into every inch of wall, brass and blackiron and things she didn't recognize, each one polished, each one labeled in his cramped hand. In the center of the room, a workbench littered with springs and pins sat beneath a single oil lamp.

"They exiled me for picking their vault gate," he said quietly. "You were there. You remember what they took."

"I remember." Eight years ago. Bramble had opened a door he shouldn't have — or refused to open one he should have; even now the story changed depending on who told it — and the guild had answered by selling his memories of the craft itself. Except the sale hadn't taken. Memory-thieves could strip a skill from a mind, but the hands remembered what the head forgot, and Bramble's hands had spent eight years relearning what was carved into them deeper than thought. He'd rebuilt himself pin by pin, spring by spring, in this cellar full of confiscated impossibilities.

"The map shows a route into the Moonvault," August said. "Under the palace. There are doors down there that haven't been opened in a century, and I need someone who can open anything."

Bramble looked at his own hands. They trembled slightly, the way they always did when he wasn't working them. "The Moonvault," he repeated. "Where the sigil sleeps under the king's crown. Where they'll kill us all slowly if we're caught."

"Yes."

"And you came to me because?"

"Because you're the best locksmith I ever met, and because they ruined you, and because—" she hesitated, hating the honesty even as it left her, "—because I don't think I can do this alone."

He was silent for a long moment. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the alley — enforcers' tread, heavy and unhurried — and both of them went still until the sound faded.

"You always did balance everything," Bramble said at last. "Debits and credits. Risk and reward." He turned to his workbench and began sorting tools into a leather roll with hands that had stopped trembling the moment they found work. "Tell me the reward again. Slowly."

"King Aldric is a prisoner inside his own skull. If the thirteenth moon completes its turn, whatever wears him finishes eating him, and then it starts on everyone else who dreams."

"And the risk?"

"Certain death, probably. Possibly worse."

He cinched the roll tight and slung it over his shoulder. "Good," he said. "Honest numbers at last. Let's go find the rest of your crew, since I assume you've got one."

"I've got a list," August said. "And a dog problem."

"A what?"

* * *

The dog problem turned out to be attached to Wren.

They found her in the Ragmarket, perched on an overturned crate outside a pie stall with the particular studied innocence of a child who has very recently committed a crime. She was small even for nine, all elbows and sharp chin under a mop of brown hair, and she wore three coats layered against the cold, none of them hers. Beside her sat a dog the size of a small pony — brindled grey, scarred across the muzzle, sitting with the patient dignity of a creature who had long ago stopped expecting the world to make sense.

"That's Biscuit," Wren said, before either of them spoke. "Don't ask about the name. It was a bad week."

"We need the sewers," August said. "Under the palace hill. All the way to the old cisterns."

Wren looked her up and down with the frank appraisal of a market inspector. "You're the one off the broadsheets. Five hundred crowns." She said it without fear, without even much interest, the way another child might note the weather. "You're worth more than that alive to somebody, or you wouldn't be walking around asking after sewers instead of running."

"Sharp."

"Hungry," Wren corrected. "Sharp costs extra." She held out a grimy palm, and August realized she was waiting for payment before negotiations continued. August fished out a copper; Wren looked at it, looked up at her, and waited. August added silver. The palm closed like a trap. "The cisterns flood when the river's high. The grate over the palace run is locked with guildwork — I've seen the sigil-stamp on it. And half the tunnels down there are older than the city, which means nobody knows where they go, which means I do, because I'm the only one stupid enough to have mapped them." She paused. "Also Biscuit comes or nothing comes."

"Why would I need—"

The dog stood up. The effect was roughly that of a wall deciding to become mobile. He looked at August with pale amber eyes, unblinking, and she revised her understanding of the word *dog* considerably.

"He smells guild enforcers before you do," Wren said. "Saved my neck twice. Also he's better company than most adults." She scratched the enormous jaw. "Present company pending."

"What's your price?" August asked. "For the whole way. Not the copper version."

Wren's eyes flicked away — the first honest thing she'd done. "Nobody sells me," she said. "Not to the watch, not to the guild, not to anybody. Whatever happens. You want my sewers, you take all of me that comes with them."

It was such a small sentence and so enormous underneath it that August felt it land somewhere below her ribs. A child negotiating for the one thing the world kept trying to take from her.

"Nobody sells you," August said. "That's a term of contract. I keep books; I'll write it down."

"You'd better," said Wren, and spat in her palm, and offered it, and August shook it because some agreements were older than ink.

* * *

Ossian was the hardest.

Not because he was difficult to find — the Broken Standard tavern kept a corner table reserved for him the way churches keep candles lit, out of habit and something like reverence. He sat there now, a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, grey-shot beard, forearms mapped with scars, drinking water because soldiers who drank didn't keep their pensions, and he had none to lose anyway. He was playing cards with himself and losing.

"Captain Ossian," August said. "Of the Ninth Spears."

"Not captain." He gathered the cards without looking up. "Not anything. Sit down or move along, ledger-keeper. I can smell the guild's paper on you even through the rain."

"It's burned off me. Mostly." She sat. Up close she could see them: the pale seam-scars at his temples and behind his ears, the marks of extraction. Every memory-thief knew them. Most people never saw one on a living face, because most people whose memories got sold wholesale didn't survive the hollowing. He had. Barely. Sixty men of the Ninth Spears had marched into a debt too deep for wages to cover, and the guild had bought their service year by year, memory by memory, until the unit dissolved and the men scattered like ash.

"I need a guard," she said. "Three weeks, maybe less. Dangerous road, worse destination."

"No."

"You haven't heard the pay."

"Don't care about pay." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the flat grey of a winter river. "You want to know why? Because the last time I guarded something, I don't remember doing it. There's a year of my life sitting in some guild vault with a price tag on it, and I wake up some nights reaching for it like a man reaching for a limb that isn't there. I don't guard things anymore. Things I guard end up gone, and I end up not remembering why it mattered."

The tavern noise seemed to recede around them. August set her hand flat on the table, and after a moment, deliberately, she drew the map's edge into view — just a corner of wax seal and parchment, quickly covered.

Ossian went very still. "Where did you get that."

"A dying courier. It shows the way under the palace. To the Moonvault."

"To the *sigil*." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "The thing in the king."

"You feel it too, then."

Every head in a ten-foot radius would have denied hearing them, but Ossian's hand rose unconsciously to the scar behind his ear. "Every night since the new moon. Like a hook behind my eyes. Like something counting." He laughed, short and terrible. "Thirteen turns. That's what the courier meant, isn't it? Thirteen nights of the moon dying, and then it's done eating him and starts on the rest of us."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I served the king for a year I can't remember," Ossian said, "and whatever's under his crown knew me well enough to leave a scar I can feel. So." He drained his water glass and set it down with a click. "You want a guard. What you actually want is a man with nothing left to lose walking into the place that took it. That's not a hire, ledger-keeper. That's a debt collection."

"Then consider it paid in advance," August said. "Because here's what I know: the sigil eats kings slowly, and it fears memory-thieves. And I intend to walk into its house and unmake it. When we're done, I'll be holding the keys to every vault the guild owns." She leaned forward. "Including, if I have any say in it, the shelf where they keep the year they sold out from under you."

For the first time, the flat grey of his eyes moved. Something surfaced in them — not hope exactly; hope was a luxury currency he'd stopped carrying. More like the memory of hope, which was sometimes enough to spend.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"If it comes down to saving me or saving the mission, you save the mission. I've been a hollow man for six years. I know how to travel light."

"That's a terrible condition," August said, "and I accept it, and I will break it, and you should know that going in."

Ossian stared at her. Then he laughed — a real one this time, startled out of him, big enough to turn heads at the bar. He stood, and standing, he filled the corner of the room like weather. "Gods help me," he said. "A bookkeeper with a thief's spine. Fine. Where's the rest of this disaster crew?"

* * *

They gathered that night in Bramble's cellar, because it was the only room in the capital with three exits, no windows, and walls hung with enough locks to arm a small revolution. Biscuit occupied the middle of the floor like a piece of furniture that breathed. Wren sat cross-legged on the workbench eating the pie she had absolutely not paid for, and no one asked. Ossian sharpened a knife that did not need sharpening. Bramble laid out the map between them all, weighting the corners with lock plates, and August watched four strangers study her mother's handwriting and felt something shift in the room — some faint tectonic movement, a fault line settling into a new shape.

"So," Bramble said, tracing the route. "Down through the Ragmarket drains — girl, you're sure about the third junction?"

"Flooded only if the river's up. River's low. I checked."

"—through the cisterns, past the sealed guildwork grate, which is mine, thank you for the gift, then up through the foundation galleries to the antechamber of the Moonvault itself." He looked up. "And the whole way, we're inside the reach of a thing that eats minds, hunted by the guild that trained our resident bookkeeper, with a bounty on her head and a clock ticking over all of it."

"When you put it in a list it looks much worse," Wren said.

"Lists are how you survive," August said. "Believe me."

Ossian set down his knife. "Then let's hear the list. All of it. Who we are, what we're good at, what gets us killed."

August looked around the lamplit cellar. A disgraced locksmith with rebuilt hands. A child who owned nothing and owed the world nothing. A soldier missing a year of himself. A dog named after bread. And her — a ledger-keeper with a stolen map and a dead mother's handwriting and a vial in her pocket she still hadn't told anyone about.

"Thieves, liars, and one very large dog," she said. "That's the list. That's the whole army."

Silence. Then Wren snorted pie crumbs, Ossian's laugh rolled through the stone like distant thunder, and even Bramble's mouth finally completed its journey into an actual smile.

It was Biscuit who ended it. The great head came up sharply, nostrils flaring, a growl starting deep in the chest like a landslide deciding whether to happen — and August knew that sound now, had learned it in one day, and was already moving toward the far door with the map in her hands.

"Someone's been watching the alley," she said. "We leave tonight."
