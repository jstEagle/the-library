## 6. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

Ossian felt it before the moon had even finished dying.

He was sitting on the cellar's third stair, the one that creaked, because he liked knowing when anyone came down, when the ache started. Not in his belly or his bones, where aches belonged, but in the scars — those pale, waxy seams across his forearms and throat and the left side of his ribs where the guild's knives had gone in to take what they took. The scars never hurt. Ten years of never hurting, and now, on the last night of the old moon, they burned like a brand being reheated.

He pressed his palm flat against his forearm. Under the skin, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, something answered.

"Ah," he said quietly, to no one. "There you are."

Above him, through the floorboards, the smuggler's cellar did its nightly work of pretending to be nothing: Bramble muttering over her lockpicks by candlelight, cataloguing them like a miser counting coins; Wren asleep in a nest of sacking with the dog — a vast, brindled beast called Duchess whom Wren swore was hers and who, in truth, belonged to whoever fed her last — curled around the girl like a wall built of fur and teeth. And August at the table with the map spread out under three candles, her finger tracing routes she'd traced a dozen times, as if the ink might change its mind if she watched long enough.

Ossian stayed on the stairs. He was good at staying on stairs, at doorways, at edges. It was the soldier's art, the one skill the guild hadn't been able to cut out of him: stand where you can see everything and be seen by nothing.

Except tonight something saw him back.

The burning in his scars climbed, slow and deliberate, like water rising in a flooded hold. And beneath it — beneath the burn — came the other thing, the thing he had no name for until that moment. A pull. A gentle, patient tug behind his sternum, the way a fisherman works a hook: not yanking, just reminding the fish that it was caught.

He knew what it was. He'd known since the courier died in August's arms two streets from here, whispering *thirteen turns* with blood on his teeth. He'd known since August unrolled the map and Ossian had looked at the sigil drawn at its heart — the coiled knot of lines around a crescent — and felt his own scars heat like iron near a forge.

The thirteenth moon was beginning. Turn one.

And whatever wore King Aldric's crown had just noticed him.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Bramble said.

Ossian blinked. She had come down the stairs without him hearing, which frightened him more than the burning did. Bramble moved like that when she wanted to — a small, grey woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that missed less than any lockpick he'd ever known. She sat beside him on the third step, uninvited, and offered him a heel of bread.

"Eat. You've been staring at your arm for an hour. In my experience, staring at a wound doesn't close it."

"It's not a wound." He took the bread anyway. "It's a scar."

"I know the difference. I made enough of both." She chewed her own piece thoughtfully. "Wounds are honest. Scars lie about how they got there. Which is it tonight?"

Ossian turned his forearm over. In the candle-spill from above, the scar tissue had changed. It was always pale; now it was *luminous*, faintly, the way bone looks in certain light — and along its length, in lines too fine to be coincidence, the tissue had risen into ridges. Patterns. Almost like script.

Almost, he thought, like a map.

Bramble went very still beside him. Then she reached out — asking permission first with her eyes, which was more courtesy than Ossian had gotten from most officers he'd served — and touched the edge of the scar with one fingertip.

"It's warm," she said. "Like skin after sunburn." Her voice had gone careful, the way it did around delicate mechanisms. "Ossian. Has this ever happened before?"

"No."

"Do you know what it means?"

He laughed, short and ugly. "I know what I know, Bramble, and what I know is mostly holes. I was in the king's guard. I can tell you my enlistment date, my training sergeant's name, the color of my first uniform. I cannot tell you one single thing about the year I actually stood post at the palace. Not one meal. Not one fellow guard's face. Not one winter morning. The guild sold that year off me in vials, and I signed the papers myself because the alternative was watching my whole unit go to debtors' prison." He flexed his hand. "Ten years I've treated those scars like a receipt. Proof of payment. Tonight they feel less like a receipt and more like a summons."

Bramble studied the ridges a long moment. "They look like writing," she said at last, "but not any alphabet I know. And I know a lot of alphabets. Locks are just alphabets you open with your hands." She paused. "August should see this."

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because she's got a map in her mother's handwriting and a guild wanting her head and a child sleeping upstairs, and she does not need an old soldier's haunted arm added to the ledger tonight." He said it flatly, but Bramble heard what was under it, the way she heard tumblers fall.

"She needs to know what's hunting us," Bramble said gently. "Even if it's wearing your skin."

* * *

He didn't tell August that night. He told himself it was strategy. He told himself the ridges might fade by morning, that it might be nothing, that a man who remembered almost nothing of his most important year had no business claiming knowledge of anything.

The lies kept poorly. By midnight the burning had spread to the scar on his throat, and when he swallowed, he tasted copper that wasn't there. And with the taste came fragments — not memories, exactly. Memories were solid things; these were smoke. A corridor of white stone. The smell of lilies and hot metal. A voice saying, in tones of absolute command, *you will not speak of what you saw*, and his own voice answering, steady, obedient: *I will not speak of what I saw.*

But he had seen something. That was the horror of it. The guild had taken the memory, but the taking had left a shape — the way a removed painting leaves a paler rectangle on a sun-faded wall. Something had happened in a white stone corridor, something important enough to be ordered silent, and Ossian had carried the silence for ten years without knowing there was anything inside it.

Now the silence was leaking.

Near dawn he gave up on sleep and climbed to the cellar's high grate, the one that opened onto an alley behind a chandler's shop, and looked up at the sky. The new moon was there, technically — a black coin occluding stars, visible only by the ring of dim light the sun threw past its edge. Invisible moon. Hollow moon.

*Thirteen turns*, the courier had said. Thirteen nights of a moon you couldn't see, and then the map's crescent would complete itself, and whatever the sigil had been eating all these years would finish its meal.

"Can't sleep either?"

Wren dropped onto the crate beside him so suddenly he nearly put a hand to his knife. The girl was nine or ten — nobody knew, including Wren — all elbows and sharp eyes and hair she cut herself with a stolen razor. Duchess flowed up after her and arranged herself across both their feet like a drawbridge closing.

"Go back to bed," Ossian said.

"The dog heard your heart going funny. She woke me." Wren scratched Duchess's ears, entirely matter-of-fact. "She hears everything. She heard Bramble's candle stop moving an hour ago, which means Bramble fell asleep sitting up again, which means tomorrow she'll be cross, so don't sit next to her at breakfast."

Ossian almost smiled. Almost. "You should be asleep."

"So should you." Wren tipped her head back and looked at the sky with the frank, appraising stare she gave to locked things. "That's turn one, isn't it? August thinks I don't listen, but I listen. Thirteen turns till the bad thing finishes. So that's—" her fingers flicked through the arithmetic, "—twelve more sleeps. Thirteen minus one. Twelve."

"You count fast."

"Wren counts everything." She said it simply, without bravado. Counting was survival; every street kid learned it before letters. "Twelve sleeps, Ossian. Are we going to be ready?"

He looked down at her — this scrap of a girl who had already decided, somewhere in the last few days, that this ragged band was hers, that these people were worth her twelve sleeps — and the pull behind his sternum tightened, and his scars burned, and he understood with sudden terrible clarity that he could not answer her honestly unless he told her everything.

Unless he told August everything.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "We're going to be ready. Go wake August. Bring her down. And bring the map."

Wren vanished. Duchess lingered a heartbeat, studying Ossian with grave amber eyes, then followed.

* * *

August came down with the map rolled under her arm and sleep still clinging to her like cobwebs. She lit the table lamp, unrolled the parchment, weighted the corners with candlesticks, and only then looked at him properly — the look she gave ledgers, he'd begun to realize, the look that found the discrepancy.

"Show me," she said.

He stripped off his shirt. It cost him something; he was not a modest man, but he was a private one, and the scars were the public record of the worst bargain of his life. August circled him slowly, lamp raised, and he watched her face as she read the raised ridges along his forearm, his ribs, his throat.

"These weren't here yesterday."

"They rose tonight. When the new moon turned." He held out his arm under the light. "Look closer. Bramble says they look like writing."

August bent low. He saw her breath catch.

"This line here —" her fingertip hovered without touching — "it matches the border of the map. Exactly. This is the sigil, Ossian. Or part of it. It's written on you."

"On all of me, maybe. Just visible now." He pulled his shirt back on, needing the weight of it. "August, I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything."

She straightened. Waited.

"When I was twenty-two, I took the king's coin. Palace guard. I served one year, and then my unit's debts came due — gambling losses, mostly, our sergeant was a fool with dice — and the Vellum Guild bought what we owed in exchange for what we carried. Memories. They took mine in a single session, a whole year, top to bottom. Everyone assumed it was mercy pricing. A year is cheap compared to a life." His voice stayed level. He'd practiced level for ten years. "Tonight I think the price wasn't fair because the goods weren't ordinary. I think the guild didn't buy my year to sell it. I think they bought it to bury it."

August had gone very still. "Why would they—"

"Because I stood guard somewhere I shouldn't have. Because I saw something in a white stone corridor and was ordered silent, and I obeyed, and I don't remember what I'm keeping quiet." The burning flared as he spoke, as if the words themselves were keys turning. "August — the sigil knows I'm here. It's been pulling at me since the moon turned. Whatever it is, some piece of it is stitched into me. Maybe into every soldier whose year was bought. We weren't paid for, we were *cleaned*. Erased witnesses. And now the erasure is starting to come undone, and I don't know yet whether what comes back will help us or destroy us."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the grate. Neither of them moved.

Then August did the thing that Ossian would remember, afterward, longer than almost anything else from that year: she picked up the map, folded it once, and set it aside. Away from the center of the table. Making room.

"Sit down," she said. "Tell me about the corridor. Every fragment, however small. Smells, sounds, the order things happened in. Memory has a grammar, Ossian — I've handled thousands of them, traded them, catalogued them, and a stolen memory always leaves grammar behind even when it takes the words." Her eyes were bright and fierce and tired. "You're not a haunted soldier to me. You're a source document. And we have twelve sleeps to read you."

He sat. He began with the smell of lilies and hot metal, and as he spoke, the ridges on his forearm warmed and shifted, very slightly, like a lock feeling for the right key.

Neither of them noticed, in the lamplight, that the newest ridge — fine as a hair, fresh as a wound — had appeared along the inside of his wrist sometime in the night.

It was shaped like a crescent.

Turn one had begun, and something far below the palace had just written its first mark on the ledger of Ossian's skin.
