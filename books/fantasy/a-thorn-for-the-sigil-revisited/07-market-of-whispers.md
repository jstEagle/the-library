## 7. Market of Whispers

The Market of Whispers opened only on nights when the fog came down off the river thick enough to drown a scream.

August had been there twice before in her years with the Vellum Guild — once to deliver a crate of memories she'd been told never to open, and once to collect a debt from an oracle who'd grown ambitious about interest. Both times she had come as guild property, wearing the guild's grey sash, and both times the market's keepers had looked through her as though she were furniture that happened to walk.

Tonight she wore no sash. Tonight she was furniture no longer; she was meat on the hoof, and every stall along the drowned alley of Candlewick Row knew it.

She felt their attention like hands.

*She has something,* the whispers said — not with mouths, but the way the market itself spoke, a pressure against the ear that arrived already inside the skull. *A runner off the guild's leash. A thief with a map under her shirt and grief in her pockets. Look how carefully she walks. Look how little she blinks.*

August kept walking. The fog curled around her ankles like a cat deciding whether she was worth the trouble of pouncing.

Three days since the rooftops. Three days since the courier's blood had cooled on her floorboards and the guild enforcers had come through her door behind him, and she had gone out the window with nothing but the map, the clothes she stood in, and the single vial she'd stolen from the counting house — the one she still hadn't drunk, because drinking it would mean admitting what it was and why she'd taken it.

Three days of Bramble's cellar, of Wren teaching Ossian which grates in the sewer grid lifted without noise, of a very large dog named Duchess who had appointed herself August's shadow and growled at anyone who stood between them. Three days of studying a map annotated in her dead mother's handwriting, and getting nowhere, because knowing *where* the Moonvault was meant nothing when she didn't know *what* slept inside it.

The thirteenth moon. Thirteen turns. *Thirteen turns*, the courier had said, with his last breath, pressing the wax seal into her palms like a man handing off a lit fuse.

She needed answers. And there was exactly one place in the capital where answers could be bought by someone who couldn't afford coin.

The price here was always paid in kind.

* * *

The oracle's stall sat at the bottom of Candlewick Row, past the memory-brokers with their velvet trays of vials, past the dream-smokers huddled over brass pipes, past the woman who sold secondhand lullabies by the spoonful. It was less a stall than a wound in the wall of the alley — a doorway that hadn't existed yesterday and wouldn't exist tomorrow, framed in driftwood and hung with dozens of small bells that made no sound at all.

Inside, an old woman sat behind a table of black glass, and the table was full of water that did not ripple.

"Vellum Guild," the oracle said. Not a question. Her eyes were the pale grey of things that lived in caves and had stopped needing light. "You've cut your sash off. That leaves a scar, girl. Guilds are like mothers that way."

"My mother is dead," August said.

"Mm." The oracle turned one hand over the black water, and images moved beneath its surface: rooftops in rain, a courier falling, wax cracking under a thumb. "So I see. Sit."

"I'm not trading anything yet. First I want to know—"

"You want to know if I can be trusted." The old woman smiled, and her teeth were very white and very even, the teeth of someone who had never once needed to chew. "No. Of course you can't trust me. I am an oracle in a market built out of stolen things. Trust is the first thing we sell and the last thing anyone gets back. Sit down anyway."

August sat. The stool was warm, which she decided not to think about.

"The map shows me the Moonvault," she said. "It doesn't tell me what's in it. I need to know what I'm walking toward."

"And what will you give?"

"That depends on what you're selling."

The oracle laughed — a dry, papery sound, like pages turning in a closed book. "Oh, you *are* guild-trained. All right. Straight trade, then, since you want to pretend this is commerce. You show me your question. I'll tell you what answering it costs. Then you decide whether the answer is worth more than the piece of yourself I take for it. Fair?"

Fair was a strange word down here. August nodded anyway.

She unrolled the map across the black glass, careful to keep her palm over the annotations in her mother's hand. She didn't know why. Some instinct older than strategy told her not to let this woman read those lines if she could help it.

The water beneath the glass shivered. The bells in the doorway all leaned inward without ringing.

"Oh," the oracle said softly.

"What?"

"Oh, *girl.* Do you know what this is?" Her finger hovered over the vault's marked chamber, not quite touching the parchment. "Do you know what sleeps under the palace, ticking away inside a crown like a worm inside an apple? Because the map knows. Maps remember better than people do. This one has been *waiting* for someone to carry it."

"It's a seal," August said. "Everyone says it's a seal. The Sigil of Aldric, set into his crown at his coronation, binding him to—"

"To *what?*" The oracle leaned forward, and up close her cave-grey eyes were full of tiny moving lights, like fish seen from a bridge at night. "Finish the sentence. Everyone says *binding him to the throne*, or *binding him to the land*, or some other pretty story priests tell so kings can sleep. Finish it."

August finished it. "Binding him to the guild's ledger. To the debt. That's what the rebels say — that the sigil binds the king to a contract written before he was born."

"They're wrong too, but they're closer than the priests." The oracle straightened, and the temperature in the little room dropped, the way it does when a fire goes out in another room of the house. "Here is your answer, and then we'll discuss payment, and you'll wish we'd discussed payment first. Are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"The thing in the crown is not a seal. Seals close. Seals keep things *out*. This keeps nothing out." The oracle's voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. "It is a parasite. Old — older than the capital, older than the river, maybe older than the moon-count it's riding. It settles into a king the way a tick settles into a hound. It eats him slowly. First his sleep, then his judgment, then his years, then his name. It has eaten nine kings of this city, one after another, and each time it finishes a meal it arranges the succession so that the next crowned head bends conveniently low. Your King Aldric is nearly done. Lucid minutes, then hours of hollow. By the thirteenth turn he'll be an empty skin wearing a crown, and the sigil will step out of him and into whatever comes next."

The fog outside seemed to press closer against the doorway. Somewhere up the row, a dream-smoker began to cry without waking.

"How do you kill it?" August asked.

"You don't. It's been killed. Kings have been stabbed, poisoned, burned on pyres with the crown still on their heads — the sigil simply moves to the next crown in the line of succession, patient as arithmetic. You don't kill it." The oracle smiled again, and this time there was something in the smile that might have been pity, or might have been appetite wearing pity's coat. "You *unbind* it. There is a difference, and the difference is you."

"Me."

"A thief of memories. A reader of ledgers. Someone who can put her fingers into a mind and take a stitch out without tearing the cloth." The oracle tapped the map, right beside August's shielding palm. "That is why this exists. That is why a dying man ran it through half the city to a Vellum Guild bookkeeper instead of to a general or a priest. The sigil cannot be cut out by force — force feeds it, pain is seasoning, violence is wine. But it can be *unpicked*. Slowly. Stitch by stitch. By someone who knows how minds hold their seams. There may be four people alive with that talent, and the guild owns three of them."

"And the fourth?"

"You're sitting on my stool."

August's mouth had gone dry. "Then the map—"

"—was bait," the oracle agreed pleasantly. "Or a summons. Or both; the distinction matters less than you'd hope. Something wanted you here, August Delacroix. Something chose the messenger who died on your floor, and chose the route he ran, and chose the cellar full of thieves now keeping you company. Whether that something loves you or farms you, I genuinely could not say. Oracles are honest that way. We sell sight, not sense."

The bells in the doorway stirred, soundless.

"Now," the oracle said, "payment."

* * *

They negotiated the way two knife-fighters negotiate, circling, testing reach.

The oracle wanted the map. August refused. The oracle wanted the vial in August's pocket — and August went cold all over, because she had told no one about the vial, had barely admitted it to herself, and yet the old woman's grey eyes slid to her coat with the certainty of a compass finding north.

"No," August said.

"It's a strong vintage. Grief always is. I could taste it from across the row."

"No."

"Then something else of equivalent weight." The oracle spread her hands over the black water. "Answers of this depth aren't bought with trivia, bookkeeper. Not a face. Not a Tuesday. I need something with roots. Give me a memory of your mother."

The words landed in August's chest like a dropped stone landing in a well — a long time falling, then deep water closing over it.

Because that was the cruelty of the market, and the truth of the theme her whole life had been quietly teaching her: survival always costs something kept. Everything else was negotiable. Coin could be re-earned, doors could be relocked, names could be remade. But a memory given away didn't fade. It simply transferred. Somewhere in this city tonight, someone else would own her mother's laugh, and she would own the hole where it used to be.

She thought of the counting house. Of the ledgers she balanced, column after column of lives traded in tidy sums. *Debit: one childhood. Credit: six months of bread.* She had told herself, all those years, that she only kept the books. She had never signed anyone's memory away herself.

There was a first time for everything, and the market made sure of it.

"Which one," August heard herself ask, and hated how steady her voice came out.

The oracle considered her for a long moment. The lights swam in her eyes. "You have many," she said. "I can see them stacked like cordwood. The morning she braided your hair and called you her little ledger-keeper. The night she sang the counting song and got the numbers wrong on purpose to make you correct her." A pause. "And the last one. The fever. Her voice going thin. You were eleven."

"Which one costs least?"

"The braid."

"Then the braid," said August, and reached for it before she could stop reaching, because she knew with absolute certainty that if she gave herself one hour to reconsider, she would run, and running meant Aldric's hollow face at the thirteenth turn, and a city of sleepwalkers, and a parasite fat on royal years arranging the world's next meal.

The oracle extended one hand, palm up. "Look at the water. Hold the memory in front of you, the way you'd hold a lamp. Don't push it at me. Just... stop holding it shut."

It was like unclenching a fist she hadn't known she'd been making for twenty years. The memory rose — lamplight, the smell of tallow and lavender soap, her mother's clever fingers dividing her hair into three strands, *one for what was, one for what is, one for what's coming*, the little joke they had, the braid as a ledger —

And the oracle took it.

There was no pain. That was the worst part. There was simply a moment when the lamplight was there, warm and golden and hers, and then it wasn't — plucked clean out of her like a thread drawn from fabric, and the fabric didn't tear, it just had a place where something used to be woven in. August knew, intellectually, that her mother had once braided her hair. The knowledge sat in her head like a caption under a missing painting. *Here, once, was joy.*

Her eyes were wet. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she'd balance a column: find the error, correct it, move on.

"Well?" she said.

The oracle was quiet for longer than August liked. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost its market-hawker polish entirely.

"The sigil fears you," she said. "Not dislikes. Not avoids. *Fears.* It has eaten nine kings, and in nine reigns it has never once been unbound, and the reason is that memory-thieves are rare, and brave ones are rarer, and the guild has spent two centuries making sure its readers stay leashed. It eats memory, child. It is made of eaten memory. And you people — you can go into a mind and take things *back*." She leaned across the black water. "When it looks at you, it sees the one creature in the world that can starve it stitch by stitch. It will bargain. It will flatter. It will offer you everything you've ever lost, wrapped in gold. And on the twelfth turn, when it understands you won't be bought, it will try to eat *you* whole. Remember that. Write it on the inside of your skull in letters you can't lose."

"Thank you," August said, because her mother had raised her to say it, and the memory of being raised was still hers. Barely.

"One more thing." The oracle's hand shot out and caught August's wrist — fast, far faster than an old woman should move — and her cave-grey eyes fixed on the pocket where the stolen vial sat. "Whatever you took from the counting house. Drink it soon, or give it away, but stop carrying it like a stone you're saving to drown yourself with. Dead weight sinks the swimmer. And you—" her grip tightened, "—you are going to need to swim."

She released August's wrist and turned back to her black water, and the audience was clearly over.

* * *

Outside, the fog had thickened to wool. August walked back up Candlewick Row past the stalls that whispered about her, and found she no longer cared what they whispered, because she now knew the shape of the thing that hunted her king, and it was worse than a seal and simpler than a devil: it was a hunger with a crown.

*Bait. Or a summons. Or both.*

Someone had chosen her. Someone had spent a courier's life and a dead mother's handwriting to put a memory-thief on the road to the Moonvault. And the oracle's warning rang in her ears alongside the other thing, the practical thing, the thing that would matter in the next few days:

*The guild owns three readers. You're the fourth.*

Which meant somewhere behind her, in the grey halls of the Vellum Guild, her former masters had already done this arithmetic. They knew what she was. They knew what she could do. And they would send someone to retrieve her who knew exactly how she thought, exactly how she fought, and exactly where she'd been trained to be weak.

August pulled her collar up and turned toward Bramble's cellar, and the fog swallowed her footsteps one by one, and she did not see the tall figure detach from the shadows of the bell-stall behind her — a figure who had watched the entire transaction, and who now moved to follow her home with a blade on her hip and an old student's name in her mouth.

*Sable.*

The debt collector had found her debtor at last.
