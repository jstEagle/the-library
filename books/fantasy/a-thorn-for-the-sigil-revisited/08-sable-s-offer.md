## 8. Sable's Offer

Sable had been following her student for three days before she decided to stop pretending it was a hunt.

It wasn't a hunt. A hunt ended in blood or chains, and Sable had talked herself out of both twice already — once on the rooftops above Cordwainer Street, where August had crossed a gap no sane person would jump and Sable had let her go rather than follow; once outside the smuggler's cellar near the river gate, where August had gone in with three strangers and come out with something that looked dangerously like hope. Sable could have taken her at either moment. Two enforcers, maybe one. Quick, clean, the way the guild preferred its erasures.

She hadn't called the others in.

That was the problem, she told herself as she waited in the shadow of the tallow-chandler's awning across from August's borrowed room. That was the flaw in her professional armor, rusted through in exactly one place, shaped exactly like a girl who had once fallen asleep over ledgers with ink on her cheek and woken to find Sable had draped her own coat over her shoulders.

The lamp behind the second-floor window went out.

Sable counted to two hundred. Then she crossed the street, took the drainpipe that August had never noticed was climbed before, and let herself through the window that Bramble — because who else — had already picked so carefully that the latch showed no marks at all.

She was inside before August finished turning around.

* * *

The room was a monk's cell with better light. One bed, unmade. One table, scarred with candle wax. The map was not visible anywhere, which meant August had hidden it well, which meant Sable didn't need it anymore. She'd read enough of it during the courier's dying minutes to know what it showed. Thirteen moons drawn in a ring like beads on a string. A staircase going down beneath the palace into a darkness the cartographer had inked black.

"You've gotten slow," Sable said. "Three days to notice a tail."

August had a knife out from somewhere — she always had kept one in the small of her back, against all guild doctrine, against every lecture Sable had ever given her about the sanctity of concealment. Her knuckles were white on the grip, but her voice came out level. "You let me notice you."

"I wanted a conversation."

"Enforcers don't have conversations."

"This one does." Sable held out her hands, empty, palms up. An old gesture between them. In the training yard, ten years ago, it had meant: *no blades, just lessons.* "Sit down, August."

"I'll stand."

Of course she would. Sable almost smiled. She lowered herself onto the room's single stool instead, unhurried, letting the silence do what silences did best. August stood by the window with the moonlight cutting her face in half — half lit, half dark, the way she had always lived.

"The courier's name was Merrin," Sable said. "You knew him?"

"I knew his handwriting." August's jaw tightened. "He used to deliver my mother's letters."

"Mm." Sable had suspected as much. It explained why Merrin had run toward August's rooms instead of away, why he'd spent his last breath pressing sealed paper into her hands instead of any of the hundred safer hiding places in this city. Dying men choose their heirs carefully. "The guild has your likeness posted in every district office south of the canal. By tomorrow morning it'll be north of it too. Master Corvane wants you found before the next turn of the moon."

"Then he should have sent someone faster."

"He sent me."

"And I'm supposed to be flattered?"

"He sent me," Sable repeated, quietly, "because he believes I will do what needs doing. And I need you to understand, girl, that I am standing here in this room instead of six of my colleagues precisely because I want there to be another way. So hear the offer before you decide how brave you're going to be."

August didn't sit. But she stopped edging toward the door, and that was as much concession as Sable had ever gotten from her, even in the old days.

"Amnesty," Sable said. "Full amnesty. Your name comes off the boards tonight. Your debts are struck from the ledger — all of them, including the ones you don't know you carry. You surrender the map, unopened to anyone but the master, and you go back to your desk in the counting house. Impossible things, balanced nightly. No questions asked. No memory of the last week taken from you, though that is also on the table if you'd prefer a cleaner conscience."

For a long moment August said nothing. Sable watched her weigh it — watched the exact thing happen that she had hoped wouldn't and known would. Because August had been happy in the counting house, once, in her careful and lonely fashion. She had loved the ledgers the way some people love scripture. Order. Balance. Every impossible thing accounted for.

"You said I have debts I don't know I carry," August said.

"Everyone does."

"What debt? Mine specifically."

Sable looked at her, and for one treacherous second considered lying. It would be easier. It might even work. But August Delacroix had been raised on ledgers, and she could smell an unbalanced column through solid stone.

"There are entries in your account older than your apprenticeship," Sable said. "Payments made on your behalf before you ever signed anything. What they purchased isn't recorded in any ledger you've seen. If you come home, you can read them yourself."

Something moved behind August's eyes — quick and hungry and frightened, a girl at a locked door hearing her mother's voice on the other side. Sable watched her slam that door shut with visible effort.

"No," August said.

"August—"

"The map shows the thirteenth moon." August's voice shook, and she let it, which was new; she'd learned control from Sable and was now spending it like coin, deliberately, to make Sable feel every tremor. "It shows a staircase under the palace. It's annotated in handwriting I'd know anywhere, in a woman who's been dead eleven years, and you're telling me the price of my life is handing it to the man whose guild killed the last person who carried it. Merrin bled out on my floor, Sable. He was *flayed* with something that left burns, not cuts. Whatever your master serves, it isn't the Vellum Guild. It wears the Vellum Guild like a coat."

The silence stretched. Outside, somewhere, a drunk was singing badly, three streets over.

"You want the truth," Sable said at last. "Fine. Here is truth, and may it rot in both our ears. The master doesn't serve the guild. The guild serves him, and he serves the thing under the capital, and yes — willingly. Freely. With signatures and ceremony and a devotion I have seen in no temple in this city. He calls it the Sigil. He calls it patient. He says when the thirteenth moon completes its turn, everything we have ever traded in — every stolen memory, every impossible thing — becomes small change. Pocket money." Her mouth was dry. "He intends to feed it the whole city, August. Every mind in the capital, poured out like wine at a wedding."

August had gone very pale. "And you're still wearing his colors."

"I'm wearing the only colors I have!" It came out louder than she intended, and Sable mastered it, dragged it back down to a whisper, because the walls here were thin and her colleagues were thorough. "I was sold to the guild at nine years old. Do you understand? Everything I am — every skill, every scar, every principle I pretend to hold — the guild built it. Corvane built it. When I ask myself who I would even be without them, I get nothing. Silence. An empty ledger." She laughed, short and bitter. "You of all people should understand. You keep accounts for a living. Tell me what I'm owed if I burn it all down."

"You're owed the truth about your own life," August said softly. "Same as everyone else in this city. That's the only currency worth anything right now."

"It isn't enough."

"It's the only thing on offer."

They looked at each other across the little room, and Sable felt thirty years of craft crumble around her like wet plaster, because the argument was lost and she had known it was lost since the rooftop over Cordwainer Street, and she was still standing here, going through the motions, buying time from herself.

"Last chance," she said hoarsely. "Give me the map. I won't even take it to Corvane. I'll burn it. We'll say it was destroyed in Merrin's flight, and you vanish, and I lose my bounty and sleep fine, and you—" She gestured at the shabby room, the strangers downstairs, the whole improbable new life August was assembling out of thieves and children and broken soldiers. "You keep this. Whatever this is."

"You haven't asked what the map leads to," August said. "Not once in this entire conversation. Doesn't that worry you?"

"I know what it leads to. The Moonvault."

"Then you know the king is under there. Alive. Being eaten." August stepped forward, into the moonlight, and Sable saw that she had been crying at some point recently and hadn't bothered to hide it, which meant it had been strategic, which meant her student was learning weapons Sable had never taught her. "Corvane feeds it kings. How long before a king isn't enough? How long before it's a city, and you're standing at the top of that staircase holding the door open because he built you, and you can't imagine being anything else?" Her voice cracked. "You taught me to read ledgers, Sable. Read this one. The guild's whole balance sheet ends in a debit nobody survives. There's no version of this where you stay loyal and the city keeps existing."

"You don't know that."

"Neither do you. That's my point."

Sable rose from the stool. It happened without her quite deciding it — the body moving ahead of the mind, the old training taking over. Her hand drifted to her blade, not drawing it, resting. August's knife came up.

"Don't," August said.

"I'm not going to fight you."

"You drew first."

"I adjusted my belt." But they both knew what it had looked like, and something between them tore a little, quietly, at the sound of it.

Then Sable did draw — not to strike, but to show. She reversed the blade and offered it, hilt-first, across the space between them.

August stared at it. "What is this?"

"A demonstration," Sable said, "that I can't do my job tonight. Take it. If Corvane sends anyone else, they won't offer you a conversation first. They'll come in numbers and they won't be me. You have until the next turn of the moon — eight days, perhaps nine. After that, my orders change and so do my options, and I will not promise you what I'll do when they do." She set the blade on the table between them and stepped back. "That's not a threat. That's a deadline. Learn the difference, because it's the only honest thing I have left to give you."

August didn't touch the blade. "Why give me eight days? Why not drag me back tonight and collect whatever they're paying?"

"Because twenty years ago a woman named Maren Delacroix walked into the counting house with a newborn and no money," Sable said, "and I was the one working the intake desk, and I looked at that child and falsified my first ledger entry to keep her off the auction rolls." She saw August go still — truly still, the stillness of a held breath, a snared animal. "You were that child. Someone paid your debts before you could walk, girl, and I have spent twenty years not asking who, or why, or what they bought. Maybe the map answers that. Maybe that's why your mother's hand is on it. But if you hand it to Corvane, those entries stay sealed forever, and so does hers, and so will yours."

The room was very quiet. Down in the street the drunk had stopped singing.

"That's not fair," August whispered.

"No," Sable agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is." She moved to the window, and paused there, one boot on the sill, the night air cold against her face. "Eight days. Don't waste them on sleep."

"Sable." August's voice caught her at the edge. "Your ledger. The empty one — the one where you couldn't see yourself without the guild." She was holding Sable's blade now, offering it back, and her eyes were bright and fierce and terribly young. "There's room in mine. If it comes to it. There's always been room in mine."

Sable looked at the offered blade for a long moment. Then she shook her head once, sharply, like a woman refusing wine she desperately wanted, and dropped out the window into the dark.

* * *

She walked for hours afterward, through districts where her likeness meant safety and districts where it meant knives, until she fetched up without planning to at the counting house, its windows dark, its doors locked against her key.

Inside, she lit one lamp and opened the master's private ledger — the one she was forbidden, the one whose lock had never actually troubled her — and turned to the oldest pages, and began, finally, to look for her own name.

She found it at the bottom of an entry dated thirty-one years ago. Not as buyer. Not as seller.

As collateral.

And beside it, in Corvane's neat, fanatical hand, a note she read four times before her eyes would accept it:

*When the thirteenth moon turns, all accounts settle. The collateral comes due.*

Below that, a list. Nine names. The names of every enforcer in the guild.

Hers was first.

Sable closed the ledger very gently, the way one closes a coffin, and sat alone in the lamplight understanding, at last, exactly which side of the ledger she was on — and how few nights remained to move herself, before the moon finished its thirteenth turn and the great balancing began.
