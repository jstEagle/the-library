## 9. Under the Capital

The grate came up easier than Wren expected, and that was the first bad sign.

Anything under the capital that opened easy was either a gift or a mouth. She'd learned that at seven years old, the night she'd followed a sweet-smelling man into a cellar because he promised hot bread, and Bramble had hauled her back out by the collar of her coat with two fingers and a curse. Gifts didn't exist down here. Only mouths.

She crouched at the edge of the hole, rain running off her hood in threads, and looked down at the iron rungs slick with black water.

"Problem?" Ossian asked behind her. His voice was low the way it always was, like he was used to talking in places that listened.

"No problem." Wren spat over the edge to hear how far it fell. The splash came back late and flat. "Forty feet to water. Maybe fifty."

"Water?"

"Drowned levels. Whole quarter of the old city's under there. When they built the palace on top, they just — left it." She shrugged, trying to make it sound boring, like she hadn't spent half her life down here and half of that terrified. "Sewers feed into it. Catacombs above it if we're lucky, below it if we're not."

Bramble peered past her, one hand pressed to his side where the guild's blade had caught him two nights ago. He'd cleaned it, bound it, lied about it twice. In the lamplight his face was the color of old tallow. "I was exiled for less than this," he muttered. "Breaking *into* the palace foundations. At least when I broke out I had the decency to do it from the inside."

"You'll want to save your jokes," Wren said. "They don't keep well underwater."

August knelt beside her at the lip of the shaft. Up close, in the grey pre-dawn light, she looked like she hadn't slept since the courier died in her doorway — which, Wren thought, was probably true, and also fair. But August looked at her the way nobody else did: like Wren was the expert in the room. Like the smallest person here might be the only one who could get them through.

"Take us down," August said.

Wren took a breath, swung onto the first rung, and started climbing into the dark.

* * *

The drowned city smelled like rust and old prayers.

Their lamps found walls carved with saints whose faces the water had eaten smooth, niches stacked floor to ceiling with bones gone soft as chalk, archways that had been doors once, in streets where people had lived before the palace needed their ground more than it needed them. The water came to Ossian's chest and to Wren's chin, which meant she walked on her toes most of the time, arms up, lamp held high, feeling with her boots for the drop-offs she knew by memory and hoped still matched.

"Left here," she said. "Then straight till you hit the saint with no head. Don't touch anything that looks like a handle."

"What looks like handles down here?" Sable asked. She'd come with them three days ago, guild keys and guild blade and an expression nobody trusted yet, least of all Wren. Enforcers didn't defect. Enforcers got sent. That was what Wren believed, and she kept her distance accordingly, swimming a careful orbit around the tall woman like she was something with teeth.

"Coffin lids," Wren said. "Some of them are rigged. Old families didn't want gravers in their tombs, so they paid locksmiths to make the dead into traps." She glanced back at Bramble. "No offense."

"Some taken," Bramble said. "That was honest work, once. Before the guild made it dishonest."

They waded on. The water deepened, then shallowed as the passage climbed toward the catacomb shelf, and Wren let herself breathe again. This part she knew. Above the flood line the air went dry and dusty and the dark changed texture — closer, warmer, like being inside something's throat instead of its belly. Niches full of bones gave way to niches full of nothing; the poor quarters had been buried without bodies, just names scratched into plaster, thousands of them, row after row, the debt-ledgers of a city that had never counted its own people as anything but entries.

Wren ran her fingers along the names as she passed, the way she always did. A habit. A superstition. *You were here,* she told them silently. *Someone read you.*

"Wren." August's voice, quiet, from the rear. "How much farther to the foundation galleries?"

"Hour, maybe. If the siphon gate's still closed. If it's open—" she made a gesture like water rising, "—we swim or we turn back."

"We don't turn back," Ossian said. Not harsh. Just certain. He'd been saying versions of it all day. Turn seven was four nights off now and everyone felt it, though nobody said it: the clock under the world was turning, and somewhere beneath the palace a king was losing another piece of himself to the thing wearing him.

Wren nodded and kept walking, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the dust.

* * *

The trap was a corridor that shouldn't have been easy.

That was how Wren knew, twenty steps too late, standing on flagstones worn smooth and dry and clean, that she'd made a mistake. Nothing down here stayed clean. Clean meant maintained. Maintained meant somebody wanted this path walkable — for somebodies exactly like them.

"Stop." Her voice cracked out sharper than she meant. "Everyone stop. Don't move your feet."

Five people froze mid-stride in a corridor of pale stone, lamps held high. Ahead of them, the gallery stretched thirty feet to an arched gate of black bronze — the foundation gate, the first real door to the vault approach. Behind them, the way they'd come. Underneath them—

Underneath, Wren saw it now that she made herself look: hairline seams in the flagstones, laid in circles, concentric, like ripples. Pressure plates. Dozens. And along the walls, set into the mortar every few feet, little bronze spouts shaped like open mouths.

"Oil?" Ossian guessed.

"Worse." Wren's mouth had gone dry. "It's a kill-floor. Old royal design. Weight holds the plates down fine while you're standing still — it's the *moving* that kills you. Step wrong and the mouths open, and what comes out burns worse than oil. Guild called it widowswater. I've seen what it leaves." She swallowed. "There's a sequence. There's always a sequence. Safe stones. You just have to know which."

"And do you?" Sable asked.

Wren looked at the floor of circles and seams and mouths, and the truth sat in her throat like a stone: she'd heard about kill-floors. She'd heard smugglers brag about crossing them. She'd never actually crossed one. She'd led five people forty feet underground on a child's map-memory and a pickpocket's confidence, and now they were standing on the jaws of it, and the water behind them was rising — she could hear it, faint and steady, the siphon gate groaning open somewhere far below, the drowned city drinking the river like it did every spring thaw, except this year they were inside its stomach.

"I can find it," she said. "The sequence. It's weight and rhythm. If I go slow—"

"If you go slow and you're wrong, you die fast," Bramble said. He eased himself down against the wall, one arm wrapped around his side, and Wren saw fresh red seeping between his fingers and felt her stomach drop. "Girl. Listen to me. I built locks like this before the guild exiled me for knowing too much. The safe stones are the ones with wear. Look at the edges — foot traffic rounds the corners off. Forty years of servants carrying things through here, they'd have had a path. Find the polished ones."

Wren crouched, lamp low, and looked, and he was right — he was right, some of the flagstone corners were rounded soft as river stones while others kept their sharp-cut edges. Relief hit her so hard she almost laughed. Then she looked at the pattern of worn stones and stopped laughing, because the path they traced didn't lead to the gate.

It led to the wall. To a service niche, half-hidden, a maintenance door barely big enough to crawl through.

"The main path's trapped," she said slowly. "Has been for years. Somebody re-rigged it and left the old servant route open — because nobody important ever comes down here, and anybody unimportant who does is supposed to use the small door." She looked up at the others. "We crawl."

"Crawl toward the gate through the walls," August said. "Like rats."

"Best thing to be down here," Wren said. "Rats survive everything."

* * *

The niche door opened onto a crawlspace of brick and old grease, and the crawlspace went on longer than courage lasted.

Ossian went first — too broad for it, shoulders scraping both walls, breathing like a bellows, but he refused to send the smallest person into the unknown end of a tunnel and Wren didn't argue, because the truth was her legs had gone strange and shaky and she couldn't have said why. It wasn't the dark. Dark was hers. It wasn't the tightness. Tightness was home; she'd slept in chimneys.

It was the sound of the water.

Every few minutes it came again, distant and vast, a groan rolling up through the bones of the city, and each time it was a little louder. The siphon was open. The drowned level they'd waded through was drinking. By her count the water would climb the stairs they'd ascended within the hour, and fill the kill-floor corridor behind them, and every way out of this crawlspace would be underwater except forward.

Forward, toward a gate that Bramble would have to pick with a wound going bad in his side, on the twelfth night of a moon-count none of them fully understood.

"Talk," August said suddenly, from behind Wren. Her voice bounced strange off the bricks. "All this silence is making my head loud. Wren — tell me something. Anything. How'd you learn the sewers?"

Wren crawled and thought about lying, which was her native language, and decided against it, which was new.

"Lived in 'em. Off and on. My mum worked the tanneries till the fumes ate her lungs, and after she died there wasn't—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a whole city of kids under the rich city. We run messages, lift pockets, find things. The guild buys from us. Information, mostly. Things people lose." She hesitated. "Things people *want lost.*"

"Did the guild buy from you?" Sable asked, and her voice through the tunnel was unreadable.

"Once. They offered to buy my memories of my mum. Said grief was worth good coin to certain clients." Wren's laugh came out wrong. "I said no. First thing I ever said no to. They laughed and said every thief sells eventually." She crawled faster. "Anyway. Then I met Bramble, and he fed me actual food and taught me locks and never once asked to buy anything off me, and that was that."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then August's voice, very quietly: "You said no to the guild when you were nine years old."

"It was a bad offer."

Ahead, Ossian made a sound that might have been a laugh. The crawlspace bent, widened, and cold clean air moved across Wren's face, and they spilled out one by one into the gallery behind the gate — a square chamber of black bronze and older stone, the foundation gate looming on their right, its lock a masterpiece of interlocking wards even Wren could see was beyond her. Beyond her, but not beyond the man already kneeling in front of it with his picks laid out on a scrap of cloth like surgical tools, despite the fever-bright look in his eyes.

"Bramble," Ossian said, "you should rest—"

"I'll rest when the king's soul isn't on a deadline." Bramble's hands found the first ward. "Watch the corridor. And somebody keep an eye on that water."

Wren went back to the crawlspace mouth and looked down the long throat of it, and listened.

The groan came again. Closer. And underneath it now, a new sound: rushing. A thin, steady, growing rush, like a kettle deciding to sing.

* * *

It happened fast, the way disasters rehearse themselves.

One minute Bramble was three wards deep and swearing with scholarly precision; the next, the water arrived in the crawlspace — not a trickle but a shove, black and muscular, bursting out of the tunnel mouth in a waist-high sheet that knocked Wren off her feet and sent their lamps swinging wild. The chamber floor tilted drainage-grates that gulped greedily but not fast enough. Water spun at their ankles, their knees.

"The gate room floods!" Wren screamed over the roar. "When the siphon peaks, this whole chamber goes under — we've got minutes, maybe—"

"Then I need six minutes I don't have." Bramble's voice was calm, which frightened her worse than screaming. His hands never stopped moving. "Fourth ward's been re-keyed. Someone rebuilt this lock within the last decade. Give me a moment to be insulted and I'll—"

A cry from the tunnel.

Wren turned. Sable stood hip-deep in the flooding crawlspace mouth, holding her lamp high, and downstream of her — wedged against a brick lip where the current had slammed her — August was struggling upright, map-case clutched to her chest, and the water was pulling her feet out from under her, dragging her back down the throat of the tunnel toward the drowned level, toward the kill-floor corridor filling like a bathtub, and Wren's body understood everything before her mind did:

If August washed back into that corridor, she'd hit the plates. The mouths would open. And the woman who'd looked at Wren like she mattered would die in the dark before anyone reached her.

And Wren was the only one close enough. And the water was cold, and the tunnel was a throat, and every instinct that had kept her alive for eleven years in the under-city was screaming the oldest lesson she knew: *save yourself, save yourself first, always yourself first, because nobody else will.*

Wren dove.

The current took her instantly, tumbling her against August, and she got a fist in the woman's collar and braced her boots against a brick seam and held, and the river pulled, and her shoulders burned, and she thought distantly: *this is what dying feels like, this is stupid, this is exactly what the smart kids never do—*

Then Ossian's arm closed around them both like a dock piling, immovable, and Sable was there, and together they hauled August and Wren up out of the flood into the gasping lamplight.

Wren lay on her back in an inch of water, coughing, shaking, furious at herself for shaking. August rolled over beside her, soaked, map intact, and stared at her.

"You came back for me."

"Course I did." Wren wiped her nose with her sleeve. "You're family. Family's the thing you don't leave in the water." She said it fast and mean so it wouldn't sound like what it was: the first time she'd ever said the word out loud and meant it.

August's face did something complicated. Then she pulled Wren against her, wet as they both were, and held on, and Wren let her, for exactly three seconds, before wriggling free with as much dignity as a drowned cat could manage.

Across the chamber, Bramble made a sound of pure triumph. The black bronze gate swung inward on a breath of stale, ancient, *dry* air — air from below the waterline, from the vault approach, from the place where thirteen moons ended.

"Sixth ward was a fake-out," he said, swaying as Ossian steadied him. "Sentimental old fraud who re-keyed this left himself a signature. I taught him that trick myself." He smiled, and the smile slid sideways into a grimace, and he listed hard into Ossian's grip, and Wren saw the flush on his face and the shine in his eyes and knew, with a sick certainty, what festering looked like.

Behind them, the water kept rising. Ahead, the gate breathed its cold dark. And far above, through fathoms of stone, dawn was breaking over the palace where a hollow king sat lucid and drowning in front of his court — and Wren, hauling herself up on Ossian's arm, understood that whatever waited through this door, they would reach it just in time to watch something terrible begin.
