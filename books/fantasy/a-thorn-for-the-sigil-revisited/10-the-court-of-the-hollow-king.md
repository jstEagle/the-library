## 10. The Court of the Hollow King

The servant livery smelled of someone else's sweat.

August tugged the grey collar away from her throat for the hundredth time and told herself it was the wool that made her skin crawl, not what she was about to do. The uniform had come off a laundry line behind a nobleman's townhouse, along with three others in varying states of dampness, and Wren had carried them through the drainage grate grinning like a girl who'd been given a festival.

"Stop fidgeting," Ossian murmured beside her. He wore his servant's coat like a man wearing a lie over a larger one — even hunched, even with his hair bound back and a tray of wine glasses balanced on one scarred hand, he moved like a soldier pretending to be furniture. "You carry a tray like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money," August said. "Everything in this palace owes somebody money. I've read the ledgers."

"Then carry it like you've already been paid."

She adjusted her grip. The glasses chimed softly. Somewhere ahead, past two checkpoints where bored guards glanced at forged passes Bramble had spent four sleepless nights perfecting, the doors of the great hall stood open, breathing out candle-heat and the low roar of a court at feast.

Bramble was already inside. That had been the hardest part — splitting up. He'd gone in with the kitchen staff an hour before dusk, pushing a cart of braziers, his bad leg hidden beneath a porter's apron, and August had watched him go with her heart climbing into her mouth. The wound on his arm had stopped bleeding days ago, but it hadn't stopped being wrong. The flesh around the stitches had gone a bruised, glossy pink, and he'd taken to keeping his sleeve rolled down even when no one was looking, which was itself a kind of confession.

*He'll be fine,* she told herself, and did not believe it, because August Delacroix had spent eleven years reading other people's truths in ink and vellum and she knew exactly how much weight a sentence like *he'll be fine* could bear.

Almost none.

Wren went in last, as a scullery girl trailing a mop bucket, and if any guard looked twice at a child dragging dirty water through a royal feast, they kept their opinions to themselves. The palace had a thousand servants. It had never once learned all their names.

* * *

The great hall of Vael Corrin had been built to make visitors feel small, and it succeeded on August entirely. Pillars rose into shadow like the trunks of petrified trees. A thousand candles burned in tiers along the walls, and above them — where a ceiling should have shown gods or kings — someone long dead had painted the twelve moons of Aldric's calendar, each one silvered and serene, circling a central medallion of gold leaf so worn by centuries of candle-smoke that it had gone the color of old teeth.

Twelve moons. Everyone in the hall looked up at them without seeing them. August looked up and saw a countdown.

"They're serving the third course," Wren's voice said in her ear — the girl had pressed a sliver of whisper-glass into her palm outside, a smuggler's trick, a matched pair the size of a lentil that carried a voice across a crowded room. "I'm by the east doors. There's a table with a cloth down to the floor right next to me. If it goes wrong, get under it."

"If what goes wrong?" August asked.

"I don't know yet. Something always goes wrong."

Ossian drifted toward the dais with his wine tray, positioning himself within a sword-length of the king's chair, which was either excellent tradecraft or the most dangerous thing a man with his particular scars could do. August took up a station near the middle of the hall, among a knot of servers waiting on the long banquet tables, close enough to see the throne clearly and far enough to be furniture.

And then the king came in.

She had expected a monster. She had prepared herself for a monster — had rehearsed the sight of him the way you rehearse a blow, tightening everything inside so it couldn't knock you down. The sigil had eaten King Aldric for years; the market oracle had described it as a parasite, something that wore its host the way a glove wears a hand, hollowing him turn by turn, moon by moon, until what sat on the throne was mostly appetite wearing a crown.

What came through the doors was a tall man in his forties with a grey-shot beard and his father's heavy shoulders, walking with the careful precision of someone who has rehearsed walking. His face was handsome in the way portraits are handsome. His eyes were the problem. They were clear — terribly, unnaturally clear — and they went everywhere and held nothing, like windows into a house where every room had been emptied and scrubbed.

He smiled at the assembled court. The smile fit him the way the crown fit him: correctly, and by force.

"There he is," Wren whispered, very small. "That's him? He looks like... he looks like a teacher."

"He looks like a man who has been robbed," August said, before she could stop herself.

Around her, the feast roared on. Lords and ladies laughed at jokes that weren't funny. Ministers leaned and murmured. A string orchestra played something in a minor key that everyone pretended was celebratory. And at the center of it all, King Aldric IX — the Hollow King, the children called him, though not where adults could hear — sat through the courses and the speeches with the patience of the buried.

August watched him work.

It was the only word for it. Every gesture cost him something. When a duke addressed him, there was a pause — half a heartbeat, no more — before the king answered, and in that pause his eyes flickered upward and sideways, as though consulting something just over his own shoulder. When the wine was poured, he drank exactly as much as protocol required. When he laughed — twice during the evening, both times at the correct moments — the laugh arrived a fraction late and left too soon, like a messenger who delivers the letter but forgets the reply.

*He's piloting himself,* she thought. *Or something is piloting him, and he's watching from somewhere far inside, pressing his hands against the glass.*

Her fingers found the map's absence under her shirt — she hadn't brought it, of course, wouldn't have dared, but her body kept reaching for it anyway, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was.

Turn seven was six days gone. Six more remained after tonight. Somewhere above them all, past the painted ceiling, the thirteenth moon was coming around on its impossible orbit, the one no astronomer would admit to charting, the one that would complete its turn and seal whatever was left of Aldric beneath the sigil forever.

* * *

It happened between the fish course and the meat.

A chamberlain struck the floor with a staff three times and announced the Gift Presentation — the tedious ceremony wherein loyal lords competed to give the crown things it didn't need — and the hall rearranged itself into lines. August used the shuffle of bodies to drift closer, trading her empty tray for a full one from a passing steward, until she stood perhaps thirty feet from the dais with an unbroken view.

The gifts were gold plate, a tapestry, a hunting hawk, a chest of pearls from the southern coast. The king received each one with the same measured grace, and each time, August saw the flicker — up and to the side, consulting, confirming — and each time it lasted a little longer than the time before.

Then a woman stepped forward whom August did not know: plainly dressed, a merchant's widow by the cut of her gown, holding a small lacquered box in both hands as though it were a sleeping bird. The chamberlain hesitated — she wasn't on the list; August could see him checking his scroll, frowning — but the widow walked forward anyway, and something in her face made the guards decide she was harmless, because harmlessness is a costume anyone can wear.

"My king," she said, and her voice carried in the sudden quiet. "My husband traded in curiosities. Before he died, he left me one thing he said must never be sold, and never be opened, and I have obeyed him for six years. But I am tired, majesty, and I am frightened of it, and I thought — if anyone in the world has the right to look inside a mystery, it is the crown." She knelt, and held up the box. "It is yours, if you will take it."

The hall murmured. A strange gift, a strange moment. August felt the hairs rise on her arms before she understood why.

King Aldric rose from his throne.

He descended the dais steps slowly, and the whole court watched him go, and August watched harder than anyone. Because as he walked, the flicker changed. Up and to the side became up and *inward*. His jaw tightened. His hands, hanging at his sides, curled slowly into fists and then forced themselves open again, finger by finger, like a man unclenching against terrible pain.

He reached the widow. He looked down at the box.

"No," he said.

It was barely a sound. No one else heard it — the court was still rustling, the chamberlain still frowning at his list. But August had spent her life listening for the truth underneath words, and the word fell into the silence between heartbeats and she caught it whole.

"No," he said again, louder, and now people did hear, and the murmur died. The king of Vael Corrin was standing in front of a kneeling widow, staring at a gift box, and saying no to it in a voice like a man arguing with a fever.

"Majesty?" the chamberlain ventured.

Aldric's hand came up — slowly, shaking — and hovered over the lacquered lid. Beneath the skin of his wrist, August saw something move. Not muscle. Something *under* the muscle, a shifting like a shadow behind silk, tracing the line of his veins up toward his forearm, his shoulder, his throat.

The sigil. She was looking at the sigil.

It was not what the oracle had described — not a parasite, not a worm, nothing so simple. What she saw, in the candlelight, in the space of two seconds, was a pattern. Lines, fine as engraving, moving beneath his skin like ink flowing through paper, drawing and redrawing themselves into shapes that hurt to follow — angles that bent the wrong way, curves that suggested meanings and then revoked them. It gathered at his wrist where the veins met, and for one instant it resolved into something almost legible:

A seal. A wax seal, pressed into living flesh, and the flesh around it swollen with everything the seal was meant to hold in.

*Thirteen turns,* the courier had whispered, dying on her floor. *Thirteen turns.*

Six left.

The king snatched his hand back from the box. He turned — not smoothly, with a lurching, puppet-at-the-end-of-its-string quality that the court politely ignored, because courts are practiced at ignoring — and his eyes swept the hall. Past the lords. Past the guards. Past Ossian, standing rigid by the dais with his knuckles white around the wine tray.

They landed on August.

For a moment — one second, maybe two — she looked directly into the face of the thing that owned the kingdom, and it looked back, and she understood that she had been wrong. It wasn't consulting something over its shoulder. The king's eyes weren't flicking away at all.

They were flicking *up*, the way a prisoner's eyes go up when he speaks through the slot in his door.

"Please," Aldric said.

The word came out of him cracked and human and raw, and it was not addressed to the widow, or the chamberlain, or the court. He was looking at August — at her, a servant girl with a tray of wine glasses — and his voice dropped to something that only reached her, that crossed thirty feet of stunned silence like an arrow finding its mark:

"Please. You. Whoever you are. You have steady hands — I can see it, I can always tell — please, before the moon turns. Find a knife. Any knife. Come to me in the night and do it quickly and don't let them stop you, don't let it stop you, it will try to talk, it talks *so well*—" His breath hitched. The shadow-lines surged up his neck toward his jaw, and his next words came out flattened, smoothed, polished by something else: "Forgive me. Forgive my confusion, good lady. The heat of the hall. Take your box home, madam, and my thanks."

But his eyes stayed on August, and in the last instant before the sigil pulled him under — before the smile reassembled itself and the king turned back to his throne like a door closing on a lit room — those clear, empty, desperate eyes did one more thing.

They flicked to the floor. To the shadow of the nearest pillar, where the light pooled dark. And then back to her.

*Come where they can't see,* said nothing at all, said everything.

The hall exhaled and resumed its noise. The widow was escorted out, bewildered, clutching her box. The meat course arrived. Somewhere in August's chest, her heart was trying to climb out through her ribs.

*Kill me.* The king of Vael Corrin, the hostage face of her enemy, had looked at a stranger across a feast hall and begged her for a knife.

* * *

"You heard it too." Wren materialized at her elbow during the clearing of the tables, mop bucket abandoned, whisper-glass cold against August's ear. "Everyone's saying the king went strange. They're saying it was the heat. They're saying—" The girl's voice wobbled and steadied. "You went white as whey. I thought you were going to drop the tray."

"I nearly did."

"What did he say to you?"

August looked out across the dying feast. Servants snuffed candles tier by tier, and the painted moons overhead went dark one at a time, first to last, like a count running backward.

"He said he wants to die," she said quietly. "Before the moon turns."

Wren was silent a moment. "Do we... do we want that?"

"No." The answer came faster than thought, faster than sense, straight up from some floor of herself August hadn't known was load-bearing. "No. We want him *back*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, Wren. Anyone can kill a king. We're here to return stolen property."

"That's a strange way to put treason."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. It's how I put everything."

Ossian joined them at the servants' door, shrugging off his tray like a man shedding armor. His face was grim in the torchlight of the passage beyond. "Bramble's not at the rendezvous point."

August's stomach dropped through six floors. "What do you mean, not at the rendezvous point?"

"He sent a runner — one of the kitchen boys he's bribed. Message was four words." Ossian recited it flatly, but his eyes betrayed him, flicking once toward the upper reaches of the palace, toward the sealed administrative wing where the vault gates lived behind doors that had gotten a man exiled and ruined. "'Found the master keyhole.'"

"The gate," August breathed. "He's gone to look at the gate. Alone. With that arm."

"With that arm," Ossian agreed, "and a fever he's been hiding since turn five, and about forty pounds of pride."

Above them, faint through the stone, the last candles of the great hall guttered out, and the twelfth moon on the painted ceiling vanished into darkness, leaving only the worn gold medallion at the center — blank, patient, waiting to become thirteen.

August pulled the servant's cap from her hair and let it fall to the flagstones.

"Then we go get our locksmith," she said, "before the palace keeps him."
