## 11. Bramble's Locks

The gate had a name, and the name was Vesper.

Bramble had given it that name himself, twenty-two years ago, on the last night he'd been allowed to love his work. Locks deserved names. A lock was a conversation, and you didn't have a conversation with something you refused to know. Vesper was the vault gate beneath the palace of Vharen — three feet of black iron and silver inlay, a wheel of tumblers arranged like a clock face, each one keyed to a different hour of a different day, so that the lock's answer changed depending on when you asked the question.

He had built the answer. He had built the question, too. And then the Master of the Vellum Guild had taken the answer out of his head, sold it, and let the palace guards find Bramble's tools in the rubble of a robbery Bramble had never committed.

Twenty-two years. And now here he was again, flat on his back on cold flagstone, staring up at the underside of the only thing he had ever made that was worth making.

"You're smiling," Wren whispered from the shadow of the stairwell. "Why are you smiling? We're about to die and you're smiling."

"Because it still remembers me," Bramble said. He ran his thumb along the gate's lower edge, where he'd once left a flaw on purpose — a maker's signature, a single tooth cut a hair too shallow, invisible to any eye but his. "Hello, Vesper. You old witch. You've been lonely, haven't you?"

The iron did not answer. The iron never answered. But the faint hum of the sigil's magic threaded through it, and Bramble felt the vibration in his teeth like a held note, and he knew — the way a father knows a child's breathing through a wall — that the gate was straining against its own enchantment, wanting to be opened by hands that understood it rather than hands that merely commanded it.

Ossian crouched beside him, huge and quiet, the lamplight catching the pale scar-tissue lattice across his forearms where his stolen year had been carved out of him. "How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less." Bramble unrolled his kit on the stone: picks cut from clock springs, a tension wrench he'd forged from a broken guild seal, wax, graphite, a vial of vinegar for the silver inlay's old corrosion. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady. It was everything above the hands that shook. "If I'm right about what they've done to her since."

"If?"

"They'll have changed the hours." His voice went flat. "The Master knew I'd come back someday. He's not clever, but he's thorough, and thorough men change locks they're ashamed of. The question is whether he changed her *soul* or just her *clothes*."

August knelt on his other side. In the lamplight her face was pale and set, the map's coordinates memorized and burned, and she said, quietly, "Bramble. If it comes to it — if you can't—"

"I can." He said it before she could finish, because he'd heard the shape of that sentence a hundred times in twenty-two years, in his own head, every night: *if you can't open it, what are you for?* He would not let her finish it. "I built her. Whatever he's bolted onto her since, the bones are mine. You don't forget your own bones."

He heard Wren shift behind him, and Ossian's low breath, and somewhere far above, muffled through forty feet of palace foundation, the bells marking the sixth hour of the night. Turn six of thirteen. The sigil's hum deepened by a fraction, the way a sleeping animal's breathing deepens, and Bramble felt it and hated it and set his picks against the first tumbler anyway.

* * *

The first hour of work was memory.

That was the secret nobody understood about locks: you didn't fight them, you *remembered* them. The tension wrench went in at the four o'clock position because Vesper had been born on a Tuesday and Tuesdays leaned right. The first pick found the tumbler keyed to dawn, and Bramble closed his eyes and was twenty-three years old again in the guild workshop, filing the tooth by lamplight while the Master stood behind him saying *again, too deep, again, too shallow, again* — and the tumbler gave with a click like a knuckle cracking, and he was back on the flagstone, sweating, grinning.

"First hour," he breathed. "She kept the Tuesday. He didn't change the Tuesday."

"Why would that matter?" Wren whispered.

"Because it means he never understood her." Bramble set the second pick. "He only ever understood what she was *worth*."

The second hour was harder. The tumbler keyed to noon had been re-cut — he could feel it the moment his pick touched it, a tooth ground down and rebuilt by a cruder hand, the guild's own smiths, competent and soulless. He worked it for ten minutes, sweating, the sigil's hum crawling up through the flagstone into his knees, and when it finally gave he lay back and laughed, a short ugly sound.

"He re-cut it wrong," he said. "He re-cut it *wrong*. The man bought the answer to my lock and sold it, and his smiths copied it badly, and now there's a flaw in her that wasn't there before, and the flaw is *mine to use*." He wiped his face. "Twenty-two years I thought he'd beaten me. He didn't beat me. He *plagiarized* me. There's a difference, and the difference is that a plagiarism always has a seam."

August's hand settled briefly on his shoulder. She didn't say anything. She'd learned, somewhere in the last six turns, when not to talk. Bramble was grateful. There was nothing to say that the work wasn't already saying.

The third hour was midnight, and midnight was where the Master lived.

* * *

The tumbler keyed to midnight did not move.

Bramble tried everything he knew. He tried the original cut, the re-cut, the counter-cut, the trick with the vinegar that softened old corrosion, the trick with heat that he'd sworn never to use on Vesper because it scarred the silver. Nothing. The tumbler sat in its housing like a held breath, and the sigil's hum through it had changed — no longer the deep sleeping note but something quicker, thinner, almost amused.

And then the gate spoke.

Not with a voice. With a *memory*. The way the sigil worked on all of them, Ossian had said — it didn't show you things, it *reminded* you of things, and the reminding was the trap. Bramble's workshop bloomed behind his eyes: lamplight, the smell of oil and iron filings, the Master's hand on his shoulder, heavy as a yoke.

"You built a door, boy," the Master said, in the memory, in the exact patient voice Bramble had spent two decades trying to forget. "Do you know what a door is? A door is a promise that the thing behind it can be reached. You promised the whole city the king's vault. You just didn't know who'd come to collect."

"I didn't build it for you," Bramble said aloud, on the flagstone, to no one, to the gate, to the ghost.

"You built it for the *guild*," the memory said, gentle, reasonable, the voice that had talked a nineteen-year-old apprentice into signing away the contents of his own head. "Everything is built for the guild. Your hands are guild hands. Your lock is a guild lock. And when the palace came asking who'd sold the answer, we needed a thief, and you were standing there with thief's hands. It wasn't cruelty, boy. It was *accounting*."

Bramble's pick hand trembled. Just once. Just a hair.

And then Ossian's hand closed over his wrist — not gripping, just resting there, enormous and warm and steady, the way you'd steady a bird. "He's not here," Ossian said quietly. "The man's not here. It's the sigil wearing your worst night. I know the fit of that shirt. Take your wrist back."

Bramble took his wrist back. He breathed. He looked at the tumbler keyed to midnight, and he understood it all at once, the way you understand a sentence you've been reading wrong for years.

The Master hadn't changed the midnight tumbler. The Master had made the sigil *be* the midnight tumbler — a living lock, a lock that fed on the person picking it, that opened not to skill but to surrender. That was why it wouldn't move. It was waiting for him to give it something.

"No," Bramble said to it. "No, you don't get my midnight. I've got a new one." He thought of Wren asleep against Ossian's side in the smuggler's cellar, of August's terrible careful kindness, of tea made badly by people who loved him. "You want a memory? Here's one. I *survived* you."

He set the pick at an angle no guild smith would ever find, an angle that existed only in the flaw the plagiarists had ground into Vesper's silver, and turned.

The midnight tumbler gave like a heart unlocking.

* * *

The wheel turned. All twelve hours, all twelve tumblers, the whole clock face of it, and Vesper — his Vesper, his old witch, his one true work — swung inward on hinges he'd greased twenty-two years ago and that still moved like silk because he had *built them to*.

For one moment Bramble knelt there with his hand flat against the open iron, and he did not feel triumph. He felt something stranger and more painful, the feeling of a letter finally delivered to an address that no longer existed. *I came back,* he told the gate silently. *I came back, and I brought better people than you were built to keep out.*

"Vesper," he said aloud, and named her, because a lock deserved a name and he'd denied her one for too long. "You're free. Go back to sleep."

Then the crossbow bolt took him in the side.

* * *

There was no pain at first. That was the thing about bolts that he'd learned from soldiers and never believed until now — the body simply refused the information, filed it under *later*. He was aware of Ossian roaring, of Wren screaming something, of the lamplight swinging wildly, of a figure in the vault's antechamber beyond the gate: a narrow man in guild grey with a crossbow already reloading, and above the man's collar the sigil's mark worn openly, a silver thorn-crown on black silk.

The Master of the Vellum Guild. In the flesh. Standing in the doorway Bramble had just opened, wearing the face from Bramble's worst memory, aged twenty-two years and no kinder.

"You," the Master said, and there was something almost like wonder in it. "The lock-boy. I told them you'd come back. I *told* them. Nobody ever believes me about the sentimental ones." He raised the crossbow. "You've cost me a gate, Bramble. Do you know what a gate costs, in the ledger I keep?"

Ossian hit him like a landslide.

The crossbow went off into the ceiling. The Master was old but the sigil was not, and it moved him the way a puppeteer moves a marionette — too fast, wrong-jointed, a man-shaped thing wearing a man's face — and Ossian caught a backhand across the jaw that would have killed anyone without his shoulders, and went down, and came up bleeding and grinning. Wren darted in low and cut the Master's hamstring with a pickpocket's blade, a small vicious wound, and the Master stumbled, and August did something with her hands that Bramble didn't see and didn't need to — the sigil's hum shrieked through the antechamber like a struck wire, and the Master screamed and clutched his own head, and then he was running, stumbling back into the dark of the vault approach, leaving a thin trail of blood and a smell like burnt paper.

Silence. The bells above struck the seventh hour.

"Bramble," August said.

"I'm fine." He tried to stand up and discovered that *later* had arrived all at once, a white-hot fist under his ribs, and the flagstone came up and met him, and Ossian's hands caught him before he finished falling.

"Fine," Ossian said, in the tone of a man cataloguing a lie. He'd torn Bramble's shirt open and was looking at the bolt wound below the ribs, and his face did the thing that Bramble had learned, in the weeks since the cellar, to read: the stillness, the slight narrowing, the jaw set a fraction too tight. "It's in deep. It's out, though — I've got it out. But it went through something dirty, and the sigil's been breathing on this stone for years, and—" He stopped. Started again. "It's going to fester. Maybe. Probably. I've seen this wound fester and I've seen it close, and the difference is the man, and I don't know you well enough yet to know if you're a festerer."

"I'm a locksmith," Bramble said, through his teeth. "We don't fester. We rust."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Ossian bound the wound with strips of Wren's cloak, because Wren had already cut them before being asked, and packed it with the vinegar from the lock kit because it was what they had, and Bramble lay on the flagstone with his hand pressed flat to the bandage and watched the open gate — *his* gate, *his* open gate — and felt the vindication and the cost arrive together, the way they always did, the way every door he'd ever opened had demanded its toll. No door opens free. He'd known that at nineteen. He'd just never thought the toll would come due with interest, twenty-two years later, paid in blood on the far side of his own best work.

"Go," he said. "All of you. The vault approach is through there, and the turns don't wait, and I can walk."

"You can't," Ossian said.

"I can *lean*," Bramble amended. "Lean and walk. It's how I got out of the guild the first time."

August crouched beside him. Her eyes went to the wound, then to his face, and he watched her do the arithmetic that she was always doing — the cost of everything, the ledger of it, the price — and he watched her arrive at the same answer he had.

"We carry him," she said. "Turn seven's coming, and whatever's coming, we don't leave pieces of this family on the floor of it. Ossian, take his right side. Wren, lights."

Ossian gathered him up with a gentleness that would have embarrassed them both if either of them had been the sort to mention it. Bramble's head lolled against the big man's shoulder, and through the haze of the pain he saw the antechamber opening ahead of them, and beyond it, faint as breath on glass, the sigil's light — silver, thorned, patient — and he heard, or thought he heard, or dreamed he heard, the Master's voice echoing up from the deep of the vault, no longer gentle at all:

*Seven turns remain. Bring me the thief who reads, and I'll let the lock-boy keep what's left of his blood.*

Bramble laughed, wet and weak, all the way down the stairs.

"Shut up," Ossian said fondly, and carried him into the dark.
