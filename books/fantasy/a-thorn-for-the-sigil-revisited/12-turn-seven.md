## 12. Turn Seven

The scar on Ossian's forearm woke him before the moon did.

It had been quiet for six turns — a pale seam of skin, smooth as candle wax, the kind of mark you could almost convince yourself meant nothing. He'd spent years practicing that particular lie. *Nothing happened to me. I gave nothing away. The year between the border war and the day I woke in a debtor's cart is simply missing, like a tooth, and a man can chew around a missing tooth.*

But tonight, with the moon a half-circle of bone above the rooftops of Salt Row, the scar itched from the inside. Not pain, exactly. Pressure. Like something written under his skin was trying to read itself aloud.

He sat up in the narrow room they'd rented above the cooper's shop — Bramble snoring in the next bed, tools stacked by the door; Wren curled in the window seat with the dog's head heavy across her feet; August awake too, of course, because she never seemed to sleep so much as stand watch over her own thoughts. She sat by the lamp with her ledger open, and when she looked up at him she knew.

"It started," she said.

Not a question. He hated that she didn't ask, and he loved it too, because asking would have made him say it out loud, and saying it would have made it realer than the pressure already building behind his eyes.

"Turn seven," he said. "Halfway." His voice came out gravelled. "August, my arm is trying to remember."

* * *

The first fragment came while Bramble was picking the lock on their landlady's pantry for reasons he refused to explain, and Wren was teaching the dog to carry a stolen roll without eating it. Ossian was standing at the window, doing nothing, being nobody, when the scar split its silence.

He saw rain.

He saw a bridge of grey stone, slick with it, and torches hissing in brackets along the rail. He saw himself — or wore himself, the memory arriving not as watching but as *wearing*, which was infinitely worse — standing at the bridge's center with a captain's badge on his collar. A captain's voice came out of his own mouth, flat and certain:

*"No one crosses after dark. That is the order. Anyone who tries is carrying messages for the rebels, and rebels don't get warnings."*

And then he saw them coming up the road through the rain. Not soldiers. Farmers. A man driving a cart of what might have been turnips or might have been rifles — there was no way to tell at that distance, in that light, and Ossian felt his own past self decide that the not-knowing was reason enough. He felt his own hand lift. He heard his own command: *"Loose."*

The fragment ended before the arrows landed. It always would, he understood dimly, end there — because whoever had cut this year out of him had cut it cleanly, at the seams, and the seams fell where the guilt began.

He came back to the room with his knees on the floorboards and no memory of going down. Bramble had stopped mid-pick, tension wrench in one hand, staring. The dog was pressed against Ossian's side, whining low. Wren stood in the doorway with the roll still in her fist, looking at him the way children look at grown men who break.

"What did you see?" August asked. She knelt across from him, careful, keeping just outside his reach — she'd been a soldier once too, or near enough, and she knew better than to touch a man who was still inside a burning house.

"A bridge," he said. "Rain. My orders." His hands were shaking. He watched them shake as if they belonged to someone else, some other Ossian, the one who had worn his body during the year he'd sold. "There were people on the road. Civilians. And I—" His throat closed. He worked it open again, because he owed them the truth if he owed them anything. "I gave the order anyway. I couldn't see what they carried. So I killed them for not knowing."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog barked twice and gave up.

"That wasn't you," Bramble said gruffly. "That was the man who had your face before they scraped it."

"It was my face," Ossian said. "My voice. My hand." He looked at August. "You've seen ledgers. Tell me how this works. When they take a year out of a man — do they take what he did? Or only his knowing of it?"

August's jaw tightened. "They take the knowing," she said quietly. "The deed stays where deeds stay."

"Then the deed is mine."

* * *

By the fourth night of turn seven, the fragments came in floods.

He learned to dread sleep and to dread waking equally, because sleep brought the bridge and the granary and the night patrol he'd ordered into an ambush he should have read in time, and waking brought the scars — all of them now, not just the arm, the whole constellation of pale marks the guild's harvesters had left across his back and chest like a map of everywhere they'd cut. Each scar itched its own itch. Each one held a splinter of the erased year working loose.

He remembered the granary most clearly, worse than the bridge, because at the granary he had hesitated. A boy had run out of the burning doors — Ossian saw it now in perfect, merciless detail, the boy maybe nine years old, hair smoking, arms wide — and the old Ossian, the captain-Ossian, had shouted *"Hold your fire"* and then, three seconds later, seeing movement in the loft above, corrected himself: *"Fire on the building."* The correction had cost the boy everything. Three seconds. Ossian lay awake measuring those three seconds against every hour of every day he had lived since, wondering which version of the order was the true one, the mercy or the correction, and understanding that both were him.

On the fifth night he stopped speaking at meals. On the sixth, he took his bedroll to the roof and told no one why, though everyone knew: he didn't trust what he might say in his sleep.

On the seventh night, he sharpened his sword.

Not to use on anyone. Just to have his hands full of something older than the memories, something with rules. Whetstone, oil, the long patient strokes. He was halfway down the blade when the door opened and August came out into the cold with two cups of tea and no apology for intruding, and sat down on the parapet beside him as if men sharpening swords on roofs at midnight were a thing that happened every week.

"You're going to cut yourself," she said.

"I'm going to be fine," he said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"Mm." She drank her tea. The city lay below them, ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke into the dark, and above them the moon hung at seven turns' fullness, a bowl filling with light. "Do you know what I did before the guild taught me ledgers?"

"You were nobody's business but your own," he said. It was what she always said.

"I burned a house down." She said it evenly, watching the moon. "With people in it. I was nineteen. They told me it was empty — a guild job, a rival trader's warehouse, clean the books by cleaning the building. And I believed them, because believing was easier than checking, and checking was dangerous, and I wanted to be good at the work. I lit the oil myself." Her cup steamed in the cold. "Three people died. A woman and her two daughters, hiding in the cellar from creditors who weren't me. I found out eleven days later, from a gossip sheet that cost a copper."

Ossian set the sword down slowly across his knees. "You've never told anyone that."

"No one alive needed telling until tonight." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes in the lamplight spilling up from the street were steady and terribly kind. "Here's what I learned about guilt, Ossian, and it took me fifteen years and a ledger full of other people's sins to learn it. Guilt isn't proof you're damned. Guilt is proof the part of you that gave the order is dead, and the part that grieves it is alive. Dead men don't lose sleep over the bridge. Only living ones do."

"The bridge," he whispered. "You know about the bridge."

"Wren listens at doors. Bramble talks in his sleep. You shout orders in yours." She shrugged slightly. "We're a household. We hear things."

He looked down at the blade, bright and blameless. "What am I supposed to do with it, August? With what's coming back? Every turn brings more. By turn thirteen I'll have the whole year back — every order, every face. I spent four years learning to live without that weight. Four years learning to be a man who hadn't done those things. Now the guild's sloppy stitching is unraveling and I'm expected to just—"

"To carry it," she said. "Yes."

"That's not mercy. That's just cruelty with better bookkeeping."

"It's survival." She reached over and put her hand flat on the blade between his fingers, forcing him to either lift it away or acknowledge her. He acknowledged her. "Listen to me. You think the price of remembering is unbearable. But ask yourself what the sigil wants. Ask what the thing under the palace feeds on. Kings who forget themselves. Soldiers who forget what they did. Whole cities walking around hollow, their worst years cut out, their best years sold off, until there's nothing inside anyone but appetite and obedience." Her grip firmed on the steel. "Memory is the only weapon we're bringing to that vault that the sigil doesn't already own. Even the ugly parts. *Especially* the ugly parts. Because a man who remembers his worst order will never let a king be eaten while he stands there polishing his conscience."

Ossian was silent a long moment. Below them, the dog barked once at the moon and thought better of it.

"When we go down there," he said finally, "and the sigil shows me the perfect lie — it'll show me the bridge, won't it. Rain, and the farmers, and me ordering them shot. But changed. Fixed. The cart empty, or the order never given, or the boy running from the granary catching fire and *living*. It'll show me the year the way I'd have written it if I'd been allowed."

"Yes," August said simply. "And you'll want it. Gods help us all, you'll want it so badly it will feel like dying to walk past. I know, because mine will show me my mother's kitchen, and she'll be in it, and she'll turn around and smile." Something moved behind her composure then, brief and vast, like a whale surfacing under ice. "And we will walk past anyway. Together. That's the whole trick, Ossian. There's no surviving clean. There's only surviving *together*, and letting each other hold the weight on the days it tips."

He laughed — a cracked, wet sound, more grief than humor, but real. "You make it sound like arithmetic."

"Everything is arithmetic eventually." She took her hand off the blade and offered it instead, palm up. "Come inside. It's cold, the tea's getting worse, and Wren has decided — loudly, to the dog — that if you die of guilt on the roof she gets your boots, and she'd rather have the company."

He took her hand. It was smaller than his and much stronger than it looked, and it pulled him up off the parapet as though the weight of seven turns of recovered sin weighed nothing at all.

At the roof door she paused, silhouetted against the lamplight below, and said, quietly, almost to herself:

"Bramble found something today, poking through the cooper's ledgers. A delivery notice. Guild seal. Addressed to the counting house on Vellum Street — marked *private, for the Master's eye only.* Dated three days ago." She glanced back at him, and the kindness in her face had cooled into something sharper, the look of a woman balancing columns that refused to sum. "Shipments of blank vials. Two hundred of them. Enough to bottle the memories of an entire district."

Ossian felt the scars along his spine go cold, all at once, like frost finding every seam at once.

"Turn seven," he said. "Halfway. Whatever they're planning to harvest—"

"They're planning to harvest it soon," August finished. "And I intend to find out exactly what, and where, before the eighth turn rises. Get your boots, soldier. We've got a counting house to burgle."
