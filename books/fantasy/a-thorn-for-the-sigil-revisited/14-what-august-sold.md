## 14. What August Sold

The guild master's ledger did not want to be read.

August felt it the way she felt a locked drawer in a dark room — a pressure, a wrongness, the sense that the book itself was aware of her fingers on its spine. She sat in the counting house she had once ruled with quiet competence, in the chair she had occupied for nine years, while the agents who had dragged her here waited in the hall and pretended not to listen. The candle guttered. The ledger lay open across her knees, bound in vellum so old it had gone the color of old teeth.

She had told them she wanted to bargain. That was the lie that bought her the hour alone. Guild masters loved bargains the way moths loved flames — helplessly, and to their own ruin.

*Page one hundred and fourteen*, the courier's dying whisper had not said. But the map had. She had understood it only now, in this room, under this light: the wax seal on the map bore a sigil pressed at an angle, and the angle matched the crease of the ledger's hundred-and-fourteenth page, which the master dog-eared with a ribbon the exact red of dried blood.

She turned to it.

The page was titled in the master's small, precise hand: *Acquisitions, Voluntary. The Kavanagh Accounts.*

Her name. Her mother's name. And beneath them, in columns she had spent her whole adult life writing for other people, the terrible arithmetic of her own childhood.

* * *

Nine years earlier, and eleven years old, August Kavanagh had stood in this same counting house on a night much like this one, rain hammering the windows, and watched her mother sign the first vial away.

She remembered it with the terrible clarity of a thing she had replayed ten thousand times: her mother's coat still dripping, her mother's hands steady on the counter, the guild master's voice like oil poured over gravel. *The debt is considerable, madam. The guild does not extend charity. But we do extend terms.*

Her mother had owed them. August had never learned why — only that the debt predated her birth, that it was old and compound and hungry, and that the guild's terms were always the same: memories, weighed and vialled, against principal and interest. Her mother had sold the wedding. Then the sea voyage. Then her own sister's laugh. Each vial a little lighter than the last, each withdrawal leaving her mother a little more hollow, a little more polite, a little more like a woman reading her own life from someone else's notes.

August had hated the guild for it. She had hated it with the clean, total hatred of a child, and then she had done the most sensible thing a hated child could do.

She had gotten close. She had learned the ledgers. She had made herself so useful, so precise, so quietly indispensable, that by sixteen she was keeping the accounts of impossible things, and by nineteen she had found the flaw in the guild's arithmetic: debts could be transferred. Not forgiven — the guild forgave nothing — but transferred. Assumed. Signed over to any party of sound mind and sufficient collateral.

Sufficient collateral. She had been nineteen, and she had owned exactly one thing of value.

* * *

In the present, the candle hissed, and August's finger stopped on the line.

*Item: One (1) full set, maternal — visual, auditory, tactile. Seller: Kavanagh, August, age 19. Condition: excellent, unfragmented. Buyer: retained, guild reserve. Consideration: discharge in full of debt account K-114, transferred from M. Kavanagh, deceased, to B. Fenwick (aka "Bramble"), locksmith, upon said locksmith's release from guild custody.*

She read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder. They sat on the page like stones at the bottom of a well, and she was looking down at them, and the water was very cold, and very clear.

*Voluntary*, the column header said. *Seller's mark:* and there it was, her own signature, nineteen years old and already precise, the letters neat as stitches. She knew that signature. She had watched her own hand make it a thousand times in a thousand ledgers. She had simply never once looked for it here.

The room tilted. She gripped the desk.

She had a memory of the day Bramble was released. Of course she did — it was one of her favorites, one she had polished by handling until it shone. The locksmith walking out of the guild's custody, blinking in the daylight, his ruined left hand cradled against his chest, looking at her with an expression she had never been able to name. Gratitude, she had thought. Gratitude with something underneath it, some sediment of pity or grief she had never bothered to dredge.

Now she knew what the expression was. It was the look of a man who knew what she had paid and could not bear to tell her, because she had bought his freedom with the one thing she would have refused to sell if she'd known what it was.

No. That wasn't right either. That was the coward's version, the version that let her off the hook.

She had known. She had known exactly what she was selling. She had sat across from the guild master at nineteen with her chin up and her heart hammering and she had said, *the whole set, every memory of her, visual, auditory, all of it — I want the debt gone, all of it, every copper* — and the guild master had looked at her with something almost like respect and said, *you understand you will not know what you have lost. You will not even know there is a hole.*

And nineteen-year-old August had said, *that's the point.*

* * *

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing that lived under her ribs like a swallowed hook: she had done it on purpose. Not the transfer — that was tactics, that was the ledger's cold arithmetic, one debt extinguished, one useful man freed, one more piece on her board. But the choice of collateral. The choice of *her*.

She had been nineteen and drowning in a mother who was being emptied vial by vial, a mother who was becoming a polite stranger with her own face, and August had understood — with the awful clarity available only to the young and the desperate — that she could not survive watching it happen. That every memory of her mother was becoming a wound, and that the wounds were compounding faster than the debt.

So she had sold them all at once. Cut the gangrene off in a single stroke. She had told herself it was mercy to her mother — that the memories would live in a guild vial, preserved, instead of rotting in a daughter's chest. She had told herself it was mercy to herself.

She had told herself so many things.

The truth, which she had never once let herself think at full volume in nine years of keeping other people's accounts, was simpler and uglier: she had sold her mother because her mother was already gone, vial by vial, and August could not stand the slow version. She had chosen the fast grief. She had chosen to not-know over knowing.

And the guild — the patient, monstrous guild — had let her believe it worked.

* * *

The candle burned down another inch. In the hall, an agent coughed, and August did not move.

Here was the shape of it, laid out now in the master's own hand, in the guild's own ink: her mother had died — *deceased*, the ledger said, with the flat economy the guild reserved for the inconvenient — still owing on account K-114, because the debt had never actually been hers to discharge. It had been *August's* from the moment she signed. The transfer to Bramble had cleared the principal, but the guild's interest clauses were older and hungrier than any contract, and the master had simply carried the account forward, dormant, accruing, waiting.

Waiting for what?

She turned the page. Her breath stopped.

*Note, private, master's hand:* *The Kavanagh girl's set is the finest acquisition in the reserve. Voluntary sales carry a different weight — a memory given freely keeps its warmth. The mother's set, taken under duress, has already gone thin. But the daughter's burns bright. Hold it. She will come for the map eventually. They always come back to what they sold.*

*The sigil prefers willing memories. It says they taste of choice.*

The candle went out.

August sat in the dark with the ledger open on her knees and understood, all at once and completely, why the map had been bait, why the courier had bled out on her floor, why every road of the last weeks had bent toward the Moonvault. It had never been about the map. It had never even been about her talent, not entirely. The sigil had been *fed* — fed for years, fed on the guild's reserve of willing memories, and somewhere in that reserve, in a vault beneath the palace, sat a small glass vial containing every moment her mother had ever turned toward her, every lullaby, every scolding, every time her hand had smoothed August's hair off her forehead — and the thing wearing the king's crown had been sipping at it, slowly, for years, tasting her mother's love secondhand.

*It says they taste of choice.*

Her mother's memories had been taken under duress and gone thin. Hers had been given freely, and they burned.

She had fed the parasite her mother. Not with her body, not with her blood — with the only part of her mother the debt hadn't already claimed. She had walked into this counting house at nineteen and handed the monster the last of her family, and it had said *thank you*, and filed it, and waited nine years for her to be useful.

* * *

The door opened. The guild master stood in it, silhouetted against the hall's lamplight, and August closed the ledger with a calm she did not feel and would not have been able to name the source of if asked.

"You've read it," the master said. Not a question. The voice was the same as it had been nine years ago, oil over gravel, and August realized she had heard that voice across a counter when she was eleven, when her mother signed the first vial, when she herself signed the last. The master had been there for all of it. The master had *curated* all of it.

"I've read it," August said.

"And?"

"And you kept her." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of that. "You kept my mother in a drawer and let me balance your books for nine years."

"I kept *you*." The master stepped into the room, and the lamplight caught the smile, which was small and patient and terrible. "You were the finest acquisition this guild ever made, August Kavanagh. You simply hadn't finished being made. A thief who sells herself — do you know how rare that is? How *valuable*? The sigil has been asking after you for years. It remembers your signature. It says your memories taste of choice."

"Then it will be disappointed." August stood. Her legs held. "I'm not here to bargain. I never was. I needed the hour, and I needed the page, and now I've had both."

The master's smile did not waver. "The door is locked, child."

"The door was locked when I was eleven, too." She lifted the ledger. "But my mother got out of this building with something you wanted, once. It's in the family."

She threw the ledger through the window.

Glass went everywhere — the old leaded panes shattering outward into the rain, and the master lunged not for August but for the book, because the book was the master's whole soul, and that lunge was the half-second August needed. She went over the desk, through the broken window, into the wet dark of the counting-house roof, with the master's shout behind her and the rain hitting her face like a hand trying to hold her back.

She ran. She had gotten good at running.

But she ran differently now. Three days ago she had been fleeing a map. Tonight she was running toward a vault, because somewhere beneath the palace, in the sigil's reserve, in a drawer the master had kept for nine years the way a miser keeps a coin — there was a vial with her mother's voice in it. Her mother's laugh. Her mother's hand on her hair.

And on the final turn of the thirteenth moon, the sigil meant to drink it.

She dropped from the roof into the alley, rolled, came up running, and did not stop until she reached the smuggler's cellar door and beat on it with the flat of her fist, rain streaming down her face, her breath tearing.

The door opened. Bramble stood there with a lamp, his ruined hand braced on the frame, and when he saw her face he went white, because Bramble had always known, and he had carried it for nine years, and it was finally standing on the doorstep in the rain.

"You read it," he said.

"You knew." It wasn't an accusation. It was barely even a question. It was just the two of them in the doorway, the truth between them, cold as the rain.

"I tried to buy it back," he said, and his voice broke on the second word. "Every coin I've touched since. I tried, August. The guild doesn't sell what it's feeding to a god."

Behind him, Wren appeared at the top of the cellar stairs with Ossian's shadow behind her, and the dog pressed his great head against August's hip, and she stood in the rain with her found family around her and her real family in a glass drawer beneath the palace, and she said the only thing left to say:

"Thirteen turns. We're running out of moon."
