## 16. The Descent to the Moonvault

The last stair before the vault approach was made of moonstone, and it was warm.

Wren knew stone. She'd slept on it, stolen across it, bled on it in a hundred alleys of the capital's underbelly, and she knew the way cold lived in it like a tenant that never paid rent. This stone was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and that was worse than any trap Bramble had warned them about.

"Everyone stop touching the walls," Bramble said, which was the first sensible thing anyone had said in an hour.

They came down single file: Sable first with her blade drawn and her guild keys strung along her belt like a rosary of thefts, then Ossian with his sword slung low and his scars hidden under a wrapped shirt that did nothing to hide how he flinched at every flicker of light, then August, then Wren, then the dog.

Nobody had wanted to bring the dog. The dog had simply been there when they left the smuggler's cellar, sitting in the doorway like a debt come due, and Wren had looked at him and said *he comes or I don't*, and that had settled it, because everyone in this ridiculous family had learned by now that Wren's threats were small but absolutely load-bearing.

She named him Turnip. Nobody had voted on it. That was how families worked, as far as she could tell — nobody voted, things just stuck.

"Turn eleven wanes," Ossian murmured. He said it the way other men counted heartbeats. Two nights left. Maybe less. The sigil beneath the palace was turning toward its final meal, and every hour they wasted was another hour it grew fatter on the king it wore.

The stair opened into a corridor that should not have existed.

Wren had grown up in the drowned catacombs. She knew every crack the old city had, every flooded chapel and bone-cellar and smuggler's run from the River Gate to the palace foundations. This corridor wasn't on any of her maps. It stretched ahead of them pale and smooth as the inside of a shell, lit from nowhere, and the air tasted faintly of copper and candlewax — the smell, Wren realized with a lurch, of the Vellum Guild's counting house, where August used to work, where memories were bottled and sold.

"It knows we're here," August said quietly.

"Course it does," Wren said. "We're in its throat."

* * *

Bramble found the first door forty paces in.

It stood alone in the middle of the corridor, freestanding, hinged to nothing — an oak door with iron studs, the kind you'd find on a townhouse in the Merchant Quarter. There was no wall around it. Just the door, standing in the pale light like a man waiting to be robbed.

"That's not real," Ossian said.

"Everything down here is real," August said. "That's the problem."

Sable approached it the way she approached everything, flat and unhurried, and pressed one gloved hand against the wood. Her face did something Wren had never seen it do. It softened.

"I know this door," Sable said.

"Don't open it," August said.

"It's my mother's house." Sable's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Before the guild bought me. Before any of it. There's bread on the table. I can smell it."

"Bramble." August turned. "What's behind our doors?"

The locksmith had gone grey. He was staring past the free-standing door at nothing, or at something only he could see. "A workshop," he said. "Mine. Before they exiled me. My tools are on the bench. My name is on the master's roll." His hand rose toward a handle that wasn't there. "I'm still on the roll. I never stopped being on the roll—"

"Bramble!" Wren grabbed his sleeve and yanked, hard, the way you'd grab a mark drifting toward a cutpurse's blade. "Oi. Look at me. Your tools rusted years ago. You told me. You told me over soup."

He blinked. The fog went out of his eyes slowly, like water draining. "Soup," he repeated.

"You complained about the salt. At length. You're very boring when you're sad, did you know that?"

Something like a laugh cracked out of him. "Yes," he said. "You've mentioned."

Behind the free-standing door, faintly, someone began to hum a lullaby.

Sable stepped back from it. Her jaw was tight enough to crack walnuts. "It builds doors out of what we want," she said. "And it puts them in our road."

"Then we don't walk through doors," August said. "Any of us. Whatever we see — we keep walking past it. Agreed?"

Nobody agreed loudly. But nobody disagreed, and in Wren's experience that was the best contract you ever got from frightened people.

* * *

The second door was Wren's.

She should have known. She should have smelled it coming, the way she smelled watchmen and bad luck. But it didn't look like a door at first. It looked like an alley mouth off the corridor's side, narrow and familiar, brick slick with rain, and leaning in the alley mouth was a woman with a shawl over her hair and flour on her hands.

"Mum," Wren said.

The word got out of her before she could stop it, small and stupid, and the woman smiled, and it was her mother's smile exactly, the one with the chipped tooth on the left side, the one Wren had spent three years deliberately forgetting because remembering it hurt more than hunger.

"There you are," her mother said. "You're late for supper. I kept yours warm."

"She's dead," Wren said, to the others, to herself, to the corridor. "Fever, when I was eight. I checked the burial rolls myself. Twice."

"Wren." Her mother tilted her head. "Love. Come inside. It's cold out there, and those people you're with — they'll leave you somewhere, eventually. People always do. But supper's warm, and I waited."

That was the knife in it. Not the face, not the smile. The truth underneath. Everyone left. Wren had learned it in orphanages and workhouses and on the streets, learned it so deep it was practically a bone in her body. August would finish her quest and go back to being a great lady. Ossian would find his lost year and not need a child guide anymore. Even Turnip would wander off someday after a better-smelling beggar.

But supper was warm. Supper was always warm, once, a long time ago, in a room that smelled of bread.

"Wren." August's voice, close behind her. Not grabbing. August never grabbed; she just put her voice where you could hold onto it. "What do you see?"

"My mum."

"What's she holding?"

Wren looked. Her mother's flour-white hands were empty. They were always empty. In the fever weeks, at the end, her mother hadn't been able to lift a cup.

"Nothing," Wren said. "Her hands are empty."

"And your mum never had empty hands. Not once. What did she always have?"

"A spoon." Wren's eyes stung. "Wooden spoon. Whacked me with it when I stole from the till."

"So walk past her, and if she whacks you with a spoon on the way, we'll all pretend we didn't see a legendary sewer-thief get beaten by a ghost with kitchenware."

Wren laughed, wet and ugly, and walked past her mother, close enough that the illusion's flour-dust smell washed over her, close enough to see that the chipped tooth was on the wrong side, and the thing wearing her mother's smile let the smile fall off its face all at once, like a mask dropped, and for half a heartbeat Wren saw what was underneath — something vast and patient and hungry, folded into a shape too large for the alley, watching her with interest.

Then she was past, and the alley was just brick and shadow again, and her legs were shaking.

"Good girl," Ossian said gruffly, and ruffled her hair, and she let him, because family.

* * *

Ossian's door was a battlefield.

It assembled itself out of the corridor's pale light as they watched — mud walls, sandbags, a sky the color of a bruise, and men in the uniforms of the Ninth Regiment moving among the wounded. Ossian stopped walking. Wren watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"That's Kell's Ford," he said. "The year I don't remember."

"The year they sold," August said gently.

In the illusion, a younger Ossian knelt beside a boy soldier and pressed a canteen into his hands, and the boy's mouth moved, and though no sound crossed over, Wren knew what dying men say. *Tell my mother.* They always said tell my mother.

"I gave orders there," Ossian said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "I know I did. The fragments — turn seven showed me. I pulled the pickets back to save my own wounded and left the village open, and they—" He couldn't finish it.

The illusory Ossian looked up, across the impossible distance, straight at the real one. And smiled. Held out a hand.

Come back. Come be judged. Come carry it properly, alone, the way you've been carrying everything since they scraped your year out of your skull and sold it by the vial.

"No," Ossian said.

It wasn't loud. It was quiet, actually, quieter than anything he'd said all day, and he said it while looking directly at the ghost of his own guilt.

"You're not my memory," he told it. "You're its bait. My memory has mud in it and fear in it and a boy named Tam who owed me two coppers, and you've got none of that, because you were built by something that's never bled. I'll remember Kell's Ford when I'm ready. On my terms. With these people standing next to me." He jerked his chin at the company. "Not alone. Never alone again. That's the whole point of what we're doing, you great pale leech. Did you think you could tempt a man with the thing he's most afraid of? We walk *toward* our fears. It's called courage. Look it up."

Turnip barked once, supportively.

The battlefield guttered like a candle in wind, and went out.

* * *

August's door was the worst one, and Wren knew it before August even stopped walking, because August had gone very still in the particular way she did when she was about to pay a price she'd already agreed to.

The door was small. Wooden, blue paint peeling. A cottage door, with herbs hanging above the lintel, and through the window glowed firelight, and a woman's silhouette moved within it, humming.

"Your mother," Wren whispered.

"The map was in her handwriting," August said. Her voice was careful, sanded smooth, like she'd practiced it. "Every route. Every annotation. I've been chasing her hand across this whole cursed city." She took one step toward the door. "Through that door she's alive. She's making tea. She'll ask me why I sold her, and I'll finally get to answer."

"August." Sable, of all people. The enforcer who'd hunted them across eleven turns of the moon, who'd turned her coat in a cellar full of ledgers, stood with her hand hovering near August's shoulder without quite touching. "I read the ledger. Your mother stole that map to protect people. Would she want you to take the door, or the road?"

August laughed — a broken, lovely sound. "You've gotten good at this, mentor."

"I had a brilliant student."

"The tea will be cold anyway," August said. "It's always cold, in the ones they build. That's how you know." She squared her shoulders and walked past the cottage door without looking at the window, though Wren saw her hand trail along the doorframe as she passed, one fingertip, barely, like touching a grave marker. "I'll see you at the end of this," she murmured, to the humming silhouette or to her mother or to no one. "When the count is clean. Wait for me there."

The door faded. The corridor went on.

* * *

Sable's door tried a different trick. It didn't build a memory. It built a future.

It showed a hall — the guild's great counting hall, scrubbed clean, sunlight through high windows, and Sable standing at the master's desk with the master's chain of office around her neck, and the guild reformed under her hand into something honest. Ledgers balanced. Debts forgiven. Children apprenticed instead of purchased. Every cruelty she'd ever enforced, undone by her own decree, and the whole thing shimmering with the terrible plausibility of a dream you almost believe.

Wren watched the enforcer look at it for a long time.

"You'd be good at that," Wren said.

"I would," Sable said. "That's why it's a lie. Redemption isn't a room you walk into. It's a thing you do with your hands, one day at a time, and it starts with finishing what we came here to finish." She drew her blade and — Wren's breath caught — drove it through the center of the illusory door, pinning the dream to whatever nothing stood behind it. The vision shrieked, soundlessly, and dissolved around the steel. "Also," Sable added, retrieving her sword, "I look terrible in chains of office."

"You look terrifying in everything," Bramble said.

"Thank you."

* * *

Bramble's door was last, and it was the smallest, and it broke Wren's heart.

It was a cell. A guild cell, whitewashed, with a cot and a bucket and a slot in the door, and on the cot sat an old man with ruined hands, and the old man looked up and said, in Bramble's own voice: "You could have stayed honest. You could have taken their pardon. All you had to do was open the vault for them, all those years ago, and you'd be free now. Free, and forgiven, and not limping through sewers with children and dogs."

Bramble stood before it with his picks in his hand — he'd been carrying them since the gate above, turning them over and over like worry-beads.

"I know," he said to the old man. "I've thought about it every day for nine years. Here's what I know that you don't." He held up his hands, the scarred, clever, ruined-and-ruinous hands. "These aren't for sale. Not to guilds. Not to sigils. Not to gods, if any ask." He turned his back on the cell. "Locksmith's rule. The lock serves the door. The door serves the house. And the house—" he glanced back at the four ragged souls and one enormous dog behind him, and his voice caught, just slightly, "—the house is whoever's walking with you."

The cell went dark. The corridor brightened.

Ahead of them, at the end of the pale passage, stairs descended — real stairs this time, Wren was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of true locks and false ones, stone worn hollow by ancient feet, breathing cold air up from the dark. And below, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to count.

*Twelve,* it said. *Almost home.*

"Right," Wren said, and wiped her face with her sleeve, and picked up the dog's lead, because somebody had to be brave first and it might as well be the smallest one. "Last two turns. Let's go ruin its appetite."

They started down. And behind them, at the top of the stairs, every door they had refused opened at once — all together, all silently — and did not follow.
