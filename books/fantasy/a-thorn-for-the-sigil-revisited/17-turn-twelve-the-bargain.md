## 17. Turn Twelve: The Bargain

The antechamber of the Moonvault was a bowl of white stone, and it drank the light.

August felt that first — before the cold, before the weight of twelve turns pressing down on her like a hand at the back of her neck. The walls were pale marble shot through with veins of silver, and every lantern they carried seemed to give up half its flame upon entering, as though the room levied a tax on brightness. Their footsteps came back to them wrong: too slow, too soft, like sounds wading through deep water.

Behind her, Ossian's breathing had gone shallow and deliberate — the breathing of a man managing pain. Bramble's bandage had soaked through again on the last stair, and he'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt somewhere around the third landing. Wren pressed close against August's side, small and warm and vibrating with the effort of being brave. Sable brought up the rear with her blade drawn and her guild keys strung across her chest like a rosary she no longer believed in.

And above them all, through a hundred feet of living rock, the twelfth moon was rising.

August could not see it. She could feel it — a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like copper coins and old paper at the root of her tongue. Every memory-thief in the city would be feeling it tonight. Every ledger in the Vellum Guild would be trembling on its shelf.

"Steady," Ossian murmured, though whether it was for Wren or for himself, August couldn't say.

The antechamber opened before them like the inside of a bell. At its far end stood a door.

It was not grand. That was the horror of it. After the drowned catacombs, after the Court of the Hollow King with its gilded rot, after the vault gate Bramble had bled to open — this final door was plain grey stone, uncarved, unadorned, with no lock that August could see. It simply waited, the way a debt waits.

And standing before it, hands folded behind his back like a man admiring architecture, was Master Corvane.

* * *

He did not turn when they entered. He let them see him first in profile: the long grey coat, immaculate despite the descent; the silver chain of office; the narrow face August had known since she was nine years old, when he'd signed her intake ledger himself and told her, kindly, that forgetting was a mercy the guild sold at fair price.

"Sable," he said pleasantly. "You're late."

"I'm gone," Sable said. Her voice didn't shake. August was proud of her for that, and knew it was costing her everything. "Your keys opened the way down, Corvane. They don't open you a path back up."

"Mm." He turned then, and his eyes found August as if the others were furniture. "August. Look at you. Rooftop grime, smuggler's dust, a child's handprint on your sleeve. You were always one for pockets, but I never taught you to pick up strays."

"You never taught me anything," August said. "You *priced* me. There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was warm. It was the smile from the counting house, from the nights she'd balanced impossible ledgers and he'd brought her tea like an apprentice worth cultivating. "There always has been. That's why we're here, you and I. Not them." A small gesture dismissed Bramble, Wren, Ossian, even Sable. "This is between the guild and its finest auditor."

"The guild master serves the sigil," Sable said flatly. "We read your ledger, Corvane. We know what happens on the thirteenth turn."

"Do you?" He tilted his head. "Then tell me. Go on. I'll wait."

Sable's jaw tightened. Because the ledger had been explicit about the feeding — the whole city's memories poured down the sigil's throat like wine into a bottomless cup — but it had said nothing about what came *after*. Nothing about what the sigil became when it finished eating. Nothing about what Corvane expected to receive for his service.

"That's what I thought," Corvane said gently. "You know the shape of the cage. You don't know the door." He spread his hands. "So let me make an offer. One offer, honestly given, which is more than the sigil ever gave any of us."

* * *

The change came over him slowly, and August watched it happen because someone had to.

First his shoulders dropped, as though strings had been cut. Then his breathing lengthened into something too even, too patient, the respiration of a thing that wore breath the way a man wears a borrowed coat. His eyes stayed open, but the light in them went still — pond-water eyes, depthless and cold — and when he spoke again, the voice was Corvane's voice layered over something older, something that resonated in the marble itself, in August's teeth, in the scar-tissue of Ossian's memory-scars, which August knew without looking had begun to ache.

"*Thief,*" it said.

Wren made a small sound and grabbed fistfuls of August's coat. Ossian stepped forward, planting himself between the creature and the boy, and August saw his hands tremble — saw him force them still, the soldier's discipline holding against whatever the sigil was showing him behind his own eyes.

"You came all this way," the sigil said through Corvane's mouth, and Corvane's lips barely moved, the words arriving more than spoken. "*Down drowned stairs. Past your dead mother's handwriting. Past the dog you buried and the friend you betrayed and the year you cannot remember.*" Its gaze slid to Ossian, and Ossian flinched as if struck. "*I have been reading you since the ninth turn, thief. Do you know how rare you are? How delicious? A mind that opens locks made of memory. Do you know what I am?*"

"A parasite," August said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The oracle told me. You eat kings slow."

"*I curate,*" the sigil said, with something like offense. "*A king who remembers everything drowns in guilt. A king who remembers nothing is a puppet. I keep only what serves. For three hundred years I have kept Aldric of House Veyra alive, beloved, and perfectly manageable. And now my turn comes due, and my vessel fails, and I require—" it paused, savoring— "*a new arrangement.*"

"And that requires a memory-thief," August said. "That's why the map existed. That's why my mother's handwriting was on it. You needed bait good enough to drag a thief across the city and down into the dark, and you couldn't write it yourself, so you used hers. Stolen from the guild's vaults. From her effects." Her throat closed around the words. "You used my dead mother as a fishhook."

"*Ledgers require balancing,*" the sigil said. "*Yours required a debt worth chasing.*"

Bramble spat on the marble. Wren was crying silently, furious at crying. But August stepped forward — one step, two — until she stood closer to the thing wearing her former master than anyone sane should stand.

"Say your offer," she said. "You didn't come all this way to monologue."

* * *

The sigil smiled with Corvane's mouth, and it was almost human, and that was the trap of it.

"*Leave,*" it said. "*That is all. Turn around. Climb the stairs. By morning you will be in Vellum House with the ledgers balanced and the guild dissolved or reformed as you please — it is yours, August. I have watched you count for fifteen years. No one counts like you. No one sees where the numbers hide their sins.*"

"Corvane's offer," Sable said sharply. "Not yours."

"*His terms, amended by me. Hear the whole of it.*" The voice dropped lower, and the marble carried it into August's bones. "*The king dies on the thirteenth turn. That is fixed; even I do not unmake what I am. But the city need not burn. The guild master lives. Your companions live — the locksmith's wound closes clean, the soldier keeps what shreds of himself remain, the child grows up in a house with a roof, the traitor-warden retires with her pension and her regrets. And you—*"

It let the pause stretch until August's heart was hammering against her ribs.

"*—you keep everything. Every memory. Your mother's face. The sound of her voice saying your name. The night you sold yourself for the locksmith's freedom and every night before and after. Fifteen years of counting house candlelight. The courier dying in your arms. All of it stays exactly where I cannot reach it, because I will not need to reach it. Walk away, and I never touch you at all.*"

"*Live rich,*" it said softly. "*Remember everything. Lose only a king you met once, lucid for a single minute, begging a stranger for death. He is already lost. He has been lost since before you were born. What is one hollow man against everything you love?*"

And oh, it was good. It was so good. August stood in the white silence and felt the offer settle over her like a coat cut precisely to her measurements, and she understood, with the clarity of a woman reading a forged ledger line, that this was the true trap. Not the illusions on the stairs. Not the drowned catacombs. This. The sigil had eaten kings for three hundred years not because it was strong but because it was *accurate*. It knew exactly what a soul was worth to itself.

Her mother's voice. She had traded it away once already, to an oracle in the Market of Whispers, and she remembered the hole it had left, the shape of an absence shaped exactly like a woman singing. If she walked away, she would carry that absence to her grave. If she stayed—

If she stayed, she would carry worse. She would carry nothing.

She thought of chapter nineteen of her own life, though she could not have named it: the blank-eyed waking among strangers. Some part of her, the part that kept ledgers, had already run the numbers and seen the red ink waiting at the bottom of the page. The sigil would not let her walk out of the king's mind whole. It would take payment in the only coin she had.

But knowing a debt is coming and choosing it anyway — that was different from being robbed. That was the one freedom the guild had never figured out how to price.

"August," Ossian said quietly. "Whatever you're weighing — we're not collateral. Don't let it put us on the scale."

"It already did," August said. "That's the whole trick." She looked at each of them. Bramble, grey-faced, gripping his wounded side, nodding anyway. Wren, tear-streaked, chin up, glaring at a god with the ferocity of a child defending a home. Sable, blade bare, eyes wet and level. Ossian, whose erased year had cost him everything and who had come down here regardless, because some debts are paid forward.

"No," she said aloud, to the sigil. "No deal."

* * *

The stillness that followed lasted three heartbeats. In it, August heard the twelfth moon crest the rock above them — felt it, rather, a click like the last tumbler of a lock seating home, deep beneath the world.

Then Corvane's face broke.

Not into rage — into grief, which was somehow far worse. The sigil withdrew from his expression like a tide going out, and what remained was just a tired old man in a fine coat, sagging under the weight of his own spine, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on his cheeks.

"You stupid girl," Corvane whispered, in his own voice, cracked and human. "Do you know what I gave up? Do you know what I was promised? Three hundred years it has worn thirteen kings, and every one of them it ate from the inside out, and I thought — I thought if I served it faithfully, if I fed it well, it would *spare* me. It showed me my daughter, August. My daughter, who died forty years ago. It shows her to me every night. And you think I wouldn't trade a city for one more evening of her?"

"I know," August said, and her voice gentled. "That's exactly why I'm going to stop it. Because it does that to everyone. It's doing it to you right now, and you're helping."

"She's *real* to me—"

"She's a hook," August said. "And so was I. So was the map. It baits every trap with the thing you can't refuse." She drew a slow breath, and the copper taste on her tongue sharpened, and beneath her feet she felt the vault door begin — very faintly, very terribly — to warm. "Turn twelve, Corvane. One turn left. Help us, or get out of our way, but either way the thirteenth moon rises on a free king or it doesn't rise at all."

The old man stared at her. Behind his eyes, something vast and patient recalculated, and August felt it look at her — truly look, the way an appraiser looks at an object whose value it has finally, reluctantly, revised upward.

"*Then come in, thief,*" the sigil said, from nowhere, from the marble, from the marrow of her bones. "*The door is open. It was always open. It was built for you.*"

The plain grey stone split down the middle without a sound and swung inward onto darkness — and from the darkness came a smell August knew better than her own name: vellum and ink and candle smoke, the counting house smell, the smell of every ledger she had ever balanced.

It smelled like home. That was how she knew she was walking into the thing's mouth.

Ossian caught her arm. "Last chance to be sensible."

"Sensible left with the courier," August said. She looked at her family — her strange, damaged, impossible family — and found she was smiling. "Hold me steady in there. Whatever happens. Whatever I forget. Hold on to me, and when I wake up hating strangers, be very annoying about reminding them who they used to be."

"We can do annoying," Wren said wetly.

"Better than anyone," August agreed.

She stepped through the door, and the darkness closed behind her like a ledger shutting, and somewhere above, the thirteenth moon began to climb.
