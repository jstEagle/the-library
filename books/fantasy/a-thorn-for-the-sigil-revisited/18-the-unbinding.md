## 18. The Unbinding

The antechamber of the Moonvault was a throat of white stone, and at the far end of it, behind a gate that had cost Bramble three broken picks and a ribbon of blood, the king sat on a throne that was not his.

Aldric had been lowered there — or had crawled there; August would never know — and bound to the seat with bands of silvered light that pulsed in time with nothing living. His eyes were open. They had been open for eleven turns of the moon, if Ossian's fragments were to be believed, and they were not empty. That was the horror of it. They were *full* — full of everything the sigil had ever eaten, every memory it had stripped from him like meat from a bone, stored somewhere behind his ruined gaze so it could be savored slowly over years.

"August." Sable's voice came from her left, low and even, the voice she'd used in training yards a lifetime ago. "Your hands are shaking."

"They're supposed to shake. If they weren't shaking I'd be worried about them."

Behind her, Wren made a sound that wanted to be a laugh and couldn't quite manage it. Bramble was leaning against the wall, his wounded arm pressed tight to his ribs, gray-faced. The festering worry in Ossian's eyes had gone past worry into something harder, but he said nothing about the arm now. There would be time for arms later, or there wouldn't.

The guild master lay where he had fallen — no. Not fallen. *Kneeling.* He knelt in the center of the antechamber with his head bowed as though in prayer, and out of his mouth, when it moved, came a voice that was not his. It was older. It was patient. It was the sound a ledger makes when it closes.

*Thief,* it said. *You came after all.*

"You knew I would." August stepped forward. The white stone drank her footfalls. "That's what the map was for. You drew my mother's hand onto it because you knew grief would bring me faster than greed. You needed someone who could unbind you without cracking the vessel." She looked at Aldric, at the terrible fullness of his eyes. "You need a thief."

*I need the best thief,* the sigil said through the master's slack mouth, and there was something almost like admiration in it. *And here you are. One last bargain, then, before we begin. Walk away. Keep every memory you own. The girl in the cellar with the smuggler's candles. The oracle's booth. Your mother's face — oh, I know what you traded for me, I know precisely what you paid. I will give it back. All of it. Every stolen thing restored to its shelf. You will die rich in yourself, August Delacroix, in a warm house, with your family around you, and I will find another way. There is always another way.*

For one breath — one whole turn of her heart — she saw it. The house. Candlelight. Her mother's voice saying her name the way it had said it before the vials, before the ledgers, before the debt. It was so vivid she could taste the tea.

"That's the trick, isn't it," she said quietly. "You show me exactly what I'm about to lose. That's how you feed. You make people choose the smaller keeping."

*Everyone chooses the smaller keeping.*

"Not everyone." She pulled off her coat, folded it, set it down. Beneath her sleeve, the needle-scar from the oracle's trade still showed pale. "Ossian. Sable. Hold my shoulders. Whatever happens — whatever I say, however I scream — don't let go, and don't let me fall out of it halfway. If I come back with only part of the sigil loose, it'll burrow deeper than before and we don't get a second window."

Ossian crossed the floor and knelt behind her, his big hands settling on her shoulders like an anchor dropped. His scars were glowing faintly, she noticed — thin silver seams along his forearms, brightening as the vault's pulse quickened. He had told her once, in the drowned catacombs, what it felt like when the erased year pressed against the inside of his skin. He looked at her now with the expression of a man holding a door closed against weather.

"I've got you," he said.

"And I've got the rest of you," Sable said, taking the other shoulder. Her grip was steel wrapped in leather. Her voice cracked, just once, just slightly: "I trained your hands to be steady, girl. Be steady."

"Bramble. Wren."

"We're here," Wren said. The child's voice was very small in the great white throat of the room. "We'll be right here when you — when you come back."

*When,* August thought. Not *if*. The family had voted, in their way. They had decided the word for her.

She put her fingers to the king's temples, closed her eyes, and went in.

* * *

The inside of Aldric's mind was a burned library.

Shelves rose out of smoke in every direction, miles of them, sagging under the weight of nothing — because the books were hollow. She pulled one down at random and it crumbled: *a coronation, banners, rain on gold.* Ash. Another: *a queen laughing in a garden.* Ash. The sigil had eaten with method, shelf by shelf, leaving the spines standing like gravestones. What remained whole clustered in the low places, near the floor, in the dark — small things it hadn't bothered with yet. A smell of hay. A lesson in falconry. Someone singing badly.

And in the center of everything, crouched on the ruin of the throne dais, the sigil itself.

It did not look like anything. That was the first thing she understood, and the thing that nearly broke her before the work began: the sigil had no shape because it was a *stitch*, a seam, a knot tied through the fabric of a mind, and knots have no faces. It was tension. It was the pull of thread drawn too tight across twenty years. When it noticed her — and it noticed immediately, the whole library shivering like water — it simply tightened, and somewhere far above, in the world outside, a king's body arched against silver bonds.

*You cannot unpick me,* it said, without speaking, in the language of tautness itself. *I was sewn by gods.*

"No," August said aloud, and her voice worked strangely here, arriving flat and close as if her mouth were at her own ear. "You were sewn by something that wanted to live forever and didn't want to earn it. That's not a god. I've read the ledgers. Gods pay for what they take."

She took out her needles.

They were not metal. In this place, tools were made of what you knew, and what August knew was thread — the tensile strength of a binding, the direction a stitch runs, the difference between a lock meant to keep people out and a lock meant to keep something *in*. She had spent ten years balancing accounts of impossible things. She had spent ten years learning exactly how much force a structure can lose before it falls.

She found the first knot at the edge of the library, where the sigil's oldest work had scarred over, and set her fingers into it the way Bramble set picks into a ward.

And the sigil began to eat her.

* * *

It did not take memories all at once. It took them in order, deliberately, like a diner working through courses, and it made sure she felt each plate leave the table.

The first stitch came free with a sensation like tearing silk, and in payment the sigil reached into her and took the smell of her mother's kitchen — bread and tallow and dried lavender — so cleanly that August could not afterward have said what a kitchen smelled like at all. She gasped, kept her fingers in the knot, and pulled the second stitch loose.

It took the sound of rain on the counting-house roof.

Third stitch. It took her tenth winter, entire — the fever, the woman who had nursed her through it, the name of the woman, gone, gone, a hole in her personal history shaped like kindness.

*Stop,* the sigil said, almost gently. *Look at the arithmetic. You are spending a life to save a stranger. No ledger balances that way.*

"Wrong," August said through her teeth. Blood was running from her nose now; she could feel it, hot on her lip, absurd in this place of ash and shelves. Outside her body, dimly, she heard Ossian's voice saying her name, steady as a metronome, and Sable's grip shifting to catch the tremor that had begun in her spine. "Ledgers balance *both ways*. You taught me that yourself, you bastard. You've been eating this man for twenty years. Every stitch I pull is a repayment. We're just — deciding — whose account it comes out of—"

Fourth stitch. Fifth. Sixth.

It took the guild years in a long slow swallow. Master Veyre's face dissolved mid-memory while she stood in it, so that she watched a man she had known for a decade become a stranger wearing familiar clothes. The first map she ever copied. The first rule she ever broke. The night she learned what happened to donors who gave too much — the night her rules were born — unraveled into blank paper. She felt her wariness lifting like fog and understood, distantly, with a kind of academic horror, that the woman who emerged on the other side of this would trust too easily, because the scars that taught her caution would be gone.

*Good,* the sigil crooned. *Yes. Become simpler. Become soft. A soft thief drops her needle.*

Her hand did not drop the needle.

Seventh stitch. Eighth. The burned library groaned around her; whole corridors of ash-shelves were collapsing now, and in the gaps, released things were stirring — fragments of Aldric coming loose from the sigil's grip like fish freed from a net. She saw a young man in a guard's uniform laughing with another soldier whose face she recognized from Ossian's descriptions, and realized with a lurch that Ossian had served him, that these two men had been friends and neither remembered it. The fragment of Aldric looked at her with his own eyes — young, unruined — and mouthed something she couldn't hear, and was swept away into the smoke.

Ninth stitch. Tenth. It took her mother's face.

Not her mother's voice — that had never been hers to lose, not entirely, not since the vial — but the face above it, the particular tilt of her head, the line of her jaw, the exact brown of her eyes. August stood in the smoking library and reached for the image and found smooth blankness where it should have been, a wall with no painting, and the grief that hit her was worse for being *abstract* — mourning without a memorial, loss without a shape. She was crying and she did not know at what.

*Now you understand,* the sigil said. *This is what I do. This is all I do, forever, and I do it well. Join me instead of fighting me and I will give you back more than you lost — I will give you her whole—*

"She's not currency," August said. Her voice came out wrecked and did not care. She found the eleventh knot, the twelfth, deep in the architecture now, where the stitching ran through load-bearing memory, through the king's sense of his own name. "Nothing is. That's the whole — the whole ledger — that's the whole *count*—"

Twelfth stitch. Free.

The library screamed. Not the king — the *seam*, the tension itself, snapping back through twenty years of tautness, and every hollow book on every shelf rang like a struck bell with the ghost of what it used to hold. The sigil made one last reach, desperate now, flailing, and took the only thing left within grasp:

Her family's faces.

Bramble's crooked grin, gone. Wren's gap-toothed scowl, gone. Ossian's scarred forearms, Sable's steel-and-leather grip — she felt them being lifted out of her even as, in the world outside, those same hands held her body upright. Somewhere very far away a child was screaming her name and August Delacroix, deep in the unraveling mind of a king, could not remember which child, or why the sound mattered, only that it mattered *enormously*, that it was the most important sound in any world—

The thirteenth stitch.

She took hold of it blind. She had nothing left to see with. She pulled.

* * *

The knot let go.

There was no light, no thunder. There was a sound like a sigh — twenty years of held breath leaving one body — and the burned library filled, from the floor upward, with clean dark water, and the water was warm, and the hollow books began to swell and soften and remember their pages, and August sank into the water knowing, in the last coin of awareness she possessed, that she had won, that she had paid, that she could not remember what she had paid *with*, and that this was fine, this was the arithmetic, this balanced—

Then nothing.

* * *

Outside, the silver bands shattered like sugar glass.

Aldric came off the throne already screaming — not the sigil's scream but a human one, ragged and wet, the sound of a drowning man surfacing — and collapsed into Bramble's arms, and Bramble went down under him, bad arm and all, and did not care. The king's eyes were streaming. "The garden," he was saying, over and over. "The queen, the garden, she was laughing, I remember, I remember—" Twenty years returning all at once, and he clutched at each of them like a man catching falling plates.

The guild master also fell — forward, empty, the puppet cut from its strings, and Sable was on him in a heartbeat with her knee between his shoulders and her blade at the tendons of his hands. "Not dead," she snarled, to no one and everyone. "*Not dead.* He answers for the final turn. He answers for all of it." But her eyes weren't on him. Her eyes were on August.

August lay in Ossian's arms, breathing shallowly, blood dried black from her nose across her lips and chin. Her body was whole. Her hands were steady — Sable had trained them well; even unconscious, some steadiness remained.

"August." Ossian's voice was rough as gravel. "Hey. Hey. You're through. You did it. Come back now."

Her eyelids fluttered. Opened.

She looked up at him with the polite, faintly frightened blankness of a stranger woken on a train. Her gaze traveled his face — the scars, the gray in his beard — and found nothing there. It traveled to Sable, kneeling over the prone master, and found nothing. To Wren, who had gone absolutely still, one small hand pressed over their own mouth.

"Hello," August said. Her voice was hoarse and gentle and wrong, wrong, wrong. "I'm sorry — you have me at a disadvantage. Do I..." She frowned, searching, and the searching came up empty, and she was too honest, even now, especially now, to pretend otherwise. "Do I know you?"

Wren's hand slid slowly down from their mouth.

"Yeah," Wren said, in a voice that shook only once and then chose, deliberately, not to. "Yeah, you do. And we're going to tell you all about it."
