## 19. Aftermath of a Crown

Ossian had held dying men in his arms before. He had held them in the mud at Cael's Ford and in the snow outside Varrow Keep, and he knew the particular weight a body took on when the thing inside it finally let go — heavier, somehow, as if the soul had been holding itself up all along and only now surrendered to gravity.

August's body went heavy in exactly that way.

The light went first. It had been pouring out of her for what felt like hours — threads of silver tearing free of her eyes, her mouth, the seams of her scars, streaming across the vault floor into the writhing knot of stitched light that was the sigil. Ossian knelt behind her with Sable, one arm braced across her collarbone, the other pinning her wrist, because when the unbinding began she had convulsed like a woman being flayed from the inside out. His own scarred arms burned where they touched her. Every stolen year in him had woken up screaming since they crossed the antechamber threshold.

Then the screaming stopped. Hers, and the thing's.

The sigil came apart the way a rotted rope comes apart — not cut but crumbled, stitch by stitch unraveling into dust that drifted upward instead of down, rising toward the vault's painted ceiling where thirteen moons circled in silver leaf. Twelve were dull. The thirteenth, the false one, flared once — a last indignant glare — and went dark forever.

And August slumped against Ossian's chest like a child asleep.

"August." He shook her shoulder. Gently at first, then not. "August. It's done. You did it. Come back."

Her head lolled. Her breathing was slow and even and terribly calm, the breathing of someone very far away. Her face, which had been a mask of agony for six turns of the moon, was smooth now. Blank. He had seen that smoothness before, on the faces of men after the guild's memory-wrights finished with them. He had worn it himself once, waking in a barracks infirmary with a year missing and everyone around him pretending it was nothing.

"No," he heard himself say. "No, no — August—"

Sable caught his forearm. "Don't shake her. You'll do damage."

"She gave *everything*, Sable. I felt it go through her. I felt—" His voice cracked, which embarrassed him even now, even here. A soldier's voice should not crack. "I felt my own years come back while hers left. Like a tide going out of her and into me."

"I know." Sable's voice was low and steady, but her hands, when she reached past him to press two fingers beneath August's jaw, were shaking. "Pulse is strong. She's alive. She's just—" Sable stopped. Started again. "She paid."

Across the antechamber, King Aldric made a sound none of them had ever heard him make.

It started as a rattle, deep in his ruined chest, and built into something between a sob and a laugh, and then it was simply weeping — great, ugly, human weeping from a man who had not wept in eleven years, kneeling on the cold mosaic floor with his crown knocked askew and his hands pressed over his face. The sigil's husk had fallen from his brow like a burnt crown of thorns. Where it had sat, the skin was raw and pale as new flesh.

"Eleven years," the king gasped. "I was — there were people — my wife — I could see her the whole time, I was *in* the room with her, I watched myself—" He couldn't finish it. He doubled over, and the sound he made then was the sound Ossian remembered from Cael's Ford, from men finding the bodies of their brothers. Grief without any armor left on it.

Wren appeared at the king's side before anyone thought to stop her — small, quick, filthy from the catacombs, still clutching the iron pry-bar she'd refused to put down since turn ten. She looked up at the weeping monarch of Vael with the frank appraisal of a child deciding whether a stray dog was safe.

"You're the king," she said.

"Yes," Aldric managed.

"You cried," she said, with approval. "Good."

And then she did something no courtier would ever have dared: she patted his knee twice and stood guard beside him, pry-bar across her thin shoulders, glaring at the dark doorway as if daring the whole palace to interrupt a man's grief.

Bramble came next, limping, one hand pressed to the bandage on his side that Ossian had been quietly terrified about for two days. The old locksmith surveyed the ruined antechamber — the shattered guild master's chair, the scorch-fan of sigil-dust, the dead vials of the master's private collection cracked open along the wall like a nest of broken eggs — and then his eyes found August in Ossian's arms, and his face did something terrible.

"No," Bramble said quietly. "Oh, girl. No."

"She's breathing," Ossian said. It was the only sentence he had. He kept saying it. "She's breathing. She's breathing."

"Of course she's breathing, you great ox, that's not the—" Bramble crouched beside them, wincing, and took August's limp hand in both of his cracked ones. "August. It's Bramble. Can you hear me? We got in because of you. The lock opened for you, do you remember? The lock *sang* for you?"

Nothing moved in her face.

Sable rose. She had been standing over the guild master.

He lay where the unbinding's backlash had thrown him — alive, breathing shallowly, his fine coat scorched down one side, his eyes open and fixed on the ceiling where the thirteenth moon had gone dark. He didn't struggle when Sable turned him over. He seemed to have gone somewhere far away himself, though his was a different country than August's.

"You're under arrest," Sable said to him. Her voice was flat as a ledger line. "By the authority of the Crown and the evidence in this book." She lifted the master's private ledger — the one August had read in chains, the one Sable had read in the guild house three nights later, the one that named every payment the sigil had taken and every city district scheduled to be drained on the final turn. "Every entry. Every name. Every price. It goes to the king now."

The guild master smiled faintly at the ceiling. "It's dead," he said. His voice sounded almost peaceful, and it made Ossian's skin crawl worse than any threat had. "Do you understand? Eleven years I carried it. Fed it. Kept its ledgers. And it never once spoke to me kindly until tonight, when it begged." He laughed, soft and awful. "It begged, and a thief unpicked it anyway."

"A thief," Sable said, "is going to testify against you. So will I. So will this ledger." She bound his wrists with cord from her belt, quick and professional, the way she'd taught August to tie knots in another life. "Get up. Walk. Or I'll have the soldier carry you, and he'll enjoy it."

Ossian would have enjoyed it. He said so.

But mostly he stayed where he was, on the floor of the Moonvault, holding a woman who had forgotten him.

* * *

They came up out of the vault in the grey hour before dawn, when the capital's bells were silent and the streets belonged to cats and lamplighters.

Aldric walked on his own feet, refusing every offered shoulder, bareheaded — Wren had quietly pocketed the crown when no one was looking, and no one was going to ask for it back. He wept intermittently the whole climb, silently, wiping his face with the sleeve of his servant disguise like a man relearning the motion. Twice he stopped and gripped the wall and whispered a name. Ossian didn't learn whose until later: his daughter, who had been four years old when the sigil settled in, who was fifteen now and had been raised to fear her father.

At the palace's service gate, the guards took one look at the hollow-eyed king and dropped their halberds. Then they took one look at Sable marching the guild master in cords, and picked the halberds back up, and pointed them at *him*, which Ossian found deeply satisfying.

Word spread the way fire spreads in a dry summer. By the time they reached the great hall, half the household staff had abandoned their duties to line the walls, and someone had run for the Royal Physician, and someone else had run for the Lord Chancellor, who arrived at a dead sprint in a nightrobe, took in the scene — the weeping king, the bound guild master, the bleeding locksmith, the soldier carrying an unconscious young woman — and displayed the good sense to simply stand aside.

"The physician," Aldric said, and his voice, though wrecked, had something in it Ossian hadn't heard in eleven years of watching that puppet-king from the ranks of the honor guard: authority. Real authority, bought with suffering. "See to her first. She bought back my soul; the least the Crown can do is buy back her body. Whatever it costs. Whatever she needs." He looked down at August's face, slack against Ossian's shoulder. "I don't know her name."

"August," Ossian said. "August Delacroix."

"August Delacroix," the king repeated, tasting it, committing it. "Write it in the morning's first dispatch. Write it so large the historians can't misspell it."

The physician came at a run, saw the blankness in August's open-but-unseeing eyes within thirty seconds, and did everything right: warmth, fluids, checks of pulse and pupil, murmured questions that drew no answer. In the end he straightened, met Ossian's gaze over her body, and delivered the verdict with the gentleness of his profession.

"Her mind isn't hurt, as far as I can tell. But the rooms are empty. Whoever she was — whoever she knew —" He spread his hands. "You'd know better than I whether anyone lives in those rooms still. Sometimes they come back on their own. Sometimes they don't."

Ossian nodded and kept holding her, because putting her down felt like a second theft.

* * *

They gave her the best bed in the guest wing, which Bramble inspected with professional contempt and pronounced adequate, and then the strange vigil began.

The physician had said watch her, keep her warm, talk to her if you like — talking does no harm. So they talked.

Ossian took the first watch because he couldn't imagine leaving. He sat in the high-backed chair by the window with his sword across his knees out of pure habit and told her things, quietly, the way you'd talk to a sleeping comrade on night picket.

"You're in the palace. The real one, above ground, no water anywhere, which I know you'll want verified. The king is alive and crying in the next room, which sounds bad but is the best news this country has had in eleven years. Sable arrested the guild master with his own ledger and he didn't even fight her. Bramble is downstairs terrorizing the kitchen staff into making soup. Wren fell asleep sitting upright on the windowsill like a gargoyle and nobody has the heart to move her. There's a dog. His name is Turnip. You love the dog. That's important, write that down."

Nothing. Her breath rose and fell. Candlelight moved on her still face.

His own arms ached in the old places, the places where the memory-wrights had cut the year out of him, and he understood — distantly, dreadfully — that some part of the tide had indeed flowed both ways. Fragments were surfacing. A banner. A burning granary. An order given in a voice that had been his. He would have to deal with that soon. He had promised August, back in the cellar on turn seven, that they would carry their ghosts together, hers and his, because shared weight was the only kind that didn't crush.

Now she had set down her whole load, and he was holding both.

Near dawn, Wren woke, padded across the room, climbed onto the foot of the bed without asking anyone, and sat cross-legged, studying August's face from a distance of about eight inches.

"She looks like me," Wren announced, "when I'm pretending I don't know somebody."

"Wren—"

"When the caravans change crews. When the old crew sells you on to the new one. You look at everybody like, *who are you supposed to be to me.*" Wren's chin came up, defiant, eyes too bright. "That's what she's doing. Even asleep."

Ossian had no answer for that, so he didn't insult either of them by inventing one.

When Sable relieved the watch at midmorning, she paused in the doorway a long time — Sable, who never paused in doorways — before crossing to the bed. She stood over August the way she must once have stood over a recruit, and Ossian saw her mouth move around words that never quite became sound. Then she pulled the blanket up over August's shoulders, precise as folding a flag, and took her post by the window without a word.

Bramble brought soup at noon. Wren brought the dog, who was smuggled in under a stolen tablecloth and immediately laid his enormous head on August's legs and refused all commands to move. Ossian brought the story, again and again, hour after hour — the courier, the map, the cellar, the market, the catacombs, the court, the vault, the bargain, the choice. He told it plainly and completely, and each time he ended at the same place:

"—and then she chose the hard road, out loud, in front of the thing that was trying to buy her. And then she paid. And now we're here, waiting for her to wake up and find out what it cost."

On the third telling, late in the afternoon, her eyelids fluttered.

The room went silent so fast that Turnip's tail-thump against the mattress was deafening.

Her eyes opened. They were clear, and calm, and utterly unrecognizing — the eyes of a polite stranger waking in an inn. They traveled the room: Ossian in his chair, sword across his knees. Sable at the window, rigid. Bramble frozen mid-step with a soup bowl. A child on the windowsill. A monstrous dog. Rich hangings, unfamiliar ceiling.

"Where am I?" Her voice was hoarse but steady. "Who are all of you?"

Nobody breathed.

"We're—" Ossian started, and found his throat had closed. He tried again. "My name is Ossian. This is Sable, and Bramble, and Wren, and the dog is called Turnip, and you're in the royal palace in Vael, and—"

"Why are you all staring at me like that?" She pushed herself up on her elbows, wary, courteous, a stranger's smile assembling itself out of sheer social reflex. "Have I been ill? I'm sorry — I don't seem to—" Her brow creased, the first flicker of distress. "I don't seem to know my own name."

"It's August," Wren said, from the windowsill, very fast, before her voice could break. "Your name is August Delacroix. You're twenty-six. You hate coriander. You're afraid of deep water but you went down into the flooded catacombs anyway because Wren needed help, and you told Wren—" Her voice did break. She plowed on anyway, chin up, furious with grief. "You told Wren that families aren't the ones you're born to, they're the ones who stay. And you stayed. For all of us. You stayed."

August stared at the small furious child, and something moved behind her eyes — not memory, Ossian realized. Just the shape where memory used to live, aching like a healed wound aches in rain.

"I'm sorry," she said softly. "I don't — I don't know any of you. But you clearly know me, and you're all so—" She looked around the room again, at the soldier, the enforcer, the old man with soup, the child, the dog. At the vigil of them. Her stranger's smile faltered into something more honest: bewilderment, and beneath it, faintly, wonder. "Why are you all *here*? You've been keeping watch over someone you don't owe anything to. Haven't you?"

Ossian looked at Sable. Sable looked at Bramble. Bramble looked at Wren.

And Wren, tear-streaked and resolute, hopped down off the windowsill, dragged the chair closer to the bed, climbed onto it, folded her arms, and said:

"No. We owe you everything. And we're going to tell you the whole story — all of it, every day, as many times as it takes — starting right now, with the night a dying man fell through your door."

August blinked at her.

Then, slowly, she shifted back against the pillows, making room — an instinctive courtesy, the first small kindness of whatever person she was going to become — and said, "Well. Then you'd better start at the beginning."
