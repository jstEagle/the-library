## 20. Strangers Who Stay

Wren had stolen a lot of things in her nine years — bread, knives, a whole afternoon once from a fat wool merchant who deserved it — but she had never tried to steal something back for somebody else. That was the trouble with August now. Everything she'd been had been eaten, and there was no lock to pick, no pocket to dip. You couldn't lift a person's life back out of the thing that ate it.

So Wren decided they'd have to build it again instead, piece by piece, out loud, every day, until it stuck.

She announced this at breakfast on the fourth morning after the vault, standing on a chair because Bramble's kitchen table was too tall and Wren had never once let that stop her.

"She doesn't know us," Wren said. "So we'll tell her. Every day. All of it. Even the boring parts." She pointed her spoon at Ossian. "Especially your boring parts."

Ossian, who was holding the dog's head in his lap like it weighed nothing, which it didn't, said, "My parts aren't boring."

"Your parts are *incredibly* boring. You have a scar story about a fence post."

"It was an interesting fence post."

August sat at the end of the table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't drunk, watching them argue with the careful blank politeness of someone waiting to be told what game she'd wandered into. Her eyes went around the table and found no one. Wren watched her look, and watched her find nothing, and something in Wren's chest went hard and small as a swallowed stone.

"Tell me what," August said quietly.

"Everything," Wren said. "Starting now. My name's Wren. I'm nine. I picked your pocket the first time we met and you caught me doing it and instead of turning me in you bought me dinner and made me pay for half of it, which was the meanest kindest thing anyone ever did to me." She hopped down off the chair. "That's lesson one. There's going to be a lot of lessons."

August looked at her cup. "I don't remember any of that."

"I know," Wren said. "That's why it's lesson one and not lesson forty."

* * *

They made rules, because Sable said everything survived longer with rules, and Sable had walked out of a guild on fire so maybe she knew.

Rule one: nobody lied to August, not even kindly. Rule two: nobody rushed her. Rule three — Wren's rule, fought for and won — every evening, tea, and stories, whether August wanted them or not, though she could always say *not tonight* and they would come back tomorrow.

The dog helped. The dog was called Turnip, which August found inexplicable until Ossian explained, on the fifth evening, that Wren had named him after the first thing he'd ever stolen, off a market cart, in front of four soldiers, while Ossian was trying very hard to pretend he wasn't involved.

"He stole a turnip," Ossian said, "and then he stole my rations for a year, and then he stole my heart, and I resent all three."

Turnip put his enormous head on August's knee and sighed like a bellows.

"He does that," Wren said. "He's decided you're his. He decided fast with everybody. It's his only flaw besides the turnips."

August's hand came down slowly, like she was afraid the dog might be a trick, and settled between his ears. "He's warm," she said, sounding surprised.

"You're allowed to be surprised," Sable said from the doorway. "You're allowed to be anything. That's rather the point of what you did."

August looked up at her — at the woman with the guild blade and the guild keys and the new-worn coat of a defector — and Wren saw her frown, working at something just past reach, like a name on the tip of the tongue.

"Were you important?" August asked. "To me?"

Sable's face did something complicated and then chose stillness instead. "Ask me again in a month," she said. "I'll give you a better answer then, when I've earned it twice."

* * *

Bramble taught by objects. He was a locksmith; he thought in things you could hold.

Every morning he laid three items on the table before August woke: a bent pick, a wax impression of a keyhole, a chipped brass gear he claimed had come out of the Moonvault gate itself. "Pick one," he'd say. "Any one. And I'll tell you its story."

The first week she picked the gear, and Bramble told her about the gate under the palace, and the master who'd ruined him, and how a woman named August Delacroix had stood in a smuggler's cellar and said *I'll get you your door, Bramble, if you get me through mine*, and how he had believed her, which for Bramble was a miracle on par with walking on water.

"I don't feel like that person," August said, turning the gear over.

"You don't have to feel like her yet," Bramble said. "You just have to know she existed. The rest is plumbing."

"The rest is *what*?"

"Plumbing," Bramble said firmly. "Everything's plumbing if you're patient enough."

Ossian told stories badly, which somehow made them better. He'd start in the middle, lose the thread, blame the dog, and end up somewhere true. He told her about the year he couldn't remember — his own erased year, the one the sigil's death hadn't given back — and how August had sat with him through the worst night of turn seven and said, *the price of surviving is remembering what you'd rather forget, and I would rather forget plenty, but I'm still glad I'm here.*

"Did I really say that?"

"You said it better than I'm saying it now," Ossian admitted. "I've been paraphrasing you for weeks. It's a good living."

And Wren told the small ones. The sewer run where August had held her hand in the dark water and said *count with me, out loud, and the fear can't find a gap to get in*. The market day when Wren had lifted a purse and August had made her carry it back through the whole crowd and apologize, and then bought her honey-bread on the way home, because justice and kindness were supposed to travel together even when it was inconvenient.

"That sounds like a lecture," August said.

"It was a lecture," Wren agreed. "Best one I ever got."

* * *

On the twelfth evening, August cried.

It happened over nothing — over the ledger, actually. They'd started rewriting it together, the old Vellum Guild accounts turned inside out, debts becoming returns, trades becoming restorations. August's hand knew the columns even if she didn't know why. She could total a page faster than Sable, and when Wren pointed that out, laughing, August stared at her own fingers like they belonged to a stranger, and then her face crumpled, and she wept into her sleeves without a sound, the way people do when they've learned crying is dangerous.

Nobody rushed her. That was rule two.

Ossian moved the tea closer. Bramble pretended to be fascinated by a column of numbers. Turnip leaned his whole weight against her legs. And Wren climbed onto the bench beside her and said, "You cry exactly like that when you're angry, too. You did it the day we found out what the guild master sold. You were furious and sad at the same time and you wouldn't pick one, so you did both."

August wiped her face. "Why do you all keep me? You could start over. Find someone who remembers being your friend."

"Because," Wren said, "you don't throw away a person because they lost a book. We're just giving the book back. Slowly. One page a night."

"And if the pages never come back?"

"Then we write new ones," Wren said simply. "You're still the hand that holds the pen. We checked."

* * *

The hook came on the nineteenth evening, unlooked-for, the way hooks do.

They were deep in the rewritten ledger, August reading aloud in a voice that grew steadier every night, when she stopped mid-line. Her finger rested on an entry near the beginning — an old debt, transferred, paid, closed. A child's sale. A memory vial. A price.

"This one," August said. Her voice had gone strange. "This one's mine, isn't it. The handwriting — I know this handwriting. I've been knowing it all week and telling myself I didn't."

The room went quiet. Sable set down her cup very carefully.

"Yes," Sable said. "We think it is."

August looked up, and her blank eyes weren't blank anymore; something behind them had caught light, like a lamp carried down a long stair. "Then there's more of me in these books than any of you knew," she said. "And I want it back. All of it. Starting tonight."

She pulled the ledger toward her, and outside the window, over the rooftops of the city, the first moon of a clean count rose thin and white as a fresh page.
