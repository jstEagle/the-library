## 1. The Apprentice Who Left

The trouble with knowing every smuggler's road through the Thornfell mountains was that eventually everyone knew you knew it.

Freya Reyes had learned this the hard way, which was the only way she ever learned anything, according to her former master. Maren used to say Freya could burn her hand on a kettle twice and then argue with the kettle about whose fault it was. Three years gone from that cottage and Freya still heard the old woman's voice in her head at inconvenient moments—usually when she was doing something exactly as foolish as Maren would have predicted.

Like now. Like standing in the back room of the Drowned Goose inn in Lower Hobb, counting out forty-one luck-charms into a merchant's greasy palms while trying very hard not to look like she was counting out forty-one luck-charms.

"Forty," said the merchant, Tam Voss, who had a face like a dropped pie and the soul of an abacus.

"Forty-two," said Freya. "You shorted me two at midsummer and I let it go because I'm generous."

"You let it go because you were drunk on Ostara's plum brandy and couldn't count past your own fingers."

"I counted your fingers too. There were nine and a half worth of coin in them." She folded his hand closed over the charms. "Forty-two, Tam. And if you haggle with me again before the thaw, the next batch will be *un*luck charms and you won't know which is which until your wagon axle snaps on the Kettle Road."

That wasn't true. Freya didn't know how to make unluck charms. She'd once tried, out of spite, and produced something that made bread rise beautifully for a week straight. But Tam Voss didn't know that, and there was a particular pleasure—a warm, guilty, slightly shameful pleasure—in trading on knowledge she'd been sworn never to use this way.

Maren's voice again: *A charm is a promise, girl, not a product.*

*It's both, you old crow,* Freya thought back, as she always did, and pocketed the coins.

She left the Drowned Goose by the kitchen door, because the front door meant conversation and conversation meant questions, and Freya Reyes had built three years of quiet survival on the principle of answering no questions she didn't have to. The kitchen door opened onto the alley where her mule, Duchess, stood tied to a post looking profoundly unimpressed with existence. Duchess was gray, ancient, and possessed the temperament of a tax collector. Freya loved her more than she loved most people.

"Home," Freya told her, and Duchess flicked one ear, which from her constituted enthusiasm.

Home was a lean-to cabin two hours up the shepherd's track above Lower Hobb, with a slate roof that leaked in exactly one spot, directly over Freya's bed, which she suspected was the house's idea of a joke. She'd built most of it herself, badly, and patched the rest with whatever the mountain offered: moss, shale, stolen shingles. It smelled of dried yarrow and woodsmoke and the tallow candles she made herself. It was hers. That was the whole point of it. Hers, and answerable to nobody.

She repeated this to herself sometimes, the way other people recited prayers.

* * *

Autumn came down off the peaks early that year, the way it did when the mountain was in one of its moods. By the second week of Harvestmonth the larches had gone gold all at once, as if someone had set fire to the valley without bothering with smoke, and the mornings arrived cold enough to bite.

Freya spent them making stock for winter. Charms against rheumatism for the shepherds, charms for safe birthing for the goats and occasionally the wives, fever-salves, cough syrup, and the little knotted threads she sold as "traveler's blessings" to merchants passing through, which were honestly just knots tied while thinking kind thoughts, but kindness concentrated had power, Maren always said, even cheap kindness.

On a grey Thursday she went down to Lower Hobb for salt and lamp oil and found the village in one of its rare states of excitement: a courier had come over the pass from the east, the first of the season, riding a horse far too fine for these roads.

Freya would have ignored this entirely, except that the courier was standing in the square asking after her by name.

"—Freya Reyes, yes or no? It's a delivery, nothing sinister." He had the bored tone of a man who delivered sinister things regularly and had stopped distinguishing.

Every instinct Freya owned stood up at once and said *no*. She considered walking away, taking the long way around the square, letting some other Freya Reyes claim it. But half the village was watching, including Widow Marsh, who missed nothing and forgot less, and running would only make it a story. Stories traveled. Stories were worse than letters.

"That's me," she said, and hated how carefully casual her voice came out.

The courier handed over a small packet, oilskin-wrapped, sealed with a blob of green wax. She took it the way you take a live coal.

He touched his cap and was gone, horse clattering up the eastern road toward whatever larger and more interesting life he had.

Freya stood in the square holding the packet and did not look at the seal. She looked at everything else: the well, the geese, Widow Marsh pretending to examine turnips with scholarly intensity.

Green wax. A stamp pressed into it in the shape of a crow's foot.

She knew that seal. She'd helped mix the wax for it, years ago, standing on a stool in a smoky cottage while an old woman told her that patience was a virtue and also that she was holding the ladle wrong.

Maren.

Freya walked home. She didn't remember deciding to walk home; her feet simply conducted the meeting without her. Duchess followed, because Duchess was loyal to whoever held the lead rope and no further.

The whole two hours up the shepherd's track, the packet sat in her satchel like a stone. Not heavy, exactly. Worse than heavy. *Present.* It had weight the way a debt has weight, the way a name spoken in another room has weight.

By the time she reached the cabin the light was going amber behind the western ridges and the wind had picked up, smelling of iron and coming snow. Freya built a fire, fed Duchess, ate bread and hard cheese standing up because sitting down felt like surrender, and finally, when there was nothing left between her and the thing, took the packet out and set it on the table.

She didn't open it.

She looked at it for a long time instead. Turned it over once. The oilskin was good quality, oiled twice, the kind of wrapping you used for things that needed to survive weather. Maren had wrapped it like it mattered. Like it was meant to reach her no matter what.

Three years ago Freya had left that cottage in the dark, with one bag and a heart full of righteous fury, and she had told herself the story so many times it had worn smooth as river stone: *I left because she lied to me. Because she kept things from me. Because I deserved to know what I was carrying and she decided I didn't.* All true. Every word true, and worn smooth from handling, and underneath the smoothness, if you pressed your thumb into it, the other truth: *I left because leaving was easier than staying and finding out why she lied.*

She hadn't written. Neither, in three years, had Maren. The silence had been its own agreement, its own treaty line, and now here was a letter crossing it with a crow's-foot seal and oilskin fit for a storm.

Freya put another log on the fire, mostly to have something to do with her hands.

*It'll be a bill,* she told herself. *Some apothecary's debt she wants me to settle. Or a complaint—I taught her neighbor's boy a knot-charm and now she's furious. Or she needs something hauled up from Lower Hobb and remembered I exist because I'm convenient.*

All plausible. All probably true. And none of it explained the double-oiled wrapping, or the fact that couriers didn't ride fine horses over autumn passes to deliver bills.

The wind rose. Somewhere up on the high shoulder of Thornfell, the first real gusts of the season were combing through the passes, and Freya felt it in the walls of her bad-built cabin, a low shudder like the mountain clearing its throat.

She picked up the letter. Held it near the fire. Told herself she'd open it tomorrow, in daylight, with tea, like a reasonable person.

Then she burned it.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the shameful part—she'd imagined, in the three years of silence, that if Maren ever reached across that distance it would be a moment, a crossroads, some grand internal reckoning. Instead it was a Tuesday-ish evening and her hands simply moved: oilskin into the flames, wax cracking, the paper inside catching with a soft whump and curling up orange at the edges. She watched Maren's handwriting blacken and flake without reading a single word, and felt—nothing clean. Not relief, not grief. Something more like the ache after you've bitten down on something you thought was soup and it turned out to be a bone.

*There,* said the part of her that had built the cabin and named the mule and answered to nobody. *Free and clear.*

*You fool,* said the part that sounded like Maren. *That was the only door she was ever going to knock on.*

The ashes settled in the grate. Outside, the wind had changed its note—deeper now, steadier, the sound it made when it was pushing snow ahead of it instead of just threatening to. Freya went to the door and looked out.

The sky over the pass had gone the color of pewter, low and bruised, and the air had that dense, waiting stillness under the wind that every soul in Thornfell knew. Early snow. Too early, the shepherds would grumble. The passes might close weeks ahead of season.

"Good," Freya said aloud, to the mountain, to the wind, to nobody. "Seal them. Let everyone stay where they are and mind their own business."

The mountain, being a mountain, declined to answer.

She banked the fire, checked the one leak in the roof out of habit, and lay down in her bed beneath it. Sleep took its time. When it finally came, she dreamed—not of the cottage, for once, but of something stranger: a great hall under stone, lit by no flame she could name, and somewhere in the dark of it, a sound like a ledger's pages turning, patient and vast, as though something below the world were counting.

Counting, and finding the sum not yet complete.

She woke before dawn with the taste of cold iron in her mouth—and froze, listening.

Snow was falling past the window, thick and silent, the first of the year.

And far down the shepherd's track, barely audible under the wind, something was moving uphill toward her cabin. Small footsteps. Unsteady ones.

Coming through the storm, through the dark, through the first snow—

as if the debt had come due.
