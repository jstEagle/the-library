## 2. A Knock in the Dark

The first snow came early that year, the way bad news does—before anyone was ready for it, and with more of it promised behind.

Freya Reyes had lived alone on the shoulder of Craghollow for two years and change, in a cottage she'd won from a dying smuggler in a card game she'd cheated at, which was, she felt, the most honest way she'd ever come to own anything. The cottage sat where three trails met and none of them were on any map worth trusting. She liked it that way. Maps meant visitors. Visitors meant questions.

She was restocking her charm-boxes when the wind changed.

Anyone who'd wintered in Thornfell learned to read the mountain's moods the way farmers read clouds. There was the ordinary howl, the moaning kind that just wanted attention, and then there was *that* sound—the flat, pressing silence underneath the storm, like the whole sky leaning its weight against one small roof. Freya set down the jar of dried rowan berries, looked at her shutters, and said aloud to no one, "No."

The mountain did not care what she thought. It never had.

By dusk the blizzard had erased the world. She lit every lamp she owned, warded the door with a charm her master would have sneered at for being crude and effective, and settled in for a siege: tea, bread gone slightly stale, and a half-finished charm for a shepherd up-valley whose ewes kept wandering into bogs. Ordinary work. Safe work. Work that required nobody's company but her own.

She was threading the third knot when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a fist the size of a plum might make if it were very determined and very cold.

Freya went absolutely still, needle between her fingers. Wolves didn't knock. Smugglers didn't knock—they scratched the second pattern on the lintel, or they didn't bother coming at all. Grey-robed tithe-collectors, when they still bothered the high villages, announced themselves by standing outside your window until you noticed them, which was worse than knocking.

Three more knocks. Softer now. Almost apologetic.

"Go away," Freya told the door, because it seemed polite to say something first. "Whatever you are, I'm armed, unimpressed, and mid-charm."

Silence. Then, faintly, through the gap under the door: a sound like breathing. Small breathing. Fast breathing—the shallow, sawing kind that meant someone had been running, or freezing, or both.

Freya swore with the fluency of a woman who'd spent her apprenticeship around soldiers' widows and goat merchants, set down her needle, and opened the door on the storm.

A child stood on her step.

Not a large child. Not an old one either—young, six winters perhaps, maybe seven, though it was hard to tell under the rime. A girl, or a boy; the hooded coat gave away nothing but size. Frost had climbed the child's sleeves and turned the frayed cuffs white as candle-drips. Snow crusted the lashes over eyes that were open and dark and looking straight at Freya with an expression that was not fear, exactly. It was more like recognition, which was far more unsettling, because Freya was quite certain she'd never seen this child before in her life.

"Well," Freya said. "You're a long way from anywhere."

The child said nothing. The child's lips moved, though—worked around words that wouldn't come, breath ghosting silver—and then simply stood there, swaying slightly, like a fence post deciding whether to fall over.

Every sensible instinct Freya owned lined up in a neat row and voted to close the door. Solitude was a thing she'd built plank by plank, year by year, and she knew precisely what happened to people who took in strays off mountainsides. They got attached. They got found out. They got letters with seals they burned unopened.

But the child swayed again, and one small hand came up—not reaching for Freya, just resting against the doorframe to stay upright, fingers blue to the knuckle—and Freya heard herself say, "Oh, for the love of—" and stepped aside.

"Inside. Quickly, before we both regret it."

* * *

Getting the child thawed was a business.

The coat was frozen stiff and came off in pieces, crackling like autumn leaves. Underneath: a thin shift, homespun, no dye, stitched in a style Freya had never seen—no buttons, no lacing, just seams folded in a pattern that almost looked like writing. The child was all elbows and collarbones, shivering so hard the bench rattled, so Freya wrapped the whole small body in her good wool blanket, propped it by the fire, and set about the slower work: warm broth in sips, snow rubbed from ears and fingers, a poultice for the early frostbite on two toes that had thankfully not gone black.

The child bore all of it without a sound. That was the strangest part. Freya had treated injured smugglers, bitten drovers, one bear cub with a thorn in its paw, and every last one of them had complained. This child watched everything with those dark, level eyes and made no noise at all—not a whimper when Freya worked feeling back into the frozen toes, not a flinch at the sting of the salve.

"Mute?" Freya asked, mostly to herself.

The child considered her. Then raised one hand and tapped twice against their own chest, in a way that managed somehow to convey *yes, and also, that is not the whole of it.*

"Right." Freya sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her apron. "I'm going to ask you some questions. You can nod or shake your head. Nodding means yes. Can you do that?"

Nod. One precise little dip of the chin, formal as a courtier.

"Good. Do you have a name?"

A pause. Then the head tilted, slowly, side to side—a motion that wasn't quite a shake. More like the answer to a question Freya hadn't figured out how to ask yet.

"Do you know where you are?"

Head tilt, same direction. Same meaning. Whatever it meant.

"Can you point me toward home?"

This time the child did something different. They lifted one hand, palm up, and then—slowly, deliberately—turned it over, until the palm faced down, and pressed it gently toward the floorboards. Twice. As if laying something to rest beneath the house. Beneath the floor. Beneath the stone under the floor.

Freya felt the hairs rise along her arms.

"Underground," she said. "That way." She pointed at her own feet, and then, since pointing straight down seemed absurd, angled her finger toward the mountain's roots, south and deep.

The child nodded once, firmly, and then—apparently satisfied that communication had been established—held out both hands, palms up, in unmistakable request for more broth.

Despite herself, despite everything, Freya laughed. It came out rusty, like a hinge on a door long unused.

"All right," she said, ladling another portion. "You're bossy for someone who can't talk. We'll get along fine."

* * *

The storm raged through the night, and the child slept in Freya's bed while Freya dozed in the chair by the fire, telling herself she was only keeping watch because the child might be bait, or cursed, or followed. All true possibilities. She checked the wards twice. She sharpened her belt knife, which was purely decorative against anything that mattered in Thornfell, but it made her feel better.

It was near midnight, tending the fire, that she made her mistake—or her discovery, depending on which account you believed later.

The child had kicked the blanket loose. Freya leaned down to tuck it back, and the small hand slid out from the covers and caught her wrist.

Not grabbed. Caught—deliberately, gently, the way you'd catch a falling cup before it hit the floor. The dark eyes were open. Freya hadn't seen them open. But they were looking at her, and the small fingers tightened around her wrist, and the child spoke.

Words came out. Sound, finally—but nothing like any language Freya knew, and Freya prided herself on knowing a scandalous number of languages, including three that were technically illegal. These words were low and resonant, more felt in the floorboards than heard in the air, each syllable round as river stones worn smooth. The fire guttered. The lamp flames bent sideways though there was no draft.

And Freya touched the child's hand, skin to skin—

—and the cottage was gone.

She stood in a hall. A vast hall, vaulted and dim, built of stone so old the blocks had begun to forget they were cut and dreamed of being mountains again. Pillars rose beyond counting into darkness overhead. The floor gleamed like black water, polished by centuries of feet, and along its length stretched tables—rows upon rows of them, laden with... she couldn't say. Light? Objects that were light, folded and stacked like linen. And figures moved among the tables, grey-robed and faceless-smooth, sorting, counting, filing, forever.

At the far end of the hall stood a scale. Not a merchant's scale—a scale the height of a cathedral door, and on its pans rested things that glimmered and struggled, faintly, like fireflies in jars.

Somewhere in that hall was a voice, vast and dry as a ledger page turning, saying a number. Just a number. Reciting it patiently, endlessly, and Freya understood with sick certainty that the number was owed, and that somewhere in the arithmetic of that terrible place, a small dark-eyed child was a line item.

Then the child let go of her wrist, and the cottage slammed back around her—firelight, stew smell, storm hammering the shutters—and Freya discovered she was on her knees on the cold flagstones, heart slamming, hands shaking.

The child watched her from the bed, blanket clutched to chin, expression unreadable.

"What," Freya managed, "was *that*?"

The child pointed down. Palms flat. Pressing toward the floor. The same gesture as before, patient, certain.

"No." Freya got up, retrieved her tea, drank it even though it had gone cold, because her hands needed something to do. "No, thank you. Whatever's down there, I'm sure it's lovely, and I want no part of it. You fell down a mountain. You're concussed. People see strange things when they're concussed. I'm concussed. That's my story and I'm keeping it."

The child blinked at her. Then, with great deliberateness, they reached under the pillow—*her* pillow—and produced a small object, holding it out.

It was a stone. No bigger than a hen's egg, smooth, dark, shot through with veins of faint gold light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat. Freya had handled charms her whole life. She'd sold love-lures and bog-wardens and luck-knots, cheap magic for cheap prices, and she knew what real enchantment felt like when it brushed her senses: a prickle, a warmth, a whisper at the edge of hearing.

This stone didn't prickle. It *sang*. Low and wordless, in the same round river-stone language the child had spoken, and the gold light pulsed in time with the child's breathing, and Freya understood—without knowing how she understood, which was rapidly becoming the theme of the evening—that this was the most valuable and most dangerous thing ever to sit on her kitchen table.

Including the table.

"That's yours?" she asked.

The child shook their head. Tapped their chest. Pointed at the stone. Tapped their chest again. *Me. This. Me.*

And then the child did the thing that cracked Freya's solitude clean through, though she wouldn't realize it until much later.

They held out the stone to her. Offered it. Open-palmed, the way you offer the only thing you have to the person who fed you, and looked at her with those steady dark eyes as if to say: *you saw where I come from. You saw what's waiting. Here. Keep it safe. Or keep me. Whichever costs you less.*

Freya looked at the stone. At the child. At the storm-blinded window, where the wind screamed past like something searching.

She thought about the letter with Maren's seal, burning in her hearth three months ago, curling to ash unread. She thought about two years of doors kept shut and trails kept secret and the careful, comfortable loneliness she'd built like a wall, stone by stone, and how one frostbitten stray had walked through it in a single evening by the simple expedient of nearly dying on her doorstep.

"I don't want it," she said—which was a lie, because her palm ached with wanting it, the way you ache for a song half-remembered. "I don't want any of this. I want you fed, thawed, and gone down-mountain the moment this storm lifts, delivered to whatever abbey or grandmother or circus misplaced you. And I especially want whatever lives under the mountain to stay under the mountain."

The child regarded her for a long moment. Then they drew the stone back, tucked it under the pillow, and lay down with the air of someone concluding a negotiation had gone acceptably well.

Freya banked the fire, wrapped herself in her cloak in the chair, and did not sleep. Somewhere in the smallest hours the storm began to die, the way storms do, reluctantly, in stages. And in the hush that followed, as the wind lost its grip on the eaves, Freya heard the child murmur in their sleep—one soft phrase in that old, round, river-stone tongue—and though she couldn't translate a word of it, some part of her, some hedge-witch part that knew things before she did, translated it anyway:

*The debt comes due at first snow.*

Outside, far below in the valley, a church bell that hadn't rung in years began tolling. Once. Twice. Three times.

Nobody rang that bell. Freya had made sure of it herself, two winters ago, when she'd paid the sexton to let it rust.

She stared into the dark, the child's slow breathing beside her, and reached for her boots.
