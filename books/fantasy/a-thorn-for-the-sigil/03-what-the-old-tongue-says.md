## 3. What the Old Tongue Says

The child slept through the second night and most of the third, curled in Freya's one good chair with a quilt pulled up to the chin, breathing in that small, stubborn rhythm of someone who had decided, against all evidence, to keep living.

Freya did not sleep much at all.

She sat by the fire instead, mending a charm-bag she didn't need mending, and watched the frost climb the window in feathered patterns, and listened to the wind worry at the eaves like an old dog at a bone. Every creak of the timber made her look up. Twice she got as far as the door before common sense caught her by the collar and dragged her back to the chair.

*It's a child,* she told herself for the hundredth time. *Frostbitten, half-starved, mute. Whatever it is, it's also that.*

But when she'd touched the small hand — just to check for blackened fingers, nothing more — she had seen a hall beneath stone. Vast and cold and lit by no flame she knew, pillars going up into dark like trees going up into sky, and somewhere in it, something turning pages. Not a dream. Dreams don't leave a taste in your mouth. This had tasted like iron and old paper, and it had lingered for two days.

The child stirred, opened its eyes — dark ones, watchful, far too old for the face they lived behind — and looked at her without fear. That was the strangest part. Freya had been stared at by wolves, by customs men, by one very disappointed goat, and she knew what wariness looked like. The child looked at her the way you look at a hearth.

"Right," Freya said aloud, because talking to herself kept the nerves quiet. "You're not a demon. Demons don't eat three bowls of barley porridge and then apologize with their eyebrows."

The child's eyebrows moved apologetically, on cue, and Freya laughed despite everything — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years.

She needed help. She hated needing help, but there was exactly one person in the Thornfell foothills who might know what language lived in that small mouth, and he owed her nothing and she owed him two bottles of plum brandy and an apology of indeterminate size.

Brother Callum had been defrocked before Freya was born, which in his telling made it sound like a promotion. He lived at the edge of Lowmere in a house that had once been a chapel and now was mostly books — stacked, shelved, wedged under table legs, and in one memorable case used to prop open a window that had long since rotted shut anyway.

"You'll want to go early," Ostara had told her once, years back, over cards in a smuggler's den. "He's sweet as honey till midday and a hedge-thorn after."

Freya wrapped Wren — she had decided on the third morning that a child needed a name even if the child couldn't offer one, and *Wren* had felt right: small, quick, surviving things bigger than itself — in every layer she owned, and set out along the river path while the frost still smoked off the reeds.

* * *

Lowmere smelled of woodsmoke and sheep, as always, but today there was something else threaded through it. Freya noticed it first at the bridge: a knot of villagers standing around a man who did not belong. Grey robes, travel-stained, hood back, a face so unremarkable that looking away from it felt like forgetting where you'd put your keys. He was asking questions. Freya caught fragments as she passed with her head down and Wren's hood tugged low.

"—small child, come down out of the high passes—"

"—speaks no tongue any of you know? Certain?"

"—the Court thanks you for your diligence—"

*The Court.* Freya's spine went cold in a way the morning air couldn't account for. She had spent three years trading charms along roads where the Sigil Court was spoken of the way sailors speak of reefs: with respect, with distance, and never, ever at night. Everyone knew somebody whose grandmother had paid a tithe. Nobody knew anybody who'd gone beneath the mountain and come back.

She did not run. Running draws eyes. She walked, unhurried, a charm-seller with a sleepy child on her hip, past the grey-robed man and on up the lane, and only let her breath out when Callum's crooked door was between them and the world.

The knock brought a shuffling, muttering, and then Brother Callum himself, round as a barrel and twice as warm, spectacles pushed up into wild white hair.

"Freya Reyes." His whole face folded into delight. "Three years, girl, and not a word, and now you appear on my step looking like something the mountain chewed and spat back out. Come in, come in, you're letting the cold in with all its boots on."

"I brought the brandy," Freya said, holding up the bottle.

"Then I forgive you for two of the three years." He ushered them inside, and then his gaze dropped to Wren, and Freya watched the delight bank itself down to something sharper and more careful. "And who is this?"

"That," said Freya, unwinding scarves, "is what I've come about."

Callum cleared a bench of books — relocating rather than removing them; Freya suspected those books had not touched the floor since the reign of the old king — and sat Wren upon it, and knelt, and studied the child the way he studied manuscripts: patiently, hungrily, with total respect.

"Well now," he murmured. "Well now. Open your mouth, little one? Say anything at all."

Wren looked at Freya. Freya nodded. And Wren opened their mouth and spoke — a soft cascade of syllables, rising and falling, nothing like the trade-tongue, nothing like the high speech of the valley lords, nothing like the clipped mountain dialect or the river-folk drawl. The sounds were older than any of those. Listening to them was like listening to bedrock think.

When Wren stopped, the fire crackled into the silence.

Callum had gone very still. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on his robe, put them back on, and said, "Say it again."

Wren said it again.

"Hm," said Callum. "Hm hm hm." He began pulling books.

For two hours Freya watched a defrocked monk fight a dead language. He hauled volumes off shelves, blew dust off pages, compared scripts, swore gently in four different tongues (two of which, Freya realized with a chill, were among the ones he was checking). Wren sat obediently repeating phrases on request, patient as a saint, occasionally stealing biscuits from the plate Callum kept refilling without noticing he was doing it.

At some point Freya found herself making tea because standing still was unbearable, and Callum drank his without tasting it, and finally — near midday, when his sweetness was due to curdle — he sat back with a groan that shifted several books.

"I know this," he said. "That's what frightens me. I know this, and I shouldn't. There are maybe four people alive who could read it, and I'm not certain I'm one of them anymore." He tapped a page covered in angular script. "This is from before the kingdoms. Before Thornfell, before the valleys, before anyone thought to write down where they'd buried their kings. The scholars call it the Root Tongue. Everything grew out of it — every language on this side of the mountains carries its bones. But nobody's spoken it in—" He stopped. Frowned. Looked at Wren for a long moment.

"In how long?" Freya asked quietly.

"Longer than my order existed," Callum said. "Longer than the mountain had a name." He rubbed his eyes. "The child isn't speaking a dead language, Freya. The child is speaking a language that hasn't died yet. There's a difference, and the difference is that dead languages stay buried."

"What does it say?" Freya asked. "What Wren keeps saying. It's the same words, over and over, especially at night. Like a prayer. Or a warning."

Callum was quiet for a moment. When he answered, all the warmth had gone out of his voice, leaving something old and careful behind.

"It says: *the debt comes due at first snow.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a beam settled with a sigh.

"And the rest of it?" Freya said. "There's more than that. Whole sentences."

"There is more than that." Callum turned back to his notes, and his hand wasn't quite steady. "Most of it I can't parse — invocations, genealogies, words for things that don't have words left. But the refrain, the part repeated — it's a claim, Freya. In the oldest legal formula I have ever read. It's not a prayer. It's a title deed. The child is saying, in the voice of whoever taught it, *this soul belongs to me, and I have come to collect.*"

Freya set down her tea before she could drop it. "Belongs to whom?"

"That's the other thing I can't parse." He hesitated. "Or won't. Girl — what did you find in the snow?"

Freya told him. Not all of it — she kept the vision of the pillared hall behind her teeth, that iron-and-paper taste — but enough. The child appearing at her door during the blizzard. The frostbite. The silence. Callum listened the way good listeners listen, entirely, without once interrupting, and when she finished he sat looking into the fire for a long time.

"The Sigil Court takes tithes," he said at last. "Everyone knows that. Souls pledged, names pawned, debts carried down generation to generation until the mountain calls them in. What nobody asks — what I never asked, in thirty years of collecting dead things — is what the Court does with all of it. Where it all *goes*." He glanced at Wren. "A child speaking the Root Tongue turns up out of the high passes the same autumn that grey robes start walking village to village below the snow line. You saw one already, didn't you? At the bridge. Don't look surprised, girl, you walked past my window with your shoulders up around your ears."

"They were asking about a child," Freya admitted. "Out of the passes. One who speaks no known tongue."

"Mm. And there are grey robes in Harrowdale too, by all accounts, and in Millbeck, working upstream toward the high country. Asking their questions politely. Paying for information handsomely." Callum took his spectacles off again, which Freya was learning meant bad news. "They're narrowing the search, and the passes will seal within the month, and when the snow comes—"

"*The debt comes due at first snow,*" Freya finished. Her mouth had gone dry. On the bench, Wren was watching her with those too-old eyes, biscuit forgotten in one small hand.

"Now you understand why I'm frightened," Callum said. "Not for myself — I'm sixty-eight and my sins are all paid for. For that child. And, if I'm any judge of the company you keep and the trouble you attract, for you."

"What do I do?"

"Do?" Callum snorted, and some of the warmth came back into him like blood returning to a numb limb. "Girl, I translate. I don't advise. My last piece of advice got me defrocked." He paused. "Although." He heaved himself up, went to the deepest shelf, behind a locked case Freya had never seen open, and came back with a slim book bound in leather gone dark with age. He didn't unlock it so much as simply look at it until it gave up. Inside were pages of the angular script, interleaved with cramped modern annotations in his own handwriting.

"This came through my hands twenty years ago," he said. "A fragment. I copied it, translated what I could, and returned it, and I've regretted both halves ever since. It concerns the Court. Its founding. Its *accounting*." He found the page he wanted and turned it toward her. "Here. The law the Court runs on — older than the Court itself, they say, older than the mountain having a name. Everything beneath it is a ledger. Every debt must balance. Every entry must be honored. And here—" his finger stopped on a line, and his voice dropped, "—here is the clause that got my order's archives burned in three countries: *what is freely given may be freely taken back.*"

Freya stared at the angular marks without understanding a single one. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning that in the Court's own founding law, a debt can be broken by the thing that made it. If a soul was sold — truly sold, entered in the ledger — then the sale stands forever. Unless." He looked up at her over his spectacles. "Unless something of equal weight is given back. Freely. By someone with standing."

"With standing meaning what?"

"Meaning someone the ledger recognizes." Callum closed the book gently, like shutting a coffin lid. "I don't know what that means either. I've had twenty years to wonder and I still don't. But I'll tell you what I do know, Freya Reyes, free of charge, and it's this: whatever you're planning — and I can see you planning, you have your mother's forehead when you scheme — do not plan it alone, and do not plan it slowly. The snow doesn't wait for clever people."

Outside, the light had begun to fail. Freya gathered Wren up, and the child went willingly into her arms, cheek settling against her shoulder with a trust that sat in her chest like a hot coal.

"Callum," she said at the door. "Why help me? You don't know me. Three years, not a word, remember?"

He smiled, and it was a tired smile, but underneath it ran something fierce.

"Because I've spent my whole life reading languages that everyone else agreed were dead," he said, "and being right. And because when that child spoke just now, Freya, I heard something I have read about in forty books and never once believed." He leaned on his doorframe. "I heard someone answering. Half those phrases weren't the deed, girl. They were replies. Something down there has been calling for a long, long time — and something up here has finally started to talk back."

Freya walked out into the blue dusk with Wren warm against her chest and those words ringing in her skull like a struck bell.

* * *

She was halfway home before she understood what was wrong with the road.

No smoke. Lowmere's chimney smoke should have followed her for a mile along the river path, a grey smudge against the last light. Instead the sky behind her was clean, and ahead, at the bend where her cottage hid in its fold of hill, there was smoke — plenty of it, rising straight and unhurried from her own chimney.

Freya stopped. Set Wren down behind a boulder. Put a finger to her lips, and the child, watching her with perfect comprehension, nodded once.

She circled wide through the bracken, the way she'd learned running charms past toll-keepers, silent on frozen ground, and came up behind her own house from the high side, and looked down into her own yard.

Two figures stood by her door. Grey robes. The taller one held something — paper, she realized, her heart dropping like a stone — a sheet of paper, and was studying it, and then studying the cottage, with the patience of someone comparing a description to the thing described. The other knocked. Waited. Knocked again.

On the paper, Freya would have bet every coin she had, was a drawing of a child.

Beside her, in the shadow of the bracken, Wren went rigid. Not with fear — that was the terrible thing, the thing Freya would lie awake turning over for nights afterward. The child went rigid the way a drawn bow goes rigid, eyes fixed on the grey robes with an intensity that made the air feel thin, and beneath Freya's hand on the small shoulder she felt a trembling that had nothing to do with cold.

And Wren spoke — barely a breath, barely sound at all, but the Root Tongue carrying even in a whisper like water carrying ice — one short phrase, aimed at the figures below.

Freya didn't need Callum to translate this one. She'd heard it enough times now to know its shape.

*The debt comes due.*

Below, both grey-robed heads lifted at once, and turned, smooth as weathervanes, toward the high side of the hill.

Toward them.
