## 4. Grey Robes at the Ford

The mountain did not send its collectors out in bad weather, because the mountain did not believe in bad weather. Weather was a thing that happened to people who had names and houses and opinions about the cold. Ashen had none of these. He had a grey robe, an oath, and a warrant written on vellum so old it had gone the color of teeth.

*Vessel escaped. Retrieve before first snow seals the passes.*

That was all it said. Warrants from the Sigil Court were never generous with words. Words cost something down there—everything cost something—and the Auditor wasted nothing, not even language.

Ashen stood at the edge of the Thornfell lowlands where the smuggler's road met the river, and he watched the water run brown and fast with the week's melt-rain, and he thought, as he always thought at fords: *I hate this part.*

Not the crossing itself. Crossing was nothing; his boots were waxed against wet and his robe shed rain like a duck's back sheds it. What he hated was what fords meant. Fords were where roads decided things. On one side of the water you were going somewhere, and on the other side you had gone there, and there was no standing in between unless you were willing to stand in the river, which was where Ashen felt he had spent most of his existence.

He crouched at the bank and pressed two fingers to the mud.

The Court's craft was not hedge-craft. Hedge-witches asked politely; the Court *took*. He let the warrant's sigil warm against his chest and read the ground the way a collector reads anything—as a ledger. Here, small feet, bare or near-bare, days old now but the frost had been kind and kept the print edges sharp. Beside them, larger prints, a woman's stride, long and impatient, the heel dug deep like someone who walked fast because stopping was a habit she distrusted. And here—

Here was the interesting part.

A third set of tracks, older, half-eaten by rain: heavy boots, a man's weight, walking up the road and then walking back down again, twice, pacing. A watcher. Someone had been waiting along this stretch of road, watching for the woman and the child, and had given up—or been called away—or been told to wait somewhere else.

Grey robes worked in threes this far from the mountain. Ashen knew this because he was one of the three. His brothers-in-oath, Vessel and Corvane—those were not names, of course; names were struck from collectors at their making—but those were the sounds they answered to, the way dogs answer to whistles they didn't choose. Vessel would be working the eastern villages, asking his terrible polite questions. Corvane had taken the western ford. Which left this third set of boot-prints unaccounted for, and Ashen did not care for mysteries that arrived ahead of him on his own assignment.

He stood, brushed the mud from his fingers with the particular thoroughness of a man who has no other comforts, and crossed the river.

* * *

The village was called Hobb's Rest, which Ashen found darkly funny in the way he found most things darkly funny, privately, where nobody could see. Collectors were not permitted humor. It was in the oath somewhere, probably, wedged between *thou shalt not keep what is not entered in the ledger* and *thou shalt not remember thy mother's face*, though Ashen had long suspected the oath was less a list of rules than a list of things the Court was afraid of.

He came into the village at dusk, hood up, hands folded into his sleeves, doing the walk. Every collector had a walk. You could not hurry—that frightened them, and frightened villagers lied badly and ran worse. You could not dawdle—that made them curious, and curious villagers remembered details. You walked the way the Court thought about everything: steadily, without appetite, without mercy, without any visible interest whatsoever, and somehow everyone told you everything anyway.

The inn was the only building with light spilling warm through shutters, and under the smell of stew and wet wool Ashen caught it immediately—the thread he'd been following since the ford. Fear. Fresh fear, the specific flavor of a village that had recently been asked questions by someone in grey.

He pushed open the door.

Conversation died the way it always died, in stages: first the loud laughter, then the murmuring, then even the fire seemed to crackle more quietly. The innkeeper, a broad woman with flour to her elbows, looked at him the way people look at weather they've heard coming for days.

"We're full," she said.

"I haven't asked for a room."

"Then we're out of stew."

"I haven't asked for stew." Ashen let his hood fall back. Grey robes did not hide their faces; hiding suggested shame, and the Court felt none. His face did its work for him—it was a forgettable face, deliberately so, the kind of face you struggled to describe to the reeve afterward. "I'm looking for travelers. A woman, alone, and a child who doesn't speak."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked with the guilt of someone listening.

"They went through three days ago," said a voice from the corner—an old shepherd, wine-flushed, either brave or too drunk to be sensible. "Heading for the high passes. Mad, this time of year. But then—" he hiccupped, "—then your lot turned up asking after them, and I thought, well. If grey robes want them, maybe they're worth helping."

Ashen turned to him slowly. This was the moment, and he knew exactly how it went, because he had performed it perhaps two hundred times. You let the silence do the work. You let them understand what disobedience cost, or seemed to cost. You wrote the village in the ledger as *cooperative* or *obstructive*, and obstructive villages learned better by spring.

But the old shepherd looked at him with watery defiance, and behind that defiance—Ashen saw it clearly, the way you see a coin at the bottom of a well—there was grief. Old grief. The specific grief of a man who had once handed over something small and beloved and been told it was law.

And Ashen, instead of letting the silence do its work, heard himself say:

"The high passes are closed by first snow. Anyone chasing them will have to be quick about it."

It was, technically, information. It was also, technically, a warning. The shepherd blinked. The innkeeper's ladle stopped mid-stir. And Ashen walked out of the inn into the blue evening feeling something he had no ledger-entry for, some small hot coal lodged under his breastbone, and he thought: *that was inefficient.*

The Court punished inefficiency.

* * *

He made camp that night in a hollow above the road, cold-fire burning in a dish of stone—a collector's fire, blue and heatless, that gave light without warmth because warmth was a thing the Court considered wasteful expenditure. He sat with his knees drawn up and did the thing he was not supposed to do, which was think.

There had been another child. That was the trouble. There was always another child, if you stayed in the trade long enough, but there was one in particular, forty years gone now, and Ashen had been young in the trade then—younger than a collector should ever be, though collectors were made young precisely so they wouldn't know enough to object.

Her name had been Liss. He knew that because he'd known her before—not before the oath, that was impossible, but before *his* part in it. In the village of Undermere, where he'd been sent as an apprentice-collector to observe, and she'd been the miller's daughter with a gap in her teeth and a way of whistling through it that made the whole yard laugh. He'd watched her for a season. Watched her carry flour sacks too big for her. Watched her teach her little brother to whistle.

Then the warrant came, and the taking happened, and Ashen had stood at the edge of the mill-yard in his grey robe while Liss's mother held onto the doorframe so hard the wood split, and Liss herself had not cried. She had looked at Ashen—at *him*, specifically, the apprentice, the one whose job was only to watch—and she had whistled at him through the gap in her teeth. One note. Low and clear.

Like a question.

He had not answered it. He had written in the ledger: *Vessel retrieved. Village cooperative.* And the Court had entered Liss into the treasury, and the miller's family had paid their debt, and the world had continued, and Ashen had continued in it, efficient and obedient and hollow in the shape of a man.

Forty years. And every ford since, every grey dawn, every child's footprint in the frost—he had told himself it was duty, and duty was a fine word, a load-bearing word, a word that held up roofs. But lately—and *lately* was the worst part, lately meaning the last decade, lately meaning he could no longer pretend it was new—the word had begun to creak.

Because here was the thing Ashen had learned in forty years of reading ledgers: debts that can never be paid are not debts. They are just a way of owning someone forever. And the Court's whole architecture, its beautiful glittering arithmetic, rested on debts that could never be paid. That was not accounting. He had come, slowly, shamefully, to suspect it was something else entirely.

The cold flame flickered. Ashen fed it nothing, because it needed nothing, and thought: *even the fire down there takes nothing and gives nothing. Even our fires are balanced to the penny.*

Somewhere north, a woman with impatient heels was carrying a silent child toward the high passes, and Ashen's warrant wanted her intercepted, and his brothers-in-oath were flanking the routes, and everything was proceeding according to the ledger.

Except that tomorrow, when he caught up to them—and he would catch up to them, he was very good, that was the tragedy of it—except that tomorrow he would have to look at the child. At whatever was behind the child's eyes. And he had a suspicion, growing in him like rot in a beam, that he already knew what he would find there.

He would find a question. Whistled low and clear across forty years.

* * *

Dawn came grey and mean, first snow threatening in the clouds like a debt coming due. Ashen broke camp and took the high trail, moving faster than a collector should, telling himself it was efficiency.

He found them at midday, at the second ford—the upper one, where the river ran shallow and quick over white stones. They were crossing. The woman was ahead, pack high on her shoulders, testing each stone with her boot like she'd been born on this exact river. Behind her, picking careful steps, came the child.

Small. Dark-haired. Wrapped in a coat three sizes too big, sleeves rolled and rolled again. Moving with the strange, deliberate grace of someone listening to the ground beneath the water.

Ashen stepped out from the alders, and the woman saw him instantly—of course she did, she moved like a person who noticed everything—and she put herself between him and the child so fast it was almost impressive, one hand already inside her coat where charms lived.

"Collector," she said. Not a question. She knew the robe.

"Ashen," he said, which surprised him, because collectors did not give sounds of themselves freely. "Of the Sigil Court. I've come for the vessel."

"You've come a long way for a child."

"The Court comes exactly as far as it must."

"And no further?" Her chin lifted. Rain-wind pulled at her hair. She had the look of someone who had been running from something for years and had gotten very good at turning to face it.

"No further," Ashen agreed quietly. "That's rather the problem."

The child stepped out from behind her.

Later—much later, in a firelit room, in a life he could not yet imagine—Ashen would try to explain this moment and fail. But in it, simply: the child looked at him. Not with fear. Not with the animal cunning of the hunted, not with tears or bargaining or the flat acceptance he had seen two hundred times on two hundred faces. The child looked at him the way you look at a door you intend to open.

And behind the child's eyes, faint as a whistle through a gap in the teeth, Ashen felt something stir. Something old. Something patient. Something that knew his name had been taken and knew where it was kept and was, even now, in that silent small skull, deciding whether he was worth saying it back to.

The warrant warmed against his chest. *Retrieve.* His hand rose, as it had risen two hundred times, and began to trace the sigil of taking in the air.

The child tilted its head.

Ashen's hand stopped.

Behind him, far to the south, thunder rolled along the mountain's flank—though the sky held no storm—and the first flakes of the year's snow came sifting down through the alders, fat and slow, like pages falling from a ledger no one was writing anymore.

"Well," said the woman, watching his frozen hand with narrow eyes. "This is interesting."

It was. It was the least efficient moment of Ashen's entire existence, and standing there in the falling snow with his oath cracking quietly down the middle, he had never in forty years felt more awake.
