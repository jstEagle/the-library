## 5. The Smuggler's Road

The grey robes reached Hollowmere two days after Brother Callum translated the phrase, which told Freya everything she needed to know about how fast the mountain moved when it wanted something.

She'd seen the first of them at the well that morning—tall, patient, asking the miller's wife questions in a voice like a ledger being opened. *Have you seen a child? Small. Dark-haired. Does not speak.* The miller's wife had shaken her head, and Freya had kept her own head down and walked home the long way, heart drumming against her ribs like a trapped bird.

By noon she'd packed. By dusk they were gone.

"You can't just take a child into the mountains in November," she muttered to herself as she buckled the straps of her pack, because saying it out loud made it sound insane, and if it sounded insane enough maybe some sensible part of her would put its foot down. The sensible part did not put its foot down. The sensible part was busy stuffing dried plums and a jar of goose fat into every available pocket.

Wren sat on the floor by the hearth, watching her with those enormous dark eyes, holding the one thing the child refused to leave behind: a smooth river stone, grey as the robes hunting them. When Freya shouldered her pack and pulled open the door, Wren stood up without being asked and took hold of the hem of her coat, the way a burr takes hold of a shepherd's sock.

"I'm going to regret this," Freya said.

Wren patted her knee twice, gently, as if to say *no you won't,* or possibly *there, there.* It was impossible to tell. It was going to be impossible to tell for a very long time, Freya suspected, and there was something almost restful about that.

The snow started before they cleared the tree line.

* * *

There were three roads through the Thornfells, and every map in the kingdom showed all three. What no map showed were the eleven others—the smuggler's cuts, the drover's shortcuts, the paths worn into existence by people who had excellent reasons for not wanting to be found. Freya had learned them from Maren, who had learned them from the woman Maren bought herbs from, who had learned them from nobody anyone could name. They passed hand to hand like stolen silver, and like stolen silver, they spent best quietly.

She took the cut behind the Hanging Stone, where the cliff leaned so far over the trail that it seemed to be eavesdropping, then down through the birch hollow where the trees grew white and thin as candle tapers, then up again along the spine of Wolfjaw Ridge, where the wind came off the high snowfields with an edge like a honed knife.

Wren kept pace. That surprised her more than anything else that day. The child was small—not much past six years, she guessed, though it was hard to say—and should have been exhausted, crying, begging to be carried within the first hour. Instead Wren climbed like the mountains were an old story Wren already knew the ending to, sure-footed on scree, unbothered by the cold that was turning Freya's fingers into ten separate complaints.

When they stopped to eat at midday, huddled in the lee of a boulder with their backs to the wind, Freya dug out bread and hard cheese and watched Wren eat with the focused solemnity of a monk at prayer.

"So," she said, mostly to hear a voice. "I can't keep calling you *the child.* People will think I'm strange."

Wren looked up. Crumb on chin. Eyes ancient and enormous.

"Names matter," Freya went on, tearing her own bread with unnecessary force. "My mother always said a name is the first spell anybody casts on you. You want to pick a good one." She hadn't thought about her mother in months. She resented the snow for making her do it now. "Something small, I think. Something quick. You move like—" She gestured vaguely. The child had, earlier that morning, gone up a rock face in a way that could only be described as *flitting.* "Like a bird. A little brown one. Scrappy. Loud when it wants to be, probably, once it decides talking is worth the trouble."

Wren tilted their head.

"A wren," Freya said, and felt it land somewhere in her chest before it even left her mouth. "That's you. Wren."

The child considered this for a long moment. Then Wren picked up the river stone, pressed it briefly against their own chest, and set it down between them on the stone—as if offering it, or naming it too, or sealing some bargain whose terms only the old tongue knew.

Freya decided not to ask. Some bargains were better signed than read.

They walked until the light went copper behind the western peaks, and then the light went rose, and then the light went away entirely, and the temperature dropped so fast that Freya heard the frost forming on the scrub brush—a faint crystalline ticking, like a thousand tiny clocks all deciding to stop at once. Too cold to camp. Which meant—

"Oh," she said aloud, and stopped so suddenly that Wren bumped into her legs. "Oh, no."

Wren looked up questioningly.

"There's someone I have to see," Freya said grimly, "and she's going to be insufferable about it."

* * *

Ostara Vane lived in a crooked cabin at the mouth of Bearhollow Pass, halfway up the mountain's shoulder, in a clearing that smelled overwhelmingly of pine, smoke, and bear. The cabin was built at an angle that suggested either remarkable engineering or none whatsoever, and around it ranged three brown bears the size of haycarts, who greeted Freya's approach not with roars but with a low, rumbling chorus of recognition that vibrated up through the frozen ground and into her boots.

"Hallo, Bramble," Freya said to the largest. "Hallo, Clover. Hallo, you enormous disaster." This last was directed at the third bear, named Turnip, who expressed his joy at seeing her by attempting to fit her entire head inside his mouth, affectionately.

"*Freya Reyes.*"

Ostara stood in the cabin doorway, backlit by firelight, a one-armed silhouette with her remaining hand planted on her hip. She was broad as a doorway herself, grey-streaked hair braided with hawk feathers, and she was looking at Freya with an expression of such profound, theatrical disappointment that Freya felt eight years old again.

"It's been two years," Ostara said.

"One year and four months."

"You said you'd come for midsummer."

"I got held up."

"With what?"

"Work," Freya said. "Charms. Smuggling. The usual."

"You said that last time."

"And I meant it both times."

Ostara glared at her for a moment longer, and then her face cracked into a grin as wide and weathered as the pass itself, and she crossed the distance between them and gathered Freya into a hug with her single arm—an embrace that had the structural properties of a rockslide and about the same capacity for escape.

"You're freezing," Ostara said into her hair. "Both of you. Inside. Now. Bramble, don't eat the child, we've only just met her."

Inside, the cabin was warm and dim and cluttered with the specific chaos of a life lived alone but never lonely: harnesses, honey jars, a half-carved yew staff, a wall of dried herbs that Freya recognized as superior to her own stock and tried not to feel competitive about. Ostara stoked the fire, put water on to boil, and only then turned around slowly, arms—arm—crossed, and looked pointedly at Wren, who had installed themselves in the corner nearest the hearth with the stone in their lap and an expression of cautious wonder, presumably at the bears visible snuffling at the window.

"So," Ostara said. "You've got a child now."

"She's not—I haven't *got* her, she's—" Freya gestured helplessly. "It's complicated."

"When is it ever not?" Ostara ladled out barley soup with the ease of long practice, and set a bowl in front of Wren, and another in front of Freya, and a third on the floor for Turnip, who had let himself in through the door that Ostara had clearly given up trying to keep shut years ago. "Start at the beginning. And don't skip the parts that make you look bad. Those are usually the important ones."

So Freya told it. The knock in the blizzard. The frostbitten hands. The language that matched nothing Callum had ever collected except fragments older than the kingdoms. The words *the debt comes due at first snow.* The grey robes at the well, patient as winter itself, asking after a small silent child.

Ostara listened without interrupting, which was unlike her, and when Freya finished she was quiet for a long moment, stirring her soup.

"The Sigil Court," she said at last. "My grandmother used to say the mountain had a tithe. One soul in a generation, or thereabouts. She said it like you'd say the river floods in spring. Just weather." She looked at Wren, who was eating soup with the same grave concentration as the bread. "And now the tithe-collectors are walking around in the lowlands asking after her personally."

"They're calling her a vessel," Freya said. "Like she's a jug somebody filled up."

"And what do you call her?"

Freya glanced at Wren. Wren, sensing herself observed, looked up with a smear of barley on her chin.

"Wren," Freya said.

Ostara's eyebrows went up. Then she smiled—not the big grin, the smaller one underneath it, the real one. "Good name," she said. "Scrappy birds, wrens. Loudest thing in the hedge for their size." She stood, cracking her back, and began pulling blankets from the chest by the wall. "You'll sleep here tonight. Both of you. Tomorrow you'll want the Hollow Road under the ridge—it's the only path the grey robes won't find unless they've got a traitor, and nobody's been fool enough to sell these passes in forty years." She paused. "Because the last fool who tried, I broke his arm."

"You have one arm," Freya said.

"He has one arm *now,* yes."

* * *

Later, when the soup bowls were washed and the fire burned low and amber, Wren fell asleep in a nest of blankets by the hearth with the grey stone tucked under one small hand. Ostara poured two cups of nettle wine and sat down across from Freya, and for a while neither of them said anything. The bears breathed outside the window. Snow ticked softly against the shutters.

"Two years," Ostara said again, quieter now.

"I know."

"You could've written."

"I did write."

"You wrote *one letter,* and it was mostly complaining about the price of wax."

"It was a very good letter."

Ostara laughed, low, so as not to wake the child. Then she leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the humor drained out of her face the way color drains out of the sky before snow.

"Freya. Whatever you're running from—you know I'd walk into that mountain myself if it came to it. You know that. You pulled me out of the ravine with your own hands and set my arm with a stick and a bootlace, and you stayed three days in the cold keeping me alive, and you never once asked me why I was up there. So whatever debt you think I owe you—" She shook her head. "Stop thinking of it as a debt. Debts end. This doesn't."

Freya stared into her cup. Her throat had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

"I didn't stay away because I forgot you," she managed finally. "I stayed away because—" She waved a hand at the cabin, the bears, the warmth, the whole improbable soft-edged life of it. "This. Places like this. People like you. I'm bad at them, Ostara. I run. It's the one thing I've always been world-class at."

"Mm." Ostara sipped her wine. "Funny thing about running, though." She nodded toward the sleeping shape by the fire. "Usually you run lighter than that. Nobody carries a child over Wolfjaw Ridge in November unless they've already stopped running and started *going somewhere.*"

Freya had no answer for that. She looked at Wren—at the small rise and fall of breath, the fist curled around the stone, the absolute trust of a body asleep beside a stranger's fire—and felt something shift in her chest with an audible creak, like ice on a river deciding, at last, to break up.

Outside, Bramble raised her head and growled.

Not the friendly rumble. Not the Turnip-wants-something rumble. A low, rolling thunder that brought Clover up on her haunches and sent Turnip crowding against the cabin wall with his ears flat, staring west, into the dark, at something coming up the pass.

Ostara was on her feet before Freya finished setting down her cup. She blew out the lamp and peered through the gap in the shutters, and Freya saw her shoulders go rigid.

"Lanterns," Ostara breathed. "Four of them. On the low trail. Grey cloaks." A pause. "They're not even trying to hide."

The knocking, when it came, was polite. Three slow raps, spaced like entries in a ledger.

Freya scooped Wren up—awake now, silent, arms locking around her neck—and Ostara pressed her good hand flat against Freya's sternum and steered them both toward the back of the cabin, toward the trapdoor beneath the bears' winter feed that led into the old mining galleries below the hollow.

"Bearhollow runs clean through to the far side," Ostara murmured. "Follow the rope. Don't stop for the echoes—they lie." She was already reaching for her axe, unhurried, terrible, calm as deep winter. "Now go. And Freya—"

Freya looked back from the edge of the dark.

"If I'm not ahead of you on the far side," Ostara said, grinning, "tell my bears they're in charge of the cabin."
