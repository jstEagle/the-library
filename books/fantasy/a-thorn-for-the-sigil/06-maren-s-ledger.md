## 6. Maren's Ledger

The cottage sat where Freya had left it, which was somehow worse than if it had burned.

Three years, and the thatch was still the same crooked brown, sagging on the north side where Maren had always promised to patch it and never had. The herb boxes under the windows were overgrown now—gone properly feral, yarrow elbowing out the feverfew, the whole bed a green argument with itself. The chimney smoked. That was the part that made Freya's chest go tight and strange. Someone was home. Someone had been keeping the fire all this time, all through the three winters Freya had spent telling herself the cottage might as well be a ruin, might as well have fallen into the mountain, because it would hurt less that way.

"Is that your house?" Wren asked. Not in words—the child didn't have words yet, not ones Freya could use—but in the language of tugging her sleeve twice and pointing. They'd developed it over the weeks on the road: one tug for *look*, two tugs for *question*, a sharp pull for *danger*, though so far Wren had only used the danger-pull once, to alert Freya that a squirrel was watching them with suspicious intensity.

"It's not my house," Freya said. "It hasn't been my house for a long time."

Wren looked at her with the particular flat patience of a child who has heard an adult say something obviously untrue and is deciding whether to make an issue of it. Then they tugged her sleeve once more and pointed at the cottage again, and Freya sighed and gave up arguing with someone who communicated exclusively in sleeve-tugs.

"We're only here for supplies," she said, mostly to herself. "Medicine for Ostara's arm, willow bark, comfrey, the good honey Maren keeps—kept—in the east cupboard. In and out before anyone knows we've come down this far."

The grey robes had been asking questions in every village for days now. Coming down from Ostara's high cabin toward the lowlands was either very brave or very stupid, and Freya hadn't decided which, but Ostara's wound had gone hot and angry at the edges, and bear-trainers who saved your life did not get to die of a bad poultice because you were squeamish about old ghosts. So: supplies. In and out. She told herself this with the firm conviction of someone who has already lost the argument.

She knocked. Three years of running, of building a whole new life out of smugglers' roads and traded charms, and when she reached the door of the woman she'd fled, she knocked. Like a customer. Like a stranger.

There was a long silence, long enough that Freya started composing her retreat, and then a voice—thinner than she remembered, papery as old vellum—said, "If you're selling something, I haven't got any coin, and if you're collecting debts, you're four hundred years too late, everyone in this valley owes everyone else."

Freya's throat closed up entirely.

The door opened anyway. Maren stood in the frame, small and stooped and leaning hard on a stick of blackthorn, her silver hair escaping its braid in every direction like she'd fought it that morning and lost decisively. Her eyes were the same fierce hazel as ever. They went wide. Then narrow. Then they did something complicated that Freya couldn't read at all.

"Well," said Maren. "Of all the roads in all the mountains."

"I need supplies," Freya said, because apparently her mouth had decided to lead with commerce rather than the three years' worth of things actually stacked behind her teeth. "Willow bark. Comfrey. Honey. I can pay."

"You can pay." Maren's mouth twisted. "Three years, and my apprentice comes back offering coin. Get inside before the wind gets in too, you'll let all the warm out standing there."

It was such a *Maren* thing to say that Freya almost laughed, and almost crying, and ended up doing some terrible combination of both as she stepped over the threshold. The cottage smelled exactly as it always had: dried lavender, woodsmoke, the faint mineral tang of the sigil-ink Maren used for binding charms. Home, said something deep and stupid in her chest. Shut up, she told it.

Wren slipped in behind her and stopped dead just past the door, head tipped back, staring at the rafters as though reading something written there. The child's nostrils flared. When Freya glanced over, Wren was looking at the corner where Maren kept her ledger-desk—the heavy oak thing with its iron-bound drawers—with an expression no seven-year-old should have been able to produce. It was the look of someone recognizing a place they'd seen in a nightmare.

"Maren," Freya said carefully, "this is Wren. They're—I'm looking after them."

Maren's eyes went from Wren to the window, to the thin grey light beyond it, and something moved across her face like a shadow under water. "First snow's coming early this year," she said, which was not an answer to anything. "Sit down, both of you. I'll put the kettle on, and you can tell me why a hedge-witch who ran off without so much as a by-your-leave is dragging a silent child through the passes in late autumn."

* * *

Maren moved slowly around her own kitchen, and Freya watched her do it and finally let herself see what she'd been refusing to see since the door opened. The way Maren braced a hand on the table edge crossing the room. The grey undertone beneath her skin, the way her breath came shallow after even that small effort. The little shelf above the hearth where empty medicine bottles stood in a row like soldiers after a lost war.

"You're ill," Freya said.

"I'm dying," Maren corrected, setting down two cups instead of three with a matter-of-factness that was somehow the cruelest thing about it. "Since spring. The lungs, the healer says, and then she uses words I don't like, and I tell her I don't like them, and she keeps using them anyway. Healers are very stubborn. You'd know."

"Don't." Freya's voice came out cracked. "Don't do that. Don't make jokes at me like I've been here the whole time."

"Where else would I keep them? You weren't here to give them to." Maren lowered herself into her chair by the fire, and for a moment the fierceness guttered, and Freya saw underneath it just how tired she was, tired in a way sleep couldn't fix. "You left in the night, girl. No note. No quarrel we'd finished. Just gone, like smoke up a chimney. Do you know what that does to an old woman?"

"You know why I left." Freya kept her voice low, conscious of Wren sitting cross-legged on the hearthstone, watching them both with those grave dark eyes. "I found the letter. From the Sigil Court. Asking after me. *Asking after me by name*, Maren, and you wrote back to them, I saw your seal—"

"I wrote back to a great many people about a great many things over forty years of hedge-craft." But Maren's hands had gone still around her cup, and stillness, in Maren, had always meant guilt. Freya had learned that at eleven years old, when asked who ate the last of the plum preserves.

"The ledger," Freya said. Her heart had begun to hammer somewhere up around her ears. "Where is it?"

"Freya."

"*Where is it.*"

For a long moment neither of them moved. The fire popped. Outside, the first flakes of the coming storm ticked against the window glass like fingernails. And then Maren closed her eyes, and reached into the pocket of her shawl, and set a small iron key on the table between them.

"Bottom drawer," she said. "Under the false bottom. Where you found the letter, if you remember, which I suppose you must, since that's the night you ran."

Freya crossed the room on legs that felt borrowed. The drawer stuck; it had always stuck; she hauled it open with the same angry wrench she'd used at fourteen. The false bottom came up. The ledger sat there, fat and ancient, its cover stamped with a sigil she knew now—knew with sickening intimacy—from the grey robes and their wax-sealed writs.

She carried it back to the fire and opened it on her knees.

Names. Pages and pages of names, in Maren's cramped, slanting hand, each entry dated, each with a price beside it. A cure for the Harrow cough, 6 marks. A charm against the blight years. Rent. Seed grain. A new roof, half paid. And against each name, in a different ink—drier, redder, official—a counter-mark. The court's mark. The mountain's mark.

*Received in surety,* the red ink said, over and over. *Received in surety. Received in surety.*

Freya turned the pages faster. Ten years of entries. Fifteen. The valley's whole quiet history of desperate bargains laid out in two inks, and she was nearly at the end, nearly at the last written page, when her eyes caught on a name near the bottom and everything in her stopped working at once.

*Reyes, Freya.*

In faded ink. Faded the way things get when they were written long ago—or faded the way things get when the thing they named has already been partly taken, already thinned, already *paid*. Below it, in the court's red hand: *Received. Held against account.*

The floor tilted. Or Freya did. She heard the ledger hit the hearthstones and heard Maren say her name and heard none of it properly, because a sound had started in her ears like the deep roads themselves, like stone grinding on stone, and every strange familiarity she'd ever felt toward the court's magic—every time a ward of theirs had bent toward her instead of against her, every time the grey robes' sigils had seemed to *recognize* her—came flooding back with the force of an avalanche.

"That's why," she whispered. "That's why the wards never stopped me. That's why I could read the collector's marks without being taught. Part of me is already *theirs*."

"Freya—"

"You sold my *name*." She rounded on Maren, and the grief in her came out as fury because fury was easier to carry. "When? How long have I been collateral? My whole life? Was I even yours, or was I just—an asset you were keeping until the mountain called the debt?"

"The plague year." Maren's voice was steady, which was its own kind of damning. Steady the way a woman is steady when she has rehearsed a confession for twenty years. "You were six. You were dying. Both of us were. Half the valley was digging graves, and I had tried every craft I knew and half I didn't, and nothing worked, and you were burning up in that bed—" Her hand shook now, badly, blackthorn-stick forgotten. "And a man came. Grey robes. Said the Court held cures for those who could pay, and that names were legal tender, and that a child's name, held in surety, would buy what I needed. He said it wasn't a sale. He said it was a loan against a future. He said I could buy it back whenever I liked."

"And you never did."

"I never could." For the first time Maren's voice broke. "The interest, Freya. The *interest*. Every year the price went up. Every charm I sold, every winter I scraped, and the debt grew faster than I could earn. That's the trick of it, don't you see? It's not a loan, it's a hook. They write it so no one can ever pay. I told myself I'd find a way. I told myself every single year. And then you turned nineteen and you were so *bright*, so full of road-songs and rebellion, and I thought—they'll come for her eventually, and better she hears it from strangers far away than watches me fail to save her up close—"

"So you let me run." Freya laughed, and it came out awful. "You let me think I escaped."

"You did escape." Maren leaned forward, fierce again, fierce right to the edge of falling out of her chair. "Listen to me, girl, because I haven't the breath to say it twice. A name in surety is a claim, not a possession. They hold a piece of paper. What they don't have—and what they have wanted for three years, and what I saw in that child's face the moment she walked in—is *you*, walking in on your own feet, bringing yourself to the mountain like a parcel. I bought your life with your name, yes. But you are the one who decides what that name gets spent on."

The fire crackled. On the hearthstone, Wren had picked up the fallen ledger and was holding it in both small hands, head bowed over it—not reading it, Freya realized. *Listening* to it. The child's lips moved slightly, shaping sounds in the old tongue, and where their fingers touched the cover, the court's red sigil flickered like a candle in wind.

Maren stared at the child. At the flickering sigil. And Freya watched the understanding arrive on her old master's face, entry by entry, like ink spreading through wet paper.

"Oh," Maren breathed. "Oh, you poor lamb. You're not running *from* the tithe-collectors, are you. You're what they're collecting." She looked up at Freya, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "The debt comes due at first snow. That's what the old rhyme says, the one my grandmother sang. First snow. Which is—"

The window rattled. Not wind this time. A knock—three measured raps, patient as arithmetic, from the front door of a cottage at the end of a hidden track where nobody was supposed to know they'd come.

Grey, said the light through the curtains. The daylight itself had gone grey.

Freya snuffed the candles out of pure habit and pulled Wren close, and Maren was already reaching for her blackthorn stick and rising from her chair with the grim dignity of a woman who had one scene left in her and intended to play it standing.

"Back garden," Maren murmured. "There's the old root-cellar door under the yarrow. It opens onto the quarry cut, and the quarry cut runs down to the ford road. Take the ledger."

"The ledger's *theirs*—"

"The ledger's *evidence*, and evidence is a weapon, and you'll want it where you're going." Maren pressed it into her hands along with a sack she'd evidently packed while Freya wasn't looking—willow bark, comfrey, the good honey, everything, the old thief. Then she gripped Freya's wrists, hard, with the last of her strength.

"Whatever happens next," Maren said, "know that I never once regretted the price. Only the interest. Now go, before I have to answer that door, and Freya—"

The knock came again. Closer to the kitchen door now, though no footsteps had sounded.

"—when you break into whatever vault they keep these names in," said Maren, with the ghost of her old wicked smile, "take mine while you're at it. *Vale, Maren.* Tell them an old woman sent you, and that she's sorry for the mess."

Freya went out through the root-cellar with the ledger against her chest and Wren's cold little hand in hers, down into the dark of the quarry cut, while behind her, above, the kitchen door swung open and a voice like dry paper began, politely, to ask its questions.
