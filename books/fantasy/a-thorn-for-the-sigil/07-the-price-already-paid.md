## 7. The Price Already Paid

Freya did not remember leaving the cottage.

She was aware, in a distant and unhelpful way, that her feet had carried her down the overgrown path behind Maren's house, past the beehives with their snow-capped lids, all the way to the old stone well where she used to sit as a girl and practice sigils in the frost. Her body had simply decided, without consulting her, that it needed to be somewhere else, and the somewhere else it chose was the one place in the world most saturated with Maren.

Which was stupid. She should have walked to the road. She should have walked to the next valley.

The ledger was still clutched against her ribs like something she'd stolen, which, in a sense, she had. *Reyes, Freya.* In faded ink, in Maren's cramped and careful hand, on the same page as a dozen other names she'd never known and two she had. Sold. Collateral. A line item.

She sat down hard on the well's rim and laughed, once, a sound like a branch cracking.

Three years. Three years of running from Maren, three years of telling herself the story — *she only wanted an apprentice, she only wanted someone to carry the water and grind the herbs, she never loved me, I was a tool* — and all that time, apparently, Freya had been worth exactly enough to buy a cure for a plague.

That was the part that broke her open. Not the selling. The price. Somewhere in the world there existed a number, and the number was what Freya Reyes cost, and Maren had paid it without hesitation, and Freya would never, ever be able to hate her cleanly again.

"You absolute *woman*," she said to the well, because the well couldn't answer, and neither could Maren anymore, not really, not with the fever eating her mind the way it was. "You couldn't have just let me catch it?"

Behind her, small boots crunched through the crusty snow. Wren came and stood at her elbow, solemn as a little judge, breath fogging in quick silver puffs. The child had followed her out without being asked, the way Wren did everything — silently, inevitably, like weather.

"I'm fine," Freya said, which was a lie so transparent that even a six-year-old who spoke no common tongue could see through it, and Wren looked at her with those dark, unhurried eyes and made it clear that they saw through it and were choosing, graciously, not to mention it.

Freya scrubbed her sleeve across her face. It didn't help. Nothing about her face was behaving.

"Here's the thing," she said, because talking to Wren was easier than talking to nobody; the child never interrupted and never pitied, just listened with the whole of their body. "It wasn't even for herself. That's the rotten heart of it. If she'd sold my name for a grimoire, or a lover, or drink, I could hate her till doomsday and enjoy every minute. But she sold it to keep me breathing." She swallowed. "I was the debt. I've been the debt since I was nine years old, and I didn't even know it."

Wren crouched in the snow and began, with great concentration, to draw in the white surface with one mitten. Freya watched idly, expecting a stick figure, a bird, some ordinary childish thing.

What the child drew was a spiral. Tight-ruled, precise, each ring equidistant from the last, and around its edge a series of small angular marks that Freya's hands recognized before her eyes did — because her hands had been drawing those same marks since she was nine years old, because Maren had taught them to her, because Maren had learned them *from the court*, from the ledgers, from the tithe-takers who came every seventh winter.

"That's court-script," Freya whispered. "Where did you—" But she knew. She knew before the question finished forming, and the knowing dropped through her like a stone through thin ice.

The spiral wasn't decoration. It was a seal. And Wren hadn't drawn it to teach her anything. Wren had drawn it to *ask*, in the careful way of someone checking whether the ground would hold: *you feel it too, don't you? You've always felt it.*

And Freya had. Gods help her, she had.

Every charm she'd ever smuggled along the Thornfell roads had come out of Maren's cottage, and half of Maren's craft was court-craft — hedge-witchery grafted onto something older and colder, the way a wild plum might be grafted onto iron rootstock. Freya had spent years assuming the strangeness in her own magic was Maren's strangeness, Maren's bargains, Maren's sin. She'd never once considered that the graft might have taken into *her*, that the familiarity she felt when she handled certain charms — the way the sigils seemed to rise to meet her fingers, the way deep places under stone had always felt less like danger and more like *recognition* — might belong to her alone.

Part of her already belonged beneath the mountain. Had belonged there since before she could read. The court had held a piece of Freya Reyes for fifteen years the way a moneylender holds a promissory note — quietly, patiently, waiting for first snow.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the mountain looming grey above the tree line. "No, you can't have—"

Her voice cracked apart. She pressed both fists against her mouth and breathed, and breathed, and the cold air went in and out of her like she was bellows-work, like she was a charm being charged, and she thought, wildly, absurdly: *this is how it feels, then. This is how all those souls in the ledger felt, right up until the collectors knocked.*

Wren tugged her sleeve.

Then the child took off one mitten — bare fingers, small and alarmingly warm for a creature who'd been frostbitten to the edge of death a week ago — and pressed a palm flat against the back of Freya's hand.

* *

The vision came the way it always did with Wren: not as pictures arriving but as *places* she was suddenly standing in, realer than the well, realer than the snow.

A hall beneath the mountain. Vast, vaulted, lit by no flame she could name — light simply pooled in the stone itself, pale gold, like honey held too long in the dark. Columns rose out of sight. Along every wall stretched shelves, and on the shelves sat stones — sigil-stones, thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one glowing faintly, each one pulsing with a slow rhythm like a heartbeat slowed almost to stopping.

And the sounds. That was what undid her. Each stone hummed, very softly, and the hums braided together into something that was almost music, almost weeping, almost a market-square on a summer morning. Voices. All those voices, filed and shelved and patient.

She understood, in the boneless way one understands things in dreams, that these were names. Every stone a name. Every name a person who had once been somebody's daughter, somebody's nuisance, somebody's whole entire world, and who was now, down here, an entry.

One shelf, near the end of an aisle that went on forever, glowed warmer than the rest as she passed it — not brighter, just *warmer*, the way a hearth is warm, the way a kitchen smells — and Freya reached toward it without deciding to—

And knew, with sick certainty, whose stone it was.

She came back to herself sitting in the snow. She didn't remember sitting down. Wren was kneeling in front of her, both mittens off now, holding her hand in both of theirs, and the child's face was wet — Wren, who never cried, who hadn't made a single sound since appearing on Freya's doorstep, had tears freezing on their cheeks.

"You saw it too," Freya rasped. "All this time. You weren't running *from* it, were you? You were carrying it."

Wren didn't answer, of course. But the child squeezed her hand, once, deliberately, and the gesture said more plainly than any language: *I know what's down there. I know what you are to them. I am sorry, and I am here.*

They stayed like that while the wind moved through the pines. Freya's tears stopped eventually, mostly because she ran out of the heat required to make them.

"Right," she said finally, and her voice came out rough but level, which surprised her. "Here's where we are, then. There's a court under the mountain full of stolen names. One of them is mine. One of them is yours, except yours isn't a name, yours is your actual soul, which is worse, and they're coming for it before the passes close. And the woman who taught me everything I know spent fifteen years pretending none of it mattered because she loved me too much to tell me." She exhaled. "Have I got all that straight?"

Wren considered this summary gravely, then nodded, then added one correction: they pointed at Freya's chest, then drew in the snow — quickly, fiercely — a line through the spiral, breaking it.

"Breaking the seal," Freya translated slowly. "Breaking the debt."

Wren nodded harder.

"Wren." Freya huffed something that was half laugh, half sob. "I smuggle charms. I know which customs men take bribes and which pass-forts have bad drains. I don't know how to break a bargain with something that's been collecting souls since before there were kingdoms. Nobody knows that. That's not knowledge anyone living has."

Wren tilted their head. Then, with the terrible patience of the very young, they pointed at themselves. Then down. Through the snow, through the frozen ground, through however many fathoms of rock, down to the glittering hollow dark where the shelves waited.

*You're looking at the one person alive who does know,* the gesture said.

And Freya looked at this small, stubborn, silent child — this child who had walked out of a blizzard to find her specifically, who had known somehow, the way the old blood always knows, exactly which door in all the Thornfells would open — and felt something shift in her chest. Grief was still there. Fury was still there. But underneath them, like bedrock under scree, something new had settled into place: a cold, clean, load-bearing anger. The kind you could build on.

Maren had spent Freya's name like coin. Fine. Then Freya would go down to the mountain and take inventory of what it bought.

* *

They buried Maren's ledger — temporarily, Freya told herself, firmly — in the oilcloth packet inside the hollow of the dead rowan behind the cottage, because carrying proof of debts into the high passes was smuggler's foolishness, and because she could not bear to burn it, and could not bear to hold it either. Then she raided the pantry of everything that would keep: smoked fish, hard cheese, the black bread Maren had always baked too dark, jars of rendered fat, a twist of dried elderflower for fevers that she took with hands that shook only slightly.

Maren slept through all of it, sunk in her fever, and Freya stood at the bedside longer than she meant to, memorizing the face. It was the same face, older. The same stubborn jaw. When she'd left at nineteen she'd memorized it too, out of spite, planning to enjoy forgetting it.

Some plans fail better than others.

"You could have just *told* me," she whispered. "I'd have stayed anyway, you impossible old goat. That's the worst of it. I'd have stayed."

Maren stirred and murmured something. Freya bent close, hoping — foolishly, hopelessly — for absolution or instruction or anything at all.

"...the blue jar," Maren muttered, "goes... to the left..."

Even dying, giving orders about the pantry.

Freya laughed until she cried, cried until she laughed, kissed the papery forehead, and left a charm of easy passing on the headboard that she wove with her own court-touched sigils, and if the sigils came faster and surer than any hedge-magic ever had — if the mountain's script rose to her fingers like it was glad of her — well. She was done pretending she didn't know what she carried. She'd need every ounce of it where she was going.

* *

They made Ostara's cabin by nightfall of the second day, taking the smuggler's cut above Harrow Ford where the drifts piled soft and the wind couldn't follow. Wren traveled like nothing Freya had ever seen — tireless, sure-footed, occasionally walking with a hand trailing along the rock as though reading it, and maybe reading it, and Freya had stopped flinching at that particular thought somewhere on the second ridge.

Ostara met them at the gate with a crossbow in her remaining arm and lowered it the moment she made out Freya's hat.

"Well," the bear-trainer said, "the prodigal hedge-witch. And this'll be the little shadow everyone's whispering about in the valley, I suppose." She squinted at Wren. "Grey-robes came through asking after a child two days back. Paid in old coin. Old coin, Freya. Nobody's minted coin like that in three hundred years."

Freya's stomach dropped through the snow. "How many robes?"

"Two I saw. Smelled like paper and cellars, both of them." Ostara's jaw tightened. "Told them I hadn't seen hide nor hair. They wrote something down. I didn't care for the way they wrote it."

Inside, the cabin was warm and smelled of beast and woodsmoke, and one of Ostara's bears — Bramble, enormous and honey-colored — lifted his head from beside the stove, sniffed Wren thoroughly, and then, with the gravity of an ambassador, laid his chin across the child's knees and went back to sleep.

Wren went absolutely still with joy. It was the first thing resembling a smile Freya had seen on the small face, and it cracked something in her chest that had nothing to do with grief.

"There's stew," Ostara said, "there's room by the fire, and there's a bolt on the door that's held against worse than monks. Sit. Eat. Then you can tell me why the mountain's tax-men are writing down *my* name."

Freya sat. Wren burrowed into the bear. Outside, far off toward the pass, the wind changed direction, and beneath its howl — so faint she might have imagined it, so faint she prayed she'd imagined it — Freya thought she heard footsteps on the frozen trail behind them.

Steady ones. Patient ones. The tread of someone who knew exactly where the smuggler's cut came out, because he'd been following it for days.

She put her hand on the knife at her belt, and did not take it off.
