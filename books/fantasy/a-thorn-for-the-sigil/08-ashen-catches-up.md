## 8. Ashen Catches Up

The snow had been falling for two days, which meant the trail should have been cold, and Ashen was grateful for it in a way he could not have explained to his superiors. Gratitude was not an efficient use of a tithe-collector. Neither was the way he kept pausing to look at things: the fox prints stitching across the drifts, the way the pines wore their snow like borrowed finery, the small blue light of late afternoon settling over the pass like a hand smoothing a blanket.

*You are an instrument of the Ledger,* he reminded himself, for perhaps the hundredth time that day. *Instruments do not admire weather.*

But the thought slid off him the way water slides off oiled wool, and Ashen had begun, privately, in the sealed room behind his ribs where he kept everything that did not fit the oath, to wonder when exactly that had started happening.

He found the cabin at dusk.

It sat in a fold of the mountain like something the mountain had decided to keep—a squat stone structure with turf on the roof and smoke curling from the chimney in a fat, unapologetic plume. Fences radiated from it in lopsided concentric rings, and beyond the fences, enormous shapes moved through the falling snow.

Bears. Three of them, each the size of a hay wagon, ambling their enclosure with the unhurried confidence of creatures who had never once needed to hurry. One of them paused, lifted its head, and looked directly at Ashen through the snowfall.

Ashen had faced down debtors who wept, debtors who begged, and one memorable debtor in the Karstlands who had tried to eat his ledger page rather than let him record it. He had never been looked at by a bear before. He found the experience clarifying.

"Good evening," he said to the bear, because his mother—no. Because someone, long ago, before the grey robe and the seal-ring and the ledger-sense humming in his blood, had taught him manners, and the teaching had outlasted the teacher's name entirely.

The bear exhaled through its nose, a sound like a bellows considering its options, and went back to its snow.

The cabin door opened before he reached it.

He should not have been surprised. A woman who trained bears would hear him coming long before her animals did, or perhaps the bears told her, or perhaps she simply had the kind of hearing that came from living alone with large predators for decades. She filled the doorframe the way a mountain fills a horizon—one-armed, silver-haired, wearing an apron that had clearly lost several arguments with her profession.

"You've been walking my fence line for ten minutes," she said. "Rustle here wanted to come introduce himself. I told him to give you a chance to run first." She tilted her head. "You didn't run."

"I'm not here for trouble."

"Everyone says that." She stepped back from the doorway, which was either an invitation or a trap, and with bear-trainers it was genuinely difficult to tell. "Come in where I can see you, then. Trouble looks better by firelight."

Inside was warmth so dense it was nearly a substance. A fire cracked and spat in a stone hearth. Herbs hung from rafters. A kettle muttered to itself. And there, at the table—

There they were.

The hedge-witch apprentice, Freya Reyes, stood up so fast her chair skidded, one hand already moving toward a knife at her belt. Beside her, the child—the vessel, the asset, the escaped property of the Sigil Court, item seven thousand four hundred and twelve in the great ledger of the mountain—sat perfectly still over a bowl of stew, spoon halfway to mouth.

And looked at him.

That was the thing. That was the thing Ashen would carry out of this room in whatever remained of him afterward. The child looked at him the way no one had looked at him in forty years of collecting: without fear. Without pleading. Without the quick arithmetic of desperation behind the eyes. The child looked at him the way you look at weather, or at an old scar—something known, something already survived—and then, slowly, deliberately, set down the spoon and turned both small hands palm-up on the table.

An offering. Or an invitation. Ashen could not tell which, and the not-knowing cracked something in him that had been load-bearing for a very long time.

"No," he said, to the child, to the room, to himself. "Don't—"

"Don't what?" Freya Reyes had positioned herself between him and the child, and her voice had the particular edge of someone who has already calculated three exits. "Come to take what's owed? Go ahead. Say the words. I'd love to hear how they sound out loud in a kitchen that smells like cinnamon."

"The taking doesn't require words," Ashen said. It was true, and it was meant to frighten her, and it frightened him instead, because saying it made him hear it.

"Then why haven't you done it?" Ostara asked mildly, from the hearth, where she had resumed stirring something with the serenity of a woman who owned three bears and feared nothing smaller than an avalanche. "You've been standing in my doorway working up to something for five minutes. In my experience, men who mean violence don't spend that long admiring my herb rack."

"It's a fine herb rack," Ashen said, before he could stop himself.

"It is," she agreed. "Thyme's gone mad this year. Now sit down before Rustle gets ideas about hospitality."

He did not sit. He stood in the warm room with the snow melting off his shoulders into a dark puddle that seemed, somehow, accusatory, and he held his oath up against the scene before him and watched it fail.

Here was how the taking worked. He would extend his hand, and the sigil-seal would wake, and the ledger-sense would find the thread of ownership that ran from the mountain down into the child's chest, and he would pull, and the soul would come away clean as a tooth pulled from a gum, leaving the body breathing and empty and compliant. He had done it eleven times. He could recite all eleven faces. He had never once been able to recite them without flinching, which the Court called *residue* and treated with additional oaths.

Twelve times now, if he did it.

The child watched him decide. That was the unbearable part—the child *watched him decide*, patient as stone, hands open on the table, as if to say: *I know what you were sent to do. I will not make it easy, but I will not make it cruel.*

And in that look, Ashen saw the other thing. The thing he had seen once before, thirty years ago, at another doorway, in another village, on the face of another child whose name he had been sent to collect. He had pulled the thread then. He had felt it come away. And the child had smiled at him—not in forgiveness, which would have been bad enough, but in *recognition*, as if they had met somewhere before the world, and would meet again after it, and the taking was merely an intermission.

He had never learned that child's name. Collectors never learn the names; names are struck from the vessels before collection, that the Court may hold what the world cannot miss. But he had dreamed of that smile for thirty years, and in every dream, the child was asking him a question, and in every dream he woke before answering.

The question was on Wren's face now.

"No," Ashen said aloud, and his voice came out wrong, scraped raw, like a hinge unused for decades. "No, I won't."

Freya's knife-hand lowered an inch. "Won't take them?"

"Won't do any of it." He heard the words leave him and could not call them back and found, to his astonishment, that he did not want them back. Forty years of obedience went out of him all at once, like breath after holding, and what rushed in behind it was terror, and beneath the terror, absurdly, was relief. "I resign."

"You can't resign," said Ostara. "Men like you are born resigned. It's the rest of us who have to work at it."

"He means he defects," Freya said. Her eyes had not left him, and they were sharp, and tired, and missing nothing. "Which raises the obvious question of why. You tracked us through a blizzard. You had them cornered in a kitchen. What changed?"

"The child looked at me."

"That's it? They looked at you?"

"You wouldn't understand."

"Try me. I've got nowhere to be until spring."

Ashen looked at Wren—at the open hands, the steady eyes, the terrible patience of someone far older than their body—and made the second decision of the evening, which was harder than the first, because the first only cost him everything he was, and the second might cost everyone else everything they had.

"Sit down," he said. "All of you. Because what I have to tell you is going to ruin your dinner, and I'd rather you were sitting."

* * *

They listened while the fire burned low and Ostara fed it without being asked, and Ashen told them about the Sigil Court.

Not the version from the songs, which was mostly glitter and menace. The real one: a bureaucracy older than the kingdoms, built on accounting so absolute it had become theology. Every soul in the deep roads was an entry. Every entry had a value. The Court's power was not magic in the way hedge-witches understood it—it was *bookkeeping*, raised to the level of natural law, the principle that all debts must balance enforced by beings who had forgotten there was ever anything other than debts.

"And the winter?" Freya asked. She had not touched her stew since he began. Wren had moved closer to her sometime during the telling, a small shoulder pressed to her arm, and Ashen noted it and did not note it, the way you note the sun rising.

"Your Brother Callum translated 'the debt comes due at first snow.' He was more right than he knows. First snow isn't a deadline. It's a *harvest*. The Court extends the long winters by consuming stored souls—each consumption feeds the cold, and the cold drives folk into bargains, and bargains fill the ledgers, and the cycle turns. It has turned for centuries." He paused, because the next part deserved its own weight. "Wren's soul is old. Older than the Court's current reserves. The Auditor has marked it for the rite that ends winter permanently—not a longer winter, but an endless one. A kingdom of ice with the Court eternal beneath it. No spring anywhere, ever again."

The silence lasted long enough for the kettle to cool audibly.

"Well," said Ostara. "That does ruin dinner."

"They're children," Freya said, and her voice had gone quiet in the dangerous way. "The souls they consume. They're people."

"Some of them were. Some of them were sold by their own kin, or gambled away, or traded for cures and harvests. The Court prefers consented debts—it makes the entries cleaner." He said it flatly, because flinching at it now would be obscene; he had processed those entries for decades, stamping each one *received in good order*, and if he had not let himself feel it then, he would not perform feeling it now, in front of these people, for comfort.

"Consented," Freya repeated. Something crossed her face—quick, private, gone—and Ashen, who had spent a career reading faces for signs of hidden assets, filed it away without understanding it yet.

"There's more," he said. "And this is the part I need you to believe, because I nearly didn't. When I refused the taking tonight—" he turned his right hand over; the sigil-seal on his palm had gone from white to a sullen grey, cooling like a dying coal— "the Court felt it. They'll know within hours that I've broken. They won't send one collector now. They'll send a retrieval party, and they'll arrive before the high passes close, and they will not negotiate. Which means—"

"We're already running," Freya said. "We were always already running. You've just given the running a deadline."

"I've given it worse than a deadline." He met her eyes, because she deserved the whole weight of it. "I know the deep roads. All of them. Including the ways in. If we go to the Court—if we go *down*—we might reach the treasury before the rite. Steal the soul back at the source. But the same knowledge that gives us a road gives them a map of everywhere we'll hide, because I helped draw it."

Ostara snorted. "So you're useful and a liability. Welcome to most households."

"Why?" Freya asked suddenly. "Why defect? Really. Not the child's face—I've seen them charm hard men into softness since the day they arrived on my doorstep. There's more in you than that."

Ashen looked at his hands. At the cooling seal. At the faint ghost-marks on his wrist where, he knew, though no mirror would show it, a name used to live.

"Because I was collected too," he said. "Thirty-seven years ago. From a village much like the ones we've been passing through. I was eight years old. I remember my mother's hands—that's all, just hands, flour to the wrists. Everything else about her is gone. Struck. The Court takes the name first, then the face, then the voice from memory, until the person you were is a shape with hands." He flexed his fingers. "I have spent my life taking from others what was taken from me, because the alternative was admitting there was nothing left under the robe worth saving. Tonight a child who cannot speak offered me their open hands while I came to empty them, and I found I couldn't be the instrument anymore. That's all. There's no nobility in it. I'm just a man who ran out of somewhere to put the horror."

Wren moved then. Slid off the bench, crossed the floor, and stood in front of him—small, frost-pale, utterly certain. And took his hand. The ruined one, the collector's hand, the one with the dying seal.

The seal flickered.

Not white again—something else, something warmer, a color Ashen had no ledger-term for. And through the contact, unbidden, came a fragment: *hands, flour to the wrists, a song hummed off-key, the smell of bread.* Just a fragment. Just enough.

He looked down at the child and could not speak.

"There it is," Ostara said softly, into the silence. "Knew you had it in you. Took you long enough to get to my kitchen, though."

"You knew?" Freya demanded.

"I know everything that comes up my road. Bears talk." She jerked her chin at Rustle, visible through the window, watching the cabin with the serene menace of professional security. "Question isn't whether the defector stays. Question is what we do about the army coming up behind him."

"Ashen," Freya said slowly, testing the name—or the lack of it, Ashen corrected, *ashen* was what they called the collectors, ash-colored, unnamed, interchangeable. "If we go down. Into the Court. What are our actual chances?"

"Terrible," Ashen said honestly. "But better than waiting here, and better than any road I can't navigate. And—" he hesitated, then gave her the last piece, because she had earned the truth entire, "—there's a reason the Court's magic has always felt familiar to you, hedge-witch. I felt it the moment I scented your trail. There's an entry in the ledgers with your shape around it. I don't know what it is. But the mountain knows you."

Freya went very still. Wren, still holding Ashen's hand, looked up at her with an expression of such gentle, terrible knowing that Ashen understood, in that moment, that the child had already seen whatever it was—had probably been trying to tell her for days in a language none of them could read.

Outside, the wind shifted, and carried with it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of horns from the valley below. Not hunting horns. Accounting horns. The sound the Court made when it opened a new file.

"They're early," Ashen said, standing, feeling forty years fall off him and a different weight settle on—lighter, somehow, though it would kill him faster. "Ostara, how well do you know the caves behind your north fence?"

"Well enough to lose three bears in them for a week."

"How many entrances do they have that the Court's maps don't show?"

She grinned, and it was not a comforting grin. "All of them, probably. I dug half myself."

"Then we go down tonight," Ashen said, "before the snow seals everything, and we finish this beneath the mountain—where it started."

Freya rose, knife at her belt, grief and fury banked to coals in her face. Wren squeezed his hand once and let go.

And far above, past the storm gathering on the peaks, the first stars of evening came out—cold, distant, and counting.
