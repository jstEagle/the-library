## 9. Ostara's Stand

The bears knew first.

Freya was in the middle of arguing with Wren about the merits of turnips—Wren had pushed theirs to the edge of the bowl with the solemn disapproval of a food critic, and Freya had been delivering a passionate defense of the humble root vegetable as "the backbone of civilization"—when Bruma lifted her head from beside the hearth and made a sound Freya had never heard her make before.

It was not a growl. It was lower than that, a rolling thunder that lived in the bear's chest and came out through closed teeth, and it made the cabin's windows hum in their frames.

Ostara went still over her whetstone. She had been sharpening the same knife for an hour, which Freya now understood had nothing to do with the knife.

"That," Ostara said, "is my girls telling me something's coming up the pass."

"More snow?"

"Snow doesn't make Bruma angry." Ostara set down the whetstone with exaggerated care, the way a person does when they're buying themselves a moment to be calm. "Snow is boring. Snow gets one sniff and everyone goes back to sleep." She rose, joints popping like green kindling, and crossed to the single window that faced up-valley. "Something the girls hate."

Freya joined her. The storm had spent itself in the night, leaving the pass washed clean and blinding under a hard blue sky, and against all that white she could just make out them: figures in grey, moving up the switchbacks without hurry, the way people move when they know exactly where they're going and are certain it will still be there when they arrive.

Seven of them. Maybe eight.

"Grey robes," Freya said.

"I've got eyes, girl." Ostara squinted. "And they've stopped at the shrine stone. Reading it." There was a pause while everyone—including, apparently, the bears—considered what that meant. The shrine stone at the mouth of the pass was older than any kingdom, older than the smugglers' roads, and it said, in a language nobody living spoke: *the mountain keeps its own.* Freya had always found it comforting. She suspected the grey robes were finding it something else entirely.

"They're counting," Wren said.

Both women turned. The child stood by the fire, bowl forgotten, and though the words were common-tongue—Wren's small hoard of them, doled out like precious coins—the voice underneath was that other voice, the old one, the one that made Freya's fillings ache. *They're counting,* said the voice beneath the words, and the floorboards shivered.

"Counting what?" Freya asked, though she already knew.

Wren looked at her with those too-old eyes and didn't answer, because the answer was obvious. They were counting heads. Hers included.

Ostara cracked her knuckles, one hand, then the stump of her other wrist against her belt, a habit Freya had seen her perform before arm-wrestling merchants twice her size. "Right," she said. "Here's how this is going to go."

* * *

The argument lasted eleven minutes, and Freya lost it in nine.

"There's a cave behind the smoke-shed," Ostara said, talking over Freya's third objection with the ease of long practice. "Goes back into the mountain half a mile, comes out below the treeline on the south face. My grandfather used it for smuggling brandy during the Excise War, and if it was good enough for smuggled brandy, it's good enough for you."

"And you'll—"

"I'll be here," Ostara said, "with my girls, in my house, defending my property. Which is legal, incidentally. Mountain law. A woman's hearth is sovereign ground. Any tithe-man who crosses my threshold without invitation is committing trespass, and trespassers get what trespassers get." She smiled, and it was the smile she gave to tax assessors and to the traveling dentist, warm as a hearth and about as reassuring.

"They're going to burn the cabin, Ostara."

"They're going to try." Ostara patted the wall affectionately, the way one might pat a stubborn mule. "Stone foundation, turf roof, timber I cut and cured myself and treated with fire-salts because this region has bandits and I am a careful woman. It's burned down four times in my life and every time I rebuilt it meaner." She paused. "Five times, actually. Don't tell the bears about the fifth, they were there and they're still embarrassed."

"You can't hold off eight collectors with two bears."

"Three bears." Ostara nodded toward the pen behind the cabin, where a shape the size of a haywagon had risen to its feet and was regarding the valley with small furious eyes. "You haven't met Ursa Major. She's shy around strangers. And by shy I mean she once ate a man's tent while he was still in it. He lived. Barely. He gave up banditry and joined a monastery, so arguably we did him a favor."

Freya opened her mouth to argue further and found, to her horror, that her eyes were doing something embarrassing.

"Oh, none of that." Ostara took Freya's chin in her one good hand, callused and gentle, and tilted it up. "Listen to me, hedge-witch. Three years ago you dragged me out of a ravine with a broken arm and a busted leg, in a whiteout, after I'd been stupid enough to argue with a river about whose fault the flood was. You carried me. All five feet of you carried all eleven stone of me, uphill, for six miles, singing—badly—to keep me awake." Her grip tightened, just slightly. "I have been waiting three years to pay that back. Do not rob me of it. That would be crueler than anything the grey robes could do."

"The life-debt doesn't work like that," Freya managed. "I never asked—"

"No, you didn't. That's rather the point of it." Ostara let her go and turned to the bears, who had arranged themselves at the door like a honor guard. "Besides. Look at them. When's the last time Bruma had a proper fight? She's been despondent. Listless. This is practically veterinary care."

From Bruma came another roll of that chest-thunder, and Freya could have sworn the bear was agreeing.

* * *

They left through the smoke-shed at midday, while the light was flat and white and would hide movement against the snow. Ostara loaded them down with dried fish, hard cheese, a jar of honey that she called "medicinal" with a completely straight face, and a flask of plum brandy "for courage or cleaning wounds, depending on how the day goes."

Wren stopped at the threshold and looked up at Ostara for a long moment. Then the child reached into their coat and produced something—a carved wooden bear, no bigger than a thumb, worn smooth by years of handling. They pressed it into Ostara's palm and closed her fingers over it.

Ostara looked down at the little bear. For the first time since Freya had known her, the big woman seemed briefly, genuinely lost for words.

"Well," she said at last, gruffly, tucking it into the breast pocket of her coat, over her heart, where a person keeps things that matter. "Now I absolutely cannot die today. Bad for business." She crouched, joints protesting, and put her forehead against Wren's, an old gesture from some culture neither of them shared but both understood. "You keep these two alive, little one. They're hopeless without supervision. Especially the witch—she thinks turnips are food."

Wren made a sound that might, in any language, have been a laugh.

Then Ostara stood, rolled her shoulders, picked up her axe—a real one, woodsman's weight, the handle worn to the shape of her grip—and walked out into the snow toward the pass, with a bear on either side and a third rising up behind, and Freya watched her go and understood with terrible clarity that she was watching someone walk toward the rest of their life, however much of it remained.

"Move," Ashen said quietly, at her shoulder. He had barely spoken since the ford, since he'd knelt in the shallows and torn his own oath out of himself like a rotten tooth. Now his grey eyes were fixed on the figures coming up the switchbacks. "Every minute you spend watching is a minute she spends alone later. Go. Let her spend her time buying us distance instead of buying us goodbyes."

It was the kindest cruel thing anyone had ever said to Freya. She went.

* * *

The cave behind the smoke-shed smelled of brandy and bat and old stone, and it was narrow enough in places that Freya's shoulders brushed both walls at once. Ashen went last, walking backward, watching the daylight shrink behind them. Wren went in the middle, holding Freya's hand, humming—that low old-tongue hum that made the rock feel attentive.

For an hour, there was only the sound of their footsteps and dripping water and, distantly, Freya's own heart refusing to behave.

Then the mountain shook.

Not an earthquake—earthquakes rumble deep and even, like the world clearing its throat. This was sharper. A crack, then a boom that came through the stone and up through their boots, and fine grit sifted down from the ceiling and glittered in the light of Ashen's cold-flame lantern.

"That was the door," Ashen said. His voice was very level. "Or the shed."

Another boom. Closer, or bigger, or both. Between the booms, faintly, threaded sounds that might have been shouting, might have been roaring, might have been both braided together—and once, unmistakably, a human voice bellowing something joyful and obscene across a valley, and Freya laughed, a wet cracked laugh, because of course. Of course Ostara would taunt them. Of course she'd make it a performance. She'd spent her whole life making the unbearable bearable by making it ridiculous, and she would do it to the end.

Wren stopped walking.

The child turned in the narrow passage and pressed both palms flat against the stone wall, and the humming changed key, dropped, became something felt more than heard, a note that lived in the bedrock itself. And for one strange suspended moment Freya felt the whole mountain listening—the mineral trees far below, the upward rivers, the echoes of the taken—all of it turning, like Bruma lifting her head at a sound only she could hear.

"What's it doing?" Freya whispered.

"Listening for her," Ashen said softly. He had gone pale. "The mountain knows its own. The shrine stone says so." He swallowed. "I never believed it before. It was a story we told the collected. Comfort-words." His hand came up and touched the rock too, hesitantly, a man greeting something enormous and possibly forgiving. "It's true."

The mountain's attention passed over them like weather, like a great slow eye, and moved on—up, toward the pass, toward the noise and the blood and the small fierce woman with the axe.

Then the booms stopped.

That was worse. Freya stood in the dark with her heart hammering and counted seconds, twenty, fifty, a hundred, waiting for the next crash of axe or spell or bear, and it didn't come, and the silence filled up with every possibility at once.

"We have to keep moving," Ashen said finally.

"She might be—"

"If she is, she bought it. If she isn't, she's hiding, and she'll find us, because Ostara finds everything and everyone she decides belongs to her." His voice cracked, just once, on *belongs.* "Either way, standing in a tunnel crying serves nothing. I learned that under the mountain. Grief down there is currency. Up here it's just grief. Move."

They moved. Freya held Wren's hand and Wren held hers back with that grip like iron wrapped in velvet, and somewhere in the dark behind them the mountain hummed a single low sustained note, like a bell struck once and left ringing, like mourning, like memory.

They came out below the treeline as the light was failing, into blue shadow and the smell of pine, and Freya turned to look back up at the pass one time—one time only, she promised herself—and saw smoke rising from the saddle between the peaks. Thin. Controlled. Cabin-fire smoke, chimney smoke, the smoke of a house still standing, or standing enough.

And on the wind, faint, impossible, carried down from the high white silence:

Singing. Badly.

Freya sat down right there in the snow and put her face in her hands and cried until Wren patted her knee with grave compassion and offered her the jar of medicinal honey.

Ashen stood watch, facing north, and did not say *I told you so*, which Freya would remember later as one of the great kindnesses of her life.

But the smoke was thin, and the singing had verses missing, and when night fell fully the mountain kept humming that low mourning note, and Freya lay awake under the pines knowing—with the certainty of someone who has read enough ledgers, including ones with her own name in them—that the bill for this escape had been paid in full, by another, and that someday, somehow, she intended to settle accounts.

Below them, far under the stone, something vast began, very slowly, to wake.
