## 10. Under the Mountain's Skin

The cave mouth behind them sealed itself like a wound closing, and Freya learned in that first hour that the deep roads did not care what she thought of them.

She had expected dark. What she got was light without a source—a grey-gold luminescence seeping from the stone itself, as if the mountain had swallowed a sunset generations ago and had been slowly digesting it ever since. It was enough to see by. It was not enough to be comfortable with.

"Right," she said, mostly to herself, because Wren didn't answer and Ashen only answered when the question concerned survival. "Upward rivers. Mineral trees. Echoes of the dead. Nothing a hedge-witch can't handle."

"You're talking to yourself again," Ashen said.

"I'm talking to the mountain. It seems rude not to, given where we are."

He didn't smile. Ashen rarely smiled, but there were moments—usually involving Ostara's memory, or Wren eating something with disastrous enthusiasm—when his mouth twitched toward it before catching itself. Freya had begun keeping a private tally. Three twitches since the cabin. She intended to get him to a full smile by spring, if they lived that long.

Wren tugged her sleeve and pointed ahead.

The passage opened into a vast gallery where the floor dropped away into a channel cut smooth as glass—and in it, water ran *upward*. Not spraying, not defying anything so dramatic as gravity; simply flowing, unhurried, from the lower dark toward the higher one, as though the mountain had decided long ago which direction rivers ought to go and the surface world had simply gotten it wrong.

Freya crouched at the edge and watched a leaf—impossibly, a green birch leaf, fresh—drift past them at knee height and rise into the dark above.

"That's going to bother me," she said.

"It bothers everyone," Ashen said. "It's how the court moves its tithe. Water carries what's owed upward. Souls, memories, names." He said it flatly, the way he said everything about the court, but his hand had gone to the collector's sigil at his belt—the iron token he'd kept even after defecting, turning it over and over like a man worrying a loose tooth.

"And we're walking beside the river of stolen everything," Freya said. "Wonderful. Any other cheerful facts you've been saving?"

"One." Ashen looked at the water a moment longer than he needed to. "Don't drink it."

* * *

They walked for what felt like a day and might have been three. Time behaved strangely down here, stretching and pooling like honey. Freya marked hours by meals instead of light: hard bread and dried apples at "morning," cheese at "evening," and between them an endless corridor of glowing stone that hummed faintly underfoot, like a hymn heard through a wall.

Wren, meanwhile, was *changing*.

Freya noticed it first in small things. The child's frostbitten fingers, which should have taken weeks to heal, were pink and whole by the second meal. The shadows around Wren bent slightly wrong—not following the light-sourceless glow but leaning toward the child, the way plants lean toward sun. And Wren had begun to *see* things. More than once, the child stopped mid-stride, turned toward some empty stretch of stone, and waved.

"Who's that?" Freya asked the first time, trying to keep her voice light.

Wren held up one small hand, then pressed it flat against their own chest. Then pointed at the empty air. Then back at their chest. A gesture Freya had come to know: *this is me, that is also me.*

Echoes, Ashen called them later, when Wren did it within his sight. Remnants. Everyone the court had taken, worn thin as breath on glass, still drifting through the roads they'd been carried along. Most vessels couldn't perceive them. Most vessels weren't carrying a soul this old.

"They can't hurt you," Ashen said. "They're less than ghosts. They're *leftovers*."

But Wren didn't seem frightened. Wren seemed, if anything, *delighted*. The child would stop and listen to nothing, and nod, and sometimes laugh—a small silent laugh, shoulders shaking, because Wren still hadn't spoken a word aloud since the night at the ford, though Freya had begun to suspect that silence was becoming a choice rather than a cage.

That was the part that frightened Freya. Not the echoes. The *strength*.

Every mile downward, Wren grew more solid somehow, more *present*, as though the child she'd pulled out of a blizzard was being slowly inflated by something underneath the mountain. The old tongue came off Wren easier now—little murmured syllables that made pebbles roll uphill and made their campfire burn blue and cold and bright without fuel. Once, Freya woke in the night to find Wren sitting upright, hand raised, holding a small sphere of that cold blue flame like a lantern, watching the darkness with ancient patience while Freya slept.

A child of perhaps eight years. Keeping watch with fire older than kingdoms.

Freya lay very still and pretended to sleep, and felt the claustrophobia settle over her like a second blanket: the weight of ten thousand tons of stone, and beneath it, the growing certainty that the thing she loved most was becoming less the child she'd found and more whatever the mountain wanted it to be.

* * *

The mineral forest announced itself by sound before sight: a high, faint ringing, thousands of tiny voices, like wind through chimes or rain on a tin roof.

Then the tunnel opened, and Freya forgot how to breathe.

Trees. Actual trees, or the memory of trees, growing from a cavern floor dusted with glittering sand. But their trunks were veins of crystal and ore—quartz white, copper green, one magnificent giant veined through with gold that caught the sourceless light and scattered it into slow-moving rainbows. Their branches bore leaves of mica that trembled and rang against each other. Where a branch had broken, the sap that bled out was garnet-dark and hardened into jewels.

"Oh," Freya said. Just that. Just *oh*.

Wren ran ahead immediately, because children are children in any world, and began carefully—surprisingly carefully—touching the trunks. Each touch made a tone, low and resonant, and the tones layered into something almost like music.

"Don't—" Ashen started, then stopped. His jaw worked. "They won't harm it. They're... it recognizes them."

"Recognizes who?"

"The heir." He said the word reluctantly, like paying out coin. "These forests grew in the age before the court. They're older than the ledger. Older than the Auditor." He watched Wren move from tree to tree, trailing chiming harmonies. "The court cut these roads through living country and pretended the country was always theirs. But the stones remember who the old tongue belongs to."

Freya looked at Wren—at the small figure moving through the rainbow light, ringing the forest like a bellringer who knew all the bells—and felt wonder and terror arrive simultaneously, holding hands.

Because here was the truth she'd been avoiding since the blizzard night: she hadn't saved Wren. She'd *escorted* Wren. All this way, through snow and ambush and grief, she had believed herself the rescuer, the smuggler running contraband souls to freedom—and the deeper they went, the clearer it became that Wren wasn't fleeing the mountain at all. Wren was *going home*. And home, apparently, was a place where her small companion could wake stone forests and bend shadows, and Freya could do exactly none of those things.

"So what happens," she asked Ashen quietly, "when we get there? To the court. If Wren is what you say."

"I don't know." He met her eyes, and for once he didn't look away. "I've served the Auditor my whole life and I don't know what happens when the debt comes due and the debtor turns out to be the rightful owner. No one knows. It's never happened."

"Wonderful. I do love an unwritten ending."

"You say that like it's a virtue."

"Everything's a virtue if you survive it."

He almost smiled. Four, she thought. *Four.*

* * *

They camped that night—or what passed for night—in a hollow among the crystal trees, wrapped in blankets with the mica leaves whispering overhead. Wren fell asleep mid-chew on an apple core, the way children do, ancient soul or no, and Freya sat watching the slow rainbow drift across the child's face.

"She looks happy," Ashen said.

"She does."

"That frightens you."

Freya pulled her knees up. "Everything frightens me lately. That's not new. What's new is *what* frightens me." She nodded at Wren. "Down here, that child gets stronger every hour. Up top, Wren was a mute foundling I fed porridge to. Down here, Wren's..." She gestured helplessly at the singing trees, the upward river, the humming stone. "*This.* And I keep thinking—I'm leading this child deeper on purpose. Toward a court full of things that eat souls. Because a monk with dead languages told me a sentence, and a traitor told me a story, and I just... believed both, because believing gave me somewhere to run *to*."

Ashen was quiet a long time. Above them the mica whispered.

"When I was collected," he said finally, "I was six. I remember crying for my mother until they struck her name from my memory, and after that I cried for someone whose face I couldn't find anymore. That hole followed me for forty years." He looked at Wren sleeping. "The court will do worse to that child than make a hole. It will hollow them entirely and wear the shape. So yes—you believed a monk and a traitor. You also believed a frostbitten child who walked out of a blizzard to find *you*, specifically, out of every door in Thornfell." He paused. "Souls this old choose carefully. Whatever Wren is becoming, they chose you before they chose any of it."

Freya discovered her eyes were stinging and blamed the mineral dust, loudly, twice.

* * *

The echoes got worse on the fourth day.

Or better, depending on whether you asked Wren, who had begun greeting certain ones like old friends, or Freya, who had begun hearing them too.

It started as a feeling—that prickle of being watched in an empty room. Then fragments: a snatch of lullaby in a language she didn't know, drifting up from the upward-flowing river as if the water remembered carrying someone who used to sing it. A woman's laughter around the next bend, gone when they rounded it. Once, horribly, a voice that sounded almost like Maren, saying *mind the third step, girl,* in a cottage kitchen that had been burned to ash and grief weeks ago.

Freya stopped walking. Stood in the glowing tunnel with her fists clenched, shaking.

"That wasn't real," she said.

"No," Ashen said gently.

"She's dead. I saw her. I *buried*—"

"The echoes aren't her. They're what the river carried of everyone who ever walked this road owed and unwilling." He put a hand near—not on—her shoulder; he'd learned that much about her. "Grief wears familiar shapes down here. It wants you to stop walking."

"Well, it can want in line," Freya muttered, and wiped her face with her sleeve, and kept walking.

But Wren had stopped too, and was looking at Freya with that ancient, patient expression, and held out one small hand. When Freya took it, the vision came unbidden:

*A hall of pale stone, vast as a cathedral. Thousands upon thousands of niches in the walls, and in each niche a small glowing stone, and the stones pulsed in rhythms almost like breathing. In the center of the hall, a figure of impossible geometry stood before a desk of black glass, writing, always writing, in a book whose pages were faces—*

Freya wrenched her hand free, heart slamming.

"What was that? What did you *show* me?"

Wren pointed forward, down the tunnel, toward whatever waited below. Then pressed a hand flat against their own chest. Then, carefully, touched two fingers to Freya's sternum.

*Me. And you. Both of us belong in that hall.*

"No," Freya said, before she could think. "No, absolutely not. My name's mine. It's the only thing Maren ever gave me that wasn't a lesson or a lie, and I'm not—"

She stopped. Because Wren was looking at her with something terrible and tender in their eyes, and slowly, deliberately, traced a shape in the air: the old-tongue character Freya had seen carved once on Maren's oldest charm-box, the one the hedge-witch had never explained.

*Debt,* the gesture said. *You already carry it. You always have.*

And somewhere far above them, muffled by a thousand fathoms of stone, Freya heard—she would swear forever after that she heard—the first snow beginning to fall on the passes, sealing shut the way home.

Ahead, the tunnel sloped downward toward a sound like enormous pages turning, and Wren took her hand, and led her on.
