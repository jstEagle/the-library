## 11. The Tithe-Taker's Confession

The deep roads did not believe in night, exactly. There was no sun to set, no moon to fail them—only a slow dimming of the mineral light that seeped from the walls, as though the mountain itself grew tired and let its glow soften toward sleep. When that dimming came, Ashen called it evening, because the child had begun calling it evening, because Freya had accepted it, because even in a place without sky a person needed some shape to hang her hours on.

They made camp in a hollow where three tunnels met—a place Wren had chosen, tugging Ashen's sleeve and pointing with the absolute certainty of someone who could hear the stone thinking. The floor was smooth basalt, warm underfoot in a way stone should not be, and the walls were furred with veins of pale crystal that gave off light like remembered candlelight: close, but never quite right.

Ashen built the fire.

He did it the way he'd been taught, which was the only way he knew. He knelt, drew a circle in the dust with one grey sleeve pushed back, and spoke the collector's word for *burning*—a short, sharp syllable that tasted like coins on the tongue. Cold flame rose from nothing, blue-white and utterly silent, casting shadows that fell the wrong way. It gave heat without smoke, light without consumption, warmth without mercy. It was, like everything else the Court had ever given him, efficient and soulless.

Wren watched it with narrowed eyes, the way one might regard an animal of uncertain temperament.

"It's not real fire," Freya said. She was sitting with her back against her pack, boots still on, arms crossed—not hostile, exactly. Guarded. She had been guarded since the cave, since Ostara's bears had thundered past them into the dark so the rest of them could run. Three days underground, and she hadn't once said the bear-trainer's name aloud, but Ashen had seen her mouth it sometimes when she thought no one was watching. *Ostara.* Like checking that a wound was still there.

"No," Ashen agreed. "It isn't."

"Does it hurt anything? To burn like that?"

Ashen considered the question honestly, which surprised him. He was out of practice at honesty; his training had been decades of the opposite. "It burns nothing," he said finally. "That's rather the point. The Court doesn't approve of taking what can't be entered in a ledger. Fire that consumes is fire that wastes. This—" he gestured at the blue-white tongue licking silently upward, "—is fire that owes nothing and takes nothing. Perfectly balanced." He heard how it sounded as he said it. "Perfectly dead, you mean."

"I didn't say it."

"You were going to."

The corner of her mouth twitched. It was the first almost-smile he'd gotten from her since the ford, and he found—he noted this with the detached curiosity of a man examining a specimen—that he wanted another one. "You're learning to read me, hedge-witch. That's a dangerous habit."

"I read everyone. Occupational hazard of smuggling charms to people who lie about wanting them."

Wren, settled between them with the boneless ease only children and cats possess, reached into the cold flame and pulled their hand back quickly, shaking it—not burned, Ashen saw, but startled. The child looked up at him with an expression of profound betrayal.

"It doesn't burn," Ashen said. "It also doesn't warm. Not really. It's heat the way a painting of bread is food."

Wren's face scrunched. Then the child held both hands out over the flame anyway, palms down, patient, waiting—and something in the cold fire flickered, guttered, and turned gold at its edges. Just barely. Just at the tips, where Wren's fingers hovered. Warmth, real warmth, bled outward from the flame like ink through cloth.

Ashen stared. In forty years he had never seen the cold flame do anything but be cold.

Freya stared too. Then she looked at Ashen, and whatever she saw in his face made her expression change—from suspicion to something more complicated. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"Wren," she said softly, "don't exhaust yourself."

The child ignored her, cupping the golden-edged fire like something rescued from a flood.

* * *

They ate—dried apples from Freya's smuggler stores, hard cheese gone pleasantly waxy, and a strip of smoked goat that Ashen produced from his own pack with such sheepishness that Freya raised both eyebrows.

"Tithe-collectors carry rations?"

"We carry everything," Ashen said. "Rations, rope, restraints, record-books. A collector must be prepared to take custody of a vessel in any condition, in any season, for any duration of transport." He tore the goat strip in thirds and handed pieces across the fire. "We are, if I may say so, extremely well organized."

"You may say so," Freya said. "I'll reserve judgment until you explain why a well-organized institution needs to steal children."

And there it was. He had known the question was coming since the cave, since the moment he'd knelt in the snow and put his seal-stone in Freya's hand instead of using it. She hadn't asked yet. She'd been saving it. Hedge-witches, he was learning, hoarded their questions the way other people hoarded coin.

He chewed. He swallowed. He looked at Wren, who was watching him with those old, old eyes, chewing the goat with small fierce concentration, and he thought: *they deserve the whole truth, or as much of it as I have left.*

"My name was Tomas," he said.

The words came out rusty. He actually heard the rust—the little catch in his throat, like a hinge unused for years.

Freya went very still.

"I was born in a valley on the mountain's far side. Vetchling Hollow. There were forty-one people in it, and my father was a wheelwright, and I was seven years old, and I had a dog named Biscuit who was mostly ears." He smiled, and the smile felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat. "This is the part where you say no one's name was ever really Biscuit."

"I wasn't going to say that."

"You were thinking it."

"...A little," Freya admitted.

"Biscuit was real. So was the hollow. So was I." He fed a splinter of cheese into the cold flame, watching it neither burn nor melt, just sit there glowing faintly blue. "But valleys on the mountain's far side pay their tithes too, hedge-witch. Every seventh year. Grain, iron, livestock—and once in a long while, when the ledger runs thin, a child."

"The debt comes due at first snow," Freya murmured. Brother Callum's translation. He saw her realize she'd said it aloud, and saw her decide not to apologize for it.

"Just so. I don't know why they chose me. I've wondered for forty years, and I'll tell you the honest answer: there was no reason. That's the thing about the Court that no one understands until they see inside it. It doesn't choose out of malice. Malice would require wanting something, and the Court doesn't want. It balances. My village owed a surplus; I was unclaimed collateral; the arithmetic worked." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than he expected. "My mother ran after the collectors for two miles. I know because I watched her from over a collector's shoulder. She stopped at the treeline because the treeline is where the Court's claim ends—or began, then. She stood there in the snow holding her hands up like this—" he raised his own hands, palms open, empty, "—like she was showing the mountain she had nothing left to fight with. And then we went below, and the door closed, and that was the last time anyone in the world knew me by name."

The cold flame crackled. Except it didn't—it couldn't—but Ashen heard it crackle anyway, and decided he didn't care whether the sound was real.

"What happens below?" Freya asked quietly. "To a collected child?"

"They're kept. Fed, schooled, treated gently—gently the way you'd treat a valuable instrument. And when they're old enough to be useful, they're remade." He rolled up his sleeve. On the inner forearm, pale as scar tissue against skin that had forgotten sunlight, sat the sigil—a closed circle crossed by a single vertical line, the mark of the Auditor's service. "There's a rite. I remember fragments. Chanting. Cold. A sensation like being *edited*." He laughed shortly. "That's the best word I've found. They don't take your memories, contrary to what the surface stories say. They leave every memory intact and take the *name*—the thread that ties all the memories to a self. You remember having a mother. You remember Biscuit. You remember loving things. But the love sits in you like furniture in an abandoned house. Nothing lives there anymore."

Freya's hand had drifted, without her noticing, to the pendant at her throat—her focus-charm, the little hammered-silver thing he'd seen her touch a dozen times when she thought no one observed. "And your name?" she asked. "Tomas. Where did it go?"

"Into the treasury. Where all the names go." He said it flatly, because flatly was the only way to say it. "Every soul the Court collects is rendered down to a sigil-stone and shelved. The stones hold what we were. The shells—" he touched his own chest, lightly, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure was yours, "—walk around doing the work."

"And the work?"

"Tithes. Collections. Audits. Keeping the ledgers balanced so the winter keeps coming on schedule, so the passes stay sealed, so the surface folk stay frightened enough to keep paying." He paused. "And lately—this cycle—the special work. Retrieving escaped vessels." He nodded at Wren, who had gone quiet, golden firelight moving on the child's face. "Which brings us, I suppose, to why I'm sitting at your fire instead of dragging the both of you below in chains."

"I was wondering when we'd get there," Freya said, but gently.

"So was I." Ashen leaned forward, elbows on knees, and found that his hands were not entirely steady. "I told you I hesitated at the ford. I gave you the version I understood myself, then: duty strained, memory stirring, the sight of a child making an old collector sentimental. All true, as far as it went." He drew a breath. "It didn't go far enough. What I didn't tell you—what I only understood myself when I saw Wren walk into that river without flinching—is what happens to the vessels after collection. What the Court has planned for this one."

Wren looked up.

"The vessels aren't shelved," Ashen said. "They're spent. A vessel carries a whole soul, intact—do you understand what that means, below? Everything else in the treasury is fragments, leavings, names scraped thin. But a vessel is a complete soul, undivided, and the Court has learned to burn one the way a miser burns his final candle: slowly, over centuries, to light the dark." His voice roughened. "The long winter isn't weather, Freya. It never was. It's a *draw*. The Court spends souls to hold the seasons in place—to freeze the world at first snow forever, because first snow is when the tithes come due, and a world stuck at first snow is a world that pays forever. And Wren is the last full soul left in the treasury's keeping. When the passes seal this year, they mean to light the child like a lamp."

Silence. Even the false crackle of the cold flame seemed to hush.

Freya had gone white. Not with fear—with fury, a banked, structural fury that Ashen recognized because he'd spent forty years mistaking it for calm. "They'll burn the child," she said, "to keep the world paying rent."

"To keep the world *frozen*," Ashen corrected. "Rent implies they want something. They just want the ledger never to close."

Wren made a sound. Small, low, not quite a word—but both of them turned, and the child was looking into the golden-edged fire with an expression no seven-year-old should own: the look of someone hearing their own death described and finding it unsurprising. Old eyes. Eyes that had already done arithmetic.

"Don't," Freya said to the child, fierce and soft at once. She reached over and gathered Wren against her side, and the child came, tucking in like a bird in a storm. "Don't you dare do that math. Nobody's lighting anybody. We're getting your soul back and we're walking out of here and the Auditor can audit the empty shelves."

Something loosened in Ashen's chest. He examined the feeling with mild alarm. It had been a very long time since anything had loosened anywhere in him.

* * *

Later—when Wren had gone down into sleep curled against Freya's pack, breath slow and even, one hand still stretched toward the fire's golden rim—Freya spoke again, low, so as not to wake the child.

"How did you get out?" she asked. "Collectors don't just walk away. You told me about the oaths."

"There are no oaths," Ashen said. "That's the joke of it. The Court doesn't bind us with promises—it binds us with emptiness. An oath requires a self to swear it. We simply... comply. For forty years I complied the way water flows downhill. It never occurred to me to do otherwise, because 'otherwise' requires a someone to choose it." He turned his hands over, studying the palms as though expecting to find instructions written there. "Then I stood at the ford, and Wren looked up at me. And do you know what the child did? Everyone fears us. Mothers pull children behind them. Grown men cross themselves. Forty years, and every face I've ever collected has shown me fear or bargaining or despair. Wren looked at me—" his voice caught on the rust again, "—and the child looked *sorry for me*. Like I was something lost. Like I was the one standing in the snow while someone carried me away."

Freya said nothing. But she had gone still in that particular way of hers, the listening way.

"And something in me—" he searched, "—*remembered itself*. Not a memory. Not Tomas. Nothing so kind. Just—for one moment, the abandoned house had someone standing in it. Looking out a window. And I thought: I am forty-seven years old and I have never once chosen anything. And then I chose."

"That's not nothing," Freya said quietly. "That's the whole everything. That's the only part that counts."

He glanced up. She was watching him across the cold fire, and her expression had changed—had softened into something he distrusted on principle and wanted desperately anyway. Recognition. That was the word. She was looking at him the way you look at a mirror you weren't warned about.

"You understand," he said. It wasn't a question. "Your master sold your name. Part of you is already theirs. You've felt it—the Court's magic tasting familiar, the deep roads pulling at you like a tide." He'd seen it in her, these three days underground: the moments when she moved through the mineral forests a half-step too surely, when the upward-flowing rivers seemed to greet her. "You're looking at your possible future, hedge-witch. Me. Forty years of compliance with a hole where a person used to be. If we don't win—if the Auditor keeps what it's owed—that's you. That's exactly you."

"I know," Freya said.

And then, with the terrible directness that he was beginning to suspect was her native language: "So we won't lose. Because look at you—you got out. Forty years under the ice and you still came up breathing. If that's my future, it's not a warning, Ashen. It's proof it can be survived."

He stared at her. The cold flame threw its wrong-way shadows across her face, and Wren slept on between them, and somewhere far above—so far above that it was more rumor than memory—snow was falling on the passes, sealing the world shut.

"The treasury," he said at last, because hope was a currency he'd forgotten how to spend and needed to count carefully. "If we reach it. If I can still open the doors—my credentials may survive my defection; the Court is slow to amend its records; bureaucracy is the one immortal thing below." He hesitated. Then, before caution could find him: "I'm going to look for a stone. One specific stone. Vetchling Hollow, forty-one souls, a boy with a dog named Biscuit. If it's there—I'm taking it back. Defection was the first choice I ever made. I intend to make a second."

Freya smiled. Fully, this time, no twitch about it.

"Well then," she said, and fed another piece of cheese to the fire that refused to eat it, "when we get there, you'll have help. Stealing is rather my trade."

Far down the tunnel behind them—farther than any torchlight reached, deeper than the crystal-glow—something shifted. Not a sound, precisely. A pressure. Like a great ledger being opened somewhere in the dark, and a page turning, and a dry voice beginning to read their names.

Wren opened their eyes in sleep, sat bolt upright, and spoke one word in the old tongue—a word that made the crystals along the walls flare bright and brief, like a held breath.

Freya was on her feet instantly. "What was that? What did the child say?"

Ashen had gone rigid, every collector's instinct ringing like a struck bell. He knew the word. He had spoken it ten thousand times in ten thousand villages, kneeling before frightened families with his seal-stone in his hand.

"*Auditor*," he said. "The child said: it knows we're coming."
