## 12. The Gate of Names

The deep road ended where the mountain began to think.

Freya felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her eyes, like a word she'd forgotten hovering on the tip of her tongue. The tunnel they'd followed for two days opened into a cavern so vast that Wren's old-tongue glowstones, floating ahead of them like patient fireflies, could not find its far wall. And there, rising out of the dark floor, stood a gate.

Not a gate of stone or iron. A gate of *names*.

Thousands of them hung in the air in a lattice of faint silver script, each one a small burning thread: *Aldous. Mirren. Kettlewick. Sorrel-of-the-Ford.* They drifted and rearranged themselves slowly, like fish in a dark pond, and when Freya looked at any one of them too long, it seemed to look back.

"Well," she said, because her voice worked better than her courage did. "That's not ominous at all."

Wren pressed closer against her side. The child had been quiet even by Wren's standards since Ostara's stand at the pass—not the peaceful quiet of sleep, but the held-breath quiet of someone listening for something terrible. Now those small fingers found Freya's sleeve and gripped it.

Ashen stepped forward first. He'd been stepping forward first for two days, and Freya suspected he knew she noticed, and suspected he did it anyway because it was easier than explaining why.

"The Gate of Names," he said. His voice had gone flat and official, which Freya had learned was what Ashen sounded like when he was frightened. "Every traveler surrenders one memory to pass. It's how the court keeps its borders. You can't carry what you know about yourself through a door that takes pieces of you."

"Charming," Freya said. "And if we refuse?"

"There's no refusing. There's only turning back." He paused. "And the passes above will be sealed within the day."

"So it's the door or the snow. Wonderful. I do love a fair bargain." She squinted up at the drifting silver threads. "How does it choose? What memory?"

"You choose. That's the cruelty of it." Ashen's jaw tightened. "The gate doesn't want just anything. It wants something true, something *yours*. If you offer it something shallow—a bad meal, a rainy afternoon—it knows, and it turns you away. It has to cost."

Freya looked at him properly then. In the light of the glowstones his face was grey as his old robes, and she understood, suddenly and completely, that he had walked through this gate before.

"When?" she asked quietly. "When did you last pay it?"

He didn't answer. Behind him, Wren made a small sound—not a word, never a word, but a note of warning—and the gate stirred.

* * *

The lattice of names parted as they approached, threads sliding aside like a curtain drawn by unseen hands. Beyond it stood an archway of black stone, and set into the archway was a basin of the same material, wide as a wash-tub, its surface perfectly still. Not water. Something darker and more patient than water.

Above the basin, letters of silver light assembled themselves, letter by letter, in the common tongue:

**ONE MEMORY. FREELY GIVEN. TRULY HELD.**

"It reads," Freya said. "The door reads. Of course it does. Why wouldn't the door read."

"It's not reading," Ashen said. "It's *weighing*."

"That's worse. Thank you for making it worse."

She was talking too much. She knew she was talking too much; it was what she did when the alternative was thinking, and Maren had scolded her for it a hundred times across a hundred long winters—*Freya, girl, your mouth runs faster than your wits, and someday your wits will need to catch up.* The thought of Maren arrived uninvited and sat down heavily in her chest, and Freya pushed it away, because now was not—

Now was exactly the time, actually. Because the ledger in Maren's cottage had listed *Reyes, Freya* in faded ink, and every step of this descent had been a step toward understanding what that meant, and the gate was going to ask her for a memory, and she already knew which memories were the true ones. The true ones all had Maren in them somewhere.

"Let me go first," Ashen said. "So you can see it's survivable."

"How generous. A demonstration."

"I'm not being generous. I'm being selfish. If I watch you hesitate, I'll hesitate longer." He rolled his shoulders back the way a man does before lifting something heavy. "Watch Wren instead. Don't watch me."

"Why would I—"

But he was already walking forward, and she understood, and her heart did something complicated and painful in her chest, because Ashen—the disgraced tithe-collector, the man who had been collected himself as a boy and remade into a tool of the court—was about to hand over another piece of himself to the thing that had eaten the rest, purely so that two people he'd known for less than a season would be less afraid.

"Don't," Freya said. "Don't give it something big. Give it a bad meal. Give it a rainy—"

"It knows," Ashen said simply. "It always knows."

He knelt briefly beside Wren. Whatever passed between them passed without words—Wren touched his wrist, once, deliberately, the way Wren touched things that mattered—and then Ashen stood, squared himself before the basin, and spoke.

"My mother's face."

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well.

"I give it freely," he said, and his voice cracked on *freely*, and he steadied it. "I have carried it forty years and it is the only thing I own. My mother's face. Take it."

The basin moved.

There was no splash, no ripple. But the darkness in it *rose*—a slow tide of black reaching up toward Ashen's hands—and from the basin's surface, light bloomed: a memory given shape. Freya saw it reflected in the black stone, ghostly and enormous. A kitchen. A woman with flour on her forearms, laughing at something out of view. Sunlight through a window. A face—round, warm-lined, alive with a smile so specific and so ordinary that Freya's throat closed around it, because it was the kind of face that only mattered enormously, the kind of face that was somebody's whole world.

Ashen was watching it too. He was watching it the way a drowning man watches the shore.

"Goodbye," he whispered. "I'm sorry I kept you so long and used you so little."

The woman laughed. The sunlight held. And then the basin drank, and the image folded inward like a candle flame pinched out, and the blackness settled, still and satisfied, and Ashen staggered back from the archway with his hand pressed flat against his chest.

"Ashen—" Freya started toward him.

"What was her name?" he asked. His voice was steady. That was the worst part. His voice was perfectly steady. "Her name. I know I had a mother. I know I loved her—I can feel the shape of it, like a coat left behind in a house I can't picture. But her *name*—"

He stopped. Searched. Freya watched him search, watched the panic move behind his eyes like a fish under ice, finding nothing, finding nothing, finding nothing.

"Gone," he said at last. "No. Not gone. *Never mine.* The name went into the ledger when they took me, and the face went into the gate tonight, and between the two of them there is no record that she ever—" His breath shuddered out of him. "There is no record. That's what the court is. A place where love goes to become arithmetic."

Wren crossed the space between them and wrapped both arms around his waist, cheek pressed to his ribs, holding on with the fierce practical grip of a child who has decided this adult is theirs now. Ashen's hand came down on the small head, trembling.

Freya turned to the basin.

* * *

"One memory," she said. "Freely given. Truly held. Fine. Fine." She paced a short line before the archway, three steps each way, because pacing was free and everything else cost. "Let's talk strategy. I've got decades of material here. Bad meals alone—there was a mutton pie in Harrow's Ford, urchin-sting summer before last, I remember it *vividly*—"

The basin did not move. Somewhere in the lattice behind her, a silver thread flickered, almost amused.

"Right. Yes. It knows." She stopped pacing. "It wants the true ones."

The trouble was that she knew exactly which memory the gate would weigh heaviest, and it wasn't the plague winter, and it wasn't the night she'd fled. It was the good one. The court had a talent, she was learning, for pricing things at their true value, and the truest thing she owned was this:

Maren, three days after the fever broke, sitting on the edge of Freya's cot with a bowl of broth she'd clearly gotten up at dawn to make, saying, *You scared ten years off me, girl, don't you ever—* and then not finishing the sentence, just pressing the warm bowl into Freya's hands, and the look on her face. The look on her face that Freya had spent three years telling herself had been relief, only, just relief, nothing else, because the alternative was admitting that the old woman had loved her and that Freya had repaid it with a burned letter and an empty road.

That was the memory. That was the coin the gate wanted. And the horrible, clever, court-shaped truth of it was that giving it up might almost be a mercy—no more lying awake replaying that morning, no more guilt with Maren's face on it. The gate wasn't asking for treasure. It was asking for the wound, dressed up as treasure.

"No," Freya said aloud. "You don't get to make it easy. If I'm paying, I'm paying with something I *want* to keep."

Because that was the difference, wasn't it. That was the whole difference between her and the court. The Auditor weighed and took and called it fairness. Freya Reyes smuggled charms down forbidden roads and gave Ostara her life-debt freely and named a mute child after a bird because the child's laugh reminded her of one. Everything she was, everything good in her, ran the other direction from the ledger. She would not let the gate teach her its arithmetic.

She thought about what to give. She thought about kindnesses, because the plan said kindness, and Maren had been kind—but every kindness led back to the same face, and she refused to hand over the whole face.

Then she found it. Tucked away, small, worn smooth by handling: the memory of the *second* best day of her life.

The day Maren taught her to mend a broken charm.

Not the lesson itself—she could keep the lesson, the technique, the craft. But the moment after. The moment when the cracked luck-charm had knit whole under Freya's clumsy fingers, and Maren had picked it up, turned it over, and said, *Huh. Didn't expect that till spring.* Three words. No praise in them, not really. But underneath the words, unmistakable, the old woman's pride glowing like a banked coal, and twelve-year-old Freya standing in the cottage doorway with the evening light coming gold through the glass, feeling—for the first time in her ragged orphan life—that she was *good at being loved.*

That was hers. That was the truest small thing she owned.

She walked to the basin before she could talk herself out of it.

"I give it freely," she said. Her voice only shook a little. "Truly held. The evening I mended my first charm, and my master was proud of me, and I knew it. Take it."

The black tide rose to meet her hands, gentle as a dog taking food. And the memory bloomed above the basin one last time—the golden light, the little charm, Maren's gruff voice saying *didn't expect that till spring*—and Freya watched it with her whole heart, memorizing the fact of it if not the feel, and then the basin drank, and it was gone.

Gone the way a tooth is gone. She could feel the absence. She knew there had been a moment there, an evening, a warmth—and around the hole where it had been, everything else sat strangely loose, like furniture in a room with one window bricked over. She remembered Maren's betrayal. She remembered the ledger. She remembered *Reyes, Freya* in faded ink. She could no longer remember why any of it had ever hurt so much, or why she had ever, stupidly, hopelessly, loved—

Oh. Oh, that was the trick of it. The gate hadn't taken the pain. It had taken the *reason*, and left the pain, and the pain was now a cold shapeless weight with no name, and she understood with perfect clarity that this was what the court did to everyone, forever, and called it accounting.

"Freya?" Ashen's voice. Careful. "Are you—"

"I'm fine," she said, and found she mostly meant it, and the part that didn't mean it was very small and very cold. "Ask me something. Anything. Test me."

"What's your name?"

"Freya Reyes. Hedge-witch, smuggler of dubious charms, better cook than Ostara claims." The answers came easily. Good. Good. "What year did I meet Maren? ...I don't—no, wait. I was eight. I was eight, and she fed me stew, and—" The stew-memory was intact. Only the one window was bricked. Only the one. "I'm fine. Mostly fine. Statistically fine."

Wren appeared in front of her and took both her hands, and looked up at her with those old, old eyes, and shook their head slowly.

"What?"

Wren pointed at her chest. Then at the basin. Then pressed their small palm flat over her heart, and held it there, and their expression was grief and fury braided together—grief for her, fury at the gate—and Freya realized the child could *see* it. Could see the hole. Could see the missing piece the way you can see a missing tooth in a smile.

"It's all right," Freya lied gently. "It's one memory. I've got thousands more."

Wren didn't believe her. Wren held on a moment longer anyway, and then stepped back, and turned to face the gate, and lifted their chin in a way that was somehow both six years old and older than every kingdom in the world.

"No," Freya said sharply. "Absolutely not. You're not paying. You *are* the payment, that's the entire point of this mad errand—you're not giving it a souvenir on top."

Wren looked at her. Looked at the basin. Looked back at her, and raised one eyebrow with devastating precision, and Freya had the sudden sinking certainty that the child had learned that expression from *her*.

"You don't have anything to give anyway," Freya reasoned. "Do you? You don't even talk. What's the gate going to take, your favorite rock?"

The basin stirred.

Silver light gathered above it—not a memory this time, but letters, assembling themselves in careful, deliberate strokes of the common tongue, letter by letter, as though the gate were speaking slowly for a child's benefit. Or as though the child were speaking *through* it.

**THE LITTLE ONE HAS ALREADY PAID.**

Freya went cold. "What does that mean?"

**EVERYTHING IT HAD WAS TAKEN BEFORE IT CAME. THE GATE DOES NOT CHARGE TWICE.**

Behind her, Ashen made a sound like a man who has just understood a joke told at his expense forty years ago. "Of course," he breathed. "Of course. Wren isn't passing through the border. Wren *is* border goods. The court's claim. The gate recognizes its own ledger." His voice dropped. "It was never going to stop Wren at the door. Wren is expected."

The letters faded. The lattice of names parted wider, threads drawing aside like courtiers bowing, and beyond the archway the dark opened downward—a stair cut from living stone, descending into a glow that was not warm, a glitter that was not kind.

Wren slipped a small hand into Freya's. And Freya, nameless-pained and one memory poorer, looked down at the child and up at Ashen—at the man who no longer knew his mother's face but still knew how to stand between danger and other people—and felt the cold shapeless weight in her chest shift, just slightly, into something with edges. Something she could hold.

Whatever waited down those stairs had taken enough from her family already.

"Right," she said. "After you, Master Ashen. You know the way."

"I know the way," Ashen said grimly, and led them down into the glittering dark.
