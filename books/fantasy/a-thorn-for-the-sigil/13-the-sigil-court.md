## 13. The Sigil Court

The gate took Ashen's mother's face and Freya's memory of Maren's kindness, and then it let them through, the way a toll-keeper lets through travelers who have paid in teeth.

Freya didn't cry. She noted that about herself, distantly, the way you note the weather. The grief was there—it was enormous—but it had no name attached anymore, only a shape, like a dent left in a chair cushion by someone who has gotten up. She kept reaching for the memory the way your tongue finds the gap where a tooth was, and every time she found the gap instead of the tooth, something in her chest went quietly, terribly *oh*.

Wren held her hand. That helped. The child's grip was small and cold and absolutely certain.

"Right," Freya said, because her voice needed to do something or she was going to scream into the beautiful dark ahead of them. "Everyone still themselves? Ashen?"

Ashen flexed his fingers one at a time, checking. His face was very pale, and very empty around the eyes, like a room that had been swept for company. "I don't remember what I gave," he said. "Which is the point, I suppose." A pause. "I remember that I had a mother."

"Yes."

"I remember *that* she existed."

"Yes," Freya said again, gently. "Hold on to that. It's more than they wanted you to keep."

They walked on, into the mountain's heart.

* * *

No description Freya had ever read—and Maren's cottage had shelves of them, books about the Sigil Court written by people who had never been—came close, and she understood quickly why: the place refused comparison. It ate metaphor the way the deep roads ate sound.

They emerged from a corridor of black stone onto a ridge, and below them the court opened up like a wound full of light.

Caverns, yes, but caverns the way an ocean is a puddle. Vault above vault, descending farther than the eye could follow, every surface worked and gleaming—veins of silver traced through black rock like handwriting, galleries carved in spirals, bridges of pale stone arcing over air that itself seemed polished. And everywhere, everywhere, *light*: not from torches or crystals but from the stone itself, a low warm gold glow, as if the whole mountain were a lamp lit from inside by something patient.

"It's beautiful," Wren said.

It was the third thing Wren had ever said in the common tongue—the first two being "bread" and "no"—and Freya nearly missed the rest of the descent because of it. Beautiful, yes. Wren was right. And Wren's voice, small and rough from disuse, saying it, made it worse somehow, made the beauty feel like a trick being played specifically on her.

Because she could see now, as they came down the switchback stair into the upper galleries, what the light *was*.

The nameless walked the courts below.

There were hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe, spilling down through the vaults like stars reflected in dark water: figures graceful and unhurried, dressed in clothes that shifted color as they moved, faces so lovely that Freya's eyes kept sliding off them the way water slides off grease. They danced in slow measures in the great halls. They conversed in alcoves, heads bent together. They sat at long tables set with food that never diminished and drank from cups that were always full.

And every single one of them was faintly, horribly *dim*. Like lamps running low. Like rooms where someone had opened all the windows and let the warmth out years ago and no one had noticed, or no one had minded, or no one had been capable anymore of minding.

"They're the collected ones," Ashen said quietly. He had put his grey hood back, though there was no one here left to hide from; perhaps it was habit, perhaps armor. "The ones whose names are in the ledger. They serve. They shine. They don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I stood in these halls for sixty years, Freya. I greeted friends here. I remember liking it."

"Do you remember their names? Your friends'?"

His jaw worked. "No. Only that I had them."

A party of three nameless courtiers drifted past them on the gallery, close enough to touch. One turned its head as it passed—a face like a portrait of a person painted by someone who had only ever had the person described to them—and smiled at Wren. The smile was radiant. The eyes behind it were two dim windows in a house where nobody lived.

"*Pretty*," it said, and the word came out shaped wrong, worn smooth from use, a coin rubbed featureless. "*Pretty little pretty.*"

Then it drifted on, smiling at nothing, shining at nothing.

Wren's hand tightened around Freya's hard enough to hurt. Freya was glad of it. She suspected if Wren let go she might float upward and shatter like a dropped plate.

"Don't look at them too long," she murmured. "That's advice my master gave me about bog-wisps and it seems to hold." She heard her own voice shake and hated it, and pressed on anyway, because humor was a rope and she meant to climb it. "Also don't eat anything. Also don't say thank you. Also, if anyone offers you a dance, I want you to run so fast you leave a Wren-shaped hole in the wall."

Something huffed out of Wren that might have been a laugh. Might have been. Down here, in the land of the hollow, even almost-laughter felt like contraband.

* * *

The treasury revealed itself gradually, the way the truth does.

First there was simply another hall, grander than the rest, domed in midnight stone shot through with silver script. Then Freya realized the script was moving—that the walls were *writing themselves*, line after line after line, an endless quiet scribbling like rain on a roof. Then she saw what filled the floor.

Shelves.

Rows upon rows upon rows of them, rising in tiers toward the distant dome, and on every shelf, stones. Small ones, fist-sized, cut smooth and glowing with that same inner gold as the cavern walls—except each stone glowed differently. Some steady. Some flickering. Some guttering so faintly that Freya's heart clenched before she knew why, before her mind caught up with her body and understood what her body already knew: those were the old ones. Those were souls close to going out.

"There," Ashen breathed.

He pointed with his chin, because his hands had gone to fists at his sides. On a shelf near the hall's center, apart from the others, on a stand of white bone like something precious set out for display—

A sigil-stone the size of an egg, burning blue-white. Not the court's honeyed gold. A clean, cold, fierce light, the light of frost at dawn, of snow-blindness, of a voice calling across a valley. It pulsed slowly, steadily, like breathing. Like a small chest rising and falling in sleep.

"Wren," Freya whispered.

Beside her, Wren stared at the stone, and the stone flared—once, bright, urgent—and Wren's free hand rose halfway toward it without any permission from Wren at all.

"That's you," Freya said. Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "That's *you*, sweetheart, that's—"

"Home," Wren said. In the old tongue first, a word like ice cracking on a river, and then, fumbling, in the common one: "Home. Half of home. Half of me."

And Freya looked at the thousands of shelves, and the thousands of stones, each one pulsing its private pulse, and thought: *every one of those is somebody's half. Somebody's whole. Somebody standing right now in these galleries, dim and lovely and hollow, walking past their own soul on a shelf and feeling nothing, remembering nothing, not even enough to grieve.*

Revulsion hit her so hard she had to swallow against it. It wasn't the ugliness that did it—there was no ugliness here, that was the devilry of the place—it was the beauty. Beauty as a warehouse. Wonder organized by catalog number. Somewhere in this glittering dome was a shelf holding whatever was left of the three courtiers who had called Wren *pretty little pretty*, and somewhere a ledger entry to match, neat as arithmetic, and somewhere an Auditor who considered it all, Freya understood suddenly and completely, *balanced*.

"How many?" she asked Ashen. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"No one counts," Ashen said. Then, bitterly: "That's not true. One counts. Exactly one. It is counting right now, I promise you. It is always counting."

* * *

They found that out within the hour.

You couldn't loiter in the treasury. Ashen's collector's credentials—a grey seal sewn into his cuff that he'd been stroking nervously since the gate—got them past the threshold guards, who were not people at all but tall constructions of shadow and silver wire that smelled, absurdly, of ink. But the hall itself watched. Freya could feel it: the moving script on the walls pausing, fractionally, whenever they paused. Reading *them* the way they read it.

"We can't take it tonight," Ashen murmured, drawing them back toward the gallery doors. "There's a protocol for removal. Everything here has a protocol. You present credentials, the ledger verifies your claim, the Auditor—" he swallowed "—the Auditor countersigns. Without that, the moment we touch the stone, every bell in this mountain rings."

"Then we get the counter-signature," Freya said. "How?"

Ashen looked at her the way you look at someone who has just suggested politely asking the avalanche to step aside.

"You request an audience," he said. "With the Auditor itself."

* * *

It came for them before they could decide how.

They were crossing one of the pale bridges between galleries—Wren in the middle, Freya and Ashen bracketing, all of them trying to walk with the aimless grace of people who belonged here and managing, Freya suspected, the aimless grace of sheep in a parlor—when the light changed.

Every golden glow in the caverns, every stone on every shelf leagues away in the treasury, every vein of silver in the black rock, dimmed. All at once. Together. Like a held breath.

And then, at the far end of the bridge, where the gallery opened into the great central hall, something arrived.

It didn't walk. It resolved. One moment the doorway was empty; the next, a figure stood there, and Freya's eyes watered trying to hold it. It was tall and thin as a stylus. It wore robes that weren't robes but columns of small grey characters, scrolling slowly downward, endless entries flowing over its shoulders and pooling around its feet and dissolving. Where a face should be there was a smooth plane of pale stone, and set in the stone, where eyes should be, two lines of light that moved ceaselessly, reading. Reading everything. She felt them pass over her like the flat of a cold blade, and inside them she felt herself being *weighed*—not judged, judged would have been almost human, but weighed, measured, entered.

When it spoke, its voice was the most terrible thing yet: perfectly kind, perfectly neutral, the voice of a scale coming to rest.

"Three entries unaccounted for," said the Auditor. "One vessel, escaped, returned. One collector, absent from post four hundred and twelve days. One name I have not seen written in nineteen years."

The two lines of light settled on Freya and stopped moving for the first time.

"Hello, Reyes, Freya," it said gently. "You are overdue. Won't you come and settle?"
