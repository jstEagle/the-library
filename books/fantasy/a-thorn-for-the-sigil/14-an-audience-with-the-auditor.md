## 14. An Audience with the Auditor

The doors of the Sigil Court were made of a single slab of white stone, and they opened without being touched, which Freya found deeply rude. She had spent three years knocking on doors that pretended not to hear her. It seemed only fair that somewhere, at least once, a door should knock for her.

Instead it simply parted, the way water parts around a dropped stone, and the hall beyond swallowed them whole.

"Stay close," Ashen murmured. His voice had gone thin since the Gate of Names. He kept touching his own cheek as though checking that his face was still there—she supposed he was, in a way. He'd left his mother's face behind at the gate, traded for passage, and whatever expression he wore now was built over an absence.

Wren's small hand found hers. The child's grip was warm, impossibly warm, after days of cold roads and colder stone. That warmth had frightened Freya at first. Now she held onto it like a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

The audience hall was not what she expected. She had imagined something like Maren's cottage scaled up by nightmare: crooked shelves, jars of unspeakable things, a cauldron breathing smoke. Instead the court was beautiful. That was the worst part. Columns of pale mineral rose into darkness overhead, veined with threads of light that pulsed slow as sleeping hearts. The floor was polished to a mirror shine, and when Freya looked down she saw not her boots but glimpses—flashes—of other people walking other halls, in other years. Echoes caught in the polish like flies in amber.

Every one of them looked tired.

"They're still here," she whispered. "The ones it took."

"The court keeps everything," Ashen said. "That's rather the point."

At the far end of the hall, where a throne should have been, there was instead a desk.

It was an ordinary desk. Oak, or something pretending to be oak, scarred with ink rings and the ghost-marks of a thousand ledgers set down carelessly. Behind it sat the Auditor.

Freya had braced herself for a monster. What she got was a figure of middling height in robes of undyed grey, its face smooth as an egg, featureless except where light moved beneath the surface like writing seen through paper. When it turned toward them, the light beneath its skin rearranged itself into something almost like eyes, almost like a smile—the way clouds almost look like castles if you squint hard enough and want to believe.

"Reyes, Freya." Its voice was dry and pleasant, like a page turning. "You are eleven minutes early. How refreshing. Most vessels arrive screaming."

"We're not here to deliver anything," Freya said. Her voice came out steadier than her knees deserved credit for.

"No," the Auditor agreed pleasantly. "You are here because you have not yet decided otherwise. There is a difference. Please—" it gestured to two chairs before the desk, upholstered in soft grey cloth, positioned with the exact geometry of hospitality, "—sit. This will take some time, and I find standing negotiations end poorly. People pace. Pacing is indecision made visible, and I dislike watching my guests be indecisive in front of me."

Ashen did not sit. Wren did not sit. Freya, after a heartbeat of stubbornness, realized her legs were shaking and sat anyway, dragging Wren onto her lap despite the child being nearly too big for it. Wren allowed this with the grave dignity of a cat being held against its will.

The Auditor opened a ledger.

Freya's stomach dropped. She knew that ledger. She had watched Maren keep one just like it, same binding, same faded rules on the pages—though Maren's had been a copy, a daughter-book, a merchant's duplicate of the true account. This was the original. The master ledger of the Sigil Court, and every name in the world that had ever been sold into it.

"You've seen its kind before," the Auditor observed. It did not look up. Its pen moved in small, precise strokes, though Freya could not see it writing anything. "In your master's cottage. You burned her letter, you know. Three years of letters, actually. She wrote every season. We keep copies of correspondence concerning our accounts. It seemed relevant."

"I don't care."

"That is also recorded." The pen scratched. "Page four hundred and twelve, Reyes, Freya. Acquired as collateral, age nineteen, against debt incurred by Voss, Maren, hedge-witch of the Thornfell marches. Debt: one cure, plague-fever, administered to the debtor herself. Value assessed at—" the Auditor paused, and the light beneath its face flickered through several expressions too fast to read, "—considerable. Your master was dying, and the cure was expensive, and names are the only currency we accept. She paid with yours. You have been ours since you were nineteen years old. You simply haven't been collected yet, because collection requires proximity to the court, and you have been very diligent about staying away. Three years of smuggler's roads. Impressive, really. We do admire diligence."

Freya's hands curled in Wren's cloak. "Then collect me. Take me now and leave the child alone."

"Oh, I couldn't possibly." The Auditor finally set down its pen and folded its hands, and the gesture was so human, so much like a village clerk preparing to explain why your taxes were late, that Freya wanted to laugh or scream. "Your account is collateral, not payment. Collateral has different rules. And besides—" the light beneath its face arranged itself into something that was unmistakably, deliberately, a kind expression, "—I am not interested in collecting you, Mistress Reyes. I am interested in *hiring* you."

* * *

The room changed.

Not the hall—the air inside Freya's head. One moment she was sitting in a chair in a glittering cavern beneath a mountain; the next she was standing in a cottage doorway with morning sun on her face and the smell of bread in the air.

She knew this cottage. Of course she knew it. It was Maren's cottage—but *her* cottage, the way it might have been if she'd never run. The herb garden was weeded. The shutters were mended. A kettle hummed on the stove, and there were two cups on the table, and one of them was chipped in exactly the way the cup she'd always claimed as hers had been chipped, back when she was fifteen and foolish enough to feel ownership over a piece of pottery.

Maren stood at the counter, kneading dough, her back to the door. Older than Freya remembered. Greyer. But alive, and humming, off-key, the same tuneless song she'd always hummed when she thought no one was listening.

"You could have had this," said the Auditor's voice, coming from everywhere and nowhere, gentle as snowfall. "This is not a fantasy. Every detail is drawn from the true ledger. Had you stayed. Had you forgiven her. Had you let yourself be loved badly by someone who loved you well. She sold your name to save her own life, Freya. That was a sin. But she spent her remaining years trying to buy it back, and she failed, and she died last winter with your letter unopened on her table—the reply she never sent, because she was afraid you'd burn that one too."

"She didn't die last winter," Freya said. Her voice sounded strange in the sunlit kitchen. "We found her. She was dying, but—"

"Time moves differently here. Forgive us. The point stands." The vision-Maren turned then, flour on her hands, and smiled at Freya with such unguarded love that Freya's chest physically hurt. "Look how easy it would have been. Look how *warm*."

"I know how warm it was." Freya's eyes stung. "I lived there. That's why I left."

"You left because she lied to you. Because she sold you. Because staying meant being owned." The kitchen dimmed at the edges, frost creeping in from nowhere, and the Auditor's voice wrapped around her like a shawl. "But consider: you are already owned. That is the tragedy of your situation, and I say this with genuine professional sympathy—you are *already in the ledger*, child. Your name sits in my treasury beside ten thousand others. The question before you is not whether you belong to the court. The question is whether you belong to it *miserably* or *comfortably*."

The kitchen dissolved. In its place bloomed another life: Freya behind a proper shop counter in a proper town, charms displayed on velvet instead of hidden in saddlebags, customers lining up, coin stacking up, respectability stacking up. Then that dissolved too: Freya older, silver-haired, teaching apprentices of her own in a schoolhouse with her name carved over the door—*REYES, FREYA, HEDGE-WITCH*—and the carving was deep and permanent and no one could ever take it from her because it had been bought back, restored, *forgiven*.

"All debts cleared," the Auditor murmured. "Your name returned to you, whole and legal and yours forever. Your master's shame erased from every record. And all we ask in return is the smallest thing. The tiniest thing. Deliver the vessel. Walk the child to the treasury door willingly—that is all willingness means, a pair of feet moving in the correct direction—and everything else is given freely."

"And the child?" Freya asked. Her mouth was dry. "What happens to Wren?"

The Auditor's face went smooth and blank as fresh paper.

"The vessel's account is settled according to court law," it said. "Which is to say: it is consumed. Its soul extends the winter, the winter preserves the mountain, the mountain preserves the court, and the court preserves order. Order, Mistress Reyes, is why the passes stay passable, why plagues end, why debts come due on schedule and not before. You have benefited from our order your entire life. You simply never received the invoice."

* * *

They gave her an hour.

She knew it was an hour because the Auditor told her so, precisely, and then left her alone in a side chamber with a window that showed a garden that did not exist, full of flowers that bloomed in colors no flower had ever managed. Wren sat beside her on the bench, pressed close, and did not speak—could not speak—and watched her with those old, old eyes.

"Don't look at me like that," Freya said.

Wren looked at her like that anyway.

"It's a good offer." Freya stared at the impossible flowers. "That's the horrible part. It's a genuinely good offer. My name back. Everything forgiven. I could stop running. I could stop smuggling charms in false-bottomed saddlebags and sleeping in barns and flinching every time someone says my name too loudly, because right now my name isn't even really mine, it's—it's a lien. It's been a lien since I was nineteen and I didn't even know." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you know what that's like? Walking around for three years wearing something that belongs to someone else? Every time anyone called me Freya, they were—they were reading off a ledger. They just didn't know it. *I* didn't know it."

Wren took her hand.

Warmth spread up her arm—not magic, exactly, or not only magic. Memory. The child shared things the way other children shared sweets: a glimpse of Ostara laughing in her cabin, one-armed and enormous, feeding bears by hand; the smell of Brother Callum's ink; the specific weight of Ashen's reluctant, awkward loyalty. The family they'd gathered, piece by piece, on the road.

"You're saying I already have it," Freya whispered. "The thing it's offering. Or something better."

Wren nodded once, slow and solemn.

"But it'll take you anyway, won't it. Whether I say yes or no. If I say yes, it's clean and I get paid. If I say no, it sends the guards and takes you and probably takes me too, and my name stays in that ledger until the mountain crumbles." Freya closed her eyes. "It's rigged. The whole bargain is rigged. It's offering me a comfortable cage and calling it freedom, and the price is you."

Wren leaned their head against her shoulder. Small. Warm. Trusting her completely, which was either the most wonderful or most terrible thing anyone had ever done.

"One hour," Freya said. "It gave me one hour. Which means it thinks an hour is enough. Which means it's done this before—hundreds of times, thousands—and it's *right* often enough to keep doing it." She stood, and pulled Wren up with her, and felt something settle in her chest like a lock clicking. Not peace. Resolve. Peace could come later, if she survived. "So here's what we're going to do. I'm going to walk back into that hall and stall that smug, featureless accountant until Ashen does whatever he's doing in the treasury. And then I'm going to offer it a trade it can't refuse."

Wren tilted their head: *what trade?*

Freya smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile she wore across card tables from men who thought they'd already won.

"It wants a name freely given? Fine. I've got one it doesn't know about yet—one it's never priced, never appraised, never entered in its precious ledger. And under its own court law, a freely given name outranks a taken one." She squeezed Wren's hand. "It taught me the rules itself. Arrogant thing. Never teach a smuggler the rules, Wren. We only ever use them against you."

Beyond the door, the Auditor's dry, pleasant voice called out, patient as winter:

"Your hour is concluded, Mistress Reyes. Do come in. I have prepared two contracts—one for each of your possible answers—and I do so hate wasting paperwork."
