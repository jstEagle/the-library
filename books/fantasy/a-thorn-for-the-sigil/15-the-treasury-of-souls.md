## 15. The Treasury of Souls

The Auditor's hall had a way of making a person feel like an entry in someone else's book.

Ashen knew the feeling intimately. He had been that entry once — a line of ink in a ledger, a debt collected, a name struck through with the particular neatness of a clerk who took pride in his work. He had spent forty years on the other side of the desk since then, and he had told himself, all those years, that being the pen was better than being the page.

Standing in the antechamber while Freya Reyes did the most reckless thing he had ever witnessed — talking to the Auditor as though it were a man who could be flattered, delayed, argued with — Ashen finally admitted the truth: it was not better. It was only quieter.

"—and of course I understand the necessity of procedure," Freya was saying, her voice pitched just high enough to be annoying, which Ashen suspected was deliberate. She had discovered, somewhere between the gate and this moment, that the Auditor could not abide inefficiency, and she was weaponizing it with the shameless glee of a woman who had spent three years cheating customs men. "But surely you can't process the vessel's intake without verifying the chain of custody. That would be *sloppy*."

The Auditor's many faces rippled. Somewhere in its mass of parchment and quill-light, something ground its teeth.

Ashen turned before he could be caught watching, and walked away down the corridor of grey stone, his collector's sigil held loose in his fist. The sigil was a small thing, a disc of cold iron stamped with the Court's mark — a circle bisected by a line, the symbol for *accounted for*. Every door in the deep roads answered to it. Every lock. Every ward.

He had used it ten thousand times without thinking. It had never once occurred to him to wonder what it opened.

Until now.

* * *

The treasury lay below everything else, at the bottom of a stair so long that Ashen's knees began composing complaints somewhere around the two-hundredth step. The walls sweated cold. His breath came out in little ghosts, and each ghost seemed reluctant to leave him, hanging about his shoulders like a nervous dog.

He passed a landing where a door stood ajar. Through the gap he saw a room full of chairs facing a blank wall, arranged with terrible precision, and he understood without knowing how that these were the seats where collectors waited to be sent out, and that he himself had sat in one of them, once, being told what he was for.

He kept walking.

The stair ended at a door unlike the others. It was not iron or stone but something pale and smooth, like bone polished by centuries of hands, and across its face ran lines of script in the old tongue — the language Wren spoke, the language the mountain dreamed in. Ashen could not read it. But he could feel it reading *him*, a slow tickling pressure behind his eyes, like a clerk checking his credentials against some vast and patient list.

He raised the sigil.

"Collector," he said. His voice echoed strangely, coming back to him thinner, younger. "Ashen. Returned under writ. Escorting a recovered vessel." A pause. "Requiring access to the vaults."

The script brightened. The door thought about it. Ashen had the distinct impression of being weighed — not his body but his *balance*, his outstanding debts, his years of service, the exact weight of his obedience measured against some invisible scale.

Then the door opened, and warm gold light spilled out over the cold stair, and Ashen stepped into the Treasury of Souls.

* * *

He had imagined, in the few unguarded moments he'd allowed himself to imagine it at all, a vault. Strongboxes. Chains. Something grim and martial, guarded by things with too many teeth.

It was nothing of the kind.

It was a cavern the size of a cathedral, and it was full of light. Sigil-stones hung from the ceiling in their thousands — no, tens of thousands — suspended in the dark air like a sky seen from inside the earth. Each one glowed with its own color and pulse. Some burned steady and white, old souls grown calm with long storage. Some flickered restlessly, amber and rose, still fighting after all this time. Some were barely embers, dim as coals at dawn, and those made Ashen's chest hurt in a way he had no vocabulary for.

They hung in rows. Of course they hung in rows. Everything here was in rows. But between the rows drifted motes of light, stray sparks that had come loose from their stones, wandering the cavern like fireflies that had forgotten which flower was home.

And the sound. No one had ever told him the treasury *sang*. It was not music exactly — it was more like the hum a hearth makes on a winter night, if the hearth were alive and homesick. Thousands of voices, wordless, blended into one low note that Ashen felt in his back teeth and behind his ribs and in the scar tissue where his name used to be.

"Oh," he said aloud, very quietly, to no one. "Oh, you're all still *in there*."

He had known this, in the abstract way a man knows the meat on his plate was once a creature in a field. He had carried stones to this place. He had watched them hung. He had never once listened.

Now he could not stop.

A stone near the entrance pulsed a stubborn, irritable orange, and when Ashen came close he heard — felt — a thread of song within the great hum that sounded like a woman arguing. Actually arguing. With the dark, with the stone, with the whole indifferent architecture of the Court. *I want to speak to whoever is in charge,* the thread insisted, in a tone Ashen recognized from every market day of his childhood. *This is not what I paid for.*

He laughed. It came out cracked and wet, and he wiped his face with the back of his hand like a boy, and kept walking.

* * *

There was a system. There was always a system; the Court was nothing *but* system. The stones were sorted by age of acquisition, then by quality, then by intended use. Ashen found the racks of recent tithes near the far wall, each tagged with a slip of vellum in the Auditor's immaculate hand. He found the section reserved for vessels — living carriers whose souls were stored elsewhere, in flesh, until collection — and noted with distant professional approval that the record-keeping really was impeccable.

He found the section marked, in the old tongue he now understood well enough to dread, *for the winter*.

It was the largest section. It stretched back into the dark farther than his lantern reached, row upon row upon row of stones, each one brighter than the last, each one tagged with a date. He read the dates and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one prayer the Court had left him, and the answer made him sit down abruptly on the cold floor.

They had been saving up. For generations. Tithe by tithe, soul by soul, a harvest laid in against a need — and the need, Ashen knew now, was the winter itself. Not to survive it. To *make* it. To stretch the long white months across the world forever, fueled by everything the Court had ever taken, and Wren's soul — the oldest, the brightest, the last heir of the people who had built this place — was meant to be the keystone.

All this light. All these thousands of stubborn, arguing, singing, grieving souls, hoarded like coins in a miser's cellar, waiting to be spent on an endless cold.

Ashen sat on the floor of the treasury and felt vertigo open beneath him like a second stair going down forever.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he had been walking around for forty years the way a man walks around a hole in his own floor. He had been a collector. He had been good at it. And goodness at a thing requires believing the thing is worth doing, and so he had believed — quietly, efficiently, without examining the belief — that the people he collected went *somewhere*. That they were kept. Preserved. Perhaps even comfortable, in the way of valuable property.

He had never let himself think that they were *waiting*. That they could hear the hum of each other through the stone. That a woman collected sixty years ago might still, at this very moment, be demanding to speak to whoever was in charge.

His mother had been collected. He knew that the way he knew his own hands — not as memory, because they had taken the memories, but as a fact printed somewhere beneath them. She had gone into a stone. She might be here. She might be one of these lights, one of these voices in the hum, and he would never know which, because they had taken her face from him at the gate only hours ago, and her voice with it, and her name before that.

He looked up at the tens of thousands of burning stones, any one of which might be her, all of which deserved better than maybe, and something in Ashen finished breaking and began, in the same motion, to mend into a different shape.

* * *

It was Ostara — dead these two days, gods keep her, gods forgive them for leaving her — who had said it, the night before the cabin burned. They'd been sitting by her stove, and she'd been mending a bear harness with one hand and her teeth, and Ashen had asked her, because the question had been gnawing him, how she could stand owing a life-debt to a smuggler.

Ostara had snorted. "Debt's just a story you tell about a thing you did," she'd said. "Only question worth asking is whether the story's still true. Hers is. Mine is. When it stops being true, you stop paying, and nobody's owed anything." She'd bitten off the thread and squinted at him. "You've got a story like that, tithe-man. I can see it hanging off you like a wet coat. One day you'll notice it don't fit anymore."

He noticed now.

He stood up. His knees protested. He ignored them, which was becoming a theme of his late middle age, and walked to the nearest rack of stones, and stood before it for a long moment, doing the last piece of arithmetic.

If he took one stone, the wards would know. If he took one stone, the Auditor would know, and the Auditor knew everything that happened in its ledgers, and Ashen was a line in those ledgers, had always been a line in those ledgers —

Except.

Except that Freya Reyes was downstairs right now, talking to the Auditor about *chain of custody*, and the Auditor was listening, because the Auditor was bound by its own rules, and its rules said a bargain fairly offered must be fairly heard. Its attention was *elsewhere*. Its attention was, for the first time in the history of the Court, occupied by a hedge-witch with a smuggler's tongue and nothing left to lose.

And Ashen — Ashen had credentials. Forty years of credentials. A sigil that opened every door, and a lifetime of being so reliably obedient that no one, in forty years, had ever once checked what he was carrying out.

The humor of it hit him sideways, there in the singing dark, and he laughed again — properly this time, a real laugh, rusty and astonished. Forty years of perfect service, and it turned out the reward for being trusted was that no one watched you. The Court had built its own blind spot and put him in it.

"Well," he said to the stones. "Right. Let's be thieves, then."

He got to work.

* * *

He could not take them all. He knew that with a clarity that felt like grief's colder cousin. The treasury held tens of thousands, and he had two hands and perhaps an hour, and a body that had already climbed one impossible stair today. So he did what a collector does best: he assessed, prioritized, and selected.

The newest first — the ones taken within living memory, whose families might still be above ground, still setting places at tables for people who never came home. He worked down the rack, pulling stones free, and the moment each one left its cradle the humming changed — a new thread joining the song, louder, closer, a voice suddenly in the room instead of behind glass. He wrapped them in his cloak, and his cloak began to glow through the wool like a basket of coals, and he decided that if anyone stopped him he would simply have to be the most terrifying smuggler the underworld had ever produced.

Then he paused, because a thought arrived, unwelcome and enormous.

The stones nearest the wall of *for the winter* were the oldest. And among the oldest, sorted with the Court's usual merciless precision, was a subsection he had walked past twice without letting himself look at: stones collected from collectors. The ones who had failed. The ones who had been remade. The ones whose names had been struck and whose souls had been filed.

His own was here. He knew it the way you know your own footsteps on a stair. Not by sight — they all looked alike, glowing, patient — but by the pull. Standing before that rack, Ashen felt the same tug behind his ribs that a compass feels toward north, steady and undeniable, and he understood that whatever was left of the boy he had been had been waiting in this rack for forty years, humming along with the rest, demanding to speak to whoever was in charge.

His hand shook. He was mildly disgusted to note that it shook.

"It's fine," he told it, and himself, and possibly the stone. "Worst case, we find out we were insufferable as a child."

He lifted it from its cradle.

The change was immediate and total. Warmth flooded up his arm, through his chest, into the hollow place behind his sternum where the cold had lived for four decades — and the hollow place *filled*. Not memories; those were gone, sold and spent. But something older than memory, something structural, the load-bearing wall of a self. He gasped. He sat down again, harder than he meant to, clutching the stone to his chest like a child with a stolen hen, and the treasury sang around him, and one thread of the song was suddenly, unmistakably, *his*.

When he could stand, he stood taller. When he spoke, testing, his voice came out lower and rougher and somehow more *occupied* than it had been in forty years.

"Right," said Ashen, who was not entirely Ashen anymore, and was glad of it. "Upstairs. Before the Auditor finishes its arithmetic."

He was three steps toward the stair when the whole mountain shuddered.

Dust sifted from the ceiling. The stones swung on their invisible threads, and the hum broke apart into ten thousand separate voices — surprise, alarm, and underneath it all, rising like water finding a crack, something that sounded almost like *language*. Old words, rolling through the cavern, spoken in a child's clear and furious voice.

Wren.

Somewhere above them, in the court proper, the last heir of the consumed had opened their mouth at last — and the mountain, the treasury, the tens of thousands of imprisoned lights, all of it leaned toward that voice like flowers toward a window.

Ashen clutched his bundle of stolen souls and started to run.
