## 16. Wren Speaks

The trouble with stealing from a court of accountants, Freya thought as the doors slammed shut behind them, is that they always know exactly what's missing.

She had Ashen by the sleeve and Wren by the hand and no plan worth the name, which was, if she were honest with herself, roughly her standard operating procedure. The treasury corridor stretched ahead of them, long and pale as a bone, lit by sigil-stones set into the walls like captive stars. Behind those doors, thousands of souls sat in their velvet-lined drawers, humming to themselves. Somewhere among them was the one they'd come for.

Somewhere behind them was everything else.

"They'll have sealed the exits," Ashen said. His voice had gone flat and collector-calm, the voice he used when fear got too big to hold any other way. "The Auditor will have counted us twice already. Once when we entered, once when we ran."

"Then we run a third time and confuse it."

"Freya."

"I'm joking," she said, which was only half true. Humor was a hedge-witch's tool same as iron or rowan, and right now she needed every tool on her belt.

Wren tugged her hand. The child pointed—not at the corridor ahead but *up*, at the ceiling, where the pale stone curved away into darkness. Freya followed the small finger and saw nothing, then saw what Wren meant: hairline seams in the rock, branching like frost on a window. Old cracks. Old wounds in the mountain.

"You're thinking we could climb out through the bones of the place," Freya murmured. "Clever. Horrifying. Clever."

Then the light changed.

It didn't dim so much as *tighten*—every sigil-stone in the walls flaring from soft gold to a hard white that made the shadows stand up straight and sharp. And down the length of the corridor came the grey robes.

There were six of them. No—seven. They moved without hurry, which was somehow worse than running. Tithe-collectors, though not collectors the way Ashen had been; these were the ones who'd never doubted, whose faces had gone smooth as river stones from centuries of not being asked anything difficult. In their hands they carried rods of black metal, and the ends of the rods hummed a note Freya felt in her back teeth.

"The vessel," said the foremost one. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It sounded like a clerk confirming an appointment. "And the two irregular entries. You are all owed."

"We're owed nothing," Ashen said, stepping forward, and Freya heard something in his voice she'd never heard there before: his own name, spoken inside himself, holding him upright. "I'm Ashen. I was a man before I was a rod. And I'm telling you to let us pass."

The smooth faces did not change. But the rods rose slightly, the way a dog's hackles rise.

"Ashen," repeated the foremost collector, tasting the word. "That designation was struck. It does not exist. Nothing here exists unless the ledger says so." It tilted its head, courteous as death. "You are standing in the one place in all the world where you cannot lie about what you are."

"Well," Freya said, "that's convenient, because I've been lying about what I am my whole life, and I'd love to finally get it right. Freya Reyes. Hedge-witch. Smuggler of charms, dabbler in things Maren told me not to dabble in, terrible at saying goodbye, worse at staying gone. Write that down."

The collector did not write it down. The rods hummed louder.

They were going to take Wren. Freya understood it suddenly and completely, the way you understand a falling thing has hit the ground. Not fight them—there were seven and the rods would do whatever rods like that did, unmake or unravel or file away—and not run, because the doors had already grown shut behind them like scar tissue. She pulled Wren behind her hip and felt the child's small fingers dig into her coat, and thought, absurdly, of the first night, the blizzard, the knock at the door, the frostbitten hand she'd taken without thinking because some hands you just take.

*I should have burned that letter,* she thought wildly. *I should have opened it. I should have—*

Wren stepped out from behind her.

"Wren—" Freya reached, missed. The child walked three small steps into the middle of the corridor, between the hedge-witch and the collectors, between the past and the accounting, and stopped, and looked up at the seven smooth faces without a flicker of fear.

Freya had seen Wren afraid. Frostbitten and lost in the snow, yes. Startled by Ostara's bears, once, briefly, before deciding they were large soft furniture. But this—this was different. This was a child looking at a thing it had already grieved and buried and dug up again, and finding it smaller than remembered.

The foremost collector extended its rod toward the child, gentle as offering a sweet. "Little vessel. You were sold fairly and carried long. Come and be weighed."

And Wren spoke.

Not the broken syllables of the first weeks, not the wordless sounds the child had made around Ostara's fire, not even the phrases Brother Callum had labored over. This was the old tongue whole and awake, rolling out of a body barely taller than a fence post, and the mountain *heard it.*

Freya felt it in her feet first—the stone under the corridor shivering like a struck bell. Then in her chest, where the words landed somewhere older than language, somewhere she hadn't known she had. Every sigil-stone in the walls flared and guttered and flared again, and in their stuttering light Freya saw the collectors' smooth faces do something they had plainly not done in centuries.

They hesitated.

"*Kaelen veth moru,*" Wren said, and the black rods in the collectors' hands cracked—one, then another, then all seven, splitting along their lengths like ice on a thawing pond. "*Kaelen veth moru, i thal en dorath.*"

"What did it say?" Freya whispered. "Ashen, what—"

"I don't know." His voice shook. "But the stones know. Look at the stones."

The sigil-stones lining the corridor had begun to pulse in rhythm with Wren's voice—no, *answering* it, call and response, like a congregation remembering a hymn. And in the flashes of light Freya saw something else, something that turned her blood to snowmelt: shapes moving in the stone itself. Faces. Hands. Figures pressed into the rock like flowers pressed between the pages of a book, and as Wren spoke they stirred, turning toward the sound the way sunflowers turn toward morning.

"Oh," Freya breathed. "Oh, Wren. Who are they?"

Wren looked back at her, and the child's eyes were wet and blazing at once, and when Wren answered, the old tongue softened into something almost like the common speech, enough for Freya to catch the shape of it:

*My people.*

The revelation didn't arrive all at once. It arrived the way dawn arrives—by degrees, each one making the last undeniable. Wren raised both small hands and pressed them flat against the corridor wall, against the pressed-in figures, and spoke again, and this time Freya understood without translation, the meaning arriving beneath the words like water under ice:

*You took them. You wrote them down until they forgot they had ever been anything but writing. You put them in drawers and called it order. But I am the last door, and I remember the house.*

The foremost collector took a step backward. A tithe-collector of the Sigil Court, stepping backward from a child of perhaps eight winters. Its smooth face had developed, for the first time, lines.

"That is not possible," it said. "The line was ended. The vessels were emptied. The ledger shows—"

"The ledger," said Wren—in the common tongue now, clear and cold and terribly young, "shows what you told it to show."

The stone *moved.*

It did not collapse; it obeyed. Ribs of rock rose from the floor and ceiling like the bars of a cage assembling itself around the seven collectors, and shadow poured into the gaps—not the absence of light but a presence, thick and old and gentle as soil, winding between the bars. The collectors' rods lay broken at their feet. One of them dropped to its knees. Another began, very quietly, to weep, and the sound of it was awful and human and Freya thought: *they were people once too. Everyone here was people once.*

"Don't hurt them," Wren said—to the stone, to the shadow, in the tone of a child who had learned gentleness somewhere no gentleness had been taught. "They forgot. That's punishment enough."

The bars held. The shadow settled like a sleeping animal. And the corridor went still except for the pulsing of the stones, which had found a slow, deep rhythm now, steady as a heartbeat, steady as breathing.

Freya became aware that her mouth was open. She closed it. Opened it again.

"Right," she managed. "So. When you said 'the debt comes due at first snow,' Brother Callum maybe should have asked *whose* debt."

Wren turned to her, and all the fire went out of the child's face at once, replaced by something small and exhausted and achingly young. Wren's knees buckled; Freya caught the child before the stone floor could, gathering her up the way she'd once gathered an armful of smuggled charms off a cart tipping over a ravine—instinctively, swearing under her breath, heart hammering.

"I've got you," she muttered into Wren's hair. "Whatever you are—last heir, rightful claimant, walking apocalypse—I've got you. That part hasn't changed since the blizzard and it isn't changing now."

Wren's arms tightened around her neck. And into her ear, in a whisper that was mostly common tongue with old tongue bleeding through the edges like dye through cloth, the child said:

"The treasury. Hurry. While they sleep. Before It wakes all the way."

"It?"

"The one who counts." Wren shivered against her. "It doesn't sleep. But it *pauses.* When the old tongue speaks, it must pause to check the figures." A pause, and then, with the ghost of Wren's old dry humor surfacing like a fish in winter water: "It hates checking the figures."

Despite everything—despite the caged collectors and the weeping and the pressed-in faces of a murdered people stirring in the walls—Freya laughed. It came out ragged, but it came out real.

"There's my girl," she said. Then, catching herself, glancing at Ashen: "Or boy. Or sovereign monarch of everything under this mountain, honestly, whoever you turn out to be, there's my—person. My Wren. Titles pending."

Ashen had not moved. He stood staring at the kneeling collectors, at the broken rods, at the wall where the pressed figures had stilled but not vanished—you could still see them if you knew how to look, a crowd waiting patiently behind frosted glass. His face was doing several things at once and none of them well.

"My own stone is in that treasury," he said quietly. "I saw it. Row nine, drawer four hundred and eleven. I've been walking past myself for three hundred years and calling it duty." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and free. "If your heir commands stone and shadow, then let's go commit the largest theft in the history of accounting."

"That's the spirit." Freya shifted Wren higher on her hip—the child was heavier than she looked, or perhaps Freya was simply more tired than she'd admitted—and started down the corridor at a pace just short of running. "What happens when the Auditor finishes checking its figures?"

Wren's answer came muffled against her shoulder, and it was delivered with the flat calm of a child stating weather:

"It comes to collect."

* * *

The treasury doors stood open still—Ashen's credentials had bought them that much—but beyond them the great vault had changed. The humming of thousands of sigil-stones had shifted key. Where before it had been a lullaby, a drone of contained and dreaming souls, now it was a chord building toward something, note upon note upon note, and Freya realized with a chill that the stones were singing along with the heartbeat rhythm of the corridor. With Wren's rhythm. Everything under the mountain was falling into time with the child on her hip.

Row after row of drawers stretched into the dark, and now, in the changed light, Freya could see them properly for the first time: not storage but a *graveyard arranged as a library*, every drawer a coffin, every stone a person folded small. And the stones nearest the aisle had begun to glow brighter as Wren passed, leaning toward the child like grass toward wind.

"Which row?" Freya asked Ashen.

"Nine." He was already moving, counting under his breath, a collector to the last even in treason. "Four hundred and—"

The lights went out.

All of them. Every stone, every seam of glow in the walls, snuffed in a single instant, and in the sudden absolute dark Freya heard a sound she would carry to her grave: the sound of ten thousand voices inhaling at once.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls and the floor and the space between her own ribs, a voice like a quill scratching across vellum said:

*"An entry has been altered."*

Wren's grip tightened on Freya's collar. In the dark, very close to her ear, the child whispered the old tongue—two words, urgent, and though Freya couldn't translate them she understood them anyway, the way you understand a hand pulling you toward a door:

*Run. Now.*
