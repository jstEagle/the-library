## 17. Ledger-Breaking

The Auditor's voice was the sound of arithmetic.

Freya had decided that somewhere in the last quarter hour, while it recited her debts in a tone like a clerk reading a grocery list: *one name, purchased 14th of Harvestmonth, nineteen years past; interest compounded; one soul, held in escrow, vessel designation Wren; one memory of maternal kindness, surrendered at the Gate; sundry trespasses—*

"Sundry," she muttered. "You've been keeping *sundry* accounts on me?"

The Auditor did not blink. It did not have eyes to blink, exactly—more a suggestion of eyes, the way a statue suggests a face, or a ledger suggests a life. It stood at the center of the Sigil Court's great hall, which glittered like a cave full of chandeliers having a quarrel, and its robes were the grey of old snow and older paper.

"Accuracy is courtesy," said the Auditor. "I am nothing if not courteous."

"You're nothing, full stop," Ashen said quietly beside her. He stood with his hands folded, collector-calm, though Freya could see the tremor in his fingers where they pressed together. Somewhere below them was a treasury full of stones, and one of those stones was his own stolen name, and every heartbeat he spent up here pretending to be their prisoner instead of their accomplice cost him something Freya couldn't measure.

She could measure other things, though. She'd been measuring things all her life—the weight of a charm-bag, the price of silence at a border ford, the exact number of steps between Maren's cottage and freedom (four hundred and eleven; she'd counted them out of spite). And right now she was measuring the distance between herself and the master ledger that sat upon the Auditor's dais, chained shut with silver, glowing faintly like a hearth seen through a keyhole.

Twelve paces. Thirteen if she took the scenic route.

The problem was that the scenic route went directly past six guards of the nameless court, who were beautiful in the hollow way of things that have been sanded smooth, and who were watching Wren.

Wren stood between Freya and Ashen, small and frost-scarred and silent as ever, looking up at the glittering vaulted dark with an expression Freya had never seen on the child's face before. Not fear. Recognition. Like someone coming home to a house where something terrible had happened, long ago, in a room nobody talked about.

*Don't look at it too long,* Freya thought desperately. *Look at me. I'm much better company. I have a plan. Well. I have a shape of a plan. Plans are mostly shapes until you commit.*

"—therefore," the Auditor was saying, in its voice like sums being done aloud, "the efficient resolution is as follows. The vessel returns to the treasury. Its soul extends the winter by ninety-one years, per schedule. The apprentice Reyes—" a pause, the sound of a page turning somewhere that had no pages— "is owed consideration for services rendered to this court's continuity, and will be compensated with restoration of her sold name, plus forgiveness of accrued interest, plus a modest cottage in the upper valleys, furnished."

"A cottage," Freya repeated.

"Furnished."

"With what? Furniture made of debt?"

"With comfort," said the Auditor, and here it did something new—it leaned forward, slightly, the way Maren used to lean over a kettle when she wanted you to smell what was cooking. And the hall changed around them.

It happened the way the Gate's memory-taking had happened: not violently but thoroughly, like ink spreading through water. The glittering cavern softened into lamplight. The cold became the particular chill of a mountain evening in early autumn. There was a fire. There was a kettle on the fire. There was bread, and honey, and a cat—an orange cat, fat and self-important, asleep on the warm stone by the hearth—and there was a chair by the window, and through the window Thornfell rolled away gold and green under a kind sky, and there was a woman standing at the counter with flour on her hands—

Freya's breath stopped.

The woman turned. Brown hair going grey at the temples. Laugh lines. Freya's own stubborn chin, Freya's own crooked smile.

Herself. Older. Settled. Safe.

"This is the life forgone," said the Auditor gently. "This is what your name would buy back, freely given to us along with the vessel. No more running. No more smuggler's roads. No more winters spent counting your charms to see if there'll be enough coin for coal. You were a child when it was taken from you. We return what was taken, plus damages. That is the law of this court. We are *bound* by the law of this court." A pause, almost tender. "All we ask is the child."

The orange cat yawned.

And Freya, standing in a lie so well-built it nearly had foundations, felt something crack in her chest—but not in the way the Auditor intended.

Because she looked at the woman with flour on her hands and she thought: *that woman looks happy.* Genuinely. Warmly. And then she thought: *but who taught her the roads? Who pulled Wren out of the blizzard? Who argued with Brother Callum about verb declensions over burnt soup until the old man laughed for the first time in a decade? Who held Ostara's one good hand and promised her the bears would be fed?*

That woman had a cottage. Freya had a family, scrappy and bleeding and half-mad, currently standing behind her in an enemy fortress because they'd chosen to be there. Ashen, who had burned his whole existence to stand at her side. Wren, who had no words in any living tongue but had found a hundred ways to say *stay* without them.

"I have a question," Freya said.

"Questions are free," said the Auditor. "Answers accrue interest."

"Naturally they do." She stepped forward—one pace toward the dais, twelve remaining, and the illusion flickered at the edges of her vision but held. "Here's my question. Court law. You're bound by it—you've said so twice now, very proudly, the way people say things they want believed. So tell me about trades."

"Trades?"

"Exchanges. One thing of value for another. Freely given." She kept walking. Ten paces. The beautiful guards watched her, faces smooth as river stones. "Because I've been doing business on the Thornfell roads for three years, and I know how contracts work. And I noticed something interesting about your offer just now. You offered me my name *plus damages*. Which means—" she smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, it was the smile she used on toll-keepers who tried to double their rates— "*you can afford to give more than you're asking for. Which means the thing you're asking for is worth less than the thing you're offering. Which means your accounting is wrong.*

"And I don't think," Freya said, eight paces now, voice rising, echoing off the glittering dark, "*that anything in this entire mountain is wronger than your accounting.*"

The hall went still.

Even the false fire seemed to hold its crackle. The Auditor straightened, and for the first time its suggested-face suggested something like attention.

"The vessel," it said slowly, "contains the last unclaimed soul of the Old People. Its value is incalculable. Ninety-one years of winter—"

"Incalculable," Freya agreed. "So let's calculate. Let's do it properly, out loud, in front of everyone. Because I read your little gate rules on the way in—they're carved above the arch, very proud lettering, somebody here likes their own handwriting—and rule three says *all obligations shall be settled by exchange of equivalent value, freely given, witnessed by the ledger.*"

Seven paces. Six. The guards hadn't moved. She didn't understand why the guards hadn't moved, unless—she glanced sideways—unless a certain disgraced tithe-collector had, in the last hour alone, done some quiet rearranging of credentials and orders, and she could have kissed him.

"So here's my offer," Freya said. "Freely given. Witnessed by your ledger. Take it or explain to your whole hollow court why the Auditor broke its own first law."

She stopped. Five paces from the dais. Close enough to see the master ledger clearly now: massive, ancient, its silver chain humming, its pages glowing with the soft light of ten thousand stolen names. Somewhere in that glow, in faded ink, was *Reyes, Freya*, nineteen years accruing.

Somewhere else in that glow was a sigil-stone with a child's soul inside it, warm as a coal, singing a song older than kingdoms.

"My name," said Freya Reyes, and her voice only shook a little. "Not the one you bought from Maren—that one's worn out anyway, it's got smuggler's dust all through it, you'd have to launder it. This one. The one I built myself. Three years of it. Every road, every deal, every friend. Freely given, no damages asked, no interest forgiven needed. Clean title. Full transfer. In exchange for the vessel's soul, returned whole, and the vessel walks out of here with us, and the debt of the Old People is settled forever. Closed account. Final entry."

Silence.

Then the Auditor laughed.

Freya hadn't known it could laugh. It was a horrible sound—like an abacus falling down stairs, like a thousand quills scratching at once—and it rang off the cavern walls until the glittering dark itself seemed to flinch.

"You would be *nothing*," said the Auditor, with something horribly close to delight. "No name, no claim, no memory-anchor beyond what you carried through the Gate—and you already gave a piece of that away. You would fade within a season. Perhaps within a month. The nameless do not survive long outside our walls, child. I have watched hundreds try."

"I know," Freya said. "I met one. His name was Ashen before it wasn't." She didn't look at him. Looking at him would break something she needed intact. "He lasted twenty years. He says it gets easier after the first decade."

"That is not *easier*, that is *less remaining*—"

"Mm. Bad accounting again." Four paces. Three. Her hand was close enough now that she could feel the ledger's warmth on her palm, like standing near an oven door. "But here's the part you haven't calculated, because you've been down here so long you think names are just entries. Rule three says *freely given*. Do you know what makes a gift valuable, Auditor? It isn't the object. It's that the giver chooses loss. The more it costs, the more it settles. Your own law says so—I read it twice, I told you, I'm thorough."

Two paces.

"A name I built myself, freely given, knowing exactly what it costs?" She laid her hand flat on the master ledger's cover, and the silver chain sang a low warning note, and every beautiful hollow face in the hall turned toward her. "By your own rules, that settles more than ninety-one winters. That might settle *everything*. All of it. Every name in this book. Are you sure you want to open negotiations?"

The Auditor regarded her for a long moment.

Freya had faced down bandits, tax men, a bear named Duchess, and Maren on a bad day, and none of it had prepared her for being regarded by a creature that was essentially a very large, very patient invoice.

"You love the vessel," the Auditor said at last. Not a question. An entry, being recorded.

"Obviously."

"That is inefficient."

"It's the most efficient thing I've ever done," Freya said. "Everything I am points at that kid. Consolidate the portfolio. One asset. Fully committed."

Another silence. Then, softly, from behind her, Wren spoke—not in words, never in words, but Freya felt the touch against her mind like cold clean water: *you don't have to.*

"Yeah, I do," Freya said out loud, because she was tired of secrets and the whole court was listening anyway. "Turns out somebody already sold a piece of me to this place. Fine. Then I decide what the rest of me is worth. That's the whole trick, Wren. Nobody gets to price you but you." She swallowed. "Even when the price is everything."

*Stupid,* said Wren, in the old tongue's wordless way, and the feeling attached was unmistakably affectionate. *Brave stupid.*

"Family trait. Probably."

The Auditor moved. Not toward her—toward the ledger, and its suggested-hands hovered over the silver chain, and its voice when it came was stripped of all its courtesy, down to bare arithmetic, and somehow that was worse.

"The terms are stated. The exchange is lawful. The value is—" a pause, a sound like a vast page turning, "—*sufficient.* The court is bound." And Freya heard it, buried under the flatness, the tiniest note of something that in any other creature she'd have called reluctance, and she understood suddenly and completely: the Auditor did not want to accept. But it had written its laws so tight, over so many centuries, that it had finally built a trap even it couldn't slip out of.

Good, Freya thought. Drown in your own paperwork.

"The chain requires assent," the Auditor said. "Spoken. Both parties. Speak yours, and the entry is made."

"Wait." Ashen's voice cracked across the hall. He stepped forward, and Freya saw his collector's composure had finally shattered entirely—he looked young, and terrified, and alive. "There's a clause. I know the clauses. Settlement of a soul-debt reverts *all* collateral registered against the debtor line. Check the registry. Check Maren's entry. If Freya's name goes into the book as payment, then every obligation touching that ledger-line closes. Including—" his voice steadied— "including mine. My collection was processed through the same registry. If her trade clears, my debt voids. That's not mercy. That's *law*. Look it up."

The Auditor looked it up. Freya actually saw it happen—a flicker across the suggested face, the sound of pages turning in a book that contained all books—and something in the hall's atmosphere shifted, the way air shifts before a storm breaks.

"...Confirmed," the Auditor said at last, and it sounded like a man swallowing glass. "Collateral reversion applies. The collector's obligation voids upon settlement." Its suggested-eyes fixed on Freya. "You planned this."

"I planned *most* of this," Freya admitted. "Ashen filled in the boring legal bits. We make a good team. It's disgusting."

"The terms," said the Auditor, "are accepted. Pending your spoken assent."

The hall waited. The false fire had gone out; the false cottage had dissolved; there was only the glittering dark and the ledger's warm glow and the sound, somewhere far below, of a treasury full of souls that had begun—very faintly, very softly—to sing.

Freya put both hands on the ledger. It was warm as a sleeping animal. Through its cover she could feel the hum of every stolen name, and underneath it, faint but insistent, a rhythm she recognized: Wren's soul, beating like a second heart.

*Last chance to run,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had run from Maren's cottage at seventeen with four hundred and eleven steps' worth of spite.

*Nah,* said the rest of her.

"I, Freya Reyes," she said, and her voice rang clear through the glittering dark, "give my name freely. Take the whole thing. Roads and scars and all. And give me back the child."

The chain unlocked with a sound like a bell forgiving someone.

The ledger opened beneath her palms by itself, pages turning, turning, turning, until they reached a page near the end where faded ink spelled *Reyes, Freya*—and beside it, fresh as blood, fresh as snow, new lines began writing themselves. She watched her own name flow onto the page letter by letter, and with each letter she felt something lift out of her: not pain, exactly, but *weight*, twenty-six years of it, pouring out of her chest and onto the page like water finding its level.

It should have hurt. Everyone always said it hurt.

Mostly it felt like setting down something she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

The letters finished. The ink dried. The ledger glowed.

And across the great hall, on a shelf of black stone that Freya had not dared look at directly since they arrived, a single sigil-stone flared bright as dawn.

Wren made a sound.

It was the first sound Freya had ever heard the child make—not a word, not yet, but a cry, raw and human and utterly ordinary, the sound any child makes when something lost comes home—and Wren tore across the hall toward the shelf, and the guards parted like water, and the stone rose from its resting place and drifted into the child's cupped hands and sank in through skin like light sinking into a window.

Wren lit up from the inside. Freya saw it happen: color flooding back into frost-pale cheeks, eyes going from grey to a deep warm brown, the whole small body straightening as though a string had finally been cut—or tied, she couldn't tell which, and didn't care.

Then Wren turned around, glowing faintly at the edges like a lantern, and looked at Freya.

And opened their mouth, and said—in the old tongue, in the tongue older than kingdoms, in a voice that made the cavern walls themselves sit up and listen—one word.

Freya couldn't translate it. But she didn't need to. Some words you understand the way you understand fire is hot.

It meant *mine*, and it meant *found*, and it meant, unmistakably, in the grammar of a language built before grief was invented:

*sister.*

"Oh," Freya said, and burst into tears, which was undignified, and she did not care even slightly. "Oh, you absolute menace, don't you dare make me cry in front of the—"

The mountain groaned.

Every light in the court guttered at once. The ledger slammed shut under her hands. Far below, in the treasury, thousands of sigil-stones had stopped singing and started *shouting*, and the deep roads rumbled with a sound Freya knew from the surface world, from avalanches, from the moment the snowpack decides it has had enough.

The Auditor staggered. Actually staggered, its grey robes rippling like disturbed water, its suggested-face fracturing into suggestions it had never shown before.

"What," it said, "is *happening*—"

Above them, the vaulted dark split with a crack like the sky breaking, and through the fissure poured a light that was not the court's cold glitter but something older, warmer, furious and joyful at once—and in that light, Freya heard voices. Thousands of them. Names, being remembered, all at once.

Beside her, Ashen went white as salt. "My stone," he whispered. "Freya—it's calling. They're all calling—"

The mountain woke, and the ledger in Freya's arms began, very softly, to burn.
