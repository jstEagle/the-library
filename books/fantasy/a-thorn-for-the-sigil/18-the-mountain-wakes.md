## 18. The Mountain Wakes

Ashen had spent forty years learning that the Sigil Court did not make mistakes. It made entries.

So when the treasury door burst open behind him and a sound came out of it—not a scream, not an explosion, but a *word*—his first thought, absurdly, was that somewhere in the deep ledgers, an error had finally been found, and the mountain was correcting it.

The word was in the old tongue. He did not know it. He had never known it. And yet he understood it the way you understand your own heartbeat: *return.*

The sigil-stones were singing.

He stood in the corridor outside the treasury with Wren's empty cradle of glass still warm under his arm—he had carried it three steps before the whole room began to shake—and watched light pour through the stone walls like water through cloth. The stones in their niches, thousands upon thousands of them, each one a soul folded small and filed away, had begun to glow. Not the cold, orderly glimmer they'd held for centuries. This was firelight glow. Hearth-glow. The glow of something remembering it was alive.

And with every stone that brightened, a name came loose.

He felt the first one hit him like weather. It wasn't his—it was a woman's, old and plain and stubborn, *Maret Doss, Maret Doss, Maret Doss*—and it went past him down the corridor to where a grey-robed guard stood frozen at her post. Ashen saw the guard stagger, saw her hand rise slowly to her own throat as if checking whether something was there that hadn't been there a moment ago. Her hood fell back. She was crying without any expression at all, the way people cry when they've forgotten how.

"Maret," she said. Just that. Testing it. Then again, louder, delighted and terrified: "*Maret.*"

The second name arrived before the first had finished echoing. Then a third. A fourth. They came up through the floor, out of the walls, humming in the mineral trees of the deep roads, rolling through the upward-flowing rivers like fish going home to spawn. The Court's perfect silence—the silence Ashen had lived inside for four decades, the silence that was supposed to be peace—was breaking apart into thousands of voices saying their own names over and over, the way you'd say them to a mirror after a long illness.

Wren's soul, free, had done what souls do. It had started talking.

* * *

Ashen ran.

Not away. He was done running away; he'd done his lifetime's ration of it at a ford in the rain, hesitating, and everything since had been interest accruing on that one cowardice. He ran *toward*—toward the treasury, toward the vault where he knew, with the terrible certainty of a man who has done the accounting himself, that his own name sat on a shelf between a shepherd boy from two hundred years ago and a midwife who had traded hers for her daughter's fever.

The corridors were chaos now, but it was the gentlest chaos he had ever witnessed. Grey robes everywhere, and every one of them stopped mid-stride, clutching at themselves like people waking from anesthesia. One sat down right in the middle of the hall, cross-legged, mouthing syllables. Another had torn off his sigil-badge and was staring at the blank spot on his chest beneath it as though expecting to find writing there. An elderly collector Ashen had known for thirty years—a creature so perfectly obedient that the others called him the Quill—stood at a junction pointing travelers this way and that, except now he was pointing them *outward*, toward the gates, and weeping, and saying "go, go, you're free, go" in a voice cracking with disuse.

"The Auditor will restore order," someone said near Ashen's shoulder, but the voice wavered even as it said it, because everyone could hear the flaw in the sentence now. Order was the Auditor. If order was dissolving, then—

The ceiling groaned. Somewhere far above, in the court proper, something vast turned over in its sleep.

* * *

The treasury doors stood open. Of course they did. Doors only ever closed here for things that belonged to the Court, and nothing in this room belonged to the Court anymore.

Inside, the singing was almost too much to bear. Every niche blazing, every stone a small sun, and threaded through all of it the names—thousands of names, layered and braided, a choir with no conductor. Ashen walked down the central aisle with his hands over his ears, which did nothing, because the names weren't coming through his ears. They were coming through the part of him the Court had hollowed out forty years ago, and that part was ringing like a struck bell.

He found his shelf.

He had known he would. He had catalogued this aisle himself, twice a century, inventory duty, the punishment detail for collectors who hesitated. He had run his fingers along these very niches and felt nothing, because feeling nothing was the price of staying functional. Row nine. Niche forty-one. Between the shepherd boy and the midwife.

His stone sat there, dull grey, the only dark thing in a corridor of light.

It was smaller than he'd imagined. That was the joke of it, the cruel little joke he would have laughed at if he remembered how: forty years of servitude, of taking children from doorsteps, of being the hand that reached out of the mountain—and what it had cost was this. A pebble. A grey pebble with a name folded up inside it so tight it couldn't even hum.

He lifted it out.

The moment his skin touched it, the name tried to come home, and Ashen learned what drowning felt like from the inside. It poured into him—all of it, all at once, no gentleness, no acclimation, forty years of self arriving in a single breath—and he went to his knees on the treasury floor with a grey pebble clenched in his fist and a name crashing through him like a river through a broken dam.

*Ashen.*

No. That was the collector-name, the made-up thing, the label the Court stitched on its tools.

The real one rose underneath it, older, smaller, warmer. A kitchen with a crooked door. A woman humming off-key. Rain on a roof in a village whose name he suddenly knew, whose fields he could smell, whose church bell he could hear though it had stopped ringing before the mountain took him. A boy who was afraid of geese. A boy who had a mother with flour on her hands—

The memory of her face was gone. He'd paid it at the Gate of Names, hours or a lifetime ago. But the *name*—the name held the shape around the hole, the way a burned house holds the shape of its rooms. He knelt there and said it aloud, once, quietly, and the treasury choir picked it up and carried it outward through the stone, adding it to the flood.

Somewhere above, the mountain shuddered again, harder.

* * *

He found Freya in the great hall, and he almost didn't recognize her, which was its own kind of horror—because he knew exactly what a person looked like when the Court had finished with their name, and she wore that look already. Faded at the edges. Slightly translucent, like a figure seen through ice. She was leaning on Wren, or Wren was leaning on her; it was hard to tell which of them was holding which upright. The master ledger lay open on the floor between them, its final entry still wet and glowing, and the Auditor—

The Auditor was *falling apart.*

That was the only word for it. The being of pure accounting stood at the head of the hall, and its edges were arithmetic failing. Columns of light that had composed its body were drifting out of alignment, numbers sliding loose like beads off a snapped string. Where its face should have been, figures churned faster and faster, recalculating, recalculating, unable to balance. Around it, the nameless of the court stood in a widening ring—not attacking it, none of them attacking it, just *watching*, just refusing, and the refusal was doing more damage than any blade.

"You cannot—" the Auditor said, and its voice was the sound of a ledger tearing. "The accounts must balance. The accounts have *always* balanced."

"They don't," Ashen said.

Every face turned to him. Freya's eyes found his and widened—at his voice, he realized, at the sound of a man speaking with his own throat instead of the Court's.

"I kept your books for forty years," he said, walking forward, holding up the grey pebble, which wasn't grey anymore; it had begun to glow the moment he'd spoken his true name aloud, and it lit his hand like a coal. "I know your ledgers better than you do. And I'm telling you they never balanced. You wrote down what you took. You never wrote down what it *cost*. Every soul in this mountain is a debt you owe, and debts, Auditor—" he smiled, and it felt wonderful, and terrible, like using a muscle long atrophied—"*come due.*"

The Auditor's form flared, contracted, flared again. All around the hall, the sigil-light was rising off the stones and streaming upward, out through vents and fissures, up through the mountain's bones toward the surface, toward the snow, toward the world. And with it went the winter. Ashen could feel it—the long winter, the engineered winter, the Court's great machine for keeping the passes sealed and the tithes flowing—stuttering, sputtering, losing power as the souls that fed it left it.

"The vessel," the Auditor rasped, fixing what remained of its attention on Wren. "The heir. The last claimant. Without a vessel, the old tongue is only—"

"Only words," Wren said.

Everyone froze. It was the first time Ashen had heard the child speak anything but the old tongue, and the common words came out rough and new-minted, a coin from a fresh die. Wren stepped forward, small and frost-scarred and utterly unafraid, and raised one hand, and the floor of the great hall answered—stone rippling like pond water, rising into shapes, letters, the sweeping curves of a script older than any kingdom.

"My people wrote those words," Wren said, in that rough new common speech, and then slid back into the old tongue for the rest, and the old tongue did not need translating, because by now the whole court understood it in their marrow: *My people built this ledger. My people are owed it. And the mountain remembers whose it is.*

The hall itself agreed. There was no other way to put it. The walls *agreed*, a deep structural groan of assent, and the Auditor's remaining columns of light bent like reeds in wind.

It looked at Freya then. At the faded, edgeless girl who had beaten it with its own law.

"Entry... closed," it said, and the words came out not defeated but *filed*, and somehow that was worse and better at once. Its form dimmed. "Balance... achieved. The account of Reyes, Freya... settled. The account of the heir... restored. Residual obligations..." The churning figures slowed. Slowed. Stopped. What remained of the Auditor stood very still, and when it spoke again its voice was almost small. "...Residual obligations: none."

Then it came apart—not violently, not in fire, but the way frost leaves a window when the sun finds it. Quietly. Completely. Into light.

* * *

Afterward there was shouting, and weeping, and a great deal of standing around looking lost, which Ashen recognized as the universal behavior of people who have just gotten back something enormous and don't know where to put it down.

The ex-collectors clustered in knots, trading names like children trading treasures. Maret Doss had found three sisters among the taken and was laughing and sobbing in equal measure. The Quill sat against a wall with a young collector's head on his shoulder, both of them asleep, truly asleep, perhaps for the first time in centuries. The upward rivers had reversed—of course they had—and somewhere in the deep roads the mineral forests were shedding their crystal leaves like trees finally allowed autumn.

Freya found him by the treasury door. Up close, her fading was worse—her fingertips had gone faintly transparent, and when she spoke, her voice seemed to come from slightly further away than her mouth.

"So that's what it sounds like," she said. "Having a name." She smiled, and it was a good smile, wry even at the end of the world. "Sounds loud."

"It is," Ashen said. "You get used to it." He hesitated. Everything he wanted to say next was enormous, and he had spent forty years saying nothing, and the muscles for this were weak. "Freya. When we go up. You can't remember why you'll be running. But I can remember for you. Both of us can. Wren can. Whatever the Court took as payment—you won't carry it alone."

She looked at him for a long moment, this disgraced tithe-taker who had once stood at a ford deciding whether to take a child, and whatever she meant to say, she got no chance to say it.

Because the mountain chose that moment to wake all the way.

The whole court lurched. Dust cascaded from the ceiling in golden sheets. Deep below them—deeper than the treasury, deeper than anything on Ashen's maps—something ancient shifted its weight, and the sound it made was not the old tongue and not the Auditor's arithmetic but a third thing, a vast slow voice like a glacier clearing its throat, and every sigil-stone left in the walls flared white at once.

Wren's head came up. The child's eyes had gone wide and shining, listening to something none of them could hear, and when Wren spoke, it was in the old tongue, urgent, urgent, urgent:

*"The roads are closing. The mountain is sealing its wounds. We have minutes, not hours—and the snow above has already begun to fall."*

Ashen grabbed the ledger off the floor, tucked it under one arm, and took Freya's fading hand in his solid one.

"Then we run," he said. "And this time, I know the way out."
