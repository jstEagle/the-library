## 19. Running Before the Winter Seals

She did not remember her name.

She remembered that this was supposed to be a problem. She remembered that names mattered, that people carried them like coins in a purse, and that somewhere—recently? long ago?—she had spent hers. The memory of the spending was gone too, which seemed unfair. If you must pay a price, you ought at least to remember the buying.

What she had was this: a child's hand in hers, small and warm and certain, and a tall grey figure walking ahead with a lantern of cold blue flame, and the absolute, bone-deep knowledge that she would rather come apart entirely than let go of either of them.

"Stay close," the tall one said. His voice was rough, like a door that hadn't been opened in years. He'd had a name once. She could see it bothering him, the way a splinter bothers a finger—something under the skin of him that he kept pressing at. "The roads are coming apart behind us. Don't look back."

She looked back.

It was hard not to. Behind them, the corridor they'd just crossed was folding in on itself like a hand closing. The walls down here were not stone, exactly, or not only stone—they had been grown, she thought, or remembered into being, and now that the thing that sustained them was dying, they were forgetting their own shapes. Whole galleries of mineral trees shed their crystal leaves in silent showers. Somewhere far off, a river that flowed uphill had stopped flowing and was simply hanging in the air, a silver rope of water slowly unraveling into mist.

"Freya—" the tall one started, then stopped. Frowned. "I almost had it. Your name. It was on the tip of my tongue."

She shook her head. She tried to say *it's gone, I gave it away, it's fine*—she was fairly sure it was fine, that this had been the plan, that the plan had worked—but the words came out strange and thin, and halfway through she lost the thread of what she'd been saying, like a path vanishing under snow.

The child squeezed her hand.

That was the thing. That was the one fixed point. Everything else slid—her own face when she caught it in the polished walls (a woman, dark-haired, tired, someone she felt she ought to apologize to); the reason they were running; the shape of the world above, whether it had sun in it or only the idea of sun. But the child's hand in hers was solid as an anchor-stone, and when the panic rose in her like floodwater, the child would squeeze, and the flood would find its banks again.

The child could not speak. Not in any way she understood. But the child hummed sometimes—a low, sweet, wordless note that the walls themselves seemed to lean toward, the way flowers lean at a window—and in the humming she could feel meaning the way you feel warmth through a closed door. *This way. Keep walking. I have you.*

"Here," the tall one said, and stopped so suddenly she walked into his back. "Bridge is gone."

* * *

The bridge had not fallen. That would have been simple. It had simply *stopped*, mid-span, its far end still hanging in the air across a chasm that had not been a chasm an hour ago but was one now, because the mountain was forgetting what lay beneath it.

The tall one—Ashen, something in her insisted, the word arriving like a fish surfacing—held the cold lantern out over the gap. The blue flame bent toward the far side, hungry.

"Air's still solid over there," he said. "The mountain's only forgotten the middle." He looked at the child. "Can you—"

The child was already walking forward.

"No—hey—" She grabbed for the small shoulder and the child turned and looked up at her, and she saw, with a clarity that cut through the fog in her head like a knife through whey, that the child was not afraid. The child looked at the gap the way she herself had once looked at a locked pantry: as a problem that had not yet met its match.

The child raised one hand, and hummed.

The note went out over the chasm, and the chasm *answered*. Stone groaned somewhere below—not collapsing, but *remembering*. And then, plank by plank, in reverse, the bridge began to rebuild itself, the mountain's memory flowing backward along the child's song like water finding an old channel. It took the shape it had once had. Not perfectly. Some of the planks came back crooked, some came back as something else entirely—a section of stair, a length of root, one piece that was unmistakably a door, lying flat—but it held.

The child looked back at her, and there was something in the small face that was almost smug.

"All right," she heard herself say, and her voice surprised her—it was warm, wry, someone who made jokes in bad places. Whoever she had been, she had been someone who made jokes in bad places. "Show-off. When we get out of here I'm putting you in lessons. Humility lessons. First one's free."

Ashen crossed first, testing each plank with his boot, and then it was her turn, and the chasm yawned below her full of dark and falling crystal leaves, and her legs went strange and far away, because she could not remember—she cast for it and cast for it and came up empty—*why any of this was worth it*. Why she was crossing. Why she didn't simply sit down on this last solid plank and let the mountain have her, quiet and easy, like sleep.

But her hand knew. Her hand was holding the child's hand, and her hand was not confused at all.

She crossed looking at that grip, one step and then another, and when her feet found the far side she laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound—and Ashen stared at her like she'd done something alarming.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," he said. "It's just—you laughed. Back in the court, you didn't. I watched you for three days down there and you never once—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "It's a good sound. That's all. Keep making it."

* * *

They climbed for hours, or what felt like hours. Time down here was another thing the mountain had stopped keeping track of. Ashen navigated by the cold lantern and by a habit of touching the walls every few paces, two fingers flat against the stone, the way a man checks a horse's neck.

"Still warm," he'd mutter. "Still warm. Good."

"Is it?" she asked once. "Warm being good, I mean. It's a mountain. I feel like mountains are supposed to be—" She gestured vaguely. The gesture felt familiar, and that was a small comfort: her hands remembered her even if she didn't.

"Warm means the old tongue's still moving through it," Ashen said. "Means the child's song is still holding the roads open ahead of us. Cold means—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Behind them, in the distances, they had all heard it: the long, grinding sigh of passages closing like throats.

They ate when Ashen made them eat—hard biscuits from his collector's pack, which tasted the way parchment smells. She couldn't remember whether she liked food. She ate anyway, because the child watched her eat with grave approval, and that was reason enough.

Once, in a wide gallery where the mineral trees grew thick as an orchard, she stopped and said, "I had a garden."

Ashen turned. "What?"

"I had—" She pressed her hands to her temples. It was like reaching into a river for a specific fish. "I had a garden. Or I wanted one. There was a door, a green door, and herbs in pots on the step, and someone told me I was—she told me—" The fish slipped the net. She was left holding nothing, and to her horror her eyes were burning. "There was someone. She was important. Was she important? Why can't I—"

The child took her face in two small hands.

The hum, this time, was different. Not a note but a chord, low and layered, and through it came pictures—not words, the child had no words to give, but pictures pressed gently against the inside of her mind like flowers between the pages of a book. A cottage. Smoke from a chimney. A woman's hands, older hands, kneading dough. A feeling like being scolded and loved in the same breath.

"She was real," the child's song said. "You gave her up so someone else could keep their own true thing. It was a fair trade. It was a brave trade. The missing is not a punishment. The missing is the shape of what you paid."

She didn't know how she understood all that from a hum. She understood it completely.

When the chord faded she was sitting on the ground with the child in her lap, and Ashen was standing with his back to them, very still, doing an extremely poor job of not listening.

"Your turn," she said, when she could speak.

"Hm?"

"Whatever it costs you. To be down here, near the treasury, near all those stones. Your—" she groped for it— "your piece. The one they kept. I can see you listening for it."

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he sat down across from her, folding his long grey frame like a folding chair, and looked into the cold lantern.

"It hums," he said. "Did you know that? All of them do. Every stone in that treasury, every soul they've ever taken—it's a chord, down there. Thousands of voices, all humming the same note. You get so you can't hear anything else. You forget there was ever another song." He turned his hand over, looked at his palm as if expecting to find a mark there. "When the child broke the ledger, the chord changed. For the first time in—gods, in as long as I can remember, which granted, isn't saying much—it changed. It went *wild*. Like a flock of birds all leaving one tree at once." A ghost of something moved across his face, too fast to name. "I'd like to hear what they sing now. When they're out. If they get out."

"They'll get out," she said. She found she believed it the way she believed in the child's hand: not with her mind, which was full of holes, but somewhere lower and more reliable. "You said it yourself. The mountain's forgetting. That's not the Auditor winning. That's the whole rotten ledger coming apart."

"The Auditor doesn't come apart," Ashen said quietly. "It *amends*."

* * *

The last road was the worst.

They felt the storm before they saw it—felt it as a pressure in the stone, a shudder running down through the mountain's bones like a shiver through a coat. Ashen stopped so abruptly she bumped into him again.

"What—"

"Snowmelt," he said. "The passes are sealing. The storm's pushing water into every crack in the upper rock, and the cracks are freezing, and the mountain—" The floor lurched. Somewhere above them, vast and muffled, came a sound like the world's largest door being slammed. Dust sifted from the ceiling in grey curtains. "The mountain is *shrugging*."

"Is there a way through?"

"There's always a way through. That's the first thing they teach you." His mouth twisted. "Second thing they teach you is that most people don't like the way through."

The way through was a chimney of stone, near-vertical, slick with meltwater, that climbed toward a distant grey coin of light. The child went up it like a squirrel, finding holds that should not have existed, humming all the while—and where the child hummed, the stone held, freezing itself firm under small fingers. Ashen went second, lantern clenched in his teeth, swearing around it in a language she suspected was older than any kingdom and ruder than most.

She went last, because they would not have it otherwise, and because she understood without being told: if she faltered, they would come back for her. Both of them. Every time. And she could not remember her own name, but she remembered *that*, with her whole body, and it was the most precious thing she owned.

Halfway up, the mountain shrugged again.

The crack she was holding *closed*.

Not crumbled—closed, the rock flowing together like wax, smooth and featureless where her fingers had been, and she dropped, and caught, and dropped again, and the world went white and roaring, and she was falling, and the only thought in her head, the thought that outlasted the falling, was not a thought at all but a grip: *small hand, warm, certain, do not let go, do not let go, do not—*

Something caught her.

Not stone. Not Ashen, though Ashen was there, stretched flat along a ledge with his arm down into the dark, his face grey with strain. It was the humming. The child had wedged herself into the chimney above and was singing *down* the shaft, and the song had filled it like water fills a cup, and the song was holding her—cradling her, actually, the way you'd carry something breakable—lowering her inch by inch until Ashen's hand closed around her wrist and hauled.

They lay on the ledge together, all three, gasping. Above them the grey coin of light had grown. She could see snow in it now—real snow, whirling, fat-flaked, the first snow of the winter coming down like the sky emptying its pockets.

"The pass," Ashen croaked. "If that seals before we're through—"

The child was already climbing.

* * *

The last hundred feet, she did not remember afterward, and never would, and came in time to be glad of it. There was climbing, and cold, and the child's song fraying at the edges with exhaustion, and Ashen's voice below her saying *almost, almost, almost* like a prayer he'd only just learned. And then her hands were on snow—real snow, cold and clean and *above*, and the wind hit her like a wall of needles, and she was out, she was out, they were all out, sprawled on the near side of the pass with the storm sealing the gap behind them like a wound closing.

She looked back once. Just once. The mountain stood against the churning sky, white-capped and silent, and if it had ever held a court of the nameless in its belly, it gave no sign. Snow filled the pass behind them in long white banners. By morning there would be no way back in. By morning, whatever was still down there would be down there for the winter, and the winter, Ashen had said once, was going to be long.

"Can you walk?" Ashen shouted over the wind.

She tested her legs. They answered, mostly. "I can walk," she shouted back, and then, because it was important, because it was the truest thing left in her: "I don't know my name. I don't know where we're going. I don't know why any of this—" She gestured at everything: the storm, the mountain, the whole staggering improbability of being alive. "But I know I'm not leaving these two. Whatever I am, I'm *theirs*. Write that down somewhere. That part's true even if nothing else is."

Ashen looked at her for a long moment through the snow. Then he did something she had never seen him do. He smiled—crooked, rusty, a smile like a door opening after years.

"I don't remember much either," he shouted. "But I remember a woman who burned a letter rather than read it, and crossed a bridge made of a door, and laughed in a collapsing orchard. Whoever that was—" he shouldered the cold lantern, which flickered now in the wind like a candle, almost warm— "I'd follow her anywhere. And I think she'd want to know: there's a light in the valley below. A cottage light. Somebody's home."

The child took her hand. The child's other hand took Ashen's sleeve. And together, three nameless things against the first snow, they started down toward the light.

The child was humming again as they walked—a new tune now, or a very old one, and it sounded, Freya thought, like a name being woven.
