## 20. What a Name Is Worth

The world above the mountain was almost painfully loud.

Freya had not remembered that about it—the noise of everything. Wind through bare branches had a voice. So did snow creaking underfoot, so did her own breath, so did Ashen grumbling as he broke trail ahead of them with a collector's staff repurposed into a walking stick, which he insisted was a practical decision and not a petty act of vandalism against a dead institution.

"Everything echoes down there," she'd said, when they first stumbled out into daylight and she'd flinched at the sound of her own boots.

"Everything *means* something down there," Ashen had corrected grimly. "Up here, most of what you hear is just weather."

She had loved him a little for that.

But three days out from the mountain's mouth, camped in the lee of a granite shoulder while the storm that would seal the passes for winter howled somewhere above them like a thing denied its dinner, Freya noticed the other problem.

She was fading.

Not dramatically—not the way Ostara's blood had soaked red and final into the snow at the pass, a cost paid all at once. This was quieter. She kept losing the middle of sentences. She'd reach for the word *flint* and find only the shape of it, the gray spark of it, without the name. She'd look at Wren sleeping against her side and feel an enormous, wordless tenderness—and beneath it, a small cold panic, because she could no longer have said, if pressed, *how long*. How long since the cottage. How long since the letter she burned. The years were going soft at the edges, like ink left out in rain.

A person without a name, she was learning, did not simply walk around being mysterious and free. A person without a name was a house with the door standing open in winter. Everything warm in you slowly walked out.

Wren woke at dusk and looked at her.

The child had changed on the deep roads—grown somehow, though not in height. There was a gravity to them now, a stillness like deep water. They had spoken whole storms of the old tongue in the collapsing halls beneath the mountain, commanded stone and shadow, woken thousands of stolen souls to themselves. And now, above ground, they had gone quiet again—but it was a different quiet. Not the silence of someone who has no words. The silence of someone choosing which ones to spend.

Wren looked at her now with those too-old eyes and said, in careful, broken common tongue: "You are *going*."

"I'm fine," Freya said automatically.

Wren's expression made it very clear what they thought of that.

* * *

Ashen built the fire while Wren arranged things.

That was the only word for it. The child moved around the campsite with terrible purpose, setting stones in a ring within the ring of their ordinary fire, placing three objects at the points between: a smooth river pebble that Ostara had once given Freya, back before any of this, with the instruction *"for luck, or for throwing at fools, whichever comes first."* A splinter of black stone from the treasury floor, which Ashen had carried out clenched in his fist like a man holding onto the edge of a cliff. And a small carved bird—crude, lopsided, unmistakably whittled by child hands—that Wren had made during the long climb, working on it in every rest stop with the ferocious concentration of someone building a weapon.

"What are we doing?" Ashen asked, lowering himself carefully onto his bedroll. His knee had never quite forgiven the stairs of the court. "If this is a ritual, I should warn you, I've seen several hundred rituals and most of them end with somebody owing money."

Wren ignored him. Wren had gotten very good at ignoring Ashen, in the way of children and cats.

They knelt at the center of the stone ring and put both hands flat on the frozen ground, and began to speak.

The old tongue didn't sound like language, exactly. It sounded like the mountain remembering itself. Each syllable landed with weight, and the air over the fire bent, and the snow around their little camp stopped falling—as if the whole night had leaned in to listen. Freya felt the words go through her chest like a plucked string finding its resonance in another string across the room.

And she understood, suddenly, what Wren was doing, and her heart cracked open with terror and love in equal measure.

"No," she said. "Wren. No—you don't have to—"

Wren kept speaking. But one small hand lifted off the ground, palm up, an open door. An invitation.

"They told me what you gave," Ashen said quietly, beside her. He wasn't looking at the ritual. He was looking at her, and his face held the particular gentleness of a man who had been nameless himself and knew the shape of that hunger. "At the ledger. Your name, freely given. The Auditor took it into the master book, and the master book is ash now—but the taking happened. Names don't come back from that, Freya. Not even Wren can dig yours out. What's sold and spent is spent."

"Then what—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "Then what is it doing?"

"I think," Ashen said slowly, watching the child work, "it's doing what nobody in that court ever thought to do. I think it's making you a new one."

* * *

Here is what Freya knew about names, after everything:

Her mother had given her *Reyes*, and Maren had taken it and signed it away in faded ink to pay for a cure, and the Sigil Court had held it in a treasury among thousands of glowing stones, and Freya had traded it back for a child's soul and called it a fair bargain and meant it, every hour since, even now, even fading.

But underneath all of that—underneath the ledger and the law and the accounting—there was a truth she had learned on smuggler's roads and in Ostara's smoky cabin and in the cold-fire dark while a defrocked tithe-taker confessed his childhood to strangers: a name was not a possession. A name was a story other people agreed to tell about you, and that you agreed to be.

Maren had named her *apprentice*, and she had run.

The road had named her *smuggler*, and she had worn it like a coat that mostly fit.

Ostara had called her *girl* and *fool* and, once, memorably, *the reason I'm not dead, so sit down and eat*, and each of those had been a kind of christening.

And Wren—mute Wren, who had taught her that the deepest things need no words—had named her, without ever saying it, *mine*. Family. The person whose hand you grab in the dark because you know, absolutely, it will be there.

The court had understood none of this. The court thought a name was a coin. That was why the court was rubble now.

Wren spoke on, and the words began to gather.

* * *

Later, Freya would try to describe what it felt like, and fail, and try again, and settle for this:

It felt like being introduced.

Like standing in a warm room full of people who already loved her, and hearing someone say, *this is—*, and the pause before the name stretching sweet and endless, and then the name arriving, and it fitting her the way a good coat fits: not perfectly, not tailored, but chosen, worn-in, hers by right of having lived in it.

The old tongue has no word for *name*, Ashen explained afterward, in the hushed voice of a scholar encountering a live miracle. It has a word that means roughly *the shape the world agrees you make*. And Wren was not reciting a name from anywhere. Wren was weaving one—out of the river pebble and the treasury shard and the crooked wooden bird, out of Ostara's laugh and Ashen's stubbornness and the specific way Freya said *hold on* when she meant *I've got you*—and offering it up to the listening night, to the fire, to the two witnesses sitting frozen with tears on their faces, asking the world: *do you agree?*

"Yes," Freya whispered, before she knew she was speaking. "Yes. I agree."

The words crested. Broke. Fell silent.

Snow started falling again, soft as forgiveness.

And Freya Reyes—who was no longer exactly Reyes, and had never been less than herself—opened her eyes.

* * *

She remembered everything.

That was the first thing, and it undid her. Not just the recent years—the deep ones. Maren's kitchen, the smell of bread and bitterroot. The plague winter, and Maren's shaking hands, and the stranger in grey who sat at the table counting coins that weren't coins. The letter she'd burned unopened, and the guilt she'd carried for three years like a stone in her boot. All of it, returned, but *lighter*—the way a wound hurts differently once it's been cleaned.

And threaded through the memories, new ones that were not memories at all but gifts: Ostara's bears huffing warm breath into the cold. Callum laughing until he wheezed over a mistranslation. Wren asleep against her side, breathing slow. Ashen burning his collector's robes rather than wear them home. A table in a cottage that didn't exist yet, crowded with strays.

"That's cheating," she said thickly, wiping her face. "You can't just—give someone a past."

Wren tilted their head. Then, deliberately, in their careful, accented common tongue: "It is not a past. It is a *promise*."

"Oh," said Freya, and cried harder.

Ashen cleared his throat with tremendous dignity, which was how he handled emotion, and poked the fire. "So. Are we doing introductions, or do I keep calling you 'the witch'?"

"You," Freya said, "can call me whatever you like, as long as you call me for supper."

He snorted. But later, when Wren had finally been bullied into sleep and the fire had gone down to embers, he said her new name—quietly, testing it, the way you test ice before trusting it with your weight—and it held.

* * *

They came down out of the high country over two more days, following the storm's tail south toward Thornfell's lower valleys, where the passes would stay open and the villages still had bread and gossip in equal supply. Word traveled faster than they did; by the time they reached the first inn, everyone had heard some version of what happened under the mountain, and every version was wrong in a different and increasingly flattering way.

"The way I heard it," said the innkeeper, a broad woman with flour to her elbows, "you fought the Auditor single-handed."

"There were three of us," Freya said.

"And a bear," added a drover at the corner table, with conviction.

"There was not a bear *present*," Ashen began.

"There was a bear *in spirit*," the drover said, and would not be moved, and honestly Freya decided the world could keep that version.

What struck her, though—what she turned over that night in a real bed for the first time in weeks, with Wren a warm comma against her back—was how people said her new name. Strangers, who had never stood in any stone circle, who owed the night no agreement. They said it easily, naturally, as if it had always been true, as if the world had merely been waiting for someone to write it down.

Because that was the secret, she realized. Wren hadn't forged the name alone. Every kindness Freya had ever done had been raw material. Every debt forgiven, every door opened, every fool spared the throwing-stone. The name had been accumulating in the world for years, waiting for someone with the right tongue to speak it into shape.

You could sell a name. The court had proven that. But you couldn't sell what people knew of you. That stayed. That compounded, like interest, in places no auditor could count.

She fell asleep smiling, and dreamed of a cottage with its door propped open.

* * *

On the last morning, at the crossroads where their roads would split—Ashen south toward the lowlands and a life he'd have to invent from scratch, Freya and Wren west toward Maren's cottage and whatever waited there—Wren tugged her sleeve and pointed at the signpost.

The old sign had rotted to illegibility years ago; everyone navigated the crossroads by memory and argument. But someone had recently nailed up a fresh board, white wood, new-carved letters. Probably the innkeeper. Probably as a joke, or a blessing, or both.

It read: **THORNFELL — ALL WHO ARE LOST, KEEP WALKING. YOU'RE CLOSER THAN YOU THINK.**

Wren studied it for a long moment. Then they looked up at Freya, and touched their own chest, and then hers, and said—in the old tongue, softly, the last time they would need it for her—one word.

*Home.*

Not a place. The shape the world agrees you make.

"Well," said Freya, when she could speak again. She shouldered her pack. Took Wren's hand, small and certain in hers. "Then let's not keep it waiting."

Behind them, Ashen laughed—a real laugh, rusty from disuse—and followed. Ahead, the road ran west through melting snow, and at the end of it a cottage stood empty with its chimney cold, and Freya found she was already furnishing it in her mind: a school, maybe. A table that seated more than it should. A door that locked from neither side.

They walked. The first thaw dripped from the branches like the mountain, far behind and far below, letting go of everything it had taken.

And at the crossroads, nailed to a signpost, a brand-new name caught the morning light—hers, chosen, owed to no one—pointing the way home.
