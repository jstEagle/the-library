## 21. First Snow, Last Debt

The pass had a name once, but nobody remembered it, so the people of Thornfell simply called it the Shoulder — the place where the mountain shrugged the sky down close enough to touch.

Freya climbed it on the first morning of true winter, her breath hanging in front of her like a small ghost that kept trying to say something and failing. Wren walked beside her, wrapped in three of Ostara's old sweaters despite Ostara being gone and the sweaters therefore belonging to no one, which was exactly the sort of thing Wren found funny. Ashen came behind them, carrying the shovel, because Ashen always carried the heavy thing without being asked. He'd been doing it for six days now, since the mountain let them go, as though he was still making up for every heavy thing he'd ever set down wrong.

They stopped at the cairn where the smuggler's trail split toward the low country.

"This is where she told me to run," Freya said. Her voice came out strange in all that white silence. "Right here. She said, *girl, you run, and don't you dare look back, I've got two bears and a bad temper and I intend to use both.*" She laughed, and then the laugh turned into something else, and she let it, because Wren's hand was already in hers, and Wren never minded what people did with their grief as long as they didn't hide it.

Ashen set down the shovel and began to dig. The ground fought him — first snow always does — but he worked steadily, the way he did everything, and after a while there was a hollow in the earth about the length of an arm.

Ostara's token was a bear claw on a leather cord, worn smooth from forty years of a thumb worrying over it. She'd pressed it into Freya's hand through the crack in the cabin door while the grey robes were still shouting below, and she'd said — this part Freya would keep forever, nameless or not — *"Take this up the mountain for me. I'd go myself but somebody's got to stay and be rude to these bastards."*

Freya had looked back. Of course she'd looked back. Everyone tells you not to look back, and everyone looks back, and that is how we know we're human. She'd seen the cabin door swing shut, and seen one enormous brown shoulder fill the gap in it like a doorstop made of muscle and fury, and then the snow had come down between them like a curtain drawn by someone who loved them both too much to watch.

They buried the claw at the top of the world, under a stone that Wren chose personally, inspecting four others before deciding this one had the right weight. Wren knelt and said something in the old tongue — short, low, the words falling into the snow like seeds.

"What was that?" Freya asked softly.

Wren considered. Then held up one finger: *wait.* A week ago that gesture would have been all they got. But the child had been trading lately — one word of theirs for one word of the old tongue, a lesson given for a lesson received — and so Wren tried, carefully, in the common tongue, shaping each syllable like something fragile carried across a long distance:

"She... sleeps *under* stone. Not *in* it. Under. Like... root."

"That's beautiful," Freya said, and meant it.

"It means," Wren added, with the grave precision of someone reciting their first complete sentence, "*good dirt.*"

Freya laughed until she cried, which was probably the correct way to do both things at once.

* * *

They built the cairn taller afterward, each of them adding stones. It took most of the morning because Ashen insisted on finding flat ones, and Freya teased him about it — "she wasn't fussy, Ashen, she slept in a barn full of bears" — and he replied, deadpan, "I am aware. I have been bitten." And Wren giggled, high and bright, a sound none of them had heard before the mountain and none of them would ever get tired of hearing after.

They ate lunch there. Hard bread, hard cheese, tea brewed over a little fire that Ashen lit with a snap of cold flame he no longer needed permission to make. That was another thing about Ashen since the collapse: he used his court-craft casually now, the way a man whistles at work, and every time he did it he seemed faintly surprised that nothing punished him for it.

"You could go back," Freya said, around a mouthful of bread. "What's left of it. The deep roads are open till spring. Someone should see what's down there. Count what can be counted. You're good at counting."

"I was," Ashen said. He looked out over the white valley, at the smoke rising thin and straight from Thornfell village far below. "Do you know what I remember, when I try to remember the court? Nothing. That's the point of a collector — you're a hand that carries things and feels nothing about any of it." He turned his palm up, studied it. "I have my name back now. Ashen. It turns out it isn't even my real one — it's the shape my mother gave me when she couldn't say my true name aloud, because names were dangerous where she came from." He smiled, small and crooked. "A false name that became a true one. That seems like your kind of magic, Freya Reyes."

"Freya Reyes doesn't exist anymore," Freya reminded him. "I checked. The ledger broke. I'm just—" she waved a hand vaguely at the mountains, the snow, herself— "a woman."

"A woman," Ashen agreed solemnly, "who owns a cottage, a goat, an alarming number of charms, and apparently a defrocked tithe-collector, if the defrocked tithe-collector has any say in it."

"Does he?"

"He's decided he does, yes."

Wren, who had been listening with the intense stillness children reserve for decisions being made over their heads, tugged Ashen's sleeve and pointed downhill, toward the village, then made the sign they'd all come to know: a roof, hands peaked together, then arms crossed over the chest. *Home. Stay.*

"Yes," Ashen said. "Stay."

Wren nodded once, satisfied, and went back to the important business of feeding the last crust to a bold mountain sparrow.

Freya looked away quickly, because her eyes were doing something inconvenient again. Six days. The man had been their enemy for months, had cornered them with iron and oath and the whole weight of the mountain behind him, and it had taken Wren looking at him without fear — just looking, the way only children and very old dogs can look — and it had taken Ostara bleeding into the snow so they could run, and it had taken the entire Sigil Court tearing itself apart around them, and somehow the sum total of all that was: Ashen stayed. Carrying the shovel. Finding the flat stones.

"Grief and gratitude," she said aloud, not really meaning to say anything. "That's what today is. I can't tell which one is heavier."

"They weigh the same," Ashen said. "That's the trick of them."

* * *

They were halfway down when the weather turned, because it was Thornfell and the weather always turned, usually out of spite. Snow came sideways off the ridge, and the trail vanished, and Freya — who knew every smuggler's road through these mountains better than anyone alive, which was now a slightly smaller claim than it used to be, given that several of those roads no longer existed — called a halt at a sheltered fold in the rock.

"We'll wait it out," she said. "An hour. Maybe two."

Wren sat down in the snow immediately, tipped head back, and opened their mouth to catch flakes, which was either a universal childhood instinct or something specific to children raised beneath a mountain, and Freya decided she didn't want to know which.

But the child's eyes were distant, focused on something past the falling white, and after a moment Wren raised one hand and drew shapes in the snow between them. Slow, deliberate strokes. Not letters of any alphabet Freya knew — older, rounder, more like footprints than writing.

"What's that?"

Wren pointed at the marks, then at the storm, then at the marks again, then made a sound like wind through a cave.

"The storm?" Freya guessed. "You wrote 'storm'?"

Wren shook their head, patient, then pointed at the marks again and tapped their own chest.

"You? These are yours?"

Another headshake. Then Wren pointed at the marks, swept an arm at the whole white mountainside, and spoke in the old tongue — one short phrase, and the snow around them shivered, actually shivered, flakes reversing mid-fall like startled birds before settling again.

Freya stared. "You named the mountain."

Wren nodded, pleased, and patted the snow-writing gently, the way you'd pat a horse.

"You named the mountain," Freya repeated, "with its actual name. Which is older than every kingdom. Older than the court. Older than—" She stopped. Laughed helplessly. "Wren, do you understand that half the scholars in the lowlands would trade their teeth for what you just did with your finger?"

Wren looked entirely unbothered by this, which, Freya reflected, was probably the healthiest possible response to being the last heir of a consumed people. Then the child took her hand, pressed it flat against the snow-writing, and — deliberately, generously — let her feel it: a hum, deep and slow, like a sleeping heartbeat under the stone, like something vast turning over in its sleep and murmuring *yes, that's mine, who's asking.*

And underneath the hum, faintly, almost shyly:

*Reyes.*

Not her name. Her master's name for her — the false one, the sold one, the one struck from every ledger. But the mountain remembered it anyway, the way mountains remember everything, and it offered it back to her like a dog returning a dropped mitten. Not hers. Just... familiar. Kept.

"Oh," Freya whispered, throat tight. "Thank you."

The mountain hummed, and Wren grinned, snowflakes melting on their cheeks, and Ashen — who had seen none of this and understood all of it, the way he increasingly did — quietly handed them both cups of tea.

* * *

They reached the village at dusk, blue-lit and chimney-smoked, and Brother Callum met them at the edge of it, trudging up through the drifts with his robes tucked into his boots and his breath going like a locomotive.

"There you are! I've been worried sick. There's a blizzard coming, in case you hadn't noticed, it's been coming since Tuesday, and I've had to entertain myself, which as you know is a danger to the entire community—" He stopped. Caught his breath. Looked at the three of them, snow-crusted, exhausted, alive, and his voice gentled all at once. "Well. You went up a mountain as three strangers and came down as a family. That's the oldest miracle there is. Come inside, all of you, I've got soup and it's terrible and there's plenty of it."

Inside Callum's drafty chapel-house, over soup that was indeed terrible and plentiful, Wren held court.

It happened without anyone planning it. Wren finished a bowl, looked at the empty spoon with scholarly dissatisfaction, and announced — in careful, halting, absolutely radiant common tongue: "More. Please. Thank-you."

Callum dropped his own spoon.

"Do that again," he breathed.

"More. Please." Wren paused, considering. "Thank-you."

"My dear child," Callum said, gripping the table, "do you know how long I have wanted to hear a living speaker of the old tongue conjugate a *polite request?* Every text I own is inventory lists and funeral rites. Do you know what I wouldn't give for a single sentence about soup?"

And that was how the exchange began, right there over the terrible soup, before Freya had even finished her second bowl. One word of the old tongue for one word of the common. Callum produced slate and chalk from somewhere — the man had slate and chalk in every pocket, like a squirrel with a theology degree — and soon the whole table was covered in two alphabets leaning against each other like drunks.

"*Vael,*" Wren said, drawing the round footprint-letters. "Means... snow. Also means..." Long pause. Consultation with some inner dictionary older than kingdoms. "...means *what falls when the sky remembers.*"

"Hm," said Callum. "In common we just say 'snow.'"

"Your language is lazy," Wren said serenely, and Callum laughed so hard he had to leave the table.

Later, while Callum snored in his chair and the fire burned low, Wren leaned against Freya's side, drowsy, chalk dust on their fingers.

"Teach me more tomorrow," the child murmured. "I teach you old tongue. Fair trade."

"Fair trade," Freya agreed.

"First lesson." Wren yawned enormously. "Old tongue word. *Serra.* Means..."

Freya waited. Outside, the storm the mountain had named rattled politely at the shutters, asking rather than demanding, as if the whole night had learned manners.

"*Means,*" Wren said at last, eyes closing, "*the people who carry you home.*"

Freya sat very still for a long time, one hand resting on a sleeping child's hair, in a warm room, in a valley full of smoke and lamplight, with a reformed tithe-collector asleep in the corner and a mad monk drooling over his slates.

She had no name. The ledgers of heaven and hell alike had lost her. By every law of the Sigil Court, she was nothing at all — an unaccounted entry, a blank space, a debt cancelled so thoroughly it had never existed.

And yet she had never once, in all her life, felt so completely known.

"Goodnight, Wren," she whispered. "Goodnight, serra."

Outside, the snow fell on the Shoulder, on the new-taller cairn, on a bear claw sleeping under good dirt, on the whole white listening dark of Thornfell — and far to the south, though none of them knew it yet, on a cottage with a cold hearth and a locked door and a ledger lying open on its table, waiting patiently for spring, and for whoever would come knocking.
