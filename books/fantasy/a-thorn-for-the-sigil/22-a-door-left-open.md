## 22. A Door Left Open

The first thing Freya did that spring was argue with a goat.

"You can't sleep in the herb bed," she told it, hands on her hips, in the tone she'd once used on smugglers who tried to shortchange her at the ford. "Those are feverfew starts. I've been coaxing them since the thaw."

The goat—named Turnip, because Wren had pointed at it one morning and made a sound like *turnip*, and everyone had agreed this was as good a name as any and better than most—chewed thoughtfully on a leaf of exactly the feverfew in question.

"That's not an argument," Freya said. "That's evidence."

From the doorway of the cottage, Ashen called out, "You're losing."

"I'm negotiating."

"You're losing to a goat." He came down the steps with a bucket in each hand, sleeves rolled to the elbow, looking—for all the world—like a man who had never worn grey robes in his life. The mountain had given him back a name in its dying: *Aldous*, he'd said when he found it written in his own reclaimed sigil-stone, turning the word over like a coin from a country he'd only heard of. But he still answered to Ashen more easily, the way a dog answers to the name it learned first, and so Ashen he remained, mostly.

He shooed Turnip out of the bed with the bucket, which the goat permitted as a matter of dignity rather than defeat, and stood beside Freya surveying the garden.

"It's strange," he said.

"What is?"

"Planting things. Knowing they'll come up." He turned a handful of dark soil over in his palm. "For thirty years I only ever took inventory. Nothing grew under the mountain. Nothing was *meant* to grow."

Freya bumped her shoulder against his arm. "Well. You're out from under it now. Best get used to things coming up whether you want them or not."

As if summoned by the remark, Wren's head appeared over the garden fence—a small, sharp-eyed apparition with dirt already on their chin and something clutched in one fist.

"Look," Wren said. It was still, after all these months, a small miracle every time they spoke in the common tongue; Freya doubted she would ever stop feeling the words land like warm stones in her chest. "Look what came up."

They opened their fist. In it lay a crocus, purple-white, the first of the year, roots and all—evidently harvested rather than discovered, if the disturbed patch by the fence was anything to go by.

"Wren," Freya began, "we don't pick the—"

But Wren pressed it into her hand, solemn as a tithe-collector presenting a writ, and said, in the old tongue, a word Freya had learned by now. *First-bloom.* An offering. A beginning.

"Well," she said, throat tight over a flower worth perhaps nothing and certainly everything. "Thank you. We'll put it in water."

Wren beamed and ran off toward the goat pen, where Turnip awaited with what could only be described as conspiratorial patience.

* * *

Maren's cottage had needed everything.

When they'd come down out of the mountains at the end of that terrible winter—Freya nameless and threadbare at her own edges, carried half the way between Wren and Ashen, who had argued the whole descent about whose turn it was to take her arm—the cottage had stood empty and slumped-shouldered under the snow, waiting for someone to claim it or let it fall.

Freya had stood in the doorway a long time that first day. She couldn't remember Maren's face anymore—that memory had gone through the Gate of Names and stayed there, ground into the court's ledgers along with her kindnesses, her lullabies, the smell of bread on a winter morning. What remained was stranger and harder: a shape of feeling without a picture attached. Grief with no portrait to hang it on. She knew she had loved her master. She knew her master had sold her. Both truths lived in her now like two birds in one small cage, occasionally squabbling, unable to be parted.

"She'd have hated this," Ashen had said behind her, reading her silence the way he read everything now, having spent decades learning to read people instead of ledgers. "The dust. The disorder."

"She'd have hated that I'm the one cleaning it up more," Freya had said, and found she could laugh about it, mostly, and that had been the moment she decided to stay.

It had taken the whole of spring's first month. They mended the roof with shakes Ostara's cousins brought down from the high valley—Ostara had survived, barely, dragged out of the pass by her bears with a torn shoulder and a grin she wore like a medal, and her family had descended on Thornfell that spring like a warm invasion, bringing meat, timber, opinions, and two more goats nobody had asked for. Callum arrived within the fortnight, having heard—through channels he refused to specify, though Freya suspected the defrocked monk still had friends in high and low places alike—that a school of forgotten languages was opening in a hedge-witch's cottage, and that its first student spoke a tongue older than any kingdom.

"A school," Ashen had said flatly, when Freya announced it over supper. "You're going to teach."

"I taught you the difference between a charm and a curse, didn't I?"

"You threatened me with both until I learned."

"There's your pedagogy."

Now, in the full green of late spring, the cottage had become something none of them quite had a word for. There were three students besides Wren: Tam, the miller's boy, who wanted charms against flour-dust lung and had a surprising gift for sigil-work; Bess, a widow from the ford village who had lost her husband to the long winter and wanted, in her own words, "to know what took him, and how to spit in its eye"; and little Sorrel, a stray of no particular origin whom Ostara had found sleeping in a bear's straw and delivered to the cottage with the breezy verdict of "yours now, I've got enough."

They came three mornings a week. Freya taught them knot-charms and weather-sense, the reading of smoke and the manners of bees, and—on the days when Wren consented, which were becoming more frequent—the old tongue, syllable by careful syllable, the language that had once commanded stone and shadow and had lately taken to commanding, mostly, the kettle.

Because that was the other thing. The old tongue had woken in the world when the mountain fell, and it did not sleep properly anymore. Words of it drifted up in odd places—in the babble of children, in the muttering of old women at wells, in dreams that left people waking with their mouths full of sounds they didn't know but understood. Callum wrote them all down, weeping freely and without embarrassment, filling book after book with a grammar resurrecting itself across the countryside like crocuses.

"The language isn't being remembered," he'd said once, ink to the elbows, eyes shining. "It's remembering *us*. Do you understand? It was never dead. It was only ever buried."

Freya understood. Some things went under the mountain and came back changed. She was living proof, nameless and new-made, stitched together out of choice instead of inheritance.

* * *

On a bright morning in the season's turn, with the students gone home and the cottage smelling of bread and drying yarrow, Freya sat at Maren's old worktable—her worktable now, though the wood still held grooves she didn't remember making—and practiced the hardest craft she knew.

She was writing her own name.

Not *Reyes, Freya*, faded ink in a dead woman's ledger. Not the name the Auditor had weighed and sold and struck. That one was gone, spent honestly, and she did not regret it; regret would have cheapened the trade, and Wren's soul sang in the world now, bright and unencumbered, and no ledger anywhere held a lien on either of them.

No—this was the other kind of naming. The kind Wren had worked on the mountainside in the first snow, weaving syllables of the old tongue around her like a cloak cut to fit. The kind that meant *the one who opens the door*, roughly, or *she who keeps the hearth for strays*, depending on how literally you translated a language built for a people who measured wealth in guests.

She wrote it out in chalk on slate, the way Wren had shown her, and studied it.

"It's crooked," said Wren, appearing at her elbow with the silent-footed suddenness that still startled visitors badly enough that Ostara's cousin Jem had once thrown a whole tureen of stew.

"It's expressive."

"It's crooked," Wren repeated, with the gentle ruthlessness of a good teacher, and took the chalk, and drew the strokes again—longer here, tighter there—until the name sat on the slate like it belonged there. Like it had always belonged somewhere, even when Freya hadn't.

"There," Wren said. Then, in the old tongue: *Say it.*

Freya said it. Her mouth knew the shape now. And the strange alchemy of it still worked every time: the name didn't describe her so much as *assemble* her—all the pieces she'd chosen, the roads she'd kept, the people she'd gathered—into one sound that fit her the way her own hand fits her glove.

Outside, Ashen was splitting wood with the methodical violence of a man making peace with labor, and Turnip was supervising from atop the woodpile, and the bread was nearly done, and the light came through the east window gold as honey.

Freya looked at all of it—at the whole improbable, patched-together, stubbornly alive fact of her life—and thought: *this is what a name is worth.* Not what it costs. What it holds.

Then someone knocked at the door.

* * *

It was a small knock. That was the first thing she noticed. Not the confident rap of a neighbor or the double-knock of the miller's boy arriving early. A small knock, hesitant, the kind made by someone who has knocked on many doors and been turned away from most of them.

The second thing she noticed was the cold.

It came through the door seams like a draft, except it wasn't spring outside—it was high spring, warm and green, the air thick with blossom-smell. This cold was older than the season. It had a texture Freya knew down in her bones, in the part of her that had walked the deep roads and seen rivers run upward. A cold with *accounting* in it.

Ashen stopped splitting wood. He'd felt it too; she saw it in the set of his shoulders, the way his hand drifted—not to a weapon, he owned none now, but to the axe, which was close enough. Across the yard, Wren went very still by the goat pen, head cocked, listening to something beneath the world.

Freya crossed the room. Her heart was loud. Her hand, when she raised it to the latch, was steady—three years of smuggler's roads and one winter under a mountain had settled that in her forever.

Through the window she could see the doorstep.

A girl stood there. Young—younger than Freya had been when she ran, maybe twelve years at most—thin as a winter branch, dressed in clothes too fine for the road and ruined past saving, mud to the knees, frost in her hair despite the warm day. Behind her, up the lane, the shadows lay wrong: pooled too deep, arranged too neatly, like ledgers waiting to be filled. Something was following her. Something patient. Something that believed, with the whole weight of its ancient arithmetic, that the girl belonged to it.

The girl looked up and met Freya's eyes through the glass, and Freya saw in her face the exact expression she must have worn once, three years ago, standing outside a stranger's door in a blizzard with nowhere left in the world to go.

The knock came again. Small. Stubborn. Asking anyway.

Behind her, Wren had come inside without a sound and stood at Freya's shoulder, and Ashen filled the doorway to the kitchen with the axe still in his hand and the old collector's knowledge waking grim in his eyes, and Callum was reaching for his books, and somewhere Turnip was bleating encouragement to absolutely no effect.

Freya looked at the frost in the child's hair. At the wrong shadows gathering up the lane like a debt come due. At the door—Maren's door, her door, the door she had burned a letter rather than answer, once, in another life, and had spent ever since learning what doors are *for*.

She thought: *they will always come. The old debts, the grey robes, the things that count souls like coin. There will always be another knock.*

She thought: *let there always be someone to open it.*

She unlatched the door and pulled it wide, and the warm bread-smell of the cottage rolled out to meet the cold, and Freya Reyes—who had no name but the one her family gave her, and no home but the one she'd built, and no intention whatsoever of turning away whatever came next—smiled at the trembling child on her step and said:

"You're just in time for supper. Come in, love. Tell me who's chasing you—and don't spare the details. We've dealt with worse."

And far up the lane, the shadows paused—recounted, as it were—and found, to their evident astonishment, that the door before them was not a door at all.

It was a wall. With a hearth behind it. And everyone she loved on this side.
