## 1. The Valley That Swallows Magic

The valley ate magic the way the sea eats fire. I'd learned that in my first winter here, when I tried to light a candle with a whispered word and got nothing but a dead wick and my own breath fogging the dark. Twelve years since, and I still caught myself reaching for it — the old warmth behind the sternum, the brand on my forearm humming like a struck bell. Silence now. It was always silence.

That was the point. That was why I'd come.

I was mending a fishing net on my porch when the child came out of the treeline, and I knew something had followed her before I saw what, because the pines at the valley's mouth had gone wrong. Birds lifting all at once, no sound of wings. The particular quality of quiet that means men are being careful.

She couldn't have been more than eight. Barefoot, bleeding from one ear, moving with the stiff, deliberate gait of someone who has decided not to fall down. She stopped ten paces from my porch and looked at me, and her eyes did the thing that eyes do when they've seen too much too fast: nothing at all. Flat as coins.

Then she spoke.

I want to say I understood her, but that would be a lie I don't owe anyone. What came out of her mouth was language — real language, structured, urgent — but it sounded like it had been carved rather than spoken. Consonants like chisel-blows. Vowels that went somewhere under my skin and stayed there, itching. The brand on my arm woke for the first time in twelve years. Not warmth. A single pulse, deep, like something enormous turning over in its sleep one valley away and one world down.

"Slowly," I said, in common tongue. "Again."

She said it again. And this time, though I understood no word of it, I understood *that* — because my god's mark burned once more, in rhythm, like an answer. Or a warning.

Behind her, the treeline broke.

* * *

There were four riders. They came down the switchback trail at the valley's mouth at a canter, unhurried, which told me they thought this was already over. They wore gray without livery, but I knew the sigil anyway — every mercenary who ever took the coin knew it, even the ones who'd never seen it worn openly. A circle broken by a vertical line. The Mark of the Hushed One. My god's own seal, stitched on the shoulder of strangers.

My hand found the axe leaning against the porch rail before I made any decision about it. Some parts of me were still faster than the rest.

The riders stopped where the child stood. She didn't run to them. That was the first thing I noticed. The second thing was that the lead rider raised his hand — two fingers, a gesture I knew intimately, the opening motion of a binding-cant — and nothing happened.

He did it again. Fingers trembling now. Again.

The valley had swallowed him whole.

I watched four armed men discover, in the space of three heartbeats, that they were only men. It is a specific kind of fear, watching it happen. Their horses felt it first and sidled. The leader's hand dropped to steel instead of sorcery, and that, more than anything, told me what they were: church-trained, ward-sworn, and lost without their gifts the way a rich man is lost without servants.

"You're inside the boundary," I called down, in the flat voice I used for contracts. "Nothing you brought works here. Nothing but what you were born with."

The leader looked up at me. He was younger than his voice. "Woman. That child carries stolen property of the Church of the Hushed Word. Surrender her and walk away."

Stolen property. I looked at the girl. She was swaying on her feet now, blood crusted black at her jaw. Stolen property with eyes flat as coins.

"This is my valley," I said. "Not the Church's. Not anyone's."

"It's no one's," he said, and there was something almost gentle in it, the gentleness of a man explaining weather. "It's a wound. And wounds get closed."

They dismounted. All four. That was a mistake born of arrogance — or maybe they simply didn't believe the stories, the ones every hired blade hears around fires and half-believes: that there's a valley in the far north where the gods go mute and the spells die and the only law is whatever walked in first.

I'd been walking in first for twelve years.

The first one died on the stairs of my porch, surprised, because I hadn't postured or threatened or given him the courtesy of a duel. I'd been a mercenary for nine years before the silence took me in, and the first lesson that trade teaches is that mercy is a budget item. The second man got his sword clear. It didn't matter. The third ran for his horse, and I let the axe answer that question too. The last one — the leader, the young one — I left alive long enough to talk, kneeling in the mud with his hand pressed to his side, looking at me with the expression of a man revising his theology.

"The Church knows where she is," he gasped. "We're the fourth company sent. There will be others. There are always others."

"Then they'll die the same way."

"They won't cross the border next time," he said, and smiled through the blood, and that smile has lived in my head since. "They'll wait. Nothing crosses out of your wound, woman. Not her. Not you. Your valley isn't a sanctuary." His eyes flicked to the girl, and there was reverence in it, foul and hungry. "It's a cage. Ask yourself who built it. Ask yourself whose mark you wear."

I killed him then. Not because he lied. Because I believed him.

* * *

I buried them behind the smokehouse, past the rowan trees, in ground so used to receiving that the spade went easy. It was evening work. The child sat on my porch steps the whole time, watching me with those coin-flat eyes, and when I finally climbed back up and washed my hands at the rain barrel, she held out her cupped palms.

In them lay a sliver of something that wasn't quite bone and wasn't quite glass. When my shadow crossed it, the brand on my arm sang one low note — the first sound my god had made in twelve years — and the sliver glowed faintly, briefly, like a coal remembering fire.

First Tongue, written small. I didn't know how I knew that. I knew it the way I knew the valley ate magic: completely, and without evidence, and with the certainty of a scar.

"What are you?" I asked her.

She looked at me for a long time. Then she touched my forearm, right over the brand, and said one word in that chiseled, ancient speech — and this time, whether it was the touch or the mark or some door my god had finally deigned to crack open, I heard the meaning land inside my skull like a stone dropped down a well:

*Mother-of-the-seal.*

"I'm nobody's mother," I said.

But I built up the fire anyway. I put broth in front of her and watched her eat like a small animal, fast and sideways, ready to bolt. I spread a blanket by the hearth because the nights were turning cold and because twelve years of numbness apparently had a floor, and tonight I'd found it.

Outside, the wind moved down from the pass where four men had ridden in and wouldn't ride out. Somewhere beyond the valley's rim, wherever the world still worked, someone was counting companies. Fourth sent. Fifth coming.

And my silent god — my vanished, voiceless, sworn-to lord — had hummed one note at the sight of this child, and I could not stop hearing the shape of that note. It had not sounded like joy.

It had sounded like guilt.

I banked the fire, checked the axe, and sat awake in the dark with a stranger sleeping by my hearth, listening to the valley be silent, wondering — for the first time since I'd crawled into it bleeding and grateful — what exactly I had been hiding from all these years.

Or what had been hiding from me.
