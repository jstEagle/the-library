## 2. What the Silent God Swore

I buried the riders in the morning, when the frost was still hard enough to keep the earth honest.

Three graves behind the cabin, past the goat pen, where the soil turned to clay. I dug them myself because there was no one else to dig them, and because the work was good for a body — shovel, breath, ache, repeat. The kind of arithmetic that kept a mind from wandering into places it shouldn't go.

The men had worn the sigil of my god on their tabards: the closed eye inside a ring of thorns. The same mark that sat branded on my left forearm under two layers of sleeve and eleven years of scar tissue.

They had come for the child. They had crossed the valley's border expecting their blessings to carry them through, and instead their magic had died at the treeline like a snuffed candle, and they had become what every man is underneath his miracles: meat and fear and a sword arm. I'd killed them anyway. That was the part I kept turning over while I shoveled. Not the killing — I could justify killing in my sleep, and often did — but the *anyway*. They were helpless. Helpless men don't usually make it to my door twice.

The child had watched from the doorway the whole time, wrapped in my spare blanket, saying nothing. Then she'd said plenty, in that language like water running over old stones, and I hadn't understood a word of it, and she'd looked at me with an expression I recognized from the mirror: *you were supposed to be different.*

* * *

The memories came to me the way fever dreams come — uninvited, wet, burning.

She slept in front of my hearth that first week, and when she slept, she leaked. Not magic; the valley swallowed magic whole. This was something older. Images, pushed across whatever thin membrane separates one sleeping mind from another. I'd wake with the taste of ash in my teeth and a picture behind my eyes that didn't belong to me:

*A hall of black stone lit by a thousand hanging flames, and wings folding against the rafters like the closing of a cathedral.*

*A hand — not a human hand, too many joints, scaled like a riverbed — pressing a seal of white fire into the chest of a kneeling figure, and the figure screaming without sound.*

*A voice, vast and tired, speaking in the water-over-stones tongue, and beneath it a second voice answering in the same tongue, and the second voice I knew. I knew it the way you know a scar. It was the voice that had gone silent in my head eleven years ago, mid-sentence, mid-prayer, mid-*something*, leaving me alone in a valley full of quiet.*

I woke from that last one with my brand burning cold and Mira sitting up in her blankets, watching me with those gray eyes.

"You saw," I said, uselessly, in trade-tongue.

"Veth-ka," she said, and pointed at my arm. My brand. Then she pointed at herself, and said a longer phrase, and though I couldn't parse the words, the shape of it was unmistakable. A name. Her name, or her office. Something she carried the way I carried my mark.

"I don't understand you," I told her.

She frowned — genuinely offended, as if comprehension were simply a matter of effort — then got up, went to my table, and swept the crumbs aside with her sleeve. She dragged her finger through the dust and drew.

A circle. Inside it, a smaller circle. From the outer ring, lines radiating outward like spokes, and at each spoke-end, a shape: wing, flame, crown, thorn.

Then she pressed her finger hard into the center of the inner circle until the dust showed wood, and looked up at me.

"Thorn," she said, in my language, the single word she seemed willing to spend. "Spire." Two more words. Three coins from a hoard.

Then she drew a line from the outer circle inward, crossing it, breaking it, and made a sound low in her throat that no child should know how to make. Grief, older than her body.

* * *

It took me nine days to translate enough of the First Tongue to hate my god properly.

Not that I learned it cleanly. There are no books in the valley; my god's church never wasted scripture on mercenaries, and I'd burned most of my own kit for warmth the second winter. What I had was a child, a stick, a floor of packed dirt, and long nights. She drew. I guessed. When I guessed wrong she corrected me with the flat patience of a schoolmarm three hundred years old wearing a body of maybe eight years.

Piece by piece, the picture assembled itself, and I want to say I fought it, but I didn't. I'd been assembling it for eleven years without knowing. Every unanswered prayer. Every winter the valley felt less like sanctuary and more like a held breath.

Here is what the drawings said:

There was a covenant. Between the realms — hers and mine, the fire-blooded and the flesh-bound — sealed long ago in blood and sworn at the Spire. Five seals held it, five living anchors, each one a person marked in white fire, each one bound to a god who spoke through them and for them. The covenant kept dragon-fire from scouring the world of men, and kept the hunger of men from devouring the world of dragons. Balance. Boredom, even. Peace so old it had forgotten its own name.

And four of the five seals were broken.

Mira drew them breaking. One by one. And each time she drew a seal shattering, she drew the same figure standing over the pieces — not a dragon, not a man, but something between, robed, crowned with thorns, its face a smooth blank oval with no eye drawn in it.

A closed eye inside a ring of thorns.

"No," I said out loud, to a dirt floor, like a fool.

She looked at me with terrible gentleness. Then she drew the fifth seal: a small circle, a small figure, alive, whole. Herself. And around it, a ring of mountains, and within the mountains, a valley.

This valley.

I sat down on the bench I'd built six years ago and did not move for a long time.

The valley wasn't refuge. I had known that much already — you learn fast that a place where your prayers die in your throat isn't anyone's idea of mercy. But I'd told myself it was a quarantine, a wound the world was healing around, a place my god had set aside for the broken ones like me. His faithful, shelved. Safe.

It wasn't a shelf. It was a hiding hole. He had built it — carved it out of the world with whatever remained of his power after he did what he did — and hidden the last living seal of a dying covenant in it, along with a garrison of hollowed-out worshippers too numb to notice we were guarding a prisoner.

Me. Eleven years a jailer who thought she was a guest.

And his silence — his silence began the day he broke it. I understood that now with the ugly clarity of things that cannot be unknown. He hadn't lost his voice. He'd spent it. Broken the covenant, shattered four seals, and gone quiet in shame or strategy or simple exhaustion, leaving his branded faithful scattered in a valley he'd nailed shut, holding a child he'd never told us about.

Loyalty curdles slowly, then all at once. Mine turned somewhere between the drawing of the fourth broken seal and the moment Mira took my branded arm, turned it over in both her small hands, and said — translating for me, coin by precious coin — *"He swore. Then he broke. You carry his oath. Oaths must be paid."*

* * *

On the eleventh day, I climbed to the ridge above the cabin, where the valley wall rises in grey shelves of stone, and I looked north.

They were gathering.

Fires, dozens of them, strung along the far side of the border like a necklace of orange beads. Tents. Banners too distant to read, but I didn't need to read them. Riders wore the closed eye; these would be riders' kin, and kin bring numbers, and numbers bring priests, and priests bring the kind of magic that doesn't need to cross the border to be dangerous. Ropes. Hooks. Siegecraft. Patience.

Word had gotten out that the riders had failed. Word would also get out, eventually, about what the valley did to power — and someone clever among them would realize that if magic dies inside the line, then anything kept inside the line can be starved out of it. All they had to do was wait for winter and let the valley do its quiet work on our stores.

Or worse: someone clever would realize that the child was a seal, and seals can be moved. Dragged across the border, where her power would return the moment she crossed — a lantern relighting in a dark room, visible to every hungry thing for a hundred miles.

Behind me, gravel shifted. Mira had followed me up, blanket around her shoulders like a mantle. She stood beside me and looked at the fires without fear, which frightened me more than the fires did.

"They will not stop," she said. Four words of trade-tongue, purchased dearly. Then, in the First Tongue, a longer phrase, and this time — whether from nine days of immersion or because she wanted me to understand more than grammar allowed — I caught the bones of it. Enough.

*The Spire. Before the last flame gutters out. Or everything ends.*

I flexed my branded arm. Under the scar tissue, the closed eye felt warm for the first time in eleven years — not my god's presence, nothing so kind. Just the oath itself, waking up, remembering what it was for.

I had sworn loyalty to a silent god. I was starting to suspect he'd never deserved it. And now his enemies were camped on his doorstep, and his victim was standing next to me asking me to walk her into the teeth of the world.

"We leave tomorrow," I said.

Mira nodded once, like a queen confirming a treaty.

Then she looked back at the fires, and added, in careful trade-tongue: "Bring the shovel."
