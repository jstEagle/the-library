## 4. A Thorn for the Spire

The churchmen came at dawn, when the Spire's light was thinnest.

Ada heard them on the stair before she saw them — boots on stone, unhurried, the tread of men who believed the outcome was already decided. She stood between them and the chamber where Mira slept, and behind that chamber, past walls veined with dying fire, the dragon Vaelithras coiled around the covenant's broken heart like a hand cupping a guttering flame.

There were six of them. The lead one wore white, which in her experience meant either purity or money, and it was never purity.

"Mercenary Nakamura." He said her name like a receipt. "You've been offered terms. Kill the child. Your god's voice returns to you. Your contract is fulfilled and your soul is unencumbered. There is no version of this where you walk away whole."

"You're right," Ada said. "I've never once walked away whole."

She rolled up her sleeve.

The brand sat on her forearm, old scar tissue shaped like a thorn wrapped around a circle — the mark every sworn soldier of the Silent God carried, burned into them at induction by a priest who no longer had a god to channel through his hands. Twenty years she'd carried it. Twenty years it had been nothing but dead skin, proof of service to a voice that stopped answering before her first kill.

"The church says this mark is his," she said. "His signature on our flesh. His claim."

"It is."

"Then he can spend it."

* * *

Mira woke while Ada was sharpening her knife, because children always wake when you're doing something quiet and terrible nearby.

"You're going to do something stupid," Mira said. Her First Tongue had softened over the weeks of travel; now she spoke the common tongue with an accent like wind through high grass, and she used it mostly to insult Ada's judgment.

"Yes."

"In the valley you told me stupid was the one thing you'd retired from."

"I lied. I lie professionally." Ada tested the edge against her thumb. "Come here. Give me your arm."

Mira came. That was the part Ada would carry longest, she thought — not the dragon or the god or the burning light bleeding through the Spire's stones, but the fact that the child came without being asked twice. Feral, hunted, the last living seal on a pact older than kingdoms, and she trusted a mercenary with a knife because Ada had fed her first and asked questions second.

Ada turned Mira's wrist over. Beneath the skin, faint as veins under ice, the seal-light pulsed — gold going grey at the edges, the covenant failing in real time. Above them, through the Spire's open crown, they could hear Vaelithras breathing. Each breath was shorter than the last.

"When I cut," Ada said, "you'll feel it pull. Like something leaving. It will want to take everything with it — the language, the memories, all of it. Let it go."

"And what happens to you?"

"I'm holding the door open from the other side."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ada agreed. "It isn't."

She kissed the child's forehead, because some things you do even if you're bad at them, and then she drew the blade across her own branded arm instead of Mira's wrist.

* * *

The blood hit the Spire's heart-stone and the world went silent in a new way.

Not the silence of the valley — that had been a held breath, a prison built to muffle. This silence was the pause before speech. Every thread of the broken covenant, all those fraying lines of dragon-fire and oath-light that the priests' maps showed running out beneath the mountains, snapped taut and turned toward her.

Because the brand was his. Because his mark was written in her flesh, and a mark, unlike a word, cannot be unsaid.

*You are not qualified,* said the silence, which was how the Silent God spoke when he finally chose to.

"No," Ada said aloud, kneeling on the heart-stone with her arm opened to the elbow and her blood running into channels carved by hands ten thousand years dead. "I'm mortal, magicless, and I don't believe a single doctrine your church sells. Which makes me exactly qualified. You broke the covenant because keeping it cost too much. So did staying loyal to you. I know how that arithmetic goes, and I'm done doing it."

*It will not be painless.*

"Nothing has been."

*It will not end.*

"I know what a seal does. I read Mira's memories, remember? I know I become the hinge. I know I stop belonging entirely to myself." She pressed her palm flat against the stone. "Do it anyway. Or stand there being silent forever, and let the girl burn instead. But if you let her die after everything, I will find whatever hell you gods go to when you fade, and I will make it loud."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then, faintly, in the tone of a man who has lost an argument and knows it: *You were always my worst soldier.*

"And your best investment."

The brand began to burn.

* * *

It did not feel like becoming a seal. It felt like being unmade as a mercenary and remade as a wall.

Ada had spent twenty years learning to hold ground — to plant her feet where someone needed a line drawn and refuse to move until relieved or dead. What happened in the Spire's heart was that discipline made metaphysical. The covenant poured into her through the wound in her arm, and it was heavy, so heavy, centuries of promises and debts and dragon-hunger and human stubbornness, all of it looking for somewhere to rest. She rested under it. She held.

Through the roaring in her skull she heard Vaelithras exhale — one long breath, longer than any breath should be, the dragon pouring the last of himself down the threads and into her, spending his final fire not on war but on transfer. The grey edges of the seal-light flushed gold again. Not the gold of the old covenant. A duller color. Iron-gold. Mercenary gold.

When she surfaced from it, hours or years later, the dragon was already going.

He was smaller than he'd been. Scales dimming like banked coals, the great head lowering until one ancient eye — amber shot through with exhaustion and, unmistakably, relief — levelled with hers.

*Little thorn,* he said, in the First Tongue, which she somehow still understood, though she suspected that gift would fade. *Ten thousand years I have kept this post alone. You held it for one night and did not flinch. I am grateful in a way your language has no word for, so I will use the oldest one:* thank you.

"Don't be grateful," Ada rasped. "Be dead. You've earned it."

*Yes.* Something like a smile moved the vast ruined face. *Tell the small one — tell her the tongue is hers now, truly hers, not a burden. Tell her dragons were never meant to be gods. We were meant to be neighbors.*

Then he laid his head down the way an old soldier lies down, and the fire went out of him gently, from the tail forward, like a tide withdrawing, and the greatest living thing in the world became stone and memory.

* * *

The god spoke once more. Only once.

She felt him everywhere and nowhere in the moment after Vaelithras died — present the way pressure is present, the way grief is present. All the power the church had promised her, all the restored glory of his voice, and he spent none of it. He gathered what remained of himself, this god who had gone mute for twenty years rather than confess what he'd done, and he said:

*I hid the seal because I could not bear to watch it break. I built the valley to keep the ending out. You have done the braver thing: you let the ending come, and then you stayed.*

A pause, and then, quieter — almost human:

*Goodbye, Ada. Thank you for killing those riders for her. I saw. I have always seen. Silence was never absence. Remember that, when you hate me.*

And then nothing, truly nothing, and Ada knelt alone on the heart-stone of the Spire with a scar on her arm shaped like a thorn wrapped around a circle, except now the circle was closed, and it was hers.

* * *

Mira found her at the Spire's crown, watching the sun rise over a world where the covenant ran quiet and strong through iron-gold lines no priest's map had ever charted.

The child climbed onto the parapet beside her, looked at Ada's bandaged arm, and burst into tears — furious ones, wiping them away with her sleeve like they'd betrayed her.

"You didn't even ask me."

"There wasn't time."

"There's always time to ask!" She punched Ada's good shoulder, not hard. "You don't get to just — you don't get to decide to be the wall! Walls don't get to choose!"

"They don't," Ada agreed. "That's what makes them walls."

Mira glared at her, red-eyed, and then folded herself against Ada's side with the fierce gracelessness of a child who had learned affection from no one and was improvising. They watched the light climb.

"Is he gone?" Mira asked eventually. "Your god?"

"Gone."

"Are you sad?"

Ada considered it honestly. "I'm lighter. Sadness can catch up later. It usually does."

"What happens now?"

"Now?" Ada stood, rolled her sleeve down over the scar, and picked up her pack. "Now we walk back to the valley. Someone should live there who knows what it really is. Not a prison. A keeper's house." She glanced down at the child. "And someone should teach its keeper the First Tongue properly, since she's apparently the last fluent speaker on earth."

Mira sniffed, wiped her face, and fell into step beside her. "You're a terrible student."

"I'm a professional liar and a retired killer. Teaching me a dead language is going to be the easiest thing anyone's ever asked of me."

They descended the Spire together, two small figures against the vast stone, and behind them the morning came on clean and ordinary, and ahead of them the long road home waited — no longer a sentence, only a road.
