## 1. The Ledger of Small Sins

There is an art to writing nothing, and I have practiced it for fifteen years.

The temple scriptorium smells of tallow and iron-gall ink and the particular staleness of men who believe themselves righteous. I sit at the third desk from the window—third, because the first two are for senior scribes with names worth remembering, and the last four are for novices whose mistakes are still forgivable. The third desk is where the invisible work happens. Someone has to copy out the tithe rolls. Someone has to enter the weekly charm-confiscations into the registry. Someone has to make the numbers dull enough that no one ever looks at them twice.

That someone is me.

This morning's work: forty-one confiscated charms to be catalogued and destroyed. A knotted red thread from a fisherman's wife in Lowmarsh, meant to keep his boat from the deep water. A dried sprig of something that might once have been rowan, folded into a child's shoe. A coin with a hole drilled through it, worn smooth by a thumb that rubbed it for luck on long roads. Small sins. Harmless ones. The kind that kept people upright through winters and funerals and the long gray stretch between paydays.

I entered each one in the ledger in my careful, characterless hand—the hand I had trained myself into over a decade, so uniform that even I sometimes couldn't tell which entries were mine and which were forged. Then I adjusted the count. Forty-one became thirty-eight. Three charms would simply never have existed. They would go into the destruction brazier as ash, unrecorded, and somewhere in Lowmarsh or the Warrens or the road south, someone would find their little knot of hope still working, still warm, and never know why the Edicts had missed them.

Watered-down charms, watered-down mercy. It was not heroism. I want to be clear about that, because heroes get remembered, and remembering is how you die here. It was maintenance. The way you patch a roof. The way you feed a fire so small it doesn't throw light—just enough heat to keep one room of the world from going entirely cold.

"Delacroix."

I didn't flinch. Fifteen years of practice there too. I turned with the mild, slightly put-upon expression of a scribe interrupted at tedious work, which was even true.

Brother Anselm stood at the end of the row, wringing his hands the way he did when he carried news he thought was important. Everything Anselm carried was important; he was a man who treated a paper cut as a crisis of faith.

"Auditor," he said. "From the Office of Purity. Arrived this morning. He wants the registries. All of them."

My quill did not tremble. I had spent years teaching it not to. "All of them," I repeated, in the tone of a woman being asked to move furniture.

"The fifteen-year registries especially." Anselm lowered his voice, savoring it. "It's the anniversary soon. You know how they like to audit before the anniversary."

I knew. Every year, as the Day of Cleansing approached, the Office of Purity grew restless, like a dog circling before lying down. They pulled records. They re-interviewed witnesses. They checked that the country's great forgetting was still holding its shape, that no name had grown back like scar tissue.

"Which auditor?" I asked, already knowing, already hating myself for knowing.

Anselm consulted his slip of paper as though it mattered. "Halloway. Inquisitor Halloway."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Inquisitor Halloway: he has never raised his voice in my hearing. He does not need to. Some men hunt with dogs and fire; Halloway hunts with patience, which is worse, because patience cannot be poisoned or outrun. He is a narrow man, gray at the temples, with the kind of face you forget while looking directly at it—a professional advantage, I assumed, though I have wondered since whether it was a wound.

And here is what you must understand about me: fifteen years ago, when they took my mother, I stood in the square with the crowd and I denounced her. My voice cracked on the words but it did not stop. *She practiced the old arts. She kept forbidden books. She taught me things.* I gave them everything they needed, and in exchange they wrote down my name in a column marked *cleared*, and Inquisitor Halloway, who conducted the interview himself, looked at me across his desk with those forgettable eyes and said only:

"You may go, Miss Delacroix."

No questions about what she'd taught me. No follow-up. No second interview, ever, in fifteen years of audits. Everyone else the Office touched got touched again—once you were in their ledger, you stayed in it, re-inked annually like a grave that must be tended. But my page stayed clean. And I have spent every year since trying to decide whether that cleanliness was mercy or a trap left baited, and every year deciding it was better not to know.

Now he was coming to my scriptorium, and the fifteen-year registries were on his list, and somewhere in those registries—in the confiscation logs of the Year of Cleansing itself—there was an entry I could not forge my way around, because I hadn't written it. *Delacroix, Maren. Hearth-charms, eleven items. Books, three. Taken into custody.*

My mother's name, sitting in the public record like a bone under floorboards.

* * *

He arrived at midday, when the light through the scriptorium windows goes flat and honest, the worst kind of light for hiding anything. Two clerks trailed him carrying document cases, and Brother Anselm fluttered around him like a moth that had decided, on reflection, to be honored.

"Scribe Delacroix." He said my name the same way he'd said it across the desk fifteen years ago. Not warm. Not cold. Filed. "You maintain the confiscation ledgers?"

"I do, Inquisitor."

"And the destruction records?"

"Also mine."

"Efficient." He set his case on my desk without asking, which was its own small message: *your space is now administrative territory*. "We'll begin with the current year and work backward. Routine. You needn't hover."

I hovered anyway—or rather, I stationed myself at the copying desk nearby with a stack of tithe rolls I had no intention of reading, positioned precisely where I could see every page he opened. This is the vigilance I was telling you about. It looks like idleness. It costs more than labor ever did.

For two hours he worked through the current ledgers, and I watched him find nothing, because there was nothing to find—I had built the nothing myself, entry by entry, over years. He read the way other men pray: completely, without hurry, as if the pages might answer him. Twice he paused at one of my doctored counts, and both times I felt my heartbeat in my teeth, and both times he simply made a small notation and moved on. Perhaps he noticed the arithmetic was too tidy. Perhaps tidiness was all he expected from a careful scribe.

Then he reached the older volumes.

The fifteenth-year registry came off the shelf trailing dust, and when he opened it, the smell rose up—that particular smell of old iron-gall ink, browned and faintly rust-sweet, the smell of the year my mother stopped existing. I copied tithe rolls. I did not read a word of them.

He turned pages. Confiscation log, spring quarter. Summer. His finger moved down columns of names, most of them crossed through with the Office's cancellation mark, the single stroke that meant *processed*—which was the word they used instead of the words they actually meant.

His finger stopped.

I could not see the entry from where I sat. I did not need to. I knew the page. I had known it existed for fifteen years, had calculated its position the way a prisoner calculates the distance between bars. *Delacroix, Maren.* My mother's name in a stranger's handwriting, followed by her crime in the Office's flat vocabulary: *hearth-craft, proscribed texts, instruction of a minor.*

Instruction of a minor. That was me. That was the lullabies. That was a language sung soft over a candle flame, words I was told to forget and never managed to, words that lived in me now like a splinter too deep to dig for.

Halloway looked at the entry for a long time. Long enough that Anselm, sensing significance the way moths sense lamps, drifted closer. Long enough that I ran through the arithmetic of my life: what I had, what I could lose, how fast I could get to the lodging house, whether the loose floorboard under my bed was loose enough.

Then Inquisitor Halloway did something I had never seen him do in fifteen years of anniversaries.

He turned the page.

Not past it—over it. He lifted the corner of the leaf, held it a moment as if weighing something invisible, and continued to the next entry as though the name beneath his thumb were any name, a name with no daughter three desks away pretending to count sacks of barley. He made no notation. He asked no question. He did not look at me.

But as he closed the volume and moved to the next, he said—mildly, to the room, to no one—"The old registers are poorly indexed. Names repeat. One can waste a whole career chasing a name that turns out to mean nothing."

Anselm nodded sagely at this wisdom. I copied a line of barley figures twice by mistake.

They finished near dusk. Halloway repacked his case, thanked Brother Anselm with a courtesy that visibly disappointed him, and paused at my desk on his way out. Up close he smelled of cold air and lamp oil, and his eyes were the color of wet slate.

"Your hand is very consistent, Scribe Delacroix," he said. "Year after year. Almost as if it never changes at all."

"Inconsistency is a flaw in a record-keeper, Inquisitor."

"It is." He considered me for a moment longer than the sentence required. "Happy anniversary, Miss Delacroix."

And then he was gone, down the stairs, out into the street, taking the flat honest light with him.

I sat at my desk until the bell rang for evening offices, doing the thing I do every year on this date: inventorying myself. Name intact. Position intact. Three charms smuggled through, unrecorded. Mother's name unread, unspoken, still buried under a page-turn that might have been mercy or might have been a man showing me he knew exactly where the body was.

That night, back at my lodging, I checked the floorboard out of habit. Under it: three forbidden books, a bundle of letters tied in string, and a pressed sprig of rowan that had been my mother's. All present. All silent.

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark rehearsing my nothing, the way I did every night—my plain history, my dead parents, my unremarkable years—and I had just about talked myself into sleep when I heard it.

Footsteps on the stair. Small ones. Uneven, the gait of someone exhausted or hurt. They stopped outside my door.

And then, through the wood, thin and clear, in a language the Edicts had burned out of every hearth in Vellamere—a language I had not heard spoken aloud since I was nine years old—

a child's voice said my mother's word for *open*.
