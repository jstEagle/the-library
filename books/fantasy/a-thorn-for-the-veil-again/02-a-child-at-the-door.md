## 2. A Child at the Door

The anniversary of the Edicts falls on the longest night of the year, which the temple fathers insist is a coincidence of the calendar and not, as the taverns whisper, the gods' own commentary. In Vellamere we are required to observe it with candles extinguished and shutters drawn, a city-wide rehearsal of the darkness the Purists claim they saved us from. I observe it the way I observe everything: precisely, quietly, and with one eye on the door.

I had planned to spend it as I spent every evening—copying the *Litany of Cleansing* for the hundredth time, watering down three more charms for customers too poor to be dangerous, and eating a supper of bread and salted fish over my ledger while I checked, for the fourth time that week, that no line in it could be traced back to a woman named Maerin Delacroix.

That was the name in the old registry. My mother's name. The name Inquisitor Halloway's finger had rested beside for one terrible, endless moment that afternoon, before moving on. I had gone home with my hands steady through sheer force of will and my stomach in knots beneath them, and I had told myself, as I always told myself, that the danger was passed, that vigilance was a muscle and mine was strong, that I would live to be old and dull and unremarkable in every record kept by every clerk in Vellamere.

I was halfway through the fish when someone knocked.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when they have been taught that loud knocking is rude, or when they are too tired to knock properly, or when they have knocked on a great many doors already and been turned away from most of them.

I did not answer. That is the first rule of the Edict years: you do not answer a knock you cannot account for. I put down my bread, lifted the lamp, and stood to the side of my door where the shadow would hide me, and I listened.

The knock came again. Then a voice—small, cracked, exhausted—and it said something that stopped my heart the way a hand stops a clock.

Not Vellamere speech. Not the trade-tongue of the river towns, not the clipped temple Latin of the liturgies, not the gutter cant of the Docks. The words were older than all of those. They had vowels that opened like doors into warm rooms, consonants that fell soft as ash. I knew the language the way you know a song you heard before you could speak: not the meaning, but the shape of it. The melody of it.

My mother used to sing in that tongue. She thought I was asleep.

*Áthel vor, áthel síren,* she would murmur, combing my hair by candlelight. *Sleep, little ember, sleep.* I had not heard it in fifteen years. I had made a discipline of not hearing it. And here it was on the other side of my door, in a voice no bigger than a wren's, saying something over and over—*síren, síren*—which might have meant *open* or might have meant *please* or, given the hour and the knocking, almost certainly meant both.

I should have blown out the lamp. I should have gone to bed and lain awake until the knocking stopped, and in the morning told myself I had dreamed it. That is what a sensible woman does in Vellamere. That is what a woman does who has spent fifteen years sanding herself down to something no ledger could catch on.

I opened the door.

* * *

She could not have been more than seven or eight, though it was hard to say, because she was caked in mud to the eyebrows. Road mud, river mud, the grey clay of the ash country beyond the hills—she had come a long way, and she had come it mostly on foot, if the state of her boots meant anything. They were good boots, once. Now they were ruins held together with string and stubbornness.

She looked up at me, and I looked down at her, and neither of us said anything for a long moment, because there is no common language between a temple scribe and whatever she was.

Then I saw what she held.

Her right hand was clenched around something at her side, and it was glowing. Not brightly—the way a banked fire glows, the way coals look in a hearth at three in the morning when everyone has gone to bed and the fire is deciding whether to die. A dull red pulse, steady as a heartbeat, leaking light between her small grimy fingers.

"Let me see your hand," I said, and then felt foolish, because she wouldn't understand.

But she understood the gesture. She opened her fingers.

It was a coal. An ordinary-looking piece of char, black and cracked, no bigger than a plum. Except that it glowed, and it had clearly been glowing for some time, because her palm beneath it was not burned. Not reddened. Not marked at all. It sat in her hand like a bird that had decided she was its nest, and the light of it rose and fell, rose and fell, with a rhythm I recognized before I could name it.

It breathed.

I am a scribe. I deal in ink and vellum and the careful falsification of records. I do not believe in things that breathe. But there in my doorway, on the night when every candle in Vellamere was snuffed by law, a child held a piece of fire that was alive, and the light of it fell on my face, and some traitor part of me—some part I had buried under fifteen years of careful, dull, invisible living—recognized it the way you recognize a face across a crowded market.

*Áthel síren.* Sleep, little ember.

I had heard that word before. In a lullaby. From a woman the Edicts erased.

I stepped back and pulled her inside, and shut the door, and threw the bolt, and only then let myself shake.

* * *

She would not sit until I fed her, and she would not eat until I ate first, which told me someone had taught her caution worth more than any charm I could forge. I gave her bread and the rest of the fish and watered wine cut heavily with water, and she ate like someone who had learned not to trust the next meal, which is to say quickly and without pleasure. While she ate, I examined her by lamplight, the way you'd examine a document for forgery.

Her boots said the ash country. The grey clay in the seams of her clothes said it too—I had copied enough requisition manifests for the border garrisons to know the particular ash of the Avelorn flats, the stuff they carted away by the ton because it would not grow anything and would not wash away either. Her cloak had been fine once. There was embroidery at the hem, worn nearly to nothing, and I found myself leaning close to it without meaning to, because the pattern of it was vines and thorns, and my mother had worked the same pattern—no. Not the same. Similar. I made myself believe *similar*.

And she was mute. Not deaf—when a cart rattled past in the street below, she turned her head toward the sound, quick as a bird. But she made no sound herself. When I spoke to her she watched my mouth with fierce attention, the way the deaf do, and when I wrote words on my slate—*your name?*—she looked at the slate, and then at me, and then shook her head slowly, and touched her own throat, and shook her head again.

Whatever she had to say, she could only say it in a language the entire country had spent fifteen years burning out of existence.

I wrote: *where is your family?*

She took the chalk from me. Her hand was steady. She drew, in a few strokes, a house—a long low house with a thatched roof—and then a line of smoke rising from it, and then she crossed the whole drawing out with one hard stroke, and set down the chalk, and looked at me with an expression I knew intimately, because I wore it every day in the temple scriptorium. It was the expression of someone describing an erasure.

Then she opened her hand again and showed me the coal, and pointed at it, and pointed at the crossed-out house, and pointed at herself, and I understood, and I wished with everything in me that I did not.

She was what was left. The way a coal is what's left of a fire.

* * *

I made her a bed of blankets by the hearth, though the hearth was cold—the Edicts require cold hearths in the city, or at least heavily taxed warm ones, and I had long ago stopped paying the tax—and she lay down without protest, curled around the coal like it was a doll or a sibling. The light of it pulsed through my dark room, painting the ceiling in slow tides of red.

I sat at my table with my ledger open in front of me and did not write a word.

Here is what I knew, and I will set it down plainly because I spent the whole night trying not to: the Old Tongue was the language of Avelorn. Avelorn was the country that the histories now say never existed, the kingdom the Purist Edicts unmade—its fires smothered hearth by hearth, its books burned, its name struck from the maps and replaced with the words *Territory Nine*. My mother had been of Avelorn blood. She had sung in the Old Tongue. She had been taken for it, in the second year of the Edicts, and I had survived because I stood in a stone hall at fourteen years old and said the words they put in my mouth: *she is a heretic, and I renounce her.*

I had built my whole life on the foundation of that sentence. And now the language I had renounced along with her was lying curled on my floor, breathing light.

I should have carried her to the temple in the morning. That was the law. Unregistered children of unknown origin, presented to the Wardens for examination. It was what a loyal scribe would do. It was what a woman who had spent fifteen years becoming invisible would do—hand the danger to the authorities and go back to her bread and her falsified charms and her careful, hollow, unremarkable life.

I looked at her instead. At the coal's slow pulse. At the thorn-pattern on her ruined hem.

And she opened her eyes—she had been awake the whole time, of course she had been awake, children on the run sleep like soldiers—and she looked at me, and she said, in that soft ash-fall language, one word. Just one. Quiet as a lullaby.

I did not know what it meant. That is a lie. I knew what it meant the way I knew the melody of every song my mother ever sang with the words sanded off. The word was *Maerin*.

My mother's name. The name that had nearly surfaced in the registry that afternoon, under Halloway's finger. The name I had spent fifteen years erasing from every ledger in Vellamere, and here it was, spoken into my dark room by a child who could not possibly know it, wrapped in a language the Edicts had murdered.

Outside, the bells began to toll midnight—the anniversary hour, the city's annual minute of mandated darkness—and in the pulse of the coal's light I saw my own future split into two roads, and understood, with a scribe's precision, that whichever one I took, I would not come back the same.

The child said my mother's name again. And held out the coal.
