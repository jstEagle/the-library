## 3. What the Edicts Burned

There is a particular skill to sleeping with one ear open, and I mastered it at fourteen. It is the skill of hearing boots on stairs without waking fully—the trick of surfacing just enough to count footsteps, match them against the rhythm of temple patrols, and sink back down. Fifteen years on, I still do it. The Edicts took the country's magic, its dragons, its language, and its history, but they left me this: an ear tuned like a lark's trap-wire and a heart that starts running before my eyes are open.

So when I woke in my narrow lodging with the candle still burning and the child asleep on my floor pallet, coal tucked against her chest like a bird that had flown into her ribs and decided to stay, my first thought was not *who is she*.

It was: *I know what that coal is.*

And knowing it felt like swallowing something that had been waiting fifteen years to go down.

* * *

Let me tell you about the fires, since no one else will. The Edicts saw to that.

Avelorn was never on the maps they gave us in school. That was the first cleverness of the Purists—not burning the kingdom, which would have left a hole shaped like grief, but redrawing the map around it so the hole became simply the shape of the world. There was Vellamere, there were the ash country roads, there were the eastern provinces with their obedient chimneys, and there was nothing between them because nothing had ever been there. Ask any child of ten and they would tell you: Avelorn is a word grandmothers use for nonsense.

But I had a grandmother's word for it too, and mine was not nonsense. Mine was sung.

My mother sang in the Old Tongue the way other women hummed—absently, while kneading bread, while trimming wicks, while braiding my hair so tight I complained and she laughed and said, *Lirien, sulen var,* which meant, as near as I ever translated it, *beauty can bear a little pulling.* She never told me what the words meant outright. She said a language explained is a language half-buried, and she intended hers to stay above ground.

The dragon-fires—yes, there were dragons once, or something the word *dragon* is the smallest shirt that fits—and their fire did not die when the last of them died. Fire like that doesn't live in creatures. It lives in hearths. In ovens and forge-stones and the flat iron griddle where a woman bakes honey-bread on feast days. Avelorn's fire was distributed the way wealth should be: everywhere, in small amounts, owned by everyone. Every hearth in the kingdom held an ember of it, passed hand to hand for nine hundred years, mother to daughter, neighbor to neighbor, a flame you could not buy because it could only be given.

You see, then, why the Edicts came for the hearths first.

They called it the Season of Rectification. The soldiers went door to door with iron pails and tongs, and householders were required to surrender their fire into the pails—every coal, every banked ember, every spark in the flue—and receive in exchange a government-issue flame, clean, cold-hearted, and identical from province to province. My mother called the new fire *widow-light*, because it kept you warm the way a portrait keeps you company. It burned. It cooked. It did not remember anything.

Most people surrendered their embers weeping. Some surrendered them gratefully—there were always those who found the old fire inconvenient, too alive, too prone to flaring when someone in the house lied. A few hid theirs. The ones who were caught were taken to the Rectification Squares, and what happened there I will not describe except to say that the Purists learned early that fear spreads faster than fire ever did, and cheaper.

My mother hid ours in the one place their pails never reached. Not under the floorboards, not in the well. She pressed it into bread. A coal wrapped in dough, baked until the loaf rose golden and ordinary, sitting on the sill where any soldier could pick it up and put it down again. *Nothing hides better than breakfast,* she told me, and I laughed, because I was eleven and my mother was the funniest person in the world.

Four years later they came for her anyway. Someone talked. Someone always talks; that is the true alchemy of terror, turning neighbors into informants at the rate of one betrayal per two loaves of bread.

I have told this part before—to no one, in the dark, in the ledger of my skull where I keep the accounts that don't fit anywhere else—but I have never told it awake. So here it is, in the light, once.

They came at night, four of them and an Inquisitor, and my mother had time exactly long enough to press the coal-loaf into my hands and say, in the Old Tongue, three words I have spent fifteen years refusing to translate because translating them would make them a promise I'd have to keep. Then she walked me to the door, opened it, and stepped out to meet them as if she'd been expecting callers.

They took her to the Square. And I—here is the part that curdles everything since—I followed. Fourteen years old, clutching nothing, having already eaten the evidence or buried it, I honestly couldn't tell you, I stood at the edge of that crowd of neighbors and shopkeepers and children, all of us pretending we happened to be passing through the Square at midnight, and the Inquisitor read her name from a list, and then he read the accusation, and then he asked the question they always asked:

*Does anyone claim this woman?*

Claim her. As if she were luggage. As if kinship were a legal liability—and it was, that was precisely the point; claiming her meant sharing her sentence. The Square was silent. It was very good at silence, our Square. Fifteen years later it is still good at it.

And a girl's voice said, clear across the frozen air, the voice of a stranger wearing my throat:

*I don't know her.*

That was the denunciation. That was all of it. Three words, same number as whatever my mother had said to me at the door. I have often thought the universe has a cruel sense of symmetry and a cheap sense of humor.

The Inquisitor—young then, gray-eyed, with a voice like a key turning in a lock—looked at me for a long moment. Long enough that I understood he knew. Of course he knew. I had her chin. I had her habit of standing with my weight on one hip. He knew, and he wrote something in his book, and he said, "Release the child," and turned away, and my mother was led off to whatever the Edicts did with unclaimed women, and I walked home past our house, past the windows dark and correct, and I did not cry until I was inside, and then I cried in the manner of someone drowning rather than someone grieving, which is uglier and less dignified and leaves you alive.

She was gone by morning. No execution notice, no body, no grave. The Edicts were efficient that way: they didn't kill people so much as subtract them.

* * *

Here is what I believed for fifteen years, because believing it let me get up in the morning and sharpen my pens: that our coal had died with her. That the last ember of Avelorn's fire had gone into an iron pail or a soldier's boot-heel or simply out, the way fire goes out, without ceremony, while I slept.

Beliefs are lodgers. They pay rent in comfort and leave when the building changes hands.

Because now there was a child on my floor, breathing in the small whistling rhythm of the deeply exhausted, and in her hands was a coal that had not cooled in—who knew how long? Weeks. Since the Rectification anniversary preparations began and the old registers got pulled. However long it was, it exceeded every law of fire I had been carefully taught by a government that taught me fire.

I sat on the edge of the bed and watched it glow. Not orange—that would have been simple. It glowed the color of the light through eyelids on a summer morning. It glowed like memory. And when I leaned close—foolish, I know, I am a scribe, curiosity is supposed to be beneath me—it did not burn me. It warmed. And the warmth arrived somewhere specific, somewhere behind my sternum, and it said, without words, in the cadence of a woman braiding a girl's hair too tight:

*Lirien, sulen var.*

Beauty can bear a little pulling.

I sat back hard enough to crack the bed frame against the wall, and the child stirred, and the coal dimmed politely, the way a sleeper dims a lamp.

It was hers. Or from hers—from my mother's hearth, from the loaf on the sill, from the ember that should have gone into the pail and instead had gone, somehow, hand to hand, hidden to hidden, given and given and given for fifteen years across a country that officially contained nothing worth giving. The last ember. The whole living memory of Avelorn, folded down small, carried by a mute child to the door of the one scribe in Vellamere who could recognize the Old Tongue.

Coincidence, says the sensible part of me, the part that survived, does not exist. There is only erasure working unevenly. Things the Edicts missed have a way of finding each other.

And with that recognition came its twin, the way guilt always travels: if the fire had lived, then my three words in the Square had bought nothing. She might have been taken regardless, yes, but the coal had survived, and the coal was the point, and I had spent fifteen years paying penance for a crime that had purchased nothing at all. Not even safety—look at me, auditing my own handwriting twice a day, sleeping with one ear open. I had denounced my mother for a life I didn't even get to have.

The child woke then. She looked at me, and then at the coal, and then she did something that undid me more thoroughly than any Inquisitor's list: she held the coal out. Toward me. An offer. A gift. Because that is how the old fire works—it cannot be sold, only given—and some inheritance skips every generation between and lands where it lands, whether the heir deserves it or not.

I did not take it. I want that recorded somewhere truer than any ledger. I looked at that small dying sun in a child's palm and I was afraid of it, properly, righteously afraid, because I knew—if I know anything—that keeping it meant becoming her, and becoming her meant the Square had won after all.

Then, below my window, on the cobblestones, boots. Not patrol-rhythm. Slower. Deliberate. The gait of a man who walks like a key turning in a lock.

Gray eyes, fifteen years older, coming up my street at midnight, and I had not yet reported the child, and the coal was glowing through the floorboard gaps like a secret told aloud.

Some debts, my mother used to say, are paid only in kind. I blew out the candle and reached for the child's hand, and understood, finally, what mine would cost.
