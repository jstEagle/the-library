## 4. The Inquisitor's Mercy

*Rosalind*

The child had been with me three days, and in those three days I had learned two things: that the Old Tongue, spoken by a small voice at midnight, sounds less like a language and more like a door creaking open somewhere in your chest; and that a coal which never cools is remarkably difficult to hide from a landlady who inspects hearths for a living.

I kept the coal wrapped in oilskin, buried in my flour jar. The child slept curled around it like a dog around a bone, and I slept—or lay awake performing the theater of sleep—on the chair by the window, cataloguing everything that could go wrong.

The list was short and terrible. Everything.

On the third morning I went to the temple as usual, because a scribe who stops appearing is a scribe who starts being noticed, and being noticed was the one sin I had spent fifteen years avoiding. I left the child with instructions delivered half in trade-tongue and half in gestures: do not open the door, do not speak above a whisper, and if anyone knocks claiming to be me, remember that I am me, and they are not.

The child looked at me with those too-old eyes and said something soft and liquid in Avelorn's dead language. I didn't understand the words. I understood them anyway. *Be careful.* My mother used to say it the same way, in that same tongue, every morning of my childhood, as though careful were a place you could live if you tried hard enough.

I had never managed to find it.

* * *

*Halloway*

Inquisitor Halloway had eaten breakfast in forty-seven cities, and the market square of Vellamere was, he had long ago decided, among the worst of them—fish blood in the gutters, priests selling blessings at a markup, and now, on the eve of the Edicts' anniversary, bunting in the Purist white strung between stalls like laundry for a giant with poor taste.

He was not here for the anniversary. He was here because audits turned up anomalies the way riverbeds turned up bones, and Rosalind Delacroix's ledgers were a riverbed with very clean bones indeed. Too clean. Fifteen years of records without so much as a smudged margin. He had audited enough frightened people to know that frightened people made mistakes, and Delacroix made none, and that absence of error sang to him like a struck glass.

He was turning this thought over when the market screamed.

Not the ordinary screaming—the price-of-fish, my-husband-drinks variety—but the particular scream that Inquisitors are trained to hear through any crowd: the sound of a person witnessing something impossible.

He found them at the fruit stall. A woman backing away from a child—a mud-crusted, hollow-eyed child of perhaps eight—who stood holding an apple she had not been given. The woman was shouting about thieves, but that wasn't what had stopped the market. What stopped the market was that when the child opened her mouth to apologize, the words that came out were wrong.

Old. Halloway knew them the way you know a scar in the dark. He hadn't heard the Old Tongue spoken aloud since he was six years old and a woman with his mother's face had sung over his cradle in it, and then the Edicts came, and the singing stopped, and everyone agreed very quickly and loudly that there had never been any singing at all.

The child said something else. The fruit-seller's wife shrieked, *"That's witch-talk, that's Edict-talk,"* and the white bunting overhead seemed to lean in and listen.

Halloway moved before he had decided to move. It was, he would reflect later, the oldest reflex he owned: insert yourself between the crowd and the thing the crowd wants to hurt, and control the story.

"Witch-talk?" he said, loud, bored, official. "That's gutter babble. Half the orphans on the eastern road pick up gibberish from caravan drivers." He took the child's arm—not gently, but not cruelly either; there was a difference, and he had spent fifteen years learning to walk that knife's edge—and raised his voice for the crowd. "This is Inquisition business. Go back to your fish."

They went back to their fish, grumbling, disappointed. Crowds always were, after an Inquisitor arrived. You could see them hoping for a burning the way children hope for snow.

In the alley behind the fruit stall, out of the bunting's sightline, he knelt to look at the child properly. She stared back with eyes that had seen something no eight-year-old should carry, and her fist was closed tight around something in her pocket, and when she breathed, Halloway would have sworn the air smelled faintly—faintly, impossibly—of woodsmoke and summer.

"Where is your family?" he asked, in trade-tongue.

She looked at him and answered in the Old Tongue, and the words went through him like a hook through cloth.

He did not understand them. That was the official truth, the Edict truth: the Old Tongue was outlawed, unlearned, burned. But understanding lived deeper than law, in some room of himself the Edicts had sealed but never emptied, and the words meant—I was almost certain they meant—*the fire is going out.*

Halloway stood up slowly. Somewhere behind his ribs, fifteen years of careful ash shifted.

There was only one person in Vellamere whose registry trail touched the old Avelorn parishes. Only one name he had spent fifteen years not saying aloud, keeping safe the way you keep a debt you can't repay: by never looking at it directly.

"Come along," he told the child. "We're going to see a scribe."

* * *

*Rosalind*

I heard the commotion before I saw it—heard the market's voice change pitch, that hungry note it gets when someone has broken a rule in public—and by the time I reached the scriptorium steps my hands were shaking badly enough that I dropped my key twice.

Because the child was supposed to be under my bed with instructions, and instead the child was standing in front of Brother Anselm's fruit cart holding a stolen apple, and standing beside the child, with his hand on her shoulder like a man steadying a candle flame against wind, was Inquisitor Halloway.

Fifteen years, and he hadn't changed much. Gray had come into his hair the way frost comes into a field—not all at once, but claiming the high ground first. He still had the face of a man who had practiced kindness until it passed for cruelty, or the other way; I'd never decided which.

"Master Delacroix," he said. His voice carried just far enough to be official, no farther. "This child was found speaking sedition in the market. Sedition in a language, I'm told, you have some scholarly familiarity with."

Every ledger I had ever forged flashed through my mind and found nothing useful. "My scholarship is limited to sanctioned tongues, Inquisitor."

"Of course it is." Something moved behind his eyes—something old and tired and terribly patient. "Then translate nothing for us. Simply take custody of the child while I consult my superiors. She'll be held in the archive. Dead letters. Nothing in that room is read by anyone; surely even a dangerous little witch-child is safe among documents nobody will ever open."

It was absurd. It was insane. It was, I realized with a lurch, mercy—clumsy, deniable, wrapped in procedure the way contraband is wrapped in linen. He was handing me the child and calling it custody.

"The archive is not a nursery, Inquisitor."

"No," he agreed. "But it is quiet. And quiet, Master Delacroix, is something you and I both value professionally." He leaned in slightly, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "You have until the anniversary fires are lit tomorrow night. After that, my superiors will want a name attached to this child. And I have been very good, for a very long time, at not attaching names."

He straightened, restored his official face, and addressed the courtyard at large: "The scribe has the matter in hand. Praise the Edicts."

"Praise the Edicts," the courtyard murmured back, including me, including, horribly, the child, who repeated it in her small clear voice with the accent of a kingdom that no longer existed.

* * *

*Halloway*

He watched her lead the child into the temple and told himself the familiar lie: *I am following procedure.*

Procedure. Fifteen years ago, in a smoke-stinking courtroom, procedure had demanded a girl's signature on her mother's death warrant, and Halloway—then a young interrogator with a reputation for thoroughness—had slid the paper across the table and waited for her to sign it and end it quickly, the way the merciful ones did.

Rosalind Delacroix, seventeen years old and gray-faced with terror, had picked up the pen.

And then put it down.

And said, in a voice cracked down the middle: "No. Whatever happens to me. Not her name. Not in my handwriting. You want my denunciation, write it yourself. My hand won't touch it."

She had signed the renunciation eventually—everyone did, or there was no surviving—but she had refused, with a stubbornness that should have gotten her killed, to be the instrument. And Halloway had sat there feeling something crack in his own chest, because he had done exactly what she refused to do, at exactly her age, with his own mother's name, and he had written it in a beautiful hand, and they had praised his penmanship.

So he had falsified the record. Said the girl's testimony was insufficient. Watched her walk out of that room alive and never explained why, because the explanation was a confession, and confessions, under the Edicts, were how you died.

Now he stood in the temple courtyard with the anniversary fires twelve hours away and understood that his fifteen years of careful not-looking were over. The coal in that child's pocket—he'd seen its glow through the fabric, felt its heat like a hand pressed flat against his own past—was the last ember of everything he had helped burn. And it had come to Rosalind Delacroix, of all the scribes in all the world, as if the dead kept better books than the living.

He could report the child tonight and be clean. Safe. Comfortably hollow, like the collaborators in the ash country villages, who traded their memories to the Inquisition for pensions and slept wonderfully.

Or.

He turned toward the archive stairs, and found Rosalind waiting at the bottom of them, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had already calculated every way he might betray her and priced each one.

"You spared me once," she said. "I've spent fifteen years wondering what I owed you for it."

"Nothing," Halloway said. "That was rather the point."

"And now?"

He looked up the dark stairwell, where a child was sleeping among the last undelivered words of a murdered country, and heard himself answer in a language the Edicts had outlawed—one word, soft, the word his mother used to say over his cradle, meaning *be careful*, meaning *I see you*, meaning more than he could say in any sanctioned tongue.

Rosalind went white.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "You don't speak—"

"I don't," Halloway said. "I remember it. There's a difference, and tomorrow night, when they light the fires, one of us is going to learn whether it matters."
