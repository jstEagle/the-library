## 5. The Dead-Letter Archive

The dead-letter archive lived in the temple's lowest cellar, below the reliquary, below the wine stores that no one admitted to keeping, in a room where the Purist Edicts had never quite bothered to look. This was not an oversight. It was policy. The Inquisition burned what it could prove and confiscated what it couldn't, and letters—private, rambling, half-literate letters full of love and grievance and recipes for fever tea—were impossible to prove anything with. So they piled up instead. Fifteen years of them. Every word someone had written to someone they weren't allowed to write to anymore, sealed in wax or folded into themselves, addressed to villages that had been renamed, to people who had been renamed, to a kingdom that had been renamed *nothing at all*.

I had keys to that room because I was useful, and I was useful because I was careful, and I was careful because the alternative was fire. For fifteen years those three clauses had been the whole architecture of my life.

The child sat on a crate of undelivered grief and ate a heel of bread like it was a theological argument she intended to win.

"You can't stay here," I told her, for the fourth time. "This is where the temple keeps everything it took from people."

She looked at me with the particular patience of the very young and the very old, which is to say she looked at me as though I were the one who didn't understand how rooms worked. Then she said something in the Old Tongue—soft, round, unhurried—and patted the crate beside her.

"I don't know what that means," I lied.

The coal in her fist pulsed once, faintly, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about confiscation: it is archival work. The Inquisitors who came through Vellamere in the first years after the Edicts were not poets of destruction. They were clerks. They catalogued. They numbered. They tied bundles with government twine and wrote summaries on the outside in that terrible upright hand they all seemed to be issued along with their badges. *Correspondence, seditious, 41 items.* *Correspondence, sentimental, 12 items.* As if love could be sorted from treason by weight.

I went down into the archive on the third night, after the child was asleep under my cloak among the crates, because I had convinced myself I was looking for nothing. That's the trick of it—you have to tell yourself you're looking for nothing, or your hands shake too badly to untie the twine.

I started with the oldest bundles, from the year of the Edicts themselves, because if my mother had written anything in her last months, it would be there, in the stratum where everything confiscated was still fresh and the clerks hadn't yet learned to summarize efficiently.

Bundle 1147. *Correspondence, miscellaneous, 63 items.*

I untied it the way you'd approach a dog you suspected of biting.

Most of it was exactly what the label promised: miscellaneous. A woman writing to her sister about a cow. A man writing to his son about a debt, then crossing out the part about the debt and writing about the weather instead, which is its own kind of confession. A letter consisting entirely of one sentence—*come home*—written in a hand that had pressed so hard the pen had torn through.

And then, near the bottom, a letter with no addressee at all. Just my mother's handwriting, slanting rightward the way it always did, as if the words were leaning toward something just out of frame.

I want to tell you I read it calmly. I read it sitting on the floor of a cellar full of other people's stolen voices, holding a page that trembled because my hands were trembling, and I did not cry until the third paragraph, and I counted that as composure.

*Rosalind, if this reaches you—and it won't, because I am writing it knowing they will take it, which means I am really writing it to whoever keeps what they take.*

Well. Hello, Mother. Fifteen years late, but delivered.

*They came for the hearths first, you know that already. One fire at a time, politely, with receipts. What they don't understand—what no clerk understands—is that Avelorn was never the country. Avelorn was the remembering. You can burn every hearth and the country persists wherever someone carries the memory of warmth. That is why they will come for me next, and why they will fail even then, if even one person remembers me truly.*

*There is a door. There has always been a door. The Threshold stands in the old heart of the kingdom, past the ash country, and it does not open for keys or force or prayer. It opens for payment. One true memory, freely given—not taken, not traded under duress, given—and in exchange it holds itself open long enough for what must pass through to pass.*

*I have paid things before. I know what I'm asking you never to pay. But if you are reading this, then someone kept my words, and keeping is already halfway to giving, and maybe the door will count that.*

*Don't come looking for me. Come looking for the door.*

*You were the best thing I ever made and I made you out of lullabies. Forgive me the ones I taught you. They were the only inheritance I had left to steal for you.*

I read it four times. On the fifth reading I stopped reading and started memorizing, because the Inquisition could confiscate paper but they'd never yet figured out how to confiscate a skull.

Then I heard the child behind me, awake, watching, and she said—in the Old Tongue, in a voice that was not a child's voice, in a cadence I knew better than my own name—

*"Come looking for the door."*

* * *

I did not sleep well after that. I say "sleep" generously; what happened was that I lay on my cot in the lodging house while the child slept beside me, and somewhere past midnight she began talking in her sleep, and it was my mother's voice coming out of her mouth.

Not approximately. Not "a voice like hers." My mother's voice, with its habit of letting sentences trail off unfinished when she thought you weren't listening, with the slight catch before words beginning with *th*, as if she distrusted them. The child lay there, small and mud-splattered and eight years old at most, and spoke in the voice of a woman who had been ash for fifteen years, and what she said was:

*"Count the lullabies, little wren. Count them and see if any are missing."*

I got up and lit a candle I didn't need and recited the lullabies in my head like inventory. Seven. There had always been seven. I sang through all seven silently, and they were all there, and I sat back down on the cot feeling like a woman who has checked her purse twice and found her money intact and is somehow more frightened than if it had been stolen.

Because why would she say that? Unless something *could* go missing. Unless memory was a ledger too, and somewhere a line item was being moved.

The next night it happened again. *"The door takes only what you give freely. Remember that. It cannot be tricked, and neither can you, though you'll try."* And the night after: *"The coal is dying, Rosalind. Whatever you're waiting for, stop waiting for it."*

I began sleeping in the chair by the window instead. It didn't help. Dreams don't require proximity.

In the dreams, my mother stood in the temple scriptorium of my childhood, at my old desk, copying out lines in the Old Tongue, and when I asked her what she was writing she said, *"Your part,"* and slid the page across, and the page was blank, and I woke with ink-stained fingers that weren't stained.

* * *

On the sixth day, Halloway came to the scriptorium.

He did it properly—an audit, announced, two junior clerks with him, the whole theater of procedure. He examined ledgers. He examined me examining him. Somewhere in the middle of it he set down his magnifying lens and said, conversationally, "There was an incident in the Tanners' Row market last week. A child. Speaking a dead language."

"Children speak nonsense constantly," I said. "It's most of what they do."

"It wasn't nonsense." He aligned a ledger edge with the desk edge, a small violence of tidiness. "I knew a woman who spoke that way. She used to sing while she worked. Do you know what I remember most clearly about her voice?" He looked up. "Neither do I. Isn't that remarkable? I remember that I remembered."

My hands were very steady on the ledger. I had trained them for fifteen years and they performed beautifully. "That sounds like a personal loss, Inquisitor. I'm sorry for it."

"Yes," he said. "So am I." And he gathered his clerks and left, and I stood in the emptying scriptorium understanding that the net had not closed—it had been closed for days, and he was simply now informing me of the fact with the courtesy of a man who owed me something.

That night I moved the child to the cellar permanently. I brought down blankets, bread, the coal-warmth to sleep beside. I brought down my mother's letter, wrapped in oilcloth, and I sat with the child between crates of confiscated lives and told her, in the Old Tongue, badly, the seven lullabies.

She listened with her head tilted. When I finished, she reached out and touched my mouth with two fingers, the way you'd hush someone, and said in my mother's voice:

*"Closer. You're getting closer. The ash country knows the way even when you don't."*

And the coal flared—actually flared, a bloom of orange light in the dark cellar, throwing our shadows enormous against the shelves of undelivered words—and for one moment I saw, reflected in the child's wide eyes, not the cellar at all but a field of grass, impossibly green, and a door standing alone in it like a held breath.

Hope, I thought, is the cruelest thing they ever confiscated, because they never managed to confiscate it—they just stored it here, in the dark, in everyone else's handwriting, waiting for someone desperate enough to come read it.

I packed a bag that night. Two changes of clothes, bread, my mother's letter, a knife, and the temple's seal, which I stole without ceremony because ceremonies are for people with futures.

At dawn, Halloway would learn the child was gone. At dawn, the hunt would begin in earnest.

But tonight, in a cellar full of letters that were never allowed to arrive, one finally had.
