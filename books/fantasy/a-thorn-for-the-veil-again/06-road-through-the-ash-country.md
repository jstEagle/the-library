## 6. Road Through the Ash Country

We left Vellamere before dawn, when even the temple bells were too ashamed of themselves to ring.

I had spent fifteen years learning how to be invisible in a city, which turned out to be useless training for being invisible on an open road. In the scriptorium, invisibility meant small handwriting and smaller opinions. Out here it meant walking at the edge of ditches, keeping my head down when carts passed, and wearing the gray cloak of a woman going nowhere for no particular reason. The child, at least, was a natural. Wren—I had begun calling her that because she refused to tell me her name and because she ate like a bird that had never once seen winter—had a genius for stillness. She could stand beside a fence post and become one.

She could not, however, stop speaking Avelorn.

It came out of her in the quiet moments, the way breath fogs glass: little syllables with edges like river stones, words I half-knew from lullabies I had spent fifteen years pretending not to remember. *Sereneth*, she said, pointing east, where the sky went pale over the rooftops. *Duvain*, she said at the crossroads beyond the grain market, and did not take the south fork, and would not, though I pulled her sleeve twice before I understood.

The road signs said ASHFIELD PROVINCE — QUARANTINE OF RECORDS. Wren's hand said *duvain*. I trusted the child. It seemed increasingly clear that the road signs were written by men who had never been anywhere.

* * *

The first night we slept in a shrine to a saint whose name had been chiseled off during the second year of the Edicts. Someone had left wildflowers anyway, stubbornly anonymous. I built no fire—Wren didn't need one, which is a sentence I am still getting used to having written—and lay listening to the wind move through a country that had been swept too clean.

"Tell me where we're going," I said, in Temple Cant, which she understood but would not speak. "Words, Wren. I need words."

She considered me with those eyes that were far too old for the face they'd been issued to, then pressed the coal into my hands.

It should have burned me. Fifteen years ago it would have been evidence enough to hang us both, and some tired, obedient part of my mind kept waiting for the pain anyway, the way you flinch from a blow that has become familiar. But the coal only sat there warm as a sleeping cat, and in its warmth I felt—not saw, nothing so decent as seeing—a line of hills that no longer existed, a river with nine names, a door.

Then she took it back, cupped it against her chest, and said one word.

"*Emberlyn.*"

Home. My mother had sung that word into my hair every night of my childhood until the Edicts came and I sang silence instead.

"We're going home," I said, and laughed, because the alternative was weeping, and I had budgeted this journey for neither. "Child, home is ash. Home is a quarantine notice and a mass grave and a very thorough list of everything that was there, compiled by people who alphabetized it. You can't walk into a memory."

She looked at me with something dangerously close to pity.

* * *

By the third day we had left the last farm behind and entered the country that officially did not exist, which at least meant nobody checked our papers.

They call them the ash-fields now, and the maps show them blank, which is the cartographer's version of a lie. Blankness implies nothing ever happened there. But walking that ground was like reading a page someone had tried to scrape clean—you could feel the pressure of the letters underneath. The land remembered in the way land does: reluctantly, structurally, in its bones. There were hedgerows running to nowhere, fencing nothing, planted by hands fifteen years dead. There were wells without villages, still sweet, still cold, still holding their breath. And there were the roads—the old roads, sunken below the new ones, paved in a white stone that no quarry in the province admitted to having produced.

Wren walked the old roads. Always. Where the modern track swung east around some bureaucratic inconvenience, she would drop down onto the sunken way without breaking stride, and I learned quickly not to argue. Once, when I steered us up onto the new road to save an hour, she stood at the top of the embankment and simply looked at me until I climbed back down, feeling like a woman who had corrected a priest's Latin and been wrong.

"It knows things," I muttered to myself. "The road knows things and the child knows things and I know arithmetic, which has thus far been the least useful of the three."

But here is what frightened me, and I will say it plainly because no one is left to read this: it frightened me how much I knew too. Not remembered—knew. My feet knew where the sunken road bent before it bent. My mouth shaped *sereneth* correctly the first time I tried it, and I had not spoken that word aloud since I was nine years old. Erasure, it turns out, is a job half done. They burned the books and struck the names and salted the registries, but they could not get at the place where my mother's voice had worn grooves into me, and those grooves were still there, singing under everything, like water under a frozen field.

The Edicts had outlawed magic. They had not managed to outlaw my mother. That felt like treason and relief in equal measure.

* * *

On the fifth night the coal dimmed.

Not went out—nothing so merciful as a clean ending—but dimmed, the way a lamp dims when the oil runs low, and Wren changed. She had been a quiet child; now she became a fading one. Her footsteps shortened. Her color went the gray of old paper. When I fed her bread she ate mechanically, as if remembering that bodies required this, the way I filed reports: because the procedure existed.

I made her sleep against me for the warmth, which was absurd, because the warmth was supposed to come from her. I talked to her all night, nonsense mostly, temple gossip, complaints about the quality of road bread, anything to keep a thread of sound between us while the dark did its work.

And in the morning, Halloway found us.

* * *

We saw him before he saw us—he was always easy to see, Halloway; the man had never in his life done anything casually, including ride across open ground. He came along the new road with six riders behind him and a banner snapping above them bearing the sun-and-scythe of the Purist Office, and my heart performed a maneuver I refuse to describe because it was undignified.

But he stopped. Half a mile off, maybe more. He reined in and sat his horse looking at us across the ash-fields, and I looked back, and between us lay fifteen years and the ground where Avelorn had been.

He could have taken us then. Open country, exhausted woman, failing child, six riders. Instead he raised one hand—not a threat, not a blessing, something older than either—and turned his horse away along the ridge, riding parallel to us, patient as weather. Herding. Keeping us in sight, keeping us moving, letting distance do his interrogation for him.

Because he wanted to see where we were going. He already knew, and he wanted to watch us arrive.

That was the thought that settled into me as I took Wren's hand and led her down off the embankment, down onto the white sunken road, walking faster than wisdom recommended. Halloway was not pursuing us the way a wolf pursues. He was pursuing the way a man follows a map he drew himself and lost, and I understood—with the sick clarity that comes from too little sleep—that whatever waited at the end of this road mattered more to him than my arrest ever would.

"He knows," I told Wren, and she looked up at me and answered in the Old Tongue, one soft phrase I didn't understand, though I caught the shape of it: a question about doors.

"Yes," I said. "Doors. All of them, I think."

* * *

The coal guttered worse each night after that. By the eighth evening it gave off light but no heat, a red point burning cold as a scar, and Wren had stopped speaking entirely except in her sleep, where the Old Tongue poured out of her in fluent, urgent rivers—and sometimes, horribly, in another voice beneath her own. Older. Warmer. A voice that once, in a kitchen smelling of cardamom and tallow, had sung *emberlyn* into a small girl's hair.

I told myself it was grief inventing ghosts. I told myself a great many things that week. I had gotten good at telling myself things; it was practically my trade.

What I could not tell myself was that we would make it slowly. The fire was dying at the speed of a fire, not a pilgrimage, and penance, however sincere, does not put oil in lamps. So I stopped sleeping properly, and I drove Wren and myself down the sunken white roads at the pace of desperation, past wells and headless hedges and fields of grass that grew greener the further east we came—greener, I noticed on the ninth day, than any grass had a right to be in a quarantined province, growing in lines, following patterns, spelling something in a script I almost recognized.

On the tenth day the ridge ahead of us resolved into ruins, and the ruins into walls, and the walls into a village that appeared on no map I had ever forged—which is saying something, because I have forged most of them.

Smoke rose from its chimneys. Real smoke, woodsmoke, ordinary human warmth. Behind us, distant on the ridge, Halloway's riders halted as one, and their banner snapped in the wind like something impatient.

Wren lifted the coal toward the village, and it flickered—faintly, faintly—in answer.

"There are people living here," I said stupidly. "In Avelorn. People are *living* here."

Wren smiled for the first time in three days, and it was not, I would later reflect, an entirely comforting smile.

She took my hand and walked us down toward the smoke.
