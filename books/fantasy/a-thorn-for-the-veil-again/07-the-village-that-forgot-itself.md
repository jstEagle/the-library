## 7. The Village That Forgot Itself

The village of Emberhollow announced itself the way a liar announces himself: too cheerfully, and a half-second too late.

We had been walking the ash roads for six days. The coal in the child's fist had gone from furnace-red to the dull orange of a banked hearth, and the child had gone from walking to being carried, which meant I was carrying her, which meant my spine had filed a formal complaint. So when we crested the ridge and saw smoke rising from chimneys — real smoke, from real fires, over fields that were not ash — I nearly wept with gratitude. That should have been my first warning. Gratitude is how traps get you. Traps know that nobody ever looks closely at a gift.

The village sat in a fold of green country like something out of a tapestry. Thatched roofs. A millpond. An orchard on the near slope with trees so old their branches had gone architectural, all buttresses and flying buttresses of apple wood. A church — no, not a church. A temple of the Purist rite, whitewashed, its bell hanging silent in a tower built for a bell three times its size, because the original bell had been melted down fifteen years ago along with everything else that rang true.

A sign at the crossroads read: EMBERHOLLOW — A CONTENTED COMMUNITY. Underneath, in smaller letters, as if the paint itself had been embarrassed: *Report Unlicensed Memory.*

"Charming," I said. The child looked up at me. She hadn't spoken aloud in two days — she never spoke aloud, not in any tongue — but her dreams had been getting louder, and lately they had been getting louder *in my mother's voice*, which is a thing I will be discussing with a professional just as soon as the Purist Edicts are repealed and professionals are legal again.

We went in. Of course we went in. I told myself it was for bread and a bed, and that was even true, in the way that a drowning man tells himself he's going in for a swim.

* * *

*The child's account, as best it can be rendered:*

*The village smells of nothing. That is the first wrongness. Every place has a smell — smoke, dung, bread, fear — but this place smells of scrubbed floors and whitewash, of a room after the body has been carried out. The people smile. Their smiles arrive on schedule, like the millwheel turning, like the bell that does not ring.*

*One woman is watering a dead orchard. She waters it the way one waters a grave: faithfully, without expectation. The coal in my hand stirs. It is not much of a stirring. A coal the size of a plum, and it has crossed a country of ash, and it is tired, and so am I, and the woman in whose arms I ride smells of ink and guilt and lullabies she pretends not to remember. I hold the coal a little tighter. It holds me back.*

* * *

The innkeeper was a broad, pink man named Master Cobb, and he was the friendliest person I have ever wanted to strike. He beamed at us from behind a bar polished to the shine of a saint's relic, and he said, "Travelers! We don't get travelers," with the particular satisfaction of a man announcing that his pond has no fish.

"Road-worn, I'm afraid," I said, in my best scribe's voice, the one that promises paperwork and no trouble. "My niece and I. We're bound for the river country."

"River country!" he said, as though I'd named a wonder of the world. "Well. Bread's fresh. Beds are clean. And there's no trouble here, miss, none at all. That's the thing about Emberhollow. No trouble." He said it twice, and the second time his eyes did something I recognized, because I had seen the same thing in my own reflection for fifteen years: they flicked toward the door, toward the road, toward the horizon, checking.

People who check the horizon are people who remember something worth forgetting.

I paid for bread and a bed and sat by the window to watch the village do its evening. And that was when I understood what Emberhollow was, and what it cost, and why the orchard was dead.

They were the collaborators. Not the informers — the informers had been paid in coin and moved on to better towns. These were the ones who had been paid in the other currency. When the Edicts came, the Inquisition had offered a bargain to border villages like this one: give up your memories of the old ways, the old tongue, the old fires, and be left in peace. A small surgery. A forgetting of exactly the heretical parts. And Emberhollow had said yes, all of them, together, the way a congregation says amen.

I watched them and I saw it in everything. The miller who paused at his own doorway as if he'd forgotten which house was his — not lost, just *unmoored*, a man with no reason to come home except habit. The old woman who hummed while she churned butter, and stopped mid-note every time, frowning at a melody that had been amputated below the knee. Children who had no songs at all, who played games with rules but no names, who would grow up and water dead orchards and call it contentment.

And I thought — with the clarity that comes only when you're too tired to lie to yourself — *there I am. There I am at sixty. There I am if I make it.*

Because that was the thing about my life in Vellamore. I had always told myself it was temporary. A disguise, a waiting room, a ledger I'd balance someday. I'd kept my mother's name alive in secret, her lullabies in my skull, my guilt as a kind of heirloom. I had thought the haunting was the price. But Emberhollow showed me the alternative, the *upgrade*: a life with the haunting surgically removed. Comfortable. Unhaunted. Hollow as the bell tower, and just as silent.

The old woman watering the orchard caught me watching. She smiled her scheduled smile and called over, "Poor trees. Blight, years back. We keep hoping."

"Years back," I repeated.

"Fifteen," she said, "or thereabouts," and her face did the flicker again, the small private earthquake of a person approaching the edge of something and stepping back. "Funny. I can't recall the year the blight came. Only that we stopped hoping around then." She laughed. It was a terrible sound, a laugh with nothing inside it, like a bell made of paper.

The child tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the orchard. Then she pointed at the coal.

"No," I said, before I even knew what she was asking.

She pointed again. Her eyes were the color of banked coals themselves, and they were very calm, and they were very certain, and I recognized that too — I had seen that certainty in my mother, the certainty of people who know exactly what things cost and pay anyway.

"You don't have enough left," I whispered. "You've been guttering for days. If you spend it on *trees*—"

She climbed down off the bench, walked out into the lane, and did not look back, which is the most mother-like thing I have ever seen a child do.

* * *

*The child's account:*

*The orchard is a graveyard that still stands up. I walk between the trees and they lean toward me the way the starving lean toward bread. Fifteen years of thirst, of roots gripping ash-hard ground, of the village forgetting their names — because trees have names, every planted tree has a name, and the forgetting took the names first.*

*I open my hand. The coal is small now, small as a seed, which is what it always was. My mother-voice — the one that lives in my chest, the one the scribe-woman hears in her dreams and will not name — says: fire is not for keeping. Fire is for giving. That is the whole of the old law, and it is the law they burned the books to erase.*

*I press the coal to the oldest tree's bark. It does not blaze. It does the quieter thing, the harder thing: it lets go. All of it. Fifteen years of a kingdom's last warmth, poured into one dead orchard on one green hill in a village that sold its songs.*

*The tree remembers its name. The tree says it out loud, in the way trees do, in blossom.*

* * *

I saw it happen and I still don't have the vocabulary. The old woman was standing at her row with her watering can, and the nearest apple tree — dead, grey, sculptural — *shivered*. Then it bloomed. Not gradually, not politely, but all at once, a detonation of white blossom that raced down the row tree by tree like a rumor, like a fire, like a psalm. The whole orchard went up in flower in under a minute, petals snowing, and the smell — the first real smell the village had produced in fifteen years — rolled down into the square.

People came out of their houses. They stood in the falling blossom and their faces did something extraordinary: they *hurt*. Because you cannot smell apple blossom and remain entirely empty; something in them reached for the memory that had been taken, and found the scar, and the scar ached. The old woman dropped her watering can. She put her hands over her mouth and wept without knowing why, and I thought: *there it is. The price, made visible. They kept the safety and lost the sweetness, and one breath of blossom just handed them the bill.*

The child swayed. I caught her. She was light — lighter than six days ago, lighter than paper — and her hand was open and empty, and the coal was gone. Not spent into ash. *Given.* There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and I understood it the way you understand a debt when you finally read the terms.

"What did you do," I said. Not a question. A lament.

She looked up at me, and for the first time since the ash country she *spoke* — not aloud, not in the Old Tongue, but in the dream-voice, the one that was and was not mine, the one that was my mother's: *"He is behind you, little ledger. He has been behind you since the ridge. Go and hear what he has carried."*

I turned.

At the far end of the lane, where the blossom-thick air met the road, a man sat on a tired horse with an Inquisitor's warrant tube across his saddle, and he was not looking at me. He was looking at the orchard. And Inquisitor Halloway was crying.
