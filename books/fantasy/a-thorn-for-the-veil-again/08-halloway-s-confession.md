## 8. Halloway's Confession

I have chased a great many heretics across a great many miles, and I will tell you what no one tells you about the work: it is mostly waiting. Waiting at crossroads. Waiting in doorways. Waiting for the moment when fear does your interrogation for you and the guilty hand you their own confession like a beggar handing over his last coin.

But I had never waited for Rosalind Delacroix before, and that made all the difference.

The river crossing at Merrow's Ford was the only way east out of the ash country. Every other road had been closed by my own order three days past—warrants nailed to every post, soldiers at every gate—so there was nowhere else for her to go. The ferryman took one look at my gray coat and developed a sudden devotion to the far bank. Good man. Sensible. I tipped him anyway; guilt makes me generous.

So it was just the two of us, when she came down through the reeds at dusk with the child on her hip and the last of the daylight dying behind her like a coal going out.

She saw me and stopped. She didn't run. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that undid me: fifteen years ago she would have run. Fifteen years ago she would have lied, wept, groveled, done all the small ugly things survival requires of the young. Now she simply stood in the mud with her skirts black to the knee and looked at me with the flat patience of a woman who has already calculated every exit and found them all wanting.

"Inquisitor," she said.

"Madam Scribe." I inclined my head. Somewhere in the reeds a marsh bird was making its idiot complaint, and I remember thinking that even the birds of this country had learned to testify falsely. "You've been difficult to find."

"I've been easy to find. You've been slow."

There it was. That tongue. Her mother had it too—I remembered that from the trial, from before the trial—and hearing it again after fifteen years was like pressing a thumb into an old scar to check whether it still hurt.

It still hurt.

"The child," I said. "Give her to me, and you walk free. Full pardon. Your ledgers stay unexamined. Whatever you've been erasing from the temple records stays erased. I'll sign it tonight."

"You don't know what I've been erasing."

"I know exactly what you've been erasing." I let that sit between us. "That's rather the point."

The child turned her face against Rosalind's shoulder and looked at me. She couldn't speak—the Old Tongue doesn't travel well through Purist air—but her eyes did something no child's eyes should do, which is recognize things. She looked at me the way you look at a house you lived in before the fire.

"Say it plainly," Rosalind said. "You want the ember."

"I want the ember contained. There's a difference, and the difference is whether this country burns again or merely keeps smoldering where I can watch it."

"And if I refuse?"

I sighed. It was an honest sigh; I'd earned it over thirty years of service. "Then I take her, and you, and we go back to Vellamere together, and the Edicts do what the Edicts do. I would rather not. I have grown tired of watching them do it."

* * *

Here is what I did not say, standing in the mud at Merrow's Ford with my warrant dry as kindling in my coat pocket.

My name was not always Halloway.

I was born Hallovan, in Avelorn, in a house with a green door on a street whose name I burned so thoroughly that some mornings I wake and reach for it and find only the shape of the hole where it used to be. My mother kept a hearth-fire that never went out—not banked, not tended, just *never went out*, the way certain fires in Avelorn didn't, because they weren't fire exactly. They were memory wearing the costume of fire. When you warmed your hands at ours, you could smell bread baked forty years ago. You could hear my grandmother laughing at a joke nobody living remembered.

When the Edicts came, they came hearth by hearth. Not armies—clerks. Men with buckets and ledgers and a form to fill out in triplicate. *Date of extinguishment. Resistance offered. Names of kin.* We watched the smoke rise from our neighbors' chimneys and told ourselves each plume was ordinary smoke, because ordinary smoke was survivable.

On the last night, my mother woke me. She pressed my father's signet ring into my hand—it was warm, though he'd been dead two years—and she said, "They will ask you to say the words. Say them quickly and mean nothing, because meaning nothing is the hardest spell there is, and it may save you."

Then she walked out to meet the clerks herself, so they wouldn't come looking for what was left indoors.

I said the words. Quickly. Meaning nothing. It worked exactly as well as she hoped and exactly as badly as I feared. I survived. I am, as far as I know, the last living graduate of that particular school, and I can tell you what its diploma looks like: it looks like a gray coat, and a rank, and a talent for finding embers, and a heart that beats with the steady mechanical diligence of a man who checked it long ago and found it empty.

I became an Inquisitor because it was the safest place to hide. No one hunts a hunter. And for fifteen years I have done the work well enough to be trusted and poorly enough to sleep—finding heretics, yes, but slowly, with paperwork errors, with warrants that expired, with suspects who escaped through doors I had somehow forgotten to guard. I told myself I was keeping my hands clean. I was lying. Dirty hands are honest. Mine were merely gloved.

* * *

And then there was the matter of Maren Delacroix.

Fifteen years ago, in the purge after the fall of Avelorn's remnant, they brought in a woman who had been smuggling children out of the burn-zone under sacks of grain. Small children. The ones too young to say the words convincingly. She hid them in the false bottoms of grain carts and paid farmers in charms and blessings, and when they caught her she gave up nothing—not names, not routes, not one syllable of the Old Tongue, which they wanted desperately because a language is harder to kill than a person and they knew it.

I was junior then. Freshly promoted, freshly hollowed. They assigned me her case file, which meant I assigned myself her interrogation, which meant I sat across from her for six nights and asked questions I already knew how to make fatal.

On the sixth night she looked at me—this broken clerk in a gray coat asking his scripted questions—and she said, "Hallovan. Green door. Your grandmother laughed like water going over stones."

I have been interrogated since, in dreams, and I can report that nothing has ever struck me the way those words struck me.

"How—" I began.

"I carried you out," she said. "You were four. You cried the whole road and I sang to you in the Old Tongue until you slept, and you slept so soundly I nearly forgot to give you back. I know your name, boy. I'm the only one left who does. So here is our bargain: you lose me my file tonight—a clerical error, a mislaid warrant—and I keep your name alive somewhere safe. You get your life. I get mine remembered. Fair trade."

I lost her file. I have never been sure whether it was cowardice or gratitude or the first honest act of my second life. She was taken anyway, months later, by other hands. But the file was lost, and in the confusion one name went missing from the record of her known associates, and that name was Delacroix, and attached to it a daughter of eleven.

Rosalind Delacroix owes her survival to a clerical error I committed out of love for a woman who bought my silence with the only coin I had left: my name.

Her mother spent hers saving me. I have spent fifteen years pretending otherwise, and the pretense has fit me like my coat—well-tailored, gray, and suffocating.

* * *

"So," Rosalind said, and I realized I had been silent long enough that the marsh bird had given up and gone elsewhere. "What happens to her, if I give her to you? Truly. Not the ledger answer. The true one."

I looked at the child. She was asleep now, or pretending to be, which children learn early in this country.

"She goes to the Vault," I said. "Containment, not extinguishment. I have seen to it that the Vault protocols have certain... gaps. Drafts that go unanswered. Records that require my personal review before any procedure is authorized." I heard my own voice doing the thing it always did, dressing cowardice in procedure. "She would live. Coldly. But live."

"And grow up to be what? Another you?"

"Yes," I said, because she deserved the truth and because the truth cost me nothing anymore; I'd spent it all years ago. "Another me. A child who learns that survival means saying words quickly and meaning nothing. Who grows up gray and diligent and very good at finding fires, because it is safer than admitting she misses the warmth. That is what I'm offering you, Madam Scribe. Not mercy. Continuity. The Edicts won, and I am what winning looks like."

Rosalind was quiet for a long time. The river went by, brown and patient, carrying whatever the country shed into it.

"Why spare me?" she asked at last. "Fifteen years ago. Everyone asks why the Inquisitor spared the traitor's daughter, and you've never answered, and I stopped believing you didn't know."

"Because your mother saved my life and I never repaid it," I said. "Because sparing you was the only payment left to me. And because—" and here my voice did something I did not authorize, something cracked and old and Avelornish, "—because when I look at you, I see the woman I might have been if I had run instead of kneeling. If I had kept the name instead of burning it. You are my unfinished sentence, Rosalind. I have been trying to close you out of my books for fifteen years, and you will not close."

"That's not love," she said quietly. "That's bookkeeping."

"It's all I have left. Take pity on a poor accountant."

She almost smiled. Then the child stirred, and opened her small fist, and I saw it: the ember, dimmer than the reports described, guttering like a lamp in wind, and around its light—for half a heartbeat—the smell of bread baked forty years ago, and laughter like water over stones.

My knees nearly went. Fifteen years of discipline, and a smell undid me.

"It's dying," I said. "You know it's dying. Every day on the road it dims. By the time you reach wherever you're reaching, there'll be nothing left to carry but a cold stone and a dead language. Give her to me while she can still be kept alive. That is the only offer in which anyone survives."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I arrest you both at dawn, and we all march back to Vellamere together, and I spend the rest of my life being what I am."

She shifted the child higher on her hip. East, behind her, the ash country lay flat and silver under the rising moon, and somewhere out in that emptiness stood a door I had spent fifteen years telling myself was a rumor.

"Halloway," she said—my stolen name, in her mother's cadence, and I confess it went through me like a blade finding the one gap in the armor—"what was your street called? Before you burned it?"

And I opened my mouth to say *I don't remember*, which is the lie I tell everyone including God—

—and instead heard myself say, "Rue des Trois Tilleuls. Three lindens. They cut them down first, before the houses. They always start with the trees."

The Old Tongue surfaced in me unbidden, the words arriving like water finding an old channel, and Rosalind's eyes went wide, because she understood them, and so did the child—who lifted her head from Rosalind's shoulder, looked straight at me, and spoke her first word in weeks:

*Grandfather.*

The ember flared once in her fist, bright as my mother's hearth, and the river ran dark and quick toward the sea, and I knew, standing in the mud with my warrant in my pocket and my whole burnt life in my mouth, that I had already lost—that I had perhaps lost fifteen years ago, on a night when a smuggler sang a child to sleep and the child grew up to be me.

"Take her east," I heard myself say. "Before I remember how to be what they made me."
