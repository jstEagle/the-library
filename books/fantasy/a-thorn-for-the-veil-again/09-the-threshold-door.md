## 9. The Threshold Door

I had expected a ruin. What I found was a garden.

The maps my mother left behind—the ones I had copied, hidden, burned, and copied again—placed the heart of Avelorn at the center of the ash country, and everything I knew about ash countries told me to expect desolation: black fields, skeletal trees, the bones of a kingdom picked clean by fifteen years of Edicts. But the road ended at a ridge, and beyond the ridge the ash simply *stopped*, as if someone had drawn a line and said *no further*, and past that line lay grass. Living grass, knee-high and green as any heresy, moving in a wind I could not feel on my own face.

The child felt it. She walked into it with her arms out like a swimmer, and for the first time since she'd arrived at my door mud-splattered and mute, she laughed. The sound went through me like a hook through cloth.

At the center of the field stood the Door.

Not in a wall—there was no wall. Not in a hill or a hollow or any structure a sensible architect might have attached a door to. It simply stood upright in the grass, tall as two men, its frame carved from a wood so dark it seemed to drink the light, and where a door should have had panels there was instead a slow silver shimmer, like water deciding whether to be ice. No hinges. No handle. Just the frame, and the shimmer, and around its base a ring of flowers I could not name in Purist Vellamere's tongue but which my hands, of their own accord, wanted to call *velsirane* — the little lights, the lantern-blooms, the flowers that open only for people who are going home.

My mother used to sing about them. That was the trouble with my mother. Everything was a song, and every song was evidence.

"She's fading," the child said, in Avelorn's Old Tongue, and then, because the coal had taught us both some terrible fluency these last weeks, she repeated it in words I could pretend were ordinary. "She's fading. The fire wants to go home."

I looked down at her cupped hands. The coal that never cooled—the ember of the last dragon-fire, the living memory of everything the Edicts burned—was guttering. It pulsed now like a failing heart, bright, dim, bright, dim, and each dimming lasted longer than the one before. Three days ago it had been warm enough to heat water. Last night it had barely warmed her palms. This morning she'd slept against my back on the road and I had kept checking, the way you check a sleeping child's breathing, to see if it still glowed.

"How long?" I asked.

She shrugged with the terrible pragmatism of children. "Before the moon is up. Maybe."

Maybe. In Vellamere I had built an entire life out of maybe. Maybe the audit would pass. Maybe Halloway wouldn't look twice. Maybe this forgery would hold, that ledger would balance, the small sins would stay small. And every maybe had cost me something I hadn't noticed spending until it was gone.

I knelt in the grass—it was soft, absurdly soft, soft as the lullabies I wasn't supposed to remember—and took the child's thin shoulders in my hands.

"Listen to me. If the Door needs something, tell me now. Whatever it asks. We didn't cross half a country to stand outside it admiring the architecture."

The child looked at me with eyes too old for her face, eyes that had seen whatever the Edicts left of Avelorn, and said:

"It wants a memory. One true memory, freely given. To hold the Door open while someone passes." She paused. "It has to be true. It can't be a thing you're glad to lose."

Of course. Of course it couldn't be simple. Nothing in this country was ever simple; everything was a ledger, and every ledger balanced, and I had spent fifteen years learning exactly what that meant. My mother's final letter had said as much—I had read those confiscated words in the dead-letter archive with my heart hammering like a debtor at the door—and I had let myself hope, all these miles, that hope meant I wouldn't actually have to pay.

"You know what it will ask for," the child said quietly. "Don't you."

I did. There was really only one memory I owned that was both true and precious. Everything else I had already sold off in installments—a name here, a face there, whole years filed under erasures of my own hand.

* * *

Halloway came at dusk.

I heard him before I saw him—his horse refusing the ash-line, stamping and snorting at the border of the living grass as though the ground itself offended it. He dismounted and walked the rest of the way, warrant case under his arm, boots dark with ash, and he looked older than he had in the scriptorium, older than he had at the river crossing where he'd told me what he was. Avelorn-born. Name burned. Spared by my mother, spared me in turn, and hunting us anyway because duty was the only country that would have him.

He stopped ten paces from the Door and did not draw anything. His hand didn't even move toward the warrant case.

"Rosalind Delacroix," he said. Formal. Almost gentle.

"Inquisitor Halloway." I stepped between him and the child, which was ridiculous—he could have moved me aside with one arm—but some postures are not strategic, they are declarative. "You've read the warrant. You know what happens if you take her back. She'll burn in the square on the anniversary, and the last fire of Avelorn will go out in front of a crowd eating roasted chestnuts."

"I know what the warrant says." His voice was flat. Then it wasn't. "I wrote it. Do you imagine I don't know what I wrote? I've written forty-one of them over fifteen years, Rosalind. I remember every name. That's my penance—that they made the man who remembers do the burning."

Behind me the coal pulsed, dimmed, pulsed. The child made a small sound, and I felt it in my chest like a wire pulled tight.

"Then stand aside," I said.

"The bargain stands," he said. "Give me the child. Walk away free. I'll lose the paperwork. God knows I'm good at losing paperwork; I learned from the best falsifier in Vellamere." A ghost of the old dryness crossed his face and died there. "You survive. You've always survived. It's what you do."

"Yes," I said. "It's what I do. And look what surviving got me."

I turned and looked at the Door—at the silver shimmer holding its slow argument with itself—and I thought about the village we'd passed, the one that had traded its memories for safety. Comfortable houses. Hollow faces. An orchard the child had brought back to life with the coal's last warmth, and villagers who watched the blossoms come in and felt nothing, because feeling had been the first thing they'd sold. That was survival. That was the country Halloway and I had both been citizens of for fifteen years, paying our taxes in amnesia.

And I thought about my mother.

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, not even the pages of my own forged ledgers: I remembered her every single day. Fifteen years, and I had never once managed to erase her, though I'd erased everything else. Her voice singing in the Old Tongue while the neighbors' shutters slammed closed. Her hands copying charms by candlelight. Her face in the moment before they took her—the moment after I denounced her, when she looked at me and did not hate me, which was infinitely worse than hatred, which has haunted me more thoroughly than any Inquisitor ever could.

They say debt is inherited in Avelorn. My mother's whole crime, in the Edicts' arithmetic, was believing that memory was worth more than safety. I had paid my whole adult life trying to prove the opposite, and the interest alone had ruined me.

"Halloway," I said. "Your mother spared you. Mine spared me. Every day of my life she gave me without being asked, and the one time she asked me for something—" My voice did something humiliating. I let it. "The Door wants a true memory. Freely given. Not one you're glad to be rid of."

He understood immediately. He was Avelorn-born; understanding was in his blood even if he'd burned the language. His face went gray.

"Rosalind. Don't."

"Somebody has to hold the Door open," I said. "And I am so tired, Halloway. I am tired of vigilance. I am tired of erasure. I have kept every record of my life except the one that mattered, and I have paid for her death every day for fifteen years in the only coin I had, which was remembering. Let me pay it properly. Let me pay it once, and be done."

"That's not payment, that's mutilation—"

"It's the only honest currency I have left." I smiled, and I suspect it was not a nice smile. "Besides. Think of it from your professional standpoint. The great scribe Rosalind Delacroix, caught at the Threshold Door, resisting arrest, and vanishing into legend. Your superiors will love it. Very tidy. No body, no trial, no awkward questions about why you took three weeks to catch a woman and a child."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh and entirely a sob.

* * *

The child was failing fast now. She sat in the grass with the coal in her lap, and the pulses had slowed to one faint flare every few breaths, a firefly deciding whether to bother. The moon was coming up over the ash-ridge, huge and white, and the lantern-blooms around the Door's base began to open as its light touched them—one, then ten, then hundreds, small gold cups filling with pale light, and the shimmer in the Door's frame quickened like water remembering it was a river.

"Now," the child whispered. "It's opening. It needs the memory *now* or it closes till someone else finds it, and there isn't anyone else."

I knelt in front of her. Up close I could see how transparent her skin had gone, how the veins at her temples flickered faintly gold in time with the coal. She was more ember than girl now. The fire had been keeping her alive as much as she'd been keeping it.

"What's your name?" I asked her. "Your real one. Before all this."

"Aelin," she said. "Aelin of the hearth-line of Vel Sarath." And then, in the Old Tongue, something I somehow understood without translating: *you carry me now.*

"No," I said. "You carry you. I just hold the door."

I put my hands on either side of her face, the way my mother used to when she checked me for fever, and I reached for the memory the way you reach for a splinter you've carried so long it's grown into the flesh.

Fifteen years of mornings. Candlelight on her ink-stained fingers. The lullaby in the Old Tongue, low, so the shutters wouldn't hear. The smell of her shawl. The look on her face—not hatred, never hatred—when the Inquisitors came and I said the words that saved my own skin. *I remember,* I told the Door, silently, formally, the way you seal a document. *I give it freely. Take it.*

The Door drank.

There was no pain. That was the cruelest part. There was just a smoothness, a sliding, like a page being turned in a book by someone else's hand, and then—

Then there was a woman I had loved, once, whose face I could no longer find. There was a shape where a mother should be, warm-edged and utterly blank, and a grief sitting in my chest like a thorn with no story attached. I knew I had paid something enormous. I could feel the size of the absence the way you feel a pulled tooth. I just couldn't feel the tooth.

The shimmer blazed silver-white. The Door swung inward on air, onto light that was not light—onto a hearth-glow vast as a horizon, and within it shapes with wings, and voices in the Old Tongue rising like weather, and grass that went on forever greener than grief.

"Go home, Aelin," I said. My voice held. I want that recorded somewhere honest: my voice held.

She stood up—stood straighter than she had in weeks, lit from inside—and before she stepped through she pressed something into my hand. Small. Warm. Cooling even as I closed my fingers around it.

A splinter of the coal. The size of a seed. Enough to remember warmth by.

"Thank you," she said—in Vellamere's tongue, deliberately, a gift of translation—and then she turned and walked into the light, and the wings folded around her like a sentence completing itself, and the Door began to close.

Halloway was crying. I registered it distantly, the way you register rain. He had his warrant case open in his hands and he was feeding it page by page into the grass, letting the wind off the living field take the ashes of his career, and he was saying something in the Old Tongue over and over, and I no longer knew what the words meant but I knew what they were. They were a name. His real one, the burned one, spoken aloud at last in the place that could hear it.

The Door shut. The shimmer stilled. The lantern-blooms closed, all together, like eyes.

And I stood in a field of living grass in the heart of a country that officially no longer existed, holding a warm seed of dragon-fire and a thorn of grief I could not explain, beside a man who had just remembered who he was.

"Well," I said, because somebody had to. "I believe I'm officially a fugitive now."

Halloway looked at me with wet eyes and something new in them—not mercy, not pity, something closer to recognition.

"Rosalind," he said slowly, "there's something you should know. Something your mother told mine, the night before the fires. About what happens to the ones who pay the Door."

The seed in my palm pulsed once, bright as a heartbeat.

"It doesn't just take the memory," he said. "It leaves something behind."
