## 10. A Thorn for the Veil

There is a particular mercy in the way memory leaves. It does not tear out all at once, the way the Inquisitors take a name from a ledger—one clean stroke of black ink and done. No. Memory leaves the way water leaves a coat: slowly, in patches, so that you spend weeks noticing only that something has gone lighter about your shoulders, without ever thinking to ask what it was.

I know I had a mother. Everyone did; it is practically an Edict requirement. The registry says her name was stricken fifteen years ago, which is true, though I no longer remember what the name was before I struck it, or rather before something struck it for me, deeper than ink, cleaner than any scribe could manage. I have checked my own handiwork. Whoever blotted that line was thorough. It looks like my writing. It isn't.

This is what I know instead: I have a thorn in me. Not a physical one—I have had Master Ellery look down his apothecary's nose at me twice now, and both times he pronounced me "hale as a horse with opinions." But there is a small sharp grief lodged somewhere beneath the breastbone, the exact size and shape of a lullaby I cannot hum. It aches when it rains. It aches when I pass the bakery on Cordwainer Street where they sell honey-bread twisted into knots, because apparently some part of me believes that honey-bread knots are significant, and that part will not explain itself.

I have named it, privately. I call it the veil-thorn. A thorn for the veil—the thing that stands between me and whatever was taken, growing there in the dark like everything else in Vellamere that refuses to die properly.

The Purist Edicts turned fifteen this autumn. There were banners on the temple steps, red-and-white, and Provost Kettering read the anniversary address in a voice like wet cardboard, and everyone applauded because it is safer to applaud than to cough at the wrong moment. I stood in the second row of scribes with my hands folded and my face arranged into the mild, forgettable expression I have practiced for half my life. Invisible Rosalind Delacroix. The audit ledgers passed under my brush and out again, unremarkable.

Except.

* * *

Here is the trouble with being a woman who survives by falsifying records: eventually you notice when a record falsifies itself.

It began three days after the anniversary. I keep the minor tithe-ledgers—candle-wax counts, alms distributions, the thrilling arithmetic of who donated two eggs versus one—and I noticed, in the margin of the October folio, a word.

It was written in a hand that was mine and was not mine. Slighter than mine. Quicker. The letters leaned left, the way script leans when the writer learned it young and never fully surrendered to orthography, and the word was composed of characters I did not recognize and yet recognized completely, the way you recognize a face in a crowd from a town you have never visited.

I cannot read it. That must be understood. I have tested myself ruthlessly: copied the word onto a scrap, shut it in a drawer, come back an hour later, and felt nothing but the thorn ache faintly, like a tooth remembering cold air. Whatever language it is, it lives somewhere I no longer have keys to.

But here is what unsettles the provosts, if they knew, and what unsettles me more: the word was *warm*. I touched the margin and the paper held heat the way a stone holds sun after sundown. Not much. Just enough to make a liar of anyone who claims the fires are all out.

I did not report it. Naturally I did not report it. Fifteen years of survival teaches you exactly one lesson, and it is this: report nothing, explain nothing, and copy everything twice.

So I copied it. Into my private ledger, the one I keep sewn into the lining of my coat where Halloway's men searched twice and found only hymnals. And then, because I am apparently incapable of learning, I copied the next one too, when it appeared a week later in the alms-folio. And the next. They come irregularly, like weather. Sometimes one word, sometimes three, always in that leaning-left hand, always warm to the touch, always fading to ordinary ink by morning—which is why I copy them the same hour I find them, fast, before the warmth goes out of them.

I have filled eleven pages.

* * *

People in the temple have begun to say strange things around me, or perhaps I have begun to hear them strangely. Sister Maren, who dusts the dead-letter archive and has dusted it for thirty years, stopped me on the stairs last week and said, apropos of nothing, "You've got a light about you lately, scribe. Like a room where someone just left a candle."

I told her it was the new soap. She looked at me the way one looks at a bad excuse, which is to say knowingly, and let me pass.

And there was the child. Or not the child—you understand, there cannot be a child, because the child went through the Door, except that my knowledge of the Door is itself impossible, since when I try to think about the Door my mind slides off it the way a boot slides off ice, leaving only the sensation of having stood somewhere green. But last market-day a girl of maybe six tugged my sleeve by the fish-stalls and said, in perfectly ordinary Vellamere speech, "You're the one who carried it. Mama says thank you."

"Carried what?" I asked, and she gave me the pitying look children reserve for adults who insist on playing dumb badly, and said, "The warm," and ran off before I could ask which mama, because asking would have been dangerous, and also because my throat had closed up entirely.

That night I dreamed of a field of grass so green it hurt, wind moving through it in long silver strokes, and someone small running ahead of me toward a brightness I could not look at directly. I woke with wet eyes and no idea why, and lay there in the dark doing sums against the feeling—candle-wax counts, alms tallies, anything—and failed utterly, and finally got up and lit my lamp and copied out the newest word from my coat-lining ledger onto fresh paper, simply to watch my own hand move.

It moved steadily. It did not shake. Whatever else the years have made of me, they have made a fine copyist, and I have decided that this is enough. It has to be.

* * *

Inquisitor Halloway came to the scriptorium on the first of the frost-months. He is older now, grayer at the temples, and he carries his authority the way reformed drunkards carry sobriety—with visible effort and constant inventory. He stood over my desk while I inked a wax-count, and I felt the old familiar arithmetic run beneath my fear: *he spared you once, why, don't ask, copy the line, breathe.*

"You were away," he said. Not a question. "Six weeks this summer. Unregistered travel."

"I walked. For my health. The city air disagrees with me."

"It disagrees with a great many people lately." He set something on my desk. My heart performed its usual attempt to escape through the ribs, but it was only a lump of coal, cold, ordinary, cracked down one side. Found in the ash-country, he said. Found along a road he himself had traveled, following—he paused here, choosing words with the care of a man defusing something—"following a feeling I couldn't file a warrant for."

He looked at the coal a long moment. Then he slid it across the desk to me.

"Burn that," he said. "Or don't. I find I no longer care which." And at the door he stopped, and did not turn around, and said, very quietly, in a language I did not understand and understood completely, four syllables that made the thorn under my breastbone sing like a struck wire.

Then he was gone, and I sat there with the cold coal in my hands like a heart someone had handed back to me, and after a while I put it in my pocket, and I have carried it every day since. It stays cold. But some mornings—it was colder yesterday than today, if you follow me, and I do not recommend trying to follow me, I barely manage it myself.

* * *

Tonight is the first hard frost. I have banked my little stove, mended the shutter that whistles, done all the sensible things a surviving woman does. My coat hangs on its peg with eleven pages sewn in its lining, soon to be twelve, because this evening's folio yielded a new word, longer than the others, and warmer—the paper nearly too hot to hold for the first minute—and when I set my pen to it, my hand wrote the word and then, unbidden, added a second mark beneath it. A small flourish. A signature, almost.

I sat looking at it for a long time. Then I blew out the lamp, because the page didn't need light; the word glows faintly on its own, a thread of ember-colored script in the dark, patient as anything planted.

I don't know what it says. I know only that it is addressed, somehow, to me—that whoever's hand keeps reaching across the veil to write in my margins knows exactly which ledger to find, which coat-pocket, which thorn to press gently on the rainy days. Someone is teaching me a language I paid never to remember. One word a week. Slowly. The way you'd feed a fire you expected to keep.

Eleven pages. Twelve now. At this rate I will need another coat by spring, and God help me, I find I am already wondering where one buys coats with deeper linings.

I pressed my palm flat against the glowing word before I slept. It was warm. It was, I would swear, getting warmer.
