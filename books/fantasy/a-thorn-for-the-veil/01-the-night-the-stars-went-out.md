## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The valley smelled of woodsmoke and honey-cakes that evening, and Xanthe Oyelaran thought — as they had thought every Founding Festival since they were old enough to think it — that there was no lonelier beauty in the world than a celebration held inside a cage.

They stood at the edge of the market square with a tray of spiced pears balanced on one hip, watching lantern-lighters work their way up the poles along the square's rim. The lanterns were new this year; old Maren in the chandler's shop had dyed the wax a deep amber, and when they caught flame they threw the color of late honey across the cobbles, across the faces of children already sticky-sweet from festival fairs, across the white walls of the houses that leaned toward each other overhead like old friends sharing secrets.

Beyond the rooftops, the mountains rose. They always rose. The Veiled Valley sat in the cupped palm of them like water in a hand, ringed on every side by peaks so high and steep that the sky itself seemed to arrive only on sufferance, slipping down between the summits in a narrow ribbon of blue by day and black by night. The locals called the passes the Teeth. Xanthe had grown up beneath them the way a fish grows up beneath ice: aware, always, of the weight and the barrier both.

Twenty years ago — before Xanthe could walk, before they could remember — the valley's founders had raised the wards. Every child learned the story before they learned their letters: how the great mages of the last age, fleeing some catastrophe whose name was spoken only in whispers if at all, had sealed the Veiled Valley behind a lattice of protection older than any kingdom. The wards kept out what wanted in. That was the part told to children. What was not told to children — but what everyone knew anyway, the way you know the shape of your own hands — was that wards strong enough to keep things out were also strong enough to keep things in.

No one left the valley. No one arrived. The roads through the Teeth had been closed for twenty years, choked with rockfall no one cleared, watched by ward-stones no one approached. The Founding Festival celebrated, every year, the miracle of the valley's safety, and every year Xanthe stood in the crowd and felt the celebration sit strangely on their shoulders, like a coat cut for someone else.

"Xanthe! Pears!"

Tamsin, the baker's youngest, was tugging their sleeve, flour to her elbows, six years old and imperious as an empress. Xanthe crouched and offered her the tray. She took two, considered a third with the gravity of a merchant weighing ruin, and settled for licking honey off her thumb instead.

"Do the lights go up to the Teeth tonight?" she asked. It was the same question she asked every year.

"All the way," Xanthe said, because it always was. "Every pole, every terrace, right up to the last house under the cliffs. You'll see."

"And then the stars come down?"

"And then we sing to the stars," Xanthe corrected, "and *maybe* they come down, if they feel like it." This was the oldest verse of the Founding song, the one about the stars descending to bless the valley's founding, and every child in the valley believed it literally for approximately four years of their life. Tamsin was deep in the heart of those four years. Her eyes went wide and certain.

"They'll come down tonight," she declared. "I can feel it."

Xanthe laughed and straightened, and did not say what they were thinking, which was that the stars never came anywhere near the valley at all. That even on the clearest autumn night, standing on the highest terrace with the whole sky spread above the notch between the peaks, the stars seemed thin here. Far away. Like lamps burning in windows of a house you'd been locked out of.

It was a foolish thought. They'd had it all their life and never said it aloud, because there was nothing to say it *about*. But tonight, for no reason they could name, the thought had teeth.

* * *

The sun went down behind the western Teeth in a spill of copper and ash-rose, and the valley lit itself against the dark.

Xanthe carried empty trays back to the kitchen-tent, helped hang the last strings of lanterns over the well, let old Halric press a cup of mulled cider into their hands and tell them, for the eleventh time, the story of the night he'd danced with the woman who became his wife, forty Founding Festivals ago. The square filled. Fiddles tuned somewhere near the granary steps. The smell of roasting chestnuts and tallow and crushed thyme braided itself into the cold coming down off the mountains, and the first stars — the few that ever bothered showing themselves this deep between the peaks — pricked out above the northern ridge.

Xanthe found a place on the low wall beside the well, as they always did, slightly apart from the dancing, close enough to be warm in it without being caught by it. They sipped their cider. Below, Tamsin was spinning in circles until she fell down laughing. Somewhere a dog barked at the fiddles. A girl Xanthe had kissed once, two summers past, caught their eye across the square and smiled, and Xanthe smiled back and felt the familiar small ache of being known only a little, by people who knew only a little of them — though what more there was to know, even Xanthe couldn't have said. They were twenty-two years old. They had been born elsewhere — everyone knew that, everyone knew the Oyelaran name wasn't a valley name — brought across the Teeth as an infant in the last days before the roads closed. Their mother had died on the journey. That was the whole of the story, the whole of the history anyone had, and Xanthe had made their peace with its smallness long ago. Or thought they had.

Above the eastern ridge, the sky was very clear.

That was the first thing. Later, Xanthe would remember that: how clear it was, unnaturally clear, the air scrubbed and ringing like a struck bell, the stars above the ridge sharp as nail-heads driven into black silk. And then, between one breath and the next —

They went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. There was no cloud that could have done it, no mist, no smoke from the festival fires. One moment the sky above the eastern Teeth held forty stars, fifty, hard and bright and ancient, and the next moment it held none. All of them. At once. As if a hand had passed over the sky and pinched out every flame in a single motion.

Xanthe stopped breathing.

Around the square, the music faltered. Someone laughed — someone had to laugh, someone always laughs first, because the alternative is worse — and called out something about weather-witchery, and the fiddler scraped a hesitant bar and let it die. Heads tilted back all around the square, dozens of faces turning upward in slow unison, lantern-light gilding their chins and throats, and above them the southern sky, the western sky, the narrow ribbon directly overhead where the stars burned hardest —

Went out too.

All of them. Everywhere. In the space of a heartbeat, the entire sky, every star the valley had ever sung to, was gone, and the dark that came down in its place was not the ordinary friendly dark of a mountain night. It was total. It was a ceiling. It pressed down on the lanterns like a lid on a jar.

Tamsin began to cry. Small sound, quickly swallowed by the hush, but Xanthe heard it, and hearing it broke whatever spell held them still. They slid off the wall and crossed the square in six strides and knelt and gathered the child in, her cheek wet against their collarbone, her small fists knotted in their sleeve.

"It's just clouds," Xanthe said. "Clouds came up. That's all."

"There aren't any clouds," Tamsin said, with the terrible precision of children. "I looked."

"I know," Xanthe said, because lying to her properly would have required looking at the sky again, and they could not make themselves do it yet. "I know. Hush now. Look, the lanterns are still here. Nothing's changed down here."

But that wasn't true either, and even as they said it Xanthe felt it stop being true, felt something change *down here*, in the ground itself, in the stones of the square, in the air — a tremor too deep for ears, a shudder in the roots of things. The hair rose on their arms. The cider in their abandoned cup shivered into concentric rings.

Old Halric was standing at the edge of the square, staring up, and his face in the lantern light was the face of a man watching a debt come due.

"The wards," he said. He didn't say it loudly. Everyone heard it anyway. "The wards are singing."

And Xanthe heard it then too, and understood that they had been hearing it all their life without knowing: a tone underneath the valley, beneath the fields and the cellars and the bones of the mountains, a vast sustained note like a finger run round the rim of a glass — the wards, holding, humming, the way a rope hums when weighted. Except the note was changing. Sliding. Souring. Rising toward a pitch that made the teeth ache and the eyes water, rising, rising —

The lantern nearest the well burst.

Not shattered — *burst*, flared white-hot for an instant and went out, and in the same instant the string of lights above the granary steps died in a running crackle, pole to pole to pole, darkness chasing the flames down the line like wind chasing leaves. Then the chandler's shop went dark. Then the kitchen-tent. The fires in the braziers guttered flat, then stood up again wrong, burning blue and silent.

People were screaming now, but distantly, the way sound comes to you underwater. Because Xanthe was no longer entirely in the square. Xanthe was standing in the square while something *rose* in them — something that had been lying at the bottom of their blood for twenty-two years like a stone at the bottom of a well, and that had just begun, for the first time, to stir.

It started in the palms. A prickling, a heat, a pressure, as though they were holding their hands cupped beneath a waterfall and feeling, for the first time, how much water there was. It moved up the forearms. It filled the chest. It tasted of iron and rain and the green smell before lightning strikes, and it was enormous, it was bottomless, it was theirs, it had *always* been theirs, and it was pushing up against the underside of their skin like a bird against a window, and every instinct they owned screamed at once: *let it out, let it out, let it out.*

"No," Xanthe whispered. They didn't know what they were refusing — the feeling, the power, the night, all of it. Their hands had begun to glow. Faintly, terribly, a pale silver-blue light bleeding up between their fingers like dawn through shutters, and they clenched their fists and the light didn't stop, it only pressed harder.

Across the square, a woman pointed at them and shouted something. Xanthe didn't hear the words. The note beneath the valley was climbing toward its breaking pitch, and the pressure in Xanthe's blood climbed with it, matched to it, tuned to it — and in the last lucid corner of their mind, in the place where thoughts still wore words, one thought arrived with the calm certainty of something long known and only now remembered:

*The wards were holding me down too.*

Then the wards broke.

There was no other word for it. The note reached its peak and *broke*, and the breaking was felt rather than heard, a concussion through stone and marrow alike, and every remaining lantern in the valley died at once, and above the Teeth — visible even from the square, even through the notch of sky, even in the absolute dark — a seam of white light cracked open along the ridgeline where the great ward-stones stood. For one suspended instant the whole ring of the mountains was crowned in burning sigils, twenty years of woven protection blazing out in a final furious flare, and Xanthe saw it reflected in a hundred upturned faces, and then the light tore apart like glass under a hammer.

Shards of it rained down the slopes. Real shards, or things like shards — slivers of dying magic falling through the dark and going out before they touched earth. The pressure vanished from Xanthe's blood so suddenly they staggered, knees hitting cobbles, Tamsin wailing somewhere near, the glow dying in their hands, leaving only an ache, a hollow, an echo of a door that would no longer open.

Silence. Total silence, except for weeping, and the dog, and someone calling names into the dark.

Xanthe knelt in the market square of the only home they had ever known, under a sky with no stars in it, and breathed, and the breath tasted different. The air itself tasted different — thinner, rawer, open, like a room after a window has finally been broken. Twenty years the valley had breathed filtered air and never known it. Now the window stood broken, and everything outside was pouring in.

High on the northern ridge, where no road ran and no house stood, something answered the breaking of the wards.

It was not a sound. It was an absence of sound, a moving hole in the night's noise, and Xanthe tracked it without wanting to, head turning, eyes finding the ridge-line where the starless sky met the black mountain — and saw, briefly, outlined against the place where stars should have been, shapes coming down through the pass. Shapes that ate the lantern-glow of memory itself as they moved. Shapes like shadows cast by no fire.

Beside Xanthe, Tamsin stopped crying and went very quiet, the way animals go quiet.

"Xanthe," she whispered. "What's coming down the mountain?"

Xanthe took her hand and stood up, into a night that had ended in a single breathless instant — the night the stars went out, the night the cage broke, the night the storm woke in their blood and the first strangers in twenty years began, silently, to descend.

"I don't know," they said. "But we're about to find out."
