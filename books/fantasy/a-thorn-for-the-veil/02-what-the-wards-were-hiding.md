## 2. What the Wards Were Hiding

Serah Vane had sworn, twenty years ago, never to set foot inside the Veiled Valley again.

The vow had been easy to keep. The valley sat in its bowl of mountains like a secret buried alive, wrapped in wards that hummed along the border passes with a frequency she felt more than heard — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of copper at the back of the throat. Every knight of the Border Order knew it. Every knight pretended not to. They patrolled the rim of that silence for two decades, swords rusting in their scabbards, and told themselves they were guarding something.

They were not guarding. They were keeping watch over a grave and calling it a duty.

Now Serah staggered through a breach in her own vow, one hand pressed to her side where something without shape or mercy had torn through mail and flesh alike, and the first thing she noticed was the smell. Bread. Woodsmoke. The green bite of late-summer apples stacked in crates outside a shuttered stall. A market square, mid-morning ordinary, full of people who did not yet know that every star in the sky had gone out eight hours ago.

They would know soon. The dark was patient, but not that patient.

"Run," she tried to say. What came out was a wet rasp, barely a word at all. She planted her boot against the cobbles and pushed forward anyway, because if she stopped moving she would die here, in this place she had helped bury, and the last thing she would ever have done with her life was fail twice.

* * *

She remembered the night the stars went out the way one remembers a blow to the head — in fragments, all wrong order.

Dusk, and she had been on the wall at Caelwatch, the last manned outpost of an order that existed now mostly in ledgers. The sky above the Veiled Valley had been clear as polished glass, and then it wasn't. It wasn't clouded. It wasn't obscured. It was simply *empty*, from horizon to horizon, as though someone had reached up and wiped the night clean of everything that had ever lived there. No stars. No moon. Not darkness — darkness has texture, has depth. This was an absence so complete that her eyes watered trying to find anything to see.

Then the wards had screamed.

Every knight on the wall heard it. Not with the ears — with the teeth, with the marrow, with the small animal brain that predates language. Twenty years of humming stone had been holding a single note, and that note broke, and the sound of it breaking went through Serah like a blade finding the seam of her armor. She had fallen to one knee on the rampart with blood running freely from her nose and thought, with a clarity that still shamed her: *finally.*

Because she had known. Gods help her, she had known what the wards were for. She had known since the night a screaming child was carried across the border under seal of the highest order, since the night she stood witness to a lie so large it required a fortress to maintain. She had spent twenty years waiting for the bill to come due.

It came due before dawn. The hollow-born came out of the empty sky like ink poured upward, and Caelwatch — forty knights, four decades of standing vigil — lasted less than an hour. Serah remembered the sound their captain made when the unlight took him. She would remember it until she died, which, judging by the state of her, would be soon.

But the wards were down. The valley was open. And somewhere in that market town below the mountain road, according to records sealed behind three oaths and a lock of iron, lived a storm-caller with a name she had carried like a splinter for half her life.

Oyelaran.

So she ran. Down the switchbacks with the unlight pouring after her like a tide that hated, through the broken gate whose ward-stones were still cooling from shattering, into streets that smelled of bread and woodsmoke and did not know yet what was coming.

* * *

The market square opened around her all at once, too bright after the dark of the pass.

Canvases striped red and white. A fountain at the center with a mossy mermaid spouting water into a shallow basin. Stalls piled with autumn squash, bolts of undyed wool, candles, honeycombs, cheap tin charms sold to children as luck. And people — dozens of them, hundreds, moving through the ordinary choreography of a festival eve, hanging lanterns from the eaves, arguing about prices, laughing.

Laughing. Serah wanted to scream at them.

A woman selling pears looked up as Serah came stumbling out of the lane, and her smile died by degrees — first confusion at the stranger's armor, black-and-silver, marked with the seven-pointed star of the Border Order; then alarm at the way Serah moved, listing like a ship taking water; then horror at the blood. It had soaked through her surcoat, through the padding beneath, warm and relentless against her palm. Her left arm hung wrong at the shoulder. Something had happened to her eye — the world on that side was smeared and dark, though whether from blood or worse she couldn't tell.

"Knight," someone said. "There's a knight—"

"Listen to me." Serah's voice came out wrecked, but loud enough. Heads turned. A hush spread through the square the way cold spreads through water. "The wards are down. The stars are gone. Things are coming out of the dark that—" She coughed, tasted copper. "You have to get off the streets. Get underground, get behind stone, get your children off the—"

The light changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* As though the sun had slipped behind a cloud made of nothing, and every shadow in the square deepened by one shade too many. The pear-seller's lanterns, strung for the festival, guttered though there was no wind. Somewhere near the fountain a child began to cry, high and thin, and Serah felt her stomach drop through the cobbles because she knew that quality of crying, she'd heard it from grown men-at-arms in the last hours at Caelwatch.

"They're here," she said. "Go. NOW."

They didn't go fast enough. Civilians never do; they need the disaster explained first, itemized, justified. Serah watched the crowd hesitate — a merchant clutching his ledger, a mother gathering two children by their wrists, an old man frozen halfway through loading his cart — and she drew her sword with her good arm and turned to face the lane she'd come by, because the unlight did not need lanes. It did not need doors.

It came around the corner of the chandler's shop like smoke deciding to be a creature.

Three of them. Hollow-born. Serah had seen them only in the oldest illuminated pages of the Order's restricted archive, illustrations done by monks who must have dreamed them, because no living hand could copy what they looked like: shapes of absolute absence wearing the suggestion of limbs, faces that were not faces but *indentations*, places where the world declined to exist. Where they passed, the color drained out of things — the red-striped canvas going gray, the squash graying, the fountain water turning the color of pewter. They moved without sound. That was the worst of it. A thing that size should have made noise.

The crowd finally understood. The square erupted — screaming, stampeding, a cart overturning, honeycomb shattered and trodden underfoot. The mother with the children vanished down an alley. The old man froze, and Serah did the arithmetic she always hated doing, the arithmetic of triage, and abandoned him to save the many, and hated herself in the same breath—

—and then someone didn't run.

Someone stepped between the old man and the nearest hollow-born. Young — younger than Serah by ten years, perhaps more. Lean, sharp-faced, hair dark as wet slate tied back carelessly, dressed in a festival-goer's plain clothes with sleeves shoved to the elbow. And in their stance there was something Serah recognized from the archive illuminations, from descriptions sealed in wax and iron: the particular way a storm-caller stands when the power in them wakes and looks for a door.

"You can't fight those," Serah rasped, moving to cover them both. "Get behind me."

"They knocked over Daeren," the young person said. Their voice shook, but their feet didn't. "He's seventy. He sells candles." Something in the air around them had begun to tighten, to charge, the way air does before lightning. The hairs on Serah's forearm rose beneath her vambrace. "What are those things?"

"Hollow-born." She put herself squarely between them, sword up, and felt the nearest one notice her — felt it the way you feel a draft from an open door in winter. "Creatures of unlight. They eat warmth. Color. Sound. Life." The lead one drifted closer, and where its attention touched her wounded side, the pain sharpened as if the wound itself were being *read*. "And they've followed me here, which means I've just led death into your home, and for that I am—"

The hollow-born struck.

No warning, no wind-up. One moment it hung at the mouth of the lane; the next it was crossing the square like spilled ink running uphill. Serah met it with everything the Order had ever taught her — footwork, angle, the descending cut that had felled men twice her size — and her sword passed through the creature like a blade through cold smoke and accomplished precisely nothing. The impact, when the thing's pseudo-limb caught her across the chest, sent her skidding six feet across the cobbles, sword spinning from her grip.

She hit the fountain basin. Water, shockingly cold. She got one arm over the mermaid's tail and held on, because letting go meant dying, and dying here meant the young storm-caller died next, and the old candle-seller, and everyone in this valley she had once helped bury alive.

The hollow-born loomed over her. Up close it had no face at all — just a suggestion of a skull pressed outward from the inside of the darkness, like a memory of a person rendered by someone who hated them. It leaned toward her wound. Toward the blood. Toward—

"Hey."

The young one. Standing over Serah, arms slightly out from their sides, breathing hard. Terrified — she could see it in every line of them — and refusing, with visible effort of will, to move.

"Leave them alone," Xanthe Oyelaran said — and Serah knew, knew with the certainty of sealed records and iron locks, exactly who this was, exactly what slept in this person's blood — "or whatever you are, I swear I will—"

The second hollow-born took them by the shoulder.

What happened next, Serah would replay for years, searching it for meaning. The creature's touch should have killed. It had killed forty knights at Caelwatch in under an hour. But the moment unlight met storm-caller's flesh, the air *detonated* — not fire, not force, but pressure, a thunderclap with no lightning, a shockwave that flattened the nearest stalls and threw both creatures backward like leaves in a gale. Canvas ripped free and cartwheeled into the sky. The fountain's water leapt from the basin in a silver sheet. And Xanthe Oyelaran stood unmoved at the center of it, hair whipping, eyes gone white as the heart of a storm cloud, with arcs of pale blue lightning crawling up their forearms like living things greeting a long-imprisoned friend.

For three seconds, the square belonged to the storm.

Then it guttered. The lightning sank back beneath the skin. Xanthe swayed, blinked, and looked down at their own hands as though they belonged to a stranger — and Serah saw the exact moment terror arrived, the moment the wonder curdled. The third hollow-born was already retreating, dragging its wounded kin into the gray lane-shadow, and the square was silent except for dripping water and a distant, fading screaming.

Xanthe turned to Serah. White-eyed no longer; now just frightened, furious, human.

"What was that?" they demanded. "What did *I* just— what are those things, why did they—"

"Your name," Serah said. She got a knee under herself, water streaming off her armor, and looked up at the storm-caller whose exile she had helped forge and whose name she had carried for twenty years like a stone in her boot. "Is it Oyelaran?"

Something flickered across their face — suspicion, recognition, fear.

"How do you know my name?"

"Because I've read it," Serah said, "in records sealed before you were old enough to remember being taken across a border. Because I've spent half my life knowing it and not knowing why." She hauled herself upright, and the movement tore at her side, and she let the pain sharpen her because dullness was death now. "My name is Serah Vane, of the Border Order, and the Border Order is dead — every fort, every knight, all of it gone in one night. The wards that kept this valley closed for twenty years fell with them, and the things you just fought are the least of what's coming through."

Around them the square lay in wreckage — shredded canvas, scattered fruit, a smear of frost on stones that should have been warm. Survivors peered from doorways and windows, drawn by silence, repelled by the sight of her.

"So you'll want to hate me," Serah went on, "and you'd be right to, and I'll tell you everything eventually, I swear it on the star that used to hang over this valley. But right now, in the next hour, before the rest of the dark finds this place — you and I need to talk about the Oathstone. And you need to decide whether you're coming with me."

Xanthe stared at her. At the blood soaking her side, at the dead knight's order stitched on her chest, at their own hands, which had not quite stopped trembling.

"The Oathstone," they repeated slowly. "Why does everyone act like I'm supposed to know what that is?"

"Because," Serah said, and the truth of it went through her like the wards' last note, breaking, "you were brought here so you'd never have to."

Behind them, far up in the mountains, something howled — long, layered, many-throated, the sound of the dark discovering an open door. Xanthe flinched at it. Serah did not. She had been listening to that sound, in nightmares, for twenty years.

"We have until nightfall," she said quietly. "Maybe less. Decide fast."
