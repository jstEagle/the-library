## 3. Exile, Remembered

The dark came down the mountain road like water finding a channel.

Xanthe ran with the rest of them — the whole valley spilled out of its houses and into the night, lanterns swinging, children crying, old men shouting names into the smoke. Above them there was nothing where the sky should have been. Not cloud-cover, not storm-black, but an absence so complete it made Xanthe's eyes water to look at it, as though the darkness itself had weight and pressed down on the world like a thumb on a bruise.

*Keep moving,* someone was saying. *Keep moving, don't look up.* It might have been their aunt Maren. It might have been no one. The valley's one road out ran north through the terraced orchards, and the whole population of a place that had never needed gates now funneled through it in a single terrified river.

Xanthe did not keep moving.

They stopped at the bend where the road overlooked the valley floor, where they had stood a hundred times to watch the Founding Festival lanterns rise, and they looked back at what had been the only home they could remember. The wards had shattered hours ago — had shattered in Xanthe's own chest, that was the terrible part, like something inside them had cracked in sympathy with the sky — and now the dark poured over the southern ridge in a slow tide, swallowing the barley fields, the millpond, the bell tower of the low temple. Where it passed, the lantern-light didn't flicker and die. It simply stopped, as if it had never been lit.

And beneath the terror, beneath the grief, something else was waking.

It had stirred first when the stars went out — a heat behind the sternum, a pressure in the teeth, a sound like distant thunder heard through the soles of the feet. Twenty years the valley's wards had pressed Xanthe's magic flat, had smothered it before it could ever learn its own shape, and now that pressure was gone and the thing beneath was uncoiling, vast and unfamiliar, stretching into the space the wards had left like a body remembering how to breathe.

*Storm-caller,* the word whispered in Xanthe's blood. They had never been allowed to speak it aloud. They had only ever been told what they were *not*: not a farmer, not a weaver, not a person who belonged.

"Xanthe!" Aunt Maren's hand closed hard around their wrist, yanking them back toward the road. Her knuckles were white, her gray braid half-collapsed, her eyes red-rimmed in the lantern light. "Whatever you're looking at, it will still be there when we're safe. Come *on*."

But Xanthe wasn't looking at the dark. They were looking at the old boundary stones along the roadside — the moss-covered markers that ringed the entire valley, that every child learned were sacred, that no one was permitted to pass without the wardens' blessing. And in the new dark, for the first time in twenty years, the faint blue glow that lived in those stones was gone. Dead. Like the stars. Like the wards.

Like something else had died tonight too, some story Xanthe had believed all their life.

Because here was the thing Xanthe had not told anyone, the thing they had barely admitted to themselves while the market square filled with a wounded knight and impossible words: when the wards broke, they hadn't only felt magic wake in their chest. They had felt *memory* break loose with it — fragments rising like silt shaken from a riverbed, sudden and vivid and wrong.

Fragments of a night twenty years gone. Fragments of the border.

And none of them matched the story they'd been told.

* * *

The story everyone in the valley knew went like this: Xanthe Oyelaran had come to the Veiled Valley at age four, carried across the southern pass by wardens during the last years of the Border Wars. Their parents — storm-callers both, tainted by sky-magic, sworn enemies of the settled realms — had abandoned them at the boundary stones rather than see the child corrupted further. The valley had taken the orphan in out of mercy. The wards had kept them safe from their own inheritance. This was said to Xanthe gently, when it was said at all, usually by people who then changed the subject.

Xanthe had built a life on top of that story the way you build a house on a filled-in well. You didn't dig. You didn't listen for echoing.

But the fragments surfacing now were not abandonment. They were nothing like abandonment.

There was rain — that came first, always first, rain hammering on a hood, rain silvering in lantern light, the smell of wet stone and ozone. There were arms around Xanthe, holding tight, too tight, and a heartbeat under their ear going fast as a bird's. There was a voice above them, low and urgent, speaking not to Xanthe but to someone else: *"Not the south gate. The warden checks the south gate. Take the goat path."*

There was a woman's face, close, half-hidden by the hood's shadow, and eyes the color of the sky just before hail. She was crying. She was crying and she was smiling at the same time, and she pressed her mouth to Xanthe's hair and said words that the fragment delivered whole and merciless: *"You are not being left. Do you hear me? Whatever they tell you, whatever name they give this night — you are not being left."*

And there was the stone.

That was the fragment that made Xanthe stumble on the mountain road, twenty years later, so badly that Aunt Maren had to catch their elbow. In the memory, a small fist — their own small fist, four years old and stubborn — was clenched around a shard of pale stone that glowed faintly blue, the same blue as the boundary stones, the same blue the wards must once have been. A man in Border Order grays was trying to pry it away. The hail-eyed woman was arguing with him, voice dropping to that urgent whisper again: *"He swore it would stay with them. He swore—"*

And then the shard was gone, pulled from a child's grip by adult fingers, and the child was screaming, and the screaming was the last thing before the fragment tore—

"Xanthe." Aunt Maren's face swam close, weathered and frightened. "You've gone gray as the road mist. What is it?"

"I remembered," Xanthe said. Their voice came out strange, flattened. "I remembered the night I came here."

Maren went still. Around them the exodus flowed past, a hundred anxious faces lit by swinging lanterns, but for a moment she and Xanthe stood in a pocket of stillness, and Xanthe watched something complicated move behind their aunt's eyes — not confusion. Not surprise.

Recognition.

"You were a child," Maren said carefully. "Children misremember."

"The woman had hail-colored eyes. She was crying. She said I wasn't being left." Xanthe's hands had begun, without their permission, to tremble — or perhaps it wasn't the hands. The air around them had grown heavy, ionized, tasting of lightning about to fall. "Aunt Maren. The story says my parents abandoned me at the south gate. But there was no gate in what I saw. There was a goat path, and a stolen moment, and a man in Border Order colors taking a stone out of my hands like he was stealing it."

The pressure in the air thickened. Somewhere behind them, in the valley being eaten by the dark, thunder rolled — and there were no clouds left to roll it. The sound came from Xanthe's own ribs, and several fleeing neighbors turned to stare.

Maren looked at the sky-that-wasn't, then at Xanthe, and something in her shoulders dropped — surrender, or exhaustion, or the specific weariness of a woman who has kept a secret for two decades and feels it finally coming home to roost.

"Not here," she said quietly. "Not on the road. When we make the high caves, if we make the high caves, I'll tell you what I know. Which is less than you'll want and more than I ever wanted to hold." Her grip tightened on Xanthe's wrist. "But I'll say one thing now, because you deserve one true thing tonight: your mother put you in my sister's arms herself, the night before she went back down the mountain. However the histories dress it, no one left you, child. You were *placed*. There's a difference, and your whole life has been built on pretending there isn't."

Then the dark crested the orchard terraces below them with a sound like a held breath released, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

They ran.

The high caves had been cut into the northern cliffs generations ago, refuges against the wars that had raged before the valley veiled itself, and the exodus packed into them like seeds into a pod: farmers and weavers and wardens, goats and grandmothers, a dozen squalling infants wrapped in every blanket the valley owned. Lanterns were hooded. Voices dropped to whispers. At the cave mouths, watchers crouched with bows strung, staring down at the valley floor as the starless tide completed its slow, patient meal.

From the deepest gallery, Xanthe watched through a crack in the rock and felt their magic turn over and over in their chest like a restless animal pacing a cage that no longer existed. Every few minutes the air around them thickened; the hair on their arms rose; someone nearby muttered about storms and moved away. They couldn't control it. They had never been taught to control it because they had never been permitted to *have* it, and now it rose and fell with their pulse, answering every spike of fear and grief with pressure and static and the far-off mutter of thunder in a cloudless dark.

*You are not being left.*

Their mother's voice — because surely, surely it had been their mother, hail-eyed like the stories said the Oyelaran line ran, a cadence that fit against Xanthe's oldest memories like a key in a lock. *Whatever they tell you, whatever name they give this night.*

What name had been given to that night? Exile. Banishment. *Cast out for your own good*, the wardens said, *saved from the corruption of sky-magic*. And Xanthe had swallowed it, because a child cannot argue with a whole valley, and because the alternative — that everything they'd been told about themselves was stitched together from lies — was a door too heavy to open alone.

But the wards were broken now. And the door stood open whether Xanthe wanted it or not.

They turned the fragments over and over like stones in a hand. The goat path. The warden checking the south gate — which meant someone was evading the wardens, not delivering a child *to* them. The Border Order man taking the glowing shard. *He swore it would stay with them.* Swore who? Swore what? A stone that glowed like the boundary stones, given to a four-year-old and then snatched away by soldiers —

— and Xanthe's breath caught, because a thought arrived whole, fully formed, terrible in its symmetry:

The wards hadn't suppressed their magic all these years. Or not only. The shards of memory, the way the fragments had broken free *exactly* when the wards fell, the blue-glowing stone pried from a child's fist — what if the wards had also been holding the door shut on a room inside Xanthe's own head? What if exile wasn't a place they'd been sent to?

What if it was a thing that had been done to them, and the forgetting was part of it?

The thought made the magic surge so hard that the lantern nearest them guttered, and a woman three feet away flinched and made a sign against ill luck, and Xanthe pressed their palms flat against the cold cave wall and breathed until the thunder under their skin subsided to a growl.

*I'm sorry,* they thought — to the wall, to the valley, to whoever. To the mother with hail-colored eyes. *I'm trying not to burn anything down. I'm trying to only burn the lies.*

* * *

Near dawn — if it could be called dawn, with no sun and no stars, only a slow graying of the absolute dark that suggested the world still remembered how mornings worked — the watchers began calling softly from the cave mouths, and the camp stirred with a fear that had found a new shape.

Riders on the north road. Three of them, moving fast. Not refugees — refugees came in rivers, not spearheads.

Xanthe pushed forward with the others to the crack in the rock, and Aunt Maren crowded close, and together they watched the riders climb the switchbacks toward the caves: a tall figure in scorched Border Order grays riding lead with a sword wrapped at her saddlebow, and behind her two more in mismatched armor, and even at this distance Xanthe recognized the woman from the market square — the knight with the wound in her side and the sealed-record eyes, the one who had spoken Xanthe's surname like it meant something. Vane. Serah Vane.

She reined in below the cave mouth and called up, and her voice carried in the dead air with unnatural clarity, as if the whole soundless world conspired to let it be heard.

"People of the valley! The dark took the southern ridge in the night. It will take the north by tomorrow. There is no holding here — the wards are gone, and the wards were the only wall this valley ever had." A pause. Wind that wasn't wind moved through the grass below, hissing. "I rode to the border citadel. I rode through what it sent. And I'm telling you what I told your wardens: there is a place this can still be fought, and there is exactly one person in this valley who can fight it. You know who I mean. The wards are down. Everyone has always known why they were really raised."

The cave went very quiet. A hundred hooded lanterns, a hundred held breaths, and Xanthe felt every gaze slide toward them by degrees, the way water finds a low place — the storm-caller, the exile, the child placed among them twenty years ago, the reason half of them had crossed the road to avoid for two decades. Aunt Maren's hand found Xanthe's shoulder, whether to hold them back or steady them, even Maren probably didn't know.

And Xanthe stood at the crack in the rock, looking down at the knight, and understood with a strange floating clarity that home had stopped being an answer sometime in the night. The valley was being eaten. The story of their exile had rotted through like a beam under plaster. The mother's voice, the goat path, the stolen stone — every load-bearing truth had failed at once, and what remained was a question where a life used to be:

*If I was never abandoned, what was I? And what was taken from me to make me forget?*

The magic answered before they could — a low roll of thunder from a clear black sky, rolling down the cliff face, rattling the cave mouths, making the knight's horse dance sideways. Serah Vane looked up directly at the crack in the rock, directly at Xanthe, as if the thunder had been an introduction.

"There's your answer," the knight called up, and there was something almost gentle in it. "It knows the road already. The only question left is whether you walk it before the dark does."

Xanthe looked back once — at Aunt Maren, at the hooded lanterns, at the remnant of a home that had never quite been one — and found, terrifyingly, that they were already grieving it as something behind them.

They climbed down to the road.

The knight turned her horse north without another word, and Xanthe followed on foot into the starless dark, toward the border, toward whatever version of themselves was waiting on the other side of the veil — carrying nothing but a question, a quarter-remembered theft, and the growing, electrified certainty that somewhere ahead, someone was still holding the stone that had been pried from their four-year-old fist.

And that they wanted it back.
