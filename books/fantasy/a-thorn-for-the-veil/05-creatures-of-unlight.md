## 5. Creatures of Unlight

They walked through the dead borderlands for two days before the dark learned their names.

Xanthe felt it first as a change in the quality of the silence — not the absence of sound but its *attention*, as though the land between realms had stopped breathing and begun to listen. The road beneath their boots was old imperial flagstone, cracked and heaved by roots that had died decades ago, and every stone seemed to hold a question they couldn't answer. Serah walked ahead, sword drawn even in daylight, because daylight here was a rumor. The sky was the color of ash stirred into milk, and it had been that color since the stars went out.

"You keep looking over your shoulder," Serah said without turning.

"I keep feeling like something's reading me."

"Then stop being so interesting."

It was almost humor. Almost. Serah Vane rationed warmth the way a besieged city rations grain, and Xanthe had learned in two days of walking to treasure each crumb and suspect each one. The knight moved like a woman carrying a house on her back — everything load-bearing, nothing wasted. Her wound from the market square had been bound twice with linen torn from her own cloak, and she never once mentioned it, though Xanthe had seen the way her breath caught when she reached too far left.

The borderlands had been beautiful once. You could still read it in the bones of things: an orchard gone to silver-gray skeletons, a well with a carved archway where someone had loved someone enough to carve interlaced hands into the stone. Now the gray had gotten *into* everything — not dust exactly, not rot, but a leaching, as if color itself had been drunk down to the dregs. Even the moss was gray. Even the memory of green.

"The hollow-born hunt at dusk," Serah said. "And dawn. And any hour between, if they're hungry enough. They don't eat flesh."

"What do they eat?"

"Light. Warmth. Meaning." She glanced back, and her eyes — pale as winter water — held his for a moment longer than comfort allowed. "A man caught by them doesn't die screaming. He dies quiet. He forgets his own name mid-sentence and then he forgets he ever had one, and then there's just a body walking around wearing his face until it falls over."

"That's cheerful."

"It's true. Cheerful is a luxury I gave up at the border."

Xanthe hugged their arms tighter around themselves and thought about home — the valley, the market square with its awnings of dyed wool, the smell of honey-bread on Founding Festival mornings, the wards humming overhead like a mother's off-key lullaby. Twenty years under that hum. Twenty years of knowing themselves to be the valley's one broken instrument, the storm-child whose magic the wards pressed flat as flowers in a book. They had hated the wards. They had hated the flatness inside them like a held note never released.

Now the wards were shattered glass in the sky, and Xanthe would have given anything to feel that flatness again. Because underneath it — now that nothing suppressed it — something vast had begun to turn over in its sleep. He could feel it in his fingertips, in the fillings of his teeth, in the space behind his eyes: pressure, potential, weather waiting for permission.

"Serah. What am I? Really. Not what the records say. What *am* I?"

Serah walked four more paces before answering. "A storm-caller. Sky-blooded. There used to be hundreds of your kind. Then the covenant, and after the covenant—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "There were very few."

"And the ones that were left?"

"Were hidden." Her jaw worked. "Or exiled. Depending on which story you were raised on."

"I was raised on exile."

"I know," Serah said, and said nothing else, and the road swallowed the conversation whole.

* * *

Dusk came like a lid closing.

One moment the gray light was merely miserable; the next it was gone, and the world beyond the ruined orchard became a country of outlines. Serah made camp in the shell of a wayshrine — four standing stones and half a roof, carved with the same interlaced-hands motif, worn nearly smooth. She built no fire. "Fire is a beacon," she murmured. "Cold is a stranger." So they sat wrapped in cloaks, backs against opposite pillars, sharing dried meat and a silence that had grown, if not comfortable, at least familiar in its shape.

Xanthe was learning the geography of Serah's silences. There was the tired silence, the calculating silence, the silence of a woman editing herself in real time. This one was new: a listening silence. Her head was tilted. Her hand rested on her sword's hilt the way other people rest a hand on a lover's shoulder.

"Serah—"

"Don't finish the sentence. Breathe shallow. Listen."

He listened. At first: wind through dead branches, the tick of cooling stone, his own pulse. Then, beneath all of it, a sound he had no name for — a soft, wet dragging, like cloth soaked in river water pulled across flagstone. Many of them. Circling.

"How many?" he whispered.

"More than my sword has opinions about."

The darkness at the orchard's edge began to move.

Not figures approaching — figures *unhappening*. That was the horror of the hollow-born: the eye kept trying to see them and kept failing, sliding off like rain off oiled leather. They were tall, taller than men, and wrong in the specific way that a reflection is wrong when the mirror is bent. Where faces should have been there were suggestions of faces, smudges, as if someone had drawn people and then regretted it. They carried no light, made no shadow, and the space they occupied seemed colder than the night around it — cold you could *hear*, a fine crystalline ringing at the edge of perception.

"Hollow-born," Serah breathed, rising. "Behind me, Xanthe. Behind me, *now*."

He went behind her. It was instinct, cowardice, sense — whatever you called it, he put a pillar and a knight between himself and the unhappening shapes, and his heart slammed so hard against his ribs he was certain the creatures could hear it, count it, *want* it.

Seven of them. Eight. The count wouldn't hold still; they flowed past each other like ink in water.

"They shouldn't be this far south," Serah said. Her voice was steady in the way of a plank bridge that might hold or might not. "This is patrol-depth territory. Something is driving them."

Something was driving them, and Xanthe knew what it was one heartbeat before the knowledge arrived, because the nearest creature turned its smudge of a face directly toward him — past Serah, past the sword, past every rational target — and *reached*. Its arm elongated. Its fingers unfolded like a paper lantern opening, and where they pointed, Xanthe felt a pulling, a suction centered on his own chest, as if something inside him were being poured out through a straw.

"It's me," he said stupidly. "They want—it's *me*—"

"Dormant blood," Serah snarled, mostly to herself. "Of course. Of *course*. They can smell what the wards used to hide—" And then the first one lunged, and there was no more time for talk.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have fronts and lines and some pretense of fairness. This was a knight performing arithmetic with a blade while the numbers kept multiplying. Serah fought beautifully — that word again, *beautifully*, because there was no other word for a body that knew violence as fluently as language — but eight against one in the dark, wounded, protecting a civilian, was not a fight anyone won. She took the first one's reaching arm off at the joint; it did not bleed, it simply *ended*, dissolving into a scatter of gray motes. The second got inside her guard and laid fingers against her pauldron, and where they touched, the metal frosted and the frost spread, and Serah staggered back with a gasp like a woman surfacing from ice water.

"Run," she said. Actually said it. "Xanthe. *Run.*"

He didn't. Later, he would try to explain this to himself — the not-running — and the only honest answer was that running required somewhere to run *to*, and every direction was the same direction, and the thing inside his chest that the creature was drinking from had begun, absurdly, horribly, to answer the drinking with a pull of its own. Like recognizing like. Like a struck bell leaning toward the hand that silenced it.

Two of them flanked the shrine. One came through the roof gap, unfolding downward like a spider descending on silk. Xanthe fell backward over a fallen lintel, palms slapping cold stone, and looked up into a face that wasn't a face, and felt it *drink*.

It was not pain. That was the worst part. It was subtraction. Memories didn't vanish; they dimmed, like lamps turned low in rooms he was leaving forever. The smell of honey-bread flickered. His mother's voice — the few syllables of it he owned — guttered. He grabbed for them the way a drowning man grabs for driftwood and came up with splinters, and the creature leaned closer, patient, hungry, and the gray in the world deepened toward black, and Xanthe Oyelaran understood that he was going to die quiet, forgetting his own name mid-sentence—

No.

The word rose out of him from a place deeper than language. Deeper than the valley, deeper than the exile story, older than the wards. It was not *no* as refusal. It was *no* as thunderhead. It was twenty years of held note finally, catastrophically released.

The storm woke.

For three seconds — he would count them forever, three heartbeats, three hammerfalls — Xanthe was not a person. He was weather given a name and a grievance.

The first second was light. Not the gray, dying light of the borderlands but white-blue, branching, alive — lightning with nowhere to go except outward, and outward it went, a sphere of shrieking brilliance detonating from his chest like a second sun. The hollow-born above him didn't scream; they *unmade*, unraveling into ash and static, their stolen shapes burning away like fog before flame.

The second second was sound. Thunder answered the light, rolling out across the dead orchard, and the ground itself jumped — he felt it through his spine, through his teeth, the deep tectonic shudder of a world slapped awake. Dead trees split. The standing stones of the wayshrine rang like bells, and the carved interlaced hands glowed briefly, briefly, as if the old covenant recognized its own.

The third second was the wind. It came spiraling inward and then blasted outward in a ring that flattened grass that had been gray for thirty years, that tore the remaining roof from the shrine and hurled it spinning into the dark, that picked Serah up bodily and set her down ten feet away like a chess piece moved by a careless god. In the ring's outer edge, the last of the creatures fled — actually *fled*, the unlight pouring away southward faster than anything that shapeless should move, leaving a wake of returning, trembling, ordinary darkness.

And then it was over.

Three seconds. Xanthe lay on his back in the wreckage of the wayshrine, staring up at the starless sky, and felt the storm fold itself back down into the cellar of his bones — not gone, never gone now, but coiled, *awake*, pressing against walls that no longer existed and had somehow rebuilt themselves out of habit alone. His whole body shook. His mouth tasted of copper and ozone. Every hair on his arms stood upright, and small blue sparks crawled between his fingers when he flexed them, dying one by one like fireflies at dawn.

He laughed. He couldn't help it. Terror and exhilaration had married somewhere in those three seconds and produced this: laughter, wild and cracked, echoing off the ringing stones.

"I felt it," he said to the sky, to the dark, to himself. "I *felt* it—"

"Xanthe."

Serah stood at the edge of the blast ring, and her voice stopped his laughter like a hand around a throat. She was limping. Frost still clung to one pauldron. But none of that mattered, because of how she was looking at him — not with wonder, not with relief, but with something older and sadder and infinitely more afraid.

She looked at him the way you look at a door you helped lock, now swinging open.

"Your eyes," she said quietly. "When it happened. They weren't yours. They were—" She cut herself off. Shook her head. "Are you hurt?"

"No. Yes. I don't know." He sat up. The sparks had stopped; he already missed them, and that frightened him more than the hollow-born had. "Serah. What did you mean, *they weren't yours*? Whose were they?"

"Later." She crossed the ring, offered her hand — the uninjured one — and pulled him up. Her grip was strong and shaking, both at once. "We can't stay here. They ran, but running isn't the same as giving up. And they'll report what they found. Whatever commands them will want to know that a storm-caller lives, and where, and how strong."

"How strong?" Xanthe looked down at his own hands, ordinary again, human again, and felt the coiled thing shift beneath his ribs like a sleeper turning. Three seconds had flattened an orchard. He thought about the wards — twenty years of them — built specifically to press *this* flat. "Serah. How strong am I?"

She didn't answer immediately. She retrieved her sword from where the wind had thrown it, sheathed it, and stood a moment with her head bowed, and Xanthe watched the truth and the lie wrestle openly on her face — watched, for the first time, the lie begin to lose.

"Strong enough," she said at last, "that twenty years ago, very powerful people decided the safest place for you was a cage with your name on it. Strong enough that they'll come for you now. All of them. Every realm, every faction, every hunger." She met his eyes. "And strong enough that I'm no longer certain I can protect you. From them."

"From them," Xanthe repeated slowly. "You said that strangely. As if there's another 'from' hiding behind it."

Serah turned away, gathered her bedroll from the ruins, and slung it across her shoulders with a wince she pretended she hadn't made.

"We walk north tonight," she said. "Toward Vaelmoor. Toward answers. Sleep when we're moving."

And Xanthe followed her out of the shattered shrine, into the starless dark, hands still faintly crackling — and told himself the chill crawling up his neck was only the night air, and not the certainty that the most dangerous thing hunting him had just spent the last hour walking beside him.
