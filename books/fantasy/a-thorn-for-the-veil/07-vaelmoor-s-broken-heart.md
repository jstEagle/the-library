## 7. Vaelmoor's Broken Heart

The citadel announced itself long before we saw it.

We had walked three days through the borderlands, and the land had grown quieter with every mile — not peaceful quiet, but the held-breath kind, as though the hills themselves were listening. The grass came up gray at the roots. Streams ran clear but tasted of nothing, water without memory of stone or moss. And always, on the horizon behind us, that absence where stars should be, pressing down like a hand on the back of the neck.

Then, on the morning of the fourth day, Serah stopped on a ridge of black rock and said, "There."

I felt it before I saw it: a hum in my teeth, in the marrow of me, low and rhythmic as a second heartbeat. My storm-magic — still new, still terrifying, still mine only in stolen bursts — stirred beneath my skin like a dog hearing its name.

Vaelmoor rose out of a shallow valley ahead of us, and it was wrong in a way I couldn't immediately name. It took me a dozen heartbeats to understand: the ruin was too clean. No ivy strangled its towers. No birds nested in its broken arches. Even the wind seemed to pass around it rather than through, as if the citadel existed slightly apart from the world, a wound the landscape was careful not to touch.

"It's been dead for twenty years," Serah said quietly. "But it doesn't look dead. It looks *waiting*."

She was right. That was the worst of it. Vaelmoor did not look like something that had fallen. It looked like something that had paused.

We descended in silence. The road to the gates was paved with white stone gone yellow at the seams, and each step made the hum in my bones deepen, shift, resolve into something almost like language heard through a wall. My hands ached. Not the ache of cold or fatigue — an ache with direction, pulling forward, toward the heart of the ruin.

"You feel it," Serah said. It wasn't quite a question. She'd been watching me more closely these last days, cataloguing me the way she catalogued everything — terrain, weather, exits. "The stone."

"I don't know what I feel." That was true, and it wasn't. What I felt was recognition, and recognition frightened me worse than the hollow-born ever had. "It's like walking into a house I've never been in and knowing which door sticks."

Serah said nothing to that. But her hand found the hilt of her sword, and stayed there, the way it did when she was afraid and would not say so.

* * *

The gates of Vaelmoor had been bronze once. Now they hung from one hinge, peeled open like the petals of some enormous metal flower, and we passed between them into a courtyard where the flagstones were scored with channels — deliberate grooves, cut in patterns too regular for erosion. Water had run through them once. Or something else.

"Conduits," Serah murmured, crouching to trace one with a gloved finger. "For the covenant's working. When the Oathstone was whole, power moved through this place like blood through a body."

"And when it broke?"

She stood, brushing off her knee, and looked at the empty channels with an expression I couldn't read. "Then the body died. Come on. The sanctum is below."

The interior corridors swallowed our footsteps. Everything was pale stone and high broken windows, and the light that fell through them fell strangely — slanting where it shouldn't, pooling in places no window could reach. Twice I caught movement at the edge of my vision and twice it was only dust sifting down through shafts of gold. But both times my magic flared against my ribs like a startled bird, and both times it took me a moment to breathe past it.

The stair down to the sanctum was wide enough for ten abreast, carved in a spiral, and the deeper we went the louder the hum grew, until it wasn't a hum anymore but a chord — many voices braided together, singing just below the threshold of hearing. I pressed my palm against the wall to steady myself and the wall was warm.

"Xanthe." Serah's voice came sharp. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're—" She hesitated. "Storm-colored. Grey going silver at the edges. Like the sky before lightning."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, breathless. "That's fitting."

At the bottom of the stair stood a pair of doors taller than two men, black wood banded in metal gone green, and set into them at eye height was a seal: a circle bisected by a jagged line. A whole thing, split. I knew what it depicted before Serah told me.

"The Oathstone," she said. "Or its sigil. The covenant was sworn here, in front of this door, by the founders of all four realms. Sky and earth, bound together, so that neither could devour the other."

"And now?"

"Now the doors are open." She pushed, and they swung inward without a sound, which somehow frightened me more than any shriek of hinges could have.

* * *

The sanctum was a perfect circle, domed, lit by no torch and no window — the light simply was there, sourceless, the color of overcast noon. And in its center, on a plinth of the same warm white stone, lay half of something vast.

I had expected a stone. Some relic, some carved idol the size of a fist or a skull. What waited on the plinth was larger than I am tall — a fragment of crystal shot through with veins of silver light, sheared off along one face so cleanly the break gleamed like a mirror. It pulsed. Slowly, steadily, in time with the chord that filled the chamber, in time — I realized with a lurch — with my own heartbeat.

"That's the Oathstone?" My voice sounded small in all that space.

"Half of it." Serah kept her distance from the plinth, arms crossed tight, as though the thing might notice her. "When the wards fell, the covenant broke. This is what remains here. The other half—"

"Is missing." I stepped closer. The warmth I'd felt in the walls was stronger here, almost feverish, radiating off the crystal in waves I could feel on my face. "Someone took it."

"No one *took* it." For the first time since I'd known her, Serah Vane sounded uncertain. "The records say it shattered when the covenant failed. Half remained bonded to the sanctum. The other half..." She trailed off. Her jaw tightened. "The other half's fate isn't in the records I had access to."

I barely heard her. Because I had reached the plinth, and the moment my shadow fell across the fractured face of the stone, the voices resolved.

Not a chord anymore. Words.

They came up out of the fracture like heat shimmer given sound — layered, overlapping, dozens of voices speaking at once and yet somehow distinct, the way you can pick a single instrument out of an orchestra. They weren't speaking to me. They were speaking *of*, reciting, remembering. Memories. The stone was full of memories, stored the way amber stores insects, and my presence had woken them.

"—sworn under the third moon, the binding holds, the binding holds, the binding—"

"—the sky-children will not suffer, we swore it, we swore—"

"—and the earth remembers what the sky forgets, and the sky forgets what the earth—"

I gripped the edge of the plinth. The stone was warm as skin. "Serah," I whispered. "It's talking."

"I hear it." She had come closer despite herself, drawn the way I was drawn, and her face in the sourceless light was pale. "Gods. Twenty years I served the Order, and they told us the Oathstone was inert. A relic. A *rock*."

"The founders' voices," I breathed. "It keeps them. Keeps everything sworn upon it, maybe. Every oath, every memory—"

And then, beneath the layered chorus, another voice surfaced.

It was quieter than the rest. Older, worn soft at the edges like a letter read a thousand times. It didn't chant with the others; it drifted through them, half-lost, saying things that weren't oaths at all:

"*Little storm. Little storm, the rain is coming, can you feel it coming? Hold my hand and don't let go, whatever happens, hold my—*"

The world tilted.

I knew that voice.

I had spent twenty years not letting myself know that voice — burying it under exile and wards and the flat grey years of the valley, telling myself that grief fades, that memory blurs, that a child of five cannot be trusted to remember. But I knew it. The cadence of it, the way it lifted at the ends of words, the way it softened on *little storm* — a name no one else had ever called me, a name I had thought was invented by a child's longing because surely, *surely*, no mother would leave her child in a stranger's cart at a border crossing and walk away—

"*—let go, whatever happens, hold my hand, little storm, the rain is coming—*"

"Xanthe." Serah's hand closed on my shoulder. "Xanthe, look at me."

I couldn't. I was staring at the fracture, at the mirror-bright break in the crystal, and in it I could see my own reflection — grey-silver eyes, wide, wet — and behind my reflection, faint as breath on glass, something else. A shape. A woman's shape, dark-haired, one hand raised as though reaching through the stone itself.

"My mother," I said. The words came out of me like something being torn. "Serah. That's my mother's voice. In the stone. How is my mother's voice in the *stone*?"

Silence behind me. The particular, terrible silence of someone deciding whether to lie.

"Xanthe—"

"She was a storm-caller." I turned. My reflection's eyes went with me, silver-edged. "That's the story. That's why we were exiled — my blood, my father's line, dangerous magic, sealed away for everyone's protection. Everyone in the valley knows the story. But storm-callers don't put their voices in stones, Serah. Storm-callers call storms. So how does my mother's voice live inside the Oathstone?"

Serah's face had gone very still. Stillness, I had learned, was what she wore instead of answers.

"Tell me you've never heard that voice before," I said. "Tell me you don't recognize it. Because you went stiff when it started speaking. I watched you go stiff."

"Xanthe, please—"

"*How do you know my mother?*"

The question cracked out of me with a crackle of static, and every vein of silver light in the Oathstone flared at once, and the whole sanctum shuddered — dust raining from the dome, the sourceless light stuttering like a guttering lamp. Beneath us, deep in the warm stone, the chord of voices surged, and for one instant they were not chanting oaths at all.

For one instant, dozens of voices spoke in unison, clear as a bell struck against the inside of my skull:

"*The storm-caller returns. The half that walks carries the key.*"

Then silence. Real silence, the first since we'd entered.

Serah hadn't let go of my shoulder. Her grip had tightened instead, and when I finally dragged my eyes away from the stone and met hers, I saw something I had never seen in her before — not fear exactly, and not guilt, but the terrible exhaustion of a person standing at the edge of a confession they have been circling for days.

"The half that walks," she repeated softly. "Carries the key."

"Serah. Who has the other half?"

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, whatever she had decided, she had decided it alone, and I understood — with a sinking, sickening certainty — that whatever she was about to tell me, she had already chosen how much of it I would get.

"Not who," she said. "*Who* I can't tell you yet. But I'll tell you this much, because you deserve it and because the stone won't stay quiet now that you've woken it." She drew a breath. "The other half of the Oathstone didn't shatter. It was carried north, twenty years ago, by a knight of the Border Order. By someone the Order trusted above almost anyone alive."

The voices in the stone had gone still, but I could feel them listening. Waiting. And somewhere beneath the listening, faint and patient, my mother's cadence drifted through the layered dark like a thread of rain:

"*Hold my hand, little storm. Whatever happens—*"

"Who, Serah?" I asked again, quieter now. "Who carried it?"

Her jaw worked once. Twice. And then she said a name I had never heard before, but which I would carry from that day forward like a splinter under the skin:

"Aldric Vey."
