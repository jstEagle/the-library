## 8. The Half That Walks

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Xanthe finally slept.

Serah sat with her back against a fallen pillar, the ruined road to Vaelmoor stretching away behind them into darkness that no longer felt like ordinary night. Since the stars had gone out, darkness had weight. It pressed against the edges of their little camp like something breathing, patient, curious. She kept her sword across her knees and watched the boy — not a boy anymore, she reminded herself; twenty years old and carrying more storm than any living thing should hold — sleep in the fitful way of people whose dreams have been colonized by dread.

Xanthe's hands twitched in sleep. Even unconscious, they seemed to be listening for thunder.

*You were sent to watch them,* said the voice in her memory, her old commander's voice, dry as ash. *Not to guard them. To watch. There is a difference, Serah, and you will learn it before this is over.*

She had learned it. She was learning it still.

The wound in her side had stopped bleeding sometime after midnight, which she chose to interpret as good news rather than as the hollow-born's venom simply finishing its work. She peeled back the bandage by firelight. The flesh around the gash was gray at the edges, cold to the touch, like skin remembering winter. Not spreading. Not yet. But not healing either.

She rewrapped it without waking anyone and told herself, as she had been telling herself for three days, that there would be time to deal with it later. There was always later. Later was the lie that let soldiers keep walking.

Above the broken horizon, where stars should have been, the sky was a smooth black nothing. She found herself searching it anyway, the way one touches a missing tooth. Twenty years of nights she had stood watch under those stars, cataloguing constellations she'd never admit to loving, and now the sky looked like the inside of a closed eye.

*They took the other half north,* Commander Ilsa Renn had told her, in the refugee camp, in front of the survivors who had once been the pride of the Border Order. *Aldric Vey carried it out himself. On the Order's last sanctioned mission. Your mentor, Serah. The man who taught you to hold a shield line. He's been walking with half a god in his chest for two decades.*

She had known. That was the thing she could never say aloud, the thing sitting in her chest now like a swallowed stone: some part of her had always known. Aldric had left on that final mission when Serah was nineteen, and he had never sent word, never returned, never been declared dead — and the Order had simply... adjusted around his absence, the way water adjusts around a drowned man. No funeral. No memorial. His name struck from the rolls without ceremony, as if striking it from paper could strike it from history.

And she had asked questions exactly once, early, hungry for answers the way only the young are hungry — and then she had been given her posting. The valley. Watch the border. Watch the exiles.

She had thought, for years, that it was punishment for asking.

It had not been punishment. It had been an assignment.

*

Dawn came gray and grudging, the light seeming to fight its way up from below the world rather than pour down from above. They broke camp in silence. Xanthe moved differently since the ambush — since the three seconds of unleashed storm that had torn the hollow-born apart and left scorch-marks on the earth in the shape of a lightning tree. There was a new wariness in how they held their own hands, as if the hands belonged to someone else, someone dangerous.

"You didn't sleep," Xanthe said, falling into step beside her. Their voice was rough with dreams.

"I slept enough."

"Liar."

"Knight," Serah corrected mildly. "We're trained to function on less. It's in the oath." The words were out before she could stop them, and she felt them land wrong — felt Xanthe flinch, small and quick, at the mention of oaths. Of course. The Oathstone. The covenant. Everything Serah said now passed through the filter of what she hadn't yet confessed, and everything she hadn't said grew heavier by the mile.

They walked. The dead borderlands unrolled before them: fields gone to pale grass that crunched like spun sugar, farmhouses with their roofs caved in and their doorways open to the sky, a well with a bucket still tied to its rope, frozen mid-draw for twenty years. The Veiled Valley's exile had not ended at its walls. Everything beyond them had been dying slowly ever since, a land held in the long exhale of a covenant breaking down.

"The stone," Xanthe said, after an hour. "The other half. You knew who took it. Back at the camp — you knew the name before the commander said it."

Serah kept her eyes on the road. "I suspected."

"Don't do that." Xanthe stopped walking. When Serah turned, they were standing very straight, arms crossed against the morning chill, and behind the exhaustion in their face was something harder. "Don't ration the truth to me like bread in a siege. I've spent my whole life being managed. My whole life, Serah. Everyone in that valley knew what I was before I did, and every one of them decided what I got to know and when. I will not walk to Vaelmoor beside another person who decides things for me."

The wind moved through the pale grass, making a sound like distant applause.

"You're right," Serah said.

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — surprise, maybe, at how easily she yielded. She almost laughed. They had no idea how many battles she'd already lost inside herself on this road, how many times the truth had climbed her throat and been forced back down by duty or fear or the simple animal wish to be, for one more day, the person Xanthe thought she was.

"But if I give it to you," she said, "you take all of it. Not the part that makes me useful. All of it. And then you decide what we are to each other. Not me."

Xanthe's arms uncrossed slowly. "All of it."

So Serah told them. Or began to.

*

"My name isn't just Serah Vane of the Border Order," she said. "My name is Serah Vane, Third Watch of the Covenant Guard, sworn warder of the Veiled Valley exile. That posting wasn't a punishment. It was my life's assignment. I was stationed outside your valley for twenty years because the Order wanted eyes on you. On you specifically."

Xanthe went very still. Stillness, Serah was learning, was this one's weapon — the storm-caller's answer to a world that kept trying to provoke them. "On me."

"On your bloodline. Storm-callers don't happen, Xanthe. They're made — or rather, they're remembered. The gift runs in lines, old ones, and the covenant was built partly to track every line that carried it. Your family was one of the tracked. When you were born, the Order knew within the season. When the wards were raised, your family was inside them by design." She heard her own voice go flat, parade-ground flat, the tone she used to carry things too heavy to hold any other way. "I filed reports about you. Twice a year, every year, for two decades. Your height. Your health. Whether you'd shown signs of the gift. Whether anyone in the valley treated you strangely. Whether you seemed... discontented."

"Discontented," Xanthe repeated. The word came out with an edge of wonder, the kind of wonder that precedes rage.

"It sounds monstrous written down. It sounded monstrous spoken, too. I want you to understand that I'm not going to soften it to make myself easier to forgive." Serah made herself meet their eyes. Gray-green, her mother would have called that color; storm-light, she thought now, and hated herself for the poetry. "But you should also understand what I believed. What we all believed. The covenant wasn't presented to us as a cage. It was presented as a dam. Magic like yours, unbound, had cracked the sky once before — that was the founding catastrophe, the reason Vaelmoor exists at all. We were told the wards were mercy. For the realms, and for the storm-callers themselves, who burned out young, who were hunted, who couldn't survive their own gifts. Sealing the valley was supposed to be saving everyone in it. Including you."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the wards fall and the sky stay whole. Now I've read the fracture in the Oathstone and heard what's stored in it. Now I know the text we swore our lives to was rewritten before it was ever sworn." She paused. Here it was. The deepest cut, saved for last because she was a coward in exactly one respect and had always known which one. "And now you should know how I came to be assigned to you at all. It wasn't rotation. It wasn't seniority. I requested the valley posting. Because twenty-two years ago, I helped carry a screaming child across the border at night, and I never learned what happened to that child, and I couldn't stop wondering."

The wind died. Even the pale grass went still, as if the land itself were leaning in.

"That child was me," Xanthe said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"That child was you."

*

They stood on the dead road, ten feet apart, and Serah watched twenty years rearrange themselves behind Xanthe's eyes.

"Tell me about that night," Xanthe said at last. "Not the exile story. The real one. Whatever you remember."

"Not much, and none of it clean. It was classified above my rank even then — I was nineteen, newly sworn, and I was chosen precisely because I was expendable and obedient. A dozen of us. We crossed into the valley under cover of a storm — a real one, called up deliberately, though I didn't know then that storms could be called. There was a house on the valley's far slope. Lights inside. And a woman standing in the doorway holding a bundle, and she was—" Serah's throat tightened around the memory, unexpected, undimmed. Twenty-two years, and the sound still lived in her ears. "She was singing. That's what I remember most. The whole operation was terror and torchlight and men with drawn steel, and this woman stood in her doorway singing to the bundle in her arms like the rest of it wasn't happening. Like she could sing loud enough to drown out the world."

Xanthe's hand had risen unconsciously to their collarbone, pressing against the hollow of their throat. "What was the song?"

"I don't know the words. Low, and slow, and it rose at the end of each line like weather building. I've caught myself humming it for twenty years without knowing why." She hesitated. "There's more, and it's worse. The handover wasn't gentle. Protocol said the child couldn't see the mother's face, couldn't hear her voice again after the transfer — some doctrine about attachment and traceable bonds, as if you were a spy network instead of a baby. So when they took you from her, she fought. Of course she fought. And the squad captain ordered her restrained, and I—"

She stopped.

"You what?" Xanthe's voice was very soft.

"I was the one who restrained her." The words tasted like iron and old snow. "I was the youngest and the fastest and he pointed at me, and I did it, because I was nineteen and obedient and I believed — gods help me, I genuinely believed — that I was saving realms. I held her arms while they carried you away. She wasn't screaming. That's the detail I can't get free of. Everyone else was shouting and you were wailing, and she just went silent, completely silent, looking at me over my own shoulder the entire time. She stopped fighting the moment I had her. As if she understood something about me that I didn't understand myself. As if she was memorizing my face so she could find me later."

"Did she?" Xanthe whispered. "Find you?"

"No. She died eight years ago. Illness, the records say. I checked, once, illegally, through sealed archives. Her name was Ysolde." Serah watched the name land, watched Xanthe's lips move around it silently, tasting a mother they had never been permitted to remember. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry doesn't cover a tenth of it. I'm not asking it to."

*

Xanthe turned away and walked to the edge of the road, and Serah let them go, giving them the distance she had denied everyone else her whole career.

The morning was brightening by increments, the gray lifting toward a color that might once have been blue. Somewhere north, past the horizon's bruised line, Vaelmoor waited with its split stone and its stored voices. Somewhere farther north than that, a man she had loved like a father walked with half a god in his chest, and she would have to face him knowing what she now knew, carrying what she now carried.

Xanthe came back. That was the thing Serah hadn't braced for — she had prepared for fury, for rejection, for the small cold sentence *we're done* — but Xanthe simply came back and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked north the way she was looking.

"You were nineteen," Xanthe said. "And you believed a lie that an entire order of knights was built to make believable. And then you spent twenty years standing in the cold outside my prison, filing reports, hating yourself so thoroughly that you volunteered for the worst posting in the order just to be near the evidence of it." They exhaled slowly. "I'm not forgiving you. I want to be clear about that. Forgiveness is a debt paid, and I don't know yet whether you owe one. Maybe you owed one to my mother, and she collected it by dying quiet. Maybe you owe one to me, and I'll collect it someday in a way neither of us expects. But I'm not sending you away."

"That's more than I deserve."

"Probably." And there — briefly, terribly — Xanthe smiled, and it was their mother's smile, Serah realized, the same shape, the same sudden warmth, and grief went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. "But here's what I need from you now. Aldric Vey. Your mentor. Tell me about him — not the official version. If he has half the Oathstone, and he's been bonded to it for twenty years, then whatever walks north to meet us isn't entirely the man you knew. I need to know who he was, so I can measure what's left."

Serah looked north. The dead lands stretched toward Vaelmoor, and beyond Vaelmoor the drowned country began, where rivers had swallowed cathedrals and the fog never fully lifted.

"Aldric Vey was the best of us," she said. "That's not sentiment. Ask any survivor of the Border Order and they'll tell you the same. He joined at fourteen, commanded at thirty, and he held the line at Redford Crossing for three days with a third of his men and no relief, and when they finally pulled him out he apologized to the wounded for arriving late." She smiled despite herself, despite everything. "He taught me that a knight's first weapon is attention. *Most failures are failures of noticing,* he used to say. *Look longer. Look honestly.*" Her smile faded. "Which is why it's going to destroy me if I have to put a sword through him. Because he's the one who taught me to look honestly. And if I look honestly at what's coming down that road — a man who took the keystone of the covenant and walked into the dark with it, who let the wards fall, who let the stars go out, who let the hollow-born hunt a child grown into a weapon — then whatever I find in him, I'll have to notice it. All of it. He'd accept nothing less."

"And if what's left of him offers you something?" Xanthe asked. "People like that — people who were the best of us — they don't come back offering nothing. They come back offering exactly what you most want. What do you most want, Serah?"

The question hung between them, and Serah found, to her horror, that the honest answer rose instantly and uncontrollably, the way floodwater rises through a cellar grate.

Absolution. Someone to tell her that the girl who held a mother's arms behind her back had been right, or at least forgivable, or at least forgiven. Twenty years of penance performed in the dark, and if Aldric Vey — the best of them, the truest of them — opened his changed and terrible mouth and said *you did what was needed, Serah, stand down* — she did not trust herself to say no. That was the truth beneath the truth. That was the confession under the confession.

"He'll offer me peace," she said aloud, "and I'll have to refuse it, and I need you to remember that I know this, so that when the moment comes, you can remind me too."

Xanthe studied her for a long moment. Then they nodded, once, solemn as an oath.

They walked on. And near midday, as the drowned country's first fog reached out gray fingers across the road to meet them, Serah saw it: far off, at the limit of sight, a single figure standing motionless in the middle of the flooded causeway. Tall. Unmoving. Waiting, the way mountains wait.

He had been expecting them.

Of course he had. He had taught her himself: most failures are failures of noticing. And Aldric Vey had noticed everything, always — including, she understood now with a cold settling certainty, exactly what she would become, and exactly when she would come walking north to find him.

He raised one hand. Not a wave. A greeting, slow and formal, the salute of the old Order — the one they had used at the changing of watches, at the closing of gates, at the end of things.

*Your watch is over,* it meant. *Come and report.*

Serah's hand found her sword hilt, and beside her, she heard the air begin, very faintly, to hum — the sound of a storm deciding whether to wait.
