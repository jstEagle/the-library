## 9. Storms Without Permission

The drowned orchard below the citadel's western wall had been dead for twenty years, and it made, Xanthe decided, an excellent place to learn how to unmake things.

They stood among the blackened pear trees in the hour before dawn, when the fog came up the valley like something breathing. Frost silvered every branch. Above, through a rent in the cloud, the sky showed its wound — that starless dark, that absence with edges — and Xanthe had learned, in the weeks since the wards fell, not to look at it too long. Looking at it was like pressing a tongue to a broken tooth.

*Again,* Serah had said, an hour ago. *Again.* And then she had gone to the perimeter to check her warding-lines, because she was Serah, and Serah could not watch Xanthe work without her hands twitching toward her sword.

So Xanthe stood alone among dead trees and held out one hand and asked the sky for permission.

That was how it felt. That was how it had always felt, even as a child, even before they understood what the ache in their chest was — a door with someone standing behind it, knocking politely, forever. The wards had kept that door shut for twenty years. Now the wards were glass on the wind, and the door stood open, and the thing behind it was *patient*, and *vast*, and *theirs*.

"Come on, then," Xanthe whispered.

The storm came.

It came the way it always came now — not gradually, not as a gathering of cloud, but all at once, as if the air had only been pretending to be air. Pressure dropped. The fog flattened as though pressed by an enormous palm. Every hair on Xanthe's arms lifted, and then the lightning came down out of a cloudless sky and struck the dead orchard with a sound like the world's spine cracking.

Xanthe had learned to aim it. Mostly. The bolt took the largest of the pear trees at its heart, split it, flung its halves into the fog. Steam rose from the scorch. The smell of ozone and burned wood rolled over them, and beneath it that other smell, the one that came with the power — rain on hot stone, the smell of a sky remembering itself.

Exhilaration first. It was always exhilaration first. Three seconds of being the tallest thing in the world, of holding a piece of the sky in their fist.

Then the price.

It came quietly, the way a thief leaves. Xanthe had noticed it the third time they'd called the lightning — a small wrongness, a gap like a missing stair in the dark. They'd told themselves they'd misremembered. They'd told themselves a great many things.

This time they were watching for it.

They closed their eyes and walked the corridors of their own memory, and found the door they'd left ajar, and opened it, and — nothing. The room was empty. Not dark, not locked. *Empty*, in the way a room is empty when the furniture has been carried out and the walls left bare. They had been remembering, all week, the name of the woman in the refugee camp who had mended their coat — a round-faced woman, laughing, who'd called them *little thunder* in the Serevani fashion and given them honey-bread —

The name was gone. Not blurred. Gone. Xanthe reached for it and their mind presented them with a smooth, featureless wall where a person had been. They could remember the honey-bread. They could remember the coat, the tear at the elbow, the needle pulling through. The woman herself had been *taken*, like a figure cut from a painting, leaving a coat-shaped absence and a bread-shaped absence and nothing else.

The lightning still crackled in the scorched grass. Xanthe stood very still among the dead trees and felt, for the first time since the wards fell, genuinely afraid.

*The price the covenant exacted from all storm-callers.* Serah had said it once, offhand, in the ruins of the border chapel — *the old texts say the sky takes its toll* — and Xanthe had laughed and asked what a sky could possibly want. Serah hadn't laughed back. Serah had looked at them with an expression Xanthe was only now, standing in the empty room of their own memory, beginning to understand.

They tried to remember what Serah had said after that. The conversation was there — the chapel, the dust, the light through the broken roof — but it ended too soon, cut clean, and Xanthe could not for their life recall what had come next.

*How many?* they thought. *How many already?*

They counted what they could find missing, which was like counting holes in water. The mend-woman's name. The color of the door of the house they'd grown up in — they knew there had been a door, they knew it had been painted, and the color was a wall. A verse their mother used to sing, of which only the melody survived, humming in the dark like a bee in a jar. Small things. Small things, each one. A handful of coins from a life's purse.

But the purse did not refill.

* * *

"You're favoring your left hand."

Serah said it that evening without looking up from the map she'd spread across the chapel floor — the road south to Serevan inked in her cramped soldier's hand, the drowned cathedral to the north marked with a single ominous X. She'd taken to sketching maps the way other people took to prayer.

"My what?"

"Your left hand. When you call the storm you favor it, like the power sits heavier on that side. And you've been—" She looked up then, firelight catching the grey in her cropped hair, the scar that bisected her left eyebrow. "You've been quiet. Quieter than your usual brand of quiet, which is already a siege."

"I'm always quiet. It's one of my finest qualities. You've said so."

"I've said it's *infuriating*." But she was smiling, a little, the way she smiled when she was worried and trying not to be caught at it. Serah Vane worried the way other people bled — internally, and constantly, and without ever once letting you see the wound. "Eat something."

There was stew. There was always stew; the refugee columns had left them a pot and a sack of barley and a debt Serah kept trying to refuse. Xanthe sat across the fire from her and ate and watched her work and thought: *I could tell her.*

The thought came every evening now, punctual as the fog. *I could tell her.* And every evening it ran up against the same wall — not distrust, never that, not anymore, not after the chapel and the confession and the night she'd stood between Xanthe and a hollow-born with nothing but a broken sword and her own stubborn body. The wall was simpler and more terrible than distrust.

If Xanthe told her, Serah would make them stop.

She would make them stop, and she would be right, and then the storm would sit behind its door forever, knocking, and the starless dark would come up the valley road with nothing to oppose it but a knight with a broken sword and a storm-caller who had chosen to stay small. Xanthe had seen what the hollow-born did to small things. Xanthe had seen the refugee camp's western palisade after the third attack, and some sights did not leave you, and that was one.

So they ate their stew and said, "The lightning's answering faster. I don't have to beg anymore."

"Good." Serah rolled the map with unnecessary precision. "Faster is good. Faster means less time between the ambush and the answer."

"Serah."

"Hm?"

"Has it ever occurred to you to ask what it costs?"

She went still. It was a small stillness, the stillness of a woman who had been a soldier for twenty years and had learned to stop motion before it became a flinch — but Xanthe had learned to read her the way one reads weather, and the stillness said *yes, it has occurred to me, and I have been waiting for you to ask, and I have been dreading your asking*, all at once.

"The old texts say the sky takes its toll," she said carefully. "The founders wrote that storm-calling was never free. But the texts were written by the people who built the cage, Xanthe. Half of what they wrote was justification dressed as wisdom."

"What did they say the toll *was*?"

"I don't know. The passages were—" a fractional pause, "—sealed. Even in the Order's archives. Even from knights." She met Xanthe's eyes across the fire, and her face did the thing it did when she was choosing, deliberately, to be honest: it went open, all the defenses lowered at once, like gates. "If you know something I don't, you can tell me. Whatever it is. You know that."

And there it was. The open gate. The invitation.

"I don't," Xanthe said. "Not yet. I'll tell you when I do."

It was not even a lie. It was a lie with a delay on it, a lie on credit, and Xanthe watched Serah accept it — watched her decide to trust them, watched the gates come down again — and felt the deception settle into their chest like a swallowed stone, cold and smooth and permanent.

*This,* they thought, *is how it starts. This is how it started for her, twenty years ago, with a child and a secret and a reason.*

The fire cracked. Serah went back to her maps. And Xanthe sat in the warmth of the only person in two realms they trusted, and guarded their empty rooms, and said nothing.

* * *

They got better at it. That was the horror of it — they got *better*.

They learned to call the lightning smaller. A thread instead of a pillar, a scalpel instead of an avalanche, and the toll came smaller with it: not a whole memory but a sliver, the smell of a morning, the first line of a letter, the name of a hill. Xanthe began keeping a tally, scratched in charcoal on the inside of their coat, in a cipher of their own devising — not names, never names, just marks. Eleven marks by the fourth day. Fourteen by the sixth.

They learned to inventory themselves at dawn, walking the corridors of memory like a steward checking a house after a storm, noting what was missing, grieving quickly, moving on. They learned which rooms to stop visiting, because an empty room hurt worse the second time, when you remembered it had once been full.

And they learned — this was the worst lesson, and it came easiest — to hide it.

It was appallingly easy. Serah watched them the way a hawk watches a field, but hawks watch for movement, and Xanthe had simply stopped moving. When a gap opened, they did not pause, did not frown, did not let their eyes go distant. They kept talking. They laughed at the right moments. They had always been good with their face; now they were good with their whole self.

"You're different," Serah said one night, apropos of nothing, sharpening her sword by the fire. The blade whispered against the stone, a sound like a long exhale. "Since the orchard. You're — lighter. Looser. I can't decide if it's good."

"Maybe I'm finally getting the hang of it."

"Maybe." The whisper of the blade. "When I was young, my swordmaster told me the dangerous ones aren't the ones who struggle. The ones who struggle are fighting honestly. The dangerous ones are the ones who've stopped struggling and started *managing*." She did not look up. "I'm not saying that's you. I'm saying I was raised by paranoid old men and it ruined me for trusting anyone."

"Has it? Ruined you?"

The blade stopped. Serah looked at the fire, and the firelight moved on her face, and for a long moment Xanthe thought she wouldn't answer.

"No," she said finally. "It nearly did. And then a storm-caller fell out of a burning market square and called me a *gloriously unsubtle woman*, and I found there was still—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There was still room."

Xanthe's chest did something complicated and painful. They crossed the space between them — three steps, it was always three steps, the chapel floor had a loose stone at the second step that they had both long since stopped avoiding — and took the sword out of her hands and set it down, and Serah let them, which from Serah was a surrender of continental significance.

"You know," Xanthe said, "for a woman sworn to truth, you're very good at not saying things."

"I'm a *former* knight. The oath broke with the Order." But she was smiling, that small real smile, the one Xanthe had begun collecting the way a miser collects coins. "And you're one to talk."

"Me? I'm an open book."

"You're a locked book with the title burned off." Serah's hand came up — hesitated — and then settled against Xanthe's jaw, callused and careful, as though Xanthe were something that might startle. "Whatever you're carrying, you don't have to carry it alone. That's not a demand. It's just — a door. Standing open. However long it takes."

And Xanthe, standing in the warmth of her hand, with fourteen charcoal marks hidden against their ribs and a mend-woman's name dissolved into nothing, heard themselves say: "I know."

The stone in their chest had a twin now. It was getting crowded in there.

* * *

The hollow-born came on the ninth night, and the toll came with them.

There were only three — scouts, Serah called them later, *testing the lines* — and they came out of the fog with no more warning than the fog itself, shapes of absence wearing the suggestion of limbs, and the warding-lines Serah had strung across the orchard path sang once and snapped like harp strings, and then it was screaming and motion and Serah's sword already moving, and Xanthe did not think. Thinking was a luxury of the unattacked.

They opened the door and took the sky in both hands.

The lightning that answered was not a thread. It was everything — every reserve, every careful rationing, the whole hoard spent in a single breath — and the night turned white, and the three hollow-born came apart like smoke in a gale, and the thunder rolled away down the valley and came back twice, and Xanthe stood in the center of a circle of scorched grass with their hair standing on end and their heart slamming and the exhilaration rising in them like floodwater—

—and then they reached for the memory of the fire. The chapel fire. The night Serah had said *there was still room*, the loose stone at the second step, the sword set down, the hand against their jaw.

It was there. All of it. Xanthe could have wept with relief.

But the *smell* of it was gone. The woodsmoke, the barley, the ozone of that first storm — the entire sensory floor of the memory had been taken, and what remained was a story told about a night rather than the night itself. *I remember that this happened*, the memory said, in a clerk's flat voice. *I do not remember being there.*

"Xanthe." Serah, limping, blood on her temple from a graze, alive, staring at the circle of burned grass as though it were a scripture she couldn't read. "Xanthe, that was — I've never seen — the old records said a storm-caller at full strength could crack a hillside, and I thought it was propaganda, and you just—"

"Are you hurt?"

"Scratched. *Xanthe.*"

"We should move. Scouts mean a column. You said it yourself — scouts mean the dark is mapping us."

She let it go. That was the thing about Serah; she could be made to let things go, but she never let them *drop*, and Xanthe could feel the thing settling into her the way the stone had settled into them — carried now, quietly, to be examined later by lamplight.

They broke camp by moonlight that wasn't, and walked south through the fog toward Serevan, and Xanthe took out the charcoal and added a fifteenth mark, and a sixteenth, and then stood for a long time with the charcoal hovering over the coat's inner lining.

Because here was the arithmetic they could no longer avoid. The hollow-born were coming in greater numbers. The road to Vaelmoor's heart ran through Aldric Vey and his drowned cathedral, and beyond that through whatever the Oathstone demanded, and every mile of it would ask the sky for help, and the sky would take its toll, and the toll was not coin. The toll was *them*. Room by room. The mend-woman first, and the door's color, and the song, and now the warmth of a fire on a night they would otherwise have kept forever.

And at the end of that road stood the reforging of the covenant — the restoration of their stolen power, the full storm, everything the wards had caged for twenty years.

*How much of me will be left to enjoy it?*

"Xanthe." Serah's voice, low, from the head of the column. She had stopped. She was looking north, past Xanthe, past the dead orchard, to where the horizon should have been.

The fog was lifting — unnaturally, all at once, like a curtain drawn — and against the starless dark to the north, lights were moving. Not lightning. Not fire. A procession of pale, cold points, arranged in ranks, coming down the old road from Vaelmoor at a pace that was patient and unhurried and utterly wrong.

"Is that—" Xanthe began.

"Torches," Serah said, and her hand found her sword, and her voice was very quiet. "No one carries torches under a starless sky. Nothing that still has a shadow to lose would light one." She turned to Xanthe, and the firelight of the distant procession reflected in her eyes. "That's not the dark. That's *people* walking out of it. And they're headed straight for us."
