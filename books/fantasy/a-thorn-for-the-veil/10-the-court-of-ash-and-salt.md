## 10. The Court of Ash and Salt

The road to Serevan smelled of the sea before we could see it.

I had grown up on border winds — pine and frost and the iron tang of old wards — but this was different. Salt lay over everything like a second skin. It clung to my lips, crusted in the seams of my armor, worked its way into the grain of my sword hilt until the leather swelled soft as flesh. Xanthe walked half a pace ahead of me with their face tipped toward the horizon, drinking it in, and I found myself watching them instead of the road, which was becoming a habit I did not know how to break.

"You're smiling," I said.

"I'm deciding whether I like it." They turned, wind pulling at the dark braid Serane had woven for them that morning at the refugee fires. "It's loud."

"The sea?"

"Everything. The air here is *loud*. Like the whole coast is holding one long note." Their fingers flexed at their sides — a gesture I had learned meant their magic was restless, pressing against whatever fragile walls they'd built around it. "Do you think they'll hear us?"

*Hear us.* As if the queen of Serevan were a storm-front to be negotiated with rather than a woman on a throne. But Xanthe had spent twenty years behind wards; they had no instinct yet for courts, for the arithmetic of power. That would have to be mine.

"They'll listen," I said, and hated how much of a lie it sounded even then.

---

Serevan rose out of the mist like something dreamed rather than built. The city climbed its cliff in terraces of white stone and pale timber, every surface salt-bleached and gull-streaked, hanging gardens spilling down walls in curtains of sea-grass and silver bloom. Below it all, the harbor spread gray-green and glittering, crowded with ships whose sails bore the sigil of the realm: a wave broken by a blade.

We had sent word ahead from the last waystation — a Border Order seal on a scrap of parchment, the only currency left to an order that no longer existed. It bought us passage through the outer gates and nothing more. By the time we reached the middle city, word had outrun us. People came out onto their balconies to look. Not at me — my order was dead, my knighthood a rumor in a drowned citadel — but at Xanthe. Word travels strangely along coasts. Somewhere between Vaelmoor and here, the story had grown teeth: *a storm-caller walks again.*

Xanthe bore it the way they bore everything now — chin level, shoulders loose, eyes moving constantly, cataloguing exits. They had changed since the hollow-born ambush. Harder in some places, softer in others, in ways I couldn't name and didn't dare examine too closely, because examining it felt like theft.

"They're staring," they murmured as we crossed a plaza where fountains ran with fresh water — fresh water, wasted on decoration, and I saw Xanthe flinch at the extravagance the way a valley child flinches at anything freely spilled.

"Let them stare."

"That's what worries me." They glanced sideways. "People stare at things they want, or things they fear."

"And which are you?"

"Neither," they said quietly. "That's the problem."

* * *

The throne room of Serevan was not a room at all.

They held court in a great open rotunda at the cliff's edge, walled on three sides and open to the fourth, so that the ocean itself filled the space where a wall should be. Wind moved through the pillars unchecked. Spray drifted in on high days, and the floor of white shell-stone was worn into shallow channels where the water pooled and ran — channels that fed, I realized, into aqueducts below. Even the queen's audience chamber drank the sea and gave nothing back.

Queen Marenthe sat beneath a canopy of grey silk that billowed and snapped like a sail at war. She was older than I expected and younger than the stories claimed — silver shot through dark hair cropped short at the jaw, a face all planes and patience, hands folded on the armrests of a chair that was plainly, deliberately, just a chair. No throne. A merchant-queen's statement: *I sit as equals or not at all.*

Her court stood in ranks along the curved walls — lords of the coastal houses in blue and bone-white, admirals with sun-dark faces, and behind them, half-shadowed, the silent ranks of the Tideguard in their lacquered scale. Every eye found us as we crossed the shell-stone. Most settled on Xanthe and stayed.

A herald spoke our names into the wind. Mine landed without weight — *Serah Vane, formerly of the Border Order* — and I watched several lords exchange glances at *formerly*, filing it away like coin.

Then: "*And the storm-caller, Xanthe Oyelaran.*"

The name went through the court like a current through water. Someone drew breath. A woman near the canopy — a house matriarch heavy with rings — made a small sound, half reverence, half hunger, and covered her mouth with her fan.

I stepped forward first. Protocol demanded the petitioner speak, and protocol was the last weapon I still carried cleanly.

"Your Majesty. We come seeking aid against the starless dark. It has taken the borderlands entire. Vaelmoor stands broken. The refugees you've seen washing up on your southern shores are the visible part of a tide that will reach these cliffs within a season — perhaps less."

"We know," Queen Marenthe said. Her voice was low and unhurried, a voice accustomed to being heard over storms. "Our fishermen bring reports. Our far-seers confirm them. The dark does not advance like an army, knight. It advances like a tide — patient, total, indifferent to walls." Her gaze shifted, deliberate as a helm turning, to Xanthe. "Which is why we received your petition with such interest."

Xanthe came forward beside me. Close enough that our shoulders nearly touched. I felt the heat of them through my sleeve, absurdly, in all that cold wind.

"What do you want from us?" Xanthe asked. No preamble. No courtly varnish. I felt half the room bristle — petitioners did not ask queens what *they* wanted — and I felt Marenthe, conversely, sharpen with interest.

"A fair question deserves a fair answer." The queen rose. She descended the two steps from her dais — two steps, even that a calculated informality — and walked toward us across the wet stone until she stood close enough that the spray touched all three of us alike. "You come to me asking for ships, soldiers, grain, ward-masons. All of which I can give, and give generously, because unlike the Border Order—" a flicker of contempt, there and gone— "Serevan prepares for tides before they crest. But aid is not charity, storm-caller. Nothing on this coast is."

"Name your price," Xanthe said.

"*Allegiance.*"

The word dropped into the rotunda and the wind seemed to carry it outward, into every ear at once.

"Formally sworn," Marenthe continued, "before this court and recorded in the Ledger of Tides. Your gift bound to Serevan's service. When the dark is broken, the storm-caller's covenant passes to my crown — your power answering to my hand, as the tide answers the moon. In exchange: my fleet, my legions, my granaries opened, my masons sent to rebuild what the dark has broken. You will not find a better offer in any realm, because there is no other realm left offering."

The silence afterward had texture. I could feel the court leaning in — hungry, satisfied, certain. They had already written this scene. The homeless exile, the dead order's knight, the crown's generosity descending like weather.

Xanthe said nothing. And I understood, watching them, that they had known. Somewhere between the gates and this rotunda, they had done their own arithmetic, and arrived at this number before the queen spoke it.

"You want to own the sky," Xanthe said at last. Quietly. Almost wonderingly.

"I want to *survive*," Marenthe answered, and for a heartbeat something raw moved under the polish — grief, maybe, or fear, the honest terror of a ruler who has read the far-seers' reports. "You think I relish this? I am asking a person to become an instrument. I know exactly what that costs. I have read the histories of what was done to storm-callers under the old covenant — I helped bury those histories deeper, when it suited trade." She let that confession hang, unasked-for and unrepentant. "But if the choice is my realm's survival or your freedom, I will choose my realm. Every time. As you would, if the valley still stood behind you."

"It never stood behind me," Xanthe said. "It exiled me."

"Which makes you cheap," the queen said, without cruelty — with the terrible gentleness of pure honesty, "and makes me generous by comparison. Think on that, knight and storm-caller. You have until the evening tide."

She returned to her chair. The audience, it seemed, was over. Courtiers began murmuring behind fans; an admiral was already whispering to a treasury lord, dividing spoils that hadn't been surrendered yet.

We bowed — I bowed; Xanthe inclined their head, barely — and walked back down the length of that open hall with a thousand eyes on our backs and the sea roaring beyond the missing wall like a creditor at the door.

* * *

They gave us a guest chamber in the cliff-palace, high up, all white stone and open shutters, the harbor glittering far below. Luxury as leverage. Everything in Serevan was leverage.

Xanthe stood at the window a long time, saying nothing. I busied myself checking my gear I didn't need to check, giving them the silence the way I'd give a wounded soldier a blanket — not because it fixes anything, but because standing witness is sometimes the whole of what you have.

"They knew," Xanthe finally said. "Before we ever walked in. The herald said my name like he'd rehearsed it. The matriarch with the fan — she looked at me like I was cargo arriving."

"Yes."

"How long has the offer existed, Serah? Before us? Was there always a price waiting for whoever came out of that valley?"

I didn't answer fast enough, and they caught it — of course they caught it; they missed nothing anymore.

"There were rumors," I admitted. "In the Order. That the coastal realms kept... contingencies. Scrying contracts, contingency charters. If a storm-caller ever surfaced, certain crowns wanted first claim. I told myself it was bureaucratic superstition." My jaw tightened. "I was wrong, and I'm sorry. I should have warned you what we were walking into."

"You couldn't have known the shape of it." They turned from the window. Firelight caught the storm-grey of their eyes, and beneath it something colder settling in, layer by layer, like silt. "No. That's not true either. You probably could have. We both keep being surprised by how much everyone knew, and I'm tired of being surprised." They laughed, short and bitter, nothing warm in it. "Twenty years caged behind wards so my power wouldn't threaten anyone's borders. And the moment I walk free, the first kingdom to open its doors wants to close them again around me. Just... a prettier cage. With a sea view."

"Xanthe—"

"Don't." They held up a hand, gentle enough. "Don't defend them. Don't defend the Order. Don't tell me she's afraid too, that everyone's afraid, that fear explains it. Fear explains *everything*, Serah. That's the problem with it." They sank onto the window seat, elbows on knees, head bowed — and for a moment they looked so young, so unbearably young, the exile-child who'd been carried across a border and lied to for two decades, and my chest hurt with the effort of not crossing the room.

"Do you remember," they said, quieter, "what you told me on the road past the Veil? That the Oathstone covenant was supposed to be a sharing. Sky-magic and earth-magic, bound together as equals. That somewhere in the last centuries it got twisted into ownership."

"I remember."

"So did she." Xanthe lifted their head. "Marenthe. She didn't invent that price. She quoted it. *As the tide answers the moon* — that's covenant language, isn't it? Old words. She's offering me the same bargain the founders struck, dressed in silk instead of stone. Bind the sky to a master. Call it protection. Call it survival." Their hands had curled into fists without their seeming to notice. "Everyone keeps offering to save the world *at* me, Serah. Nobody's asked what the world costs me."

I had no answer that wasn't a lie, so I gave none. I sat beside them on the window seat instead, close, not touching, and together we watched the light die over the harbor — the water going from pewter to ink, lamps kindling along the terraces one by one like a constellation falling upward into the city.

"They'll expect an answer at evening tide," I said eventually.

"I know what they'll accept."

"Tell me anyway."

They were quiet a moment. Then, in a voice stripped of everything but fact: "If I refuse, Serevan bars its gates and we go north alone, into the dark, with nothing. Aldric Vey waiting. Half an Oathstone. Hollow-born between us and everything. We lose — probably quickly, definitely badly. And everyone the dark swallows after us dies knowing someone refused to be owned cheaply enough to save them." A breath. "If I swear, we get the fleet, the legions, the masons. And I spend the rest of my life as the moon to her tide. Owned. Recorded. *Ledgered.*"

"That's not the whole ledger," I said. "There's a third column."

"Oh?"

"You. Refusing on principle while the world burns is just pride wearing armor. But swearing yourself away to buy time — that's also a cage, and you'd be building it with your own hands, and I'd be helping you build it, and I can't—" I stopped. Started again, slower. "I didn't come out of that valley to watch you be traded. I carried children across that border once, Xanthe. I know what it looks like when someone becomes a transaction. I will not stand in another court and let it happen twice."

They looked at me then — really looked, the way they had begun looking at me lately, too long, too openly, as if I were a text they were learning to read — and the air between us went thin and bright as wire.

"And what would you have me do?" they asked softly. "Walk out? Refuse her and call it freedom?"

"No," I said. "I'd have you make her *wait*. Make every crown that wants a piece of you understand that you are not a resource that came up on their charts. Buy nothing tonight. Promise nothing. And if the day comes when you must bind yourself to save the realms—" I held their gaze, and the truth came out of me rough and unpolished and more revealing than I intended, "—then it will be because you chose it, not because a queen priced you. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters, and I will burn this city's goodwill to the ground before I watch them erase it."

The wind moved through the shutters. Down in the harbor, the evening tide was turning; we could hear the great bells of the seawall tolling the hour, bronze notes rolling up the cliff.

Xanthe exhaled slowly, and some of the frost went out of their face.

"Evening tide," they repeated. "Then let's go disappoint a queen."

* * *

The rotunda at evening was lit by a hundred lamps that guttered in the sea-wind, and the court had packed itself in tighter than morning — word had spread, and half the coastal lords had come to watch the storm-caller kneel.

Marenthe waited beneath her snapping grey canopy. She did not rise this time.

Xanthe walked the length of the shell-stone floor alone. I stayed at the edge of the court as arranged — my place, my role — and watched them stop before the queen's chair, framed against the open wall and the black, glittering immensity of the sea.

"Your Majesty," they said, and their voice carried clean over the wind, pitched — I realized with a jolt — exactly as they pitched it when calling weather. Not loud. *Carrying.* "You offered me allegiance, fleet, and law, and asked my answer by the tide."

"I did."

"Here is my answer." They let the pause stretch until the whole court leaned. "Serevan may have my *alliance*. Freely given, mutually held, revocable by neither party while the dark stands. My storm answers no crown — not yours, not the old founders', not any hand that reaches for it. What I am, I lend to the war. What I am *is not for sale*, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a treaty and a leash. Take the alliance, Majesty. Or take nothing, and explain to your grandchildren why the tide came for your cliffs and the sky declined to answer."

Somewhere in the ranks, a lord hissed. The ring-heavy matriarch's fan had stopped entirely. The Tideguard hadn't moved, but I saw their weight shift, ten spears tilting a degree toward the floor's center.

Marenthe regarded Xanthe for a long, wind-loud moment. Then — unexpectedly — she smiled. Small, sharp, genuine.

"Twenty years ago," she said, "the Border Order promised me a storm-caller would never leave that valley alive to bargain with anyone. It seems they failed at more than one thing." She rose, descended her two steps, and extended her hand — palm open, not for kneeling, but for clasping, warrior-fashion. "Alliance, then. Witnessed by sea and court. My fleet sails within the fortnight; my masons follow. And storm-caller — when this war is done, we will speak again about the Ledger. Tides return. So do I."

Xanthe clasped her forearm. "When this war is done, Majesty, we'll see which of us the tide returns to."

Laughter rippled through parts of the court — startled, uncertain, delighted. The matriarch resumed fanning, faster. Something in the rotunda's chemistry had inverted: the storm-caller had walked in cargo and walked out a power, and everyone present was already revising their ledgers.

I should have felt triumph. Instead, watching Xanthe turn from the queen with their chin high and their eyes finding mine across the crowd — watching the fierce, exhausted, blazing relief they let show for exactly one breath before the mask came back — I felt the cold slide in beneath everything, quiet and certain as water under a door.

Because I had seen Marenthe's smile. And I had heard *we will speak again*.

And I knew — the way you know weather coming in your bones, the way I had known since Vaelmoor — that this victory had purchased us days, not salvation. That every crown on this coast had just marked Xanthe as a prize worth taking later, by treaty or by trickery. That the alliance was real, and the danger was realer.

And underneath all of it, older and darker, the thought I had been drowning for chapters of road and campfire:

*Aldric Vey holds the other half of the stone. He offered it once to the Order for exactly this kind of binding. And now every realm in the world wants what he wants — a storm-caller leashed. The dark doesn't need to devour this world. We are selling it to him piece by piece, and calling it diplomacy.*

Xanthe reached me at the edge of the court. Around us the rotunda emptied into celebration — wine, lanterns, admirals recalculating futures aloud.

"Well?" they said, breathless, bright. "Did I just win us a war?"

"You won us a fleet," I said. "Pray it's enough." And I smiled for them, because they needed it, and turned toward the doors, and did not say the rest: that somewhere north, in a drowned cathedral, a half-god who had been listening to the world's hungers for thirty years had just heard every court in creation agree with him.

Three days later, a Tideguard courier woke us before dawn with a sealed letter bearing the crest of a cathedral that had been underwater for a century.

It was addressed to *the storm-caller* — and signed, in ink that shimmered faintly like starlight drowned, *A.V.*
