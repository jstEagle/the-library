## 11. Aldric Vey, Half a God

The cathedral had been drowned for a century before we found it, and the water had never fully left.

It pooled in the nave in a black mirror, knee-deep where we waded and chest-deep where the floor had buckled, and above us the vaulted ceiling opened to a sky the color of old pewter — no stars, of course. There had been no stars for weeks. But the dark here was different from the dark of the wastes. It was *occupied* dark. It had a tenant.

Serah felt it before I did. Her hand found my wrist in the shallows — not a grip, exactly. A question.

"You feel that," she said. Not really a question either.

"Like standing under a storm that hasn't decided yet," I said.

"Like standing under a *man*," she said, "who hasn't decided yet."

The drowned cathedral of Saint Vess had been a border shrine once, back when the border meant something other than a wound. Its bells still hung in the tower, green with a century of rot, and when the wind came through the broken windows they stirred against each other with a sound like drowned people knocking. The water around our legs was warm. That was the worst part. Water this far north should have been cold as confession, and instead it lay against my skin like something breathing.

We had followed the resonance of the missing half of the Oathstone for six days — a hum beneath the world that only I could hear, since the fracture at Vaelmoor had tuned me to it the way a tuning fork remembers its twin. The hum had led us off the pilgrim road, through a forest of trees that had grown pale and leafless in the starless dark, down into a basin where the land had sunk a generation ago and the sea had come in and then, inexplicably, gone out again. Leaving this. A cathedral full of warm black water and one occupant.

He was waiting for us at the altar.

I want to say I saw him and understood at once what he was. I didn't. What I saw was an old man standing in water that should have soaked him to the waist, dry as paper, dressed in the rotted regalia of the Border Order — the high collar, the silver chain of office gone black with age. What I saw was a tall figure with a scholar's stoop and a face I recognized from Serah's silences: the set of her jaw when she spoke of her mentor, the particular way her voice flattened around the name *Aldric Vey*.

What I felt was something else entirely. The hum in my blood — the Oathstone's twin-song, the resonance I had been chasing across a dead country — stopped. Because I was standing in front of it. He *was* it, or it was him; the line between the man and the stone he carried had been worn away like a coin passed through too many hands.

"Xanthe Oyelaran," he said. His voice was gentle. That was the second worst part. I had expected thunder, or the hollow hiss of the hollow-born, or the cold arithmetic of a thing that had stopped being human. Instead: gentleness, worn soft as river stone, the voice of a man who had spent decades talking to himself in the dark and had gotten good at it.

"And Serah Vane." His eyes moved to her, and something in them — regret, or the fossil of regret — shifted like sediment in still water. "You grew into your shoulders. Your mother would have been proud. Your father would have been afraid. He was always afraid of the right things."

"Don't," Serah said. One word, and it cost her. I heard the cost in it. Twenty years of cost.

"You've told them, then." Aldric's gaze came back to me. "About the night at the border. About your part in it." He read the answer in my face, and nodded slowly, as if I had passed some small examination. "Good. I won't have you making your choice on a foundation of lies. There are enough of those in the world. I helped lay most of them."

The water lapped at my thighs. Somewhere above, the dead bells knocked together, and stopped.

"You're the half," I said. "The missing half of the Oathstone. It's not something you *carry*."

"It stopped being something I carry around the time your mother stopped being something your father carried," he said. "Bonding is not a metaphor, storm-caller. It is a marriage of the self to a purpose, and like all marriages it ends either in death or in becoming something that can no longer be called by the old name." He spread his hands — dry hands, paper hands — and the light in the cathedral changed. It came from him, I realized. A pale, patient glow, the color of moonlight through ice, rising out of his skin the way heat rises off summer stone. "I am half of the covenant that holds the sky to the earth. I am half of the law that kept your magic caged for twenty years and kept the starless dark outside the walls of the world for two hundred. Both of those things are true, and I have had a very long time to make my peace with the contradiction."

Serah had drawn her sword. I hadn't seen her do it. The blade caught his pale glow and gave back nothing.

"Put it away, child," Aldric said, not unkindly. "If I wanted you dead, the water would have taken you at the door. This place is mine the way a lung is yours. I breathe it."

"Serah." I touched her arm. The sword stayed out, but it stopped rising. "I want to hear him."

"You want to *believe* him," she said. "There's a difference."

"I know the difference," I said, and I mostly did. "I still want to hear."

* * *

He made us sit. That was the strange courtesy of it — an old man gesturing us to a fallen pillar at the edge of the flooded nave, and us actually going, and the three of us arranging ourselves in the ruins like a family around a hearth that had been cold for a hundred years. Serah would not sit. She stood behind my left shoulder with her sword across her body and her weight on her back foot, and Aldric looked at her placement and something almost like fondness crossed his ruined face.

"Left shoulder," he said. "You always did guard the left. Even as a girl. Even when the left was wrong."

"Say what you brought us here to say."

He turned to me, and the glow in his skin steadied, and I understood that I was looking at the closest thing to a god this broken world still owned — half of one, at least, the way a man with half a fortune is still called rich.

"Tell me what you know of the covenant," he said. "The true text, not the catechism. You've heard the voices in the fracture. I know you have — the stone showed you, because the stone wants to be understood, and you are the first person in a century it has wanted to talk to."

"I know it was rewritten," I said. "I know it began as a treaty of shared burden and became a cage. I know the founders sealed the sky-magic out of fear, and dressed the fear up as law, and called the cage a covenant, and called the exiles a mercy."

"Yes." He closed his eyes briefly, as if the words were a pain he had been carrying so long he'd forgotten which side it ached on. "And I know the other thing you know. That the voices in the stone speak with your mother's cadence. That the woman who helped forge the original treaty — Sefa Oyelaran, storm-caller of the third line — is in there, in the memory the stone keeps, and that you have been listening to her the way an orphan listens through a wall." He opened his eyes. "I am sorry. That was cruel of me to say so plainly. But I have learned that kindness delayed becomes cruelty compounded."

The water was warm against my legs and I was cold anyway. "You knew her."

"Everyone knew her. She was the best of us." His voice did not waver, and that was its own kind of terrible. "She argued against the sealing until the last hour. She argued that a covenant of shared burden meant the burden was shared — that storm-callers would give their memories and the earth-realms would give their stone and their silence and their shelter, an exchange, a *marriage*. And when the founders rewrote it into a one-way wall, she was the first they exiled. The first of the new kind of exile. The demonstration." He looked at me, and his eyes were the pale glow now, all the way through. "You have her stubbornness. I watched you cross the dead borderlands in six days on a hum only you could hear, and I thought: there she is. There is Sefa, refusing to be reasonable, one more time, in a body twenty years younger."

"Stop," Serah said from behind me. "Whatever you're building toward — stop building it out of her dead mother."

"I'm building it out of the truth," Aldric said. "It's the only material I have left."

* * *

He rose, then. The water did not move around him — it simply was not where he was, the way a dream is not where you are — and he came down the steps of the ruined altar with the pale glow trailing from his shoulders like a mantle, and he held out his hand.

In it was the half of the Oathstone.

I had imagined it so many ways: a fist of black glass, a shard of frozen lightning, something that hurt to look at. It was none of those. It was a piece of ordinary stone, gray and veined, the size of a heart, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I hated that. The hum in my blood rose to meet it like a voice rising to a name, and my hands shook, and I was on my feet without remembering standing.

"Freely," Aldric said. "I want that word on the table before anything else. *Freely.* Not won, not traded, not bound. The Queen of Serevan would have made you kneel for it. The founders would have made you die for it. I give it to you for nothing — for one condition that is not a price, because it is not mine to charge. It is the covenant's."

"The covenant as written," I said. My voice came out strange. Steady and far away, like a voice heard through water.

"The covenant as *originally* written," he said. "Before the founders' fear got into the text. Reforge the stone, Xanthe Oyelaran, and speak the first covenant — not the cage. The treaty of shared burden. The sky-magic returned to the storm-blooded, the memories honored as a debt the earth-realms must repay, the hierarchy of the old law restored in its *just* form — not the form they used to exile your mother, but the form she fought for and lost." His hand stayed open. The stone lay in it like a sleeping bird. "You have spent your whole life as the victim of a corrupted law. I am offering you the uncorrupted one. Not mercy. *Justice.* The thing the valley took from you, given back with interest, by the only being in the world with the authority to give it."

And the terrible thing — the thing I have not told Serah, the thing I am telling no one — is that it made sense. It made sense the way a fever makes sense, the way grief makes sense. Every injustice of my life arranged itself behind his words like a witness lineup: the wards, the suppression, the childhood of being a storm with a cork in it, the exile story that was a lie, the mother in the stone's memory calling in a voice I'd spent twenty years forgetting. And here was a half-god in a drowned church offering me the one thing no one else could offer: not forgiveness, not escape, but *correction*. The world made right. The law made honest. My power given back not as charity but as *restoration* — as if I had never been exiled at all, as if the last twenty years were a clerical error the universe was finally prepared to amend.

"Say the rest," Serah said. Her voice was flat as a blade laid on a table. "Say what happens to the dark. Say what happens to the people who've already lost their stars. Say what the covenant costs *them*."

"The dark dissolves," Aldric said. "The covenant reforged is the wall the dark was gnawing at. Two hundred years it has been outside the law, starving, because the founders broke the treaty's balance and left the door unlatched. Reforge it, and the door closes — honestly, this time, with both realms' consent written into the stone. The refugees go home. The stars return." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and I understood that he had practiced it in the dark for decades until kindness and strategy had worn into each other and could no longer be told apart. "I know how it sounds, Serah Vane. I know because it sounded the same way to me, forty years ago, when the stone first showed me the original text and I wept at the justice of it. I have had forty years to look for the flaw. There isn't one. There is only the cost, and the cost is memory, and the memory is *honored* under the true text — repaid, commemorated, written into the stone so that no storm-caller's sacrifice is ever again a secret. Your mother's sacrifice. *Yours.*"

"Xanthe." Serah's hand was on my shoulder now. I could feel her heartbeat through her gauntlet, fast, faster than I had ever felt it, and her voice when it came was very quiet, the voice she used for truths she'd buried. "He's not lying. That's what makes him dangerous. Every word he's said is true, and it's all a trap anyway, because the trap doesn't need lies. It only needs you to be tired, and angry, and *owed*." Her fingers tightened. "Ask him what he was doing twenty years ago. Ask him what he told the Order. Ask him why the wards fell the night *you* came of age and not any other night in twenty years."

Aldric did not look at her. He looked at me, and the glow in his eyes was patient as tide.

"I did what was necessary," he said, "to bring you to this room. And I will answer any question you ask me, Xanthe — any question at all, truthfully, because I have no further use for lies. But first I want you to take the stone. Hold it. Feel what your mother felt when the treaty was honest. Then ask your questions." The half of the Oathstone lay in his open palm, gray and veined and singing, and the warm black water of the drowned cathedral lapped around my knees like something waiting to be asked. "Take it. It was always going to be yours. The only question left in the world is what you build with it — a cage with honest walls, or something no one has dared to imagine yet."

I reached out.

Behind me, Serah said my name — not *storm-caller*, not *Oyelaran*. Just my name, the way you say a word you're afraid is about to stop meaning anything.

And the stone, in Aldric's palm, began — very softly, like a voice through a wall, like a mother through twenty years of silence — to sing.
