## 12. What Serah Buried

The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and Serah found she could not look at Xanthe anymore.

They sat across the ruined watchhouse's hearth, the last intact room of a waystation that had once marked the border of a realm that no longer existed. Rain came through the broken roof in slow, silver threads, hissing where it struck the coals. Xanthe was watching her. They had been watching her since dusk, since Serah had gone silent mid-sentence, since the question they had been carrying for days finally found its way out of them like a splinter working loose.

*You knew. Before the wards fell, before any of this — you knew my name.*

Not an accusation. That was the worst of it. Xanthe had said it the way one says *the sky is grey*, the way one states a fact that has grown too heavy to keep holding. And Serah had sat with it, and with them, through the whole long dusk, and now the fire was dying and there was nothing left between her and the truth but her own cowardice, and that had run out at last.

"Twenty years ago," Serah said, "I was nineteen years old, and I believed with my whole heart that I was saving the world."

She heard Xanthe go still across the coals.

"I was wrong," she said. "Or I was right, and it was worse. I've never decided which. But I'll tell you all of it, and you can decide for me."

* * *

The border, in those days, had still been a border.

Serah remembered it as a scar of grey grass running between two ridgelines, marked by standing stones so old their oath-script had worn to shallow ghosts. On one side, the world she knew — the Border Order's watchfires, the long patrols, the citadel of Vaelmoor still whole on its crag, its towers catching the morning light like drawn blades. On the other side, the Veiled Valley, which was not a country so much as a sentence: *the place where storm-callers go.*

She had been a knight for barely two years. Young enough that the Order's grey cloak still felt like a costume she would one day grow into. Old enough, she had thought, to understand what she was sworn to.

The summons had come at moonset. Not a horn, not a rider — a whisper through the oath-bond, the private channel that ran between a knight and her commander, and it had said only: *Vey. The eastern stones. Bring no one. Tell no one.*

Aldric Vey had been waiting among the standing stones with two other knights she half-recognized, and with a bundle in his arms that was screaming.

She had thought, at first, *an animal.* The sound was that raw, that unhuman — a sound that had given up on words, that had passed through terror and out the other side into something older. Then the bundle moved, and a small hand came free of the blanket, and she saw that it was a child. Perhaps four years old. Perhaps five. Dark-haired, thin, with eyes so wide and so wet they seemed to hold the whole night in them.

"Storm-caller," Aldric had said, in the flat voice he used for reports. "The valley's covenant requires a tithe. The wards are hungry, and the child's power will feed them for a generation."

Serah had looked at the child, and the child had looked at her, and she had felt something crack in her chest that she would spend twenty years pretending was not broken.

"That's not how the covenant works," she had said. "We learned the covenant. It's a treaty. It's — shared burden. It's—"

"It's what the founders wrote," Aldric said, "and what the founders meant is that the realms need to sleep at night, Vey. The storm-callers burned a kingdom once. You've read the histories. You've seen the paintings of Ashfall. Do you want to explain to a grieving world why we let one of *those* grow up free because a nineteen-year-old found it sad?"

The child had stopped screaming then. That was the detail that would haunt her longest, she thought now — not the screaming, which she could almost bear to remember, but the moment it stopped. The child had simply run out of voice, and had gone limp in Aldric's arms, and had looked at the standing stones with the terrible patience of the very young, who have already learned that the world does not negotiate.

"We carry it across," Aldric said. "The wards take it from there. And the Order writes the record: the storm-caller child was exiled for crimes against the covenant. It's kinder that way. A story people can hate is easier than a truth they'd have to feel."

*Kinder.* She had wanted to strike him. She remembered that too — the hot, stupid surge of it, her hand twitching toward her sword. And she remembered what stopped her: the other two knights, watching her. Watching how she would choose. The Order was watching, always, the way a body watches a wound.

And she had thought of Ashfall. She had thought of the paintings — the sky over that drowned kingdom rendered in blacks and violets, lightning standing in the air like bars of a cage, and beneath it, small and painted with terrible care, the figures of people running. She had been taken to see those paintings at nine years old, as every Order child was, and she had had nightmares for a year.

She thought of them now, and she hated herself for thinking of them, and she thought of them anyway.

"All right," she had said.

Two words. She had carried them for twenty years like stones sewn into the lining of her coat, and she had told herself a thousand times that any knight would have said them, that the alternative had been treason, that the child would have been carried across regardless and her protest would have changed nothing except the color of her cloak.

All true. All beside the point. She had said them.

They had carried the child between the standing stones. Aldric had the shoulders; she had taken the feet, because someone had to, because the blanket was slipping, because a small foot was bare and cold as riverstone against her palm. The child had begun to scream again at the border — some animal knowledge waking in it, some understanding that the stones were not a door but a mouth — and Serah had walked faster, had nearly run, had wanted with a desperation that shamed her to simply put the child down and let it run back into the dark and the tall grass and away from all of them.

At the valley's edge the wards had risen to meet them. She had felt them the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — a hum in the teeth, a taste of copper and ozone. And she had felt the child's power answer, a wild flickering thing, like a bird beating itself against the inside of a jar. The wards had closed over it. The screaming had cut off as though a door had shut.

And then it was done, and they were walking back through the grey grass, and Aldric was saying, "You did well tonight, Vey," and the stars were out, all of them, indifferent and entire, and Serah had stopped in the middle of the border scar and been sick in the grass while the two other knights looked away with the practiced courtesy of people who have done this before.

The record, when it was written, read: *The storm-caller child of the Oyelaran line, exiled to the Veiled Valley by judgment of the Border Order, in accordance with the covenant.* She had signed it. Her name was the third signature. Aldric's was the first.

She had told herself it would fade. That was the cruelest thing memory does — it promises you it will fade.

* * *

The rain had thickened. Serah realized her hands were shaking and made herself look at Xanthe.

They had not moved. Their face in the coal-light was very still, the stillness of deep water, and their eyes were fixed on her with an attention that felt almost physical — like being held.

"The child's name," Xanthe said. Their voice was rough. "In the record. Was my name in the record?"

"No." Serah swallowed. "Aldric ordered the name struck. Exiles went into the valley nameless, officially. But I—" She stopped. Started again. "I read it before it was struck. I've carried it twenty years. *Xanthe.* I didn't know what it meant that I remembered. I told myself it meant nothing."

"And when you were stationed at the valley," Xanthe said. "When you were *watching* us. You knew. You knew me."

"I knew a child who'd grown into someone I couldn't reconcile with the bundle in that blanket." Serah's laugh came out broken. "You were loud, at the market. You argued with the fishmonger about the freshness of eels like it was a matter of state. And I would stand there in my grey cloak pretending to inspect the ward-stones, thinking: *this is the person I helped bury.* Not kill. Bury. Alive, in a valley, under a lie. And every year I told myself I'd request transfer, and every year I didn't, because leaving felt like abandoning you twice."

"Why tell me now?" It was not cruel. That was almost unbearable — that Xanthe, holding the worst thing Serah had ever done, would ask it gently. "Why not at Vaelmoor, or on the road? Why now?"

"Because we're close to the end of it," Serah said. "The stone, the reforging, Aldric — whatever happens next, it happens fast, and it happens to you. And I could not—" Her voice failed. She pressed on. "I could not stand at your side while you decided the fate of the realms with my lie sitting in my mouth. You deserve to choose what I am to you *knowing*. Not after. Knowing."

* * *

She told them the rest, and it came easier now, the way a wound bleeds easier once it is open.

She told them about the years after — how Aldric had risen, how his certainty had calcified into something she no longer recognized, how he had begun to speak of the covenant not as a burden shared but as an architecture of worthiness, some peoples made to hold, some made to be held. How she had gone to the valley not only to watch but, in her private heresy, to witness — to keep some small true record against the day the official one rotted through.

She told them about the night the wards fell. How she had felt it across forty miles, a sound like the world's largest bell breaking, and had known — had *known*, in her marrow — that everything the covenant had been holding back was coming due, and that the debt had her signature on it.

"I thought I was saving the realms," she said. "That's the truth of it, Xanthe. Not an excuse — the truth. I was nineteen and afraid and I believed the paintings. And the child I helped carry across that border grew up and became the only person I've ever—" She stopped.

The fire ticked. Rain threaded through the broken roof.

"The only person I've ever what?" Xanthe asked. Soft. Dangerous.

"You know what," Serah said.

"I want to hear it."

"Then hear it." She made herself meet their eyes. "I love you. I have for longer than I admitted it to myself, and I have no right to it, and I'm not asking you to return it. I'm telling you because you asked me for everything, and that's part of everything, and you will not stand at that altar at Vaelmoor with one more thing hidden from you. Not from me. Not ever again."

The silence went on so long she heard the rain change.

Xanthe rose. Crossed the space between them slowly, the way one approaches a wounded animal — or a wound. They knelt in front of her, so that their faces were level, and Serah saw that they were crying, silently, without any drama of it, tears simply present the way weather is present.

"I'm not going to forgive you tonight," Xanthe said.

"I know."

"Maybe not tomorrow. Maybe not—" Their breath caught. "Twenty years, Serah. My whole life was built on what you did. My mother's grief. My exile. Every time I asked *why* and no one would answer."

"I know."

"But you told me." Xanthe's hand came up — hesitated — and then settled, warm and shaking, against her cheek. "You could have carried it to the altar and past it. You didn't. So I'm not going to forgive you tonight. But I'm not going to leave, either. Those are different things, and I need you to understand that they're different things."

"I understand," Serah whispered.

"And when we get to Vaelmoor," Xanthe said, and something moved through their voice that she had not heard before — not the storm, something beneath the storm, something older and quieter and far more frightening, "you're going to help me ask the stone what the covenant *really* says. Not what Aldric says it says. Not what the Order taught you. What it says. Because if my whole life was built on a lie, then I want the lie's original text. I want to see its handwriting."

* * *

They did not sleep. They sat by the dying fire while the rain moved across the ruined borderlands like a slow tide, and once, near the grey hour before dawn, Xanthe's head came to rest against Serah's shoulder — not forgiveness, not absolution, just weight, just the body's honest surrender to exhaustion — and Serah sat unmoving beneath it, afraid to breathe, holding still the way one holds still for a bird that has landed on one's hand.

She thought of the border scar, and the grey grass, and the small cold foot in her palm.

She thought: *I cannot give you back the twenty years. But I can give you the truth, and the road, and everything I am, for whatever remains.*

And far to the north, past the drowned lowlands and the dead forests where the unlight had begun to eat the world, the broken half of the Oathstone waited in the dark with all its buried voices — and among them, Serah now understood, waited a voice with Xanthe's mother's cadence, and a text that Aldric Vey had spent twenty years making sure no one would ever read.

Dawn was coming. They would need to move.

Xanthe stirred against her shoulder and said, without lifting their head, "Tell me about the paintings of Ashfall. Everything the Order showed you. If my life was built on them, I want to know what's in the frame."

So Serah began to talk, and the road to Vaelmoor waited, and the last of the stars went out behind the rain.
