## 13. Rage Like Weather

The truth did not arrive all at once. That was the cruelest part of it.

Xanthe had imagined betrayal as a blade — a single bright moment of pain, clean enough to name and bleed from. What they got instead was Serah's voice, low and steady in the ruined watchtower's shadow, saying *I carried you across the border*, and the words going through them slowly, like water finding every crack in a wall that had stood for twenty years.

*You were screaming,* Serah had said. *You wouldn't stop. I was nineteen and I held you and I told myself it was mercy. I told myself that for two decades, Xanthe. I made myself believe it.*

Xanthe had walked away. Not run — walked, one foot and then the other, out past the tower's broken stair, past the lean-to where they'd slept the last three nights shoulder-to-shoulder against the cold, out onto the moorland above Vaelmoor where the grass grew pale and starved beneath a sky that still had no stars in it. Only the grey-green smear of the false aurora, the unlight's bruise on the horizon, and below that, far below, the scattered fires of the refugees who had begun to gather at Vaelmoor like iron filings around a lodestone.

They walked until the tower was a tooth behind them and then they stopped, because there was nowhere left to walk to, and that was when the shaking started.

Not cold. They knew cold; the valley had taught them cold every winter of their exile, the kind that got into the joints and stayed. This was different. This started in their sternum and radiated outward, and with every breath something rose in them that had no name except the oldest one: storm.

*Twenty years.* Twenty years of thin soup and thinner kindness, of neighbors who crossed the street, of wards pressing down on their skin like a hand over the mouth. And the whole time — the whole *time* — the person who had put them there had been alive, grown, armored, and eventually walking beside them. Sharing fires. Sharing watches. Sharing—

Xanthe's throat closed.

Sharing the night on the borderlands when Serah had wrapped her cloak around both of them and said, into the dark, almost too quietly to hear, *I'm glad it was you the wards hid. I'm glad I get to know you.*

*I helped forge the lie of exile,* Serah had said tonight. *I believed I was saving the realms. I don't know if that makes it better or worse.*

It made it worse. It made everything worse, because Xanthe could not even have a clean hatred for it. If Serah had been cruel, they could hate her cleanly. If she had been indifferent, they could dismiss her. But she had been *kind*, all these weeks, with this same catastrophe sitting inside her chest like a swallowed stone — and every kindness was now retroactively poisoned, every shared meal a performance, every look Xanthe had thought meant something revealed instead as surveillance wearing love's face.

*She was sent to watch the valley, not protect it.*

Had she reported on them? Had there been letters, signals, some code scratched into doorposts? Had Serah looked at Xanthe across a campfire and seen a charge, an assignment, a problem to be managed?

The shaking deepened. The air changed.

Xanthe felt it before they understood it — a pressure drop, a taste like copper coins on the back of the tongue, the fine hairs of their arms lifting. The magic that the wards had smothered for twenty years, the magic they had spent weeks learning to hold without spilling, turned over inside them like something waking from a long sleep and finding the house empty of everyone but its keeper.

No. Not empty. Robbed.

"Stop," Xanthe said aloud, to themselves, to the sky. Their voice came out wrong — doubled, resonant, as though the air itself were repeating it a half-second late.

They thought of the training. Serah's hands correcting their stance. *Storms Without Permission* — that was what Serah had called the first lesson, half a smile under the exhaustion. *Magic that answers grief is still your magic, Xanthe. It waits for your word or it takes everything.*

But Serah lied. Serah had always been lying. And the memory of her voice was itself unbearable now, and the storm knew it, and the storm rose toward that memory the way lightning rises toward the tallest thing in a field.

"I said *stop*—"

The world went white-violet.

* * *

Afterward — after, though Xanthe would never be able to say how long "after" was — they were on their knees in flattened grass, and it was raining upward.

That was the first thing they noticed, absurdly: droplets climbing off the moor in silver threads, drawn into the rotating column of cloud above them, so that the rain fell and un-fell in the same breath. The second thing was the silence inside the noise — a strange, pressurized hush at the storm's heart, as if they knelt in the eye of something enormous, and outside that eye the world was being rearranged.

Lightning walked the horizon in long deliberate strides. Not the jagged fork-and-crack of ordinary storms but something older, branching patterns like frost-ferns burned across the underside of the clouds, each flash followed by thunder that arrived not as sound but as a feeling in the bones, in the teeth, in the wet earth trembling under Xanthe's palms.

And the memories were going.

They didn't feel it happen. That was the terrible trick of it — the covenant's price was invisible from the inside, like bleeding from a wound you cannot see. But Xanthe had learned the small signs over the weeks of training: the faint wrongness when they reached for yesterday and found it smoothed over, a day with no texture, a name that took a heartbeat too long to surface. Each small working cost a small forgetting.

This was not a small working.

Xanthe felt the storm pulling from them the way a river pulls from its banks — not stealing exactly, *exchanging*, the old bargain written into the Oathstone's covenant before either realm had names. Sky-magic for earth-memory. Power for past. The storm drank, and things went dark in Xanthe's mind like windows going out one by one down a long street.

They tried to count what was leaving and couldn't. That was the horror — you cannot inventory what is already gone. They reached for their mother's face and found it whole, thank the drowned gods, found her voice still singing in the fractured cadence they'd heard echoed in the Oathstone. They reached for the valley — the market square, the baker's boy with the burn-scarred hands, the smell of the ward-stones after rain — and it was all there, intact, the exile years holding fast.

But somewhere in the middle distance of their own life, something flickered.

A fire. There had been a fire — many fires? — shared with someone. A joke about rations. The way hardtack broke. Whose voice? Whose laugh, low and surprised out of her, on what night, over what—

Gone. Smoothed over. A warm shape in memory with no face in it, like a room after the furniture is removed, the outlines still ghosted on the walls.

"No," Xanthe whispered, and the whisper came out as thunder. "No, wait—"

The storm did not wait. Storms never waited; that was the first lesson, the real first lesson, the one underneath the one Serah had taught. You could not command weather you had become. You could only decide, afterward, what ruins to live in.

So Xanthe did the only thing left. They stopped fighting the rage and aimed it.

*There.* Through the tearing wind, down the moor, toward the bruised horizon where the unlight pooled and crawled — the hollow-born, the creatures of stolen dark, the war that had eaten their whole life. If the storm wanted everything, let it take its price out of *them*. Let it go where it hurt.

Xanthe opened their hands, palms up, into the ascending rain, and gave the storm a direction.

What happened then, they would piece together later from the testimony of others, because they saw almost none of it from inside. Serah, running up the moor with her sword drawn and her face white, would describe a pillar of light standing on the hilltop, and the unlight along the horizon recoiling from it like a living thing scalded, and the false aurora ripping open in one place — one clean place — through which, for eleven seconds, actual stars were visible. Old stars. Real ones.

Eleven seconds of stars, bought with a year of Xanthe's life.

* * *

The storm ended the way a held breath ends — not gradually, but in a single gasp.

The column of cloud collapsed inward and dissolved. The upward rain became ordinary rain, falling wearily straight down, and Xanthe knelt in the wreck of the grass with their hair plastered to their skull and their hands shaking now with plain human exhaustion, and tried to remember the last year.

One year. That was what the storm had taken. Not scattered days this time — a season of them, swept clean, a swath of their life mowed like a field.

They sat back on their heels and did the arithmetic of loss. They knew the exile — twenty years, whole. They knew the night the stars went out. They knew Vaelmoor, the Oathstone halves, Aldric Vey's drowned cathedral and his honeyed offer, the queen of Serevan and her brutal contract. Those were older than a year; the storm had not touched them.

But between the night the wards fell and tonight there should have been months — the road beyond the Veil, the ambush, the refugee camp, the training —

Training. Yes. They had trained. They remembered deciding to train, remembered the resolution of it, and they remembered tonight's fury in full, vivid, merciless detail. But the middle — the weeks of it, the lessons, the failures, the slow learning of their own power — had gone soft and featureless, like a landscape viewed through fogged glass. They could see that things had happened. They could not see the things.

And threaded through the fog, those faceless warm shapes. Fires. Laughter. A hand steadying theirs during a working gone wrong. Kindnesses with the person erased out of them.

Someone had been with them. Someone whose name—

"Serah," Xanthe said aloud, testing it, and the name came easily, attached to facts: knight of the Border Order. Guide. Bearer of secrets, finally confessed. Traitor.

The name held. But everything else around it had thinned to gauze, and when Xanthe tried to picture her face — really picture it, not the idea of it — what came was oddly generic, a soldier-shaped absence wearing armor.

Which was fine. Which was perhaps for the best. She had lied to them since before they had memories worth keeping; losing the memories of her kindness was no loss at all. It was surgery. It was the storm doing them a favor, burning away the infected flesh.

They stood, swaying, and told themselves this very firmly, twice, while somewhere inside them a faceless warmth went on glowing like an ember in a burnt house, refusing to explain itself.

* * *

Serah found them by lantern-light an hour later, picking her way up the torn moor with the lantern held high and terror in every line of her body, and Xanthe watched her come and felt — nothing. Or worse than nothing: a polite, assessing blankness, the way one regards a stranger approaching across a dark field. Armor, sword, wet hair plastered to her forehead, a limp she was trying to hide. Facts. Inventory. No weight.

That absence frightened Xanthe more than the storm had.

"You're alive." Serah's voice cracked on the second word. She stopped three paces off, close enough to touch, and her eyes went over Xanthe the way a medic's do — scalp, hands, pupils — and lingered there, searching. Searching for something. "Xanthe?"

"I'm fine."

"What do you remember?" Too fast. Too raw to be anything but fear. "Tell me what you remember."

Xanthe frowned. Rain dripped off their chin. "The wards fell. We came to Vaelmoor. You—" The betrayal rose again, fresh as the first hour of it, and their hands curled. "You confessed. I walked out. Then the storm." They gestured at the flattened moor, the scorched fern-patterns of lightning still visible in the mud. "It took a year, I think. Maybe more. The middle's gone. The training, the road — pieces. Why? What am I missing?"

Something happened to Serah's face. Xanthe watched it happen and could not read it, which was absurd — they had spent weeks reading this woman, hadn't they? Hadn't they once been able to tell her mood from the set of her shoulders alone? The certainty was there; the evidence was not. A room with ghost-outlines on the walls.

"Nothing," Serah said, and her voice was perfectly level, which Xanthe recognized now — recognized intellectually — as the voice she used when she was choosing, again, to carry something alone. "Nothing you're missing. We should get back down. The storm tore the aurora — the refugees saw stars for the first time in twenty years. They'll want to see you. Both of us."

She turned to lead the way and Xanthe followed, and neither of them spoke, and the rain fell gently on the ruin of the moor, and three paces behind her, Xanthe stepped past the spot where — though the mud remembered it, though the lantern light fell across it — a cloak had once been spread over two people against the cold, and felt absolutely nothing at all.

Except that, at the edge of the lantern's reach, the ember in the burnt house flickered.

And down in the valley, in the refugee camp, the torn sky was already beginning to knit closed — and beyond it, patient and vast, the starless dark gathered itself to learn what had hurt it, and to whom.
