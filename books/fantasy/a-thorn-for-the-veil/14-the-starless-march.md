## 14. The Starless March

The land began to die from the edges inward.

Serah saw it first from the eastern wall of Vaelmoor, where the citadel's shattered ramparts overlooked the old borderlands — a horizon that had once been forest, then ash, and was now becoming nothing at all. Not burned. Burned left something behind: charcoal, bone, the geometry of what had stood before. This was different. The trees at the forest's rim were simply *absent*, as if someone had cut them out of the world with scissors too fine for the eye to follow, and behind them lay a smooth gray nothing that caught no light and cast no shadow and made her eyes water when she looked too long.

The unlight was eating the ground itself now.

"It crossed the Serevan marches in the night," reported Denna Kell, one of the few surviving officers of the Border Order, her voice flat with the particular exhaustion of people who have stopped being surprised by catastrophe. She spread a map across the parapet stones and weighted its corners with broken arrowheads. "Three villages gone. Not destroyed — *gone*. The refugees say there's no rubble. No bodies. Just a hole in the world shaped like everything that used to be there."

Serah studied the map without touching it. The gray stain crept southward like frost spreading across a window, and at its center — if the reports could be trusted — sat the place where Vaelmoor's outer wards had failed.

"How long until it reaches us?"

"At this pace? Nine days. Ten." Denna's jaw tightened. "If the pace holds."

It would not hold. Serah had been a soldier long enough to know that catastrophes did not maintain pace; they accelerated. The dark was feeding now — on land, on memory, on whatever it found — and fed things grew.

"Then we have nine days," she said, "to build something worth defending."

* * *

They came in their thousands, the way water comes through a cracked dam — slowly at first, then all at once.

Serah stood at the great south gate of Vaelmoor on the third morning and watched the road fill with them. Refugees from every realm. She recognized the tell-tales of each by now: the valley folk with their woven cloaks and wary eyes, children of exile who had never seen a border in their lives and were now crossing three of them in a week. Serevani fisher-families hauling salt-crusted carts, their queen's banners torn down or folded away — an act of quiet treason Serah chose not to notice. Hill clans from the north, silent and armed, moving in tight family units like migrating birds. Even a handful of Border Order deserters, still wearing fragments of gray-and-silver livery, unable to meet her eye.

They came because Vaelmoor was the only thing left between them and the dark, and because desperation makes pilgrims of us all.

"Water lines here," she told Denna, walking the gateyard as it transformed into a city overnight. "Fires along the east wall only — smoke draws attention, and I don't know yet whether the unlight notices warmth, but I'd rather not learn by experiment. Wounded to the undercroft. And keep the Oathstone chamber sealed. Nobody goes near it without my word."

"And the storm-caller?" Denna asked carefully.

The question landed where Serah had known it would land. She had been bracing against it for days.

"Xanthe works where they choose," she said. "No orders. They're not a soldier."

"They're the reason half these people are alive."

"I know." Serah watched the crowd part, far off across the gateyard, around a figure in a storm-gray cloak. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

Because they were famous now, Xanthe Oyelaran. Famous in the way saints are famous — hastily, desperately, with more need than accuracy. Word had run ahead of them through the refugee columns: *the storm-caller broke the ambush at Thornmere Pass; the storm-caller called lightning out of a dead sky; the storm-caller is going to save us.*

Serah had heard the stories too, and each one was true, and each one cost more than the telling suggested. She had watched Xanthe work. She had seen them stand in the open with their arms raised and their hair lifting in wind that came from nowhere, dragging thunder out of an atmosphere that should have had none left in it. She had seen the hollow-born come apart under those strikes like shadows at noon.

And she had seen what followed. The small pauses. The way Xanthe would finish speaking mid-sentence sometimes, mouth open on a word that had evaporated. The way they looked at familiar objects — a cup, a knife, a certain bend in the road — with the faint frown of a stranger reading directions.

She had seen them lose things. Small things, mostly. A childhood story about a dog. The name of the village where they'd bought their boots. Once, terrifyingly, for nearly a minute, the word for *west*.

And she had seen Xanthe hide it. The little smiles that arrived a beat too late. The careful deflections. The way they had started asking questions framed as jokes — *remind me, did I ever tell you about—* and then abandoning the question when Serah's face answered for her.

Serah kept count. It was a grim arithmetic, but she was good at grim arithmetic. By her reckoning, Xanthe had spent — had *paid* — perhaps two months of themselves since the wards fell. Two months of a life, scattered into the sky like seed grain, feeding storms.

And after the night at the drowned cathedral, after Aldric Vey and his honeyed justice, after the truth Serah had finally given them like a surgeon giving news — after all of it, Xanthe had raged, and the rage had taken a year.

A year. Serah turned the number over in the quiet hours and could not make it fit inside anything she understood. A year of Xanthe's life was simply *gone*, including — though Xanthe didn't know it, couldn't know it — most of the autumn they had spent together on the road. The shared fire. The argument about poetry. The night before Serevan, when Xanthe had almost said something and Serah had almost let them.

All of it, paid out into weather.

Now Xanthe moved through the refugee camp of Vaelmoor like a beloved ghost, and the crowds loved them, and Xanthe was kind to everyone, and when their paths crossed in the gateyard — as they did each morning, inevitably, the camp being what it was — Xanthe greeted Serah with warm, polite, terrible courtesy.

"Good morning, Serah. The east wall looks stronger today."

"Thank you. You slept?"

"Well enough." A smile. Correct, friendly, aimed slightly to the left of her heart. "Is there somewhere you need me?"

There was nowhere. There was everywhere. There was the space beside her where Xanthe used to walk without asking, close enough that their sleeves brushed, back when they had both pretended not to notice.

"No," Serah said. "Rest, if you can. Tomorrow will want you."

"Tomorrow always does." Another smile, and then they were gone into the crowd, and a hundred voices rose around them — *storm-caller, storm-caller* — and Serah stood alone in the middle of the city she was building for other people to be saved in, and felt the specific loneliness of loving someone who looks at you the way one looks at a helpful innkeeper.

She deserved it. That was the worst of it. She had carried the child across the border. She had forged the lie. She had watched the valley for twenty years and called it duty. Every polite distance Xanthe kept from her now was a wall she herself had built, stone by stone, over decades. Love without reciprocity was not even the right phrase for it. It was love meeting the exact shape of its own making.

She went back to the walls.

* * *

On the fifth day the unlight reached the river.

Serah felt it before anyone told her — a wrongness in the air pressure, a hush like the held breath of ten thousand people. From the north wall she watched the Graywater simply stop. The river ran up to a line of perfect absence and ceased, its current dissolving not into mist but into nothing, and beyond that line the far bank was already featureless gray, and the gray was widening.

Behind her, the refugees began to scream in low, hopeless registers, the sound of people recognizing a thing they had hoped never to see again.

"Get everyone inside the inner ring," Serah said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Wake the masons. I want barricades on the causeway within the hour. And find me the storm-caller."

Xanthe found her first. Of course they did — drawn to crisis the way they were drawn to storms, some instinct older than memory steering them. Serah turned and there they were at the top of the north stair, cloak snapping in the dead wind, looking past her at the dying river with an expression she remembered from another life: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone greeting an old enemy across a battlefield.

"It's faster than your maps said," Xanthe said.

"Yes."

"Nine days was optimistic."

"Very." Serah hesitated. Everything she wanted to say stood behind a door she had locked herself. "You don't have to do what they're all hoping you'll do. Whatever happens at the stone — the choice is yours. It was always yours. I just need you to know that someone—" *someone who loves you,* her mind supplied, uselessly, truthfully, "*—someone believes that.*"

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind their eyes — a shadow of a shadow, a shape glimpsed through fog. For one heartbeat Serah thought: *it's still in there. Some part of them remembers.*

"You're very loyal," Xanthe said gently. "To people you barely know."

The words went in clean, like a blade designed for the purpose. Serah nodded, because nodding was the only motion available.

"The causeway," she said. "When it comes, it'll come across the causeway. Be ready."

"I'm always ready." A pause. Then, quieter, almost puzzled at themselves: "I trust you, do you know that? I've decided to. I can't think why — you haven't earned it yet, by your own account — but there it is. Like a stone in my boot. I keep deciding to trust you, and I don't remember why, and I keep doing it anyway."

Serah's throat closed. She managed, barely: "Then trust it. The instinct. It knows better than either of us."

* * *

The seventh day, the starless march arrived.

There was no battle in any sense Serah had ever trained for. Armies clash; armies have shapes, intentions, flanks. The dark had none of these. It came across the ruined countryside like a tide with a grudge, and where it touched the outer barricades the wood did not burn or break but *unmade*, plank by plank, until the defenses looked like drawings rubbed out by a wet thumb.

The defense, such as it was, happened in the gaps between unmaking. Serah commanded from the inner wall, hoarse, bleeding from a gash on her forearm she did not remember taking. Denna held the causeway with forty survivors of the Order and lost eleven of them to nothing at all — men who simply walked into the gray and were subtracted. Serevan archers fired volleys into the dark that vanished without effect. The hill clans sang, of all things, old warding songs, and where the songs were loudest the gray slowed, and Serah filed that away with the desperate precision of a drowning woman cataloguing driftwood.

And above it all, at the breach point, stood Xanthe.

She saw them call the storm twice that day. The first time was controlled — a lance of white fire out of a black sky, splitting a surge of hollow-born into drifting cinders, and the refugees cheered so loudly the sound reached her like a physical blow. The second time was later, when the dark pressed the causeway and everything was failing, and Xanthe tore the sky open with both hands and brought down something that was not quite lightning anymore, something vaster and angrier, a whole season of weather compressed into ninety seconds of white ruin.

The dark recoiled. The causeway held. The refugees wept and sang Xanthe's name.

And Serah, watching from the wall, saw what the cheering crowds did not see: Xanthe on their knees in the mud afterward, hands shaking, staring at their own fingers as though counting something missing. Their lips moved — a question asked of no one. *What was I just about to remember?*

She climbed down to them. It took forever. Her body moved like it was wading through grief.

"You saved them," she said.

"Did I?" Xanthe looked up. Mud on their face. Exhaustion like a second skin. And beneath the exhaustion, that polite unfamiliarity — but frayed tonight, worn thin, showing glimpses of the warp beneath. "I can't hold all of it in my head anymore, Serah. The names. The faces. People thank me and I smile and half the time I don't know them, and I laugh at jokes I apparently already heard, and I—" They stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Something is leaving me and I can't even grieve it properly because I forget what I'm grieving before the tears dry."

Serah knelt in the mud beside them. Close enough to touch. She did not touch. Twenty years of discipline, and this was what it had refined into: the ability to kneel an arm's length from the person she loved most and keep her hands empty.

"When this is over," she said quietly, "whatever you choose at the stone — break it, reforge it, hurl it into the sea — I will help you. And afterward, whatever's missing, whatever's left, you won't face it alone. That's the only promise I have left to give, and it isn't much, but it's true. It's the truest thing I own."

Xanthe lowered their hands. Looked at her — really looked, longer than courtesy required — and Serah saw the frayed edge catch on something, snag, hold.

"There it is again," Xanthe murmured. "That feeling. Like a word on the tip of my tongue, except it's not a word. It's—" They frowned, shook their head, and the moment slid away like water off oiled leather. "Never mind. I'm tired. We should sleep. Tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow the dark reaches the walls."

"Tomorrow, then." Xanthe rose, swaying slightly, and offered Serah a hand up out of the mud — an absurd courtesy, a courtier's gesture in a charnel yard — and for the length of that touch, that brief grip of hand on forearm, Serah allowed herself to feel all of it at once: the weight of the arm, the warmth, the calluses she knew by name, the terrible ordinary fact of them alive and solid and looking at her with the friendly blankness of a stranger.

Then Xanthe walked toward the fires, and the crowd closed around them like water, and Serah stood alone in the mud.

From the north wall, a sentry began to shout.

"My lady! My lady — the gray — it's stopped—"

Serah turned. Beyond the causeway, beyond the churned and half-eaten battlefield, the unlight had halted — hung motionless along a line a mile distant, vast and patient and utterly silent, like a beast lying down to wait.

And rising above that line, faint against the starless sky, she saw a light that had no business existing. Cold. White. Shaped like half of a broken stone.

Aldric Vey had come to watch the end begin.

And he was beckoning.
