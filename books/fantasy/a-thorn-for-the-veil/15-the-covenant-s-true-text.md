## 15. The Covenant's True Text

The fracture sang to me in my sleep.

Not a sound, exactly — nothing so kind as sound. It was more like the memory of a voice heard through water, rising through the stone floor of Vaelmoor's broken heart and pooling somewhere behind my sternum. I woke on the third night with my mouth full of salt and the taste of someone else's grief, and I lay there in the dark of our ruined chamber, listening to the citadel breathe.

Serah slept across the room, or pretended to. She had taken to sleeping facing the door, sword within reach of her hand, ever since the hollow-born had learned to climb. Her breathing was too even for true sleep — she was a woman who had spent twenty years guarding a lie, and even now, even after everything she had confessed about watching rather than protecting, she could not quite surrender the habit of vigilance. But she did not stir when I rose, and I was grateful. Some things a person has to walk toward alone.

* * *

The Oathstone waited where it always waited: in the sunken vault beneath the shattered keep, half-buried in rubble and moonless dark, its fractured surface drinking what little light the lanterns gave and returning none. The missing half left a wound-shaped absence in its heart, an emptiness you could almost hear — Aldric Vey carried that absence north, cradled it like a child until it made him something half-divine and wholly wrong. What remained here was enough. What remained here remembered everything.

I knelt before it as I had every night since we arrived, palms flat against cold stone, and let the voices come.

They came the way weather comes. First a pressure, then a stirring, then a downpour. A hundred lifetimes pressed into mineral — the covenant's makers, its keepers, its victims, all of them speaking at once in a chorus that should have been madness but had become, over these long weeks, almost companionable. An old man reciting harvest tallies from three centuries dead. A girl laughing in a language no one alive spoke anymore. A soldier counting his remaining arrows over and over, forty-one, forty-one, forty-one, stuck forever at the number before the end.

I had learned to sort them. That was the strange gift the stone offered those who listened long enough: not power, exactly, but *order*. The voices arranged themselves around whatever question you carried into the dark.

Mine tonight was the oldest one I had: *What did they actually write?*

Because Serah had told me the covenant's public text. Every knight of the Border Order memorized it, she said, before they were permitted to swear their oaths. *Magic is a flood; the covenant is the levee.* Sealed, bound, suppressed, for the good of all realms. Clean words. Simple words. Words carved above the gates of a valley built to cage a child.

But the stone remembered what came before the carving. And lately, beneath the familiar litany, I had begun to hear another voice — older, quieter, worn thin by two thousand years of being talked over. It spoke in cadences I knew. Cadences I had no right to know, since my mother had died before I could walk, and yet there it was, in the way the voice paused before hard truths, in the way it softened at the ends of sentences like a hand smoothing a wrinkle from cloth.

I had stopped telling Serah about that voice. It felt like something I was owed privately — one inheritance the world had failed to steal from me.

Tonight, the old voice came forward first.

"You're back," it said, in the tongue of the founders, which the stone translated into meaning directly, bypassing language altogether. "You keep asking the wrong question, little storm."

"What's the right one?" I asked aloud. My voice sounded small in the vault, swallowed by stone.

"Not *what did they hide from you.* Ask instead: *what did they have to change to hide it?* Lies are heavy things. You can always find them by what had to be moved out of the way."

And then the stone opened, the way it only did when it decided to, and showed me.

* * *

I will try to set down what I saw, though words feel inadequate — the memory-voices did not show me images so much as they poured the past through me, let me wear it briefly like a coat cut for someone else's shoulders.

I stood — was standing, two thousand years gone — on the summit of this very mountain, before Vaelmoor was ruin, when its spires were whole and white as bone. The sky was full of stars then, so many stars the darkness between them seemed the exception rather than the rule. And gathered on that summit were representatives of every realm: sky-priests robed in storm-grey, their hair crackling with static; earth-wardens with soil worked permanently into the creases of their hands; sea-speakers trailing brine and kelp; and the ember-folk of the deep realms, their skin banked with slow fire.

And at the center, holding a stone the size of a heart — unbroken, whole, warm — stood a woman whose voice I recognized before I saw her face.

My mother. Or the woman whose blood had become my mother's blood, generations downstream, the cadence passed down like an heirloom. In the memory I understood that this was Maerin Oyelaran, First Storm-Caller, and that everyone on that summit had come because of her.

She raised the stone, and she spoke, and the stone carried her voice into me across two millennia:

"We are not here to bind magic," she said. "Hear me. Whoever writes otherwise has already betrayed us. We are here to *share* it."

The crowd stilled. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"Sky-magic without earth burns the world bare — I have seen the scorch-winters of my grandmother's age. Earth-magic without sky grows dense and blind, choking on its own roots. The sea drowns what it cannot carry; the ember-realms calcify into glass. None of us can hold what we are alone. So we make this covenant: that every realm carries a portion of every other realm's burden. That the storm-callers teach the wardens to read weather. That the wardens teach the callers to ground lightning. That the stone holds not our magic *prisoner*, but our magic *in common* — a ledger of debts and gifts, renewed each year at the turning, so that no realm ever again hoards power while its neighbors starve."

Her hands closed around the Oathstone, and it flared — and I felt, through her hands, what the stone actually was. Not a lock. A loom. Threads of every realm's magic running through it, woven together, each thread strengthened by the tension of the others. When one realm pulled too hard, the others felt the pull and answered. It was a promise made mechanical. A treaty you could touch.

"The price is memory," she said, and here her voice gentled, the way mine apparently gentled, the way I would learn later to hate and love. "Whoever works the loom gives it threads of themselves. Small ones. A name, a face, an afternoon. This is honest. This is how it should be — power that costs nothing is power that will be abused. We do not seal magic away. We pay for it, together, in the only coin that cannot be counterfeited."

And the assembled realms swore it. I watched them swear it, one by one, and the stone drank their oaths and hummed like a hive.

For four hundred years, the memory showed me, it worked. I saw the shared harvests. I saw storm-callers walking freely through lowland villages, teaching farmers to seed clouds, learning in turn how to root their lightning in deep soil so it fed the fields instead of scarring them. I saw children of every realm attending a school on this very mountain. I saw the ledger renewed each year, and I saw the memories paid — small losses, honestly given, mourned openly and then released, the way you release ash from a burned letter.

It was beautiful. That was the worst part. It was genuinely, achingly beautiful, and then I watched it be murdered.

* * *

The memory shifted, and the light changed. The same summit, but colder. Fewer faces. The sky-priests stood apart now, on the far side of a line someone had drawn in the frost, and the earth-wardens stood on the other side with their arms crossed, and between them the Oathstone sat on its altar looking — I cannot say how I knew this, but I knew it — *tired.*

A man stood at the altar. High Warden Corvane Ashgrave, the voices named him, and even his name sounded like a locked door. He held a chisel.

"The burden is unequal," he was saying, to a council half-empty with absences. "That is the flaw none of you will admit. The sky-realm's magic costs the most to share — every working strips memory from the caller — while the earth-realm pays in soil and season, which renew. Four hundred years of this ledger, and the storm-caller lines are *thinning*. Their memories erode faster than any other blood. Their children are born lighter. Something must be done."

"Then rebalance the ledger," said a sea-speaker. "Reweave the shares."

"No." Ashgrave set the chisel against the stone. "The imbalance is structural. Sky-magic is inherently destabilizing — look at the scorch-winters, look at the storms. The answer is not to rebalance the sharing. The answer is to *separate*. Let each realm keep what it was born with and nothing more. Let the sky-folk keep their storms — sealed behind wards, where their instability can harm no one else. Let the stone become what it should always have been: a boundary, not a bridge."

"That is not the covenant," said an old woman at the edge of the assembly, and her voice — oh, her voice went through me like a blade, because it was the founder's voice again, aged now, Maerin's granddaughter perhaps or granddaughter-of-the-heart, keeper of the true text. "Corvane, that is the *opposite* of the covenant. The stone cannot be made a cage. It doesn't work that way. It holds what was sworn into it — burdens *shared* —"

"The stone," said Corvane Ashgrave, "holds what is written into it. And texts can be edited."

He turned the chisel. And the memory showed me the rewriting — not as violence, exactly, though there was violence enough in it, but as *surgery*. The Oathstone fought him. The stone screamed in a register below hearing; every person on that summit fell to their knees bleeding from the nose. But Ashgrave had prepared. He had spent years, the surrounding voices murmured, years quietly binding lesser stones into a harness, building a frame of compulsion around the Oathstone's heart, and into that frame he carved the new text while the old text still lived beneath it:

*Magic is a flood. The covenant is the levee.*

*Sky-magic is sealed. The storm-caller lines are warded. For their protection. For everyone's.*

And beneath it, buried but never fully erased — because stone does not forget, stone only endures being silenced — the original words went on glowing, faint as embers under ash, waiting two thousand years for someone to kneel close enough to read them.

The wards went up that winter. I watched them go up — great lattices of compelled stone stretched across mountain passes, and the sky-folk herded behind them, and among the herded families a lineage of Oyelaran storm-callers who had signed the founding treaty, whose names were on the original covenant, who had every legal right to stand on this summit and were instead loaded into wagons like livestock.

The last thing the memory showed me was small, and it undid me more than all the rest. A child in one of those wagons, pressing a palm flat against the new ward as the border closed, and the ward *stinging* her, and her drawing back her hand and looking at it with an expression I knew intimately, because I had worn it my entire life in front of mirrors: the expression of someone discovering that their own skin is considered a crime.

* * *

I came back to myself on the vault floor. I don't remember falling. My cheeks were wet; the lantern had burned down to a nub; somewhere far above, wind moved through the ruined spires of Vaelmoor with a sound like a congregation whispering.

I lay there a long time, letting the truth settle into me the way sediment settles into water.

Every story I had been told was a footnote to a forgery. The exile was real — the wagons were real, the sting of the ward was real, twenty years of suppression crushing my magic flat was real — but the *justification*, the great moral architecture erected around it, the *magic is dangerous, magic must be caged, your suffering is everyone's safety* — all of it was carved by a man with a chisel and a grudge, over words that said the opposite. My people hadn't been imprisoned for their own good. They'd been imprisoned because sharing had been declared inconvenient, and inconvenient things get reclassified as threats.

And Serah — faithful, guilt-ridden Serah, who had confessed her part in carrying me across the border, who had spent twenty years stationed at the valley watching a prisoner and calling it protection — Serah had been guarding a wall built on a lie so old the lie had fossilized into scripture. She'd memorized the forged text. She'd sworn her oaths on it. She'd broken her order and her career and nearly her soul trying to repair a covenant that was never worth repairing.

Because that was the thought that rose last, and coldest, and sharpest: *reforging the covenant as written means restoring the forgery.*

Everyone — Serah, the refugee camps, the scattered Border Order survivors, the whole desperate machinery of resistance gathering toward Vaelmoor — everyone believed the mission was to reunite the two halves of the Oathstone and reforge the covenant. Restore what was broken. Return the world to balance. It sounded righteous. It sounded like the obvious ending of the story.

But the covenant *as written* — the living text, the original, the one the stone still remembered — was not what anyone meant by "the covenant." If I reforged the stone with the forged text still carved in its face, I would rebuild the cage. I would seal the sky-magic again. I would hang the wards back up myself, with my own hands, and call it victory.

Unless the reforging could be done from the buried text. Unless the original words could be brought up out of the ash and written large again — shared burden, honest cost, a loom instead of a lock. And whether that was possible, whether a stone could be rewoven around words it had been forced to swallow, whether two thousand years of compulsion could be peeled away like bark — the stone itself might know. But the stone was half a thing, and its missing heart was in the drowned cathedral to the north, in the hands of a man who had bonded himself to the stolen half for decades until the boundary between man and stone had dissolved entirely.

Aldric Vey wanted the covenant reforged *as written*. He had told me so, with the serene certainty of someone offering a gift. Now I finally understood which "as written" he meant. He meant the forgery. He had spent decades pressed against the carved face of the lie, and either he had never found the truth beneath it — or he had found it, and chosen the cage anyway, because the cage had a hierarchy and he knew exactly what rank he'd hold inside it.

The lantern guttered out.

In the dark, I put my hand against the fractured stone one more time, and the founder's voice came, faint, tired, kind.

"Now you see it, little storm. Now you know why they feared you reading."

"What do I do?" I whispered. "If I break the forging, the wards fall everywhere — every sealed realm opens at once, everything loose, everything *unpaid*. If I restore it, I restore the lie. There's no clean path."

"There never was," the voice said gently. "There was only the honest path and the carved one. Your founder-ancestors chose honesty knowing it would cost. The question is whether you're a better coward than they were."

Something in the vault shifted — a settling of rubble, or maybe just the weight of the sentence landing. And beneath it, from far away, from the direction of the coast, I felt a tremor run through the stone that had nothing to do with memory. A marching rhythm. Boots. Many, ordered, advancing — and threaded through the stone's awareness of them, the cold glinting signature of Serevan steel and Serevan salt-magic.

The queen had not waited for our answer. Her price had been refused, or ignored, or simply deemed unnecessary to wait for — and her army was coming to collect the storm-caller in person.

Above me, through the broken ceiling, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. And I climbed the stairs to tell Serah that everything we were fighting for was a lie — and that the people coming to save us intended to enshrine it.
