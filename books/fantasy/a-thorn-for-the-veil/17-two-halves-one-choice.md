## 17. Two Halves, One Choice

The two halves of the Oathstone lay on the altar like a broken mouth.

Xanthe stood before them in the ruined heart of Vaelmoor, where the citadel's dome had collapsed inward centuries ago and the sky came down to meet the stone floor without apology. Rain had found its way through the wound in the roof — thin, cold threads of it that struck the altar and ran in silver veins toward the halves of the covenant. The left half was older than language, a wedge of grey crystal shot through with veins of gold that pulsed faintly, like something sleeping. The right half — Aldric's half, carried north for thirty years inside a man who had stopped being entirely a man — was darker, denser, drinking the light of the lanterns Serah had set around the chamber.

Between them, the air hummed.

Not with sound. With *attention*. As though the stone knew what was being asked of it, and was waiting to see whether Xanthe would be worthy of asking.

Behind them, at the far end of the hall, the starless dark pressed against the broken gates.

It did not batter them. It did not need to. It simply arrived, wave after patient wave of that absolute absence, and where it touched the world the world forgot how to be anything. The eastern wall of the citadel had gone quiet an hour ago — not crumbled, not burned, but *unmade*, its stones ceasing to have ever been stones. Beyond it, Xanthe could feel the valley through their half-awake storm-sense: the wards long dead, the market square empty, the Founding Festival lanterns still hanging from last year's poles, swaying over streets no one would walk again unless this night ended differently.

Serah stood three paces behind them. Xanthe could hear her breathing — controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a woman who had decided that if the dark took her, it would take her standing. She had not spoken since they'd climbed the shattered stair. There was nothing left to say that hadn't been said in blood and confession and the terrible gentleness of chapter twelve's truth: *I carried you across the border. I helped forge the lie. I watched you for twenty years because I could not stop watching.*

Xanthe had not forgiven her. They were not sure forgiveness was the shape of what lived in their chest now. It was larger than forgiveness and colder, and it had weather in it.

Aldric Vey came forward out of the shadow of the western arches.

He moved like water remembering it used to be a river. Thirty years bonded to half a god-stone had made him tall in a way that had nothing to do with height; his outline blurred slightly when he passed the lanterns, as if the eye kept trying to file him under *weather* instead of *man*. His voice, when he spoke, was kind. That was the worst thing about him. He had always been kind. Even the exiles had been conducted kindly, Xanthe now understood. Kindness was simply the tool he reached for first.

"You're trembling," Aldric said.

"I'm cold."

"You're not." He smiled, and it was a real smile, worn smooth by decades. "I trembled too, the first time I stood here. They don't tell you that part in the histories. That the founders wept at the altar. That reforging a covenant is a kind of grief, even when it's just."

Xanthe turned back to the halves. "And when it isn't just?"

"Then it is a great deal of grief." He came to stand beside them — not touching the altar, never touching the altar; some instinct older than his transformation kept his hands folded behind his back. "Listen to me, storm-caller. I have watched you since the wards fell. I have watched you fight hollow-born with lightning in your teeth and lose years of your life to pay for it. I watched you refuse a queen. I watched you forgive—" he glanced back at Serah, "—or begin to. You are the finest vessel the covenant has produced in a century. Perhaps ever."

"I don't want to be a vessel."

"No one does. That is why vessels are chosen and not volunteered." Aldric's gaze went to the halves, and something in his face softened into an expression so old it looked geological. "But consider what refusal costs. The dark out there is not evil, child. It is *hungry*. It is what became of magic when the founders stole its share and sealed it in this stone. For thirty years I have carried half of that theft in my chest, and I can tell you exactly what it dreams of. It dreams of being whole. Reforge the covenant, restore the balance as it was written, and the hunger ends. The realms return to their shapes. The stars come home. And you—" his voice dropped, gentle as snowfall, "—you get back everything the covenant took from you. Every memory you spent. Every year you burned to save a refugee camp, a bridge, a friend. All of it returns. Isn't that worth one act of duty?"

There it was. The offer he had made in the drowned cathedral, made again now with the dark at the gates and no time left for philosophy. *Justice,* he called it. The old hierarchy restored — sky-magic bound to earth, storm-callers caged in valleys, burdens distributed by birth — but dressed in restitution. Reforge the stone, and Xanthe got their stolen selfhood back. Their mother's face. The name of the village they'd been born in. The color of the sky over a home they couldn't remember.

And all it cost was everyone else's freedom, forever, in exactly the shape that had exiled a screaming child across a border twenty years ago.

Xanthe opened their hands over the altar.

* * *

The voices woke the moment their palms crossed the space between the halves.

They always came like this — not heard through the ears but remembered through the bones, memories stored in the Oathstone's fracture by every hand that had ever touched it. Xanthe had learned to walk among them these past weeks, careful as a wader in a cold river. Tonight there was no time for care. Tonight the voices rose all at once, a flood.

*A founder's voice, cracked with age:* We seal the sky to the earth, and the earth to the sea, and let each realm keep what it can carry—

*Another voice, younger, angrier, cut off mid-word:* That is not what we agreed. That is not the treaty. You've rewritten it, Maren, you've rewritten—

*A woman singing in a tongue Xanthe's blood recognized before their mind did. Their mother's cadence. Always, somewhere in the flood, their mother's cadence.*

And beneath all of them, oldest and deepest, a voice that was not a person at all. It was the sound the stone made when it was honest. It sounded like pressure before a storm. Like the held breath of the sky.

*Xanthe Oyelaran.*

They stilled. Beside them, Aldric had gone rigid; he could hear it too, then — or hear the silence where his half of the conversation should have been. The stone was speaking to Xanthe alone.

*You stand where the founders stood. Do you know what they stood on?*

"No," Xanthe whispered aloud, and felt Serah shift behind them, felt Aldric's attention sharpen like a blade being drawn slowly.

*Read us. Not the covenant as it was written. Read us as we were made.*

Xanthe closed their eyes and sank deeper.

The flood parted. And there — at the very bottom, under twenty layers of amendment and seal and fearful revision — was the original text. Not carved. *Spoken.* The first covenant, sworn on this altar before the stone was ever stone, when it had been a piece of falling sky that four realms had caught together.

*Let no realm hold the sky alone. Let no sky fall on any realm unshared. What one cannot carry, all will carry. What all refuse to carry, none shall be forced to bear.*

Xanthe's breath went out of them.

It was a treaty of shared burden. It had never been a cage. The sealing — the suppression of storm-callers, the warding of valleys, the binding of sky-magic to earth-magic in a hierarchy of keepers and kept — that had come later. Amendments. Fear wearing law's clothing. Somewhere in the first century, the founders' heirs had looked at a treaty written in mutual aid and seen only a mechanism of control, and they had rewritten it line by line until the original promise was buried so deep that even the Border Order's sealed records called the lie the truth.

Aldric had offered to reforge the covenant *as written.*

But the stone remembered what was actually written. And it was the opposite of everything either side of this war had been fighting for.

*Now you see,* said the voice like held breath. *Reforge us, and you reforge the cage — the true one, the amended one, the one that made you an exile and made the dark out there a starving thing. Break us, and—*

"Stop." Aldric's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip. He had moved — Xanthe felt the displacement of air — and now his hand hovered above Xanthe's shoulder without landing, trembling with the effort of not gripping. "Storm-caller. Whatever it's showing you, whatever it's whispering — the stone lies to protect itself. It has done so for a thousand years. That is what covenants *are*: beautiful lies that hold up the sky."

"It doesn't lie," Xanthe said. Their own voice sounded far away to them, resonant, touched with thunder that hadn't chosen to fall yet. "It remembers. There's a difference you've spent thirty years refusing to learn."

"The difference will kill every living thing in three realms."

"The cage already is killing them. Slowly. You just approved of the pace."

For a moment neither of them breathed. Then Aldric withdrew his hand, and when he spoke again his kindness had a hairline fracture running through it. "I carried half of this stone north while my eyes went white and my shadow learned to walk ahead of me. I gave it thirty years of my life so that someone worthier could stand here tonight and make the choice I was too afraid to make. Do not tell me what I have refused to learn."

Behind them, Serah spoke for the first time in an hour.

"Aldric." Her voice was hoarse but level. "When you sent me to the valley — your last order, before you went north — you told me to watch the child. Not guard. *Watch.* I asked you why, and you said, 'Because if she ever stands at that altar, I need to know which way she'll choose.'" A pause. "You already knew about the original text, didn't you. You've known for thirty years. That's why you wanted the stone reforged quickly, by someone strong enough not to listen too closely."

The silence that followed had weight and temperature. Rain ticked against the altar. Far away, beyond the broken gates, the dark shifted like a sleeper turning over.

Aldric laughed — softly, hopelessly, a sound like a door closing after the last guest has left. "You were always too sharp for your own salvation, Serah Vane."

"Yes," she said. "It's my best quality."

* * *

Xanthe stepped back from the altar.

The voices receded to a murmur, patient now, having said what they needed to say. The rain fell. The lanterns guttered. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Xanthe Oyelaran stood in the still point of their entire life and understood, with terrible clarity, that no one was going to make this decision for them.

That was, they realized, the whole point. It had always been the whole point. Every exile, every ward, every sealed record, every knight stationed to watch a child for twenty years, every queen's bargain, every half-god's gentle offer — all of it, the entire architecture of two realms, built on the conviction that someone had to decide for everyone else. That freedom was too dangerous to distribute. That burden was safer when it was assigned.

The stone's oldest voice waited. It did not push. Cages, Xanthe thought, never had to push. They only had to wait.

If they reforged the covenant, the math was simple and brutal. The dark ended tonight. The stars returned tomorrow. Three realms resumed their ancient shapes, and Xanthe got back every memory they had ever spent — including, they suspected, the ones they'd lost in the rage-storm, the golden weeks with Serah that now existed only as a gap shaped exactly like happiness. They would be whole. They would be powerful. They would be the keel of a new age of ordered skies.

And somewhere, a child born with storm-blood next winter would be measured, and registered, and if the measurement ran deep enough, carried weeping across a border by knights who believed they were saving the world. The wheel would turn. It would turn kindly. Aldric had taught them that much: it would turn *kindly.*

If they broke the stone —

Xanthe reached out with their storm-sense, the sense the wards had suppressed for twenty years and the covenant had taxed ever since, and felt the shape of what breaking meant. Magic released equally to every realm, every bloodline, every stranger in every street. No hierarchy. No keepers. No covenant deciding who was vessel and who was weather. And no more tax — no more years bleeding out of them with every strike of lightning, because the price had never been magic's price. It had been the covenant's toll, collected by the stone, hoarded in the fracture, spent to keep the cage oiled.

But the price of breaking —

The stone showed them, because the stone was honest even about its own death. Breaking it would cost the breaker everything the stone held of theirs. Every memory tied to the covenant's long jurisdiction. The valley. The exile. The wards. The festival nights. The market square. The road beyond the veil. The ambush, the camp, the drowned cathedral, the confessions.

Serah.

All of it. Every moment since the stars went out — the whole architecture of the person they had become in this terrible, luminous season — paid out in a single instant, forever.

Xanthe looked at their hands. Ordinary hands. Hands that had held lightning and a queen's refusal and, once, in a refugee camp under a sky full of ash, another person's face.

"Xanthe." Serah's voice, close now. She had come to stand beside them, armor scorched, knighthood already publicly broken on the plains below, holding nothing but herself. "Whatever you're weighing — I can see it. Don't weigh me. I'm not on the scale. I was never supposed to be on the scale."

"That's not how scales work," Xanthe said quietly. "Everything's on the scale. That's what makes it a scale."

Beyond the gates, the dark gathered. Xanthe felt it the way one feels a wave draw back from the shore — the whole starving absence of it pulling itself together for a final surge, and this time there would be no wall, no ward, no order of knights between it and the two halves of a broken treaty lying on an altar in the rain.

Aldric spoke last, and his kindness had come all the way back, and it was almost unbearable. "Whatever you choose," he said, "choose it as yourself. That is the only counsel I have left, and I have waited thirty years to give it."

Xanthe closed their eyes.

Their mother's cadence rose once more through the stone — not words, just the sound of her, the melody of a voice singing over a cradle in a home they could not picture — and beneath it the oldest voice, the held breath of the sky, offering no argument and no plea. Only the truth, and the door.

*Choose.*

Xanthe picked up both halves of the Oathstone.

They were heavier than worlds and lighter than rain. The gold veins flared; the dark half drank the lantern-light; and somewhere in the fracture the voices rose in a single chord — founders and traitors and singers and the wordless sky — waiting to learn whether they were about to be welded shut or set free.

"Serah," Xanthe said, and their voice was steady, and it was the steadiest thing in three dying realms. "Turn around. I don't want the last thing you see of me to be this."

"What? Xanthe—"

"*Turn around.*"

A pause. Then, low, fierce, a knight's obedience and something far less obedient underneath it: "No. Whatever you're about to do, I'm watching. I owe you that much. I owe myself that much."

Xanthe almost smiled. Of course she wouldn't. Of course.

They raised the halves above the altar, and the starless dark came through the gates like the end of thinking, and Xanthe Oyelaran — exile, storm-caller, vessel, nobody's — brought them together one final time, not to join them, but to hold the whole broken covenant in two hands at the still point of the world.

And broke it.
