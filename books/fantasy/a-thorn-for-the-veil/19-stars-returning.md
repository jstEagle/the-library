## 19. Stars, Returning

Serah Vane had spent twenty years learning how to wait, and she had never once been good at it.

She had waited at the border posts through winters that froze the ink in her dispatch case. She had waited in the valley, year after year, watching a child grow into a stranger with her mother's storm-grey eyes, telling herself that vigilance was a form of penance and penance was a form of love. She had waited through the starless months while the dark ate the roads and the refugees came down from the passes like leaves before a fire. She had waited at the gates of Vaelmoor while the sky itself seemed to hold its breath.

But this — this waiting was different. This was not duty dressed up as devotion. This was a woman kneeling on broken marble in the ruined heart of a shattered citadel, holding the hand of someone who did not know her name.

* * *

The breaking of the Oathstone had sounded like nothing.

That was the cruelty of it. Serah had expected thunder, a rending of the world's fabric, some sound vast enough to match the magnitude of what Xanthe was about to do. Instead there had been only a soft exhalation, like a door closing gently in an empty house, and then light — not the violent white of lightning but something older and quieter, a gold so pale it was almost silver, pouring out from between Xanthe's fingers where they gripped both halves of the stone.

It had washed over Serah like warm water. It had passed through her without stopping, because it had nothing to take from her. The price was never hers to pay.

Xanthe had made a small sound — not a scream, though Serah would hear it in her sleep for years afterward; something smaller than a scream, more intimate, the noise a person makes when they set down a weight they have carried so long they have forgotten it is not part of their body. And then the light went out of their eyes all at once, like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, and they folded forward into Serah's arms as if she were simply the nearest surface, as if she were nothing at all.

She had caught them. Of course she had caught them. Her arms had known how to do that even when the rest of her had stopped knowing anything.

Around them, the starless dark — the hollow-born, the creeping unlight that had devoured the borderlands and half the northern realms — had come apart like smoke meeting wind. There was no battle. There was no final stand. The thing that had fed on the covenant's stolen magic for twenty years simply found itself starving, and starved, and ceased. Serah remembered thinking, distantly, absurdly, that it deserved a better ending than that. Everything else had gotten one.

And then dawn had come.

Not gradually, as dawns do, grey seeping into grey. All at once, the way the stars had gone out two years ago — one breathless moment where the world changed its mind. The eastern windows of Vaelmoor's great hall, tall as trees and half of them broken, filled with a light that had no business being so gold after so long a darkness. Somewhere beyond the walls, a bird began to sing — actually sing, tentatively, as if remembering the mechanics of it — and then another answered, and then the whole ruinous valley rang with birdsong as though the world were making up for lost time.

Serah did not look at the sky. She looked at Xanthe.

They were breathing. That was the first fact, and for a long time it was the only one. Their face was slack with a peace she had never seen on it in all the months since the wards fell — since the night the stars vanished and the valley's cage shattered and everything Serah had buried came clawing back into the light. They looked younger without their memories. Not innocent, exactly. Unburdened. A face with nothing written behind it yet.

Their hand was still in hers. She held it the way you hold something you are afraid to want.

* * *

When Xanthe woke, it was to birdsong and to a stranger kneeling beside them with red-rimmed eyes.

Serah saw the exact moment recognition failed. She had braced for it — had rehearsed it, gods help her, alone in the dark hours while Xanthe slept, mouthing words to herself: *My name is Serah. I knew your mother. I helped bring you here.* She had composed speeches. She had planned honesty like a military campaign, mapped out every approach, every phrasing, every retreat.

None of it survived contact with Xanthe's eyes.

They blinked awake slowly, wincing at the light, and their gaze traveled the ruined hall — the fractured altar, the drifting motes of pale gold still hanging in the air like snow that had forgotten how to land, the distant figures of refugees and knights and Serevan soldiers standing frozen in the doorway, afraid to come closer — and then it settled on her, and it was polite. Open. Curious, even. The look you give someone who has clearly been crying over someone else.

"You're hurt," Xanthe said. Their voice was rough with sleep and disuse. "Your hands."

Serah looked down. Her knuckles were split and crusted with blood; she had no memory of when that had happened. Some time during the breaking, or after, or during the long hours of the dark's dying. She flexed them and felt nothing much.

"It's nothing," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "How do you feel?"

Xanthe considered the question with a seriousness that made her chest ache, because it was so familiar and so utterly strange at once — the same careful tilt of head, the same habit of weighing words before speaking, and none of the history that had shaped it.

"Like I've lost something," they said finally. "I don't know what."

The words landed in Serah's ribs like a blade slipped between plates. She had prepared for fear, for confusion, for anger. She had not prepared for that quiet, honest grief — grief without an object, mourning without a grave. *I don't know what.*

"I know," she said. Which was true, and insufficient, and all she had.

Xanthe sat up slowly. Serah moved to help and stopped herself, hands hovering, unsure now of the geography of touch — whether she had earned it, whether she had ever earned it, whether it mattered. In the end she simply stayed close enough to catch them if they swayed.

"I don't know you," Xanthe said. Not cruelly. Almost apologetically. "Do I?"

"No," Serah said.

It was the truth. It was also the largest lie she had ever told, larger than the exile, larger than twenty years of watching, and she told it looking directly into those storm-grey eyes because Xanthe deserved at least one person in their life who did not open with a lie.

"You were crying," Xanthe observed.

"Yes."

"Over me?"

Serah's throat worked. "Someone I—" She stopped. Started again. "Yes. Over you. You paid a very high price last night. Higher than most people will ever understand."

"And you understand it?"

"Enough." She almost smiled. It hurt her face. "I've carried pieces of it for twenty years. You carried all of it in one night."

Xanthe looked at their own hands then — turned them over, studied the palms as though expecting to find the missing memories written there in some language they'd forgotten how to read. Lightning-pale scars laced the skin, old ones, from the training, from the storms, each one a receipt for something they could no longer name. Serah watched them find the scars and watched the confusion deepen.

"These are mine," Xanthe said. "But I don't remember earning them."

"No," Serah said softly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

By midday the citadel had begun, impossibly, to fill with people.

Word had spread overnight — word always spread faster than riders could carry it, in the way of miracles — and the survivors of the starless march came streaming up the mountain road toward Vaelmoor: Border Order veterans with their torn blue banners, Serevan soldiers who had marched north to enforce a queen's claim and arrived instead to find their claim dissolved along with the covenant, farmers and weavers and children born under a sunless sky who had never seen a star until this morning.

Serah stood on the broken steps of the citadel and watched them come, and felt the strangest sensation: that she was watching the world be reborn and attending a funeral at the same time.

Queen Ysolde's herald found her there around noon — a sharp-eyed woman in sea-green silk who looked at the ruined citadel, the returning crowds, the impossible blue overhead, and delivered her message with professional neutrality: Her Majesty wished it known that the claim on the storm-caller was void, that Serevan acknowledged the covenant's end, and that Her Majesty looked forward to discussing *what comes next* with whatever powers emerged in the north. A threat wrapped in courtesy, which was the Serevan way.

"Tell her Majesty," Serah said, "that the north will discuss it when the north has buried its dead."

The herald's mouth twitched. "She'll like that answer. She won't admit it, but she'll like it."

Then the herald's gaze drifted past Serah's shoulder, and Serah turned and saw Xanthe standing in the doorway behind her, blinking in the daylight, looking at the crowds, the banners, the sky — at all of it — with the expression of someone reading a book in a language they almost speak.

"They're the one?" the herald asked quietly. "The one who broke it?"

"Yes."

"They don't remember any of it?"

"No."

The herald was silent a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she inclined her head to Xanthe — deep, formal, the bow owed to a sovereign or a saint. "Then perhaps," she said, rising, "that is its own kind of wisdom. My queen would say that whoever holds power without grievance has already succeeded where every founder failed." She gathered her silk skirts. "We'll see how long the realms let that last. Good day, Knight Vane. Or—" a glance at the tattered remnants of Serah's oath-knot, cut from her braid weeks ago on the steps before Serevan's army, "—whatever you are now."

"Whatever I am now," Serah agreed, and the herald laughed, briefly, genuinely, and was gone down the mountain.

Xanthe had heard none of it, or seemed not to. They stood in the doorway looking at Serah with that polite, searching expression, and after a moment they said:

"People keep looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm something they lost and got back." Xanthe's jaw tightened — a gesture so familiar it stopped Serah's breath. "I don't like it. I don't know what I'm supposed to be to them."

Serah took a slow breath. Here it was, then. The campaign she had rehearsed. The speech about mothers and borders and lies, all of it queued up behind her teeth.

"You don't have to be anything to them," she said. "Not today. Maybe not for a long time. What happened last night was yours — the choice and the cost. Nobody gets to collect on it. If anyone tells you otherwise, send them to me."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — not memory, not quite, but the shadow memory leaves behind, like the dent a hand presses into a mattress. "Why would they listen to you?"

"Because I broke my knighthood in front of an army to keep them off you." Serah shrugged, awkward. "It turns out that buys you a certain reputation."

"You did that for me? Someone you don't—"

"Yes," Serah said, before the sentence could finish forming, before it could become a question she couldn't survive. Then, softer: "Yes."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. The afternoon light came through the broken windows and caught the dust motes and the last fading traces of gold in the air, and Serah thought: *say something, say anything, give me a thread to hold.*

"All right," Xanthe said at last. "I believe you. I don't know why, but I believe you."

*It's because some part of you remembers,* Serah thought, and did not say. *Some part of you remembers everything, and it's decided I'm safe, and I would burn the world down rather than betray that.*

What she said was: "Good."

* * *

Evening found them on the western wall, where the stone was old enough to have gone soft at the edges, worn smooth by centuries of elbows and watching. Below, fires were springing up among the refugee camps — hundreds of them, thousands, a constellation brought down to earth. And above—

Above, the stars were coming back.

Not all at once. One by one, hesitantly, the way a house fills with light room by room as lamps are lit. First the bright ones, the navigators, burning steady and unmistakable. Then the dimmer ones, shy, testing. By full dark the whole ancient catalogue hung overhead, complete, every constellation restored to its post, and along the wall people were pointing and naming them aloud, weeping, laughing, teaching their children names the children had never needed before.

Xanthe sat with their knees drawn up, looking up.

"Do you know their names?" they asked.

"Some," Serah said. She settled onto the stone beside them — not too close, close enough. "A knight learns navigation early. The Star of the Veil. The Shepherd. The Two Sisters." She pointed, and Xanthe followed her finger with their eyes. "That one there — the bright one low on the horizon — my mother called it the Lantern. Said it was lit every night for travelers coming home."

"Was she right?"

"I don't know. She died when I was young." Serah paused. "I liked to think so."

Xanthe nodded slowly. Then, quietly: "I keep trying to remember things. About myself. Before last night." Their voice was carefully level, and Serah recognized the effort of that levelness, had heard it a hundred times in a hundred different tones. "There's nothing. It's not like forgetting. It's like... the pages were never written. Or like someone cut them out so cleanly I can't even see the edges." They hugged their knees tighter. "Did I have people? Did anyone love me?"

The question went through Serah like cold iron.

*Yes,* she thought. *Yes. Your mother loved you so fiercely she screamed herself hoarse fighting us as we carried you over the border. Yes. A knight who was supposed to be watching you fell in love with you instead, slowly, against every vow and every scrap of her better judgment, and it ruined her and remade her, and she is sitting next to you right now trying not to shake.*

"There were people," she said. Her voice held. Barely. "People who loved you. People who wronged you, too — some of the same people. It's... complicated."

"Complicated how?"

"That story isn't mine to tell," Serah said. "Some of it isn't mine to tell. When you're ready — if you're ready — there are others who can give you the pieces. But not tonight. Tonight you should just—" She gestured vaguely at the sky, the fires, the impossible returned world. "Tonight should belong to you."

Xanthe was quiet a long moment. Then: "You're protecting me again."

"I—"

"Don't deny it. I don't know how I know that, but I know it." They turned to look at her, and their eyes in the starlight were the exact color of weather coming. "You keep doing it. Standing between me and things. You did it with that silk woman today. You're doing it right now with whatever 'complicated' means." A pause. "I don't mind. I want you to know that. I don't know why I trust you. I have no reason to. But sitting next to you feels like..." They searched for the word, frowning slightly, and Serah held her breath. "...like shelter," Xanthe finished. "Like shelter before a storm. Which is a strange thing to say, because I don't remember any storms. Do I?"

Serah laughed — a short, broken sound that was half sob and she didn't bother hiding it, because hiding things from Xanthe had cost the world too much already. "No," she said. "You don't remember any storms."

"But I was one. Wasn't I?" Xanthe looked up at the sky. "Everyone looks at me like I was a storm. And these—" they touched the pale scars on their forearms, absently, tracing one without knowing what it paid for, "—these feel like weather. Like receipts. Like I bought things with parts of myself and the shopkeeper kept the change." They shook their head. "I don't know why I'm telling you all this."

"Because I'm listening," Serah said. "And because some part of you knows I won't use it against you."

"Is that true?"

"It's the truest thing about me."

* * *

Later, when the fires below had burned low and the crowd on the wall had thinned to lovers and sentries, Xanthe fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, their head tipping sideways until it came to rest against Serah's shoulder.

Serah did not move. She barely breathed.

The weight of them was familiar in a way that undid her — the exact pressure of it, the angle of their temple against her collarbone, learned across a hundred nights of watches and hard roads and one unforgettable morning in a market square when the world ended and she had caught them for the first time. Her body remembered everything. That was the treachery of bodies: they kept what the mind surrendered.

*I chose not to impose the past upon them,* she reminded herself. *I chose to walk beside them anyway.*

It had sounded noble when she'd thought it, days ago, standing in the ruins of her own knighthood. It sounded less noble now, with her heart hammering and her throat aching and the whole lost country of their shared history pressing against the inside of her ribs like a second heartbeat. Noble was easy from a distance. Up close, love without claim was just loss wearing its best clothes.

But she had made a promise — not to Xanthe, who could not remember receiving it, but to herself, on the night the stars went out, kneeling in a market square full of screaming people: *never again*. Never again a lie of protection. Never again a decision made *for* someone instead of *with* them. She had helped build a cage once, believing it was kindness. She would not build another, however gentle its bars.

So she sat still beneath Xanthe's sleeping weight, and she let the stars wheel overhead, and she grieved quietly, thoroughly, the way she did everything — and hoped, stubbornly, irrationally, the way she also did everything.

Because here was the thing the stars had taught her tonight, hanging up there complete and restored, every one of them accounted for: things lost could return. The sky had proven it. Whole constellations, snuffed out for two years, had come home. And if the sky could remember itself, then maybe — maybe — somewhere in whatever country Xanthe's memories had gone to, a market square, a laugh, a hand in theirs might also find its way back.

Maybe the pages weren't burned. Maybe they were only misplaced.

And if they never came back — if Xanthe woke tomorrow and every tomorrow after a stranger — Serah would walk beside them regardless. She would teach them the star names. She would stand between them and the queens and heralds and founders who came to collect. She would learn to be loved as a new thing, a first impression, a stranger with kind eyes — and she would count it, she decided, counting the Lantern burning low and faithful on the horizon, she would count it a fair trade for having loved them at all.

Below, a child in the camps began singing a festival song — the Founding Festival song, the one the valley sang every year on the eve of the founding, the song that had been playing, Serah realized with a jolt, the night the stars went out. The melody drifted up the wall, thin and clear, and against her shoulder she felt Xanthe stir.

"Hm," Xanthe murmured, not waking. "Know that song."

Serah went very still.

"You do?" she whispered, though they couldn't hear her.

Xanthe's brow furrowed in sleep, chasing something. "Market square," they mumbled. "Someone laughing. A hand..."

The words trailed off into breath, into sleep, into dreams Serah couldn't follow. But she sat beneath the returned stars with her heart hammering against her ribs, staring at the horizon where the Lantern burned steady and faithful, and for the first time since the breaking of the stone, hope did not feel foolish.

Somewhere, somehow, the pages were finding their way home.

And far to the south, in the coastal courts where old powers were already stirring, in halls where queens and founders had begun asking dangerous questions about a broken covenant and an unclaimed storm-caller — questions Serah could not yet hear, but whose shape she could already feel gathering on the horizon like weather — the future was drafting itself.

She would be ready.

She would walk beside Xanthe, whatever came.

She pressed her lips — brief, feather-light, a kiss given to no one, claimed by no one — to the top of Xanthe's sleeping head, and turned her face to the stars, and kept watch until morning.
