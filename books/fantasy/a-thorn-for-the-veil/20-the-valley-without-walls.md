## 20. The Valley Without Walls

The gates came down on a morning of thin gold light.

Xanthe stood in the crowd that gathered to watch it happen — because everyone gathered, because there was nothing else the valley knew how to do with itself yet — and watched men with ropes and saws and long iron pry-bars work at the ward-stones that had framed the southern pass for twenty years. The stones had been tall as doorways once, carved with the covenant's old script, humming with a suppression so constant that no one born inside the Veil had ever known silence without it. Now they were just rock. Dead rock. When the first one tipped and fell, it did not shatter so much as sigh, exhaling a breath of dust that glittered in the sun like something released.

No cheer went up. That was the strange part. Xanthe had expected cheering — had half hoped for it, some communal roar of triumph to mark the end of the cage. Instead there was only a hush, wide and uncertain, the sound of two hundred people realizing at once that a wall's absence is its own kind of presence. You could see through the gap now. You could see the road beyond the pass, winding down into country Xanthe did not remember ever having seen, though they must have seen it — must have been carried along it, screaming, on a night they could not recall.

*You paid for this,* said the hollow place where their memories used to live. *You paid for all of it.*

They didn't know if that was grief or arithmetic.

"Move," someone said behind them, not unkindly, and Xanthe stepped aside as a cart rolled past, loaded with household goods, a family bound for the lowlands with everything they owned tied down under oilcloth. The father drove. The mother waved to people she passed as though she might never pass them again. A girl of perhaps six sat atop the tallest trunk and stared back through the broken gate with her mouth slightly open, memorizing the valley the way children memorize things — completely, and without knowing why it matters.

Xanthe watched until the cart was small enough to lose between the hills.

Then they turned and walked home.

*Home.* The word still worked, technically. It pointed at a cottage on the terraced slope above the mill-race, whitewashed walls gone grey at the corners, a roof mended in three places with three different generations of tile. They had lived there since before memory. Since the beginning of themselves, which was, as far as Xanthe could now determine, roughly a year ago — the morning they'd woken in a refugee tent outside Vaelmoor with a stranger's blanket over them and a knight named Serah Vane sitting too far away, watching them with an expression they'd assumed was simple wariness.

The path up to the cottage was worn smooth by feet. Their feet, presumably. Their body remembered the grade of it even now, the way the left knee ached slightly on the steepest stretch, the place where the path narrowed between two hedges that smelled of rosemary crushed by weather. The body remembered everything. That was the cruelty of it. The body walked this path like a hymn it had sung ten thousand times, and the mind trailed behind it like a stranger invited late to the service.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was; there was hardly anything left worth locking, and besides, the whole valley had spent a season learning that locks were a habit of the caged.

Inside, the light came slantwise through east-facing windows and lay across the floor in long warm bars. A table. Two chairs, one of them patched at the seat with leather cord. A shelf of crockery arranged by size with a precision that felt like a signature. A hearth swept clean, ash bucket empty, kindling stacked in the exact right amounts for a fire not yet lit but soon.

Xanthe stood in the middle of the room and waited to feel something.

That was what they had come for, if they were honest — and honesty was the one discipline they had left, the one thing the breaking of the stone had not taken from them. Everyone else seemed to assume the journey home would mend something. The innkeeper's wife had pressed bread into their hands that morning and said, *It'll come back to you, love, being home. Places hold us even when we can't hold them.* The miller had clapped them on the shoulder hard enough to stagger. Even the dreams — those fragments that had begun surfacing lately, a market square, a laugh, a hand in theirs — even the dreams had seemed to promise that the valley was a key, and that somewhere inside these walls the lock would turn.

Xanthe waited.

The light moved. Dust turned slowly in it. Somewhere below, the mill-race kept its one long syllable going, water over stone, water over stone.

They felt nothing.

Not peace. Not grief. Nothing — a room-shaped absence, a table and two chairs floating in white fog. They tried the trick the healers had taught them, laying a palm flat against the table's grain, closing their eyes, reaching for whatever the wood might hold of them. All they got was the texture of oak and the faint smell of beeswax, and beneath that, fainter than either, the ghost of a sensation they couldn't name — warmth? company? — gone before it could become anything at all.

"This is where I grew up," Xanthe said aloud, to test the words.

The words were true and weightless, like coins from a country that no longer existed.

They opened the shutters wider. They found the marks on the doorframe — little notches cut at intervals, each labeled in a careful adult hand with dates and heights, *X., age 7*, *X., age 9*, climbing toward the lintel like rungs of a ladder out of the world. Someone had measured them every year. Someone had stood here with a knife and a proud or frightened heart and recorded the fact of their growing. Xanthe put their finger in the deepest notch, the last one, and felt the groove fit the pad of their finger almost exactly, as if the notch had been waiting.

Nothing. Only the finger knew.

They laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it — and then, because there was nothing else to feel, they made tea. The crockery told them where everything lived. Their hands moved through the kitchen like water finding a channel, tin box third shelf, kettle on the left hook, the chipped blue cup that their body reached for before any other. They drank the tea standing at the window, looking out over the terraces and the mill and the smoke of the town below, and thought: *I was happy here. I must have been. People who weren't happy don't leave cupboards organized like a kindness to whoever comes next.*

But knowing a thing is not the same as feeling it, and the tea went cold in their hands while they stood guard over a house full of evidence and no witness.

* * *

They left in the afternoon, walking, because sitting still had begun to feel like drowning politely.

There was a road that followed the ridge north of the valley — they knew it the way they knew their own knees, though they could not have said when they'd learned it — and they took it without deciding to, letting the body choose the direction for once. The air was doing that autumn thing it did here, they supposed, though *here* and *autumn* were both just words: cold coming up out of the ground while the sun still held the tops of the trees, so that you walked through two seasons at once, summer at your shoulders and winter rising around your ankles.

The starless dark was gone. That was the miracle nobody had quite finished believing. For a season the land beyond the Veil had been eaten down to colorless bone, and then the stone broke, and the dark — starved of the covenant it fed on, of the stolen magic it had been — simply unraveled, like a knot understanding itself. And the stars had come back. Xanthe had seen them for the first time on that dawn, a sky's worth of them, and had felt — this they remembered, this was theirs — a joy so large it frightened the people standing near them. Joy with no object. Joy about nothing. Just light, arriving after an absence they couldn't recall enduring.

Now the ridgeline road ran gold and brown under that returned sky, and Xanthe walked it with their collar up and their hands in their pockets, and the emptiness where their life had been rode along beside them like a quiet companion.

Halfway to the high pasture gate, they saw the figure ahead.

A traveler, at first glance. Cloak the color of wet slate, travel-worn boots, a sword worn the way some people wear an old injury — carried easily, constantly, part of the body's new grammar. Grey shot through dark hair now, cropped short for practicality. A stride Xanthe's eyes knew before the rest of them could object: unhurried, ground-eating, the walk of someone who has spent years measuring distances by how far she can go before dark.

Serah Vane.

Something happened in Xanthe's chest.

It was not memory. They had learned the shape of memory's attempts by now — the dreams, the fragments, the way certain smells (rain on hot stone, woodsmoke, a particular laugh) would snag on something deep and pull without catching. This was different. This was faster than thought and older than it, a chord struck in the body itself: breath shortening, pulse lifting, hands wanting — *wanting*, with a specificity that appalled them — to be held. As if some part of them had heard the footsteps and answered before their mind could ask the question.

By the time Serah looked up and saw them, Xanthe had mastered it. Mostly. Enough.

"You're a long way from the road down," Serah said. Her voice was level, courteous, the voice she used for strangers and for Xanthe alike, and Xanthe understood suddenly, in one clean lancing flash, how much discipline that cost her. "The pass road's better going south."

"I wasn't going anywhere." Xanthe heard their own voice and hated how carefully casual it came out. "I went home today."

Something moved behind Serah's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. "And?"

"And it's a very nice house." Xanthe meant it as a joke. It came out as a wound. They stopped walking, and the wind moved the dry grass around their ankles, and after a moment Serah stopped too, ten feet away, close enough for Xanthe to see the new lines at the corners of her mouth, the ones a year of vigil had carved.

"It doesn't know me," Xanthe said. "The house. The valley. I stood in my own kitchen and my hands knew where the cups were and I felt—" They searched for it and found only the honest word. "Nothing. Like reading a letter written in my handwriting by someone else."

Serah said nothing. She was good at saying nothing; she'd had a year of practice, holding a whole history behind her teeth because the person it belonged to had paid it away to save the world, and what kind of monster would demand it back?

"I'm not telling you this to make it heavy," Xanthe went on, because the words had started and wouldn't stop, a thaw breaking loose. "I know what it cost. I know what I chose. I'd choose it again — I think I'd choose it again, I've decided to believe I would. But everyone keeps handing me my life back in pieces and expecting the pieces to fit, and they don't, and I'm so tired of performing gratitude for a home I can't remember living in."

The wind came up the ridge and flattened the grass and passed.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Xanthe asked.

"If you want to tell it."

"The worst part is you." They saw her flinch — actually flinch, a soldier's flinch, small and instantly governed — and pressed on, because the truth was a blade and it was already out. "Because when I saw you on this road, before I knew it was you, something in me — " Xanthe pressed a fist briefly against their own sternum, as if the chord still rang there. "My body knows you. My body stood up and paid attention, like a dog hearing its name. And my mind has nothing. No reason. No story. Just this — this *pull*, with no explanation attached, and I keep thinking, what did we do, you and I? What happened between us that my bones remember and I don't?"

For a long moment Serah looked at the horizon instead of at them. When she spoke, her voice was roughened at the edges, sanded down by restraint.

"You should ask the valley," she said. "Or the stone's ghosts, if any are left listening. Or the people who loved you for twenty years." A pause, and then, quieter, almost failing at it: "Don't ask me. I'll only tell you the truth, and the truth isn't mine to give."

"That's an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Xanthe laughed — helplessly this time, the sound cracking open into something nearer to tears. "You realize that's the most memorable thing anyone's said to me all year. *I'll only tell you the truth.* Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else in my life is a stranger being kind to a stranger. You're a stranger being *honest* with one. It's the closest thing to familiarity I own."

"Xanthe—"

"Don't." They raised a hand. "Whatever you were about to say — don't say it kindly. Kindness from you does something to me I can't account for, and I'd like to get through one conversation without my own blood betraying me."

That startled a sound out of her — half breath, half laugh, entirely involuntary — and for a heartbeat the mask slipped and Xanthe saw underneath it: a year of mornings watching a stranger wear a beloved face. Grief with nowhere lawful to go. Then it was governed again, folded away, and Serah Vane was only a tired knight on a ridge road, except that Xanthe's traitor heart had already catalogued the slip and filed it somewhere permanent.

"There's a gathering," Serah said, when she trusted her voice again. "At Vaelmoor. In three weeks. Every realm that survived the dark — Serevan, the highland clans, what's left of the Border Order, the valley's council. They mean to write a new covenant. Honestly, this time, they say." Her mouth did something complicated. "They've asked for you. By name. The woman who broke the stone. They want you at the table when the terms are drafted."

"Me." Xanthe tasted the word. "I don't know anything anymore. I don't know what magic cost, or what the old covenant was, or why anyone should trust my judgment about—"

"They shouldn't," Serah said. "That's rather the point. Half of them will come to use you and half to blame you, and you'll be the only person in that hall who owes the covenant nothing — because you already paid everything, and you don't even remember the debt." She met Xanthe's eyes at last, fully, and held it. "Which makes you the only honest voice they'll have. Whether you remember why or not."

The sun had dropped behind the western hills while they talked, and the cold came up off the grass in earnest now, and still neither of them moved to go. Xanthe became aware that they were standing closer than the conversation required. That at some point in the last few minutes the ten feet between them had quietly become four, negotiated by no decision Xanthe could recall making. The body again. Always the body, walking its worn paths, keeping its notches in the doorframe.

"Will you be there?" Xanthe asked. "At Vaelmoor."

"I was asked."

"That's not what I asked."

Serah looked away first — the first retreat Xanthe had ever seen her make, and they knew it was a retreat the way you know weather changing. "I haven't decided what I am anymore," she said. "Knight of nothing. Warden of a wall that's down. If I come to Vaelmoor, it will be as no one, and I've been someone with a duty for so long I'm not sure no one survives the trip."

"Good," Xanthe said.

She blinked. "*Good?*"

"I'd like to meet no one," Xanthe said. "Everyone I meet is someone — the storm-caller, the breaker, the poor lamb who lost herself saving us all. You're the first person who's offered to be nobody." They shrugged, and heard their own voice go quieter, more careful, the way one walks on ice. "Maybe nobodies could start from the beginning. Whatever the beginning is."

For a moment Serah said nothing at all, and the space between them filled with the sound of the wind and, far below, the small persistent syllable of the mill-race, water over stone, carrying on as if the world had not ended and been remade within earshot of it.

Then she said, "The road to Vaelmoor goes through the pass. South road. Three weeks."

"I know the way," Xanthe said, and then, hearing it, nearly laughed again — *I know the way*, the truest sentence available to them, the only inheritance left. "Apparently I know all the ways. I just don't know any of the whys."

"Come anyway," Serah said. "The whys can catch up."

She turned south. Xanthe watched her go — slate cloak, easy sword, that stride that their heart insisted on recognizing — and felt the chord ring again, fainter with distance, refusing to fade entirely. An inexplicable thing. A thing with no name and no history and no permission.

They let it ring.

Then they turned north, toward a house that did not know them, and began, for the first time in a year, to build a self out of what remained: choice, honesty, and the pull of a stranger's footsteps on a ridge road — which was, they suspected, more foundation than most people ever got.
