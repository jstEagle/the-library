## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The arsenic came in the honey.

Not the porridge, not the cream, not the poached quince or the little spiced buns the king liked to tear apart with his fingers like a man committing a small sacrilege every morning. The porridge was clean. I knew it was clean because I had tasted it first, and my tongue — that long-suffering, well-educated organ — had pronounced it merely bad, which was the usual state of royal porridge. It was the honey that carried it: a thin bitterness under the sweetness, shy as a debt collector at a wedding.

I stood at the sideboard with the golden spoon in my mouth and let the bitterness sit on my tongue like a guest who hasn't realized he's unwelcome.

Arsenic, but not honest arsenic. Honest arsenic announces itself — a bite, a metallic rasp, a flavor like licking a lock. This had been cut with something to mask it, probably orgeat, and warmed first so the crystals would dissolve properly. Someone had done their reading. Someone had wanted this to look like a stomach ailment, three days of misery, a quiet decline, a king who died of his own indulgence. A considerate poison, for a considerate murder.

Thirty years of swallowing deaths on behalf of His Majesty had taught me one thing above all others: nobody tries to kill the king with something dramatic unless they want to be caught. The dangerous ones are always gentle about it.

"Almeida." Master of the Household's voice, from somewhere behind me and slightly above, as all voices in this palace were. "Well?"

I took the spoon out of my mouth. I did not spit, though my body made its opinion known through a fresh sheen of sweat along my spine and a faint, familiar itch behind my sternum, like a cat turning over. That was the arsenic settling into me, finding the walls I'd built for it, being shown to its room. My body did this now the way other bodies flinched from loud noises: automatically, and with a certain tired courtesy.

"The honey is off," I said. "Discard the jar, question the pantry boy who stocked it — the left-handed one, third shelf, he moved it forward overnight; jars don't walk — and send for the apothecary's ledger. Someone in this palace has recently bought white arsenic and been told it was rat-wane."

"And you're certain it's poison?"

"Master of the Household." I turned and looked at him. He was a soft man shaped like a water jug, and he had watched me eat death thirty mornings a year for three decades without once wondering what it cost. "Do you imagine I describe flaws in food for pleasure?"

He went pale in the way of men who have just remembered that the woman before them is, by trade, a professional reason for caution. I take a certain numb pride in that. It is not gratitude they feel — no one thanks the taster; we are furniture that screams — but it is a kind of respect, the kind you give a blade you keep in a drawer. I have been a drawer-blade for most of my life. There are worse things to be. I have eaten several of them.

They took the honey away. The king ate his breakfast, complained about the buns being dry, and never learned that between the fourth and fifth spoonful of his morning meal, death had leaned across the table and been turned away by a woman whose only armor was her tongue. This is the shape of my service. I save the king's life and he does not know it was ever in danger, and I go home with another poison folded into my flesh like a coin into a purse, and the kingdom turns on its axis none the wiser.

That is the trade. Nobody tells you when they hire you — they tell you about the quarters, the livery, the stipend — that what you are actually signing for is loneliness with excellent posture.

* * *

My rooms sit above the Guild counting house on Ropemaker's Stair, which is not where a royal servant should live, and is exactly where Bram Almeida lives, and the two facts have never met each other socially. By day I am the Crown's taster, gray-liveried, invisible at the edge of the king's meals. By night I climb the outside stair, let myself into a room that smells of ink and candlewax and old paper doing its best, and I keep books that would get me hanged if any auditor in the kingdom ever read them.

Not stolen money. Nothing so forgivable. No — the ledgers I balance record transactions like *one borrowed hour of courage, sold to a deserter at the north gate, repayment deferred* and *three years of a widow's grief, purchased at half rate, buyer unknown* and *one name, freshly made, delivered to a man who needed to be someone else by Thursday.* Impossible inventory. Debts measured in things no mint has ever coined. The Guild of Impossible Things trades in them anyway, and someone has to make the columns come out, and that someone is me, because I am very good with numbers and very good at keeping quiet, and because Odile Voss pays better than the Crown and asks fewer questions.

That night I climbed the stair with my livery collar already loosened, and the cold went through me the way cold does when you've swallowed enough toxins to line your veins with something that isn't quite weather anymore. Below me, the counting house windows glowed amber. Above me, my own window sat dark, waiting. Somewhere in the city a bell rang nine, then thought better of it and stopped.

Odile was waiting inside, which she hadn't announced, which meant she wanted something.

She is not an old woman, whatever the apprentices whisper. She is a *composed* woman — silver hair pinned like a decision, hands that do arithmetic absently while they hold a teacup, eyes that have priced everything in the world at least twice. She looked up at me from behind the master ledger and said, "You taste of arsenic."

"Orgeat-cut," I said, hanging my cloak. "Warm-dissolved. Someone competent."

"Hm. Court's getting ambitious again." She slid the ledger aside. "Sit. I have entries."

"I haven't eaten since noon."

"You can add numbers starving like anyone else." But she pushed a plate of seed bread at me anyway, which is as close to affection as Odile Voss comes before midnight, and I sat, and I pulled the day-book toward me, and we worked.

This is what the world doesn't see, when it sees me. It sees the gray livery, the spoon, the woman who eats first and dies second. It does not see this: lamplight, two pairs of hands, and a book open to a page recording that a frightened boy in Harrowgate sold six months of his childhood last spring to pay his mother's physician, and the buyer — I checked the entry four times, because the entry wanted checking — was the Chancellor's office. The Crown buys time from its poor. Not metaphorically. Literally, in vials, through brokers who never ask why. And I write it down in a fair hand, because a ledger that lies is worse than no ledger at all, and because if I didn't write it, someone less careful would, and because — I will be honest, since no one else in this building will be — because there is a part of me that loves the work. Loves the clean weight of a balanced column. Loves knowing where every impossible thing in this city is kept.

Lonely competence. That's the whole of me, filed under two headings.

"Bram." Odile's voice had gone quiet in the way it does when she's about to hand me something heavy. "The entry from Tuesday. The memory extraction. Did you file it?"

"With the sealed lots, yes."

"Who was the buyer?"

I turned back a page. "No name recorded. Paid in crown script."

Something crossed her face, brief as a fish under ice. "Crown script," she repeated, and then said nothing else about it, and I wrote the evening's entries, and told myself the small cold feeling under my ribs was only the arsenic finding its seat.

* * *

Later — past eleven, when even the Guild's candles burn lower and slower — I climbed the inner stair to my own rooms with a lamp, and I did what I do every night before sleep: I took my own account.

It is a strange ledger. Most people's bodies keep no records worth auditing. Mine is a bookkeeping marvel. In the margin of my mind I run the totals: arsenic, forty-one exposures, tolerance near-total. Aconite, seven, permanent coldness in two fingertips. Botulinum, once, nearly the end of me, antidote bought at a price I don't like recalling. Sea-hemlock, foxglove, four varieties of nightshade, a thing from the southern swamps that has no common name because the people who live near it die too quickly to name anything. Each one survived. Each one still in me, folded small, a tenant paying rent in numbness. My body is a museum of murders that failed, and I am the only visitor it ever gets.

I stood at my window with the lamp turned low and looked out at the city — the real city, below the palace, where the snow had started again, fat slow flakes falling past the glass like something deciding whether to land. Somewhere out there were all the people whose borrowed hours and purchased years sat on our shelves. Somewhere out there was whoever had warm-dissolved arsenic into a jar of honey and walked away from it clean.

And I remember thinking — not as a premonition, exactly. More like the itch behind the sternum that means something foreign has entered the house of me and not yet settled.

I remember thinking: *the snow is late this year, and the city is too quiet, and the ledger was one page short tonight.*

One page short. I had noticed it at the bottom of the evening's balancing — a page torn from the day-book, cleanly, with a razor or a very good knife. I assumed a clerical error, an entry copied wrong, some apprentice covering a smudge. I assumed I would find it tomorrow and correct it.

Then the knock came.

Not on the street door below — on *my* door, the high one, the one at the top of the outside stair, where nobody knocks, because nobody knows a person lives here. People know a function lives here. A spoon. A drawer-blade.

Three knocks. Small ones. At the height, I would note later, of a child's fist.

I set down the lamp. Behind me, unbidden, in the dark of the room, the master ledger lay open on its stand — I had left it closed, I am certain I had left it closed — and I heard paper move against paper, softly, the way a sleeping thing shifts when it senses footsteps on the stairs.

I put my hand on the latch.

Outside, in the snow, something small was breathing, and waiting, and — I could swear this, though it makes no sense and I have made no sense before — listening to my heartbeat count the seconds, patient as a creditor.
