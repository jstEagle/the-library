## 2. Ledger of Impossible Things

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its back office behind a door that pretended to be a wall.

I don't mean it was hidden. I mean the door had opinions. It was plaster and lath like every other stretch of corridor in the counting house on Vellum Street, and if you walked past without knowing, you'd walk past forever. If you knew—if you'd been shown, which is the only way anyone ever knows—you saw the seam where the pretense didn't quite meet the floorboards, and you pressed your palm flat against the plaster and said nothing at all, because the door didn't want words. It wanted intent. Odile said it had been installed by a joiner who owed the Guild a debt he couldn't pay in coin, and that the joiner had put so much of his wanting into it that the wood had learned to be particular.

I had been pressing my palm against that particular piece of wood for six years, since the night Odile Voss caught me balancing her books better than her own clerks did.

"Numbers are just promises wearing uniforms," she'd told me then, watching me reconcile a column she'd sworn was unreconcilable. "You have an honest tongue, taster. The Guild could use an honest tongue that knows when to lie."

I hadn't understood what she meant until she showed me through the door.

Tonight, after the king's breakfast and the arsenic and the long walk home with death sitting politely in my stomach like a paid guest, I pressed my hand to the wall and thought: *let me in, I've had a day.*

The plaster sighed and swung.

* * *

The back office was not an office. It was a cathedral that had given up on God and taken up commerce instead.

The room went back further than the building should have allowed—I'd measured once, out of professional suspicion, and found the interior exceeded the exterior by a good forty feet, which Odile waved off as "an architectural arrangement" in the tone other people used for *don't ask about the architecture*. Shelves rose three stories into a gloom lit by lamps that burned without oil, or burned oil that had been traded for from someone's memory of a lamp being lit, which amounted to nearly the same thing. Ladders on brass rails ran along the stacks. Somewhere in the dark, always, something ticked—not a clock. We checked. It wasn't a clock.

I hung my coat on the hook by the door, next to a cloak that had been hanging there longer than I'd worked here. Nobody claimed it. Nobody moved it. Once a year Odile dusted it and said, "He'll be back," and refused to elaborate, and I had learned that some entries in the Guild's ledger were best left unbalanced.

"Bram." Odile's voice came down from the third gallery, where she stood at a lectern with a pen behind each ear and another tucked into the gray braid coiled at her neck like a sleeping adder. She was sixty if she was a day, and built like a filing cabinet that had learned to walk, and she had eyes the color of ledger ink—black, patient, and very hard to wash out. "You're late."

"I saved the king's life tonight."

"The king's life will keep. The Ferren account won't." She descended the gallery stairs two at a time, which should not have been possible for a woman her age and was possible anyway; I suspected borrowed knees. "Come. You're due your tour, and I'd rather do it tonight than explain tomorrow why you fainted at the inventory."

"My tour? I've worked here six years."

"You've *balanced* here six years. There's a difference. Balancing is arithmetic. Tonight you learn what the arithmetic is *for*." She took a lamp off its peg—one of the honest ones, oil-burning—and led me into the stacks. "Rule one of the Guild of Impossible Things: we serve whoever pays. Rule two: we never ask why. Everything else you're about to see follows from those two rules, and if either of them ever sits wrong with you, you come tell me, and I'll sit wrong with you, and then we'll both go back to work. Understood?"

"Understood," I said, and it sat wrong already, and I went back to work. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement.

* * *

She started me with the courage.

It lived in a case near the front, because it was the Guild's most popular line—the everyday stock, the bread-and-butter of impossible trade. Rows upon rows of vials, each no bigger than my thumb, each stoppered with wax and labeled in a clerk's careful hand. *Borrowed Courage, one hour, provenance withheld.* *Borrowed Courage, one evening, donor deceased, price adjusted.* *Borrowed Courage, battlefield grade, do not combine with wine.*

"What does 'borrowed' mean?" I asked, though I suspected.

"It means somewhere there's a person who sold us theirs," Odile said. "A man too frightened to ask his sweetheart's father for her hand sells us an hour of the bravery he wishes he had, and buys it back later at interest, and in the meantime some other man wears it to a funeral he dreads. Courage circulates, Bram. Like coin. Like plague." She turned a vial so the lamplight caught the amber inside, which moved against the glass the way living things move—slowly, reluctantly, like something asleep that would rather not be woken. "The donor feels the gap. An hour where their spine should be, they feel nothing but a polite absence. Most of them get used to it. Some of them come back and sell more. We don't ask why."

"Who buys battlefield grade?"

"People going somewhere they expect to die, and people going somewhere they expect to live, and it's frequently the second group who need it more." She set the vial down with a click. "We served whoever paid. A general once bought eleven hours of it for a man he was sending over a wall. The man held the breach for eleven hours exactly. Then the courage left him all at once, mid-scream, and what he did after that is not in our ledgers because it isn't our business. Rule two."

I looked at the little amber sleepers in their rows and thought about my own body, my ledger of swallowed deaths, and wondered—with the first small cold discomfort of the night—which shelf I'd be filed on, if I ever needed coin badly enough.

* * *

The last words were worse.

They kept them in a jar. Not vials—a single jar, wide-mouthed, stoppered with cloth instead of wax, sitting on its own pedestal under a glass dome like the relic it technically was. Inside, floating in something clear and faintly luminous, hung perhaps thirty threads of silver light, each one coiled like a hair from a star.

"Dying speech," Odile said. "Extracted in the last breath. The Guild doesn't take these often—it takes a dying person willing to sell, and most people spend their last breath on their children's names or their god's forgiveness, free of charge. But some—" she tapped the dome gently, "—some have debts they can only settle with their final words, and no heir they trust to carry them. So they sell the words to us, and whoever pays the asking price may hear them spoken once."

"Spoken how?"

"In the buyer's own voice. In the dead person's. Depends what the contract says." Her voice had gone quieter, and I noticed her hand stayed flat on the glass a moment longer than a merchant's hand needs to. "There's a woman in Harrowgate who has bought the same jar's contents four times. Her husband's last words. She listens, and forgets on purpose—there's a draught for that, we stock it—and buys again. We serve whoever pays. We never ask why. But Bram, between you and me and this jar, sometimes rule two is the only thing standing between this institution and my conscience, and my conscience is losing ground yearly."

I wrote that down in my head, in the private ledger I kept behind my eyes, under the entry for *things Odile says when the lamps are low*. It was getting to be a crowded page.

* * *

The debts written in hair were the part I already knew, because I balanced them.

Down in the deep stacks, past the courage and the years and the bottled lullabies, there was a drawer case made of black oak, and in the drawers were envelopes, and in the envelopes was hair. A lock from every debtor whose debt couldn't be priced in coin. The hair was the collateral. The hair was the *contract*. Cut from a willing head at the signing, it held the terms the way wax holds a seal—if the debtor defaulted, the hair grayed, thinned, and finally vanished, and whatever the debtor had pledged alongside it vanished in proportion.

I'd seen the columns for years. Debtor: J. Marlowe. Collateral: three years of sleep, held in store, drawer 44. Debtor: the Widow Sennet. Collateral: the memory of her mother's face, redeemable at any time before maturity. Debtor: [name redacted by request]. Collateral: one name, withdrawn from circulation, term of loan ninety-nine years.

That last one I'd asked about, early on, and Odile had looked at me with her ink-black eyes and said, "Names are the oldest currency we deal in, and the most dangerous. A person's name is the handle the world uses to pick them up. Withdraw it, and the world's hand closes on empty air." Then she'd smiled, which she did rarely and only when something had gone exactly according to plan. "Why do you think I pay you in coin, Bram? Never in kind. Take my advice. Coin, always coin."

"I'm not planning to pledge anything."

"Nobody plans to. Planning is what the pledging replaces." She closed the drawer with the gentleness people reserve for coffins and cradles. "Now. The tour's end. The part I show every new full member, and the part that makes half of them quit by morning."

* * *

She took me to the vault door.

Iron, older than the building, older than the street, set into stone that predated the city's charter. No keyhole. Instead, a shallow basin, and above the basin, carved into the iron in letters worn nearly smooth:

**THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS SERVES WHOEVER PAYS. THE GUILD NEVER ASKS WHY.**

"This is the whole constitution," Odile said. "Two sentences. Every kingdom I know of needs a thousand pages and still gets it wrong. We got it right, and look what it bought us." She gestured back at the cathedral of shelves, the circulating courage, the jar of dying voices, the drawers of hair. "Survival. Impartiality. A place where the desperate can trade on even terms with the powerful, because the powerful's coin spends the same as anyone's. Do you know what the crown would do with this inventory, Bram? What any throne would do? They'd drink the courage and hoard the years and burn the rest, and they'd ask *why* about everyone who ever came through our door, and the asking alone would kill half our clients. Our refusal to ask is not cowardice. It's the moat."

"And if the thing that pays is a monster?"

"Then we serve a monster, and we balance the books, and we light a candle after." She said it flatly, without shame and without pride, the way you'd report the weather. "I've served monsters. I'll serve them again. And I will lose sleep over every one, and the sleeplessness is the tax, and I pay it, and the Guild stands. That's the bargain I made. That's the bargain I'm offering you. Full membership. Access to the vault. A cut of the commissions. And the tax."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere in the deep stacks, the thing that wasn't a clock ticked twice, and stopped, and started again.

I thought about the arsenic cooling in my blood, about the king I'd saved who I'd met perhaps forty times and never once spoken to beyond *does it please Your Majesty*, about the widow in Harrowgate buying her grief in installments, about the man over the wall. About my rooms above the counting house, which were warm, and quiet, and mine, and which I could not afford on a food-taster's honest wage.

Uneasy complicity, Odile had called it once, years ago, describing herself to me over cheap wine. *The Guild runs on uneasy complicity, Bram. Anyone comfortable here is either new or rotten.*

"Yes," I said. "I'll take the bargain."

Odile nodded, unsurprised, and pressed her palm to the iron, and the vault door began—slowly, grudgingly, like everything else in this place—to open.

And that was when we both heard it: a knock. Not at the vault door. At the front door of the counting house, up three floors and around two corners, a small, sharp, unmistakable knock, at an hour when no client came and no courier rode.

Odile's head tilted, ink-dark eyes narrowing. "We're closed," she said, to no one, to the building.

The knock came again. Smaller. Faster. Almost frantic.

And beneath it, faintly, muffled through floors and stone and six years of my sensible disbelief, I heard something I had no ledger-entry for: a child's voice, speaking words in a language I did not know—and the master ledger, shut and locked in its case in the reading room, opened itself with a sound like a held breath let go.
