## 4. Words Older Than Kingdoms

There are three sounds in this world I have learned to dread, and I will list them in ascending order of horror: the bell that means a client has arrived with more money than sense; the creak of the third stair, which means Bram Almeida has not slept again and is about to make it my problem; and the sound of Ninepence waking up.

The first two are merely expensive. The third is historical.

Ninepence does not wake for fires. He did not wake when the counting house flooded in my grandmother's day, when the water rose to his shelf and lapped at his shins like an ambitious dog. He did not wake when thieves broke in — twice — and one of them, in a fit of either piety or panic, left a candle burning on his case. He slept through the Plague of Sighs, through three changes of dynasty, and through what I personally consider the far greater catastrophe of the Guild adopting double-entry bookkeeping.

So when I heard, at half past midnight on the coldest night of the year, the dry rustle of parchment where there should have been no parchment moving — when I heard that particular papery sigh, like a ledger being opened by hands that are no longer hands — I put down my pen, looked at the ceiling of my office as if it might explain itself, and said aloud, to no one:

"No."

He woke anyway. They always do. That is rather the point of them.

* * *

Let me describe Ninepence properly, because descriptions matter in my trade and because you will not believe me otherwise. He sits — has always sat, will presumably always sit — in the inner vault behind my office, on a chair older than the city's charter, in a case of blackened glass that Bram insists on calling his "office" and which I insist on calling what it is, which is a reliquary with bad lighting.

He was a broker once. A great one, they say, though the records of what he brokered were eaten along with most of him. What remains is a man rendered down to essentials: skin like vellum gone amber, drawn close over a frame of bone that has forgotten its flesh the way old clerks forget their first language. His eyes are closed. They have been closed for two hundred years, give or take a decade nobody kept track of. When he speaks — and he speaks perhaps once a generation, and never to anyone who wanted him to — the voice comes from somewhere behind the face, the way heat comes from coals that look dead.

We call him Ninepence because that is what he charged. Not nine coins. Nine *pence*. For anything. For a love potion or a kingdom's ransom, for a memory or a murder, the fee was nine pence, and the fee was never the price. Everyone who dealt with him understood that the nine pence was a courtesy, a receipt, a way of making the transaction legal in some jurisdiction older than law. The real cost was written elsewhere, in a book we do not have, in terms we do not want to read.

My grandmother called him the Guild's founding error. My mother called him our founding principle. I have settled on calling him *sir*, very politely, from a distance of at least four feet, and I recommend the practice to anyone.

* * *

I heard the child before I understood why Ninepence had woken, which is a sentence I did not expect to write even in the privacy of my own skull, and I keep a private skull-journal of considerable cynicism.

Bram had brought the girl up from the door an hour earlier. I heard the whole business from my office — the knock that wasn't a knock, the snow, Bram's voice going careful in the way it goes when her taster's instincts and her bookkeeper's instincts agree on something, which is rare and always means trouble. Then footsteps on the stairs, and then, from the room above mine, a sound I can only describe as language happening backwards.

You know how a word feels when you almost know it? When it sits on the tongue like a key that fits a lock you've never seen? That was the child's voice. Every syllable she spoke sounded like the beginning of a word I should know, and none of them finished. It was like listening to someone read a contract aloud in a dream — you understand that binding is occurring, you simply cannot say what is being bound to what.

I am sixty-one years old. I have heard every tongue traded through this city, including three that are supposed to be extinct and one that is spoken exclusively by eels, according to a client I no longer extend credit to. I know what languages sound like. This was not a language. This was the material languages are made from, before they get cut into useful pieces.

And downstairs, behind blackened glass, something that had not moved since my grandmother was a girl began, very softly, to laugh.

* * *

I want to be clear about the order of operations here, because later — when the histories get written, if anyone survives to write them — people will assume we went to Ninepence deliberately, sensibly, with torches and questions. We did not. Ninepence summoned *us*, insofar as a mummified broker can summon anyone, which is to say he made a noise until I came, and then he made worse noises until I fetched Bram and the child, and by then the decision had been made for us, which is how Ninepence prefers all decisions to go.

The vault was warm. That was my first wrong note. The vault is never warm; it sits over the cold cellar where we keep the bottled years, and the cold comes up through the floor like a debt. But that night the air around his case shimmered faintly, the way air does above a candle, except there was no candle, unless you count whatever Ninepence uses instead of a body.

His eyes were open.

Two hundred years of closed eyes, and they were open, and they were not white and they were not blind. They were the color of the inside of a ledger — that pale cream of good paper waiting to be written on — and they were pointed, with terrible precision, at the small figure half-hidden behind Bram's skirts.

"Ah," said Ninepence.

It is worth recording that this single syllable made both Bram and me take a step backward, and Bram Almeida does not step backward from anything, including poison, kings, and me.

"You're awake," I said, because I am a professional, and professionals state the obvious while their knees are deciding whether to file a complaint.

"I am *hungry*," said Ninepence, "which is different," and then, before either of us could ask the only sensible question, which was *hungry for what*: "Bring her closer. No — don't. Don't bring her anywhere. Keep her exactly there. Girl. Say it again."

The child looked up at Bram. Bram looked down at the child. I watched the two of them conduct an entire negotiation in the space of a breath — I have seen marriages with less communication — and then the girl stepped out from behind Bram's skirts, faced the case of blackened glass, and spoke.

I will not attempt to reproduce it. Our alphabet would break its teeth. But I will tell you what it did, because what it did is the entire reason I am writing this account instead of sleeping, drinking, or fleeing the country, three options I weighed seriously in the following hour.

When she spoke, the debts moved.

Not metaphorically. I mean the physical ledgers — the ones chained to the wall in the outer vault, the ones with debts written in hair and teeth and names — shifted on their shelves, all together, like dogs turning toward a whistle. And in the master ledger, the great book that predates the Guild and that we technically guard rather than own, I saw — through the open vault door, in the space of a heartbeat — a page turn itself.

"First Speech," said Ninepence, and his voice had changed. It had gone soft and wet and eager, the voice of a man looking at a meal after a long fast, and I found I disliked it more than any threat he had ever made. "The tongue before tongues. The language oaths were sworn in, back when swearing meant something — back when the words didn't describe the binding, child, the words *were* the binding. Everything your Guild sells — every borrowed year, every bottled memory, every name pulled out of circulation — every contract we have ever written is a *translation*. A degraded copy. A sketch of a sketch." The closed-drawer rasp of his breathing filled the vault. "And she speaks the original."

"That's impossible," I said. "First Speech died out before the—"

"Nothing dies out," said Ninepence. "That is a comfort we sell to clients. Things go underground. Things go into bloodlines. Things wait." His head turned — slowly, with the sound of a page being turned against its will — toward the girl. "Who gave you your words?"

The child frowned. She looked at Bram. Bram knelt, which I had never seen her do for anyone living, and asked her something quiet, and the girl answered in our tongue, haltingly, in the flat accent of someone translating from somewhere much deeper than memory.

"A tower," she said. "A fire. A voice saying—" and here she stopped, and her small hands curled into fists, and she said the rest in First Speech, one line of it, and every bottle on the cold cellar shelves below us rang once, softly, like a toast being drunk in an empty room.

Ninepence translated. I wish he hadn't. I wish, with a fervor I usually reserve for tax season, that I had gone to my grave without knowing.

"She says: *carry it until someone worthy reads you.*"

* * *

Afterward — after we got the child back upstairs with hot milk and a lie about everything being fine, a lie Bram told so smoothly I briefly wondered if she'd bought it from us — Ninepence and I had what I suppose historians would call a conversation and I call the worst quarter-hour of my professional life.

"You knew," I said. "You woke the moment she opened her mouth. You've known what she was since before I did."

"I woke the moment she came within a mile of this building," Ninepence said. "Her arrival has been on the books for thirty years. You simply hadn't read far enough ahead."

"We don't read ahead. That's the rule. The Guild serves whoever pays, we never ask why, and we certainly don't—"

"The Guild," said Ninepence, "was founded to hold a debt. Did Voss never tell you? Did your mother?" A dry click that might have been a chuckle. "Of course not. It's bad for morale. The Guild exists, Odile, because something was bound, long ago, by words in that child's language, and the binding required custodians. We are not merchants who stumbled into magic. We are *clerks*. We keep the accounts of an oath. Every impossible thing we trade is leakage — power seeping out of the original seal, and us, mopping it into bottles and selling it by the dram." His eyes, that paper-cream color, fixed on me. "And now the seal has sent a key to check the lock."

"What's it bound?" I asked. "What's under the seal?"

And Ninepence — who had answered nothing freely in two hundred years, who made even his silences cost money — went quiet in a new way. Quiet the way a scale goes quiet when the weights finally balance.

"Crown-shaped," he said at last. "Go look at your king sometime, Voss. Look closely. Count the seconds between his questions and his answers. And then come back and tell me what the Guild's oldest client has been paying us to forget."

I did not sleep that night. I sat in my office with the ledgers spread out before me, thirty years of royal purchases — discreet, anonymous, always paid in advance, always from the same unmarked account — and I read them the way you reread a letter after bad news, finding in every harmless line the thing that was there all along.

Below me, in the vault, Ninepence did not go back to sleep. I could hear him humming. An old tune, or the skeleton of one — a coronation hymn, I realized much too late, with several bars missing, the way a hymn goes when something else has been singing it from inside.

The past, my mother used to say, is not dead. It is a creditor. It waits, and it compounds.

Somewhere upstairs, a child slept with a language in her mouth older than every kingdom that wants her dead or owned, and the oldest thing in my building was awake, and hungry, and humming wedding music for a crown.

In the morning, I decided, I would tell Bram everything.

In the morning, the bells rang wrong, and I never got the chance to choose.
