## 5. What the Crown Remembers

There is a particular boredom that comes with surviving. Nobody warns you about it. You spend years assuming that the alternative to terror is joy, and then you arrive at competence and discover it's mostly arithmetic: sip, wait, count heartbeats, swallow or don't. The palace calls me the finest taster in a generation. What they mean is that I am very good at not dying, which is a skill I'd happily trade for being bad at something warmer.

That morning I tasted the king's breakfast and found arsenic in the honey.

Not enough to kill — barely enough to notice, if your tongue were an ordinary tongue. A whisper of it, bitter-sweet at the back of the throat, the way a lie sits behind a compliment. Someone in the kitchens was testing me. Or testing him. Or simply careless, which happens more often than the ballads suggest; poisoners are people too, and people get distracted by love affairs and debts like anyone else.

I had the honey sent back with a note about crystallization. The under-cook who'd stirred it went pale as fresh flour and was reassigned to the scullery before noon. No one was hanged. It was, all things considered, a merciful day at court, and mercy at court always makes my skin itch, because mercy is usually just cruelty that hasn't finished its paperwork.

* * *

The wine service was at four. Autumn light came through the great windows of the audience chamber in long amber bars, and dust hung in them like a congregation waiting to be dismissed. I stood at my station behind the king's right shoulder with the decanter, doing what I do: pour, sip, count, wait, serve. Thirty years of this. My body has a whole liturgy for it, and my mind has wandered off during that liturgy so many times that it could do the whole rite asleep, which is nearly what happened, which is why I noticed what I noticed.

Chamberlain Duval approached the dais with a petition — a land dispute, two barons, a river that kept changing its mind about whose it was. Duval is a careful man. He arranges his sentences like place settings, everything equidistant, nothing that might spill. He bowed, he began his preamble, he got as far as "your Majesty, regarding the matter of the Vell marshes—"

"—the eastern claim has the older charter," said the king, "but the western baron's grandfather bought the water rights in the famine year. Grant the water to the west, the reeds to the east, and have them both sign by Martinmas."

Duval blinked. So did I, though I covered it by sipping wine I'd already cleared.

Because here's the thing. Duval hadn't asked yet. He'd gotten as far as naming the marshes. The question about charters and water rights was still folded up inside him like a letter not yet opened, and the king had answered it anyway — correctly, in detail, with a solution elegant enough that both barons would go home feeling clever, which is the mark of real statecraft.

I told myself it was coincidence. Kings hear the same petitions for decades; a man can learn to anticipate. The Vell marshes dispute had been dragging on since before I started tasting, probably. Any competent monarch could guess its shape.

Then it happened again. And again.

A merchant delegation, three men deep in mourning silks, come to plead against a new tariff. Their spokesman opened his mouth and got out "Sire, we come humbly regarding the harbor duties—" and the king said, "Reduce the duty on grain, hold it on dye, and the guilds will stop complaining by spring," before the man had finished the word *duties*. The spokesman's face did something complicated — relief and unease wrestling in a small room — and then gratitude won, because gratitude always wins when there's an audience, and the delegation bowed itself out looking like men who'd been handed gifts they couldn't quite identify.

Half a second. That's all it was. A half-second gap between question and answer, except the gap was on the wrong side. Answers arriving before their questions, like thunder preceding lightning, like a letter delivered before it's written.

I have spent my life around poisons, and I'll tell you the first thing every poisoner learns: the dose makes the difference between medicine and murder, but the *timing* makes the difference between accident and intent. Arsenic in the honey once is carelessness. Twice is a pattern. An answer before its question once is anticipation. Six times in one afternoon is something else entirely.

By the seventh time I had stopped pretending to myself, and by then I'd also noticed the shadow.

* * *

You spend enough hours standing behind a throne and you learn its geography the way a sailor learns a coastline. I knew where the king's shadow fell at four o'clock in October. It fell long across the third step of the dais, angling left toward the map table, because the great windows sit southwest and the sun was doing what October sun does.

Except the shadow didn't stay put.

It's hard to describe without sounding mad, and I want to be precise, because precision is the only weapon I've ever owned. When the king gestured — and he gestured rarely, small economical movements of one hand, a habit courtiers read as dignity — his shadow lagged. Not much. A fraction. The hand moved and the shadow's hand arrived a beat late, the way sound arrives after the flash of a distant forge hammer. I watched for it deliberately after that, and once — only once, when he turned to speak to the Lord Marshal — the shadow turned *first*. Just slightly. Just enough that for one heartbeat the dark shape on the floor was already facing the Marshal while the king's body was still completing its turn, like a performer whose reflection has stopped bothering to sync.

Then it corrected, smooth as anything, and the audience continued, and somewhere a clerk scratched his pen, and the world insisted nothing had happened.

I did not drop the decanter. I am a professional. But my mouth had gone dry in a way that no amount of wine I was legally permitted to swallow could fix, and beneath my ribs, where thirty years of swallowed deaths have built me a kind of callus, something shifted. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The way you recognize a taste you've had before even when you can't name it.

I know what it's like when a body isn't quite inhabited. I've seen it in men I've pulled back from hemlock — the soul coming home to a house it half expected to find empty. This was different. This was a body worn *well*. Worn the way a good coat is worn, by someone who never paid for it.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of screaming, fainting, or writing to the Archprelate like a sensible person: I went home and opened a ledger.

My rooms above the Guild counting house are narrow and smell of ink and old paper and, faintly, of the vials downstairs — courage, calm, borrowed luck, all of it shelved like preserves. I keep a desk by the window because Odile says a bookkeeper who can't see the street deserves whatever ambushes her. I took down a slim volume with plain covers, unmarked, the kind the Guild sells to people who want records that officially don't exist, and I dipped my pen, and I wrote:

*Private ledger of royal anomalies. Kept by B.A., food-taster, fourth rank. Opened this 14th day of the Dying Month.*

Then I made my entries, because a thing written plainly is a thing you can fight, and a thing left swirling in your head will eat you alive by winter.

*Entry the first.* His Majesty answered Chamberlain Duval's question regarding the Vell marshes approximately one half-second before the question was spoken. Answer correct in detail, including a fact (famine-year purchase of water rights) that appears in no petition summary I have seen. *Entry the second through sixth.* Same pattern, five further instances, varying subjects: tariffs, a marriage dispensation, a border garrison rotation, a complaint about drainage in the Tanner's Quarter, and a request from the Dean of the Royal College for funds. In each case the answer preceded the question. In each case the answer was *good*. That last part frightens me more than the rest, and I've been sitting with why. A fraud gives wrong answers eventually. Whatever wears the crown up there is not giving wrong answers. It is giving better answers than the questions deserve.

*Entry the seventh.* The shadow. Lagging, then leading. Observed twice, confirmed once. Possible explanations, ranked by plausibility: (1) my eyes are tired; (2) trick of the light off the map table's glass front; (3) sorcery of a kind the Guild doesn't stock, which means sorcery of a kind nobody honest stocks.

I sat back and looked at those entries and felt the specific chill of a bookkeeper who has found a column that won't sum. You can hide a discrepancy in a big ledger easily enough. But once you've seen it, you see it everywhere. Every balanced total becomes suspect. Every clean page becomes a place where something was erased.

And then, because I am apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving a poisoned cup alone, I added a final line:

*Working hypothesis: the king is answering from memory. Not his own.*

I don't know where that sentence came from. It arrived the way the king's answers arrived — ahead of my reasoning, fully formed, correct in detail. I stared at it for a long time. Ink does that sometimes; it knows things before the hand does. Odile says paper remembers everything it's ever held, and that a ledger is just a memory with manners.

* * *

I should say something about what the king looked like, while I still trusted my own eyes to report it.

King Aldric the Third — though I have begun, privately, in the safety of my own skull, to drop the ordinal — is sixty-one years old and looks fifty. That alone used to be remarked upon as a blessing. Good blood, the courtiers said. Good humor. He came to the throne thirty years ago amid the tail end of the Border Wars, a young king ending an old slaughter, and the peace he brokered has held ever since, unbroken, unprecedented, the kind of peace historians write thank-you notes about. He is courteous. He remembers names — everyone's names, the scullery girl's, the gate captain's, mine, which kings are not supposed to do. He has never once raised his voice. In thirty years I have tasted for him daily and he has never been poisoned successfully, never been ill, never aged past some fixed point the way a portrait ages instead of a man.

He thanked me tonight. That's the detail I keep circling back to. After the wine service, as the chamber emptied, he turned — smoothly, correctly, his shadow behaving itself — and said, "Thank you, Mistress Almeida. The vintage suits the evening."

And it did. It absolutely did. I had chosen it that morning against a forecast of cold, and the evening had obliged, and the pairing was flawless, and none of that is strange. What is strange is that I had told no one my reasoning. What is strange is that three weeks ago, on a night I chose a white Alençaise against the heat, he said the same sentence with the same warmth, word for word, *the vintage suits the evening*, like a phrase kept in a drawer and taken out when the occasion matched.

Kings repeat themselves. Courtiers joke about it. But they repeat themselves loosely, the way rivers repeat their course. This was exact. This was a man reciting, or a thing consulting a list.

I bowed. I said, "Your Majesty is kind." And for one instant — less than an instant, a sliver, a hairline crack in the glaze — his eyes did something I have no better word for than *refocus*. As if I had moved farther away than the room allowed. As if he were looking at me down a very long corridor and adjusting for the distance.

"You taste everything," he said. "Nothing gets past you."

It was a compliment. It was also, I realized somewhere around the third step of the dais on my way out, possibly a warning. Possibly a test. Possibly the most dangerous sentence anyone has said to me in thirty years of drinking poison on purpose.

* * *

Back home, I added the day's entries by candlelight while the Guild closed up below me — the soft clink of vials, Odile's voice running the evening totals, the ordinary music of impossible commerce. I wrote about the refocused eyes. I wrote about the repeated sentence. I wrote about the peace, thirty years unbroken, and underlined it, because I have begun to wonder whether the peace and the king are the same problem. Wars need a war-maker. Something ended the Border Wars very suddenly, very completely, in a single season, and everyone was so grateful they forgot to ask what it cost or who paid it. Gratitude is the best preservative. Nothing keeps a secret like a kingdom that doesn't want to know.

At the bottom of the page I drew my closing rule and set the sums. Then, because the candle was low and my nerve was lower, I wrote one more line, small, tucked into the margin where margins belong:

*If the crown remembers what the man forgot, find out what the man forgot, and find out who took it.*

The candle guttered. Below me, Odile locked the strongroom, and Ninepence — our oldest colleague, our mummified broker, our resident relic who sleeps upright in his case like a coat nobody wears — I could have sworn I heard him shift. One dry rustle. The sound of something old turning over in its sleep, or beginning to wake.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark with my little book of wrongness, and thought: tomorrow I taste breakfast again. Tomorrow I stand behind the throne again. Tomorrow I smile at a thing wearing a dead man's courtesy and pour its wine and count its heartbeats, and it will thank me, and I will bow, and neither of us will say what we both may already know.

Unless it doesn't know. Unless I'm a fool with tired eyes and a talent for finding patterns in weather.

But I have survived thirty years on one principle: when in doubt, assume the cup is poisoned. And lately, every instinct I own — instincts trained on hemlock and henbane, on the subtle and the gross, on every way a body can be undone — every one of them is telling me the same thing.

The whole kingdom is the cup.

And someone, somewhere, is waiting to see who drinks.
