## 7. The Girl Who Won't Speak

The tower came first, always. Before the snow, before the door, before the woman with the ledger-smell and the hands that never flinched.

Wren did not remember climbing the tower. She remembered being *in* it — the way you remember being inside a bell after it has rung. The walls had been stone the color of old teeth, and every surface was carved with letters that moved when she wasn't looking at them directly. She had been small enough then to fit in the window seat, though she could not say how long ago *then* was. Time, for Wren, was not a river. It was a drawer of loose buttons: this one a tower, this one a fire, this one a voice.

The voice said: *carry it until someone worthy reads you.*

She did not know what *it* was. She only knew that she had been carrying it so long her bones had grown around it, the way a tree grows around a nail driven into its heartwood when it is young. If she were ever cut open, anyone looking would see the shape of it there, and would think: how strange, this tree grew wrong on purpose.

* * *

The woman's name was Bram. Wren knew this because the other woman — the tall one with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed ledger — said it often, in tones of exasperation that Wren had learned, in her few days above ground, meant affection. *Bram, the girl needs boots. Bram, the girl ate the last of the honey. Bram, we do not let children handle the jar of last words.*

Bram. It was a good name. It tasted like bread crusts and iron filings when Wren turned it over in her mouth, which she did sometimes at night, silently, the way other children might suck a sweet.

She did not speak. This was not because she couldn't. It was because the thing she carried sat directly behind her tongue, and she had learned — in the tower, in the burning, in the long white walking — that if she opened her mouth carelessly, things came out. Things with weight. Once, in a village whose name she'd also lost, she had been hungry and cold and a baker had refused her, and something had risen up her throat and slipped out between her teeth, one syllable, no bigger than a pebble.

The baker's ovens had gone cold. All of them. Every oven in the village, and the forge, and the hearth-fires, and the candle in the church, all in the same breath, as if winter had reached down through the chimney of the world and pinched out every flame at once. They had lit them again eventually. But the villagers had looked at her afterward the way people look at a well that has given up a drowned child, and Wren had understood two things very clearly.

One: what she carried was not safe.

Two: she would have to find someone who could read her before it found a way out on its own.

* * *

She trusted Bram because Bram smelled of swallowed deaths.

This required some explaining, even to herself, and Wren spent a good deal of time in the corner of the counting-house office — the warm corner, near the brazier, where the light from the vial-shelves made colored coins on the floor — working out exactly how to explain it, in case anyone ever asked. No one asked. But she worked it out anyway, because the tower had taught her that understanding was a kind of armor, and armor was the only inheritance she was sure she had.

Every person smelled like a house. That was the first principle. Most houses smelled of their cooking and their fires and the soap they used; most people smelled of their dinners and their fears and whatever they'd touched last. Ordinary. Readable at a glance, if you knew how to look, and Wren had always known how to look, because in the tower there had been nothing else to do.

But Bram's house was different. Underneath the bread-and-ink smell, underneath the lamp oil and the wool of her coat, there was a shelf of smells that did not belong to any living kitchen. Bitter almond, but old bitter almond, bitter almond that had been argued with and beaten. A green, swampy rot that Wren recognized from the pond behind the tower, where certain frogs sat unbothered by hawks. Something metallic and sweet, like coins left too long in a child's fist. And beneath all of them, faint as a watermark, a smell Wren had no name for except *the gray one*, because thinking about it too hard made the back of her teeth ache.

Thirty years of poisons, taken and survived, laid down in the body like rings in a tree. Wren didn't know the number. She just knew that Bram's house had many rooms, and every room held a death that had knocked and been refused.

And here was the second principle, the one that mattered: the thing Wren carried *hated* those rooms.

She had tested this without meaning to. On her first night, half-asleep on the pallet beside Bram's bed — because Bram had put her there without asking permission from anyone, including Odile Voss, who had opinions — Wren had felt the carried thing stir and lean toward the sleeping woman the way a dog leans toward a door it means to dig under. And then it had touched the edge of one of those swallowed deaths, the way you touch a stove to learn what *hot* means, and it had flinched back, hard, into the deepest part of Wren's chest, and gone quiet, and stayed quiet for two whole days.

A key does not like a lock. Wren was eight, not stupid. Whatever she was carrying wanted doors open — she could feel its wanting the way you feel weather coming in your knees — and Bram Almeida was a woman built entirely of doors that had already been tried and held.

So: trust. Not the soft, easy trust of children in stories, who follow kindly strangers into woods. A harder kind. The trust of a girl who had walked a long way through snow because a voice told her to carry something, and who had decided, with the full weight of her small serious heart, that this bitter-smelling woman with the careful hands was the first door worth knocking on.

* * *

The counting-house became, without either of them announcing it, a place of rituals.

Rituals were important. The tower had been full of them — the turning letters, the candles relit at exact hours, the voice arriving on schedule like a tax collector. Rituals meant the world still had rules, and rules meant the world could be survived.

In the mornings, Bram went to the palace, and Odile Voss sat at the great desk with her ledgers, and Wren sat in her corner and watched the Guild work. She learned the inventory without trying. Shelf one: borrowed courage, stoppered in blue glass, each vial humming faintly like a trapped bee. Shelf two: years, decanted pale and gold, the labels written in the handwriting of the people who'd sold them — some steady, some shaking. Shelf three, behind a curtain, the jars of last words, which hummed differently, lower, the way a room hums after an argument.

Odile caught her looking at the curtain on the third day.

"That one isn't for children," Odile said. Her voice wasn't unkind. It was the voice of someone stating a fact about gravity.

Wren looked at her and did not answer, because answering was dangerous, and also because she had discovered that silence made adults fill the space themselves, and filled space was information.

"They're last words," Odile said anyway, after a moment, because Wren had been right about the filling. "What people said at the end. We buy them from the dying, or their families. Some are apologies. Some are instructions." She paused. "One of them is a recipe."

Wren tilted her head.

"It's a very good recipe," Odile admitted, with the air of a woman confessing to a crime. "That is not the point."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. The muscles arranged themselves and then thought better of it, the way a bird lands on a branch and immediately reconsiders. But Odile saw it — Odile saw everything, it was her whole profession — and something in the tall woman's closed-ledger face shifted, a page turning.

"You're going to be trouble," Odile told her. "I can always tell. Trouble has a particular way of sitting still."

* * *

At night, Bram read.

Not aloud — Wren suspected Bram thought she couldn't understand, or perhaps that reading aloud to a mute child was a kindness neither of them could afford. She simply sat by the lamp with her own papers, balancing columns, and occasionally muttered numbers at them like curses, and Wren lay on the pallet and watched her and listened to the house of her breathing.

It was on the fifth night that Wren noticed the private ledger.

It was a thin book, thinner than the great ledgers downstairs, bound in plain gray cloth, and Bram kept it wrapped in a scarf at the bottom of her trunk, which meant it was important, because people only hid things that frightened them. When Bram went down to check the door bolts — she checked them three times, always three, another ritual — Wren slid off the pallet and crossed the cold floor and lifted the book out.

She did not open it. Opening it would have been theft, and theft was a debt, and Wren had begun to understand that in this building debts were the only religion. But she laid her palm flat on the cover, the way she used to lay her hand on the tower wall, and she read what she could read through the skin of it.

Names. Dates. Small precise observations in a cramped, controlled hand: *answered before asked. shadow lags on the east stair. wine untouched at the third toast.* And threaded through all of it, in the margins, in strokes pressed hard enough to tear: fear. Fear with nowhere to go, written down because writing was the only place Bram Almeida ever let anything out.

Wren put the book back exactly as it had been, scarf and all, and returned to her pallet, and lay in the dark feeling the carried thing turn over slowly in her chest like a sleeper changing position.

It had liked the book. She could tell. It had leaned toward the names the way it leaned toward doors.

She resolved, then, lying in the dark with her arms around her own ribs, holding the lid down on herself: she would have to be careful. Careful in the tower had meant keeping her mouth shut and her thoughts shallow, skating on the top inch of her mind so the deep water wouldn't notice her. Careful here would mean the same, plus one more thing.

Plus making sure the thing never, ever got interested in Bram.

* * *

On the seventh night, the dream came again, and this time it brought the fire.

The tower, but burning now. Letters peeling off the walls and curling in the heat like leaves. And the voice — the voice was there, closer than it had ever been in waking memory, close enough to have a texture, dry and papery and terribly tired, saying the words again while the world came apart:

*Carry it until someone worthy reads you.*

And Wren, in the dream, in the smoke, doing what she had apparently done once already, in a life she couldn't reach: pressing her small hands over her own mouth, because the worthy reader had not come yet, and the words wanted out, and out was the one direction the world could not survive.

She woke gasping, sitting bolt upright, and the candle on Bram's desk had guttered to a blue bead of flame, and frost had bloomed across the inside of the window in patterns that were almost — *almost* — letters.

Bram was already awake. Of course she was. Wren had begun to suspect the woman slept the way soldiers eat: quickly, lightly, with one hand on her weapons.

Bram crossed the floor, knelt, and did not ask what the dream was. Instead she did something no one had done in any of Wren's reachable memories. She took Wren's hands — both of them, gently, turning them over as if checking for burns — and then, finding nothing there, she simply kept hold of them, and sat down on the cold floorboards beside the pallet, and stayed.

Neither of them spoke. Wren couldn't, and Bram, Wren was beginning to understand, mostly wouldn't; words were currency in this city, and Bram hoarded hers like a dragon. So they sat in the shared country of silence, and gradually the frost on the window melted, and the candle stood back up, and Wren's heartbeat came down out of the tower and back into her chest.

After a long time, Bram said, quietly, to the dark rather than to her: "Whatever it is you're carrying — you don't have to carry it alone. That's not a promise I can keep. But it's an offer."

And Wren, who could not answer, did the only thing she could. She leaned sideways until her head rested against Bram's shoulder, and she felt the woman go very still — startled, the way wild animals are startled by gentleness — and then settle, deliberately, the way you settle a load you've decided to bear.

Fragile. Both of them knew it was fragile. A thing like this could be broken by one bad morning, one lie, one debt called in at the wrong hour. Wren had seen enough of the Guild's shelves to know that everything in this city was priced, and she had no way of knowing yet what price would someday be asked for her.

But that night, on a narrow pallet above a counting-house full of impossible things, an eight-year-old girl who was a living key fell asleep against a woman who was a living lock, and the carried thing in her chest lay flat and sullen and did not stir, and that was the closest either of them would come, for a long while, to peace.

* * *

Three days later, Odile Voss climbed the stairs two at a time, which she never did, and burst into the room without knocking, which she also never did, and behind her, leaning on a stick, wrapped in shawls despite the season, came a thing Wren had assumed was furniture.

It was not furniture. It was old beyond old, brown and folded like a mummy that had decided to take up commerce, and its eyes — when they opened — were the color of coins left on a grave.

It looked past Odile. Past Bram. Straight at Wren, sitting in her corner among the colored lights of the vial-shelves.

And it smiled, with lips that had dried to leather centuries ago, and it said, in a voice like pages turning:

"Say something, little one. Just one word. I've been waiting ninety years to hear it."

Behind it, the master ledger downstairs — three floors down, chained shut, warded, locked — began, softly, to open itself.
