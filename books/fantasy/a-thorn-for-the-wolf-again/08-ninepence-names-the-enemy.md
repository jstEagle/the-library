## 8. Ninepence Names the Enemy

There are three rules to waking Ninepence, and I have broken all of them at least once, which is why I am the only one allowed to do it.

Rule one: never wake him before dusk. He considers daylight a personal insult, and he holds grudges the way other men hold pensions. Rule two: bring something sweet. His tongue dried out sometime during the reign of King Aldous the Second, and honey is the only thing that loosens it—literally and figuratively. Rule three: never ask him a question you aren't prepared to hear answered.

I broke rule three on a Tuesday, in the back vault of the Guild counting house, with a mute child's arrival still sitting in my chest like a swallowed coin.

* * *

Ninepence lives—or persists, or occupies space, the verb depends on your theology—in a cedar cabinet at the far end of the oldest room in the building. The cabinet has eleven drawers. Ten of them hold things the Guild has confiscated over four centuries: a mirror that shows only debts, a bell that rings for lies told in good faith, a wedding ring that grew into the finger of whoever wore it last. The eleventh drawer holds Ninepence.

He was a broker once. A real one, flesh and appetite and a ledger hand so fine they said he could write a contract on a grain of rice. That was before my time by a comfortable margin. What happened to him is not recorded anywhere, because he bought up every record of it himself and then, as near as anyone can tell, ate them. All that remains is what he became: a small, desiccated figure no bigger than a cat, folded like a letter that has been read too many times, with skin like vellum and eyes that are still, after everything, wet.

I unlocked the drawer at half past six, when the light through the vault's high window had gone the color of weak tea. He was already awake. He was always already awake. I sometimes suspect he sleeps exactly as much as he wants people to think he does, which is to say never.

"Odile Voss," he said. His voice is dry paper dragged over dry stone, and it always takes me a moment to stop flinching from it. "You smell of worry and cheap ink. Someone has written something they shouldn't."

"The ledger wrote itself," I said. "Last night. Before the girl touched anything."

That got his attention. Ninepence's attention is a physical thing; you feel it settle on you like a hand on the back of your neck. "The master ledger does not write itself. The master ledger records what *is*. If it opened its own pages, then something arrived that the world had already agreed to notice." A pause, dry as everything else about him. "The honey, Odile. You're stalling."

I set the jar on the edge of the drawer. He took it in both hands—his fingers are long, too many joints in them, an anatomy that stopped consulting the standard model some centuries ago—and dipped his face to it like a man drinking from a well he'd dug himself.

While he drank, I told him about the girl. Eight years old, maybe. Found standing in the snow outside Bram Almeida's door with no footprints behind her and frost melted in a perfect circle around her feet. Speaking—not babbling, *speaking*—in a language that made our youngest clerk drop a tray of bottled courage and made the courage itself go flat. And the ledger opening without a pen, ink rising through the page like blood through linen, recording her arrival in letters none of us could read except that we could all, somehow, feel them.

"I need you to tell me what she said," I finished. "Or what tongue it was. Or whether I should put her on a boat and lose the boat."

Ninepence set down the honey. For a long moment he did nothing at all, which from Ninepence is louder than shouting.

Then he began to laugh.

It is important, here, to be precise about what his laughter sounds like, because I want you to understand why Bram Almeida came running down two flights of stairs with a kitchen knife in her hand. It sounds like pages turning. Hundreds of them, thousands, riffling faster and faster, and underneath the turning a thin thread of sound that might once have been a man's voice and is now just the memory of amusement, worn smooth as a river stone.

"What," said Bram, arriving in the doorway with her knife held low and sensible, the way she holds everything, "did you say to him?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "That's the alarming part."

* * *

We moved him to the reading table. This involves a lacquered tray, a velvet cushion, and a level of ceremony I normally reserve for clients who can ruin us. Ninepence sat in the middle of the table like a gargoyle that had been through a war, and looked at the girl.

Wren had been brought down from Bram's rooms at my request, over Bram's objection, which she expressed the way she expresses everything: by standing very straight and saying nothing until the silence did her arguing for her. The child sat on a stool with her hands in her lap and watched Ninepence with the frank, unafraid stare of someone who has already seen worse than a mummified broker and found it unremarkable.

"Say it again," Ninepence said to her. Gently. Which startled me more than the laughter had. In forty years I have heard him use perhaps six tones of voice, and gentle was not among the inventory.

Wren looked at Bram. Bram looked at me. I nodded, and hated myself for nodding, and would nod again, because that is the job.

The girl spoke. Four syllables, maybe five. They went through the room like a cold current through warm water. The candles leaned. The ledger on its shelf rustled though nothing touched it. And Ninepence closed his eyes and said, in the same tongue, something back—and Wren's whole body changed, the way a locked door changes when the right key turns somewhere inside it. She didn't understand the words. But something in her recognized them.

"That," said Ninepence, opening his eyes, "is First Speech."

"We have First Speech fragments in the archive," I said. "Oath-formulas. Half a coronation rite. Corvane catalogued them thirty years ago and concluded they were decorative."

"Corvane," said Ninepence, "catalogued the words and missed the grammar entirely, which is why Corvane is dead and I am merely this." He gestured at himself with a hand like a bundle of twigs. "Decorative. Yes. Let me tell you what those decorations were for."

Bram pulled up a stool. She sat the way she tastes: completely present, holding nothing back, waiting for whatever came. I have known her nine years and I have never once seen her flinch from information. It is her best quality and, I suspected even then, the one that would eventually cost us all the most.

"Before there were kingdoms," Ninepence said, "there were oaths. Not promises—the little mortal kind, the kind you break on Tuesdays—but Oaths. Bindings sworn in First Speech, the language the world was negotiated in. When you swear a thing in First Speech, you do not merely say it. You *become* the saying of it. The word and the thing stop being neighbors and start being the same object." His wet eyes moved from me to Bram. "Your Guild trades in imitations of this. Bottled memories. Borrowed years. We deal in the shadow of that language, cast on the wall of commerce. The girl speaks the fire itself."

"And what," Bram said quietly, "does the fire have to do with the crown?"

And there it was. The question I had been circling since the ledger opened itself. Because I had done the arithmetic everyone in the trade eventually does: the girl arrives speaking the oldest language in the world, and within a season the crown's chamberlain starts buying memories out of our stores, and I am old enough and crooked enough to know that coincidence is just conspiracy that hasn't introduced itself yet.

Ninepence was quiet for a while. I poured more honey. He ignored it, which frightened me more than anything else that evening.

"Thirty-one years ago," he said at last, "a young king came to the Guild. Not through the front door. Through me." One twig-finger tapped the table. "He was young. He was desperate. There was a war on—you'll find it in the histories under a name that makes it sound noble—and he had come to believe that the war would not end while men could want things. Ambition, greed, vengeance. He wanted to take wanting away. From everyone. Including himself."

"He bound the war," Bram said slowly.

"He bound the *wanting*," Ninepence corrected. "And to do it, he needed something to eat the oaths. Every oath his lords had sworn, every vow, every promise of fealty and ambition and revenge—all of it, gathered up and swallowed, so that no man's desire could feed the fighting. He asked the Guild for an instrument. An oath-eater." The tapping finger stopped. "And the Guild, being the Guild, sold him one."

The candle flames had gone very still. Still flames are wrong. Fire should move; it is the one honest thing left in most rooms.

"What was the instrument?" I asked, though I already knew, the way you know the shape of a thing from the hole it leaves.

"A thing without edges," Ninepence said. "A hunger given just enough shape to aim. We called it a binding-spirit in the contract. The king called it his cure." A pause, dry as dust. "The contract required an anchor. A willing mortal vessel, to hold the thing steady while it worked. The king volunteered himself. Of course he did. He believed—this is the part that should keep you awake, Odile—that he was strong enough to hold it and then let it go. That power was a tool and he was a careful man."

"And?" Bram asked. Though we both knew.

"And the thing ate him," Ninepence said simply. "Not his body. Bodies are cheap. It ate the *oath* of him—the self he had sworn to be, the king he had promised to become. It consumed the monarch entire and kept the shape, because a shape is useful. A crown needs a head. And for thirty-one years, that thing has worn the king the way you wear a coat, Odile Voss, and it has ruled, and the war ended, and the peace has held, and every year it grows a little better at being him and a little less interested in ever taking the coat off."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above us, the counting house settled and creaked, ordinary as bread.

"The Hollow King," I said. The name came out of me from somewhere older than my professional caution. I'd heard it once, decades ago, whispered between two brokers who both died badly within the year, and I had filed it under superstition where it had sat comfortably ever since.

"One of its names," Ninepence agreed. "Names slide off it. That's rather the point of it."

Bram stood up. She didn't pace—Bram doesn't pace, she *arrives* at conclusions the way she arrives at doorways, directly and slightly frighteningly—but her hand had gone to her chest, to the place where, I knew, she kept the private ledger of royal anomalies she thought nobody knew about. The king who answers questions before they're asked. The shadow that moves a half-beat late, like a servant who hasn't quite learned the choreography.

"It's still hungry," she said. "An oath-eater doesn't stop eating. Thirty years of oaths sworn to the crown—every coronation oath, every marriage vow, every knight's kneeling promise—it's been feeding the whole time."

"Yes," said Ninepence.

"And it felt the girl arrive." Her voice didn't change but something underneath it did, a floor joist shifting under weight. "Because she's the opposite of everything it is. It eats bindings. She—" Bram looked at Wren, who was watching her with those grave dark eyes, hands still folded in her lap like a child at lessons. "What is she?"

"She is the failsafe," Ninepence said. "The binding contract required one. Any binding strong enough to hold a hunger like that must contain the seed of its own undoing, or the world rejects it—this is not philosophy, it is bookkeeping, the universe keeps double-entry accounts same as we do. The young king's line was sworn into the contract. Born into the oath-line. Carrying the unbinding word the way you carry a name, without knowing it, until someone worthy reads you." His gaze moved to the girl, and for one unguarded moment the wet eyes looked less like a corpse's and more like a man remembering what grief was for. "'Carry it,' the contract says, 'until someone worthy reads you.' I wrote that clause myself. I have regretted it in installments ever since."

"You wrote the contract," I said. My voice came out flat. "You sold the binding."

"I was the Guild," Ninepence said. "The Guild serves whoever pays. It is on our seal, Odile. You've stamped it ten thousand times." He let that sit, and it was true, and truth is the cruelest thing he ever deals in. "Yes. I sold a young king the rope. I told myself he was careful. I told myself the peace was worth it. And for thirty-one years I have sat in a drawer telling myself that the instrument would stay aimed, that the anchor would hold, that wanting bound is wanting tamed." He turned his ruined face toward Wren. "And now the failsafe has walked to our door speaking the fire itself, and the thing in the crown has begun buying memories through our own counters, and you ask me what tongue she speaks as if the answer could possibly make this worse."

Silence again. The candles remembered how to flicker.

"Only First Speech can unbind it," Ninepence went on, quieter now. "That is the sum of it. The Hollow King cannot be killed—it isn't alive, killing is a courtesy extended to living things. It cannot be starved—the oaths of a kingdom renew daily, like grass. It can only be *unbound*: the original contract dissolved, term by term, in the language it was sworn in. The word, spoken truly. The witness, standing truly. And the willingness of the bound—" he paused, and I saw him choose, deliberately, to give us the whole of it, "—for the binding is anchored in the king, and the king, what's left of him, is still in there. Fragmented. Buried. Thirty years eaten down to rind. But the contract requires his consent to end. The prisoner must open his own cell."

"The real king," Bram said. "Isolde's father. He's still—"

"A remnant. A voice under a floorboard." Ninepence's twig-hands folded. "Perhaps enough of him left to say yes. Perhaps not. That, children, is the wager. And the house betting against you wears a crown, eats oaths, and has spent three decades learning exactly how human beings are persuaded."

I looked at Wren. She was looking at Ninepence, and her small brow was furrowed, and then she did the thing that turned my blood to well-water: she reached out and touched the master ledger where it lay on the table's corner, and the letters on the open page stirred, just slightly, like grass under wind.

She couldn't read them yet. But they knew her. The way a lock knows a key it has never met.

"Then we have a problem," I said, because someone had to say something practical before the enormity ate us all, and practicality is my particular vice. "Because the crown is already shopping in our stores. The chamberlain's purchase yesterday—I told you it was routine. It wasn't. They extracted a memory, and the memory was of her." I nodded at Wren. "They don't know where she is yet. But they know she exists, they know what she looks like, and they have the Guild's invoice."

"Then the Guild," said Ninepence, "has a choice to make. It usually does. It usually makes the profitable one." His wet eyes found mine, and there was no mercy in them and no malice either, only the terrible clarity of a man who has already seen how this ends in several versions and dislikes all of them. "So decide quickly, Odile Voss. Decide whose side the ledger is on. Because I promise you the thing in the crown has already decided, and it pays promptly, and it never, ever asks why."

Above us, faint through the floors, the counting house bell rang the hour—and rang one stroke too many, a sound with no source, a sound like a page turning somewhere it shouldn't.

Bram's hand went to her knife.
