## 9. The Guild Sells Both Sides

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over the Guild counting house when money walks in wearing court colors.

I'd learned to hear it the way a sailor hears weather. The ledgers go quiet—Odile's pen stops mid-stroke, the ink stops dripping, even the jar of last words on the shelf seems to hold its breath, which is unsettling because that jar has never once held its breath in my experience. Last words are chatterboxes. They mutter all night. Odile keeps them by her bed like a cat.

So when Chamberlain Ferren Vael came through the back door on a grey Tuesday with snow melting off his sable-trimmed shoulders and three hundred crowns' worth of intent in his walk, I knew two things before he opened his mouth.

One: the palace wanted something it couldn't ask for through proper channels.

Two: I was going to hate what it was.

* * *

Let me tell you about chamberlains. A king rules a kingdom; a chamberlain rules the king's rooms, and therefore the king, and therefore everything else, though no one says so out loud because saying so out loud is how you end up tasting soup for a living instead of eating it. Ferren Vael was the second chamberlain, which meant he did the work of the first while the first did the work of being seen near the king. He had the soft hands of a man who has never carried anything heavier than a secret, and the eyes of a man who carries nothing else.

He also had, I noticed as he removed his gloves finger by finger, a faint smell of the apothecary's quarter. Not poison. Something subtler. Ink fixative and silver polish and something underneath both that my tongue recognized before my brain did—the ghost-smell of memory-extraction, the way a room smells after someone has cried hard enough to leave a mark.

I keep telling you my body is a ledger. Sometimes it reads things I wish it wouldn't.

"Madam Voss," Vael said, inclining his head to Odile with exactly the correct degree of respect for a woman whose establishment could ruin him. "And the taster." He said my title the way you'd say *the mop*. "I'm told you handle delicate extractions."

"We handle commerce," Odile said pleasantly. She was behind her desk, small and iron-grey and utterly immovable, the way she always is when there's profit or peril in the room, and often both. "Delicacy is a matter of pricing."

"I need a memory taken. Cleanly. From a subject who will not consent."

Ah, I thought. There it is.

Now, here is the thing about the Guild of Impossible Things that I have never managed to make peace with, despite six years of balancing its books: we do not ask why. It's practically scripture. Odile can quote the founding charter from memory—*the Guild serves whoever pays, and asks nothing but the price*—and she does quote it, usually right before doing something that makes me want to put my head through the wall. A merchant buys borrowed courage to face his creditors. A widow buys one hour of her husband's voice. A man buys a name that never belonged to him and grows into it like a coat. We don't ask. Asking is how you start caring, and caring is how you start choosing sides, and an institution that chooses sides stops being an institution and starts being a corpse.

I understood this. I agreed with this, in the abstract, the way you agree that winter exists.

But Wren was asleep upstairs.

* * *

She'd been with us eleven days by then. Eleven days of a silent eight-year-old who ate like a sparrow and watched everything, who sat on the stairs during ledger-hours with her chin on her knees, who had somehow—without a single word—arranged the entire household around herself the way water arranges itself around a stone. Odile pretended to be annoyed. Ninepence, our oldest member, who is mummified and mostly sleeps and communicates in costs, had woken twice just to look at her, which for Ninepence is roughly equivalent to writing a sonnet.

The girl spoke a language older than any kingdom. The master ledger had opened itself to record her arrival before anyone touched a pen. I had not yet let myself think too hard about either fact, because thinking too hard about facts is a luxury for people without poison in their bloodstreams and a child at their kitchen table.

"The subject," Odile said, in her best merchant voice, "is unwilling. That raises the difficulty considerably. Unwilling memories sit deep. They fight the extraction. You're paying for craft, not just procedure."

Vael smiled. It was a good smile. It had been selected from a catalog. "Price is not an obstacle."

"Price is never an obstacle," Odile said. "That's why we call it a price. What is the memory?"

"A face," Vael said. "A face seen recently, in circumstances the crown considers... irregular. We need it removed from the mind of a servant girl who may have been in the wrong corridor at the wrong time."

My pen stopped moving. I hadn't realized I'd been writing until I stopped.

"And where," Odile asked, silk over steel, "did the crown learn that the Guild removes memories from unwilling subjects? Because I don't recall advertising that service. In fact, I recall specifically not advertising it."

"You have your methods of learning things," Vael said. "So do we."

Which meant someone inside the Guild was selling information to the palace, or someone inside the palace had once been inside the Guild, and neither possibility improved my morning. I filed it away in the drawer where I keep things I'll scream about later.

"The extraction requires a vessel," Odile said. "We don't destroy memories, chamberlain. We move them. Into glass. The client takes possession of the vessel and does with it what clients do." Her eyes flicked to me, one heartbeat, half a warning. "Standard rate plus the unwilling surcharge. Four hundred crowns. Half now."

Vael counted out two hundred crowns in mint-fresh coin, each piece placed on the desk with a small deliberate click, like a man laying tiles. Then he waited, gloved hands folded, while Odile summoned the extraction kit and I sat very still and did arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Because here was the thought crawling up out of the cellar of my mind: *a face seen recently, in circumstances the crown considers irregular.*

The palace had been strange lately. I want to be clear that strangeness at court is like dampness in a cellar—you expect some, you live with it—but this was a new vintage. The king answering questions before they were asked. His shadow lagging, or leading, depending on the candle. Small wrongnesses I'd been recording in a private ledger I kept locked under my floorboard, because a food-taster who writes down *His Majesty's shadow arrived before His Majesty* tends to stop being a food-taster and start being an example.

And now the crown was buying the removal of a face.

* * *

The extraction took place in the back room, and I should not have watched, and I watched.

The subject was a laundry maid, maybe nineteen, brought in by a palace guard who stood outside the door looking at the wall with the practiced blankness of a man being paid to have no opinions. She was terrified. Of course she was terrified. Strangers in dark robes, a chair with straps, a smell of silver polish and old crying—terrified was the only reasonable response available to her.

Odile worked with a focus I rarely saw from her, and I understood why: unwilling extractions are surgery, not shopping. She set the vessel—a squat bottle of smoked glass, big enough to hold a fistful of light—and began the slow business of persuading the maid's own mind to let go of one specific thing.

"It won't hurt," Odile told her, and for once I believed her, because Odile is many things but she does not lie about pain. Pain is inventory. You don't mislabel inventory. "It will feel like a word on the tip of your tongue that never comes. You'll know something was there. You won't know what."

The maid cried anyway. Quietly, the whole time, tears running into her collar, while the extraction drew the memory up and out of her like a splinter.

And I saw it.

You see, the vessels aren't opaque to everyone. That's part of my curse or my gift, depending on the day: bodies built to survive poison develop odd sensitivities. I've tasted memories by accident before—one drop of a borrowed-courage vial on my lip gave me ten seconds of a stranger's wedding day, and I cried into the dishwater and told no one. So when the smoked glass filled, when the face settled into it the way sediment settles, I felt it against my skin like heat from a stove.

A face. Small. Young. Dark-haired, sharp-chinned, with the enormous watchful eyes of a creature that has learned the world cannot be trusted to feed it.

Wren's face.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. *Hers.* Seen close, recently, in whatever corridor or courtyard or doorway the crown considered irregular—and I knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who has survived forty-one poisons and counting, exactly which irregular circumstance that was. Wren had appeared in the snow outside my door eleven days ago. Before that, she had come from somewhere. Through somewhere. Past someone. And someone in the palace had seen her going by, and the crown—which answers questions before they're asked, whose shadow moves wrong—had decided that face was worth four hundred crowns to erase.

Not to find. To erase. If they wanted to find her, they'd buy a tracking charm, or hire the informants, or simply ask the guard who'd surely already reported the sighting. No. They wanted the seeing undone. They wanted a hole in the world where a witness used to be.

The crown wasn't hunting her yet. The crown was tidying up.

Which meant the crown knew she existed. Which meant the clock I hadn't known was ticking had been ticking for eleven days.

* * *

Vael took possession of the vessel himself, wrapping it in velvet with the tenderness of a man handling a relic. He did not look at the maid, who sat slumped and empty-eyed in the chair, knowing something had been taken and unable to say what. He did not thank Odile beyond the courtesies money makes unnecessary. At the door, he paused.

"One more item," he said. "Purely administrative. The crown maintains a register of unusual persons within the city. Foreigners, hedge-witches, unlicensed speakers. If such a person were to seek shelter anywhere in the Guild quarter—" he smiled his catalog smile at both of us, including me in it for the first time, which I did not enjoy, "—the crown would consider it a professional courtesy to be informed. There's a standing fee."

"There is," Odile said, "a standing policy of neutrality."

"Neutrality," Vael repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That's what I admire about your establishment, Madam Voss. You sell to everyone. It's almost a form of honesty." He settled his gloves. "Good day."

The door closed. The silence that followed was the expensive kind.

The maid was escorted out by the guard, blinking at the world like a woman waking from a nap she didn't remember starting. I watched her go and thought: *that's what erasure looks like from the inside.* I thought it calmly, professionally, the way I think about arsenic symptoms. Then I went upstairs and stood outside Wren's door for a long moment, listening to her breathe, before I could make my legs carry me back down to the counting room.

* * *

Odile was re-inking her pen when I returned. She didn't look up.

"You recognized the face," she said. Not a question. Odile doesn't ask questions she's already priced.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And the crown knows she exists, and it's erasing witnesses, and it just invited us to sell her location for a standing fee." I pulled off my apron—I still wore the taster's apron around the counting house, a habit, armor made of linen—and dropped it on the desk. "Say the policy out loud, Odile. Say we'd take the fee."

She set the pen down. For a moment she looked every year of her age and then some, the way a fortress looks when you catch it between wars.

"The policy," she said slowly, "is that the Guild serves whoever pays. Do you know why that policy exists, Bram? Not greed. Greed is just the shape of it. The reason is that the moment we choose a side, we become a weapon, and weapons get broken. Every guild that ever picked a side in a succession war is ash. We survived three hundred years by being nobody's. That includes being nobody's when somebody innocent needs us." She met my eyes. "It's a monstrous policy. It's also the only reason there's a Guild left to be monstrous with."

"That's very tidy."

"It's very true. Tidy and true are cousins." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I'm not taking the fee. Let's be clear about that before you say something we'll both regret. But I want you to understand what I'm risking by refusing, so you understand what it costs to have a heart in this building. Vael will notice the omission eventually. The crown notices omissions the way sharks notice blood."

"Then we're already bleeding."

"We've been bleeding since the night she knocked," Odile said. "We just hadn't seen the wound." She turned the ledger toward her and began, with terrible steadiness, to write up the day's entries. Two hundred crowns. One extraction. One vessel, delivered. And then, in the column where she normally recorded the nature of the goods, she wrote something I'd never seen her write in six years.

She wrote: *sold to the wrong buyer.*

"What are you doing?" I asked. "That's not an entry. That's a confession."

"It's a note," Odile said. "For whoever balances these books after me. Someone should know we saw it and sold it anyway." Her pen scratched. "The charter says we don't ask why. It doesn't say we don't remember. Remembering is free, Bram. It's the last free thing in this building."

I looked at her—at this woman who had taught me that caring was a liability, who priced grief by the dram—and I understood, finally, that she had been carrying the same weight I had, longer than I had, and had chosen to keep carrying it rather than put it down, because putting it down meant becoming the kind of institution that sells a child's face in a bottle and calls it commerce.

"Who told the palace about us?" I asked. "About the extractions. About the girl. Someone talked."

Odile's pen stopped.

"That," she said quietly, "is the question I've been avoiding since Tuesday began. And I would very much like it to remain unanswered, because I have known everyone under this roof for decades, and I am not ready to find out which of them the answer is."

Above us, through the floorboards, I heard Wren's footsteps cross the landing—light, quick, going to the window she liked, the one that faced the street. Watching, the way she always watched. Learning the city that was learning to hunt her.

And beneath her footsteps, faint but unmistakable, the master ledger rustled on its shelf. All by itself. Turning pages.

Like something reading ahead.
