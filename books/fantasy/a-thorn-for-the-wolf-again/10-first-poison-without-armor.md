## 10. First Poison Without Armor

There is a particular confidence that comes from thirty years of swallowing death on a schedule. I'd call it arrogance, but arrogance implies you chose it. Mine was more like a callus: the body's quiet insistence that nothing served to me could do lasting harm. For three decades the poisons of the kingdom had filed past my tongue like petitioners before a bored magistrate, and I had sent them all away with barely a raised eyebrow.

That morning I woke without the callus.

I lay in my narrow bed above the Guild counting house and performed the inventory I now performed daily, the way a man with a new limp relearns his stairs. Immunity: sold. Thirty years of accumulated resistance, traded to the Guild's stores in a transaction so clean it had its own receipt. Somewhere in a vault beneath my floor sat a vial labeled *Almeida, B. — general toxicological tolerance, wholesale*, and some paying customer would drink it down and become, for a season, untouchable. Meanwhile I was what I had been at nineteen, on my first morning in the palace kitchens: an ordinary woman whose stomach could be murdered like anyone else's.

"You look pale," Wren said from the doorway. She had taken to watching me dress for court the way other children watch weather — with total attention and no comment.

"I'm fine."

"You're checking your own pulse."

"Amateurs check pulses." I tucked my shirt in. "Professionals count their fingers and move on."

She didn't laugh. She never laughed at my jokes, which I told anyway, because someone in this arrangement had to pretend it was all manageable. She held out her hand instead, solemn as a tiny tax collector, and I gave her my sleeve to hold while I laced my boots, which was apparently the ritual now. A child who couldn't speak, anchoring me to the world by my cuff. There were worse talismans.

Odile Voss was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with a cup of willowbark tea and the expression she reserved for clients who were about to make her day complicated.

"The princess ate at the palace last night," she said.

"Princess Isolde eats at the palace every night. It's where the palace keeps its food."

"She sent for a private assay this morning. Through intermediaries. Paid triple and asked no questions." Odile sipped her tea. "The Guild doesn't ask why. You know that. But I'm telling you what we didn't ask why about, because you're going to walk into that dining hall at noon, and I find I've developed opinions about your continued breathing."

"What did the assay find?"

"Nothing conclusive. Which is itself conclusive, if you think about it the way I think about things after forty years in this trade." She set down the cup. "Bram. Today, taste like a woman who can die. Because you can."

* * *

The palace at midday was all gilt and ceremony, and I moved through it feeling naked in a way no amount of livery could fix. My station was unchanged — the taster's stool beside the king's high table, the small silver spoon, the ceremonial first bite — but everything underneath had changed. Before, I tasted the way a customs officer inspects cargo: professionally, remotely, with the calm of someone who cannot be bribed by the merchandise. Now every dish arrived like a stranger at my door, and I had to read its intentions by lamplight.

Wit alone, then. Fine. Wit is what you use when armor fails, and I had spent thirty years watching cooks and cupbearers and one memorably ambitious under-chaplain, and I knew things about food that no immunity ever taught me.

Immunity tells you *what*. Observation tells you *when*, *where*, and *who flinched*. So I watched.

The soup course came up from the kitchens under a covered tureen carried by a boy named Pell, fourteen, freckled, honest to the point of disability. He lifted the lid; steam rose straight and even. Straight steam means steady heat means no volatile compounds added late. I took my spoonful and waited out the sixty seconds that used to be a formality and was now a prayer.

Nothing. The soup was venison and barley and someone's heavy hand with the juniper, but it was only soup.

The roast came next, and with it the first wrongness. Not in the meat — in the gravy boat. The king's gravy arrived in the blue-veined porcelain that lived on the third shelf of the buttery, but the princess's gravy arrived in a matching boat that I had never seen before, and I had catalogued that buttery the way other people catalogue their debts. New vessel. New vessels mean new opportunities: a glazed interior can hold residue a scrub won't reach, or conceal a lining, or simply announce that somebody wanted a specific dish to travel in a specific container and didn't want to explain why.

I drifted toward Isolde's end of the table with the lazy purposelessness that is the taster's whole art. She was seventeen and sat like a drawn bow — spine straight, hands folded, eyes doing constant quiet arithmetic on everyone in the room. We had exchanged perhaps two hundred words in three years, most of them variations on *you're in my light, taster.*

"Your gravy, Highness," said her cupbearer, and reached for the ladle.

"It smells off," I said, loudly enough. "Let me check it against the king's service. Palace regulation."

It wasn't palace regulation. Nobody checked anything against anything; the whole point of a royal taster was that there was exactly one of me. But regulations are like gods — everyone assumes they exist and no one asks to see them, and the cupbearer, who was twenty and terrified of being noticed by anyone above his station, stepped back as if the gravy had hissed at him.

I dipped the tasting spoon. Smelled first: roast drippings, thyme, wine reduction, flour. And underneath, so faint it might have been my imagination performing for an audience — a green bitterness, like the ghost of walnut shells.

Arsenic's cousin, or something dressed as him. Old friend. I knew that bitterness the way you know a voice through a wall.

Here is the arithmetic I did in the four seconds that followed, standing in a sunlit hall with a spoon in my hand and no callus on my soul. If I announced poison, three things happened: the kitchen went into lockdown, whoever planted it learned their delivery failed, and — because the assay trail led back through Guild intermediaries, because everything led back through the Guild eventually — questions started being asked that ended with a mute eight-year-old girl in a cellar above a counting house. If I said nothing and let the princess eat, then whatever paranoid instinct had driven her to order that assay was vindicated posthumously, and I got to attend another funeral knowing I'd held the answer in a teaspoon.

If I drank it myself, I might die, slowly and badly, in front of the entire court.

I have never claimed to be a good gambler. Only a committed one.

"Regulation requires the taster verify all service adjacent to the royal table," I announced to the room at large, and drank the spoonful down, and followed it with a second, fuller mouthful ladled straight from the boat, because if you're going to be an idiot, be a thorough idiot — a trace dose proves contamination; a real dose proves intent, and intent was what I needed on record if anyone was ever going to believe what came next.

The gravy tasted excellent. That's the thing they don't put in the ballads. Good poisoning requires good cooking, because nobody swallows a bad sauce twice.

Then I smiled at the table, returned to my stool, sat down beside the king's elbow, and began to wait.

* * *

The waiting is the part I'll remember longest, and not because of the pain, though the pain made its arguments. It's what the waiting showed me.

Twenty minutes, give or take. Enough time for the roast course to clear. The bitterness bloomed at the back of my throat first, polite, almost nostalgic — hello again, old acquaintance, haven't seen you since the spring you came in the honey — except that this time there was no army of thirty years standing between us. Just me, unarmored, holding the gate with a kitchen towel.

Sweat came early. Wrong sweat — cold, oily, the kind that arrives before any heat does. My fingertips went numb in the pattern I recognized from textbooks and other people's misfortunes: edges first, closing inward like a door shutting on a lit room. My heart picked up speed, then stumbled, then picked it up again with the desperate overcorrection of a horse that has felt the ground move.

And through all of it, the hall went on. Someone told a joke about a bishop. The musicians tuned. The king — and I noted this even through the ringing in my ears, because noting things is the last skill to leave — the king turned toward the commotion of a dropped goblet a full half-second *before* the goblet dropped. His shadow, cast long by the southern windows, bent at the knee when he stood still.

I wrote none of it down. I was busy dying quietly at the end of a table.

"Bram Almeida," said Princess Isolde, arriving at my side with a speed that suggested she'd been watching me longer than I'd credited. Her voice was low and perfectly level. "You're gray."

"New livery."

"Your lips are gray."

"Fashion advances."

She looked at me the way she looked at everything, running the arithmetic, and I saw the moment the sum completed — saw it land behind her eyes with the weight of a confirmed suspicion. Whatever paranoia had ordered that assay hadn't been paranoia at all. It had been the same watchfulness I kept in my private ledger of royal anomalies, wearing a crown instead of a stool.

"You tasted my gravy," she said.

"Regulation."

"There is no such regulation." She crouched, ostensibly adjusting my footstool, actually putting her face level with mine, and for one unguarded instant the drawn-bow composure cracked and I saw underneath: a young woman who had been afraid in this building for years and had finally, finally caught proof of something. "Whoever you are," she murmured, "don't die yet. I have questions."

"Get me a physician and I'll consider answering one."

"No physician. Physicians report to my father." Something crossed her face when she said it — the flicker of a person handling a live coal. She stood, raised her voice to normal carrying pitch, and said with flawless princessly authority: "The taster has taken ill. Clear the east corridor. I will supervise her removal personally — it wouldn't do for the palace to lose its only honest servant in front of the ambassadors."

Only honest servant. I'd have laughed if my diaphragm were taking instructions.

They carried me out through corridors that tilted like ship decks, Isolde walking beside my litter the entire way, issuing orders in a voice that had clearly been rehearsing command since childhood. At the servants' stair she dismissed everyone but two guards she named personally — hers, I'd have bet my remaining memories, though I didn't yet know how few of those I had left to bet.

"Where," she demanded of the air in general, "is that woman from the counting house? Voss. The apothecary-adjacent one."

* * *

Odile arrived in under half an hour, which meant she had already been within half an hour, which meant she had known, the way she always seemed to know, that today was a day requiring proximity. She swept into the antechamber where I lay on a trestle table among stacked chairs, took one look at my color, and swore with the fluency of a woman who has invoiced kings.

"Arsenic variant," I managed. "Green bitterness under walnut. Slow onset. It's elegant, Odile. Whoever built it studied."

"Hold that thought and also your lunch." She was already unpacking her kit: vials in graded greens, a mortar, a taper. "Isolde. Your Highness. Out."

"He stays," Isolde said, nodding at one guard. "He's sworn to me, not to my father. The other one can go."

For a moment I thought Odile would argue — she argues with royalty the way she argues with creditors, patiently and to victory. Instead she glanced at the remaining guard, then at Isolde, and something passed between the two women that I was too busy dying to parse. An old recognition. I'd think about that later, when later existed.

The antidote, when it went down, tasted like pennies and regret and, unmistakably, like the Guild — that faint mineral signature underlying every product in their stores, as if the vault itself seasoned the inventory. Warmth spread outward from my sternum. The numbness retreated to my fingertips, paused, reconsidered, and withdrew entirely over the course of ten minutes that I spent staring at the ceiling beams and breathing on purpose, each breath a scheduled appointment.

When I could sit up, Odile handed me watered wine and a slate.

"Not paper," she said. "Paper goes in files. Files get read." On the slate, in her cramped bookkeeper's hand, she wrote: *Antidote, broad-spectrum arsenic complex, one dose. Price:*

And below it, the figure.

I stared at it. Then I laughed, which hurt, which improved my mood considerably.

"That's not a price," I rasped. "That's an indenture."

"That's a price," Odile said, "for a dose I happened to have on hand because I have been expecting you to do something exactly this stupid since the day you signed away your immunities. Do you know what you did in that hall? You proved intent. You put a verified poisoning on record inside the palace walls, attached to the king's own daughter's service, and you did it while being *nobody* — no immunity to explain away, no tolerance for the assayists to discount. Whatever comes of it, it started today, with your throat. That's worth something. To certain parties, it's worth a great deal. And I am not in the business of giving away things worth a great deal, even to friends." She tapped the slate. "Especially to friends. Friends get fair prices. Strangers get robbed."

"Future labor," I read aloud. "Indefinite term. Rate to be assessed annually."

"You'll work it off. Tasting, assays, the odd retrieval. Your body's worth more to the Guild empty than full, ironically — a taster with no tolerances is a calibrated instrument. A taster with thirty years of calluses is just lucky." She took the slate back and, in smaller letters, added a line. I read it upside down.

*Debt secured against: Almeida, B. Collateral per prior agreement.*

My name. The one already pledged, sitting in the vault beside my bottled immunities. Now it stood surety for this too — a name mortgaged twice over, a person leveraged against her own rescue.

"If you default," Odile said quietly, "the Guild collects both. You understand that. There's no version of this where you walk away owing nothing."

"I understood that when I signed the first time."

"Understanding it and meaning it are different animals. I deal in the second animal." She capped her vials. "Sign."

I signed. The chalk squeaked. Somewhere in a ledger, in letters older than any kingdom or newer than last Tuesday, depending which ledger you mean, my future gained another lien.

Isolde watched all of it from the doorway with her arms folded, saying nothing, missing nothing. When Odile packed up, the princess stepped into the room properly and closed the door.

"The gravy," she said, "was meant for me."

"Yes."

"My father's service was clean. Every dish at his place setting was clean. Do you understand what that means?" Her voice stayed level, but her knuckles didn't. "Someone can poison anything in this palace except the king's table. Or *chooses* not to. Either way—" she stopped, recalibrated, and when she resumed, the words came slower, each one placed like a stone across a stream, "—either way, I've been keeping notes for six years, taster. Dates, dishes, servants reassigned, a chamberlain who visits the Guild quarter in disguise. I have a page of evidence and no one alive I can show it to. Until possibly today."

She looked at me, and I looked back, and between us sat the unspoken treaty: two watchers, each convinced she was the only sane mind in a haunted house, recognizing the other's handwriting.

"Not today," I said. "Soon. There's — there's someone you should meet first. She doesn't say much."

"I heard. A mute child living above a counting house." Isolde's eyes narrowed a fraction. "You'd be amazed what reaches me, even poisoned."

After they'd gone — Odile downstairs to settle accounts with herself, Isolde back to a dinner table she now knew was safe precisely because it was forbidden — I lay back on the trestle and let the ceiling hold still around me. My body felt scoured. Used. Honest, for the first time in thirty years: a thing that could break, and had nearly broken, and knew it.

I should go home. Sleep. Check on Wren, who would be pretending not to wait up, holding some poor object of mine hostage against my return — my sleeve, probably, my ritual, my little anchor.

Instead I lay there a moment longer, because the antechamber was quiet, and in the quiet I found myself thinking about the master ledger upstairs. About the page where Wren's arrival had written itself before I touched the pen. About First Speech, and Ninepence's warnings, and the word sleeping somewhere in all that ink.

If names could be withdrawn, I remembered Odile explaining once — if entries could be struck, amended, re-entered — then a ledger was never just a record. It was a mechanism. And mechanisms had keys.

Upstairs, above the counting house, a candle was surely guttering, and a silent girl was surely awake, and neither of us yet knew what happens when a key starts wondering what it opens.
