## 11. Wren Reads the Ledger

They left me alone with it on purpose, which is how I know they trusted me, and also how I know they were fools.

Bram had gone down into the counting house to argue with a man about the price of courage — actual bottled courage, which apparently costs more when you're buying it for someone else — and Odile Voss had gone wherever Odile Voss went when she wanted to be somewhere else, which was usually a chair near a window with a cup of something that smelled like burnt plums and secrets. Before she left she pointed one long finger at me, then at the master ledger on its lectern in the corner of the room, and said:

"Don't touch."

I didn't touch.

For almost an hour.

* * *

The room above the Guild counting house was Bram's room, and it smelled like her. I had learned to read smells the way other people learned letters, because words came hard and slow to me and smells never lied. Bram smelled like iron filings and cold tea and underneath all of it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, like swallowed deaths. Nine little deaths, Odile said once, when she thought I wasn't listening. Thirty years of them. The girl drinks poison so the king doesn't have to. Imagine it.

I imagined it constantly. It was better than imagining the tower.

The tower was what came back when I let my mind go quiet. Not the whole tower. Just pieces, the way a broken plate keeps its pattern even when you only find three shards. A window too high to reach. A fire that wasn't warm. A voice saying *carry it until someone worthy reads you*, and the feeling of something being set inside me the way you'd set a coal inside a paper lantern — carefully, so the lantern wouldn't burn, knowing someday it might anyway.

I didn't tell Bram about the voice. I told her about snow and being hungry and not remembering my mother's face, and those things were true, and telling true things buys more kindness than telling strange ones. I'd learned that too. Maybe before the tower. Maybe in it.

So there I was, alone in Bram's room, with the master ledger open on its lectern where Odile had been checking entries before her plum-smelling retreat, and the instruction *don't touch* hanging in the air like smoke.

Here is what nobody in the Guild seemed to understand about me: I could not read. Any of it. The alphabet everyone else used was a wall of small black birds, all facing the wrong way. Bram had tried teaching me letters on the frost of the window, drawing them with her fingernail, patient as stone, and I had watched her finger make its little shapes and felt the particular shame of a locked door in a house where you're allowed to live.

But the ledger wasn't made of ordinary letters. I'd known that since the night it opened itself for me on Bram's doorstep, before anyone touched a pen. The pages had turned toward me then the way sunflowers turn toward light, and I had felt — not seen, *felt*, the way you feel a door open in another room — something in the book notice me and decide to wait.

It had waited a long time. Books are patient. That's their whole nature.

* * *

I looked at it from across the room first. The master ledger was enormous, bigger than the cutting board in the kitchen downstairs, bound in leather gone dark as old blood, with brass corners worn round by two hundred years of hands. Its pages weren't paper exactly. They had the softness of skin that has never seen sun.

I crossed the room the way you cross ice. One heel at a time.

Up close, the writing was worse than ordinary writing, because it wasn't arranged in lines. The letters clustered. They huddled together like sheep in weather, some big, some small, some upside down or sideways or threaded through each other like roots under a road. Ordinary reading would have been impossible. But the shapes —

The shapes were familiar.

Not from books. From *before*. From the part of me that lived in the tower with the high window and the burning oath. My hands knew these shapes the way your hands know the shape of your own door latch in the dark. Somewhere under everything they'd taken from me, someone had traced these letters over and over while I watched, and the watching had sunk all the way down past memory into bone.

My fingers hovered over the page.

*Don't touch.*

But here's the thing about instructions: they're written in a language, too, and languages belong to whoever can speak them. And whatever language this book spoke, I was beginning to suspect it spoke it *to me*.

I touched the page.

* * *

Warmth first. Like touching a stone that's been in a cat's spot all afternoon. Then the letters moved.

I want to say it slowly because it happened slowly, the way a flock rearranges itself when a hawk passes over — no single bird deciding, all birds deciding, the shape changing without anything tearing. The clusters loosened. The sideways letters righted themselves. The ones threaded through each other slid apart like fingers unlaceing, and then, gently, terribly, they lined up.

Lines. Real lines, running left to right, and every letter on them stood facing forward, looking at me.

And I could read them.

Not the way Bram read, sounding things out, stumbling, correcting. The way you understand your own name being called. The words went in whole.

*Debt of Maren Ostreicher, entered winter of the old count:* said the top line, and below it, in letters that shimmered like heat off cobbles, *one borrowed year, drawn against spring, interest accrued in forgetting.* There was more — names I didn't know, terms I half understood, a whole world of owing and being owed pressed flat onto one page — but none of that mattered, because at the bottom of the entry, in smaller letters, was a word I knew.

A word from the tower.

It was only one syllable. It sat at the end of the debt-entry like a signature, except it wasn't a signature. Signatures say who wrote a thing. This word said what a thing *was*. It was the word for *paid*, or maybe for *closed*, or maybe for both at once, the way First Speech didn't seem to care much about the walls between meanings. In the tower, the voice had used that word to seal things. To finish them. To make a thing be over.

I don't remember deciding to say it. That's the honest truth and I'll swear it to anyone who asks. My mouth opened the way your mouth opens to catch snow, without any committee meeting about it, and I said the syllable out loud, soft, barely more than breath.

The candle guttered.

Not blew out — *guttered*, the flame flattening and flinching like a dog that's been kicked before, and the whole room leaned, just slightly, the way a ship leans. And on the page, the entry for Maren Ostreicher and her borrowed year simply — stopped. The ink faded from black to grey to the color of nothing, and the letters drifted apart again, back into their huddles, and where a debt had been there was now smooth blank page, pale as the inside of a shell.

Paid. Closed. Over.

Somewhere in the city below us, I understood without knowing how, a woman named Maren was going to wake up tomorrow and find that a year of her life fit her again. She wouldn't know why. Nobody would ever know why. The book would not say, and neither, I decided in that moment, with my heart hammering like a fist on a door, would I.

I had done a magic. A real one. Me, who couldn't read *dog* or *bread* or *Bram*.

I should have been frightened. I was, a little. But mostly — I'm going to tell the truth even though it makes me look bad — I was delighted.

* * *

I did it twice more before I caught myself.

The second time was an accident, if accidents can be curious. There was an entry near the middle of the page — the ledger kept turning itself to fresh pages for me, helpful as a dog bringing sticks — concerning a man who had traded his talent for whistling in exchange for a winter's worth of luck, and the closing word was the same syllable, and I said it quietly just to see if the candle would gutter again, and it did, and the man's debt vanished, and somewhere a whistler got his whistle back, and I laughed.

The laugh scared me more than the guttering had. Because it hadn't sounded like my laugh. It had sounded older. It had sounded, for half a heartbeat, like the voice from the tower — the one that said *carry it until someone worthy reads you* — laughing through me, pleased.

That was when I noticed the third thing.

At the very bottom of the newest page, in letters that hadn't been there a moment ago, fresh ink still shining wet, the ledger had begun writing something new. Not a debt. An entry about me.

*Item:* it wrote, in its slow deliberate hand, *the key has turned twice.*

Below that, after a pause long enough that I had time to stop breathing:

*The lock has noticed.*

I backed away from the lectern. Two steps, three. My legs hit the edge of Bram's bed and I sat down on it harder than I meant to, and the bedframe thumped, and downstairs I heard voices pause — Bram's low voice, the other man's whine — and resume. The candle steadied. The ledger lay open and innocent, its letters huddled back into their weather-proof clumps, just a big ugly book that nobody could read.

Except me. Except now.

*Carry it until someone worthy reads you.*

Was that what this was? Had I carried it all this way — through the tower, through the snow, through the door that opened before anyone knocked — only to discover that *worthy* meant able to cancel a whistler's debts? That felt wrong. That felt like fitting a key to the wrong door because the right door was too frightening to walk to.

Because I knew, in the place where the tower lived, what the real lock was. I'd always known, the way you know there's a cellar under a house even when the trapdoor's painted over. Something was wearing a crown in this city. Bram's face went careful and grey whenever the palace came up, careful in the specific way people's faces go around a wound. And the ledger — the ledger that recorded everything, every borrowed year and bottled courage and name that shouldn't exist — had never once, in all the evenings I'd spent nearby while Bram worked, mentioned the king.

You don't keep accounts on something you're afraid of.

I got up off the bed. I went back to the lectern, because apparently I was the kind of fool who walked toward the frightening thing, and I put my palm flat on the page, and I asked — out loud, in my own thin ordinary words, because I didn't yet trust myself with the other kind —

"Show me the crown."

The letters stirred. Huddled tighter. Some of them, at the edges of the page, began very quietly to shake.

And that was how Bram found me when she came up the stairs: standing over the master ledger with my hand on it and my shadow trembling on the wall, while beneath my palm, in letters of wet black ink, the book wrote out — slowly, reluctantly, like a witness being sworn against its will — a list of thirty years of payments made to the throne. Bottled courage. Borrowed years. Memories extracted and delivered to the palace in boxes lined with lead.

Every single one of them sealed shut with the same one-syllable word.

The word for *bound*.

Footsteps on the stairs. Bram's smell arriving before she did — iron, cold tea, swallowed deaths — and her voice saying my name in the tone people use when they've come running.

"Wren. Step away from that book."

I didn't step away. I looked up at her, and I said the only thing that mattered, in my own language, the plain one, the one with walls between the words:

"Bram. I can read it."
