## 12. The Princess Who Suspects

I keep my evidence in a music box.

Not a real one—my mother's actual music box holds actual music and I would never be so obvious as to hide treason under a dancing ballerina. No, this is a different sort of box entirely: a lacquered thing I bought from a Guild peddler when I was nineteen, enchanted so that whatever is placed inside becomes slightly less true the longer it stays. It was sold to me as a memory-preserver. It does not preserve memories. It preserves *facts*, which is a subtler and more dangerous trade, because facts cannot weep or flatter or lie their way out of a drawer.

Inside the box: eleven years of my father's wrongness, catalogued in my own cramped hand on forty-three slips of paper.

Slip one, dated the winter I was nine: *The king answered the ambassador's question before the ambassador finished asking it. Not guessed. Knew.*

Slip seventeen: *Father stood in the garden for four hours without moving. The gardener says he did not blink. When I spoke to him he smiled three seconds too late, like an echo arriving after its shout.*

Slip thirty-one, my favorite, the one I return to the way other people return to a childhood home: *He wept at Mother's funeral. Perfect tears, correct number, correct duration. But his shadow on the chapel wall kept reaching for the coffin while he stood with his hands folded.*

You may wonder why a princess who has noticed all this simply did not—what is the courtly phrase—*raise her concerns*. I did raise them. Twice, at eighteen and twenty. The first time, my father listened with warm and terrible patience, agreed with everything I said, and by morning the chambermaid who had brought me my breakfast for six years had been reassigned to a coastal province I have never been able to find on any map. The second time, I raised my concerns to the Lord Chancellor, and my concerns were received so gently that I understood, in the way one understands falling water is deep, that I had just shown him my throat.

So I stopped raising concerns and started keeping accounts. Which, as it turns out, is the family business of exactly one other person in this palace, though she doesn't know yet that I know.

* * *

The food-taster's name is Bram Almeida. She has held the post for thirty years, which is itself remarkable—the mortality rate for tasters being what it is, most careers in that profession are measured in seasons, like mayflies or bad jokes. Almeida has survived since before my father's coronation. There are servants who remember her young; there is no portrait of her young anywhere, which I have checked, because I check everything now. Checking everything is what I do instead of sleeping.

I began watching her two years ago, after slip twenty-nine (*the king's wine was changed to a red at the last moment; Almeida tasted it anyway and did not die, though the vintner was found drowned in a barrel of his own product within the week*). A woman who can drink poison meant for kings and walk away is either blessed, monstrous, or very well supplied. And a woman well supplied must be supplied by someone, and in this city *someone* always means the Guild of Impossible Things.

Tracing the purchases took me fourteen months, and I will spare you the tedium except to say that bribing archivists is easier than people claim, provided you bribe them in things they actually want. One wanted a title he could put on letters. Two wanted to be remembered fondly by history, which cost me nothing but the promise, and I intend to honor it, because I keep accounts of my own debts too.

What I found, buried in the Guild's transaction rolls like bones in a riverbed:

Almeida buys. Constantly. Small things, strange things—a vial of borrowed steadiness here, an hour of someone else's calm there, once (and this entry made me sit back in my chair) *a jar of last words, partial contents remaining*. She pays in labor, in ledgers, in years of service rendered to the counting house above which she lives. She is not enriching herself. She is *balancing*, the way a tightrope-walker balances, constantly, against something none of the rest of us can see.

And then, eight weeks ago, the pattern changed.

New entries, coded but decodable: safe passage purchased. Informants paid off. A warding charm sized for a child. And—this was the entry that made me take out my music box and add slip forty-four—*extraction refused, twice, at premium offered*. Someone had tried to buy something out of the Guild's custody involving a child, and the Guild had refused, and the Guild does not refuse. My archivists were unanimous on this point. The Guild serves whoever pays. That is its entire theology. Yet someone had asked after a child with a mute tongue and snow in her hair, and the counting house had closed its ledger like a fist.

I knew then that the girl existed. I did not know who she was. I am still not certain, but I have a theory, and my theories about my father have a distressing habit of being worse than the truth, and the truth is already bad enough to fill forty-four slips.

* * *

I chose the library for the confrontation because libraries are neutral ground in a palace—they belong to no faction, only to dead authors, and even my father behaves differently among books, as if some residue of the man who might have read them lingers in the shelves.

I also chose it because Bram Almeida crosses the west gallery every Thursday at the fourth bell, on her way to deliver the evening tasting reports, and has done so, I now knew, for eleven unbroken years. If there is one thing I have learned from mapping wrongness, it is that routine is where the truth lives. Wrongness hides in surprises. Truth keeps a schedule.

She came through the doors at the fourth bell precisely, and I will grant her this: she did not startle. Most people, finding a princess standing alone in the stacks beside the genealogies, perform some small pantomime of shock. Almeida simply looked at me the way a merchant looks at a customer who has entered the shop but not yet stated her business, and waited.

"Your Highness," she said. Her voice was dry and unhurried. "Are you lost? The romances are two floors down."

"I don't read romances," I said. "They end happily. I find it implausible."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a joke had occurred and been received. "Then what does Your Highness read?"

"Ledgers."

That landed. I watched it land. Her stillness changed quality, the way water changes when it remembers it can freeze. She glanced toward the door, gauging distances—I have spent enough years around soldiers and servants to read that arithmetic—and found, I'm sure, what I had arranged: nothing. No guards. No witnesses. Just two women and several thousand dead authors.

"You've been watching me," she said.

"For fourteen months. You've been watching my father for thirty years." I let that sit. "Though I suspect your watching began as employment and became something else. Mine began as love and became something else. We may be mirror images, Mistress Almeida. I wanted to see which of us flinches first."

"And neither of us did," she said. "Which tells you we're both telling the truth, or both very good liars, and I've tasted enough lies to know the difference." She stepped closer, lowering her voice to something that lived beneath the room. "Say what you've come to say, Princess. The evening report won't wait forever, and neither, in my experience, will you."

So I took out slip forty-four.

I had copied it, of course. The original lives in the music box with its siblings, growing no less true. I handed her the paper and watched her read the decoded Guild entries—safe passage, informants bought, extraction refused—and I watched her face do something I had not seen it do in any of my months of distant observation: it *feared*, openly, briefly, and then mastered itself with the practiced economy of someone who has swallowed worse feelings than fear and lived.

"Where did you get this?" she asked quietly.

"Guild transaction rolls. Archived copies. I have an arrangement with two clerks and a man who wanted a title." I paused. "Someone is shopping for the girl who lives above your counting house. Someone with palace money. And the Guild, which refuses no one, refused them. I want to know why."

For a long moment she studied the slip as if the ink might rearrange into better news. Then she did something I had not planned for and have come to value more than I can easily say: she told me the truth.

"Because the Guild refuses nothing," she said, "except when refusing is cheaper than the alternative. And the alternative to refusing whoever wants Wren—" she said the name carefully, like setting down something breakable, "—is a bill no counting house can pay. I've seen the ledgers, Your Highness. I keep them. I know what's in them. Including," and here she looked at me with an expression I recognized from my own mirror, the flat look of a person deciding how much of the abyss to show a stranger, "including entries concerning your father."

"My father," I said, "answers questions half a second before they're asked."

"His shadow moves wrong."

"His grief at my mother's funeral was flawless."

"Flawless," she repeated softly. "Yes. That's the word, isn't it. Grief should have seams."

We stood there among the genealogies of families mostly extinct, two watchers comparing maps, and I felt the peculiar vertigo of recognition—the discovery that the loneliness you thought was uniquely yours has been inhabited all along by someone else, standing at a different window, watching the same wrongness move through the same halls.

"Eleven years," I said. "Forty-four slips of paper in a box that makes facts truer. I have no allies, no proof that survives a court audience, and a father who reassigns people to provinces that don't exist. And now there's a child involved, and someone in this palace with deep pockets hunting her, and I find the two problems are almost certainly the same problem." I drew a breath. "I am proposing an alliance, Mistress Almeida. Unevenly matched, poorly armed, doomed on current trajectory. But I'm told those are the only kind worth having."

* * *

She didn't answer immediately. Instead she walked to the window, looked down at the courtyard where the guard was changing, and asked me a question I have thought about every day since.

"Why now? You've suspected for eleven years. Why pick up a weapon today?"

And because she had just shown me her fear, honestly, I gave her my honesty in return.

"Because of slip twelve," I said. "The earliest one I almost discarded. When I was ten, my father held me up to a balcony rail to watch a procession—he used to do that, before, he *used* to—and I looked down at his face below mine and there was nothing looking back. Not cruelty. Not coldness. Nothing. Like looking into a well and seeing the sky at the bottom instead of water. I convinced myself for thirty years of slips that I'd imagined it, that fathers are simply far away. But then the Guild records showed you buying wardings for a child, and I thought: someone found something worth protecting from whatever is in my father. And I thought: perhaps if I help protect it, I'll finally learn whether the thing that held me over that rail was my father at all." My voice did something I disliked, thickened slightly, and I pressed on before it could embarrass me further. "I want to know if I've been mourning him for twenty years or if there's something left to save. Either answer would be a mercy. The not-knowing is the poison, and unlike you, I have no immunity."

Almeida turned from the window. Whatever she saw in my face seemed to settle some private accounting of hers, because she nodded once, the nod of a bookkeeper closing a column.

"Then here's what you need to know, and understand I'm breaking oaths to tell you half of it." She spoke low and fast, a woman emptying a purse. "The girl is called Wren. She speaks a language older than any kingdom—First Speech, the tongue oaths were originally sworn in. What sits on your father's head is bound, Princess, and bindings are sworn in First Speech, and what can be sworn can be unsaid. Your father may or may not still exist inside whatever wears him. The Guild knows more. The Guild's oldest member knows everything, and he is currently a mummified broker named Ninepence who costs a fortune per conversation and is worth every coin." She paused. "And the person shopping for Wren has palace resources. Which means either your father's household acts on its own knowledge—or the thing on the throne already knows the key exists and is coming for her."

"Then she isn't safe above a counting house."

"No," Almeida agreed. "She isn't."

"The palace has a hundred thousand eyes. The Guild quarter has fewer, and they're for sale, which means they're for *our* purchase as easily as anyone else's—but saleable loyalty cuts both ways, and you know it, because you've spent years paying that toll." I took the slip back from her hand. "Bring her inside. Not to the apartments—those are watched—but the old nursery wing in the east range. Sealed since my mother died. The guards think it's haunted, which is the finest security money can't buy. Only I know the passages, and only my mother's ghost, if she's taking visitors."

"That's assuming I trust you," Almeida said. But her voice had already softened by a degree, the way voices do when the argument has left them and only the decision remains. "Princess, I deal in debts for a living. Everything has a price, everyone is selling, and I have watched better people than either of us trade away the parts of themselves that mattered. So understand my question isn't whether you're sincere. Sincerity is cheap. My question is: what will you *pay*?"

I considered lying, briefly, the way one considers a comfortable chair one cannot afford.

"Everything, probably," I said. "If it comes to it. I've been keeping accounts of my father's wrongness for eleven years, and the account has one conclusion: whatever sits on that throne unmade a kingdom slowly enough that nobody noticed. If helping you unmake it costs me my name, my place, my inheritance—" I shrugged, and hated that the shrug trembled. "My mother taught me that some debts are honors. I intend to find out if she was right."

Almeida looked at me for a long moment. Then, quietly, with the gravity of a woman signing her own name to something: "There's a Guild saying. *The ledger always collects.* Remember you volunteered."

"I will," I said. "It's going in the box."

She almost laughed. It was, I later learned, the closest thing to laughter she'd produced in weeks, and I counted it a diplomatic triumph greater than anything my ancestors won with armies.

* * *

We parted in the stacks with arrangements made and no handshake, handshakes being evidence. She went west to her evening reports, walking the gallery at the pace of eleven unbroken years, and I stood among the dead genealogies and added slip forty-five to my collection before the facts could cool:

*The taster knows. The child is the key. My father—if he is my father—is running out of time, and so are we.*

I sealed the box. I was turning to go when I heard it, faint through the library's high windows: bells. Not the fourth bell, which had already rung, nor the fifth, which was due. This was the alarm from the Guild quarter across the river—thin and urgent, the sound of a counting house discovering what has been taken from it.

Later I would learn what had burned that night, and who, and what the fire was meant to cover. Later I would stand in the wreckage of our careful plans and understand that while Almeida and I compared maps in the safety of the stacks, the enemy had already stopped reading ours and started tearing pages out.

But that evening I knew only this: somewhere across the river, in a building full of impossible things, something impossible had begun.

And I had the sudden, certain feeling—forty-five slips of intuition whispering in one lacquered box—that whatever it was, Bram Almeida was going to have to pay for it with pieces of herself I couldn't yet name.

I picked up my skirts and ran for the east passage.
