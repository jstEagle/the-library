## 13. A Name Withdrawn from Circulation

There is a particular smell to the Guild counting house at dawn, and it is not the smell of money. Money smells of nothing; that is its whole charm. No—the counting house at dawn smells of ink and tallow and the faint copper tang of debts that have been left too long in their ledgers, like meat forgotten in a cellar. I have spent enough mornings there to know it the way other people know their mothers' kitchens.

That morning I knew something else too. I knew I was about to do the stupidest thing of my life, and I was going to do it neatly, in triplicate, because that is how the Guild does everything.

Wren was asleep upstairs, curled in the window seat of my rooms with her back against the glass like a cat who had decided the cold outside could be trusted as long as she kept an eye on it. She slept the way children sleep when they've learned not to trust sleep—lightly, one hand curled near her mouth, ready to catch a word before it escaped. Lately her words had been escaping more often. Little syllables of First Speech dropped into ordinary rooms like stones into still water. A candle had guttered on Tuesday. On Thursday, the lock on Odile's storeroom had simply given up being locked, out of what I can only describe as professional embarrassment.

And yesterday, a clerk from the palace chancery had come to the Guild's front counter asking, very politely, after "a child of approximately eight years, recently entered into your records."

The Guild does not answer questions from the crown. The Guild sells answers to whoever pays. That is the difference, and it is a fine one, and fine lines are exactly the sort of thing that gets people killed.

So: the stupidest thing of my life, done neatly.

* * *

Odile Voss received me in her office, which was less an office than a museum of things that should not exist. A jar of last words sat on the shelf behind her, murmuring to itself. A drawer of borrowed courage rattled faintly whenever someone in the city below lost their nerve. Odile herself sat behind a desk older than the kingdom, small and sharp and gray as a filing knife, and she looked at me over her spectacles with the expression of a woman who has already read the last page of this conversation.

"You want to withdraw your name," she said.

"I want to withdraw Wren from circulation. Her entry, her description, the record of her arrival. Every page that says she exists." I set my hands flat on the desk, which is a trick I learned at court—flat hands look honest even when they're lying. "You told me yourself the Guild serves whoever pays. So I'm paying."

"With what?" Odile asked, though her eyes had already gone to the ledger between us, and I understood that she knew. Of course she knew. Odile always knows; it is her single most annoying quality, and she has many.

"My name," I said. "As collateral."

The jar of last words went quiet. Even the courage stopped rattling. In the counting house, silence like that means the building itself is listening, and the building has heard everything, so for it to pause—

"Bram." Odile took off her spectacles. This was bad. Odile without her spectacles is Odile preparing to be sincere, and sincerity from Odile Voss is like arsenic from me: administered rarely, in precise doses, and never without reason. "Sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"Sit down properly. You've gone taster-still. The stillness you do before you swallow something that might kill you." She pushed a chair out with her foot. "I want you to hear the terms before you sign anything, because once you sign, I cannot soften them. Not won't. Cannot. The ledger doesn't negotiate."

I sat. Properly.

"A name," she said, "is not a word. I know that sounds like broker's mysticism, so let me put it plainly. Your name is the handle by which every memory of you is filed. Every friend, every enemy, every debt, every kindness—it's all indexed under Bram Almeida. Withdraw the name, and the index burns. People don't forget you gradually, the way they forget a face across a market square. They lose you the way you lose a page torn out of a book. There's just a gap, and gaps make people uneasy, and uneasy people smooth gaps over. They invent reasons for the empty chair. They decide the room was always that shape."

"And if I fulfill the contract," I said, "the name comes back."

"If you fulfill the contract, the name is re-entered, and the world remembers you all at once, which is its own kind of violence, but a survivable one." She laced her fingers together. "If you fail—if the contract lapses, if you die before terms are met, if you default in any particular—the withdrawal becomes permanent. Unwritten. And Bram, listen to me, because this is the part brokers don't tell clients until after signing: unwritten doesn't mean dead. Dead would be kinder. Dead leaves a body, a grave, a grief. Unwritten leaves nothing. Your mother—" she caught herself, because my mother was already gone in the ordinary way, and moved on smoothly, "—everyone who ever knew you will carry a hole shaped like you and no idea what made it. You'll walk through the city invisible not to the eye but to the heart. Beggars won't remember your coin. Doors won't remember your knock."

"The Guild always collects," I said. It's practically the motto. It's stitched somewhere, probably, on something.

"The Guild always collects," she agreed. "I'd rather it collected from anyone else on earth."

"That's the closest thing to affection you've ever shown me. I'm touched."

"Don't be clever at me. Cleverness is how people talk themselves into vaults." She pulled the master ledger toward her, and I watched her hands hover over it, and for the first time in the twenty years I'd known her, Odile Voss looked old. Not gray—she'd been gray since before I was born—but old, the way Ninepence is old, the way stone is old, worn down by the same weather year after year. "Tell me why. Not the bookkeeping reason. I can read the bookkeeping reason; some chamberlain came sniffing after the girl and you want her pages gone. Tell me the real one."

I thought about lying to her. I am good at lying; it is most of my job. But Odile had taught me half of what I know about survival, and the other half I learned from poisons, and both teachers agree on one principle: never lie to someone holding the antidote.

"She has nobody," I said. "She landed on my step out of the snow speaking a language that predates the kingdom, carrying whatever terrible thing she carries inside her, and everyone who might protect her wants to use her, and everyone who might use her wants to own her, and I—" My voice did something embarrassing. I steadied it. "I have been swallowed by institutions my whole life. The palace swallowed me. The Guild swallowed me. I know what it is to be a useful object in someone else's ledger. I will not watch it happen to her. Not while I have anything left to trade."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere in the jar, a last word sighed.

"You understand," she said finally, "that this is love. What you just described. I want you to say it plainly, because the ledger needs to know what it's pricing. Contracts written around unnamed feelings go wrong in interesting ways."

"It's love," I said. It tasted strange in my mouth, like a poison I hadn't trained for. "Fine. It's love. Write it down."

"Oh, I intend to." And there she was again, my Odile, dry as dust and twice as enduring, dipping her pen. "Love prices high. Sit still."

* * *

The contract took an hour to draft, because the Guild does nothing casually that it intends to do permanently.

I read every line. Twenty years of balancing impossible accounts has made me fluent in the Guild's grammar, which is designed to sound like plain speech and behave like a snare. Clause by clause, Odile walked me through it, and clause by clause I made her say the ugly parts aloud.

*The party of the first part, hereinafter the Nameholder, withdraws from circulation the appellation "Bram Almeida," together with all diminutives, aliases, and recorded associations thereof...*

"All recorded associations," I said. "Does that include my work? The tasting?"

"No. Your office at court is a function, not a name. Functions survive. The king's household will still employ a food-taster; they'll simply find themselves unable to recall who held the post or why they trusted her." Odile's pen didn't pause. "Which, incidentally, may keep you alive. Hard to order the death of a woman whose name won't stay in your head long enough to write the warrant."

"There's a comfort."

"It's the only comfort on offer. Take it or leave it."

Clause seven covered the erasure of Wren's entries: her arrival recorded in the self-opening ledger, her description, the notation of First Speech, the incident reports—there were three now, each more alarming than the last—all of it to be struck and struck thoroughly, such that no buyer, however well-connected, could purchase knowledge of her existence from these offices.

Clause nine was the one I made her read three times.

*Should the Nameholder fail to satisfy the terms herein within the stated period, the withdrawn name shall be forfeit absolutely, and the Nameholder shall be deemed unwritten, without recourse, appeal, or remainder.*

"Without remainder," I said. "Meaning what, precisely?"

"Meaning no scrap survives. Most forfeitures leave something—a signature, a scar, a habit of handwriting. A remainder is how the clever ones claw their way back. The Guild learned that lesson." She met my eyes over the spectacles. "No remainders anymore, Bram. If you fail, there will be nothing of you to find. Not a journal entry. Not a recipe. Not a memory in anyone's head. The world will simply have always been a world where you never were."

"And the term of the contract?"

"Ninety days. Sufficient time to see the girl settled beyond reach—or to finish whatever it is you're actually planning, which you haven't told me, and which I am choosing, against every instinct of my profession, not to ask about."

I said nothing. Ninety days was generous. Ninety days was also, I suspected, Odile's way of telling me she knew there was more coming—a throne room, a word older than kingdoms, a thing wearing the king's face—and was giving me rope in the hope I'd use it to climb rather than hang.

"Sign," she said.

I signed. My hand didn't shake. Twenty years of lifting poisoned cups to my lips will steady a person's hand in ways that are difficult to explain to normal people.

The moment the ink dried, the ledger did what the ledger does. There was no flash, no thunder. There was only a sensation like a page turning somewhere behind my ribs, and then a faint, terrible lightness, as though a word I'd been carrying all my life had been lifted out of my mouth and set aside in a drawer.

"Bram Almeida," Odile said, testing it.

"Yes?"

Her brow furrowed. Just slightly. Just for a heartbeat—the furrow of a woman reaching for a file and finding the drawer empty—and then she mastered it, because she is Odile, and she said, "Nothing. Forget it. Go home. Sleep. And eat something that isn't poisoned for once; you've earned it."

I walked out through the counting house, past the vials and the jars and the drawers of hair, and every clerk who saw me nodded and greeted me by title—*taster*, *keeper of the back ledgers*, *the woman from upstairs*—and none of them used my name, and I realized with a lurch that they couldn't. It was already working. The name was already gone from circulation, and I was still standing there, walking and breathing and being, just... unfiled.

It should have felt like freedom. It felt like being a cup of wine on a table, waiting to find out what was in it.

* * *

Upstairs, Wren was awake, watching the snow with the fierce attention she gave everything.

"You smell different," she said. She speaks Common now, mostly, in a voice like a bird testing winter branches. She saves First Speech for emergencies, which we are all grateful for.

"Do I?"

"Mm." She studied me with those too-old eyes. "Like a room after the furniture's been moved."

Trust a child to say in nine words what a broker needed an hour to say. I sat down across from her and put two cups of chocolate on the table—I taste everything before I eat it, always, out of habit, and today the habit found nothing to fear, which felt strangely like a loss—and slid one toward her.

"Wren. I need to tell you what I did this morning."

She wrapped both hands around the cup and waited. She is very good at waiting. Children who arrive out of snowstorms speaking dead languages learn patience the way other children learn skipping.

"I took your pages out of the Guild's books. The records of you. Nobody can buy your name now, or your description, or the fact that you exist. The men looking for you won't find you in any ledger in this city." I turned my cup slowly. "To do it, I had to give them something in exchange. I gave them my name. If everything goes well, I get it back. If it doesn't..." I shrugged, aiming for wry and hearing, instead, something raw. "If it doesn't, everyone forgets me. Completely. As if I'd never been."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she said, "But I'll remember."

"No, little one. That's rather the point. Nobody remembers. Not even—"

"I'll remember." She said it the way she said the few First Speech words she dared: absolutely, with the weight of something older than kingdoms underneath. "You smell of swallowed deaths. I said so the first night. You carry them, all the things that should have killed you, and they didn't, and they live in you quiet. Names are the same kind of thing." She drank her chocolate, leaving a mustache, ruining the gravitas beautifully. "Things that are carried can be carried back."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue, and found I had nothing—no precedent, no clause, no poison analogy. An eight-year-old had just out-argued the Guild's own fine print, and she'd done it with cocoa on her face.

"Also," she added, setting down the cup, "you should know the ledger downstairs is doing something."

"What kind of something?"

"The pages with my name on them. Before Odile struck them out." Wren's eyes went distant, the way they did when the old language stirred in her. "They didn't want to be erased. They wrote themselves one more time first. Small, in the corner, where clerks don't look." She looked at me. "They wrote: *the key knows the keeper.*"

The fire popped. Downstairs, faintly, I heard the counting house settle on its foundations like an old dog circling before sleep.

"Well," I said, when I could speak steadily. "That's either very comforting or extremely ominous."

Wren shrugged, eight years old again, and reached for the chocolate pot. "Both," she said. "Most true things are."

Outside, the snow kept falling, and somewhere across the city a chamberlain was reporting to a shadow that moved wrong, and ninety days had begun to run. I drank my chocolate, tasting nothing and trusting it anyway, and tried not to think about how quiet my own name sounded, already, in the mouths of everyone who loved me.

Tried, and failed, and got up the next morning anyway. That, too, is a kind of survival. Perhaps the only kind that matters.
