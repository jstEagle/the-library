## 14. The Hunt Begins

### I. Bram

The bells woke me, and they were the wrong bells.

Every city has a grammar of sound. You learn it the way you learn a language — slowly, then all at once, then forever. Vellagard's morning bells speak in thirds: market at seven, court at eight, the mourning bell whenever grief requires. The midnight bell is a single low note, a period at the end of the day's sentence.

What rang now was none of these. It was every bell at once, struck without rhythm, the way a man strikes them when he has been told to make noise and not to stop until told otherwise.

I was out of bed with my boots half-laced before my mind caught up to my hands. Old habit. A taster learns to move first and think second, because poison does not wait for deliberation. Across the narrow room, Wren sat up in her cot, eyes enormous in the dark, and did not ask what was happening. She never asked. Eight years old and she already understood that some questions are just fear wearing a polite coat.

"Odile," I said, which answered everything.

Down the stairs, through the counting house's false front — a respectable dealer in imported sugars, because the Guild of Impossible Things has always understood that nothing hides better than something boring — and into the back office where Odile Voss stood at the window with a candle she had not lit, watching the street by the light of other people's fires.

Three of those fires were burning in the Guild quarter. I could smell the smoke even through glass: paper, mostly, and something underneath it that made my professional tongue curl. Vellum treated with preservative salts. Ledger-leather. The Guild's own stock, burning its way toward the sky like prayers nobody wanted answered.

"They came for Ninepence first," Odile said. Her voice was flat as an abacus. "An hour ago. They knew exactly which door."

"How?"

"Because someone sold us, Bram. Someone always sells us. The only variable is the price." She turned from the window, and in the firelight her face looked older than it had at supper — older than it had looked in years, if I'm honest, though Odile collects age the way some women collect hats and wears each decade when it suits her. "Isolde's evidence trail led back to us. We always knew it might."

"We thought we had days."

"We thought wrong. Get Wren. Not your papers, not your coin, not your mother's ring if you have one. You can buy all three again. Children are not for sale twice." Something moved behind her eyes when she said it, quick as a fish under ice, and I filed it away the way I file everything — for later, for the ledger, for the hour when filing becomes understanding.

I went back up. Wren was already dressed. She stood in the middle of the room holding the one thing she owned that mattered: a scrap of vellum on which First Speech letters crawled like patient insects. Her unbinding word, or part of it. Or all of it. Even Ninepence hadn't been sure.

"Give it to me," I said, holding out my hand. "If they take you, they shouldn't find it on you."

She looked at my hand. Then at me. And she did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any poison ever had: she folded the vellum with great care, tucked it into the lining of her boot, and shook her head.

"No," she said. It was one of perhaps forty words she'd given me since the night she arrived in the snow, and she spent them like a miser spends gold. Her meaning needed no translation. *You gave things up for me. This is mine to carry.*

I could have argued. I have argued with kings' chamberlains and won. But there is a kind of argument you lose the moment you begin it, and this was that kind.

"Stay behind me on the stairs," I said instead. "If anyone grabs you, bite. Don't threaten, don't scream — bite, hard, and run toward smoke. People flee from fire. Nobody follows a child into it."

Her mouth did the smallest possible curve. Approval, or amusement, or both. Then she took my hand, and we went down into the burning night together.

### II. Odile

The Guild has survived eleven monarchs, two interregnums, one papal schism, and the entire reign of Duke Maravel, who once ordered his own portrait arrested for treason. In three hundred years, no crown agent had breached the counting house.

They breached it in four minutes.

Odile watched from the alley across the way, Bram and the child pressed into the shadow of a rain barrel beside her, and made herself watch because watching was a discipline and discipline was all she had left that wasn't guilt.

The agents came in through the sugar shop front, twelve of them in plain dark coats with the royal cipher hidden under their collars — deniable men, the kind the palace used when it wanted work done that the palace would later deny wanting done. Their captain carried a writ. Of course he did. Odile had written enough writs in her time, on behalf of enough clients, to know one at a glance: broad powers, vague limits, a signature that would be repudiated within the year. The crown's oldest product. Deniability, bottled and sealed.

They went through the front office fast and professional. Through the archive faster and less professionally — she heard shelving come down, heard the wet crack of a storage jar meeting stone, and winced. Somewhere in that crash went forty years of borrowed courage, a widow's last conversation with her son, and a vial containing the final six hours of a man who had traded them away rather than live through them. Irreplaceable. Unspeakable. Inventory.

And then they reached the vault door, and stopped, and Odile allowed herself the evening's single smile.

The vault was warded. The wards were keyed to blood — Guild blood, specifically hers, specifically Ninepence's, and one other key she would burn before she let it be used. The captain shouted orders. His men found crowbars. Odile counted under her breath, an old habit from her years pricing miracles: *one, two, three* — the crowbars splintered — *seven, eight* — a man began screaming about his hands, which felt cold and dead and would feel so for a week — *fifteen*, and the captain stepped back, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and said the words she had been waiting for:

"Burn the shelves. Take everything portable. The old one — bring me the old one alive."

Ah, Odile thought. There it is.

Ninepence had been in the vault. Of course he had. He slept there most nights now, curled among the oldest contracts like a moth that had decided the tapestry was home. Three hundred years old, give or take a century, mummified past the point where death was an event rather than an opinion. He had heard the child speak First Speech and had gotten up from his shelf for the first time in a generation, and he had known — had *said*, in that voice like dry pages turning — that the crown would come for what the child carried.

"The Hollow King fears the word," Ninepence had told them. "It has spent thirty years making sure no one alive remembers how oaths were sworn. And now a child walks into my office carrying the alphabet of its prison in her mouth." He had laughed, then, a sound like a ledger closing. "Oh, I have waited a long time to be on the interesting side of history."

Now the captain's men were pulling open the vault, and Odile gripped Bram's wrist to keep her still, and watched.

They found him. Of course they found him; he had not tried to hide, had probably arranged himself decoratively among the contracts, because Ninepence had never met a dramatic exit he didn't like. Two agents dragged him out into the counting house floor, and even at this distance Odile could see them falter — see the animal hesitation of living men handling something that should not be animate. He weighed nothing. That was always the worst part, the first-timers said. He weighed nothing at all, like carrying a bundle of letters.

The captain crouched. Asked his questions. Odile couldn't hear them over the fires, but she knew their shape: *Who bought the child's protection? Where is the binding contract? What does she say?*

And Ninepence — she saw his head tilt, that birdlike tilt, three hundred years of commerce gathering itself behind his sunken eyes — and then she saw the light.

Not torchlight. Him. From the inside.

Later, the agents would tell it different ways, because horror fragments when you try to hand it around. Some said he simply ignited. Some swore the flames came *out of his mouth*, in shapes, in syllables. One man, the one whose hands had gone cold, resigned from service within the month and took up beekeeping and never spoke of it again, except once, drunk, to a stranger: *he was reciting*. Burning from the boots up, from the wrappings inward, and reciting — and every agent in that room heard the words whether they knew the language or not, heard them land in their chests like nails driven home:

First Speech. The last of it spoken aloud in Vellagard, spoken by a mummified broker choosing the manner of his own accounting, and the meaning — Odile knew enough to know the meaning — was simple, and old, and terrible:

*I was paid in full. No debt remains. Come and collect nothing.*

When the flames guttered out, there was ash in the shape of a seated figure, and a single coin lying in the ash, face-up, minted under a king thirty years dead.

Ninepence's price. Paid forward. To whoever came next.

Beside her, Bram made a sound. Small. Odile did not look at her, because looking would have undone them both, and there was no time to be undone.

"Now," she said. "While they're standing in their own awe. Now, now, now."

### III. Bram

We went down.

That is the whole art of fleeing Vellagard, as far as I've ever learned it: the city is a set of stairs, and everyone above you believes the danger comes from the gates and the walls. Down is where the city keeps what it doesn't want to look at — the drains, the foundations, the bones of older cities stacked beneath it like failed drafts. Odile led us through a sugar cellar, through a wall that was mostly suggestion, into a tunnel that smelled of cold water and old decisions.

Wren's hand in mine was small and dry and steady. Mine was not. I noticed that about myself the way I notice flavors — clinically, helplessly, with a taster's precision. My palm had gone slick. Thirty years of swallowing deaths had left my body a fortress, and here was the joke, the punchline I'd handed over myself: the fortress was sold. I was walking into flight and hunt and whatever came after as an ordinary woman with an ordinary woman's ordinary, mortal skin.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said ahead of me, without turning. "Where you narrate your own obituary."

"I don't do that."

"You do it constantly. It's why your ledgers balance — you treat everything, including yourself, as an entry to be reconciled." A torch flared; she'd lit it off a striker she kept, apparently, for occasions exactly like this. "Bram. Listen to me once without the ledger. Whatever happens tonight, whatever it costs — the child trusts you. That is worth more than immunity. I know you don't believe me. Believe me anyway. I've priced both."

The tunnel opened into the undercity, and I will confess what I felt, because this account deserves honesty even if I rarely practice it: I felt small. Above us, Vellagard burned its way through the Guild quarter with the thorough, joyless efficiency of official violence. Around us, the undercity breathed. Lamps in alcoves. Stairs going everywhere and nowhere. And people — that was the part that got under my skin — people everywhere, in doorways and lean-tos and niches carved into the foundations, living down here the way moss lives on a north wall. Quietly. Persistently. On time that wasn't theirs.

Debtors. The Guild's long-term accounts. People who had sold years of their lives and, unable to afford anywhere in the sunlit city, had come below to spend what remained among others doing the same.

A woman stirred as we passed, ancient at fifty, her face mapped with the particular exhaustion of someone paying interest hourly. She looked at Wren. Looked at me. And she said, in a voice sanded down to the grain:

"She's the one they're ringing the bells for."

Odile stopped. Turned. And did something I had never seen her do in nine years of working for her: she told the truth to a stranger.

"Yes. And if any of you sell her, I will personally ensure your accounts are settled in full — immediately. Do you understand what I'm saying? Every year you sold, returned at once. All of it. Tonight."

The woman's laugh was a ruin of a laugh. "Threatening us with our own youth. You're crueler than the crown, Voss." But she was already moving, calling softly into the dark in the shorthand of the undercity, and doors closed along the passage like eyelids, and lamps died, and we were wrapped and hidden by a hundred strangers who owed the Guild everything and chose, for one night, to pay in silence.

"Why?" I asked Odile. "Why would they—"

"Because down here, Bram, everybody knows what it is to be hunted by a buyer." She kept walking. "Hold the child's hand. Watch the step."

### IV. Wren

The dark under the city was full of breathing.

Wren counted the breaths the way she counted everything, because counting was a rope she could hold when the other feelings came — the tower-feeling, the burning-feeling, the voice that lived under her memory like a stone under moss: *carry it until someone worthy reads you.*

Bram's hand was warm. That was the strange thing about Bram. She smelled of swallowed deaths — Wren could taste them on the air around her, bitter layers of them, arsenic and worse, a whole library of poisons read and survived — but her hand was warm, and her heartbeat, when Wren leaned against her side, was the steadiest sound in any room.

Except now it wasn't steady. Now it stumbled sometimes, quick-quick-slow, and Wren understood why without being told. Bram had sold her armor. Wren had felt it happen the way you feel weather change — the day Bram signed that page, the air around her had gone thinner, plainer, like a room with the tapestries taken down. All those beautiful deaths she had eaten were somebody else's now, sitting in jars on a shelf, waiting to be poured into some other brave or desperate mouth.

So Bram was soft now, the way ordinary people were soft. Breakable. And she walked in front anyway. She put the breakable part of herself between Wren and everything else, and did it like it wasn't even a decision, like it was just the shape her body made.

Wren held the vellum in her boot and practiced not saying anything.

That was the whole of her life, really, the one instruction, the one law: *don't say it.* Because the letters in her mouth weren't letters like other people's letters. When she had touched the master ledger and the crawling marks had rearranged themselves into reading, she had learned what one syllable could do — the candle guttering, the debt vanishing like breath off glass. Words, in the old speech, didn't describe things. They *did* things. And somewhere in her, coiled tight as a spring, was a word that could undo a king.

Or undo everything else too. The stone-under-moss voice had never explained which.

Footsteps echoed somewhere far above — soldiers, many, moving through the burning streets like blood through a vein. Wren pressed closer to Bram's side. Bram's arm came around her without pausing, without looking, the way a wall doesn't look at the weather it stands against.

*Someone worthy,* the voice had said. *Reads you.*

Wren looked up at the sharp profile above her, lit and shadowed by Odile's torch, and thought: maybe worthy doesn't mean perfect. Maybe it means someone who keeps selling pieces of herself so you don't have to be read by the wrong people.

She decided, privately, in the wordless language she was fluent in: *her. If it must be read, let her read me.*

Above them, faintly, the bells changed. Slower now. Searching.

### V. Bram

Odile brought us at last to a door — iron-banded, older than the tunnel around it, marked with a sigil I recognized from the ledgers: the Guild's mark for *account in transit*, which meant, in plain speech, *nothing stored here may be touched, priced, or claimed.* She knocked a pattern. The door opened onto warmth, lamplight, and a low-ceilinged room where perhaps twenty debtors sat among their few possessions, and every face turned toward the child.

Word travels fast underground. Word had already arrived.

An old man rose, joints cracking like green wood, and bowed to Wren. Not deeply. Just enough. And then, one after another, the others did the same — the woman who'd raised the alarm, a pair of brothers missing decades between them, a former court musician who had sold fifteen years to buy his daughter out of a debtor's contract and now had nowhere above ground to play for. They bowed to an eight-year-old girl in a borrowed coat, and I watched Wren stand very straight under it, bewildered and grave, accepting honors she didn't understand from people who understood them better than she did.

"They're not bowing to her," Odile murmured at my shoulder. "They're bowing to what she might unbind. Every chain down here is an oath, Bram. Every debt is a binding. She's the first thing they've ever seen that could open the locks." Her voice dropped. "Which is why we cannot stay. Gratitude curdles. By tomorrow some of them will be wondering what her freedom is worth to the crown, and wondering is how selling starts. We rest till the fourth bell. Then the river gate."

"And after the river gate?"

"After that, we stop running *from* and start moving *toward*." She looked at me then, fully, and for once there was no broker's polish on her face at all — just a tired woman carrying three hundred years of other people's transactions and one very large sin of her own, which she had not yet confessed and which I did not yet know to ask about. "There's a copy of the original binding, Bram. Not the one in the vault — the true original, the one the young king signed with his own blood thirty years ago. I know where it is. I've always known where it is."

The bells overhead shifted again — slower, deliberate, spreading outward through the city like ripples from a dropped stone. Hunting bells. Somewhere up there, crown agents were kicking in doors, and every door they kicked in would yield names, and every name would yield another door.

Somewhere up there, too, a coin lay in a drift of ash, face-up, waiting.

"Sleep if you can," Odile said, and turned away, and I heard her add, very quietly, to no one: "He'd have hated the funeral. Good. It was for us, not him."

I sat down against the warm wall. Wren folded herself against my side without asking, the way she always did, precise as a cat, and her breathing slowed toward sleep while mine refused every invitation.

In my head, unbidden, the ledger opened. *Inventory: one child, priceless. One mentor, compromised. One ally in the palace, unreachable tonight. One immunity — sold. One name — collateral. One mummified broker — paid in full.*

And beneath the entries, the running total I never let myself write down, because writing it would make it real:

*Whatever this costs, it is already cheaper than letting them take her.*

The fourth bell would come. The river gate would open. And whatever waited on the far side of the water — the truth about the binding, the truth about Odile, the truth about what a willing anchor was and what it would demand of us —

we would walk into it awake.
