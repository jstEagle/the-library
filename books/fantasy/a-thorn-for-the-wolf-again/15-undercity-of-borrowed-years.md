## 15. Undercity of Borrowed Years

The undercity smells like a cellar that has learned to dream.

That was Odile's description, delivered as she pried up the grate behind a shuttered tannery and gestured us down into the dark with the brisk courtesy of a woman showing guests to their rooms. I had assumed she was being poetic. Odile is many things, but poetic is not one of them, and I should have trusted the literal reading, because four levels below the street the air does, in fact, smell like dreaming—warm, stale, faintly sweet, the exhalation of hundreds of people sleeping in shifts, trading hours of their lives for bread and rent and, in one case I would learn about, a lute.

Wren's hand found mine on the ladder. She had not let go of it since the raid, not properly, only loosening her grip when I needed both hands for something, and I had stopped pretending I minded. A child who trusts you is a weight and a gift in the same parcel, and I have carried heavier things for less reason.

"Mind the third rung," Odile called back. "It owes the Guild money."

I thought this was a joke. It was not a joke. The third rung had been traded against a debt by a debtor who owned it, and the Guild's collectors had removed it, and a man named Ferro had broken his ankle on the gap two winters ago and now walked with a stick and a grudge. Everything down here is collateral. The candle stubs. The blankets. The very hours people sleep in.

"Welcome to the ledger's basement," Odile said, pulling the grate shut above us. "Where the accounts come due."

* * *

The undercity is not, properly speaking, a city. It is the space beneath one, the old cisterns and wine vaults and unfinished foundations that the rich forgot and the desperate remembered. The Guild has kept a presence here for as long as the Guild has kept ledgers—someone has to house the debtors who can no longer afford to live above, and the Guild, whatever else it is, does not abandon its accounts. An unpaid debt is a wound in the book. Odile says it itches.

We were given a cell off the Long Gallery, which is what they call the main cistern—a vaulted corridor two hundred paces long, lined with alcoves, each alcove a household of one to six, curtained with dyers' cloth and lit by lamps that burn rendered fat because candles are a currency here. The lamps give the light a golden, greasy warmth, and everything and everyone looks a little like a painting of themselves. I found it beautiful for about an hour. Then I found it heartbreaking, and I have not stopped finding it heartbreaking since.

Wren slept the first day. She had earned it; we all had. Ninepence's ash was still under my fingernails—I had not been able to wash it properly, and I confess I stopped trying, because it seemed important that some of him stay with me. The mummified broker had burned rather than talk, and the burning had bought us the hours we needed to run, and I have been trying to balance that equation ever since and coming up short. That is the trouble with the Guild's arithmetic. It always balances. Grief does not.

On the second day, I began to meet the neighbors.

* * *

The first was Ferro, the man with the stick. He occupied the alcove across from ours with a woman named Sela and a boy of about ten who was not theirs by blood and was theirs in every way that mattered. Ferro had been a bridge engineer, once. A good one. He had built two of the spans over the Weir, and when the river took the third in a flood, he had gone to the Guild to buy back the years of his apprenticeship—the lean, hungry, brilliant years when he could still out-draft men half his age—because the crown's engineers wanted a design and his old hands had forgotten how.

The price was fifteen years.

"They priced it by the decade," he told me, showing me his ledger page with the flat pride of a man showing a scar. "Fifteen for the hands, and they threw in the tremor cure for two more. Seventeen years, gone like that." He snapped his fingers. It sounded very loud in the cistern. "I built the Weir span in eleven months. Best work of my life, they say. I've read the plans. I don't remember drawing them."

"You read your own work like a stranger's," I said.

"Every day." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has made his peace with a bad bargain by refusing to look at the interest. "But my boy eats. You want to judge me, judge the boy first."

I told him I was the last person in the world entitled to judge. I had tasted poison for a living; he had tasted time. We were both professional swallowers.

* * *

Sela I met at the fat-lamp, where the alcoves take turns filling them, a chore that rotates by ledger entry like everything else. She was rendering tallow and talking to the boy—Tam, his name was—and she had the particular stillness of a person listening to something very far away.

Her story came out over three days, the way stories do down here, in installments, paid out like the hours she was owed. She had been a midwife in the river districts. When the fever came through eight years ago, she had worked nineteen days without sleeping, and when her own strength failed she had done the arithmetic that the desperate do: she had gone to the Guild and borrowed the hours she needed from her own old age. Thirty years' worth, drawn down against a future she might not live to spend anyway.

"I pulled eleven babies through that fever," she said. "Eleven. I keep the list." She did, too—a scrap of oilcloth with eleven small marks, worn soft as cloth from handling. "People above say we're thieves down here. Stealing from ourselves, they mean. But you tell me what I stole. I stole the years when my knees ache and my eyes go cloudy. I spent them where they were worth something. Show me the crime."

I could not. I have stood in the Guild's back office and watched vials of borrowed courage change hands, and I have never once been able to find the crime in it, only the cost, and the cost is always paid by someone who could least afford the interest.

Tam was not hers. That came later, in the installment where the story bent. The eleventh baby on her list. His mother had died on the twelfth day of the fever, and Sela had traded another five years—not borrowed this time, given outright, sold to the Guild's stores—to buy the boy a name-entry, because an orphan with no recorded name cannot inherit, cannot apprentice, cannot legally exist, and the Guild sells legal existence the way it sells everything else. To whoever pays. Without asking why.

"He doesn't know," Sela said, watching Tam chase a rat with the cheerful ruthlessness of the young. "One day he'll ask why I look sixty and act forty, and I'll tell him, and he'll hate me for a year and then he'll understand, or he won't. That's the gamble of love. You never see the full terms until you've already signed."

I thought of Wren asleep in our alcove, her hand curled around the edge of my sleeve even in sleep, and I understood the gamble from the inside.

* * *

There were others, of course. There is a whole gallery of them, and I could fill a ledger of my own with their entries.

The old man in alcove nineteen who had traded forty years to the cause of the printers' guild when the crown burned their presses, and who now sat by the fat-lamp every evening copying forbidden pamphlets in a hand that shook, because, he said, "I paid for the right to do this work. I intend to do it."

The woman in alcove seven who had made a mistake—I will not record what kind; it was hers to own, and she owned it with a dignity that shamed me—had made a mistake, and had bought the forgetting of it for ten years, and had then discovered that the Guild's forgetting is a loan, not a gift. The memory sits in a vial in the Guild's stores, accruing. Someday she will have to buy it back or watch someone else purchase it, and she lives down here among the debtors because the debt is hers, in the most literal sense the word has ever carried.

The young man in alcove twenty-two who had sold five years to buy his sweetheart out of a debt-bond, and who spoke of it with the breezy carelessness of the very young, until the night I found him sitting alone by the water channel, doing sums on his fingers.

"Five years," he said, when I asked. "It sounded like nothing when I signed. I'm twenty-three. Five years is a fifth of everything I've ever been." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious. "But she's free, and she doesn't even—" He stopped. "No. She's free. That's the whole of it. She's free."

I sat with him until the sums stopped. That is most of what comfort is, I have found. Sitting with someone until the arithmetic quiets.

* * *

Wren, meanwhile, was learning the undercity the way children learn everything: completely and sideways. The debtors adopted her within days. Down here, where everyone has sold something, a child who speaks a language older than kingdoms is not a curiosity; she is a neighbor with an interesting trade. Ferro taught her to whittle. Sela taught her to render fat and to read the fever-signs in a face. Tam taught her the rat-chasing, which she proved alarmingly good at.

And at night, in our alcove, she would press against my side and I would feel her counting my breaths, the way she did, and I would think: this is what they are all doing, all of them, all down here. Counting. Every person in this cistern is counting what they have left, and what they spent, and whether it was worth it, and the answer changes nightly, and they keep living anyway.

I had thought, coming down here, that I would find despair. I had prepared myself for it the way I prepare my tongue for a suspect dish—braced, professional, expecting the worst. What I found instead was the most human gathering I have ever stood among. Above, the court performs its perfection and rots underneath. Down here, everyone is visibly damaged and nobody pretends otherwise, and the damage has made them kind in the specific, unsentimental way of people who know exactly what survival costs because they have paid it in the only currency that matters.

"You're writing us down," Ferro said to me on the fifth night, catching me at it, my little private ledger open on my knee. He was not angry. He was curious. "Why?"

"Because someone should," I said. "Because the crown's histories won't. Because—" I searched for it. "Because I've spent my whole life keeping accounts for people who could pay, and this is the first ledger I've ever wanted to keep for people who couldn't."

He considered that for a long moment, the fat-lamp gilding the ruined bridge-builder's hands.

"Put down the boy," he said finally. "Tam. Put down that Sela gave everything for him and he doesn't know yet, and put down that when he finds out, whatever he does, it will be the truest thing that ever happened to him. Put down that we were here. That's all any of us are asking. That we were here."

I put it down. I have put down a great deal in my life, in ledgers that were not mine and causes that were not mine and poisons that were very much mine. That page was the first one I ever wrote that felt like it mattered more than my own survival.

* * *

It was on the sixth night that Odile came down the Long Gallery with a face like a closed account, and I knew before she spoke that the quiet was over.

She sat on the edge of our alcove, accepted the cup of fat-lamp tea I offered—everything down here is fat-lamp tea; it is tea in the sense that a debt is a gift—and said, in a voice pitched for me alone: "The crown's agents were at the tannery grate an hour ago. Asking after a woman and a child. They had a description of you, Bram. A good one. Down to the scar on your knuckle."

I looked at my knuckle. The scar I'd had since I was nine, from the first poison that ever tried to kill me and failed.

"Someone sold us," I said.

"Someone always sells everyone," Odile said. "It's the oldest trade there is." She drank her tea, grimaced at it on principle, and set it down. "We have maybe a day. Perhaps two. The undercity has a thousand exits and the crown's men know perhaps a dozen, but they have something worse than maps now." She paused, and I watched her decide whether to say the next part, and watched her decide to say it anyway, because Odile has never once spared me a truth I needed. "They have a memory. Bought from our own stores. A chamberlain's purchase, the one I told you about. It shows them Wren's face, and it shows them the corridor she stood in, and the door she came through. They are not searching for a woman and a child anymore, Bram. They are searching for a key, and they know what the key looks like."

Across the gallery, Wren was teaching Tam a whittling cut, her small hands steady around the knife, her face lit gold and greasy and beautiful by the fat-lamp. Sela was watching them both with that faraway listening look. Ferro was copying pamphlets in his shaking hand. A whole gallery of people who had paid everything they had to be here, in this moment, in this light.

"They'll come through the Weir gate first," Odile said. "It's the nearest exit to the palace, and they'll reason like crown men reason: nearest first, hardest last." She stood, brushing tallow from her skirts. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we move, and we move fast, and you're going to need every hour you never sold."

I lay down beside Wren in the dark, listening to her count my breaths, and I thought about the gallery of them—Ferro's seventeen years, Sela's thirty, the pamphleteer's forty, all of it spent so that something could exist that otherwise wouldn't. A bridge. A boy. A truth.

And I thought: whatever the crown takes from me, it will not take the knowing of them. Some debts are worth the interest.

Then the fat-lamp guttered, and somewhere far above us, iron rang against iron—the sound of a grate being lifted by hands that did not live here.

They had come through the tannery after all.
