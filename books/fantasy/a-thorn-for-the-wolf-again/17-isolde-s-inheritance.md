## 17. Isolde's Inheritance

My father taught me chess when I was six, and he cheated.

Not obviously—he was a king, and kings cheat the way water is wet—but he cheated. A pawn would appear where no pawn had been. A knight would slide through a bishop's shoulder as though the board had briefly agreed to forget geometry. When I called him on it, he would laugh, kiss the top of my head, and say, *A ruler who cannot bend a rule will be bent by it, little star. Learn the difference between cheating and mercy.*

I thought, for years, that this was a lesson about chess.

I have since revised my opinion. It was a lesson about fathers, and I failed the examination.

* * *

The night I found the testament, I had gone to his study to steal.

There is no gentler word for it. The palace library has three keys: one for the king, one for the Lord Archivist, and one that my mother wore on a ribbon until she died and I lifted it from her jewel box before my father could remember it existed. Grief makes excellent pickpockets of daughters. For eleven years I had used that key perhaps a dozen times, always at night, always for the same shelf—the histories, the treaties, the war records of the Border Campaigns, because I had become, without quite deciding to, a woman who kept a ledger of her father's wrongness.

I should explain the ledger, since it will matter.

It began as a child's game and curdled into something with columns. *The king answered the ambassador's question before the ambassador finished asking it.* *The king did not blink for the duration of the harvest blessing—four minutes, I counted.* *The king's shadow on the chapel wall moved before he did, like a dog straining at a leash.* I wrote these things in a cipher of my own devising, in a book disguised as a book of devotional poetry, which is the only disguise in the palace that no one ever checks, because no one believes anyone reads devotional poetry. They are right, in my case. The poetry is a lie from cover to cover. So, I had begun to suspect, was my father.

For years I told myself I was mad. That is the genius of a wrongness that is small and patient and wears a beloved face: it trains you to distrust the only witness it cannot bribe. I loved my father. I remembered loving my father—his laugh, his terrible singing, the way he carried me on his shoulders through the autumn markets and let me wear the crown for exactly eleven steps. The memory was warm and whole and it argued, every single day, against the ledger. *You are grieving your mother. You are lonely. You are a princess with too much imagination and too little to do.* And I would close the book, and kiss his cheek at supper, and watch him answer a question half a second before it was asked, and add a new line.

Then, three months ago, a food-taster with a spine of iron and the eyes of a woman who has swallowed every death the kingdom could brew showed me a page from a Guild ledger, and I stopped being able to call myself mad.

Bram Almeida. I will say this for her: she is the only person I have ever met who is entirely what she appears to be, and what she appears to be is a tired professional standing in a doorway deciding whether you are worth the trouble. She was worth it. She had proof that the crown had been buying memories—extracting them, erasing them, purchasing silence by the ounce. She had a mute child who spoke a language older than the kingdom in her sleep. And she looked at my devotional poetry and said, without a flicker of a smile, *Your cipher's third symbol is wrong, Highness. It's cost you two entries.*

Two entries. I had lost two entries of evidence to a faulty cipher and this woman had read my treason in the time it takes to decline a pastry. I decided in that moment that if the kingdom had to be saved by anyone, I was glad it was being saved by someone who checked arithmetic.

* * *

So: the theft.

The Border Campaigns shelf had always been my cover. But that night, three months into our strange, airless alliance—Bram bringing me Guild records, me bringing her the palace's rhythms and passwords—I noticed something I had somehow never noticed in eleven years of nocturnal visits.

The shelf was the wrong depth.

It is a stupid detail. It is the sort of detail that only matters to a woman who has spent years measuring her father's blink-rate, but I stood there with my candle and my stolen key and I thought: *this wall is four inches too thick.* I had a hairpin. I have always had a hairpin; a princess learns early that her jewelry is a toolkit in disguise. I worked it into the seam behind the third shelf of histories and pried, and the panel came away with a sound like a held breath released, and there was a cavity in the wall, and in the cavity there was a box of black oak banded in iron, and on the box, in my father's handwriting, was my name.

Not *To Isolde.* Not *For the heir.* Just *Isolde*, the way he said it when I had done something that frightened him.

I want to be honest about what I felt in that moment, because honesty is the only inheritance I have left. I felt terror. Not the dramatic kind—the small kind, the kind that lives in the hands. Because a hidden box with your name on it, behind a false wall, in the study of a father you have spent years documenting as an impostor, can only contain one of two things: proof that you are mad, or proof that you are not. And I had lived long enough in the space between those two possibilities that either outcome felt like a kind of death.

I opened it anyway. I am my father's daughter. I have never once left a locked thing locked.

* * *

Inside was a testament. His hand, his seal, dated thirty years and one week past—the week before the coronation. Beside it lay a second document, older, in a hand I did not know, on paper that felt wrong, like vellum that had been argued with. And beneath both, wrapped in oilcloth, was a crown.

Not *the* crown. A crown—a smaller thing, a circlet really, plain gold, unadorned, the kind of thing a minor lord might wear to his own wedding. It sat in the box like a sleeping animal and I did not touch it. Some instincts arrive fully formed. I looked at it the way you look at a snake in a nursery and I closed the oilcloth over it and I did not touch it.

I read the testament by candlelight, kneeling on the floor of my father's study like a servant, which felt appropriate, because by the second page I no longer recognized the man who had written it, and one reads strangers' letters standing up or kneeling down, never sitting comfortably.

I am going to set down what it said. Not all of it—some of it is mine, and I find I am greedy with it—but the shape of it, because the shape of it changes everything, and a shape should be shared.

* * *

He had known. That is the first thing. Before the coronation, before the crown ever touched his head, my father had known what was coming, because he had gone looking for it. There had been a war—the Border Campaigns, the ones whose records I had used as my cover for a decade, which I now understand was his joke, or his penance, or both. A war that had eaten eleven years and two generations and was, by its final winter, preparing to eat a third. And at the bottom of that war he had found the thing that wars find: a hunger with a shape. An oath-eater. A thing that fed on sworn words the way fire feeds on air, that had been bound and bound and bound again by every kingdom on the border, and that had learned, over the centuries, to let its bindings rot from the inside.

The old document—the one in the wrong-feeling hand—was a Guild contract. I recognized the seal from Bram's records: the Guild of Impossible Things, serving whoever pays, never asking why. My father had gone to them in the last winter of the war. He had bought the one thing they sold that could not be unsold: a binding strong enough to hold the oath-eater forever. And the price—the price was not gold.

The price was a king.

The binding required an anchor. A will, freely offered, of sufficient weight to hold the thing down through all the years of its imprisonment. There was no one in the borderlands with a will of that weight except the man who had spent eleven years refusing to let the war end any other way. He signed. He wrote his own name into the contract as the ballast, and he wrote, in a passage I have read so many times I could recite it in my sleep, the sentence that has rearranged everything I am:

*I have learned one thing from eleven years of war, and it is this: that no one should hold absolute power, and I mean no one, and I include myself, and I include myself most of all, because I have watched myself make decisions these eleven years and I have seen what I become when no one can contradict me. If the price of ending this war is my throne, then the throne was the most expensive thing in the kingdom and the cheapest thing I own.*

He was not tricked. Do you understand? I had spent three years building toward the theory that my father had been consumed, devoured, stolen—that he was a victim wearing his own face. And here he was, in his own hand, telling me he had walked into the binding with his eyes open, that he had *chosen* it, that the thing in the crown was not his murderer but his warden's uniform.

Because that was the second thing. The binding did not merely cage the oath-eater. It *wore* it. The anchor's will and the prisoner's hunger, bound together, wrapped in the shape of the man who had offered himself. My father had not been replaced by the thing in the crown. He had become its cage, and the cage had his face, and the cage had to keep ruling, because a kingdom that lost its king mid-peace would tear itself apart faster than any war. The binding's terms—Bram will confirm this, Bram who reads contracts the way priests read scripture—required the anchor to govern. To hold the realm steady while the prisoner starved. Thirty years of starving. Thirty years of a man's will wrapped around a hunger, answering questions before they were asked because the thing inside him heard them forming, his shadow moving wrong because two wills shared one outline and could not agree on whose it was.

He was not my father. He was also not not my father. He was my father's sacrifice, still being performed, every day, at every audience, for thirty years, in a throne room full of courtiers who never noticed the difference because the difference was the point. The binding was seamless. It was meant to be seamless. He had paid the Guild extra for seamless.

* * *

I sat on the floor of that study until my candle burned down to a puddle, and I will tell you what I wept for, since I have decided to be honest: not for the father I had lost. I had already mourned him, in the ledger, line by line, without knowing it. I wept for the father I had *suspected*. For three years I had looked at the thing in the crown and seen a thief, an impostor, a monster wearing my father's face as a disguise—and I had hated it, and my hatred had been a small daily treason, and it turns out the treason was aimed at a man who had died for me before I was old enough to remember him whole.

He had known I would suspect. The testament says so. There is a passage—I will not quote it entire, I told you some of it is mine—but he writes that his daughter has her mother's eyes and her mother's habit of counting, and that she will count, and that one day she will find the wrongness, and that when she does she will think herself mad, and that he is sorry, that he is sorry, that he is sorry, written three times, the third time with the pen pressed so hard it nearly tore the page.

*If you are reading this, little star, then you counted your way to the truth, and I am prouder of you than I have ever been permitted to show. The thing wearing me will have been careful with you. It needs you. An heir is the binding's proof of continuity. Be a disappointment to it. Be a disappointment loudly.*

I laughed. I want that recorded too, because it is the strangest thing of all: kneeling in the dust of my father's hidden room, reading his apology, I laughed until I had to put my hand over my mouth. *Be a disappointment loudly.* Thirty years dead and he was still cheating at chess, still bending the rules, still slipping an extra pawn onto the board when I wasn't looking. This box, this testament, this crown-in-a-box—this was his final cheat. He had hidden the truth four inches behind a wall and waited eleven years for his daughter's hairpin.

* * *

There was more. There was the matter of the failsafe, which I will set down because Bram must see it and I am done keeping secrets in devotional poetry.

The binding had a weakness, and my father had known about it, because the Guild's broker—a creature the ledgers call Ninepence, whom Bram tells me is older than the kingdom and twice as dry—had insisted on it. No binding should be unbreakable, the broker had said, in words my father recorded with visible distaste. Not because bindings fail, but because the men who make them always believe themselves the exception. *You say no one should hold absolute power, Majesty. Very good. Then do not hold absolute power over this binding either. Leave a key in the world, outside the crown, outside your will, in a hand you cannot command.*

So there is a key. A living key. Born into the oath-line after the binding was sealed—a failsafe my father never met, a child of eight with a mute tongue and a language older than kingdoms sleeping in a room above a Guild counting house. Wren. The child Bram has been feeding porridge to and teaching her letters. The child the crown has been hunting through every memory it can buy.

My father never names her in the testament—he could not; she did not exist yet when he wrote it—but he describes her, in the broker's terms: *a reader, a speaker, one who carries the word until someone worthy reads her.* And he writes, in the last passage, the one I have read the most:

*If the day comes that the key is found, know that I do not ask to be saved. Understand what you hold. The thing in the crown has kept the peace for thirty years, and the peace is real, and the dead of the Border Campaigns are real, and if you unbind it you must be certain—certain as I was certain—that what you build after is worth the peace you end. I traded myself because I believed a kingdom should never rest on one will, even mine. Prove me right. Or prove me wrong, little star, but do it with your eyes open, the way I did it.*

* * *

I brought the testament to Bram the next morning. I will not describe that conversation here; some things are between the two people who sat up until dawn over a dead man's handwriting while a child slept in the next room, and one of those things is that Bram Almeida read the binding's terms and went very quiet, and then went very busy, and then said, in the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hates: *The unbinding needs three things. The word, the witness, the willingness of the bound. Two we might find. The third—* and she looked at me, and I understood that the third thing was my father's consent, my father's remnant, whatever is left of him inside the cage he built, and that someone would have to ask him. And that the asking would be mine.

I said yes. Of course I said yes. I have been saying yes to my father's ghost since I was six years old and he put a cheated chessboard in front of me.

But that is not the hook of this chapter. That is not the thing I need to set down before I close this page, because the page must be closed, because Bram is waiting and the solstice is coming and there is no time in a conspiracy for a princess to sit with her grief like a normal orphan.

The thing is this.

Last night, after the testament, after the plans, after Bram left through the servant stair with the binding's terms folded against her heart—I was summoned. A chamberlain, at midnight, sweating, apologetic, terrified. *His Majesty requests the princess's presence. Alone.*

I went. What else does a disappointment do loudly, except walk toward the thing that frightens her?

And my father—the cage, the anchor, the thing that wears him—received me in the long gallery where the coronation portraits hang, and he was kind, and he was gentle, and he spoke to me for an hour about my mother and my childhood and the harvest festival of my ninth year, and every word of it was warm and every word of it was wrong, and I understood, standing there, that it knew. It knew about the testament. It has always known about the box; it has been my father's will for thirty years and the box was hidden from the court, not from itself. It let me find it. It let me read it. It has been waiting for me to finish grieving.

And at the end of the hour it took my hands in its hands—my father's hands, scarred from a hawking accident I remember, warm as bread—and it said:

*Isolde. Little star. I have an offer to make you, and I think, when you hear it, you will find it is the only kind thing anyone in this palace has said to you in years.*

I did not pull my hands away. I am reporting that as a fact and letting it stand in the record, because a ledger that flatters its keeper is worthless, and mine has never flattered me.

It made the offer. And God forgive me—God forgive me, Father, wherever you are inside that beautiful, patient cage—part of me wanted to say yes.
