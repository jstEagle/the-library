## 18. The Offer from the Throne

The summons came the way a knife comes: politely, and with someone else's hand on the handle.

I was in the undercity, third night of hiding, eating a stew that tasted mostly of the pot it had been cooked in, when the boy arrived. He couldn't have been more than twelve, wearing the livery of the palace kitchens — my old territory — and he held out a folded paper as though it might bite him. Which, given the wax seal on it, was a fair assessment. The seal was the royal crest, but pressed into it was a thumbprint, and the thumbprint had too many whorls, spiraling inward like something trying to find its own center.

"Nobody sent me," the boy said. "I mean — somebody sent me, but I don't remember who. I just sort of... woke up walking."

"That's how it recruits the footwork," I said. "Eat something. You'll remember yourself by morning."

He ate. I read the letter.

*Madam Almeida. You are invited to an audience. No guards will detain you. No harm will come to you in transit. Come alone, come armed, come weeping — the door does not care. Midnight. The Weir Gate will be open.*

It was signed with the king's name. The real king's name. Which was the first courtesy and the first cruelty, because that name had not been spoken aloud in this city for thirty years, and I only knew it from ledgers.

Odile read it over my shoulder, which is a habit of hers I've stopped objecting to, mostly because objecting has never once worked.

"You're not going," she said.

"I'm absolutely going."

"Of course you are. I said it the way you say 'it's raining' — as a fact about the world, not an instruction." She took the letter from my hand and held it close to the lamp. "Look at the ink."

I looked. The ink was moving. Not flowing — *remembering*, if ink can be said to remember. Letters drifting back toward the shapes of older words underneath, like a palimpsest trying to confess.

"It's written in the same stuff as the binding contracts," I said. "Oath-ink."

"Which means it isn't an invitation. It's a term sheet. It's already been signed, Bram — by you, somewhere, in some future or past it keeps in a drawer." Odile set it down on the table the way you'd put down a spider. "The Guild has dealt with it exactly once before, thirty years ago, and the broker who handled that sale doesn't talk about it. Even Ninepence wouldn't talk about it, and Ninepence is a mummy who talks about everything."

"Then it's time somebody did."

"Bram."

"Odile." I looked at her — really looked, at this sharp, tired, magnificent woman who had spent three decades managing a guilt I was only beginning to understand the shape of. "It offered safe transit. It wants to *negotiate*. Do you know what that means?"

"It means it's worried."

"It means a thing that has worn a crown for thirty years, that answers questions before they're asked, that moves through this city like weather — it is *worried*. About an eight-year-old girl who can't say her own name in any language I know. That worry is the most valuable intelligence we've had since this started, and I intend to find out what it's afraid of." I stood, and my knees objected, and I overruled them. "Also, it knows where we are. The Weir Gate, the undercity, the stew — it knows. Staying isn't safety anymore. Staying is just dying somewhere with worse furniture."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she did something I hadn't seen her do before: she took off one of her rings — a plain iron band I'd always assumed was sentimental — and pressed it into my palm.

"Guild iron," she said. "Cold-forged, oath-marked. It won't stop it. Nothing stops it. But it will *remember* what happens to you, even if you don't. Even if nobody does." Her jaw worked. "I've watched this thing collect debts for thirty years from the comfortable side of a ledger. Don't make me watch it collect yours."

"Then look away," I said, and put the ring on my smallest finger, where it fit like it had been measured for me, which, knowing the Guild, it had been.

* * *

The Weir Gate at midnight was exactly as advertised: open, unguarded, and wrong.

The gatehouse lamps burned steady — no wind, no flicker, flames standing at attention. The water under the gate ran without sound. I noticed all of this the way a taster notices things: cataloguing, comparing, filing. A world with no randomness in it. That was its signature, I was learning. The Hollow King didn't create; it *edited*. It took the world's noise and smoothed it, and what was left was a draft of reality that had never been lived in.

Two figures waited on the far side. I knew one of them and my whole body knew it before my mind caught up — Captain Renn of the palace guard, who had taught me which stairwells had drafts, who had once covered for me when I'd miscounted a dose. Except Renn had died four years ago, of a fever, and I had tasted the fever's work in three other throats that winter and knew it for what it was.

The thing wearing Renn's face smiled with Renn's smile, and it fit the face the way a glove fits a hand: completely, and with nothing inside.

"Madam Almeida," it said, in Renn's voice. "You look well. Poison agrees with you."

"Poison and I have an arrangement," I said. "It doesn't kill me, and I don't write rude things about it in my ledger."

"Walk with me."

It turned and walked, and I followed, because the alternative was standing alone at a gate with a dead man, and I have always preferred my horrors in motion. The second figure fell in behind me. I didn't look at it. I had a suspicion about the second figure, and I was saving the looking for later, when I'd need the shock less.

The palace rose ahead of us, and it was *lit* — every window, every corridor, glowing like a festival night. But there were no sounds of a palace at night. No boots, no voices, no clatter from the kitchens I knew by heart. Just light with nobody in it, a stage dressed for a play where the audience had been eaten.

"You're wondering why I invited you," it said, conversationally, in Renn's voice, which was the worst possible voice for the sentence.

"I'm wondering why you're being polite. You didn't send guards. You could have sent guards. You could have sent anything. You've had agents buying memories from my own Guild for weeks. So either you need something from me that force can't get—" I let that hang, "—or the politeness is the trap."

"Both." It said it with such cheerful honesty that I almost laughed. "You are a woman who has spent her life detecting the false. So I will be true with you. It is the only currency you respect."

We came to the doors of the great hall, and they opened without being touched, and I finally looked at the second figure.

It was my mother.

She had been dead twenty-six years. I had sold my memories of her — of the house, of the reasons, of all of it — to get into the Guild vault, and I had thought that was the deepest cut available. But the body remembers what the mind has traded away. My body remembered the exact weight of her hand on my head. My body remembered the smell of her — bread and lamp oil and some herb I couldn't name anymore because I had sold the name along with everything else.

My eyes were burning and I made a note of that, too. Filed it. *Crying. Interesting. Continue.*

"That's not her," I said. My voice came out level, which I was professionally proud of.

"No," it agreed. "But it is *accurate*. Reconstructed from thirty years of court records, from your Guild's own files — you'd be astonished what people sell us, Madam Almeida — and from the shape of the hole she left in you. I cannot give you her back. I can give you the *feeling* of her. Most people, given the choice, take the feeling."

"I'm not most people. Most people haven't eaten enough poison to have opinions."

"No," it said, and there was something in its voice then that I would spend the rest of my life failing to describe — not menace, exactly. *Appetite.* "You are not. That is why we are here."

* * *

The throne room had been rearranged.

The throne itself stood at the far end, but the space before it had been cleared and furnished — a table, two chairs, a carafe of wine, two cups. A negotiating table. It had set a *table* for me. Somewhere in the architecture of the thing there was a sense of theater so old and so practiced that it had stopped being pretense and become simply its manner of existing.

It sat in one of the chairs, not the throne, wearing the king's body — the broad shoulders, the grey-streaked beard, the hands that had signed a thousand decrees. But it had stopped maintaining the performance. The face was the king's face and the eyes were not in it. The eyes were a place where light went and was not returned.

"Sit," it said. "The wine is safe. I have had it tested."

"By whom?"

"By a taster. You would have approved of her thoroughness. She is no longer with us, but her notes were excellent."

I sat. I poured. I tasted the wine the way I taste everything — holding it, reading it, listening to what my tongue had to say. It was a good wine. A Vellorine red, the king's favorite, and it was clean, and that frightened me more than poison would have. Poison I understood. Courtesy from this thing was a language with no dictionary.

"You know what I am," it said.

"I know what you're called. The Hollow King. An oath-eater. A thing that was bound into a crown thirty years ago by a young king who thought he was buying peace." I set down the cup. "The Guild keeps records. I keep the Guild's records. You'd be amazed what I know."

"Then you know I ended a war."

It said it simply. No flourish. And that was the trap of it, because it was true — I had read the ledgers, and the war it had ended had been three decades of border-burning that was eating a generation, and it had ended in a single season, and the peace since had been the longest in the kingdom's history.

"I know," I said.

"Thirty years," it said. "No famine I could prevent, I prevented. No war I could stop, I stopped. I have held this kingdom the way a hand holds a bird — firmly enough that it lives, gently enough that it doesn't know it's held. Ask your princess. Ask her what her father's reign has been. Ask the merchants, the farmers, the guards. The kingdom is *content*, Madam Almeida. It is the most content kingdom on this coast. And you — you and a child and a defrocked priestess and a woman with a guilty ledger — you propose to unmake all of it, for what? For *truth*?" The eyes did something I can only call a blink, though I don't believe it needed to blink. "Truth is a flavor. I have tasted it. It is bitter, and it does not keep."

"And the real king?"

The silence that followed had texture. It was the silence of a thing deciding how honest to be, which is a silence I'd heard from a hundred suspects and never before from a throne.

"A fragment," it said at last. "A residue. He is not suffering, if that is your next question. He is not *anything*. He is the memory of a decision, kept in the amber of the binding. I wear his shape because the shape was the price and the shape is the tool. When the binding ends, he ends. And so do I. And so—" the king's face turned toward me with terrible mildness, "—does the peace."

"And Wren."

"Ah." And there — there it was. The half-second pause. The thing that answered questions before they were asked, *pausing*. I had my answer before it spoke, and I watched it know that I had it, and I watched it decide to proceed anyway, which told me more than the answer would.

"The child is a flaw in the work," it said. "An anchor I did not know had been set. The binding was meant to be sealed — complete, closed, final. Instead the oath-line ran on, and ran into her, and now she is a door in a wall I built to have no doors." It folded the king's hands on the table, a courtier's gesture, patient and reasonable. "I do not want to hurt her. I want you to understand that. Hurting her would be loud, and loud things crack walls. I want her *quiet*. I want her unremarkable. I want her to grow up in a village that does not exist on any map, loved by people who do not know what she is, and I want the door in the wall to stay shut for another thirty years, and another thirty after that, until the line runs out on its own."

"And you're offering me what, to arrange this quiet?"

"I am offering you everything you have already sold."

The room changed. I can't describe it better than that — the light shifted, the air thickened, and it began to speak, and it did not speak in its own voice.

It spoke in my mother's voice.

"*You always were too clever for that kitchen,*" it said, warm and tired and real, every cadence perfect. "*Come home. I kept the house. I kept everything.*"

I gripped the Guild iron ring under the table and said nothing.

It spoke in Renn's voice: "*You could come back to the court, Bram. Full immunity, restored — thirty years of it, every death you swallowed, given back. You could taste for the crown again and never fear another cup as long as you lived.*"

It spoke in the voice of Master Corvin, the taster who trained me, dead these twelve years, whose laugh I had forgotten I remembered: "*Girl, you were the best I ever taught. Don't throw that away for a foundling and a mummy's grudge.*"

It went around the room like that, voice after voice, every dead and lost and traded-away thing I had ever grieved, each one offering me back a piece of myself with the interest compounded. My immunity. My memories. My mother's kitchen. My name — it even offered my *name*, spoken in my own voice, which was the moment I understood how deep its reading of me went, because it knew that the name was the last thing on the table, the thing I'd signed away as collateral, and it was holding it out like a treat.

"And the child?" I said, when it paused for breath it didn't need. "What happens to Wren in this village that isn't on any map?"

"She is loved," it said, in my mother's voice, which was the cruelest single choice it had made all night, and it had made some extraordinary choices. "She is safe. She is *happy*. What would you give, Madam Almeida, to know that for certain? You, who check every cup twice?"

And here is the thing I will not pretend otherwise, because this account is the only honest ledger I have left: for one full breath, I wanted it.

I wanted the immunity back. I wanted the kitchen. I wanted my name in my own mouth. I wanted to stop being a woman held together by bookkeeping and stubbornness, and here was a thing offering to reassemble me, piece by piece, with a smile in every dead voice I'd ever loved — and all it cost was an eight-year-old girl's *quiet*, a door shut on a wall, a peace that had, by every measurable account, held for thirty years.

One breath. I let it pass through me like a poison I chose not to swallow.

Then I looked at the thing in the king's face and did what I do best.

I read the cup.

"You've made a mistake," I said.

The eyes narrowed. The light went in and did not come out.

"You offered me my name back," I said. "My *name*. But you can't give what you don't hold. The Guild holds it — it's collateral on my contract, and the Guild's contracts don't transfer, not even to crowns. So either you didn't know that, which means your famous thoroughness has a hole in it—" I leaned forward, and I confess my hands were shaking, but my voice wasn't, "—or you *do* know it, and you offered it anyway, because this whole banquet of dead voices is theater, and you're not negotiating at all. You're *stalling*."

The room went very still. The wine in my cup stopped trembling, because even the wine was afraid.

"Which means," I said, standing, "that whatever you're afraid Wren will do — you're afraid she'll do it *soon*. Tonight. Before you can get your door shut. And you needed me out of the undercity, seated at your polite little table, being tempted, while—"

It rose. The king's shape came apart at the edges, just slightly, like a drawing smudged by a wet thumb, and beneath the smudge was something that was not a face and was not nothing and was worse than either.

"Madam Almeida," it said, in every voice at once — my mother's, Renn's, Corvin's, its own, and one more, one I didn't recognize, a voice like a tower burning, "the offer stands until dawn. After dawn, I collect the standard way."

I was already running.

Behind me, the doors of the throne room opened onto a corridor of perfect, silent, festival-bright light — and somewhere far below, in the undercity, in a room that smelled of stew and lamp oil, an eight-year-old girl was sitting alone with a ledger she could read.

And the last thing I heard, chasing me down the corridor like a hand on my collar, in my own voice, in my own handwriting, was:

"*Some things are survived, Bram. Some are paid for. I am very patient, and I have not yet decided which you are.*"
