## 19. Refusal and Reckoning

The Hollow King waited for my answer with the patience of a thing that had been waiting thirty years and expected to wait thirty more.

I want you to understand what the offer looked like from where I stood, because I have spent a great deal of time since then explaining to myself why it was tempting, and honesty is the only currency I've never traded away. It offered me back my immunity. Thirty years of swallowed deaths, restored to my tongue like coins returned to a purse. It offered Wren's safety in language so gentle it could have come from a grandmother: *the girl will want for nothing; the girl will be educated, sheltered, cherished.* It even offered, in passing, as if it were throwing in a set of knives with a house purchase, the restoration of small things I hadn't admitted I mourned — my chambers above the counting house, my standing at court, the particular nod the head steward gave me that meant *you are trusted here.*

And all it wanted was a child. A child who had already been carried, burned, hunted, and handed through the dark by people who loved her, to be delivered like a parcel to the thing wearing her kingdom's face.

"You hesitate," said the Hollow King, in my mother's voice.

It had started with my mother and worked outward. That was the cruelty of it — not that it spoke in the voices of the dead, but that it chose them in order. Mother first, because she was the oldest wound. Then Master Corvin, who'd taught me the taster's craft and died choking on a plum I should have tasted first. Then Odile's voice, which was a mistake on its part, because Odile was alive, and hearing her come out of that thing's mouth told me exactly how little it understood about what it was buying.

"I don't hesitate," I said. "I'm cataloguing."

"Cataloguing?"

"My mother has been dead nineteen years. She never once called me 'little ledger.' You've been reading my ledgers." I folded my hands, the way I do when I'm about to deliver an unfavorable audit. "Also, your offer of Wren's education is generous for someone planning to eat her. And you flinch when I say *eat*, which is interesting, because it means there's still something under there that knows the word for what you do."

The throne room was empty except for us and the candles, which had been burning without shortening for the entire conversation, wax pooling upward instead of down. The king's shape sat slouched in comfortable majesty, hands resting on the armrests, and it was a good shape — broad, silvering, kindly at the corners. But I had watched the real man eat breakfast every day for years, and I knew where his weight settled and where this thing let it drift. Grief does that to a body. So does having no body at all.

"Consider the arithmetic," it said, abandoning my mother's voice for something older and smoother, like river stones. "One child, against a kingdom. There has been no war in thirty years. No famine. The borders hold because the man on this throne frightens three nations at once. Give me the key, and I will keep giving you peace. Refuse, and you will learn what I am willing to break to take it anyway."

"You're right," I said. "That's good arithmetic."

Its head tilted. The real king had never tilted his head that far; something in his neck would have objected.

"But I keep books for a living," I went on, "and I'll tell you the first thing they teach you in the counting house: a sum can balance and still be a theft. Your peace isn't paid for. It's borrowed against a man you swallowed and a line of children you've been hunting since before this one was born. I don't invest in debts that big. They always come due at the worst hour, and I won't be holding the note."

"You will die badly," it said, and now the voice was its own, or near enough — a voice like a room after everyone has left, all echo and cooling air. "You understand that. You are choosing a poor death over a comfortable life, over a *safe* life, and the child will die too, later, messier, along with everyone you have dragged into her shadow. Isolde. Voss. The priestess, wherever she is rotting in my cells. All of it, because you would not hand over one small girl."

"Yes," I said.

"That is your refusal? One word?"

"It's the load-bearing word. Everything else is decoration." I bowed, shallow and insolent, the bow a food-taster gives a dish she intends to send back to the kitchens. "No. Find another broker. Oh — wait. You can't. The Guild doesn't sell living keys, and I'm the only fool who knows both your ledgers."

For a moment the crown did something I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be. It *leaned*. Not the head — the crown itself, the metal, tilting toward me like a dog scenting meat, and beneath the band of gold I saw, for half a heartbeat, the absence the shape was draped over. A hole in the world cut in the exact silhouette of a beloved man. Beautiful, Odile had warned me. Terrible, Odile had promised. Both true, and neither the half of it.

"You were offered everything," said the Hollow King, in my own voice now, which was the ugliest thing it could have done. "Remember that, Bram Almeida, when they come for you. Remember that you were offered everything, and you chose her."

Then it clapped its hands once, softly, like a man closing a very large book.

* * *

The unmaking of a royal appointment is, it turns out, mostly paperwork. I know that sounds like a joke, but I have rarely felt anything so much like being audited by an avalanche.

The herald read the proclamation in the outer court within the hour, because the Hollow King does not waste time between decision and consequence; hesitation is a human luxury and it gave up human luxuries thirty years ago. I heard it from the servants' stair, where I had gone to be sick, quietly and with dignity, after walking out of the throne room on legs that behaved better than they had any right to.

*Let it be known that the woman called Bram Almeida, sometime taster to His Majesty's table, stands accused of conspiracy against the crown, of trafficking in forbidden arts, and of the abduction of a minor of unknown parentage. Her patents of office are revoked. Her name is struck from the rolls of the palace. Let any who shelter her know they shelter a traitor, and let it further be known —*

Here the herald paused, and I learned afterward that he had been made to memorize the figure, and that he wept while doing it, and that he took the grief out on his apprentice that evening, which is how cruelty travels when it can't find its intended address.

*— that a bounty of four hundred crowns is set upon her, taken alive or otherwise.*

Four hundred crowns. I did the arithmetic on the stair, because arithmetic is where I go when I am frightened. Four hundred crowns was eleven years of a laborer's wages. It was enough to buy a modest house with a garden, enough to dower two daughters, enough that every guard, gate-keeper, guttersnipe, and greedy aunt in the city became, overnight, a locked door with my name on the other side of it. The Guild's ledgers priced betrayals precisely; I had recorded enough of them to know that four hundred crowns didn't buy a murder so much as a *market*. Somewhere in the lower city, within the week, someone would start a pool on which district produced my corpse.

Otherwise. *Taken alive or otherwise.* The otherwise was doing a lot of work in that sentence, and we both knew it. Alive, I might talk. Dead, I was simply tidy.

I stood on the stair and waited to feel ruined, and what I felt instead surprised me: relief. Thin, sharp, shameful relief — the kind you feel when a tooth that has ached for months finally cracks. Because it was done. The pretending was done. For years I had walked two lives, tasting poisons by morning and balancing impossible accounts by night, keeping every relationship at ledger's distance so that no one could ever be compromised by knowing me whole. Now the crown had done me the courtesy of burning both lives in one proclamation. I had no position, no name worth having, no safe bed in the city.

What I had was a child asleep under Odile's wards in the undercity, a priestess in the cells below my feet, a contract with terms I hadn't finished reading, and a debt I had just publicly, catastrophically refused to pay to the wrong party.

A woman could build a life on worse foundations. I had seen worse. I'd eaten worse.

* * *

They came for my rooms above the counting house that same night, which told me two things: the Hollow King kept its promises quickly, and somebody at court had always known exactly where I lived. I watched from a rooftop across the lane — old taster's habit, checking exits before I need them — as six men in plain coats with very official boots went through my belongings with the thoroughness of people looking for a reason as well as evidence.

They found the ledgers I had left behind, the decoy volumes, the ones with the boring entries: grain futures, memory-extraction fees, the going rate for a borrowed year. I watched an officer leaf through them by candlelight and saw his face do the slow collapse of a man realizing the numbers didn't resolve. Good. Let them spend a season trying to audit the Guild. It had confounded better accountants than a palace clerk, and it would confound them politely.

What they did not find, because I had carried it against my ribs for two days sewn into my coat, was the page of the original binding contract — Odile's page, the young king's signature still rust-brown on it, the terms I was only beginning to understand. Three things, Ninepence had rasped, before the fire took him. The word. The witness. The willingness of the bound. Two of three accounted for, if Wren's voice held and Cassia's faith did. The third was a conversation with whatever remained of a dead man inside a crown, and I did not yet know how to conduct it, only that it had to be conducted before solstice, which gave me nine days.

Nine days, four hundred crowns, and a city full of doors learning my name as a warning.

Below me, the officer in my rooms picked up the little brass bell I used to summon the counting-house boy, rang it once, frowned at the sound of it, and put it in his pocket. Petty theft from a traitor's goods. I found I minded that more than the rest, which tells you something about grief — it goes where it likes.

I thought about Wren, asleep somewhere below the street, smelling of snow and old languages, trusting me because I smelled of swallowed deaths. I thought about Isolde, whose careful maps of her father's wrongness had made an ally of me and a target of the crown; the proclamation hadn't named her yet, but *conspiracy against the crown* has wide margins, and the Hollow King wrote in them. I thought about Odile, who had gone very quiet since Ninepence burned, quieter than a woman with nothing to hide ever goes.

Everyone I loved was now standing inside the radius of my ruin. That was the true price on my head — not the four hundred crowns, but the fact that anyone who fed me, clothed me, or met my eyes too long in the market could expect to share the bill.

And I chose it anyway. I want that on the record, in plain ink, in my own hand: given everything back on one side of a scale and one sleeping child on the other, I weighed them honestly, as I weigh everything, and the child outweighed the world. Not because I am good. Because I have read enough ledgers to know that the entries you can't justify to yourself at midnight are the ones that bankrupt you, and I could justify this one.

* * *

I came down off the roof an hour before dawn, when the raiders had gone and the lane belonged to cats and fog. The counting house windows were dark, but the door above the Guild's back entrance — Odile's door — opened before I knocked twice, which meant she'd been watching the same rooftop I was standing on, or a different one, or had simply known, the way she always seemed to know, what the night's accounts would require.

She looked at me for a long moment in the doorway. Odile Voss is not a woman who wastes expressions; she keeps them the way the Guild keeps courage, in reserve, priced high. What crossed her face was old and tired and almost tender, and it frightened me more than the proclamation had.

"They struck your name from the rolls," she said.

"So I heard. From a stairwell. Very dramatic acoustics."

"Bram." My name, in her mouth, sounded different than it had yesterday. Heavier. Like a coin she was testing for weight. "Come inside. There are things you need to know before you trust me with her life, and I find—" She stopped. Started again, which Odile never does. "I find I cannot balance this entry alone anymore."

Behind her, in the dim of the passage, the master ledger lay open on its stand, though I had locked it shut, and the First Speech letters along its spine were glowing faintly, the way coals glow when breathed on.

Wren was awake. She was sitting beside the book, and she was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read — not fear, not quite. Recognition, maybe. Or warning.

"Odile," I said, stepping inside and letting the door close behind me on the whole city that now wanted me dead, "what did you sell the king, thirty years ago?"

She poured two glasses of wine with a steady hand, and did not answer, and did not drink, and the coal-light on the ledger's spine slowly went out.
