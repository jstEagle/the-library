## 22. Midnight Market

### Bram

There is a rule of thumb among Guild people: never buy anything at a market that can see you.

The Midnight Market broke that rule within the first ten steps. The stalls had eyes—some literal, stitched into awnings and blinking out of bread crusts—and every one of them tracked our little procession through the fog off the canal like cats watching an unsteady bird. Odile walked in front with her ledger chained to her wrist, which was either a precaution or a fashion statement; with Odile you genuinely could not tell. Wren had both her hands fisted in the back of my coat, which meant she was frightened, which meant I was permitted to be frightened too, by proxy. It's one of the few luxuries of being responsible for a child: your fear has somewhere to live.

"Keep your hood up," Odile murmured, without turning around. "Half the brokers here would sell your face to the palace for a season of good wine."

"The other half?"

"The other half already have."

The market lived in the bones of a drowned churchyard—the undercity floods twice a year, and the merchants simply moved their stalls onto the rooftops of the submerged crypts and called it waterfront property. Lanterns hung from rusted railings where gravestones used to stand. Somewhere below us, behind green glass, the actual dead were presumably annoyed about the foot traffic.

We were hunting a witness. Not the kind you hire for a contract dispute—a real one, the kind who could stand before a crown and swear truth into it so hard the truth stuck. Cassia, Ninepence had called her before he burned. Mother Cassia. Excommunicated for remembering too much. In the Guild's taxonomy, remembering too much is a disease, and excommunication is the quarantine.

"She'll be at the reliquary stalls," Odile said. "Priests always end up selling what they were sworn to protect. It's practically a vocational hazard."

"You say that like it's funny."

"I say it like it's true, Bram. Truth is funnier than jokes. That's why nobody laughs at it."

Wren tugged my coat. "What does a witness smell like?" she asked. She'd been asking smell-questions all week. She navigated the world by nose the way other children used maps, and she had informed me, gravely, that I smelled of swallowed deaths, that Odile smelled of ink and old apologies, and that the man who sold us eels yesterday smelled of lying, which had saved us three coppers.

"I don't know," I said. "Clean, maybe. Priests are supposed to smell clean."

"They're supposed to smell of incense," Odile corrected. "Which is a way of smelling clean without doing any of the work."

We found the reliquary row at the market's drowned heart, where the water came closest to the surface and lantern light turned everything the color of weak tea. Bones in jars. Saints' knuckles. A tooth that claimed to have bitten a god, priced optimistically. And at the end of the row, on a stall made from two overturned pews, a woman sat sorting rosaries with the brisk efficiency of a woman counting poison doses.

She looked up before we spoke. Her eyes went straight past Odile, past me, and settled on Wren with an expression I recognized because I wore it myself most days: the look of someone seeing something they'd been warned about in scripture.

"Well," said Mother Cassia. "You're either here to arrest me or to ruin me, and the guards never come down this far. So. Ruin, then."

"We're here to buy," said Odile.

"Everything's for sale." She set down the rosaries. "That's the whole tragedy of religion, dear. Ask me my rates."

### Wren

The priestess woman smelled of smoke and candle-fat and underneath that, faintly, of stone that had been prayed at for a very long time. Prayed-at stone smells like cold honey. Bram smelled like swallowed deaths. The eel man smelled like lying. The world was mostly smells and the smells were mostly sad, but the priestess smelled like a door that had been locked from the inside for years and years, and doors like that were interesting.

Also there was a jar on her stall with a saint's finger in it, and the finger was pointing at Wren, and Wren did not think fingers were supposed to do that, but she had learned not to say things like that out loud anymore because when she said things out loud sometimes locks opened and candles died and once a whole debt vanished, which sounded nice until you saw what vanishing did to the faces of grown-ups.

Bram was talking money-talk with Odile standing guard. Grown-ups thought children couldn't follow money-talk, which was insulting, because money-talk was just another language and Wren was very good at languages, apparently, though hers was older than kingdoms and got people hunted.

The priestess caught her looking at the finger.

"It does that," the priestess said quietly, when Bram and Odile were arguing about collateral. "Started last month. Points east, toward the palace. I've been meaning to ask it what it wants, but saints are terrible conversationalists."

Wren looked at the finger. The finger pointed at her. She put out her own hand, flat, the way you offered your hand to a horse.

The water under the stall went perfectly still.

"Don't—" the priestess started.

But Wren wasn't going to speak. She was only going to touch, and touching was safe, mostly, probably. Her fingertip met the glass.

Inside the jar, the finger curled, gently, the way a sleeping person curls toward warmth. And Wren heard—not with her ears, with the place behind her ears where words older than kingdoms lived—a sound like one word being said very patiently, over and over, the way you say a name outside a door:

*Soon. Soon. Soon.*

She took her hand back. "He says soon," she told the priestess, because the priestess had remembered too much already, so one more thing wouldn't kill her.

The priestess went white as bone meal. Then she laughed—one short, cracked note, like a bell with a crack in it, which fit.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I'm going to hell *efficiently*, then." She raised her voice. "Voss! Change of terms. I'm coming with you, and you're paying me nothing, and you're going to tell me everything."

### Isolde

I had followed them, of course.

Let no one tell you princesses cannot move quietly through the undercity. We are trained from birth to enter rooms unnoticed—it's called making an entrance, and the trick works just as well in reverse. My father's court taught me that a shadow properly placed is worth a regiment. What the court did not teach me, because the court does not know, is that my father's shadow moves wrong, and that I have spent eleven years mapping exactly how wrong.

I found them at the reliquary stalls, and I hung back in the colonnade of a drowned chapel to watch. This is not espionage, whatever Odile Voss would tell you. This is due diligence. There is a difference, and the difference is that espionage gets you executed and due diligence merely gets you disinherited, and I have decided to be optimistic.

They had found their priestess faster than I expected. Mother Cassia—I knew the name from the palace archives, from a file thin enough to be a deliberate insult: *excommunicated, cause unspecified, whereabouts unknown.* Unspecified causes in my father's reign always meant the same thing. Someone had seen something true.

The girl touched the relic jar and the priestess's face did something complicated, and then the tall one with the ledger—the Voss woman—turned sharply, scanning the colonnades, and I stepped out before she found me, because being found on purpose is the only way to control being found.

"Princess," Voss said. Her bow was technically flawless and spiritually contemptuous. A remarkable skill.

"Guildmistress. Taster." I nodded to Almeida, who watched me the way one watches weather. "Child."

"I'm Wren," said the child, which surprised everyone including, apparently, Wren.

"You shouldn't be here," Almeida said. Flat. Not rude. A statement of physics.

"And yet the physics of my life have become extremely flexible lately." I kept my voice level. Underneath it, my heart was doing something undignified. "My father's agents bought a memory from your Guild. A child's face. I traced the purchase. I've been tracing purchases for two years, Almeida—you'd be astonished what a princess can read if she reads the ledgers instead of the prayers. So let's not perform surprise. You need a witness qualified to stand before a crown. I am the only person alive who can open the throne room to one."

Cassia looked between us like a woman watching two knives decide whether they were scissors.

"And why," Almeida said slowly, "would you help us? If half of what we suspect is true, helping us costs you everything."

It was a fair question and deserved a fair answer, and I gave the only honest one I had.

"Because I want my father back," I said. "And if he cannot come back, then I want whoever is wearing him *gone*. Those are different wants. I have carried both for eleven years, and I am tired, and I am done pretending they weigh the same." I let the silence sit. "Also, I brought money, and I notice none of you have any."

"That's true," said Voss, with the serenity of a woman whose entire profession was other people's debts.

### Bram

We negotiated terms on the steps of a flooded crypt, which felt appropriately funereal for what we were planning. Cassia wanted full disclosure. Odile wanted plausible deniability. Isolde wanted a written oath, which amused Cassia enormously—"an oath, from the daughter of *that* crown"—until Isolde said, evenly, "My oaths are mine. His are his. That is rather the entire point," and Cassia stopped laughing and shook her hand.

Wren sat on the top step, feet dangling over black water, eating a candied plum some vendor had pressed on her for luck. Children absorb catastrophe the way sand absorbs rain; I envied it and grieved it in the same breath. Every adult at this negotiation had already paid for their seat in some currency or other. Hers was still in escrow. I intended to keep it that way if it cost me everything I hadn't already sold.

Which, taking inventory: my immunity, gone. My childhood home, gone. Most of my reasons, gone. My name, pledged against failure. I was running low on self, and the terrifying thing was how easy each withdrawal had been at the time. The Guild makes sacrifice feel like bookkeeping. That's its genius and its evil, and I include myself in that assessment, since I keep the books.

"Cassia," I said, when the others were haggling over route and timing. "Ninepence said your name before he burned. He said you were the only one who heard it—at the coronation. Something answering from inside the crown."

Her hands went still on her rosary. Around us the market roared on, indifferent: haggling, laughter, the slap of fish, someone singing a debt-song in the minor key the undercity uses for everything.

"I officiated," she said. "Thirty-one years ago. Young king, young queen, a kingdom desperate for peace after nine years of war. Standard rite. Crown of state, oil, the binding formula—we bind the monarch's private self to the public office, so the king can be cruel when cruelty is needed and merciful when mercy is, and never only one. It's old magic. Gentle magic. Or it was." She looked at the water. "When I spoke the words, something answered. From inside the crown. It said—" and here her voice did something I'd only ever heard in my own mouth, describing tastes that had nearly killed me— "*thank you.*"

"Thank you."

"Polite. Gracious. Delighted." Her jaw tightened. "An oath-eater doesn't get invited in, Almeida. It gets *thanked in*. Somebody wrote the invitation into the liturgy, and thirty thousand witnesses applauded it, and I was the only one who heard the door open. When I reported it, they called me mad. Then they called me heretical. Mad was kinder—they defrocked me for the madness and excommunicated me for insisting I wasn't mad." She smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile. "So yes. I will witness your unbinding. I have been waiting thirty-one years for someone to ask me the question I was excommunicated for answering."

Behind her, Wren stopped chewing.

"Bram," the girl said quietly. "The water's listening."

I felt it then—the particular pressure of attention, the sensation every taster learns, the feeling of being *sampled*. The black canal had gone glass-still in a circle around our steps. In its surface, faintly, lantern-light arranged itself into letters. Old ones. First Speech.

Odile saw it too. She went the color of vellum and began, of all things, to calculate—I could see her lips moving, pricing something.

"What does it say?" Isolde asked.

"I can't read it," I said. "But she can." I nodded at Wren, who was staring at the water the way one stares at a mirror.

Wren leaned forward. Read. Went pale. Then she did the bravest thing I saw anyone do in this entire affair, which was speak anyway.

"It says," she translated, small and clear, "*Found you.*"

### Odile

I have kept the Guild's books for nineteen years, and in nineteen years I have learned that every entry balances eventually, and that this is not comfort but horror. Tonight the entry balancing was mine.

While they argued about flight routes, I slipped away to the broker of records—a creature named Halfpenny's Ghost who dealt in information the way other dealers dealt in opium, in measured doses, always leaving you wanting more. I asked one question: *who else has been asking after Mother Cassia?*

The answer cost me a memory I'll miss: rain on the day my mother died, the last good thing attached to that day. Worth it. Because the answer was this: the crown's chamberlain had purchased a scrying contract at dusk. Target: the priestess. Payment rendered in advance. Which meant the Hollow King had not merely found us tonight.

It had *bought* us tonight. Through the Guild. Through channels I approved. Through me.

I stood in the fog with my ledger heavy on my wrist and understood, finally, what Ninepence had understood at the end, choosing fire over talk: the Guild serves whoever pays. It has no side. It has no loyalty. It is a knife that prides itself on never asking whose throat.

And I had built my entire penance on the fiction that managing the knife was the same as controlling it.

I returned to the steps. They were still arguing—Isolde wanted the river gate, Bram wanted the sewers, Cassia wanted wine—and I interrupted them the way you interrupt family, rudely and out of love.

"We leave now," I said. "Not tomorrow. Now. The crown has a scrying contract on Cassia, purchased through the Guild four hours ago, and the payment cleared, which means somewhere in this city there is a broker's apprentice reading our location out of a bowl of water at this exact moment." I looked at each of them. "And children, I know who countersigned it. Me. Nineteen years of habit. Which means the wards on my own safehouse will open for their warrant." I swallowed the thing that tasted like shame, catalogued it, moved on. Bookkeeping. "So we go somewhere even I can't betray. There's a debtor warren under the tanneries, third tier. No Guild access. People who traded their years don't register anywhere."

Bram looked at me for a long moment. She had every right to say it—to name what I'd done, what I'd been part of, what I'd sold. Instead she said:

"Then lead."

And that, I think, is the moment I stopped serving the Guild and started serving them. It happened that fast. Nineteen years, undone by four words and a woman with no name left to lose.

We moved. Behind us, in the black water, the lantern-light letters dissolved one by one, like a hand closing a book it had finished reading.

Above, far above, past the flood line, past the city, the palace lights burned steady and patient.

Something in the crown was smiling. I didn't need to see it. I've balanced its kind of accounts before.
