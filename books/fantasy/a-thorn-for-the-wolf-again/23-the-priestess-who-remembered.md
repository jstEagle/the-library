## 23. The Priestess Who Remembered

They came to me at dusk, which I took as a courtesy. Excommunicated priests are rarely consulted before dark; the Church prefers its heresies conducted by candlelight, where the shadows can be blamed for what is said.

I was mending a chasuble when the knock came—three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned patience. My door in the Weavers' Almshouse opens onto a courtyard where the sisters used to hang their washing. The sisters are gone now, scattered or silenced, and the washing line holds only my one good robe and the memory of better sermons.

I opened the door on a woman with a taster's careful hands and a child who looked at me the way I once looked at relics: as if I might be real.

"You're Mother Cassia," the woman said. Not asking. People who ask give you room to lie.

"I am what's left of her," I said, and stood aside to let them in, because I had already seen the third figure behind them—a broad-shouldered broker with ink-stained fingers and the particular stillness of someone carrying something breakable—and because thirty years of waiting teaches you to recognize the moment it ends.

* * *

You should understand what I remember, since remembering is my crime.

Thirty years ago I was young for my office and proud of it. They gave me the coronation of Aldric the Second because the old hierophant's gout had flared, and I stood before the high altar of the Cathedral of the Unbroken Oath with the crown of Vhalderia in my two hands, and I said the words every hierophant says: *By the consent of the line and the witness of the faith, receive the burden.*

And something answered.

Not aloud. That is the thing no one believes when I tell it, so mostly I don't tell it. It answered the way an echo answers from inside a well—except the well was the crown, and the echo came *before* my voice finished, arriving half a breath early, as though it had been waiting at the end of the sentence since before I began it.

*I accept,* it said. And then, softer, in a register like a struck bell heard through a wall: *He accepts too. He is glad to rest.*

I nearly dropped the crown. A young priest does not drop the crown of Vhalderia; she would be unmade for less. So I set it on the head of King Aldric, and I smiled, and I sang the recessional, and I told myself I had imagined it. Told myself for thirty years. That is the arithmetic of cowardice—one small denial, compounded daily, until the interest alone outweighs the principal.

The king looked at me afterward, at the feast, and thanked me for a beautiful ceremony. His eyes were kind and slightly distant, the way eyes are when the tenant has moved out and the landlord keeps the lamps lit. I told myself that too.

* * *

"Mother Cassia." The taster—Bram, Odile had called her, though the name sat oddly on her, like a coat borrowed from a larger woman—"we need a witness."

"For what?"

"For an unbinding," said the broker, Odile Voss, who had the decency not to dress it up. "Of the Hollow King. On solstice night, in the throne room, if our other arrangements hold. The rite requires three things: the word, the witness, the willingness of the bound. We have a candidate for the first." She nodded at the child. "We're working on the third. The second is you—or no one."

"And witnesses to unbindings," I said, "historically die."

Odile didn't insult me by pretending otherwise. "Historically," she agreed. "The Guild's records list eleven witnessed unbindings. Nine witnesses died. One went mad. One—" she paused, consulting some inner ledger, "—one simply stopped being remembered, which we've come to consider a mercy."

"A mercy," I repeated. "How generous of your profession to have opinions on mercy."

"We sell everything else. Opinions come free."

I liked her immediately, which surprised me. I had expected merchants of the impossible to be either sinister or sad, and here was a woman who was both and had made peace with it, which is a kind of holiness, if you squint.

The child had not spoken. She stood by my little fire with her hands out to the warmth, and when I looked at her properly—at the strange pale quality of her attention, the way the flames seemed to lean toward her rather than away—I felt the hair rise on my arms the way it had risen in the cathedral thirty years ago.

"She heard it too," I said suddenly. "Didn't she. Whatever answered from the crown. She's been hearing it her whole life."

Bram went very still. "What makes you say that?"

"Because she flinched when you said 'unbinding.' Not at the word itself. At the silence after it." I crossed to the girl and knelt, slowly, the way one kneels before anything that might be sacred or might be frightened—there is rarely a difference. "Child. What do they call you?"

"Wren," she said. Her voice was small and roughened, a voice used to disuse. Then, looking at me with those too-old eyes: "You were there. At the beginning. You smell like the cathedral."

"I do?"

"Like stone and incense and a promise that broke." She tilted her head. "It remembers you. The thing in the crown. It's been listening for you ever since. That's why they took your voice from the Church—not because you talked, but because you *heard*."

Well. Thirty years of exile summarized by an eight-year-old in four sentences. I confess I laughed—a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a woman discovering her life's humiliation was actually her life's qualification.

"They defrocked me for heresy," I said. "Do you know what the heresy was? I wrote to the Synod that the coronation oath had been answered by a second voice. One letter. One observation. They stripped my orders, my name from the rolls, my right to administer any sacrament." I shook my head. "They couldn't tolerate the possibility. Because if I was right, then every coronation they'd ever blessed might have been a haunting, and the whole edifice of the faith would have to admit it had been officiating at its own deception."

"The faith would rather be wrong comfortably than right catastrophically," Odile said. "We'd have charged them for the sentiment."

* * *

Here is what I did that night, after they left—or rather, here is what I had already done, years ago, without knowing I was doing it.

I had written it all down.

Under the floorboard beneath my cot there is a box, and in the box are thirty years of testimony: the coronation, the voice, the half-breath-early answer, the king's lamp-lit absent eyes, and every anomaly I gathered afterward from the edges of court gossip—the king who never aged past a certain stillness, the decrees signed in handwriting that drifted subtly leftward over decades, the queen who died conveniently quiet. I wrote it all in a cipher of my own devising, because a priestess who remembers too much learns to hide her memory where the Inquisition doesn't look: in plain sight, disguised as devotional marginalia, tucked into psalters, sewn into hems.

I am not brave. Let that be understood. I spent thirty years being a coward with excellent documentation. But there is a difference between courage and readiness, and readiness, accumulated long enough, begins to look like courage from the outside. When Bram and Odile returned the next morning and asked again—formally this time, with the Guild's own ritual gravity, Odile reciting terms while Wren watched me with those leaning-toward-the-fire eyes—I found that my answer had been sitting in a box under my cot for three decades, waiting only to be asked for.

"Yes," I said. "I will witness."

Odile's pen hovered. "You understand the mortality rate."

"I understand that nine people died doing what I'm about to do, and that each of them bought the world something with it. That is not a mortality rate, Mistress Voss. That is a martyrology. And I have spent thirty years reading about saints while being forbidden to join them." I folded my hands, the old gesture, muscle-deep. "Besides. You misunderstand the theology of witnessing, I think. A witness does not stand apart from the event. A witness stands *inside* it, holding the door open so the truth can leave the room alive. If the truth costs the doorkeeper her life, that is not tragedy. That is the fee."

Wren spoke then, quietly, from the corner. "It will try to eat you."

"I know."

"It ate the others. It eats memories first. It starts with—" she frowned, searching, "—with the warmest ones. The sweetest. It takes those so you'll chase them into it."

"Then it will find mine well-defended," I said. "I have spent thirty years praying the office of remembrance daily. Every psalm, every canticle, every name of every person I have ever buried, recited in order, twice a day, like a merchant counting stock." I smiled, and felt the smile do something unfamiliar—reach my eyes. "Let it come into a mind kept like a ledger, child. Let it try to steal from a woman who has already given everything away and catalogued the giving."

Bram looked at me then with an expression I recognized, because I had worn it myself in another life: the look of someone watching a person walk toward a fire and understanding, helplessly, why they must.

"Why?" she asked. Simply. "Why agree? You owe none of us anything. The kingdom exiled you."

I considered lying to her—some pious answer about duty and faith—but she had earned better, and besides, I was tired of piety. Piety is what cowards call their fear when they've dressed it up.

"Because I heard it answer," I said. "Because I set the crown on his head knowing something was wrong, and I let the moment pass, and thirty years of everything that followed grew from that one swallowed word. Every execution, every vanished minister, every quiet death—there is a thread running back to my silence, and I am at the end of it, holding it." I looked at my hands, the chasuble-mending hands, the hands that had held the crown. "A witness who stays silent becomes an accomplice. I have been an accomplice for thirty years. I would like, before I die—and I will die someday regardless, whether by the Hollow King's appetite or by ordinary time—to be a witness again. In full. Out loud. Holding the door."

The room was quiet. Somewhere in the almshouse courtyard, wind moved the empty washing line, and my good robe lifted on it like a sail, like a wing, like a white flag run up in surrender to something that was not defeat.

Wren crossed the room and put her small hand flat against my knee. Warmth went through me like sunlight through glass—no, not warmth. *Recognition.* As if something in me that had been speaking a dead language alone for thirty years had finally been answered in kind.

"You'll do," she said gravely. "It's afraid of you already."

"It should be," said Odile, making her final entry. "Witness confirmed. Terms accepted. Fee—" she glanced up at me, and for the first time the broker's composure flickered into something almost human, "—payable in whatever survives."

"Payable in whatever survives," I agreed. "That has always been the Church's price too. We just never printed it in the contract."

* * *

They left before dawn, as fugitives must. But I did not go back to bed. I took up my box of testimonies, all thirty years of it, and I carried it to the courtyard, and I fed it page by page to the brazier, because a witness's evidence belongs in the witness, not in paper that can be seized—and because I wanted the last thing the Hollow King's agents might find in my rooms to be ash.

The smoke rose straight in the still air. I watched it and prayed the old office, the one for the dead and the not-yet-dead alike, and when I reached the line that names the departed, I added, for the first time, my own name—Cassia, daughter of no one, keeper of a broken promise—spoken aloud in the empty courtyard, witnessed by God, the washing line, and one stray cat with an air of professional skepticism.

Nine witnesses dead. One mad. One forgotten.

I intend to complicate the statistics.

Solstice comes in six days. Somewhere beneath the city, a child is learning to speak the language that broke the world, a taster is learning to be mortal, and a princess is opening gates that have been shut for thirty years. And somewhere above us all, in a crown that no longer fits the head beneath it, something ancient is listening for the sound of my voice—

—and finding, to its considerable displeasure, that I have never once stopped talking to God.
