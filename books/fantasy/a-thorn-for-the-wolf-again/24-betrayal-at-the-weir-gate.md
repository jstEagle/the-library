## 24. Betrayal at the Weir Gate

The debtor's name was Ferren Hale, and I should have known better, because I had balanced his account myself.

That was the thing about the undercity — you learned to read people the way Odile read ledgers, and I'd read Ferren's page a dozen times. Forty-one years old. Sold eleven years of his life to the Guild to pay his mother's debts, then another three for a coat that didn't leak. He lived in a borrowed decade and smiled like a man who had made peace with the arithmetic. When he'd offered to guide us through the weirs — the old flood-gates beneath the river district where the crown's writ ran thin — I'd believed him the way you believe a well-thumbed book.

He'd even refused payment. That should have been my first warning. Nobody in the Guild quarter refuses payment. Refusing payment is how you make yourself a gift, and gifts are always given with a hook inside them.

We moved before dawn, five of us: me, Wren with her hand fisted in the back of my coat, Odile moving with surprising grace for a woman who claimed her knees were older than the kingdom, Mother Cassia in her plain gray traveling dress with her excommunication papers folded into a hatband, and Ferren out front carrying a lantern shuttered down to a coin of light.

The weirs were beautiful in the way that only abandoned civic machinery is beautiful. Vast iron gates, green with river-slime, standing open on chains thick as my waist. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a metronome. The air tasted of rust and old stone and, faintly, of nothing at all — which in the undercity is its own flavor, the taste of places where time has been traded away so often it's gone thin.

"Two more gates," Ferren said, voice pitched low. "Then the smugglers' stair comes up behind the tanneries. Crown agents don't bother with the tanneries. Too much stink for their gloves."

Wren tugged my sleeve. She couldn't speak — not safely, not since the cell-blocks, not since she'd rattled every lock in the palace with one sentence — but she'd developed a whole grammar of tugs and looks. This tug meant *I don't like him*. She'd been giving it since the second gate.

I gave her the look back that meant *neither do I, but he knows the way*.

She gave me the look that meant *that's what everyone says right before they die in stories*, and I decided, not for the first time, that an eight-year-old raised on First Speech and fragments of a burning tower had no business being that correct.

It was Cassia who felt it first. She stopped mid-stride, one hand rising, and said, quietly, "Someone has prayed here recently."

"Ferren prays," Odile said. "He told us. Morning and night. Very devout."

"Not recently," Cassia said. "Here. Recently." Her hand found the wall, pressed flat against the stone as if taking its pulse. "There's a residue. A blessing laid over this ground like fresh paint. Someone stood in this exact corridor and asked the Ninefold to make their path straight, and the Ninefold answered, because someone always answers when the asker means it."

Ferren's lantern did not waver. I'll give him that. His hand was steadier than mine would have been.

"Meaning what?" I said, though meaning what was already assembling itself in my chest with the cold certainty of a poison taking hold.

"Meaning," Odile said, in the flat voice she used for reading out debts, "that our guide sold us. Two days ago, by the freshness of it. Probably from a shrine alcove near the third weir. Probably to a crown agent who promised him something worth more than whatever we were paying him." She turned. "Which was nothing. Which was the hook."

Ferren set the lantern down on the wet stone, very carefully, the way a man sets down a thing he doesn't want to break, and looked at me with eyes that were genuinely sorry.

"They have my daughter," he said. "You remember. Page forty-seven of your master ledger, Bram Almeida. Collateral held against my balance. They've held her four years. The agent showed me her handwriting yesterday. She's grown."

I did remember. Of course I remembered. I had entered the collateral myself, in my own neat hand, back when I still believed the Guild's cruelty was merely administrative.

"I'm sorry," Ferren said, and he meant it, and it changed nothing. "They're already in the tannery yard. There were never any smugglers' stairs. I'm sorry, Bram. Truly. But there are two things a man can't be sorry enough to refuse, and one of them is his daughter's name written in their book instead of his."

The lantern went out.

Not blown — snuffed, all at once, the flame simply ceasing, and in the dark I heard the sound I will hear until I die: boots. Many boots. Coming up the flooded stair behind us and down the maintenance gallery ahead, closing the corridor at both ends with the tidy efficiency of a ledger being balanced.

"Wren," I said, and her hand found mine in the black.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and mostly badly, which is the honest way to describe most heroism.

Odile moved first — Odile always moves first, guilt makes a person quick — shoving a vial into my hand by touch alone. "Lightburst. Throw it at the far gate, not the near one. They'll expect near."

I threw it far. The world went white and screaming, and in the glare I saw them: crown agents in gray-and-gold, a dozen at least, shields up, and behind them a figure in civilian wool with the patient face of a Guild broker gone respectable. Pellam Rooke. I'd processed his commissions for years. He'd bought memory-extractions from us the way other men bought wine.

"There!" Rooke's voice, pleasant as ever. "The child! Take the child alive, the priestess if convenient, the others as they come!"

*As they come.* As if we were weather.

We scattered, because scattering was the only option left. Odile hauled Wren toward a maintenance crawl I hadn't seen; I grabbed Cassia; and for six glorious seconds we were winning, or at least losing slower.

Then a chain came off its winch.

Whether it was cut or released I never learned. It fell across the corridor between me-with-Wren-and-Odile and me-with-Cassia like a drawn line in a contract, and the agents poured over it from the far side, and Cassia pushed me.

She pushed me, an old woman half my size, with both hands, and said, "Go. You're the witness's escort, not the witness. They need me. They don't need you yet. Go be needed later."

"Cassia—"

"Bram Almeida." She said my name the way she said prayers, with full attention. "I have spent thirty years remembering too much and being punished for it. Do not take this from me. This is the first useful thing my memory has ever bought anyone." And she turned, straightened her gray dress, and walked toward the agents with her hands open at her sides, a priestess going to meet whatever god was on duty.

They took her gently. That was the worst part. No violence, no shouting. Rooke himself bowed to her, actually bowed, and said something I couldn't hear over the water and the ringing in my ears, and she went between them like a queen entering exile, and the last thing I saw was her looking back once — not at me, past me, at Wren, hidden in the dark — and nodding. Once. As if confirming a reservation.

Then the chain-gate clanged down and the dark took her, and I was running, and Wren was crying without any sound at all, which is somehow worse than the loud kind, and Odile was swearing in three dead languages with real fluency.

* * *

We surfaced an hour later through a coal chute behind a chandler's shop, filthy, soaked to the knee, and alive, which Odile pronounced "an interim condition."

I sat on a barrel of tallow candles and shook. Not from cold. From arithmetic. Because my mind, curse it, is a ledger whether I want it to be or not, and it had already run the numbers: Cassia captured. Route burned. The Hollow King now knew not only where we were but who we had — it had Rooke, it had Ferren's testimony, it had everything except Wren herself. Every plan we'd built assumed a witness qualified to stand before a crown. We had traded our witness for our escape, and escape, I noted grimly, was not on the list of things the unbinding required.

"We go back," I said.

Odile stopped wringing out her sleeve. "No."

"We go back."

"Bram." She crouched in front of me, and in the chandler's dim light her face was all angles and exhaustion. "Listen to me as your mentor, as your creditor, and as the only person here old enough to say this plainly. What you're feeling is loyalty, and loyalty is the most expensive commodity the Guild trades, and we do not stock it. Cassia knew the risk. Witnesses to unbindings historically die — she told us that herself, in her own church voice, and she came anyway. If we go back, we trade three lives for one, and the one we'd be trading is the key to everything. The girl is the word. You are—"

"Don't say it."

"—the willingness," she said anyway. "And I am the terms. Between the three of us we hold the entire contract, and you want to spend it on a kind impulse."

"It isn't an impulse." My voice came out lower than I intended. "Odile. She pushed me out of the way. She looked at Wren and nodded, like — like handing something over. Like entrusting it. You don't leave a person in the cells of the Hollow King after they've done that. You don't. If we abandon her, then everything we've told ourselves about why we're doing this — that it's about the king, the war, the oath, all of it — it's a lie. It's just survival wearing a cause as a coat. And I have worn a lot of coats, Odile. I know the fit."

Wren tugged my sleeve. I looked down. She was holding up her hands, shaping shapes in the air: a box. A small door. Then she pointed at herself, then at the shapes, then pressed her fist to her chest.

"She says," Odile translated softly, "that she was in a tower once, locked away, and no one came back for her. Or — no. Not quite. She says someone was supposed to come back for her, and she has been waiting eight years to find out what kind of person does."

The chandler's shop was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a cart passed, ordinary as bread.

"So we're idiots," Odile said at last, to the ceiling, to the Ninefold, to the general management of the universe. "Fine. We're idiots. But we are not idiots the obvious way. We do not march into the palace cells. We find out where they're keeping her, and Rooke — Rooke is Guild-trained, and Guild-trained men can be bought, because that is the entire tragedy of our profession. Give me until tomorrow's low bell." She fixed me with a look that had priced funerals before. "And Bram. Whatever happens — if it goes wrong, if they take you — you get Wren out. Swear it on your name. It's the only collateral you have left, and I intend to collect."

"I swear it on my name," I said, and felt the words settle into the ledger of me somewhere, heavy as a debt.

* * *

Odile went out into the city and came back at low bell with news that tasted like copper.

"They're not holding her in the palace," she said. "Too risky, with Isolde turning guards. They've got her at the Weir Gate itself — the upper gatehouse, the one the crown seized during the flood works thirty years ago. Rooke's idea. He told the agents it's defensible. But I know Rooke, and Rooke doesn't think in defensibility, he thinks in symbolism." She let that sit. "A binding needs a place where oaths were sworn. The weirs were consecrated ground before the crown took them. They're going to question her there because the Hollow King wants to eat her memories somewhere holy — somewhere the eating will stick."

"Tonight?"

"Tomorrow night. Solstice eve. They'll move her to the palace for the solstice, when the crown's power thins—" She stopped. Blinked. "When the crown's power *thins*. Bram. They're frightened of the solstice. They're keeping her close to consecrated stone until they can move her somewhere the King can work on her properly. Which means tonight is the only night she is exactly where we know her to be."

I was already pulling my coat on. Wren was already pulling hers. Odile looked at the two of us, sighed the sigh of a woman whose guilt has excellent posture, and reached for her satchel of vials.

"One condition," she said. "If it goes wrong — and it will, Bram, plans made in chandler's shops always go wrong — you swear the oath again. Louder."

"I swore it an hour ago."

"Swear it again. Some entries bear repeating."

So I swore it again, on my name, in a room that smelled of tallow, while outside the solstice moon rose thin as a paring over the rooftops, and the three of us went down into the dark toward the weirs where we had almost died that morning, because a woman who remembered too much had pushed me clear and gone to stand in my place, and I had finally learned the difference between a price and a cost.

Price is what you agree to. Cost is what it turns out to be.

Cassia's price had been her safety. The cost, I was beginning to understand, was going to be mine — and I paid it gladly, walking back into the trap with my hand on a child's shoulder and my name in my mouth like a coin I was saving to spend.

The gatehouse lamps were lit ahead of us. Four of them. Where there should have been none.

And in the space between the third lamp and the fourth, someone was waiting who should not have been there — someone whose silhouette I knew better than my own reflection, because I had watched it across a breakfast table for thirty years.

The Hollow King had come to the weirs personally.

And he was smiling.
