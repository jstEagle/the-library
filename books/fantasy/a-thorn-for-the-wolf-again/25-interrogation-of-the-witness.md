## 25. Interrogation of the Witness

They took my rosary at the gate, which I expected, and my shoes, which I did not. Apparently excommunicated priestesses are considered a flight risk even barefoot in a dungeon. There is a compliment buried in that somewhere, and I intend to dig it out when I am feeling less bruised.

The cell was clean. That was the first wrong thing. Palaces do not keep clean cells; dungeons are supposed to fester, to soften a prisoner's resolve with damp and despair before the questioning ever begins. This one had a dry cot, a blanket that smelled of cedar rather than mildew, and a bucket that had been scoured within an inch of its life. Someone wanted me comfortable. In my experience, comfort offered by powerful people is never charity. It is either bait or anesthesia.

I sat on the cot, folded my hands, and began the Litany of Unmercy, which is the prayer we say for enemies. It goes on for some time. That is rather the point of it.

He came at midnight. Of course he came at midnight. Things that wear crowns have a flair for the theatrical hour.

I heard him before I saw him—not footsteps but a kind of hush, as though the corridor were holding its breath. Then the door opened without a key turning, and there stood my king, or the shape my king had been wearing for thirty years, holding a small table under one arm and two chairs under the other.

"You'll forgive the accommodations," he said, setting them up with the ease of a man who had done this ten thousand times. "I find conversations go better across a table. Something about facing one another. It civilizes us."

"I wasn't aware we required civilizing," I said. "Your Majesty."

"Please." He sat, arranging his robes with a courtier's precision. "Sit. I've brought tea."

There was, in fact, tea. A pot of it, porcelain painted with cornflowers, two cups. He poured with steady hands. I have watched poisoners pour with steadier hands than honest men, so I watched very carefully indeed, and what I noticed was this: the steam rose from the pot, but not from his cup. Not a wisp.

"I won't drink anything you've touched," I said.

"Nobody does anymore." He said it lightly, but something moved underneath the lightness, like a fish under ice. "It's one of the small griefs of office. Sit, Mother Cassia. Or stand and pray at me, if you prefer. I'm told you're good at that."

I sat. Pride is a sin, and also, my knees had opinions about stone floors.

"You remember my coronation," he said.

"I officiated it," I said. "I remember everything about it."

"That," he said, "is precisely the problem."

* * *

Let me tell you what it is to be remembered *at* by a thing like him, because the histories will get it wrong. They will say he questioned me, and that I refused to answer, and they will make it sound like a debate conducted in words.

It was not a debate. It was an appetite.

He asked his first question—something innocuous, about the order of the rite—and while he asked it, I felt something begin to *pull*. Not on my arms or my hair. On the past itself. It was as if a hand had reached into a drawer of my life and begun riffling through it: the smell of incense, my mother's hands kneading bread, the exact blue of the sky over the seminary the day I took my vows. All still there, but *loosened*, like knots working free of rope. He was not asking to hear my answers. He was asking to loosen my memories so he could swallow them whole.

The coronation came loose first. Thirty years of it, suddenly slippery—the weight of the crown in my hands, the oil on the young king's forehead, the moment I leaned close to whisper the blessing and heard something *answer* from inside the gold. That memory was the reason I had been excommunicated, the reason I had agreed to witness an unbinding that would likely kill me, and it was sliding away from me like a fish returning to deep water.

So I prayed. Not because I am holy—I have been assured otherwise by an authority higher than any bishop—but because prayer is the one discipline I know that cannot be done halfway. You cannot pray with a distracted mind. The Litany of Unmercy has forty-one names of God in it, each one a syllable you must place correctly, like stones in a wall, and while your mouth is building that wall, there is no room left for anything else to be taken.

The pulling stuttered. The coronation settled back into me with an almost audible thump.

He set down his cup. For the first time, the king's face did something the king's face had never done in thirty years of portraiture: it showed effort.

"The Litany," he said. "Clever. Most people try to think of something else. Something pleasant. You cannot outrun me toward pleasure, Mother Cassia; I am made of pleasant things. But liturgy—" He tilted his head, studying me the way a jeweler studies a stone for flaws. "Liturgy has no gaps. Whoever wrote your prayers knew what I am."

"My tradition predates your kingdom by some centuries," I said. "We have always been an anxious people."

* * *

He tried again after that, and again, and each time I learned a little more about the mechanism, the way you learn a lock by listening to it fail.

He could not take what was anchored. That was the first law of it. Memories fused to habit—prayers, litanies, the sequence of the mass, the feel of a chalice's rim—those held fast, because they were not stored as pictures but as motion, as muscle, as something closer to breath than thought. He could take a sunset. He could not take the Sign of the Cross.

The second law: he could not take what I refused to value. He went fishing for my greatest shame once—he found it quickly enough, a sin from my youth involving another novice and a stolen bottle of sacremental wine, nothing scandalous enough for the ballads but heavy enough to carry—and the moment he gripped it, it turned to ash in his hands. Not because I had made peace with it. Because I had carried it so long it had become structural. Remove the sin, and the woman collapses. Some part of me, deeper than vanity, knew that, and defended it.

"Interesting," he murmured, the third time he came up empty. "You are mostly grit, aren't you. Most minds are soft through the middle. Yours is soft at the edges and stone all the way down."

"I was told at my final examination that I was difficult to digest," I said. "The examiner meant it as criticism."

"And yet here you are, in my palace, having gotten yourself arrested on purpose." He leaned back. The candle between us guttered, though there was no draft. "Tell me why, and perhaps we needn't do this the hard way. What did Bram Almeida promise you? Absolution? The Guild's ledgers are very generous these days. Or was it simply the old thrill? A woman cast out of her church, given one last chance to matter?"

Here is what I should have said: nothing. Silence is armor, every text agrees.

But I am seventy-one years old, excommunicated, sitting barefoot in a clean cell across from an appetite wearing my king's face, and I have earned the right to be reckless in at least one direction.

"You want to know why I came," I said. "Because you answered."

The room went very still. Even the candle flame stopped moving, which I am fairly certain flames are not permitted to do.

"At the coronation," I continued, "I whispered the blessing into the king's ear—*receive the crown as a trust, not a possession*—and something inside that gold said, quite clearly, though no one else heard it: *there is no one left to receive it.* And I have spent thirty years knowing that the man I crowned died before the wine was consecrated, and that everyone in that cathedral knelt to a hollow, and that I said nothing, because I was young and afraid and the alternative was unthinkable. So when a taster and a child came to my door speaking of unbindings, Your Majesty, I did not come for absolution. I came because I have owed that boy in the crown a funeral for thirty years, and I intend to see it delivered."

He looked at me for a long moment. And then he did the most frightening thing he had done all night.

He laughed. Genuinely. Warmly. With the king's own laugh, the one from the early years, the one that used to make crowds love him.

"Oh," he said, wiping his eye with one elegant finger. "*Oh.* Do you know, in thirty years, no one has spoken to me of him? The real one? His wife forgot within the year—that was my doing, a mercy, I told myself—and Isolde was an infant, and the court simply... slid over the absence, the way water slides over a sunk stone. You are the only person alive who mourns him, Mother Cassia. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Tiresome," I said.

"*Necessary,*" he said, and leaned forward, and now the warmth was gone from his voice, replaced by something older and hungrier and—for the first time—uncertain. "You are the last knot tying him to the world. Every memory of him lives in exactly one skull, and I am looking at it. If I eat you clean, there will be nothing left of Aldric but me. No remnant to consent. No fragment to grieve. The binding will hold forever, because a binding needs a bound thing, and you—" he pointed one royal finger at my chest— "are carrying the last of him in that stubborn, gritty, undigestible heart."

And that is when I understood, in the way one understands things at three in the morning in a cell, suddenly and all at once and with cold running down the spine:

He was not interrogating me to break me.

He was interrogating me to find out how much time he had.

A thing that fears nothing does not bring tea. A thing that fears nothing does not explain itself. And a thing that fears nothing certainly does not sit across from an old woman in a clean cell and ask, in eleven different courteous ways, whether anyone else knows what she knows.

"You're afraid of me," I said.

"I have ended wars," he said mildly.

"Not that. Not armies. You've eaten armies." I stood, slowly, knees complaining, and looked down at the thing in my king's shape, and thirty years of dread turned over in me and showed its other face. "You can take memories. You can wear shapes. You can answer questions before they're asked. But you cannot bear to be *seen*. Truly seen—witnessed, named, known down to the seams. That is why you erased the queen's grief. Why the cells are clean. Why nobody speaks of the old king. You have spent thirty years making sure no one ever looks at you directly, because a thing like you, seen truly by someone who understands what they're seeing—"

"—may be unmade by it," he finished.

Silence. Somewhere far above, a guard coughed.

"Yes," he said quietly. "That is the flaw in my construction, isn't it. An oath-eater that can be witnessed. My maker had a sense of humor." He rose, gathered the tea things with those too-steady hands, and paused at the door. "Kill the liturgy if you like, Mother Cassia. Starve yourself. Go mad. It won't matter. Solstice is two nights off, and I will have the girl, and the word, and no witness fit to stand against me, because the only witness who remembers my king will be a pleasant blank spot in a clean cell."

"Perhaps," I said.

He left the way he'd come, on a tide of hush.

* * *

I sat back down on the cot and did not sleep. Instead I took inventory, the way our novices are taught to examine their consciences: item by item, without flinching.

What he had taken from me, in all that rifling: almost nothing. A few hours here and there. The name of my childhood dog. The taste of a particular soup. Small coins from the edges of my purse; he had been unable to reach the purse itself.

What he had shown me, without meaning to: his fear, his timeline, and the shape of his desperation.

And what I now knew that I had not known at sundown: Bram and Wren would come for me. Of course they would. Bram Almeida had traded away her immunity and her childhood home and finally her own name for that child, and a woman who pays that much does not leave her priestess in a cell. Which meant they would come straight down the throat of the palace, into the teeth of the thing that had just told me, in detail, exactly how it planned to end them.

Unless they came *smart*. Unless they came knowing what I now knew—that the Hollow King's strength and weakness were the same door, and that door was witness.

I got up off the cot, knelt on the clean stone floor—it was, I reflected, the first time in decades the palace had given me a surface worth kneeling on—and I prayed. Not the Litany of Unmercy this time, but something older, something from before my church, a witnessing-prayer that the seminary doctors called dangerous and I had always called thorough: the rite in which the one who prays becomes, herself, a record. A living ledger. Every truth held in the body, unerasable, because it is not stored in memory at all but written into breath and bone.

If he could eat memory, then I would stop keeping mine where he could reach it.

Somewhere above me, boots crossed a floor. Two nights, he had said.

Outside my little barred window, the snow had begun again, falling soft and indifferent over the palace, the city, the Guild quarter, and whatever cellar held a taster, a child, and a plan that did not yet know it needed me.

Hold on, children, I thought, forehead to the stone. Your witness is learning the enemy's grammar. And I promise you, on everything he failed to digest—

I intend to be very, very difficult to forget.
