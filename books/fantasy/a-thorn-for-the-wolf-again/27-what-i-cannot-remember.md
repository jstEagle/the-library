## 27. What I Cannot Remember

The journals were in a locked drawer, which was the first insult. I didn't have the key. I had to pick it with a hairpin and a fork tine, breaking into my own belongings like a burglar with poor self-esteem.

There were seven of them. Cheap paper, cheap binding, water damage on two, a wine ring on one that I resented on principle because I couldn't remember drinking the wine. My handwriting — I recognized it the way you recognize your own face in an old photograph, that shape is familiar but the person wearing it is a stranger who hasn't met you yet.

I sat down at Odile's back-room table with all seven books and a pot of tea I could not taste, and I began reading my own life as evidence.

*Day one,* said the first journal, in handwriting more hopeful than anything I currently produced. *Took the position at court. Pay is obscene. The work is to eat well and not die, which I have been training for my whole life whether I knew it or not.*

There followed eleven pages of recipes. Then, tucked between a note about almond flour and a sketch of the serving protocol for the west table, this: *Mother would have wept to see me at a palace table. Or laughed. Depends which mother. Both, probably.*

I stopped there. I did not know who "both" meant.

This is what I had traded, six days ago, in the Guild vault, to get past the wards around the original binding contract: my childhood home. Not the building — the whole country of it. The smell of whatever my mother's kitchen smelled like. The names. The reason a girl from wherever-I-was-from decided her life's work would be swallowing poison on behalf of strangers.

I remembered that I had parents. I remembered it the way you remember a fact from a ledger: two entries, mother and father, closed accounts, no balance carried forward. What I did not have was a single image. I reached for a face and found the shape where a face should be, like a coin rubbed smooth.

So: evidence. I am a taster. Tasters work from evidence.

* * *

The first journal gave me a mother twice — once wept over, once laughed at, both in past tense even then — and a father exactly once, on page thirty: *Father says a talent for survival is still a talent. He says it like it's a consolation prize.*

I read that sentence four times. It told me things I hadn't known I needed to know. That I'd been ambitious and slightly ashamed of it. That someone had loved me in the specific disappointing way of a parent watching their child become useful instead of happy. That I had gone to court anyway, hungry enough to call survival a calling.

Good. One reconstructed motive. I made a note in the margin, my current handwriting next to my old one, like two strangers corresponding.

The second journal covered years three through five at court. Here the entries thinned out, which told its own story — either life got busier or hope got quieter, and in my experience those arrive together. But between the poisonings survived (arsenic, Tuesday; something herbal and clever, Thursday) there were fragments:

*Odile says the Guild never asks why. I think that's why I like her.*

*I tasted for the princess today. She's four. She asked me if the food ever fights back. I said every meal is a battle and she promoted me to general. Best title I've held.*

Isolde. That was Isolde, before she grew into a woman who mapped her father's wrongness on scraps of hidden paper. I had known her at four. I had been her general. And now when I stood across from her in safehouses planning treason, I felt only professional respect for a capable ally, warm as a handshake. There had apparently once been jam involved. There was no way to miss the jam.

I put the tea down because my hands wanted to do something and shaking them wasn't productive.

* * *

Wren found me there sometime past midnight. I heard her come in — she moved quietly but never secretly, not with me, which I had decided sometime in the undercity was one of the great luxuries of my life, though I could no longer account for when that decision had been made or by whom.

She climbed onto the bench across from me, looked at the spread of journals, at my margin notes, at my face.

"You're reading yourself," she said.

"I'm auditing myself. There's a difference. Reading is for pleasure."

"What did you find?"

"A woman who liked her work," I said. "A woman with a mother who laughed. A woman who was somebody's general." I turned the second journal toward her, to the princess entry. "Apparently I was fun once. There's documentation."

Wren read it. Her mouth did something complicated. She was nine now — nearly ten, she'd insist — and she had spent the last months learning to be the keeper of a fugitive household: counting supplies, mending, watching doors. Watching me, especially, since the vault. She had never said so, but I'd caught her doing it, the particular vigilance of a child keeping inventory of an adult who is coming apart in pieces she can see.

"Tell me about the jam," she said.

"There is no jam. There's a reference to jam. The jam itself was sold."

"To the Guild."

"To the Guild," I confirmed. "Along with the rest of the pantry."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she slid off the bench, fetched her own satchel — the small battered one she carried everywhere, containing apparently nothing, containing everything — and pulled out a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper.

"Tell me what you remember," she said, in the tone of a clerk opening an appointment book. "Even the wrong things. Especially the wrong things."

"Wren—"

"You write everything down," she said. "You taught me that. So we'll write this down too. Everything you can't remember, we'll rebuild it from outside evidence. Like a ledger." She smoothed the paper flat. "Like balancing."

"And if the accounts don't reconcile?"

"Then we find where the missing entry went," she said, "and we go get it back."

I want it recorded somewhere, even if only here, in a chapter of my life I'm writing for strangers: that was the moment the evening turned. Because she said it with such plain certainty, the certainty of a child who had once been a key to a language older than kingdoms and understood, better than anyone, that words written down can be un-written and written again. She believed the ledger could be balanced. She believed it the way I used to believe things, before I became a collection of margins.

"All right," I said. "Evidence. Ask me."

* * *

We worked through the night. It was the strangest interrogation of my career, and I have been interrogated by the crown, so the bar is high.

She asked questions like an archivist with a grudge against gaps. What color was your mother's hair? Unknown. Did you have siblings? Unknown, though journal one mentioned "the little ones" once, in passing, and then never again, which Wren flagged as suspicious. Why did you become a taster?

That one I could answer, partly, because the journals answered it for me. Page forty of the first book, in younger ink:

*Saw a man die at a feast when I was twelve. Wrong dish, right table. Nobody caught it. Everyone said what a tragedy, as if tragedy means nothing can be done. I decided that word was a lie.*

"That's it," I told Wren. "That's the whole architecture. Someone died and I decided the world was badly staffed."

Wren wrote it down carefully. *Decided the world was badly staffed.* Then she looked up. "And the little ones?"

"The little ones," I said, "are a hole in the record."

"Then we ask Odile."

"We don't ask Odile."

"Why not?"

Because Odile had sold the original binding thirty years ago. Because Odile had confessed it with the flat calm of a broker closing her last position, and handed us the terms, and I had watched Bram-the-evidence decide to keep her anyway, and I could no longer remember why that decision had been easy. Because every time I looked at Odile now, I felt the shape of trust without its contents, and I did not know how much of that hollow was the trade and how much was new and earned.

"Because some debts you audit yourself first," I said. "Give me time."

Wren wrote that down too. She wrote everything down. By the third hour the scrap had become pages, and the pages had begun to look suspiciously like a biography of a woman neither of us had met, assembled from receipts.

Somewhere near dawn she stopped consulting the paper and just talked, instead. Quietly, in the way she had — the way of a child who had learned that information given freely is a gift and chose to give me gifts.

"You let me sleep by the fire the first night," she said. "In the snow season. You gave me your cloak and sat up all night tasting the air like the cold might poison me."

"I don't remember that."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm telling you."

"Wren—"

"You smelled like swallowed deaths," she said. "That's why I trusted you. I never told you that part. You always pretended you knew already." She smoothed the page. "So. Evidence. You are the person who gave a mute child her cloak and stayed awake guarding her. Whatever else got sold, that's still true. It happened outside you. Nobody can buy a thing that happened."

I looked at my hands. A taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from decades of needles and thorns and the occasional knife that took objection to the menu. A ledger of poisons survived, Odile called them once, and everyone thought she was being poetic. She was being literal. That was always her problem.

"Nobody can buy a thing that happened," I repeated.

"No," said Wren. "But—" and here she hesitated, and I saw her decide, deliberately, to say it anyway, "—somebody might be able to write it back."

I didn't answer that. I couldn't. There was a clause somewhere in my old collateral — I had signed things in that vault I no longer fully recalled signing, terms nested inside terms, the Guild's favorite architecture — and I had a superstitious fear of hoping out loud about fine print. Hope, in my experience, was the most expensive item on any menu.

But I didn't tell her to stop hoping either. I poured her the last of the tea, and she drank it, and we sat in the grey light like two clerks at the end of a long audit, and it was — I want to be accurate here, because accuracy is all I have left — it was the closest thing to home I could currently prove existed.

* * *

Odile came in at midmorning, took one look at the table — seven journals, forty pages of Wren's reconstruction, a cold teapot, and me — and said nothing. She hung up her coat. She put water on for fresh tea. She moved around the room with the elaborate unconcern of a woman giving someone space to speak first.

I let her have a full minute of it. I'm capable of generosity.

"The little ones," I said.

Her hands paused over the kettle. "Bram."

"It's a hole in the record. Journal one mentions them once and never again. Either they died, or they left, or I sold them along with the kitchens and the doorframes, and I need to know which, because I've been reconstructing a woman all night and I refuse to finish her with a lie in the middle."

Odile sat down across from me. Up close, in honest light, she looked every year of the guilt she'd been carrying for three decades, and I noticed — new observation, entered into evidence — that she had started looking at me the way brokers look at accounts they're afraid to open.

"They lived," she said quietly. "Your brothers. Two of them. They're grown. One keeps a boatyard in Marrow's Reach. One joined the army and writes you a letter every solstice that you answer every year." She hesitated. "Answered. Every year."

The room tilted very slightly and righted itself. Two brothers. A boatyard. Solstice letters. An entire wing of my life, standing intact somewhere in the world, addressed to a woman who no longer lived at this address.

"I'll need copies of the letters," I heard myself say. "If you have them. If the Guild kept records."

"Bram Almeida," Odile said, "the Guild kept records of your birth."

Of course it had. Of course it had.

She rose, went to the iron cabinet in the corner, the one I'd never seen opened, and began working through drawers with the focus of a woman paying a debt she'd been carrying longer than the one on the books. Wren caught my eye across the table. She didn't smile. She just tapped her pencil once against her reconstruction pages — *nobody can buy a thing that happened* — and went back to writing.

And that was when the door burst open hard enough to crack the frame, and Isolde stood in it, travel-fouled, hatless, holding a piece of paper at arm's length as if it had bitten her, with the captain of the palace guard's seal broken on the floor behind her.

"Bram," she said. "The Hollow King knows about the solstice. He's moved the rite. He's moved *everything*. We have six days — and he's summoned the court to witness an execution."

Whose, Wren asked, in the silence. Isolde looked at me, and her face told me before her voice did.

"Mother Cassia."
