## 28. Isolde Opens the Gates

My father taught me to ride before he taught me to read, and I have often suspected that was deliberate. A girl who reads asks questions. A girl who rides goes places her governess cannot follow.

He never taught me to lie, though. That I learned from watching him.

For eleven years I had kept my private ledger of the king's wrongness — the same habit, I would later learn, that Bram kept, though hers was written in ink and mine was written in the particular ache of a daughter who cannot say aloud that the man at the head of the table is wearing her father the way a man wears a coat. I had the half-second answers. I had the shadow that fell a degree too far east at noon. I had the portrait in the west gallery, painted the year of my christening, where my father's eyes had been kind, and I had thirty years of memory in which they had never once been kind since.

And now I had something better than grief. I had a Guild receipt.

* * *

Captain Dain Ferros kept a office above the east barracks that smelled of oiled leather, lamp smoke, and the particular staleness of a man who has not opened a window in a decade. He was fifty, broad as a door, and had served the crown since before I was born — which meant, I realized as I sat across from him and watched him pretend to review a duty roster, that he had served the Hollow King for his entire tenure. He had never known another king. He had no baseline for wrongness. He thought the wrongness *was* the king.

"Your Highness honors me," he said, in the tone of a man who does not feel honored and hopes the discrepancy will be overlooked.

"Captain, do you know what a memory-extraction costs these days?"

The duty roster stopped moving. It was a small thing, the pause — a half-second, the same half-second my father gave before answering questions he shouldn't have needed to hear. But Ferros was not wearing a crown, and his half-second was only human caution, and human caution I could work with.

"I'm sure I couldn't say, Highness."

"Neither could I, until last month. Four hundred crowns for a single memory, extracted clean. More if it's recent. More if it's a face." I set the receipt on his desk and turned it so he could read it. I had bought it from a Guild debtor in the undercity who sold paper the way other men sold secrets, and I had paid for it with a bracelet my mother — my *real* mother, the one whose funeral the thing in the crown had attended with perfect, hollow courtesy — had left me. I regretted the bracelet. I did not regret the receipt. "This one was purchased by the First Chamberlain. The memory extracted belonged to a kitchen girl. Eight years old. Dark hair. She was seen once, in the snow, outside the Guild counting house."

Ferros looked at the receipt for a long time. Then he looked at me, and I watched a man decide how much treason he could afford.

"Highness, if the chamberlain is purchasing intelligence—"

"The chamberlain is purchasing *erasure*, Captain. There is a difference, and the difference is that one of them is what loyal servants do and the other is what the king's men do when the king is afraid of a child." I let that sit. "You have served thirty years. Tell me — in thirty years, has the king ever once been afraid?"

I had rehearsed that question on the ride over, and I want it recorded that it landed exactly as I'd hoped, which almost never happens. Ferros's jaw worked. His hands, flat on the desk, went very still.

"A king is not afraid," he said finally. "A king is *cautious*."

"Of course. And a cautious king orders the guard doubled on the solstice, does he? Every year? Tripled the year the comet came?" I had spent three nights in the palace archive pulling the solstice rosters, and I will confess to a certain pride in how thoroughly I had done my homework; my governess would have wept. "A cautious king who never sleeps, Captain. I have checked. The guard rotation on the royal wing has not been interrupted in eleven years. Not once. Not for illness, not for mourning, not for my own wedding feast, may the gods forgive me for having one. What kind of man never sleeps?"

"The kind who doesn't need to," Ferros said, very quietly, and I knew then that I had him, or that he had known for years and had only been waiting for someone to say it first.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about treason: it is mostly paperwork.

We spent four hours in that office. I laid out my evidence in the order I had practiced — the half-second answers, the shadow, the sleeping, the Guild purchases, the kitchen girl with the dark hair who had been hunted through the snow by the crown's own agents. Ferros listened the way good soldiers listen, which is to say without interrupting and with his hand never far from the door, because a good soldier also considers the possibility that the princess across the desk is mad, or worse, a trap.

At the end of it he said, "Say I believe you. Say I believe the king — my king, whom I have sworn to — is not the king. What then? You walk into the throne room and *unmake* him? With what?"

"With a word," I said. "With a witness. And with the willingness of the bound — though that last part is a longer story, and I'll need you to trust me on it, because the story involves my father, and I am not yet strong enough to tell it to a stranger."

"Am I a stranger, Highness?"

"You are a man who has guarded a lie for thirty years without knowing it," I said. "That makes you either the most useful man in the palace or the most dangerous. I've decided to gamble on useful."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, gray and tired, the smile of a man whose whole life had just been reclassified as evidence. "And if I refuse? If I decide you're mad and report this conversation?"

"Then I'm dead by morning, and you'll have a new princess to guard, and in eleven years you'll notice she also never sleeps, and you'll wonder." I stood. "I don't think you'll report me, Captain. I think you've been waiting eleven years for someone to report *to*."

He was silent for a long moment. Then he opened the drawer beneath the duty roster and took out a bottle of brandy and two glasses, which is soldier for *yes*, and poured with a hand that was not quite steady.

"To the king," he said. "Whoever he turns out to be."

"To my father," I said, and drank, and did not tell him that I could not remember my father's voice anymore, only the shape of it, the way you remember a room you lived in as a child.

* * *

The plan, when we built it, was beautiful in the way that plans are beautiful — that is, on paper, at night, with brandy, in the company of a man who had just agreed to commit the largest act of treason in the kingdom's history and was coping by treating it as a logistics problem.

"The solstice," Ferros said, tapping the palace map. "You're certain it has to be then?"

"Ninepence was certain, and Ninepence was older than the kingdom. The binding was sworn at midwinter, and what is sworn at midwinter thins at midwinter. The crown's grip loosens for one night. After dawn it closes again for another year, and we will not get a second chance, because the Hollow King knows we're coming — it has known since Wren spoke in the cells."

I did not add the rest of what I knew, which was that the Hollow King had let Cassia's interrogation stall it for three days and then stopped trying to eat her memories, which could only mean it had decided it no longer needed them. It knew where we were. It knew what we intended. It was not stopping us.

I had asked Bram about that, the night before, in the safehouse under the tannery, while Wren slept with her head on a ledger and Odile sharpened a letter-opener with the intensity of a woman who had decided pens were weapons now. Bram had been re-reading her own journals — she did that every evening, reconstructing herself from evidence like a scholar with a dead language — and she had looked up and said, in the flat voice she used for the things that hurt most:

"It's not stopping us because it doesn't think we can. Or because it wants the word spoken where it can catch it. Or because a thing that has ruled for thirty years has forgotten what resistance feels like." She had closed the journal. "Or all three. Tyrants die of that, usually. But not always, and not on the first try, and I've been poisoned enough times to know that surviving the first dose means nothing about the second."

So: the solstice, one night, one corridor, one chance.

Ferros gave me the path in three movements. First: the solstice feast, which the Hollow King insisted upon every year — a full court, a long table, the king at its head eating nothing and drinking nothing and being *seen*, because a thing that wears a shape must display it or risk questions. Every guard worth posting would be in the great hall or on the feast route. Second: the old priest's stair behind the chapel of the founders, sealed since my grandfather's reign, which Ferros could unseal and garrison with six men of his own choosing — men who owed him, men whose sons he had pulled out of trouble, men who asked no questions because he had spent thirty years making sure they never needed to. Third: the long corridor to the throne room, which no one could clear for us, which we would walk ourselves, through whatever waited there.

"And the cells," I said.

"The cells are my problem." He said it flatly, no hesitation, and I understood that he had already fought that battle with himself and won it. "If I'm treasoning, I'm treasoning completely. I'll have Cassia out before you reach the stair, or I'll die trying, and either way you don't carry it."

"You could die for a woman you've never met."

"Highness, I've been prepared to die for a man I *have* met for thirty years, and it turns out he isn't real. I'd rather spend it on something true." He rolled up the map. "My father guarded the old king. He died in the year of the binding — fever, they said. I've wondered, since an hour ago, whether they said."

I had no comfort to give him there. I gave him the only thing I had. "The binding's original terms are in Bram's hands. If your father's name is in them, she'll find it. The Guild keeps accounts of everything, Captain. Even the people it spent."

* * *

I walked back through the palace at dusk, alone, as a princess may, and I did my last circuit of the west gallery, because I had decided this would be the last night I looked at the portrait.

My father had been painted at thirty, holding me as an infant with the particular terror of a man who has just been handed something breakable and infinite. The painter had caught it — the fear and the fierceness in the same face. I had spent my childhood looking for that face in the living king and failing, and I had spent the last month learning why, and I had spent the last three days learning the rest of it, the part that reframed everything: he had done it on purpose. He had bound the thing to himself, traded himself, because he believed no one — least of all a king — should hold absolute power, and the only way to cage it was to become the cage.

He had let me grow up loving a coat that wore him, because the alternative was a war that would have eaten the kingdom. And when the failsafe came — a child born into the oath-line, a living key — he had arranged, from whatever fragment of him remained inside the binding, for her to be carried to the one person in the city whose body was a ledger of survived deaths. *Carry it until someone worthy reads you.* That was what Wren remembered of the voice. My father had chosen Bram Almeida, a food-taster he had almost certainly never met, on the strength of her record of surviving.

I stood in front of the portrait and I said, quietly, so that no one would hear and so that he might, wherever he was: "I'm coming to get you out. And I'm sorry it's taken me eleven years to notice, and I'm sorrier that noticing is the same as losing you, and I'm going to do it anyway, because you taught me — whoever taught me — that you don't leave a debt unpaid."

The portrait said nothing. Portraits are honest that way.

* * *

Two nights before the solstice, Ferros sent me a message in the agreed cipher — a laundry list, of all things, with the quantities encoding the guard rotations. Six men for the chapel stair. Cassia moved to a cell two floors up, nearer the light, which he claimed was a kindness and I suspected was positioning. And at the bottom, in plain script, because some things should not be encoded: *Your father's name is in the old contract. Third page. He signed as witness to his own binding. I have read it. I am sorry, and I am with you.*

I sat with that for a long time.

He had signed as witness. He had stood before the Guild's broker and watched himself be caged, and put his name to it, so that no one could ever claim it was done to him without his knowledge. My father, the most absolute power in the kingdom, had insisted on being a witness to his own imprisonment — because a binding sworn without witness could be broken by anyone, but a binding witnessed by the bound could only be ended by his own consent.

Which meant the third term of the unbinding — *the willingness of the bound* — was not a loophole. It was a door he had built into his own cage, thirty years ago, and left unlocked, and waited behind, all this time, for someone to come and knock.

I wrote back one line: *Solstice. We knock.*

* * *

On the eve of the solstice, the palace glittered. Servants hung the midwinter greens; the kitchens — Bram's old kingdom, though she was a traitor now with a price on her head, and the thought of her name on a wanted bill made something in my chest twist like a key — the kitchens roasted and sugared and glowed. The Hollow King moved through the preparations like a conductor through an orchestra, gracious, exact, answering chamberlains before they finished speaking. It paused once, near me, in the great hall, and looked at the throne dais with something I could only call satisfaction, and I stood there with my evidence in my head and my father's name in my heart and smiled at it the way you smile at a guest you intend to evict.

"Solstice tomorrow," it said, in my father's voice. "A good year, Isolde. A peaceful one. You'll see."

"I'm sure I will," I said.

And I thought: *we knock.*
