## 29. Solstice Eve

The kitchen of the safehouse had not been used for cooking in some years, judging by the state of it. Someone had stored rope in the flour bin. The hearth was home to a family of pigeons who regarded my arrival with the particular indignation of squatters being asked to produce a lease. I evicted them with a broom and an apology, because even on the eve of regicide, I have standards.

"You're cooking," Odile said from the doorway, in the tone other people use for *you're bleeding*.

"I'm cooking."

"With what hands?"

"The same two that have poisoned or failed to poison every royal meal for thirty years. They remember their work." I said it lightly. It wasn't light. That was rather the point of tonight.

Here is the thing about tasting nothing: you do not stop knowing food. Thirty years of immunity meant I never tasted anything anyway—every flavor arrived pre-muffled, wrapped in wool, a rumor of itself. So in a sense I've been cooking blind since I was seventeen. My tongue is a clerk who files reports without reading them. But my hands know when onions are done by the sound. My nose knows when the stock has turned. A taster who can't taste learns to cook through every other door in the house, and I had spent three decades opening all of them.

Besides. Tomorrow we walked down a corridor into the mouth of a thing that eats oaths and wears kings. Tonight, if I could give these people one hour where the world smelled like browned butter and rosemary instead of fear, then let the pigeons judge me.

I had gone to market at dawn with Cassia's hood over my head and Isolde's coin in my purse, which is a sentence I'd have laughed at a year ago. A princess's money buying turnips for a fugitive. The city was dressed for solstice—lanterns strung between eaves, pine boughs nailed over doors, children burning their fingers on spiced wine their parents pretended not to see them steal. Everyone in Vhalen was celebrating the longest night by lighting things. Tomorrow we'd celebrate it by walking into the dark. There is a symmetry there I refuse to find comforting.

By the time the light went, the long table in the back room held: one disgraced princess, one excommunicated priestess, one Guild mistress with her ledgers locked away upstairs like a guilty conscience, one child who spoke the language God used before He learned to be polite, and me, carrying a pot.

"Sit," I said. "All of you. If anyone offers to help I will consider it an act of war."

* * *

The stew was venison, because Isolde knew a butcher who owed the crown a favor and would never ask why the favor came due tonight. The bread was mine, kneaded by hand, and I confess I stood over it while it baked like a mother over a cradle. The wine was Odile's contribution—"confiscated," she said, "from myself"—and it was very good, which she announced before anyone drank, as though quality were a legal disclaimer.

For a while we just ate. You'd think five people facing death together would talk of grand things. Mostly we talked about whether the turnips were overdone. (They were not. I know this the way I know anything now: absolutely, without evidence.)

It was Cassia who broke the shape of the evening, as she breaks most things—gently, and irreversibly.

"At the Motherhouse," she said, turning her cup, "on the eve of any great rite, we did a small one. Each sister named aloud the thing she feared to lose. Not for the god's benefit. For ours. A fear spoken is a fear with a handle on it. You can carry a handled thing."

The table went quiet in the way tables do when everyone realizes the evening has just become serious and no one wants to be the first to flinch.

"That sounds like a trap," Odile said.

"It is a trap," Cassia agreed serenely. "That's what makes it a rite."

Isolde looked at her father's ring, which she wore on a cord around her neck now, too big for any of her fingers. "I'll go first, before my courage remembers it's supposed to be a princess's." She was quiet a moment. "I'm afraid of losing him twice."

No one asked her to explain. She explained anyway, because she is her father's daughter and explains everything.

"I lost him thirty years ago, whatever the histories say. The man in that crown hasn't been my father since I was six years old—I only didn't have the word for what was wrong until this year. So tomorrow, when we unbind it, I lose him again. And the second loss comes with his consent. With his *yes*. He chose to end." Her voice caught on the word and she rode it down like a rider taking a fence. "I'm afraid there won't be enough left of him in there to mean it. I'm afraid I'll spend the rest of my life wondering if the yes was really his."

"It will be his," Cassia said. "I'll witness it. That's what witnessing *is*, Isolde. Making sure the yes belongs to someone."

Isolde nodded once, sharply, the way she does when crying would be inefficient. Then she looked up, and to my lasting surprise, she looked at me.

"Your turn, taster."

"Oh, I don't think—"

"We're doing the rite," Odile said, with the mercilessness of a woman who has audited souls professionally. "You cooked. Cooks go last, that's tradition."

"It is not tradition," I said. "You invented that just now."

"I'm a Guild mistress. Inventing traditions is most of the job."

But Wren had put down her spoon, and when Wren looks at you with those gray eyes that hold more years than her body does, argument becomes a form of cruelty. So I looked into my cup and found the answer already waiting, because apparently I'd been carrying it around for weeks without letting myself read it.

"I'm afraid of losing the memory of this," I said. "Of tonight. Of all of you." I kept my voice level, bookkeeper-flat, because if I let it bend I'd never get through. "I've sold so much of myself to the Guild that I know exactly how it works. Memories don't die dramatically. They just quietly stop being yours. One day you reach for your childhood home and find a receipt where it used to be." I turned the cup. "Tomorrow I may have to buy something else out of the vaults. And whatever I pay with, I'm afraid it'll be this table. This stew. The sound of Wren arguing with Isolde about whether princesses can lose at cards fairly. I'm afraid I'll save the kingdom and stand in it afterward like a woman in an empty house, hearing echoes of a family she can't prove she ever had."

The silence after that was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of people deciding not to look away.

"Well," Odile said finally, gruffly, "that's cheating. Now everyone else's fears sound small."

"No," Cassia said. "Now everyone else's fears sound *honest*. Which is different." She folded her hands. "Mine, then. Since we're being honest." She took a breath, and I realized with a start that the priestess was nervous—that Mother Cassia, who had stared down the Hollow King's memory-eating in a cell and come out gritted but whole, was nervous at a dinner table. "I'm afraid of dying uselessly."

"Cassia—"

"Let me finish. Witnesses to unbindings die; I knew that when I agreed, and I've made my peace with dying. What I haven't made peace with is dying *wrongly*. Dying before the word is spoken. Dying at the weir gate instead of the throne room. I'm afraid my faith will turn out to be good for stalling and nothing else." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired at the edges. "There. A fear with a handle. Though I notice the handle is shaped exactly like tomorrow, and I'd have preferred something I could set down."

* * *

Odile went next, which surprised no one less than it surprised her.

She sat with her arms crossed for a long moment, staring at the candle flame like it owed her money. "I'm afraid of the ledger," she said at last. "Not the Guild's. Mine." She uncrossed her arms, recrossed them, gave up on them entirely. "Thirty years ago I sold a young king a binding he didn't understand, because he paid, and the Guild serves whoever pays, and I told myself that rule was a wall and not a window. Every kindness I've done since—every debtor I forgave, every impossible thing I refused to trade—was interest paid against that principal. And I'm afraid that tomorrow, when it's done, the account won't balance. I'm afraid forgiveness isn't a currency. I'm afraid I invented it so I could keep sleeping."

"You could have run," Isolde said. "When Ninepence burned. When the crown agents came. You smuggled us out yourself, at your age, through the flood tunnels."

"My age," Odile repeated dangerously.

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean, and I'm choosing to be flattered." She sighed, and for once the armor of irony didn't come back up. "I ran toward, girl. That's not the same as brave. Brave would have been refusing the sale thirty years ago. Everything since is a man digging himself out of a hole and calling the ladder a gift." She picked up her wine. "Anyway. Handle acquired. Pass the bread."

Wren was last. Of course she was last; she had been watching all of us with the patience of someone who reads the last page first.

She was nine now—or eight-and-something; her birthday was one of the many things the snow had erased—and she had grown into a habit of stillness that unnerved strangers. At court they'd have called it breeding. It wasn't breeding. It was a girl who had learned early that words were load-bearing.

"I'm afraid," she said, in her clear, careful voice, "of finishing."

We waited. Wren did not believe in rushing sentences.

"I was made to carry the word until someone worthy reads me. That's what the voice in the tower said. Carry it. And I've carried it, and it's heavy, and I complain about it, but—" she frowned, working at it, "—it's also the only thing I *am*. Before the tower, nothing. After the unbinding—" she shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, "—maybe nothing again. A key that's been turned is just a piece of metal. I'm afraid that when I speak the word tomorrow, I'll speak myself empty. That the First Speech is the only room I live in, and saying it out loud means moving out."

"Wren." I reached across the table and took her hand. Her fingers were cold, the way they always were, the way they'd been the night she arrived in the snow speaking a language older than kingdoms. "Listen to me. I am a professional appraiser of what people are worth. It's the family business, more or less. And I am telling you, as an expert, that you are not a key. Keys open things and stay behind in the lock. You—" I squeezed her hand, "—you are the person who walked through the door afterward. There's a difference, and it's the entire difference, and anyone who tells you otherwise can take it up with me."

"With both of us," Odile said.

"And me," Isolde said.

"And me," said Cassia, "and I'll make it official, if it helps. I still remember how."

Wren looked around the table—at the priestess, the princess, the penitent, the poisoner—and something in her face loosened, a knot untied from the inside.

"Okay," she said. Just that. But in First Speech, I suspect, it would have been a paragraph.

* * *

We washed the dishes together, because armies march on logistics and so do families. Isolde dried. Wren stacked. Odile supervised with a cloth draped over her shoulder like a general's sash. Cassia hummed something old under her breath, a coronation hymn by the shape of it, though she sang it soft enough that it sounded like a lullaby instead of a warning.

And I stood at the hearth banking the fire, and I let myself have one minute—one honest, unsold, unwitnessed minute—of looking at them.

This is what I couldn't say at the table, because the rite only takes one fear per person and I'd spent mine: I am not afraid of losing the memory of tonight. I am afraid of something worse. I am afraid I already know, in the place where bookkeepers keep the true books, that I *will* lose it—that tomorrow I'll pay with exactly this, that the Guild will collect with its usual courtesy, and that some future Bram will stand in a warm kitchen with flour on her hands and no idea why she's crying.

But here is the other thing the true books say, the entry I wrote in the dark and don't show anyone: it will still have been true. Whatever they take, they can't take that it happened. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the world, this table existed. These people loved me. I tasted nothing and trusted everything, and it was enough.

A hand touched my arm. Wren, standing beside me in the firelight, holding out the last heel of bread on her palm—an offering, or a joke, or possibly a sacrament; with Wren the categories blur.

"For the road tomorrow," she said. Then, quieter, in the old tongue, three syllables that made the banked coals glow briefly gold.

"What was that?" I asked.

"A blessing," she said. "Roughly. It doesn't translate well. It means something like *may you remain*."

"May I remain," I repeated. "I'll drink to that. Except I can't taste it, so I'll just drink."

She smiled, and outside, over the rooftops of Vhalen, the solstice bell began to toll midnight—the longest night arriving, right on schedule, indifferent to our plans.

Odile appeared at my elbow with a lantern and a face like a closing ledger. "It's time," she said. "The guard changes at the half-hour. Isolde's captain is waiting, and the corridor won't walk itself."

I took off my apron. Folded it. Set it on the chair, deliberately, like a bookmark—the mark of a page I intended, whatever it cost, to come back and read again.

"Right," I said, and picked up the lantern. "Let's go meet the wolf."
