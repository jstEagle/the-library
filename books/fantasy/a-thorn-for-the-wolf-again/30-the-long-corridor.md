## 30. The Long Corridor

There is a particular quality to walking toward your own death down a corridor lined with portraits of kings, and that quality is *quiet*. Not the quiet of safety—nothing about the palace at midnight was safe—but the quiet of a room holding its breath. The stones themselves seemed to know what we intended, and had decided, in their ancient way, not to gossip.

Wren walked at my left with her hand fisted in my sleeve, which she had done since we entered through the postern gate. Isolde walked ahead, dressed as a servant, her spine betraying her at every step. You can take the princess out of the ceremony, but you cannot take the ceremony out of the princess. Cassia came behind us all, murmuring under her breath—not prayers, exactly. Inventories. She told me once that when fear comes, she counts things: candles, steps, sins. It keeps the mind from running ahead to imagine worse.

And Odile—Odile walked like a woman returning to a shop she'd been locked out of for thirty years, and I don't think any of us understood until later how literally that was true.

The plan was simple, which is how I knew it would go wrong. Simple plans assume the world is paying attention to the same page you are. We would follow Isolde's route—the servants' stair, the gallery of lesser courtiers, the long corridor beneath the west wing where the old king's apartments used to be before they were sealed—and reach the antechamber of the throne room by the hour before dawn, when the solstice thinning began and whatever wore the crown would be at its weakest. Wren would speak the word. Cassia would witness. And I—

I was there because I was owed nothing anymore. That sounds like self-pity. It isn't; it's bookkeeping. Every piece of myself I'd traded away had purchased something: passage, silence, erasure, this night. A ledger balances or it doesn't. Mine balanced. What remained of Bram Almeida was exactly sufficient to open one door and stand in it while an eight-year-old did the impossible. There is a strange peace in being reduced to your function. I recommend it to no one.

We passed the first guard at the foot of the servants' stair. He looked directly at us—at four women moving against every regulation the palace possessed—and then looked away with the studied care of a man examining something fascinating on the opposite wall. His hand didn't go to his sword. His jaw worked once, twice, around words he decided not to say.

"Captain Veyra's people," Isolde murmured without turning her head. "She chose them herself tonight. Men who owe her, or owe me." A pause. "Or who have daughters."

That last part I understood better than she knew. Or perhaps she knew exactly; Isolde and I had long since stopped pretending our information ran along different channels.

* * *

The gallery of lesser courtiers was where the voice found us.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last discipline I have left. It did not come from behind a door. It did not echo off the stone. It arrived the way a taste arrives on the tongue—suddenly present, unmistakably *there*, and already inside your mouth before you can decide whether to spit.

"Bram Almeida."

My own name, in my own mother's voice. She has been dead twenty-two years and I would know the exact dry patience of her tone if I heard it spoken by a crow on a rooftop. My feet wanted to stop. I made them keep moving by the simple trick of counting my steps, the way Cassia counted her sins. Four hundred and eleven steps between the gallery door and the sealed apartments. I know because I had walked this corridor in other years, other errands, and a taster counts everything.

"I kept your ledgers," the voice said, gentle now, wounded. "Thirty years of poisons, and I caught every one. Come back to me. I'll give you the immunity again—all of it, every death you swallowed, returned whole. You'll never taste fear in wine again. You'll never be *ordinary*."

Ordinary. That was the hook, and it was a good one—I'll grant the thing that much. For six days I had been an ordinary woman in a city full of extraordinary dangers, jumping at bitter almonds and metallic sweetness, doing arithmetic with my pulse. It offered me back my armor.

"You're not her," I said, aloud, because saying it out loud is half the defense. "My mother never once told me she was proud of me. Whoever you are, you've read her wrong."

The voice laughed—not cruelly, which was somehow worse—and moved on. I felt it move the way you feel weather change. Down the corridor, past me, to the others.

* * *

It went for Cassia next, and I only understood afterward what it had offered her, because she told me at the end, when telling cost nothing.

But in the moment, what I saw was this: Cassia stopped counting. Her lips went still mid-inventory, and her eyes took on the terrible softness of a woman listening to something she has wanted to hear since childhood. She walked five more steps in that softness, and then she crossed herself—the old way, thumb to brow, the way excommunicated priests do it, the way that says *I remember even though you've forbidden me to*—and said clearly:

"Get thee behind me. And take your counterfeit dead with you."

The voice hissed. Actually hissed, like fat on fire, and I felt the temperature of the corridor drop by a degree I could measure on my skin—a taster's skin keeps its instruments even after the armor is gone.

* * *

For Isolde, it changed its entire approach. No dead relatives. It offered her the throne.

Not the regency. Not a stewardship. The crown itself, hers by blood, hers by right, no waiting, no councils, no barons to flatter. And underneath the offer, so quiet it was almost subliminal, the thing that made it poisonous: *your father could have been saved if you had acted sooner.*

"That," Isolde said, and her voice cracked exactly once, in the middle, like ice underfoot, "is the first true thing you've said tonight."

She kept walking. But I saw her hands, and her hands were shaking, and I filed that away in the ledger I keep behind my eyes—the one column marked *debts I will owe these people*, which grows longer every day and which I intend, someday, to settle.

* * *

It saved Wren for last, and it was careful with her, the way you are careful with the one instrument in the shop you cannot replace.

It didn't offer her things. It offered her *belonging*. It spoke in the voice from her fragments—the tower voice, the burning-oath voice, the voice that had said *carry it until someone worthy reads you*—and it said: *you were never meant to carry it alone. Put it down. Put it down and I will tell you who your mother was. I will tell you why they burned the oath. I will give you back the first eight years of yourself, the ones the binding ate.*

And Wren—eight years old, exhausted, carrying a language in her chest like swallowed coals—stopped walking.

I knelt down to her level in that cold corridor, under the painted eyes of forty dead kings, and I did the only thing I had left to do. I held out my hand.

"Wren," I said. "Look at me. Not the voice. Me."

Her eyes came up. They were wet and furious and eight years old.

"It lies," I said. "That's the first thing you learn about anything that offers you your heart's desire in a dark hallway. It doesn't have your mother. If it did, it wouldn't need you—it would have used her instead of you. It needs *you*, which means it fears *you*, which means—" I took a breath. "Which means whatever you're carrying is heavier than its crown. So we carry it together until morning, and then you put it down exactly once, exactly where we choose. Agreed?"

She wiped her nose on her sleeve, which is the most honest gesture in any kingdom, and put her hand in mine.

"Together," she said. Then, in First Speech, one syllable of it, soft as a struck match: "*Vesh.*"

Every candle in the gallery guttered at once. Somewhere far above us, something vast and bound shifted its weight, like a wolf turning over in its sleep, and the walls of the palace groaned—a sound I felt in my teeth, in the bones of my ear, in the ledger of my body.

It knew where we were now. Precisely, finally, irrevocably.

* * *

We ran the rest of the way. There was no more point in stealth; there was only point in speed. Isolde led us down the sealed stair, through the door her father had walled shut with his own hands thirty years ago—we came at it from the other side, through the forgotten chapel behind it, because every wall has two faces—and out into the antechamber of the throne room.

The doors were ahead of us. Black oak, iron-banded, tall enough to make a cathedral feel like a cupboard. From beyond them came a light that wasn't candlelight and wasn't sunlight, but the color of the space between the ticks of a clock.

Odile stopped at the threshold. She had been silent since the gallery, and now she turned to me, and her face in that strange light was older than I had ever seen it, older than the Guild, almost.

"Bram," she said. "Before we go in. Whatever happens in there—you should know that the original contract, the one I sold him. The terms are in my coat. And one of them—" her throat worked, "—one of them is going to matter more than any of us guessed. When the time comes, look for the clause about the anchor. Read it *twice*."

"The anchor? Wren—"

"Not Wren." Odile's jaw tightened. "Read it twice, Bram. There isn't time for more than that."

Beyond the doors, the false light brightened. And a voice that was not my mother's, and not anyone's, that was a chorus wearing the shape of one throat, said pleasantly, patiently, from the far side of the oak:

"Come in, taster. The account is overdue, and I have been *so* looking forward to settling it."
