## 31. Cassia Freed

The cell smelled of wet stone and old prayers.

Wren had never been inside a palace dungeon before—she had never been inside much of anything before Bram found her in the snow—but she suspected most dungeons did not have a priestess chained to the wall by manacles that were not entirely made of iron. The left shackle was plain metal, pitted and green at the edges. The right one was something else: a band of darkness that didn't catch the torchlight so much as drink it, humming a note too low for ears and settling instead somewhere behind Wren's teeth.

"That's binding-work," Odile murmured from the doorway, where she stood with her arms crossed and her whole posture suggesting she was prepared to be disappointed by reality. "Crown-grade. Old crown-grade. Don't touch it, child."

"I wasn't going to touch it," Wren said, which was true now and might not remain true, because her hands had their own opinions lately, and they leaned toward things that hummed.

Mother Cassia sat slumped against the wall beneath the shackles like a marionette someone had set down carefully but without ceremony. Her face was bruised along one cheekbone. Her habit was filthy. Her eyes, when she lifted them, were clear as a winter morning, and Wren thought: *she has been fighting in here, all alone, with nothing but her own stubbornness for a weapon.*

"Ah," Cassia said, and her voice came out cracked but amused. "The rescue party. Three children, an accountant, and a woman who frightens me more than my captors do."

"The feeling is mutual," Odile said. "Can you walk?"

"For you? I'd consider it." Cassia tried to rise; the dark shackle pulled taut and she hissed through her teeth. "It doesn't want me leaving. It's been very clear about that. Polite, actually, in its way. It keeps offering me things."

"What things?" Bram asked. She had come up beside Wren, quiet on the stone, and Wren felt her presence the way you feel a hearth in a cold room—a warmth that had nothing to do with temperature. Bram smelled of swallowed deaths, of every poison she'd ever survived, layered like rings in a tree. Wren had decided long ago, in the wordless part of herself, that this was what safety smelled like.

"Custody of my memories," Cassia said. "It offered to give them back if I told it where you were. Then it offered to let me keep them *and* my life. Then it offered me my mother's voice, saying my name." Her mouth twisted. "It has a generous streak. That's how you know it's afraid."

Bram crouched to examine the shackle, keeping her hands well back. Even from three feet away, Wren could see the moment Bram understood what she was looking at—the small stillness that came over her, the way her breath went shallow and controlled, the way she reached into her sleeve for a vial and then stopped herself, because there was no poison in the world that would help here.

"It's not holding you," Bram said slowly. "It's holding your *name*. Look—the chain runs into the wall, yes, but the anchor is the oath-ring around the iron. Someone bound her witness-oath. As long as the oath is bound, she can't leave the cell, no matter what happens to the chains."

"Correct," said a new voice, dry as paper and twice as old.

Wren turned. At the end of the corridor, sitting in the shadow between two torch-brackets, was Ninepence.

She had seen him before, once, in the Guild's oldest vault—a mummified broker who woke only when something worth waking for passed through the ledgers. He looked like a bundle of sticks wrapped in leather, except the sticks were arranged in the shape of a person who had once been formidable and had simply refused to stop being so after death. His voice came out of him like wind through a reed bed.

"You're supposed to be ash," Odile said flatly. "I watched the tower burn."

"You watched a tower burn," Ninepence agreed. "I've been watching towers burn for four hundred years, Voss. You develop contingencies." He dragged himself forward into the light, and up close Wren could see the ledger-lines inked directly onto his desiccated skin, columns of debts running down his forearms like veins. His eyeless face turned toward her, and she felt the attention of it like a hand on her scalp. "But I'm not here for reunions. I'm here because the child needs to understand what she's about to do, and none of you can tell her, because none of you speak the language."

"She hasn't spoken it," Bram said carefully. "Not since the undercity. Not on purpose."

"On purpose is exactly what she needs," Ninepence said. "Come here, little key."

Everything in Wren wanted not to move. Everything else in her moved.

* *

Ninepence settled himself against the wall opposite Cassia's cell with the creak of old leather and older grievances, and looked at Wren with whatever he used instead of eyes.

"Do you know why the palace frightens you?" he asked.

Wren considered lying. It seemed polite to at least attempt it. "Because it's the king's house?"

"Because it is the most heavily bound building in the world," Ninepence said. "Every door, every treaty-seal, every coronation vow sworn under this roof for six hundred years—it's all First Speech, fossilized into furniture. The stones are a book, child. You are a reader walking through a library, and every page has been screaming your name since you crossed the weir gate. That's why the Hollow King knows you're coming. The building itself has been telling him."

"Then we shouldn't be doing this," Bram said, low and urgent. "Every step deeper we take, we're ringing the dinner bell—"

"Yes," Ninepence agreed pleasantly. "Isn't it convenient, then, that dinner is already served?" He gestured at Cassia's cell with one stick-like finger. "You cannot cut that binding. You cannot pick it. Poison will do nothing—" he nodded acknowledgment to Bram, who looked faintly offended and faintly relieved— "and the guards will be here within minutes regardless, because the moment you entered this wing, the walls began whispering. So. There is exactly one tool in the world that opens an oath-lock, and she is standing in front of you trying very hard to look like an ordinary girl, which has never once worked."

All of them looked at Wren.

Wren looked at the floor. The floor, she noticed, was also covered in faint bindings, worn smooth by centuries of feet, and even the scuff marks spelled something in a script that predated the kingdom, the alphabet, possibly the concept of writing. She had spent eight years being nobody's anything—a tower, a fire, fragments, a voice saying *carry it until someone worthy reads you*—and the world kept insisting otherwise.

"If I speak," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted, "it'll hear. All of it. The whole palace. It'll know exactly where we are."

"Yes," said Ninepence.

"And then it comes for us."

"With tremendous enthusiasm," Ninepence agreed. "Though I'll note—and I've read every contract ever filed, so I know whereof I speak—that it already knew. The moment Isolde opened the gates, it began moving its pieces. What you're choosing isn't whether to reveal yourselves. You revealed yourselves on the stairs. What you're choosing is whether you free the witness before the thing in the crown finishes eating her."

Cassia laughed quietly from her place on the floor. "He's not wrong. It's been at me all night. It gets through maybe a layer and then my faith gums up the works—I imagine it rather like a bird trying to swallow a fistful of sand—but it is *patient*, and I am only human, and my sand is running low."

Wren looked at Bram.

This was the thing, the whole thing, the shape of everything: she looked at Bram first. Before the plan, before the danger, before the ancient dead broker with his ledgered skin. Bram was kneeling on cold stone with her sleeves rolled up and poison-scars lacing her wrists like a record of every bad idea she'd ever survived, and she was looking at Wren with an expression Wren had learned to read the way other people learned letters. It said: *whatever you choose, I will stand between you and the consequence.*

That was a terrible way to treat someone you loved, Wren thought suddenly, fiercely. Making them your shield. Bram had been traded piece by piece for Wren's sake—immunity, memories, her name sitting in some vault like a pawned watch—and if Wren let her keep doing it, then Wren was just another buyer at the market, and the currency was Bram.

"No," Wren said.

Everyone blinked.

"No," she said again, louder, and stood up straight, and felt something settle in her spine that had been loose her whole life. "Not *because* of Bram. Not to save her, not to pay her back, not because she smells nice and stands in front of things for me." Bram's eyebrows went up. "I'll do it because Cassia remembered the truth when everyone else forgot it. Because she heard something answer from inside the crown and she didn't decide she'd imagined it, which is what I would have done, probably, if I'd been raised to be reasonable. And because the Hollow King wants her memories eaten, which means her memories must be dangerous to it, which means they're weapons, and I am *tired* of weapons being taken away from our side."

The corridor was silent. Somewhere above them, distantly, boots were starting to move.

"Well," Odile said at last, into the quiet. "The Guild does love a debtor who understands the terms."

* *

Wren crossed to the cell door.

Up close, the dark shackle hummed louder, or perhaps she simply stopped pretending she couldn't hear it. Now that she let herself listen, she could hear *all* of it—the wall bindings, the door hinges, the oath-ring around the iron, each one a sentence in First Speech, frozen mid-word for decades or centuries. They weren't frightening, exactly. They were lonely. Sentences locked in place until someone came to finish them.

She put her hand on the cell door's iron bar. Beneath her palm, the lock's binding pulsed like a trapped moth.

"The words," Ninepence said softly. "Don't translate them. Don't think them. You don't think your own name before you say it. Let the sentence come the way water finds a downhill."

Wren closed her eyes.

And there it was—not words exactly, not at first, but the *shape* of words, the same shape she'd felt in the master ledger when the letters rearranged themselves for her, the same shape as the burning oath in her fragmentary memory, the voice in the tower saying *carry it.* She was made of this language. It had been poured into her before she had any others. All she had to do was stop holding her mouth shut.

She opened her eyes. She looked at Mother Cassia, who had lifted her head and was watching her with an expression of fierce, unembarrassed hope, and Wren spoke her first deliberate sentence.

"*Vaelu orren kethis. Su vaelu. An domm.*"

She didn't know the translation. She knew what it *did.*

The sound left her mouth and it was not sound, quite—it was weight. The air in the corridor bent around it. The torch flames flattened sideways as if bowing. And the binding on Cassia's shackle—

The binding *shuddered.*

Wren felt it happen in her bones: the whole palace reacting, every old oath in every stone waking at once like a sleeping animal touched by a hand it recognized. Above them, somewhere far off, a chandelier trembled. Doors groaned on hinges that hadn't moved in years. A portrait in some distant gallery, Wren would learn later, slid two inches down the wall and hung crooked forever after, and no servant would ever straighten it. The building itself flinched, six hundred years of fossilized First Speech rippling like the surface of a pond struck by a thrown stone, and at the center of the ripple, the dark shackle cracked.

Not broke. Cracked—one hairline fracture across the drinking-darkness, leaking light that wasn't light, and Cassia gasped as the pressure around her name loosened like a fist unclenching finger by finger.

Then the second shackle shattered outright, iron exploding off her wrist, and she was free.

"Good *heavens*," Odile breathed.

Wren swayed. Speaking it had cost her something—not blood, not breath, but something subtler, like she'd shouted and lost a portion of her own echo. She felt Bram's hand steady her shoulder, warm and certain, and leaned into it for exactly one second, which was all the time they had.

Because the corridor had gone quiet again. Wrong-quiet. The kind of quiet that means something enormous has stopped to listen.

And then, from everywhere at once—from the walls, the floor, the stones, the whole vast bound body of the palace—came a voice. It was the king's voice, courtly and mild, and it filled the dungeon the way water fills a lung.

"*There you are,*" it said.

Everyone froze. Bram's hand tightened on Wren's shoulder. Odile's fingers went to the vials at her belt and stopped, useless. Ninepence, incredibly, inclined his head, the way an old professional greets a new client.

"*Little key,*" the Hollow King said, gentle as a lullaby, terrible as arithmetic. "*You've cost me such a great deal, these past weeks. Do you know what you just did? Every binding older than my reign just shivered. My prisoners felt hope. My wards felt doubt. Do you understand what doubt does to a house built on oaths?*" A pause, and then, almost fondly: "*No. Of course you don't. You're a child with a hammer in her throat. Come to me, and I will teach you what you are. Refuse, and I will teach you what you aren't.*"

The voice withdrew—not gone, Wren understood, but retreating to the throne room the way a spider retreats to the center of its web, feeling every strand.

Cassia got to her feet, rubbing her freed wrist, and her face had gone pale but set. "Well," she said. "I believe that counts as accepting my application as witness."

"It counts as signing our death warrants," Odile said. "Move. Guards, four flights up, coming fast—the walls will have told them precisely where we are, since apparently the architecture is on his payroll."

Bram was already moving, pulling Wren along, and Wren ran because running was correct, but part of her mind stayed behind in that crack in the shackle, turning over what the Hollow King had said.

*A child with a hammer in her throat.*

He was wrong, and the wrongness of it steadied her. She wasn't a hammer. Hammers break things indiscriminately. What she had done in that cell was precise: one sentence, one fracture, one name released. She was not destruction. She was *editing.*

They burst out of the dungeon stairwell into a service corridor, and Odile, running with surprising speed for a woman whose entire career was conducted seated, barked: "Which way, Almeida? The kitchens or the chapel route?"

"The chapel route," Bram said. "It's older. Older means more bindings—" she glanced down at Wren, and something complicated crossed her face, pride and fear braided together— "which means more doors only she can open. We're committed now. We've rung the bell."

"We haven't just rung it," Ninepence said, gliding along beside them at floor level like a leaf blown by a purposeful wind. "We've answered it. There's a difference, and the difference is that now the Hollow King must come to *us*. A bound thing cannot leave its anchor-stone for long. Every minute he spends hunting, the solstice thins him further. Child—" the eyeless face turned toward her— "you haven't merely revealed our position. You've started a clock he cannot stop."

Behind them, deep in the palace, something vast shifted its weight, and the stones sang a single alarmed note in First Speech, and Wren—breathless, terrified, alive—understood the translation without wanting to.

*Run,* the palace said.

They ran.

But ahead of them, at the end of the corridor where it bent toward the old chapel, stood a figure Wren knew from every nightmare she'd had since the snow: tall, courteous, wearing the king's face the way a glove wears a hand, waiting with the patience of something that had ruled for thirty years and intended to rule thirty more.

He had not waited for the hunt.

He had come to meet them himself.
