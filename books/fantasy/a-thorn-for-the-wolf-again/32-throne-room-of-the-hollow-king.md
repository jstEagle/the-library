## 32. Throne Room of the Hollow King

The corridor had been long. This room was longer.

I don't mean in feet. I've stood in the throne room a hundred times, at the edge of the dais with my tasting spoon and my invisible collar, close enough to see the pores on the king's face while he ate quail and pretended I wasn't there. It's not a large room. It's the kind of room built to make you feel small by other means—height, echo, the way every footstep comes back to you a half-beat late, as if the floor is considering whether to return it.

Tonight the room felt longer than any road I've ever walked, because tonight I walked it knowing what sat at the end of it.

Wren's hand was in mine. Small, cold, gripping hard enough to hurt, which I counted as a good sign. Pain means alive. Pain means she hadn't spoken yet, hadn't turned the key, hadn't told the thing in the crown exactly where we were—which, of course, it already knew. You don't sneak up on something that answers questions before they're asked. We weren't here to surprise it. We were here because there was no other door left.

Cassia walked on Wren's other side, her excommunicated vestments hidden under a guard's cloak, her lips moving in silence—not prayer, I'd learned, but rehearsal. She was rehearsing the rite the way I used to rehearse poison antidotes: not because it would save her, but because knowing the steps was the only dignity left when the outcome was out of your hands.

Isolde was behind us somewhere, holding the doors. She'd said, "If this fails, seal them from the outside," in the flat voice of a woman who had spent years mapping her father's wrongness and had finally run out of map. I didn't look back at her. Looking back is how you lose arguments you haven't started yet.

The throne was occupied.

He looked like the king. Of course he did. Thirty years of practice; thirty years of wearing that face the way I once wore immunity, so thoroughly you forget it isn't yours. The silver beard, the heavy jaw, the eyes that had always been a half-second ahead of the world. He sat with his hands on the armrests like a man who owned the armrests, the room, the building, the idea of buildings.

"Bram Almeida," he said, warmly, before I'd said a word. "Royal taster. Keeper of small deaths. And lately—" the smile widened by a degree, precise as a ledger entry, "—keeper of rather larger things."

"You know why we're here," I said.

"I know *why* you think you're here." He rose. That was wrong, some animal part of me noted—the king never rose first, never moved toward anyone, made the world come to him—but the wrongness slid off the shape like water off wax. He came down the three steps of the dais and stopped at a courteous distance, the distance of a man greeting an old servant. "You're here because a child told you a story, and a dead broker confirmed it, and a priestess who couldn't let go of one memory gave it architecture. But stories aren't terms, Mistress Almeida. I deal in terms."

"Then let's discuss terms."

"*There* she is." He said it with genuine delight, and that was the worst moment so far, worse than the shadow moving wrong, worse than answering questions before they were asked. Because he sounded proud of me. "The woman who balances impossible books. Do you know how rare that is? To find someone who understands that everything is a transaction? Kings pretend otherwise. Priests pretend otherwise. But you—you tasted arsenic for eleven years and called it employment. You know the true grammar of the world."

Behind me, Cassia's silent rehearsal stopped.

"I'm not here to trade," I said.

"Everyone is here to trade. Some simply haven't read their own contracts." He gestured, and the torches along the walls dimmed—not extinguished, *dimmed*, the light itself leaning toward him like a courtier. "But very well. Let us do this properly, since you came all this way through my corridors, past my guards, using my daughter's name as a key. Let us review the account."

* * *

"The war ended," he said.

That was how he began. Not with a threat, not with a demand. With a fact, laid down like the first line of a ledger.

"You were a child when it ended, so allow me to summarize. Eleven years of it. The border provinces burned. Your own city hosted refugees for four winters—I believe you remember the soup kitchens, though perhaps not; you've sold a great many memories lately, and I hear you no longer recall why you became a taster at all." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "My apologies. That was unkind. But accuracy often is."

I kept my face still. Eleven years of tasting had taught me that. Whatever he thought he knew about my ledgers, he would get nothing from my face.

"Eleven years," he went on, pacing now, slow, the king's body moving with the ease of long ownership. "Two hundred thousand dead, give or take the ones nobody counted. And then, in a single night, it stopped. No treaty. No negotiated surrender. One morning the armies simply went home, and the peace has held for thirty years since. Thirty years without a famine caused by requisition. Thirty years without a single execution for treason. Ask yourself, taster: when did you last hear of a war?"

I hadn't. Not a real one. Skirmishes, bandits, a succession squabble in a country whose name I'd forgotten—but no war. I'd lived my whole adult life inside a peace so complete it had become weather.

"That was not diplomacy," he said. "Diplomacy is what men do between wars. That was *removal*. I removed the appetite for it. From him, and then—from everyone who mattered. The generals woke up tired of ambition. The chancellors woke up reasonable. The young king—who was, I will grant you, a decent man in the way that decent men are dangerous—woke up willing to trade himself for his people, and I accepted the trade, and I have honored it every day since. Every harvest brought in. Every granary full. Every gallows empty." He spread the king's hands, palms up, an accountant presenting a clean column. "Show me the ledger where that is a loss."

And here is the thing I will admit only here, in this account, which no one may ever read: for a moment—a real moment, a moment with weight and temperature—he almost had me.

Because I'm a bookkeeper. I've spent my life among numbers, and the numbers he was reciting were good numbers. Two hundred thousand dead against thirty years of peace. A king traded for a kingdom. Any auditor in the world would sign that page.

"You took a man's life," I said.

"I *hold* a man's life. There is a difference, and you of all people should appreciate it. Collateral is not theft." His voice softened, and the softness was worse than the warmth. "He consented. You'll have learned that by now—it's in the old contract Odile Voss sold him, and yes, I know she's told you, and yes, I know what it cost her to tell you. He bound me willingly. He wanted a power above kings that kings could not become, and then he discovered that the only available power was me, and he paid the price he'd set himself. I did not deceive him. I have never deceived anyone. Deception is inefficient. I simply *am*, and people arrange themselves around me, the way water arranges itself around a stone."

"And the girl?" I said. "Did she arrange herself around you too? Or did you hunt her through everyone she loved?"

Something moved behind the king's face. Not anger. Something older and more patient—the look of a man hearing a number he expected but dislikes.

"The girl is a flaw in the contract," he said. "A clause written in blood by frightened people who wanted a failsafe against their own wisdom. She is a key looking for a lock, and keys are miserable things, Mistress Almeida—they exist only to end something. Have you considered what she *is*? Not who. What. She speaks a language that unmakes oaths. Every binding in this kingdom loosens a little when she breathes. The wards on your Guild vaults. The vows of your priests. The oath your captain of guards swore to my daughter." His eyes—his borrowed eyes—found Wren, and I stepped sideways without deciding to, putting myself between them, and he smiled at that, gently, as if I'd confirmed something on his behalf. "She doesn't free people. She frees *things*. And most of what is bound in this world is bound for excellent reasons."

"She's a child."

"She's a *term*." For the first time, an edge came into the voice, thin and bright as wire. "As are you. As am I. The difference between us, taster, is that I have read all of our contracts and you have read only yours—and even that imperfectly, since you signed away your name without reading the re-entry clause. Did Odile show you that clause? No? She does that. Guilt makes poor clerks." He sighed, and the sigh was the king's sigh, the one I'd heard over bland reports a hundred mornings, and my chest hurt with the familiarity of it. "Here is my final offer, and it is generous, and I want you to understand that I know precisely what it costs me to make it."

* * *

He named it item by item, the way Odile named prices, and I understood suddenly where the Guild had learned its manners.

"Your name, restored. Not merely returned—*restored*. Everyone who forgot you will remember. The debtors you met in the undercity will speak of you fondly. Your childhood home, back in your memory, with the smell of whatever your mother cooked. Your immunity—I keep a store of thirty years' worth, purchased fairly from you, and I will return it with interest, and you will never again stand in a dining hall with your life resting on your wits alone." He paused, letting each item settle. "And the child walks out of this room untouched. Fed, clothed, educated, protected by the crown for as long as she lives. I swear it in the only language I cannot break—my own nature. I do not lie. Test me on it. Ask the dead broker."

"And in exchange," I said, because someone had to say it.

"In exchange, she never speaks the word. She goes to live—happily, safely, *willingly*—in a tower I will build her, with books and gardens and whatever a growing mind requires, and the binding holds, and the peace holds, and no one dies tonight or ever of anything I can prevent." He stepped closer, and up close the king's face was flawless in a way faces shouldn't be, like a coin worn smooth. "Bram Almeida. You have spent your whole life swallowing deaths so that others could eat safely. I am offering you the chance to stop. One word from you—one honest 'yes,' freely given, which is more than the last king managed—and every account you have ever opened closes clean."

The torches burned low. Wren's hand trembled in mine. And the terrible thing, the thing I set down here in plain ink because a ledger that hides its entries is worthless: I believed him. Every word. I looked at the arithmetic—peace against freedom, a tower against a throne-room, one silenced child against two hundred thousand already dead and none dying new—and the arithmetic *worked*. That was his genius, if genius is the word. He didn't lie. He just kept the books so well that cruelty came out balanced.

Almost.

"One question," I said.

"Ask."

"If it's all true—if you've kept the peace, honored the contract, never lied—then why does your shadow move wrong?"

Silence. Real silence, the first since we'd entered, the kind that has mass.

And the king smiled—not the king's smile, not anymore, but something underneath it, vast and patient and terribly, beautifully empty—and said:

"Because there is nothing here to cast it."

The shape came off him like water off glass.
