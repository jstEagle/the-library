## 33. The Willingness of the Bound

I had spent my whole life learning to speak to a man who wasn't listening. It turns out that was the apprenticeship. All those years of addressing my father's back as he stared at nothing over his breakfast, of delivering reports to eyes that slid off me like rain off oiled leather—I had been rehearsing for this without knowing it.

The difference was that tonight, in the crypt beneath the palace chapel, someone might actually answer.

Cassia had been arranging her rite for two hours, and she arranged it the way my father's stewards arranged a banquet: everything in its place, nothing accidental, and an air of serene authority designed to make you feel like a child underfoot. Candles in a ring of seven. A bowl of water from the old well, the one they'd sealed when I was six because children kept reporting voices in it. Salt. Ash. And at the center, on a cloth so old it had gone the color of old teeth, the crown itself.

Not the one the Hollow King wore. That one sat on a head upstairs and gleamed like a lie told beautifully. This was the true crown—the first one, the coronation crown, which Cassia had smuggled out of the reliquary in a bread basket, because apparently excommunication teaches you skills.

"Princess." Cassia didn't look up from her work. "You're pacing."

"I'm inspecting."

"You're wearing a groove."

I stopped. Wren was sitting cross-legged against a pillar with Bram's ledger open on her knees—she hadn't let it out of arm's reach since the vault—and she watched me with those too-old eyes of hers. Eight years old and she looked at me the way my tutors used to when I got an answer right by accident.

"He'll be angry," I said. "My father. If he's still in there. If there's still enough of him to be angry."

"If he's still in there," Cassia said, striking a match, "he has been buried alive inside his own triumph for thirty years. Anger would be the healthy response."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not paid to comfort you. I'm not paid at all anymore. That's rather the point of excommunication." She lit the seventh candle and straightened, and something in her face changed—the wry housekeeper fell away and left the priestess underneath, the one who had stood before an altar while something answered from inside a crown. "Isolde. Before we begin, you should understand what you're asking."

"I'm asking to talk to my father."

"You're asking to open a door that thing upstairs nailed shut. The binding holds him as much as it holds—it. They're knotted together, king and oath-eater, and when we loosen the knot tonight, both of them will feel it. The Hollow King will know exactly where we are and exactly what we're doing. Everything after this moment is a race." She met my eyes. "And there is no guarantee your father's remnant can consent to anything. Thirty years is a long time to be drowned. What's left may be very small. Very confused. It may not be able to tell you from the thing that ate him."

"Then I'll introduce myself slowly," I said. "I've had practice being overlooked."

Wren made a sound—not quite a laugh. When I glanced at her, she signed something quick with her hands, then caught herself and spoke aloud, carefully, in our tongue: *"He will know you. You smell like him."*

"I beg your pardon?"

"Bram smells of swallowed deaths," Wren said, as if explaining arithmetic to a slow student. "You smell of him. Of the king. Blood remembers blood even when minds forget." She shrugged, eight years old again, and went back to her ledger.

Cassia and I exchanged a look over the candles. Neither of us had an argument against that. Neither of us wanted one.

* * *

The rite was not what I expected. I expected incense and thunder; I got a woman murmuring over a bowl of water like a cook testing whether soup needed salt.

"Hold this." Cassia pressed the true crown into my hands. It was heavier than it looked, or perhaps it only felt that way, the way a letter feels heavier when you already know what's in it. "Cold iron under gilt. The old kings wore it to remember they could die. Then someone decided remembering was undignified." Her mouth twisted. "I officiated his coronation, you know. Your father's. He was so young. He kept looking at his hands as if he'd never seen them before, and when I asked him if he came willingly—he had to say it, it's the oldest part of the office, *do you come willingly*—he paused. Just a breath. I thought it was nerves." She shook her head. "It was grief. He'd already decided what he was going to do, and he was saying goodbye to himself. I heard it answer from inside the crown and I told myself I'd imagined it, because imagining it was survivable and believing it was not. Remembering too much is a sin in my former church. So I forgot on purpose, for thirty years, and called it faith."

"And now?"

"Now I'd rather be damned and useful than blessed and blind." She nodded at the crown. "Speak into it. Not to it—to him. The binding was sworn through this metal; it's the oldest thread of the knot. Speak as his daughter, and mean it, and if there's anything left of Aldric in there, the words will find him."

Aldric. My father's name. I realized, with a small cold shock, that I hadn't spoken it aloud since he died—since the thing wearing him took the throne and everyone agreed, in the polite way of courts, to stop using the name of the dead. Names were dangerous around here. Everyone in this conspiracy kept trading theirs away like bad coin.

I knelt. The stone was cold through my trousers. The candle flames leaned toward the crown as if eavesdropping.

"Father," I said.

Nothing. Of course nothing. I felt immediately ridiculous, a grown woman kneeling in a crypt talking to a hat.

"It's Isolde." My voice did something embarrassing on the second syllable. I pushed through it. "You won't know how long it's been. Time probably doesn't—wherever you are. It's been thirty years. I'm thirty-one. You missed all of it. You missed my mother dying, which I think might have broken your heart, so possibly that was a mercy. You missed me growing up wrong in every way you'd have disapproved of and several ways you'd have secretly liked." I laughed, or something near it. "You missed everything, and the thing that took your place kept the kingdom at peace, and I have spent years hating myself because some mornings I looked at its peace and thought: is that worth it? Was your trade worth it? And now I'm here to ask you to end, and I don't even know if asking is another kind of theft."

The candles guttered—all seven at once, though there was no draft.

The water in the bowl shivered.

"Isolde."

Not from the crown. From everywhere—from the stone, from the water, from the space behind my ears—a voice like a man speaking through a wall of deep water, each word arriving worn smooth, half-dissolved. I knew the voice. I would have known it from across a battlefield, from inside a dream, from the far side of death. I had spent thirty-one years memorizing it and telling myself I'd forgotten.

"Papa," I said, and I was nine years old again, and I hated it and I didn't care.

*Small,* the voice said, wondering. *I am very small. Have I always been?*

"No," I said fiercely. "No, you were—you were enormous. You were the biggest thing in any room. You carried me on your shoulders at the harvest festival and the whole crowd cheered and you said—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "You said, *they're not cheering for me, little star, they're cheering because I haven't dropped you.*"

Silence. The candles steadied. In the bowl, the water had gone perfectly still, mirror-flat, and in it I saw—not a reflection. A face trying to assemble itself out of dark water: my father's face, younger than the mask upstairs, younger than memory, flickering like a portrait painted on wind.

*Thirty years,* the remnant said. *I bargained for peace. Did it hold?*

"Thirty years of peace," I said. "No war. No famine. The borders held. The thing upstairs kept every promise it made to you."

*Then it will argue,* he said, and there was something in the drowned voice that might have been humor, worn thin as leaf-gold. *It argued with me for three nights before I bound it. It is very good. Do not listen to it alone.*

"I won't. I brought witnesses. A priestess who remembers too much, a taster who's survived more poisons than your physicians ever identified, and a child who is—Papa, she's the unbinding. She's the failsafe you built into the oath-line. She's been hiding in my city for weeks and the crown has been hunting her and I need—" I stopped. Made myself say it plainly, because he had taught me that plain speech was the only honest currency. "I need your consent. The binding requires the willingness of the bound. If you consent to end, the knot comes undone. If you don't—if any part of you wants to keep existing inside it—then we fail, and it keeps you, and it takes her."

The water-face flickered. For a long moment the crypt held only candle-hiss and my own heartbeat, loud and stupid in my ears.

*I dreamed,* the remnant said at last. *All this time, I think I dreamed. There was a garden. Your mother was in it. I could never reach her, but I could see her, and that was almost—* The voice frayed, reassembled. *Was the peace good, Isolde? Truly? Or did it only look good from the throne?*

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and lying to the dead is a debt you can't discharge. "People are fed. The roads are safe. But Papa—the price was paid by other people. People it hunted. People whose names it erased. Peace bought with someone else's coin isn't peace. It's just a bill that hasn't come due."

*Yes.* The word came out steady, and the water in the bowl trembled with something that was not sorrow. *That is what I told it. That is why I built the failsafe. No one should hold absolute power—not even me. Especially not me.* A pause, and when he spoke again the voice was closer, clearer, as if the wall of water were thinning. *I was young and I thought I could manage it forever. Manage the monster by feeding it my shape. But a bargain needs an ending, little star, or it isn't a bargain. It's a cage with me as the lock.*

"Papa—"

*Let me finish. I have thirty years of unfinished sentences.* The face in the water was sharper now, and I could see the gray coming into his temples, the exact age he'd been at the end. *I am tired in a way that has nothing to do with sleep. Whatever is left of me has been holding this knot from the inside, and I am so tired, and my daughter is kneeling on cold stone asking my leave to rest. Do you know what I hear, when you ask?*

"What?" I whispered.

*I hear that you grew up brave. Stubborn. Unwise in the correct directions.* The humor again, unmistakable now, my father's dry ghost of a smile. *Your mother's doing.*

"Mostly hers," I agreed, crying without any dignity whatsoever.

*Then witness me.* The voice rose, and the candles flared tall and white, and every hair on my arms stood up as the remnant of King Aldric spoke not to me but to the binding itself, to the knot, to the thing upstairs that must have felt every word like a blade between its ribs: *I am Aldric, son of Maro, and I came willingly, and I leave willingly. Let the oath be answered. Let the debt be paid. Let go.*

The bowl cracked down the middle. Clean, like an egg.

The water ran out across the stone and steamed where it touched the salt ring, and the face in it—my father's face—looked at me for one last instant with an expression I will carry until I die and possibly past it: relief. Pure, unburdened, bottomless relief, the look of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

*Little star,* he said, faint as a coin dropping down a well. *Don't drop yourself.*

And then the crypt was just a crypt, and the crown in my lap was just cold iron, and I was holding it and shaking and Wren had crossed the room without my noticing and put her small hand flat on my knee, the way you'd steady a spooked horse.

Cassia blew out the candles one by one, unhurried, a woman closing a shop at the end of a very long day. "Well," she said. "That's the third term satisfied. Word, witness, willingness." She looked at me over the last candle flame before pinching it dead. "We have everything we need."

"We have less than an hour," I said. My voice was wrecked. I didn't fix it. "The moment he spoke, it knew. It'll have sealed the throne room. It'll be gathering everything it has."

"Yes," Cassia said. "So we'd best not waste any of it grieving. Grieve tomorrow. Tomorrow is promised to no one, but tonight belongs to us, and tonight we walk down the long corridor and knock on a door that has been waiting thirty years for someone brave enough to knock." She shouldered her satchel of stolen relics and headed for the stairs, and at the foot of them she paused, without turning around. "For what it's worth, Princess—he sounded like a good man. The kingdom will want to know that, afterward. Whoever tells them should tell it true."

Upstairs, faint through the chapel floor, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The alarm.

Wren closed her ledger and stood, and looked up at me with her terrible calm eyes, and said, in First Speech, one soft syllable that made the cracked bowl hum in sympathy—

and somewhere above us, every lock in the palace opened at once.
