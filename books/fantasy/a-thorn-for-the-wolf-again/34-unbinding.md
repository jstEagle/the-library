## 34. Unbinding

The throne room at midnight on solstice is a cathedral of cold.

I have stood in this room perhaps four hundred times in my life, and every one of those times I was furniture. A taster is furniture that swallows. You learn to be invisible the way a chair is invisible, the way a candelabrum is invisible. But tonight I walk down the long aisle between the pillars with my boots ringing on marble like a woman announcing herself, and there is no version of me left that knows how to disappear.

Partly because I sold most of her.

The crown sits on the king's head the way it always has—slightly too heavy for him, slightly too still. The Hollow King has not bothered with court tonight. There are no petitioners, no chamberlains, no ceremony. Just the thing wearing thirty years of borrowed majesty, seated on a throne built for a man it ate, watching us come toward it with eyes like coins left on a dead man's eyes.

Isolde walks on my left. Cassia on my right. Wren's small hand is in mine, and her palm is damp, and she is trembling, and she does not let go.

Behind us, somewhere back through the corridors Isolde opened, the palace holds its breath. Guards who owe the princess debts of honor are looking elsewhere. Doors stand unlocked that should not be. The whole building is a held note.

"You came," says the Hollow King.

It speaks in the king's voice, of course. It has had three decades to perfect it. But I have spent years listening to that voice over poisoned breakfasts, cataloguing its rhythms the way I catalogue flavors, and I can hear the seams now—the half-second lag where it assembles each sentence from stolen parts, the faint wrongness of a mouth that learned speech from outside rather than growing it from inside.

"We did," I say.

"I offered you everything." It rises from the throne, and the movement is wrong in the way its shadow has always been wrong—a beat behind the body, or ahead of it, I could never decide. "Your name. Your immunity. The child's safety. All you had to do was nothing. Doing nothing is the one skill your kind has mastered."

"My kind," I say, "being furniture."

"Being *survivors*." The word comes out almost gentle. That's the worst of it, how gentle it can be. Thirty years of peace, it will tell you. Thirty years without war. Ask the border towns. Ask the granaries. "You of all people understand trade, Bram Almeida. Everything costs. I simply paid."

"Someone else paid," I say. "That's not the same thing."

Something moves behind the king's face. Not an expression—an absence of one, a flicker where feeling should sit and doesn't. When it speaks again, the voice has shed some of its polish.

"The real king bound me to end a war," it says. "Do you know what that war was? No. Of course not. It was before your time. Cities burned. Children starved in ditches while two armies fought over a river's name. I ended it. In thirty years I have not signed a single execution order. I have kept the granaries full and the borders quiet and the guilds fat and content. And you would unmake all of it—for what? For *authenticity*? For the principle that a throne should be occupied by something with a heartbeat?"

"For the principle," I say, "that no one should hold absolute power. Including him." I nod at the hollow shape wearing the king. "He understood that. It's why he did what he did. He just hoped someone would finish the job."

"And here you are." The Hollow King descends the steps of the dais. Each footfall lands without sound, which is somehow worse than sound. "A food-taster. A ledger-keeper. A woman who has sold so much of herself that if I cut you open, I doubt there'd be enough left to fill a thimble. What do you imagine you're carrying into this room that can hurt me?"

Wren's hand tightens in mine.

"She's not carrying anything," I say. "She *is* something."

And the Hollow King looks at Wren for the first time.

I have seen it look at many things. Courtiers. Petitioners. Its own reflection, once, in a wine glass I was tasting beside—I remember the way it studied itself, the way a man studies a coat he isn't sure fits. But when it looks at my girl, something happens in the air of the room. The candles lean. The shadows pull toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"There you are," it says softly. "The failsafe. The little anchor. Do you know what you are, child?"

Wren doesn't answer. She can't—not in any language the Hollow King would find useful. But she lifts her chin, and I see Odile in the set of her jaw, and I see myself in the flat calm of her eyes, and my heart breaks a little with pride right there in the enemy's throne room.

"She knows exactly what she is," I say.

"Then let us conclude our business." It spreads the king's hands, and the gesture is almost gracious. "Speak your word, little key. Unbind me. Witness it, priestess. Consent—" and here it pauses, and something old and tired and genuinely amused moves through the borrowed face, "—consent has already been arranged."

Cassia steps forward.

I have known Mother Cassia for less than a season, and in that season she has been captured, interrogated, nearly eaten, and excommunicated twice more by people who never liked her anyway. She is seventy years old and built like a weathered fence post, and when she walks to the center of the throne room and plants her feet, the marble seems to acknowledge her.

"I witness," she says.

Her voice fills the room. Not loud—full. It's the voice of a woman who has spent fifty years saying words that mean things, and the room remembers her. The candles straighten. The shadows recoil to their proper places. Even the Hollow King shifts, just slightly, on feet that make no sound.

"Witnessed and recorded," Cassia says. "In the name of the oath-line, in the name of the binding sworn at coronation, in the name of the man who gave himself so that no one would ever hold what he held. Let it be entered."

Somewhere far away—or very close; with the Guild I've never been sure of the difference—a page turns.

Isolde steps forward next.

She is in mourning black, though the court will assume it's fashion. Her hands shake. She clasps them together and makes them stop.

"My father," she says, and her voice cracks on the word, and she lets it crack, because Isolde has decided that cracking is honest and honesty is armor. "My father consents. He told me himself. He has been waiting thirty years for someone to read his last testament and do the arithmetic he couldn't do alone. He traded himself to stop a war. He asked only that it not be forever." She draws a breath. "On his behalf, and with his voice in my ears, I speak his willingness. Let him rest."

The room changes.

I don't know how else to describe it. The air pressure shifts. The candles flame tall and blue-white. Somewhere in the walls, in the floor, in the bones of the palace itself, something old groans like a ship coming about—and then the Hollow King makes a sound.

It is not a scream. Screams are for throats. This comes from deeper, from whatever passes for its chest, a sound like a bell being unmade, like a chord resolving into noise. The king's face begins to slide.

Not melt. *Slide.* Like a mask worn too long, like paint lifting from wet wood. Beneath it there is—

I expected horror. I expected the void, the absence, the beautiful terrible nothing that Odile warned me about. What I see instead is worse, because it is lovely. A shape made of negative space, a silhouette cut from the world rather than added to it, edges rimmed with light that isn't light. It is beautiful the way a hole in a roof is beautiful when the stars come through.

It is also, I notice, getting smaller.

The binding is unwinding around it, thread by thread, and each thread takes a piece of its size with it. Wren stands at the center of the room now—I don't remember letting go of her hand, but she's there, planted like Cassia, planted like her whole stubborn lineage of people who refuse to be furniture—and she opens her mouth.

What comes out is not a word in any language I know.

It is older than kingdoms. It is the sound the world made before anyone was around to write it down. Three syllables, maybe four—I couldn't count them if I tried; they arrive all at once, the way a wave arrives, the way grief arrives. The candles go out. Every candle in the throne room, every candle in the palace, every candle in the city below, I'd swear it—all at once, and in the dark there is only the Hollow King's unraveling light and Wren's voice holding the room together like a keel.

The dark lasts three heartbeats.

When the flames return, low and steadied, the Hollow King is the size of a child.

It kneels on the marble where the dais used to be—the dais is gone, dissolved into dust that drifts like snow—and it looks up at us with no face at all, and it laughs.

"Clever," it says, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "Clever, clever, clever. But you've read the contract, haven't you, taster? You know how bindings balance. Nothing leaves the world for free. Not even me." The laugh again, thinner. "Especially not me. Something must weigh against my going. A soul of equal weight. That was the term. That was always the term."

The temperature drops.

I feel it before I understand it—that familiar cold, the cold of the counting house at night, the cold of ledgers opening themselves. The Hollow King rises off the floor, or rather the floor stops insisting on it, and the unraveling light bends toward me like a compass needle finding north.

"*Her,*" it says. "The one who has already sold herself piece by piece. The one who is mostly collateral already. She weighs almost nothing, and yet—oh, this is delicious—she weighs *exactly enough*. The Guild's arithmetic never lies, taster. You have been pre-paid. You have been discounted inventory since the day you signed your name away. Give her to me, and the binding closes clean, and everyone walks away, and the kingdom keeps its peace, and nobody has to remember any of this."

"No," Isolde says.

"No," Cassia says.

Wren says nothing, but she moves—quick as a striking hawk, quick as the girl who unlocked a cell with a sentence—and puts herself between me and the thing, arms spread, and shouts something in First Speech that makes the stones weep dust.

The Hollow King regards her with its beautiful absence.

"Adorable," it says. "But the terms are the terms. The witness stands ready. The key has spoken. The bound consents. Only the counterweight remains, and the counterweight is *owed*." Its voice drops, goes silky, goes intimate. It speaks, suddenly, in Odile's voice. "Come now, Bram. You know how the Guild works. Debts are collected. Always."

And then—in my mother's voice. My father's. The cook from the house I can no longer remember. Everyone I have lost, everyone I have sold, everyone whose faces live now only in journals written by a stranger: "*You were always going to end up here. Empty. Spendable. Why fight the arithmetic?*"

And here is the thing.

Here is the thing I should have understood from the beginning, standing in Odile's back office among the vials of borrowed courage and the jar of last words: the Hollow King is right about the arithmetic. It is entirely, perfectly right. I have spent my whole life trading pieces of myself away—one immunity, one memory, one name at a time—and the sum total of Bram Almeida is very nearly zero. I am the cheapest soul in this room. I am discount inventory. I am exactly what the contract calls for.

But arithmetic was never the point.

Odile taught me that, in her way, back when I thought the Guild served whoever paid and never asked why. The ledgers record what was traded. They cannot record what it was *for*. There is no column for love. There is no line item for the taste of snow on a child's hair, or the sound of a princess choosing honesty, or an old priestess planting her feet like a fence post against the wind. The Guild's books balance because they leave out the only entries that matter.

So I step forward.

Past Wren. Past her outflung arms and her furious silence. I step into the cold, into the bending light, into the space where the Hollow King waits with its hands full of everyone I've lost, and I hear Wren scream—not in First Speech, just a girl's voice, raw and terrified—and I keep walking.

"You want a soul of equal weight," I say.

The unraveling light stills.

"Fine. Take stock." I spread my arms, the way Cassia spreads hers, the way you offer something rather than surrender it. "Thirty years of poison-immunity, already yours—sold fair, entered fair. My childhood home, my mother's kitchen, the reason I first learned to taste—sold. My name, withdrawn from circulation, held as collateral these six months past. Every piece accounted for. Every debt documented. Check your ledgers. I'm worth almost nothing."

"*Almost,*" the Hollow King breathes.

"Almost," I agree. "There's one entry left. One thing in me that was never sold, never traded, never entered in any book. Because I didn't sign it away, and I didn't lose it, and I didn't forget it." I look at Wren. She is crying without sound, and she is the bravest person in this kingdom, and I am going to say this next part to her and not to the monster. "The night you came to my door, I gained something the Guild has no column for. A daughter. Not by blood. By choice. By *willingness*—and willingness, as it happens, is the whole trick of this business. The king consented to end. I consent to pay. Freely given. No coercion, no contract, no collateral. A gift."

The Hollow King goes very still.

"That's not how trade works," it says.

"No," I say. "It isn't. That's rather the point."

I turn to Cassia. "Witness."

The old priestess's eyes are streaming, but her voice is iron. "Witnessed."

I turn to the empty air where the Guild lives, wherever it lives, and I speak the words Odile taught me, the ones for closing an account no contract covers:

"By my own hand, freely given: I enter Bram Almeida into the record. Full payment. Final entry. Let the books balance."

The world exhales.

The Hollow King comes apart like smoke in wind, like a chord resolving at last into true silence, and as it unravels it reaches for me—it has to reach for me, the terms demand it—and what it takes is not my heart, not my memories, not my life.

It takes my name.

All of it. Every syllable. The word my mother chose, the name Odile scolded, the name Wren learned first of all her new vocabulary. It pours out of me like water from a broken jar, and I feel it go, and I feel the shape of me go with it—the shape that other people's memories give a person, the outline drawn by being known.

The last thing I think, as the throne room tilts and the candles gutter and Wren's arms catch me before the floor can, is that the books finally balance.

The last thing I feel is her holding on.

* * *

Dust settles on marble. Dust settles on a crown lying on its side like a hoop dropped by a child. Dust settles, most of all, on the true king, who crumbles gently mid-gesture, mid-blessing, his remnant finally allowed to finish dying after thirty years of waiting permission—and Princess Isolde catches his hand as it comes apart, and holds empty air, and does not let go.

Cassia speaks the rites for the dead. Her voice holds.

Wren holds a woman who is becoming a stranger in her arms, and watches the stranger's face go smooth and open and unmarked, like a page before writing. She says the name. It slides off. She says it again, louder, and the word makes no purchase anywhere—not in the room, not in her own mouth, not in the ledger that lies open on the floor where it fell, its pages fluttering to a stop on an entry that reads, in plain ink, in a hand no one recognizes:

*Account closed. Paid in full.*

The candles burn steady. The kingdom, outside these walls, sleeps through the end of its longest captivity.

And the woman in Wren's arms opens her eyes, and looks up at the girl, and smiles politely—the polite smile of one person meeting another—and asks, in a voice with no history in it at all:

"I'm sorry. Have we met?"
