## 35. The Empty Crown

**Isolde**

I had prepared myself for many versions of my father's death. I had rehearsed them in the dark for years — the wasting death, the assassination, the quiet collapse at council that would leave me a pawn in a regency I did not yet command. What I had never rehearsed was this: my father dying twice in the same breath.

The Hollow King came apart first.

It did not scream. That was the worst of it, I think, looking back — that it did not scream. It had worn my father's face for thirty years with such care, such terrible patience, and when the unbinding word finished falling from Wren's lips it simply *stopped wearing*, the way a woman sets down a heavy coat at the end of a long day. The shape of the king sloughed off him like water off oiled cloth, and underneath there was —

I have tried to describe it since. In my private journals I have written *an absence, beautiful* and *a hunger with good posture* and once, at three in the morning, *the inside of an empty church, if the church were hungry*. None of it is right. What stood behind the shape of my father was nothing, and the nothing was lovely, and it had ruled us gently, mostly, and it looked at Bram Almeida with something I can only call gratitude.

"You would unmake me," it said, "and I find I do not object."

"Good," said Cassia, steady as a bell. She had one hand raised in the old rites, and her voice was doing what it had done in the cells below, being grit in the mechanism. "Then consent."

"I ended a war," the nothing said. Not to me. To all of us, perhaps, or to no one — the way a merchant recites his accounts at the end of the year. "Thirty years of peace. No famine came while I wore him. No borders moved. Ask her." And the absence gestured, with my father's hand, at me.

My throat closed. Because it was true. Because I had spent six years mapping my father's wrongness and the map kept showing me peace, prosperity, a kingdom that ran like a well-oiled mill grinding who knew what beneath its stone.

"It's true," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "And it doesn't matter."

"Doesn't it?" Something moved in the absence — curiosity, maybe. The last expression it ever borrowed.

"You ate him," I said. "You ate my father and you wore his hands and you signed his name and you were *better at being him than he would have been*, maybe, I'll grant you all of it — but he was mine. He was ours. And nobody gets to keep a stolen life because they kept it well."

The absence regarded me for a long moment. Then it bowed — my father's bow, the shallow courtly one he had given my mother at their wedding, I had seen it in portraits — and it began to unravel.

That is the only word. Unravel. Threads of light coming loose from nothing, unwinding upward into the rafters of the throne room, and as it went it made a sound I will hear until I die: not a shriek but a sigh, enormous, relieved, the sound of thirty years finally set down.

And then — this part happened fast, and I have ordered it wrong in every telling, so let me order it right —

Behind the dais, in the hollow where the binding had held him for three decades, the true king opened his eyes.

* * *

**Wren**

He was small. That's what nobody tells you about kings.

The thing that came out of the binding wasn't the tall man in the portraits. It was a fragment, a scrap, a man worn down to his last few coins of self — gray-faced, shaking, wrapped in light that was already coming apart at the seams. His eyes went across all of us and stopped on Izzy. On the princess. On her face.

"Isolde," he said. "You grew up."

She made a sound like something breaking kindly and went to him, and I looked away, because some things aren't for watching even when the whole room needs to witness them. That was the third term, see. *The willingness of the bound.* He had to say yes. He had to want to end. Cassia knelt beside him with her rites and her old remembered God, and the king put his ruined hand on his daughter's cheek, and I heard him say — clear, unhurried, like a man closing a ledger he'd balanced properly —

"Yes. It's time. Let it be over. Bless you, child. All of you. You were —"

And then he was dust.

Mid-blessing. Mid-word. He crumbled the way a log crumbles after the fire's gone out of it and someone brushes it — all at once, softly, into a gray heap wrapped in the last of the light. Isolde caught nothing. Her arms closed around air. The dust settled on her sleeves and she stayed kneeling there in it, holding it, and Cassia finished the blessing over the heap because a priestess finishes what she starts.

The crown fell off the dais steps and rolled, and stopped, and lay there.

Inert. Ordinary. A circlet of old gold with a dent in it, the kind of thing you'd find in a pawnshop bin. Ninepence could have told us what it cost originally. Nobody picked it up.

And Bram —

Bram was still standing where the Hollow King had gripped her. She had offered it her name — I saw her mouth move around it, saw the word go out of her like breath in winter — and the counterweight had taken it, and now the unbinding finished its arithmetic.

She folded. Not dramatically. The way a coat folds when the person wearing it stops. One moment upright, the next moment on the marble, and I was running before anyone else moved, I was faster than Odile, faster than Cassia, I got there on my knees and turned her over and her face was fine, she was breathing, her pulse was slow and even under my fingers —

"Bram," I said. "Bram. Wake up. You did it. We won, you stupid wonderful woman, wake *up*."

Her eyes opened. Brown. Tired. They went across my face politely, the way you look at a stranger who has grabbed you in the street.

"Are you hurt?" she asked. Her voice was hers. Everything else wasn't. "Someone help this girl, please — I don't — I'm sorry, I don't know you."

* * *

**Cassia**

I have officiated two coronations, forty-one funerals, and one excommunication — my own, though that was conducted by proxy and I attended as a spectator, which is a story for another ledger entirely. I know what shock looks like on the faces of the great. I watched it now, in a throne room littered with the dust of a king and the body of a woman nobody could remember.

"She's a taster," Odile Voss said, in the flat voice of a woman arguing with arithmetic. "She's been at court eleven years. She —" The Guildmistress stopped. I watched her reach for a memory and find a hole shaped exactly like Bram Almeida. "She *tastes*. There are records. There must be records."

"There are," I said grimly. "Guild ledgers. Contracts. A name withdrawn as collateral, freely given, and collected by an unbinding." I had spent enough hours in the palace cells learning how the thing thought; I understood the mechanism better than I wished to. "The counterweight took her name, and the world's memory of her went with it. That is what a name *is*, Mother Voss. The handle by which the world grips a soul. She gave hers away and everyone lost their grip."

Princess Isolde rose from the dust of her father, which takes more strength than most people will ever be asked for, and crossed the floor, and knelt on the other side of the taster.

"I know her," the princess said. It sounded like a challenge thrown at the universe. "I *know* her. She covered for me at the autumn banquet — the nightcap, the — someone laced it, and she drank it down in front of me and grinned afterward with gray lips. I have evidence. I keep evidence. I am *made* of evidence." She patted her sleeves, her bodice, the secret pockets of a woman who has spent years documenting her father's wrongness, and I saw the moment her fingers found whatever papers she carried and could not make the papers agree with her. "There's a name missing," she whispered. "Every page where she should be, there's a gap, and the gaps read smooth, like nothing was ever —"

"Wren," Odile said sharply. "Wren, love, step back from her."

Wren did not step back. Wren, whom I had known for mere weeks and loved for reasons I could not document either, had both hands on the taster's face, and was saying, over and over, in First Speech and then in plain trade-tongue and then in tears:

"You're Bram. You're Bram Almeida. You smell of swallowed deaths. You cook badly and taste perfectly. You bought me out of the snow with your own blood-armor. You're *Bram.*"

And the woman on the floor looked up at this eight-year-old girl with courtesy and terror in equal measure and said, gently, "Little one, I'm sure you've lost someone today. Everyone has. But I don't — please, I don't know whose name Bram is."

I am an old woman and I have buried most of my capacity for weeping, but I confess something moved behind my breastbone then that had no business moving.

Odile Voss did not weep. Odile Voss did what she always does, which is worse and more useful: she opened her ledger.

"The contract," she said. "The collateral clause. If a name is withdrawn as security against a contract, and the contract completes, the name returns to circulation." Her finger was down on the page, and her face, as she read, did something I had never seen it do in thirty years of uneasy alliance. "The contract completed. She paid. The clause says — it says re-entry requires a willing debtor to bear the re-inscription cost." She looked up. "The clause says it's *possible*."

"Then it's possible," said the princess, rising, dust still silvering her black sleeves like frost. "Find the debtor."

"There isn't one," Odile said. "The only person who could bear the cost — the person the debt was *for* —" She stopped. Looked down at Bram, who was sitting up now, helping Wren stand back, comforting the child who was weeping for her with perfect kindness and no memory at all.

"There's no one left who owes her anything," Odile said. "Because there's no one left who remembers her at all."

* * *

**Bram**

Here is what I knew, lying on cold marble in a room full of grieving strangers:

I knew the room. Every flagstone, every draft, the exact echo of the vault above the dais. Eleven years of walking these floors had gone into my feet if not my mind. I knew I was a food-taster; the knowledge sat in my hands, in the way they catalogued the air's bitterness without being asked. I knew I had survived something enormous, because the room was full of victory and grief in equal measure, and my body had the specific looseness of a woman who has just set down a weight she carried for decades.

I did not know why the little girl was crying into my shoulder.

I did not know the gray-haired Guildmistress, though my hand kept trying to make the Guildmistress's sign — thumb to forefinger, the counting-house greeting — as if it had done so ten thousand times. I did not know the priestess, though when she passed near me I felt safe, the way you feel safe near a stove in winter, and I distrusted feelings I couldn't account for. I did not know the princess, though she had dust on her sleeves and a jaw like someone who reads contracts for sport, and some stubborn animal part of me wanted to feed her. That was the strangest. My whole self had narrowed to a single absurd instinct: *these people haven't eaten.*

"You should eat," I heard myself say, into the ringing silence after Odile's verdict. "All of you. Grief runs on an empty stomach worse than poison does."

They stared at me.

"What?" I said. "It's true. Someone should cook. I — apparently I cook." My hands flexed. "I seem to cook well. I don't remember learning it. Isn't that funny?"

The little girl laughed and sobbed in the same breath, and clung harder, and I put my arm around her because whatever else was true, she weighed almost nothing and shook like a wire, and some debts you pay without knowing why you owe them.

Later — much later, after the guards came, after the princess stood on the dais with her father's dust on her and declared the succession in a voice that would hold, after they carried me down through corridors my feet knew and my mind didn't — after all of it, I found myself in a kitchen. My kitchen, they told me. Above a counting house. Copper pots hung in order of size, which is correct, and the knives were sharp, which is also correct, and none of it belonged to me and all of it fit my hands.

The girl wouldn't leave. The Guildmistress had tried four times to carry her off to bed and four times the girl had walked back down the stairs, and eventually the adults in the room — myself included, God help us — surrendered to the obvious.

"I'm going to fix this," the girl said. She sat at my table with a ledger open in front of her that she was far too young to be reading, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious. "I don't remember you. That's the trick, isn't it? Nobody remembers you. So nobody can owe you. So nobody can bring you back." She turned a page with a small grim hand. "But I'm going to remember you anyway. I'm going to write it all down. Everything anybody almost-remembers, everything the ledgers leak, every place your hands knew. I'm going to write you so hard the world trips over you."

"That's very sweet," I said carefully, "but I don't think —"

"And you're going to teach me to cook," she said, "because yours is better than mine is going to be for years, and when you come back you're going to want it done right."

I looked at her for a long moment. Small for eight. Snow-country pallor. Eyes older than any kingdom, said something in the back of my skull that had no owner.

"When I come back," I repeated.

"When," she said, and turned another page, and began, in a child's careful hand, to write.

I went back to my pots. I tasted the air out of habit — iron, tallow, old stone, and underneath it, faint as a watermark, a sweetness I could not name and could not stop reaching for, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

Some things, I thought, stirring, are survived.

I wondered what the rest of that sentence was.
