## 36. Aftermath of a Saved Kingdom

The first thing they asked me to sign was a proclamation of mourning.

I read it twice. *The king, our sovereign lord of thirty years' grace, has passed into the mercy of the gods, and the realm grieves.* It was true in every particular except the ones that mattered. The realm did not know what it had lost, because it had never had it. The realm had been governed for three decades by a beautiful absence wearing my father's face, and now that absence had unraveled like a dropped stitch, and the body beneath had crumbled to dust mid-blessing, and the only people who understood what any of it meant were standing in this room counting themselves and coming up one short.

"Sign it," Chancellor Merrick said gently. He was an old man who had served the Hollow King faithfully without ever knowing it, which made him either a fool or the most honest man at court, and I had decided—after much wine—both.

"I will," I said. "But we're going to add a line."

"A line?"

"*And let the record show the kingdom was saved.*" I dipped the pen myself. "If we're going to lie in official documents, we may as well lie generously."

Merrick looked at me the way old men look at young women who have recently stopped being manageable. "Your Highness—"

"Regent," I said. "We settled this yesterday. Your Highness makes me sound like I'm waiting for something."

Because I wasn't. That was the whole point of the last month. My father—the true one, the fragmentary one, the ghost who had consented to end inside his own binding—had believed no one should hold absolute power, including him. His testament said so in his own hand, in ink gone brown as old blood. So there would be no coronation. There would be a Regency Council, a charter of limits, a crown that sat on a cushion in a glass case where everyone could look at it and no one could wear it. I had argued for melting it down. Cassia—hoarse, half-dead, insufferably serene—had argued for keeping it visible. "People need to see the thing that ate their king," she said. "Otherwise they'll invent something worse." She was usually right, which I resented.

So: the Regency of Isolde, daughter of Aldric the Last, established by council vote, nine in favor, two abstentions, one lord who had to be reminded mid-session that the previous administration had eaten his memory of his own wedding and therefore perhaps owed him some deference.

That was the other thing. The reforms came fast once you knew where to look. The Guild's records—what remained of them, after the raid, after Ninepence burned rather than talk, after Odile Voss carried out armfuls of ledgers through smoke like a woman rescuing children from a fire—told us exactly which debts the crown had purchased over thirty years. Which magistrates had sold their doubts. Which historians had traded away inconvenient decades. We spent the autumn un-writing the Hollow King's shopping list, restoring memories where restoration was possible, paying compensation where it wasn't. It was the least glamorous work imaginable. I loved it with a ferocity that alarmed my advisors.

"You enjoy this too much," Merrick observed one gray afternoon, watching me cross-reference a magistrate's extracted courage against a vintner's borrowed years.

"I'm avenging my father by doing paperwork."

"Yes. Precisely my concern."

He wasn't wrong. There is a particular satisfaction in fixing things, and I had discovered I was built for it, and I suspected—I still suspect—that part of my ferocity was simply the refusal to sit still long enough to feel the shape of what was missing.

Because something was missing. Everyone felt it. No one could name it.

* * *

It showed up at dinner first.

The Regency Council dined together on formal nights, eleven places, and from the very first week there was a problem with the table. Not a shortage of chairs—we had plenty of chairs. A shortage of *space*. Servants kept setting the table for twelve and then standing over the twelfth place looking confused, as though they'd done it by accident, as though their hands remembered something their heads didn't. Cook sent up twelve portions. Twice. Three times. We finally instructed her to send ten and she sent twelve anyway, apologetically, saying the kitchen felt wrong otherwise, saying the numbers wouldn't come out right.

Cassia noticed before I did. She always does; it's her curse and her profession. She sat across from the empty twelfth setting one night and went very quiet, and then she said, "Someone used to sit there."

"Who?" I asked.

"I don't know." Her voice did something complicated. "That's the horror of it. I officiated the rite. I stood witness when the word was spoken. And there was a woman—" Her hands closed around each other. "There was a woman who gave everything, and I cannot tell you her face. I can't tell you her voice. I remember that she existed the way you remember a room you've dreamed. I know the door was there. I walk to it and there's wall."

I knew exactly what she meant, because I had it too. A door-shaped absence. I had gone back through my own journals—the meticulous, paranoid records of a princess mapping her father's wrongness—and found entries that referenced someone. *She agrees. She'll watch the wine on the feast night. If I don't return, give her my mother's ring.* Entries in my own handwriting about a person whose name had been scraped out of the world so thoroughly that even the ink seemed embarrassed, the letters gone to a faint silver shimmer, like frost on glass.

My mother's ring was missing from its box. I had no memory of giving it away. But the journal said I had, and the journal didn't lie to me; it was the only thing in that palace that never had.

"The taster," Wren said.

We were in the small library, Cassia and I and Wren, three weeks after the solstice. Wren said it the way she said most things now—flatly, precisely, with the terrible economy of a child who had learned that words have weight and chose to spend them carefully.

"There was a taster," I said carefully. "Royal food-tasters are—"

"No." Wren set down the apple she was not eating. She had grown gaunt since solstice; grief sat on her like a coat too heavy to take off. "Not a taster. *The* taster. Bram."

The name landed in the room like a coin dropped in a well. I heard it fall all the way down.

"Bram," Cassia repeated slowly, tasting it. And then her eyes filled, abruptly, incomprehensibly, tears for a stranger, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Bram Almeida. Oh. *Oh.* She stood beside me at the weir gate. She went back for me. She—" Cassia laughed, wet and broken. "I remember her now that I have the name. Names are keys. Someone took the key out of every lock in this kingdom."

"Not every lock." Wren was looking at me. "Yours held. Partly."

"Why mine?"

"Because she paid for you to keep yours." Wren said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "In the corridor, when it offered us everything—it couldn't offer you anything, because she'd already bought your remembering. It's in the ledger. I've seen the entry."

I thought of the silvered lines in my journal. I thought of the twelfth place at the table. I thought of a woman I had apparently trusted with my life and my evidence and my mother's ring, a woman whose face I could not summon if you threatened me, and I felt something crack open in my chest that I suspected would be a long time closing.

"What else is in the ledger?" I asked.

Wren looked at me with an expression I would come to know very well in the months after: the stubborn spine made visible.

"Everything," she said. "And I'm going to read all of it."

* * *

Here is what mourning looks like in a palace: bells, black cloth, a state funeral for dust. We buried an empty sarcophagus with full honors because the people needed somewhere to put their grief, and grief needs architecture or it rots where it stands. Ten thousand mourners lined the processional route. They wept for a father they'd had, a king they'd lost, a peace that had maybe—maybe—been real even if the peacemaker hadn't been. I walked behind the coffin in my father's actual cloak, the one from his testament's hiding place, smelling of cedar and thirty years of sealed air, and I let them weep, and I did not weep, because I was saving it.

I spent my weeping privately, on a woman I couldn't picture.

The reforms continued. That's the sentence I'm proudest of, actually—not any speech, not any signature. *The reforms continued.* Through the funeral and the winter and the interminable petitions, we kept un-binding the kingdom's smaller bindings. Magistrates restored or replaced. The Guild rechartered under new terms—Odile drafted them herself, and I signed them with a clause she insisted on: *the Guild shall serve whoever pays, but shall refuse any contract requiring the purchase of a child, a name freely given, or a memory of the dead.* "We were complicit for three hundred years," she told me, gray-faced, surrounded by rescued ledgers. "Three hundred years of *never asking why*. This is the smallest possible penance and I intend to perform it until I die."

"You could just retire."

"Retire to what?" she said, and I understood her completely, because retirement, for people like us, means sitting still long enough to count the empty chairs.

Wren did not attend the funeral. She attended nothing. She lived in the Guild counting house now, above the office, in rooms that had apparently belonged to someone—I found a hairpin in a drawer once, plain iron, and Wren saw me holding it and said, "Hers," and took it back with both hands, careful as a relic—and she worked. That's the only word. She worked the ledgers the way other people pray.

I visited her there in the deep of winter. The counting house smelled of ink and candle-wax and, faintly, of snow. She had cleared the great table and covered it with volumes: the master ledger, the debt rolls, the contract archives, pages of First Speech that rearranged themselves when she touched them, letters crawling like ants toward sense. She was fourteen months past eight years old and she read like a scholar and she slept, Odile told me, four hours a night, and when Odile tried to make her stop, Wren said the thing she always said:

"She wrote me into the world. I'm going to write her back."

"Wren." I sat down across from her. The candle between us guttered, steadied. "What the Hollow King took—what the unbinding cost—it isn't a debt. Debts can be collected. This was a price. Prices are paid. Paid means finished."

"Then find me the fine print," she said, "that says finished."

I opened my mouth to explain—to explain about equivalent exchange, about the nature of names withdrawn, about grief and acceptance and all the consolations adults deploy when they want children to stop hurting in ways that inconvenience them—and I looked at her face and closed my mouth again.

Because here is what I knew, from my own journals, from Cassia's fragments, from the ledger entries Wren had already shown me: Bram Almeida had signed a collateral clause. *Names withdrawn may be re-entered by a willing debtor.* She had signed it herself, months before the end, when she erased Wren from the Guild's records to keep her safe. She had written the loophole into her own erasure. Whether she'd known what she was doing—whether some part of her, the bookkeeper, the balancer of impossible accounts, had left a door open on purpose—I couldn't say. But the clause existed. Wren had found it in the second week. She had underlined it three times in pencil.

"It says willing debtor," I said at last. "You'd have to pay. Whatever the counterweight is—whatever a name weighs—you'd pay it yourself."

"I know what it costs." Wren turned the page. Her finger rested on the silvered line where a name should have been, the frost-mark of a person unwritten. "Her fluency. Her connection to the old magic. Everything I am in First Speech. I've done the arithmetic four times." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and absolutely certain, and I recognized the expression because I wore it in mirrors. "I carried the word until someone worthy read me. She read me. Now I carry her. That's how oaths work. You don't get to choose when they end."

I stayed another hour. We read together. I am a regent; I have a kingdom to run; I had a council session the next morning at which I would argue about grain tariffs with men who still occasionally set a confused twelfth place at their private tables and couldn't say why. None of it mattered less. Somewhere between the third ledger and the fifth, Wren fell asleep with her cheek on the master volume, and I sat in the candlelight watching her breathe, and I thought about my father, who had bound a monster to save a war and trusted strangers to finish the work, and I thought he would have liked her. Stubborn spine. Terrible arithmetic. Faith in fine print.

Before I left, I moved the candle away from her sleeping face, and I noticed what she'd been writing in the margin of the collateral page, over and over, in a child's careful hand, practicing:

*Bram Almeida. Bram Almeida. Bram Almeida.*

A name, rehearsed. A key, being cut.

I blew out the candle and went home to govern a saved kingdom, and I confess I did not sleep either. Because I had seen the clause, and I had done my own arithmetic, and I knew—as surely as I knew my father's testament, as surely as I knew the taste of that hollow year—exactly what Wren intended to feed into the scales.

And I knew I was going to help her do it.
