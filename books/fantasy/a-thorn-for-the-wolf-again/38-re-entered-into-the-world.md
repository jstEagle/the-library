## 38. Re-entered into the World

The clause was on page nine hundred and forty-one of the master ledger, in the margin, in a hand so small I had to hold the page close enough to smell the iron-gall ink.

*Names withdrawn from circulation may be re-entered by a willing debtor, at equivalent cost, witnessed and sealed.*

Equivalent cost. Four words that had taken me six years to understand, and another two to stop being afraid of.

I sat in the back office of the Guild of Impossible Things—which is a grand way to describe a room with one window, two lamps, and a mummified broker's empty chair that nobody had the heart to move—and I read the clause for maybe the four-hundredth time, and for the first time it read me back.

Because I finally knew what *equivalent* meant.

Odile Voss found me there at dawn, the way she always did, carrying two cups of tea and drinking both, because she said sharing a cup with someone who couldn't remember you was a habit she refused to develop. She was older now. Not old—Odile would outlast the mountains out of pure professional spite—but there were new lines around her mouth, and most of them, I suspected, had my name on them.

"You've been here all night," she said.

"The ledger was here all night."

"That's not an argument, that's an echo." She set down the empty cups and looked at the open page, and I watched her read it, and I watched her understand it, because Odile understood everything eventually; it was her curse and her trade. "No," she said.

"You haven't heard—"

"Wren. I know what the clause costs. I helped write the clause." She lowered herself into Ninepence's chair, which creaked even though nothing about Ninepence had ever creaked; he had rustled. "Thirty years ago a frightened boy came to this office wanting power he shouldn't have, and I sold it to him, and I have spent every year since trying to balance that entry. If you do this—if you pay what this entry costs—you will be paying with the only thing that makes you *you*. Do you understand what you are?"

"A living key," I said. It was what everyone called me for years, before there was no one left who remembered why.

"You're the last speaker of First Speech alive on this continent. You're the girl who unbound a king. You're—" Her voice did something complicated and then stopped. "You'd be ordinary."

"I know," I said. And then, because I had rehearsed this part too many times for it to come out dignified: "She was ordinary too. That was the whole point of her. A woman made entirely of other people's poisons, standing in doorways, keeping accounts no one thanked her for. Ordinary things, done on purpose, over and over." I turned the ledger toward Odile so she could see the name written there—or rather, see the space where the name should have been, the pale rectangle where ink refused to stay, the absence we had all learned to look at without looking at. "You told me once the Guild serves whoever pays. Well. I'm paying."

"It doesn't work like sympathy, child. The Guild doesn't take payment because it's deserved. It takes payment because it's *equal*." Odile leaned forward, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Her name went into the vault of the world at the weight of everything she was. Everything she traded. Her immunity, her childhood home, her memories of us, and at the end, freely given, her whole self. What do you have that weighs the same as *that*?"

I had known the answer for two years. I had simply been waiting for it to stop shaking me.

"My fluency," I said quietly. "Every word of First Speech I've ever spoken or will speak. My connection to the old magic—the binding-tongue, the unbinding-tongue, all of it. Whatever I am as the key, whatever I carry." I swallowed. "It's what I was born holding. Someone told me once to carry it until someone worthy reads me." I looked at the pale rectangle on the page. "Someone did. So. Entry complete."

The lamp guttered. Both lamps guttered. Somewhere in the walls of the old counting house, something vast and patient turned a page.

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them again she was a bookkeeper again, which was her way of surviving anything: reduce it to terms, then honor the terms absolutely.

"Witnessed and sealed," she said hoarsely. "I'll witness. Gods help me, I'll witness."

* * *

There is a ritual for re-entering a name, but it isn't much of a ritual. The Guild has never trusted ceremony; ceremony invites witnesses, and witnesses ask questions, and questions get itemized. What it is, mostly, is paperwork performed with your whole soul.

I washed my hands in water drawn before sunrise. I put on the apprentice's coat Bram once wore—it had been hers, before either of us knew the other existed, and it still smelled faintly of ash and cloves if I was honest with myself about how much of that smell lived in my memory rather than the wool. I sat at the master ledger with the good pen, the one with the nib that had signed away kingdoms, and Odile stood at my shoulder with her hand on the page the way witnesses stand: touching the record, not the person, because the record is what matters, the record is what lasts.

"State the entry," Odile said formally. "Speak it plain, keeper."

"Re-entry of one name withdrawn from circulation." My voice held. I'd have bet against it. "Debtor: Wren, of the undercity, keeper of ledgers. Payment tendered: fluency in the First Speech entire, and all binding therein; the key-work and the key-nature; every word she carries and every word she would have carried." I paused. "Payment accepted by the debtor freely, knowingly, and without appeal."

"And the name entered?" Odile asked softly.

I looked at the pale rectangle for a long moment. Six years of looking at it. Eight years, if I counted the ones where I didn't yet know what I was missing—because that was the cruelty of the thing, that the world had healed over the wound so smoothly that most days even I forgot there had ever been a woman in the doorway of my life, tasting the air, counting our enemies' poisons in her body like coins in a purse.

"Bram Almeida," I said.

And the letters came.

They didn't flow onto the page the way ink does. They arrived the way the First Speech arrives—already there, having always been there, merely now *permitted*. B-R-A-M. The strokes drew themselves in iron-black, and each letter landed with a sound I felt in my teeth, and with every stroke something left me.

I don't know how to describe losing a language you think in. It was like a tower in my mind, one I hadn't known I lived in until the stones began withdrawing, floor by floor, word by word. Words I had spoken to unlock cells. Words I had used to make candles behave and debts vanish and one locked gate on a solstice night swing wide for three fugitives and a freed priestess. Words that had been mine since before memory, since the tower and the burning oath and the voice saying *carry it until someone worthy reads you*.

They went out of me like tide going out of a cave: slowly, hugely, with a suction I could feel behind my ribs.

I kept writing. That was the discipline of it—that was the *bookkeeping* of it, and I thought, absurdly, *this is why she balanced ledgers through the worst nights of her life, because the numbers hold you up when nothing else does.* Debtor: Wren. Creditor: the world. Terms: total.

My tongue changed shape somehow, sitting in my mouth. The old syllables slid off it, ungraspable, the way a dream slides off waking. I tried, deliberately, to hold one word—the unbinding word, the one I'd spoken in the throne room while a beautiful absence screamed—and it dissolved even as I reached for it, and what replaced it was a strange, clean silence.

Ordinary. She'd be ordinary, Odile had warned.

So was I, now.

I wrote the final letter. The A closed itself with a sound like a door shutting gently in an empty house. The ledger shivered down its length—I felt it through the desk, through the floor, through the stone bones of the Guild—and somewhere far above us, in the city, in the morning, something completed.

"Witnessed and sealed," Odile whispered, and pressed her thumbprint below the entry, and then she did something no witness is supposed to do. She took her hand off the record and put it on me, on the back of my neck, and held on.

"I'm sorry," she said into my hair. "And thank you. And I hope to every god listening that it was worth equal."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about paying an enormous price: the world doesn't pause to admire it. The kettle still needs filling. The debtors still queue. I walked out of the counting house an hour later with a headache like a pulled tooth and no language older than any kingdom in my head, and a baker swore at me for standing in his doorway, and I apologized in perfectly ordinary Common, and wanted to laugh and weep at once because it came out so *easy*, my tongue moving in just the one tongue, like a woman walking on one leg after a lifetime of flight.

Odile walked with me. Neither of us said where we were going. We both knew.

The city was hung with solstice-leftover garlands going brown at the edges, and Regent Isolde's new proclamations fluttered on the posts—reforms, reforms, a kingdom learning to be governed by a person instead of worn by a thing—and the morning smelled of bread and river and horse, just smells, no meanings underneath, and I grieved that and marveled at it in the same breath.

We took the long way. Of course we took the long way. We passed the doorway where it had happened eight years ago—the coronation aftermath, the dust, the crowd standing in the square unable to explain why they were crying—and the doorway stood empty now, sunlit, a woman-shaped patch of shadow nothing made anymore.

We crossed the weir bridge. We came up past the chandlers' row.

And there—

Not in the doorway. In front of it. Standing very still in the middle of the morning foot-traffic, a woman in a coat that fit her badly because it had been fitted to a thinner, more careful version of herself, with gray coming into her dark hair and lines around her eyes that eight years had carved without anyone home to feel the chisel.

She was tasting the air.

That was the first thing, the true thing, the thing I would know anywhere: her lips slightly parted, her chin lifted, drawing the morning in slow deliberate pulls—bread, river, horse, tallow, rain coming from the west—as though every scent were a sentence she was reading for its second meaning. As though her body were still a ledger and the whole world was writing entries in it.

Then she stopped. Because under the bread and the river and the horse, the wind carried something else, and her face—her plain, stubborn, once-forgotten face—began to come apart.

She turned.

People often say *time slowed*. It didn't. Time did exactly what time does; it was my heart that ran ahead without me, arriving in her arms some seconds before the rest of me did. She caught me or I caught her, I genuinely could not tell you, and she made a sound I had never heard her make—not when poisoned, not when betrayed, not in the throne room with the Hollow King's hands closing around her soul—a cracked, disbelieving sound, half laugh and half the noise grief makes when it finds out it was wasted.

"Wren," she said. My name. Just my name, in perfectly ordinary Common, and it was the finest sentence ever spoken in any language including ones no scholar knows. "Wren. I remember. I remember *everything*, it all came back at once, the cellars, the ledgers, the solstice, the—" She pulled back and gripped my shoulders and looked at me the way she must have looked at suspect wine, hunting the flaw, finding none, being terrified by the absence of one. "Eight years. You were eight years old, and now you're—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "What did it cost? Don't lie to me, I can taste a lie, tell me what it *cost*."

"Nothing I need," I said. Which was true. Which was also a lie, and she knew it, and I saw her decide—there, in the doorway, in the sunlight—to let me keep it a moment longer, the way she'd once let me keep my fragments instead of prying them loose.

Behind us, Odile stood with her fists at her sides and her jaw set like a woman refusing to cry in public, and Bram saw her, and something moved across her face—recognition, guilt, love, all itemized—and Odile said, gruffly, "You owe the Guild eight years of labor, Almeida. Interest accrues."

"Put it on my account," Bram said.

And then she laughed, actually laughed, and hugged Odile with one arm and kept the other around my shoulders, and steered us both out of the foot traffic and toward the counting house, already talking—about the ledgers, about whether Ninepence's chair had finally been thrown out, about whether the Guild was still selling both sides to anyone with coin, because some things survive erasure and she was evidently determined to be one of them.

But I saw the moment her steps slowed. I felt it through her arm on my shoulders—the slight change in pressure, the way her head turned, the taster's instinct catching a flavor in the air that didn't belong.

Down the street, near the old palace gates, a courier in palace colors was asking directions. And he wasn't asking for a street.

He was asking, politely, at every door, whether anyone had seen a woman who tasted the air.

Bram's grip tightened almost imperceptibly on my shoulder. "Well," she murmured, wry as ever, steering us calmly toward the alley mouth as though we were three friends out for a stroll. "Seems somebody's ledger has me marked as returned. Let's go find out who's been keeping accounts on me—before they come to collect."
