## 39. A Thorn for the Wolf Again

I had been a woman without a name for two hundred and eleven days when I finally got it back, and the first thing I did with it was cry into a stranger's doorway.

Not a stranger, of course. That was the whole point. But my body hadn't caught up to the news yet. My body still operated on the old arithmetic—the one where every person in the street was someone I'd have to survive, every kindness was a transaction I couldn't remember agreeing to, every hand extended toward me was either offering help or reaching for something I owed.

The hand belonged to Wren. Twenty-two years old now, or thereabouts—time moved strangely for her, the way it does for people who were born as clauses—and she stood in that doorway on Cordwainer Lane with my name held out in front of her like an offering plate.

"Bram," she said. "Bram Almeida."

And the name went in through my ears and down through me like warm water into frozen ground, and things began to thaw in an order I did not choose. First the taste—suddenly I could taste the air, the coal smoke, the wet stone, the ghost of last night's rain on the cobbles, everything at once, unfiltered, unblessed by any taster's discipline, and I understood dimly that this was what she had paid: her fluency, her connection to the First Speech, all of it poured into ink to write me back. Then came the memories, arriving not gently but in a flood, all at once, the way debts arrive—

Odile Voss's kitchen. Ninepence burning rather than talk. Cassia's hands steady on the rite. The Hollow King wearing thirty years of borrowed peace like a coat. Wren at eight years old in the snow, speaking a language older than kingdoms, and me deciding—without knowing I was deciding—that whatever this cost, I would pay it.

"You're crying," Wren said. She said it the way she reported everything: precisely, without judgment, waiting to see if the fact required action.

"I'm remembering," I said. "It's very similar."

She laughed, and then she cried too, and we stood in the doorway of a shuttered candlemaker's shop on Cordwainer Lane holding each other up while the city walked past us and did not look twice, because why would it? Two women crying in a doorway. The city had seen worse. The city had seen me poison-proof its king for fifteen years and never once look up from the cup.

That part, at least, was over. The crown sat empty on a cushion somewhere in the palace, and Isolde wore a regent's circlet instead, and the kingdom was learning the strange new flavor of being governed by a person who answered questions after they were asked.

"Come inside," Wren said eventually. "There's tea. Odile brought honey, which means she's feeling guilty about something."

"Is she?"

"She's always feeling guilty about something. It's how she shows love."

* * *

The Guild counting house looked smaller than I remembered, which is a lie, because buildings don't shrink; people do their remembering at the scale of grief. It was the same narrow building wedged between a chandler and a pawnbroker, the same door that stuck unless you lifted it as you pushed, the same smell of vellum and vinegar and, underneath it all, faintly, of bottled years.

Odile was in the back office, and she had aged. Not kindly—Odile didn't do anything kindly—but honestly. Her hair had gone fully white in the months since I'd been unwritten, and she'd stopped dyeing it, and the effect was of a woman who had decided to let time collect openly rather than pretend she wasn't paying.

She looked up from the ledger she was balancing, saw me, and put down her pen with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing you might otherwise throw.

"Well," she said. "The dead walk."

"The forgotten walk. There's a difference, and you'd know it if you ever read your own fine print."

Something broke open in her face then—all at once, like ice going off a roof in spring—and she crossed the room and grabbed me by both shoulders and shook me once, hard, as if to verify I was real and not another Guild product, some bottled reunion sold to grieving women at market rates.

"You absolute idiot," she said, thickly. "You signed your own name away as collateral and then gave it *freely*, do you know what the odds—do you know what clause—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Two hundred and eleven days. You counted them."

"Wren counted them. She wrote them in the margin."

"Of course she did." Odile released my shoulders and stepped back, and her face reassembled itself into the broker's mask, though it fit worse than it used to. "Well. You'll want work. A woman who tastes nothing and remembers everything shouldn't be idle—it makes them philosophical."

"That's not a job offer."

"It's absolutely a job offer. Co-keeper of the ledgers. You and the girl. Equal shares, equal liabilities." She paused, and the mask slipped just enough. "I'm retiring. Badly. The way I do everything."

* * *

We talked until the candles burned down, the three of us, around the scarred table where I had first learned what the Guild really traded. And here is what I want recorded, because recording things is what I do now:

The Guild of Impossible Things survived the fall of the Hollow King the way a river survives the drowning of a child in it—indifferently, and flowing on. The court bought fewer memories, maybe, with Isolde asking pointed questions about provenance. But the undercity still needed borrowed courage, and debtors still lived on traded time, and somewhere in the vaults there were still jars of last words and ledgers written in hair.

Which meant the Guild still needed watching. Especially now. Because I knew—I had *been*—what happened when an institution served whoever paid and never asked why. I had watched the enemy shop our shelves. I had felt the wards take my childhood home as payment without blinking. An institution like that doesn't reform. It can only be guarded, the way you guard anything dangerous that you also love.

"So that's the offer," Odile said, near the end. "Co-keepers. You and Wren. You balance the books; she reads the fine print; between you, maybe, the Guild stops eating people who don't read theirs." She turned her pen over in her fingers. "It won't make you rich. It will make you complicit, but the managed kind. There's a difference. Ask anyone I've ruined."

"Why us?" I asked. "You've spent thirty years managing your guilt alone. Why hand it off?"

"Because guilt hoarded spoils." She said it flatly, like a price quoted. "And because you, Bram Almeida, are the only person alive who has read this Guild's ledger from the inside of a forfeited entry. You know what we take because you paid it. And the girl—" she glanced at Wren, who was watching her with that terrible patient attention she'd had even at eight— "the girl knows what it costs to buy a person back. Between the two of you, maybe the next Bram Almeida won't have to sign herself away. Maybe we'll find another way. Or maybe you'll just fail more slowly. Either way, it's better bookkeeping than mine."

* * *

The court's invitation arrived three days later, delivered by a captain of the guard who kept glancing at his boots as though the message might be written on them.

Her Highness the Princess Regent requests the pleasure of Lady Almeida's company at the solstice observance, in recognition of services rendered to the crown and realm.

Lady Almeida. I turned the word over. Somewhere in the wreckage of the old reign, Isolde had found paperwork enough to make me a lady—or perhaps she'd simply written it herself; she'd always struck me as a woman who understood that titles are just entries in a ledger, and she now controlled the ledger.

Wren read over my shoulder. "You're going, obviously."

"I'm considering it."

"You're standing in the middle of the room looking at it like it's poisoned."

"Everything at court is poisoned. That was my whole profession."

"Bram." She took the letter from my hands and set it down, and fixed me with the look she'd been perfecting since she was eight—a look that had opened locks and unmade bindings and, once, written a woman back into the world. "You don't have to go. But tell me the true reason you don't want to, because 'everything at court is poisoned' is a deflection, and you taught me better than that."

I sat down. That's the honest version. I sat down on the counting-house stool, among the smells of vellum and vinegar, and told her the truth.

Because the truth was this: I missed it. God help me, I missed the tasting. Fifteen years of my life I had stood behind kings and princesses and paranoid duchesses with a silver spoon in my hand, and my body had been a fortress, a ledger of swallowed deaths, and I had been proud of it in the way you can only be proud of a thing that costs you everything. And now the immunity was gone—sold to the Guild stores years ago, spent buying a child safe passage—and the court would seat me at high tables and pour wine, and I would lift the spoon knowing that this time, one mistake was death. Ordinary woman. Ordinary tongue. One arsenic lacing and done.

"They'll toast me," I said. "They'll raise glasses to the woman who saved the kingdom, and they'll hand her wine, and I'll have to taste it like anyone else, with nothing between me and it but craft." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know what I am without the immunity? Just good. Just careful. Just a woman who happens to be excellent at her job, and dies the first time someone's better funded."

"Yes," Wren said. "I know exactly what you are." And then, with the precision she used for First Speech syllables and everything else that mattered: "You're the person who taught me that worth isn't what survives you. It's who stands beside you. So stand beside yourself for once. Go to the solstice. Taste the wine with your ordinary tongue and your extraordinary care. And then come home."

Home. She said it like it was already settled. Like the counting house above the Guild office, with its sticking door and its vinegar smell, was simply where I lived now, no further negotiation required.

"When did you get wise?" I asked.

"The year I lost my fluency. Everything costs something, so I decided to spend on wisdom." She smiled, small and crooked. "Also, I've been reading Odile's correspondence. She's terrible at hiding things."

* * *

I went to the solstice. Of course I went. You don't refuse a regent twice, and besides—some small stubborn part of me wanted to see it. Wanted to stand in the great hall where I had once watched a shadow move wrong across the floor, and see the light fall honestly now, on honest faces.

The hall had changed. Banners of the old reign were gone, replaced with Isolde's plain grey-and-silver. The throne stood empty—they'd moved it against the wall, ceremonial, a piece of furniture with a history no one wanted to sit in—and Isolde received from a simple chair, which I thought was the most royal thing I'd seen a monarch do in thirty years.

She saw me across the crowd and crossed the floor herself, dismissing protocol with a flick of her hand, and took both my hands in hers.

"Lady Almeida," she said. Then, quieter: "Bram. I owe you a debt the crown can't enter in any book."

"Then don't," I said. "Some things are survived, some are paid for, and the difference is who stood beside you. You stood beside me. We're square."

"I'm offering you a position. Mistress of Tasters. Your own offices. Staff. A salary that would embarrass a duke."

"And a spoon."

"A spoon," she agreed, and her mouth twitched. "Silver. Engraved."

I looked past her shoulder, at the hall. At the nobles glittering and murmuring, at the servants moving with trays, at the wine dark in its cups. At the empty throne against the wall, wearing its inert crown like a scab. And I felt the old pull—the lonely competence of it, the pride, the taste of survival—and I noticed, with the clarity of a woman who has recently been erased from existence and re-inked, that what I actually wanted was smaller and stranger.

I wanted the counting house. I wanted the sticking door. I wanted to spend my mornings teaching Wren the difference between a fair price and a predatory one, and my evenings arguing with Odile about interest rates, and my nights balancing books in which no entry was ever again a person's name.

"Your Highness," I said. "Thank you. No."

Isolde studied me for a long moment. She has her father's eyes—not the Hollow King's borrowed ones, the real ones, the ones from before—and I watched her decide whether to be insulted, and watched her choose not to be.

"He refused the throne too, at the end," she said softly. "The real king. He bound himself rather than hold absolute power. I used to think it was weakness." She squeezed my hands once, and let go. "Now I think it might be the only kind of strength that lasts. Very well. But I'm appointing you Court Taster Emerita anyway. It comes with no duties, no salary, and a lifetime supply of the palace's best honey. Consider it the crown's way of saying it knows better than to argue with you."

"And if the new court needs a taster? Truly needs one? Without armor?"

"Then I'll send a letter," she said, "and hope like hell you say yes." She paused. "One condition. Come to dinner sometimes. Not as staff. As family. Wren too. Odile, if she can bear to leave the ledgers for an evening."

"Odile will come if there's honey in it."

"There will be honey in it."

"Then yes."

* * *

We walked home through the snow, Wren and I, and the city was beautiful in the way cities only are when you've nearly been deleted from them—the lamplight gold on the drifts, the bells ringing solstice changes, the smell of roasting chestnuts from a brazier on the corner.

"Regrets?" Wren asked.

"One," I admitted. "I'll never taste the way I used to. The immunity's gone, sold and spent, and the stores won't sell it back even at ruinous rates—Odile checked. Every cup for the rest of my life is just... craft and luck."

"Craft and luck kept you alive for fifteen years before the immunity. The immunity was the armor. The craft was always you." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Besides. You're not the thorn anymore, Bram. You're the wolf."

I stopped walking. "What?"

"The title. Odile says you never asked what the manuscript was called. The one the voice in the tower told me to carry until someone worthy read me." She smiled in the snow-light, tired and fond and twenty-two years old and older than kingdoms. "It was called *A Thorn for the Wolf*. The binding oath, written out entire. The thorn was the failsafe—the anchor born into the oath-line, pricking the wolf awake if it ever slept too deep. Me. I was the thorn. And the wolf—" she shrugged— "well. The wolf wore the crown."

"And now?"

"Now the wolf is dust, and the thorn keeps the ledgers." She took my arm, and we walked on. "But Odile says titles outlive their oaths. She wants to rename the office. *A Thorn for the Wolf Again*—because apparently the Guild can't resist a callback, and because the new meaning fits: a sharp thing beside a strong thing, keeping each other honest. That's us, if you'll have it."

Snow settled on our shoulders. Somewhere behind us, the palace bells rang the hour, and ahead the counting house window glowed with Odile's lamp, and the door that sticks was waiting to be lifted-and-pushed.

"If I'll have it," I repeated. "Wren. I crawled out of my own erasure for you. I sold my childhood home out of the vault of my own memory to reach a contract. I gave my name freely to a thing made of hunger. If you think there's any version of the rest of my life in which I decline to keep books beside you—" I stopped, because my throat had closed, because two hundred and eleven days of not-existing had left me permanently susceptible to being offered a place in the world. "Yes. Obviously yes. Now come inside before I cry again. It's undignified. I have a reputation as a hard-bitten professional."

"You're crying already."

"I'm remembering. It's very similar."

* * *

Upstairs, Odile had laid out the master ledger on the table, open to a fresh page, with three pens and a pot of ink and—she is not subtle—an entire comb of honeycomb on a chipped plate.

"Before you sign anything," Odile said, "read the terms. All of them. I wrote them myself, and I still recommend distrust."

I read. Co-keepership, joint and several. Equal authority over entries, withdrawals, and prices. A standing veto over any trade involving names, memories of children, or years taken from anyone under forty. A clause requiring both co-keepers to witness any extraction. And at the bottom, in Odile's cramped hand, a line I made her read aloud twice:

*No entry in this ledger shall ever again be a living person.*

"No exceptions?" I asked.

"No exceptions. If the Guild can't profit within those bounds, the Guild deserves to starve." She slid the pen toward me. "Sign."

I signed. Wren signed beneath me, her letters precise as First Speech script. Odile signed last, and then closed her own pen with the finality of a woman locking a door behind her.

And then, because the moment demanded it, because I am who I am, I reached for the master ledger and made the first entry of the new era myself. Plain language. No code, no cipher, no fine print. Just the record, in my own hand, for whoever balances these books after us:

*Solstice, first year of the Regency. The Guild of Impossible Things re-founded under joint keepership. Terms as stated above. Witnessed: Bram Almeida, returned from the blank page; Wren, late of the oath-line; Odile Voss, retired badly.*

*I note for the record that I remember everything now—every sale, every sacrifice, every swallowed death and given name—and that remembering turns out to be survivable. Expensive, but survivable.*

*Some things are survived. Some are paid for. The difference is who stood beside you.*

*Entry closed.*

Odile blew out the candles. Wren banked the fire. And outside, the snow kept falling on a city that no longer remembered what it owed us—which was, I reflected, pulling the quilt up against the cold, exactly how a saved kingdom ought to sleep.

Tomorrow there would be ledgers. Tomorrow there would be debtors at the door and Odile's guilt and the slow, unglamorous work of guarding a dangerous thing we loved. Tomorrow, possibly, a letter from a regent, or a problem with teeth.

But tonight, home. Redefined, re-entered, and—for the first time in a long life built on surviving—mine.
