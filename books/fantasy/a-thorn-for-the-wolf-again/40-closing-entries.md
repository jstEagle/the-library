## 40. Closing Entries

The counting house smells of ink and candle smoke and, faintly, of the plum wine Odile swears she only keeps for medicinal purposes. It is the last night of the year in the Guild's new ledger—new to me, at least, though the book itself is older than the kingdom it sits beneath—and I am balancing the final page.

This is what I know how to do. When everything else was taken from me—my immunity, my childhood home, my name—I kept my arithmetic. A body is a ledger of poisons survived; a life is a ledger of debts paid. You can strip a woman down to nothing and if she still knows how to enter a figure in a column and make the columns agree, she is not nothing. She is an accountant of her own ruin, which is at least a respectable profession.

Wren is asleep upstairs. She sleeps in the room that used to be mine, back when I had rooms above this place instead of simply living here, which is not quite the same thing but close enough for government work. Odile is asleep in hers, or pretending to be, which for Odile is the same posture. Cassia has gone back to her ruined chapel to sit out the night among her remembered gods, because some habits are load-bearing.

And I am here, with the lamp turned low, doing what tasters do best: taking the measure of things by putting them on my tongue.

* * *

Let me set down the whole affair in plain language, as plain as I can make it, because the Guild's habit is to write everything in terms so baroque that no one can be held to them, and I have decided, in my old age—which is to say, in my two years since being re-entered into the world—that someone ought to write one honest book.

Here is the entry, then, from the top:

A king died thirty years ago. What wore his crown afterward was an oath-eater called the Hollow King, bound into him by his own hand, purchased through this Guild, priced against a war he could not otherwise have stopped. He believed no one should hold absolute power, including himself, and he was right, and the price of being right was becoming a shape that something else filled.

A child was born into the oath-line as a failsafe. She carried the unbinding word in her mouth before she had teeth to say it with. She arrived at my door in the snow, mute in every language anyone knew, and my ledger opened itself to record her before I touched the pen, which should have been my first warning and was instead my first wonder. I have never been good at telling those apart.

I traded thirty years of poison-immunity to buy her safe passage. I nearly died proving I could still taste without armor. I withdrew my own name from circulation as collateral on a contract I fully intended to break. A priestess who remembered too much stood witness knowing witnesses die. A princess opened her father's gates with evidence gathered like a surgeon gathers scars. And on solstice night, in a throne room where a beautiful absence argued its case better than any devil ever has—it ended a war, it kept thirty years of peace, and both of those things are true, and I have made peace with the truth of them—Wren spoke, Cassia witnessed, the king consented, and when the Hollow King demanded a soul of equal weight as counterweight, I gave it my name.

Freely given. That matters. The Guild's oldest law: nothing taken binds like something given.

Then the thing unraveled, the true king crumbled mid-blessing into dust and mercy, the crown fell inert, and everyone I had saved looked straight through me, because a name freely surrendered is a name withdrawn from every ledger including theirs.

I lived unrecorded for two years. I will write about that another time, or I won't. Some entries resist balancing.

What matters tonight is this: a grown woman named Wren Voss-Almeida—who took both our names onto the Guild's rolls the day she became keeper, over my objections and Odile's delighted applause—found the clause I once signed. Names withdrawn may be re-entered by a willing debtor. She paid the price. Her fluency in First Speech, her connection to the old magic, gone, spent like coin. She wrote me back into the world, and I woke in a doorway tasting the air, remembering everything at once, which is either a blessing or a very sophisticated form of torture depending on the day.

Today it is a blessing. Today the columns agree.

* * *

There are figures I must enter honestly, because a ledger that flatters is worse than no ledger at all.

Ninepence burned rather than talk. I did not know Ninepence well—he was mostly a mummy with opinions—but opinions like his deserve recording. He identified First Speech from a child's sleep-talk and gave us the word *enemy*, named and shaped, before the crown agents came. They found him in his alcove already alight, seated, dignified, refusing with his entire dessicated body. The Guild keeps his jar of last words on the shelf now. It is empty. We think he said everything he meant to while burning.

Isolde rules as regent and is very good at it, which would have surprised her father less than it surprises the court. She has abolished the office of royal food-taster.

I want that noted plainly, because it was the first thing I lost after my name and the one I grieved least and longest. There is no longer a position whose entire purpose is a human body standing between a monarch and death. Isolde said, when she signed the abolition, "If I cannot trust my own kitchen, I do not deserve a kingdom." I have put that sentence in the ledger twice, once under *politics* and once under *things people say that turn out to be true*.

Odile confessed, two years ago now, that she sold the original binding to the young king. Thirty years of managing guilt through commerce. She does not manage it anymore; she manages the Guild instead, badly on purpose in places, generously in others, and last month she forgave every debt held by debtors under sixteen, which is not sound business and is exactly the kind of thing guilt looks like when it finally learns a useful trade.

Cassia still officiates nothing and remembers everything. She comes Thursdays. She brings bread. She says grace over it in a language only three of us have ever heard, and one of us has forgotten it now, and she says it anyway, for the two of us and for the ghost of the girl Wren used to be.

And Wren—

Wren cannot speak First Speech anymore. Some mornings she reaches for a word the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and finds floor, and stands there a moment with her hand out. I know that gesture. I made it for months over my own journals, reading a stranger's handwriting, learning why I became a taster from evidence rather than memory.

But she taught me who I was once, page by page, when I couldn't remember. So now, on the dark-stair mornings, I do for her what she did for me. I tell her about the tower she remembers in fragments, the burning oath, the voice that said *carry it until someone worthy reads you*. I tell her she was worthy before she could read herself at all. She pretends this helps. It does help. We both pretend otherwise, because we are family, and family is largely a system of mutual pretenses that somehow balances.

* * *

Which brings me to the line. The one this whole book has been walking toward, the way every long road walks toward a door.

Some things are survived, some are paid for, and the difference is who stood beside you.

I have tested this line against the evidence, because I don't put anything in a ledger I haven't tasted first.

The arsenic in the king's breakfast, all those years ago: survived. Nobody stood beside me in that dining hall except my own tongue, and my tongue was enough, because surviving is what bodies do alone.

My immunity: paid for. Thirty years swallowed in trade, and I stood alone at that counter too, by choice, because the passage I bought was for a child who hadn't asked to be bought for. Paid-for things leave a cleaner taste than survived things, oddly. Survived things sit in you. Paid things pass through.

My name: paid, then returned, and the return is the whole proof of the line. Because a name given up alone is just erasure with paperwork. Mine came back because a girl stood beside me across two years of absence and refused to accept the loss. Mourning with a stubborn spine, I called it once. It is also called love, if you want the technical term.

The Hollow King: neither survived nor paid for, exactly. Ended. Witnessed. Which may be a third column entirely, one I am only now learning to keep—the column for things that are neither endured nor purchased but *answered*, met, refused their argument in a throne room by a taster with no armor and a priestess with no fear and a princess with receipts. I don't have a header for that column yet. I'm working on it. Ledgers, like lives, get amended.

The real king: paid. Thirty years ago, by himself, for strangers who never learned his name was not the name they cheered. His daughter knows now. She lights a candle in the palace chapel that isn't dedicated to anyone, and the priests have stopped asking. That candle is an entry in a ledger only she keeps, and it balances, because grief that is *known about* balances differently than grief that is secret. Ask me how I know. Don't, actually. The answer takes two years and a doorway.

Wren's fluency: paid. For me. Which means I am carrying a debt I can never directly repay, only forward, which is how most of the important debts in this world work. Interest accrues in kindness. I intend to be delinquent forever.

* * *

The candle is guttering. In this room that used to mean a debt vanishing, back when a child's voice could rearrange the letters of reality. Now it just means I should wrap up, which is fitting, since wrapping up is the assignment.

Let me do the final reconciliation properly.

Assets: one Guild counting house, solvent; one mentor, reformed-ish; one priestess, faithful; one regent, just; one king, dust and peace; one crown, inert, displayed in the archive behind glass with a placard reading ONLY OBJECT IN THE KINGDOM THAT HAS NEVER BEEN TRADED, WHICH WE INTEND TO KEEP TRUE.

Liabilities: one debt to Wren Voss-Almeida, perpetual, nontransferable, gladly owed. One debt to a dead king, payable in remembrance. One small ongoing obligation to a plum wine merchant in the undercity whom Odile insists on cheating slightly, for tradition's sake.

Equity: whatever remains when you subtract what you've lost from what you've loved. In my case the figure is positive, which surprised me, and continues to surprise me, and I have decided that being surprised by your own solvency is not a bad way to grow old.

I write the closing line at the bottom of the final page, the one I'll repeat until my hand forgets how, in plain language, no Guild cipher, no hair-bound clauses, no fine print:

*Some things are survived, some are paid for, and the difference is who stood beside you.*

Then I sign it. My own name. Bram Almeida. Written by my own hand, in a book that belongs partly to me, witnessed by nobody, needing nobody's witness, because the whole point of the last forty chapters of my life is that I am entered in the world again and the entry is permanent.

I blow out the candle. The columns agree. The book closes with the soft sound a finished thing makes.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaks—Wren, awake, or Odile, or just the house settling around its people—and then footsteps on the stairs, and a knock at the counting-house door, three raps, the Guild's old signal for *we have a customer, and you are going to want to see this one.*

Because there is always another impossible thing. That is what a Guild is. That is what a life is.

I pick up the lamp.
