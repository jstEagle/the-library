## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The rats in the guildhouse walls were arguing about bread again.

*Crust first,* said the one behind the pantry, a female with three litters to her name and opinions to match. *Soft part last. That's how you make it last.*

*That's how you make it stale,* her mate answered. *You eat the soft part while it's still soft.*

Ivo Nystrom kept his eyes on the ledger and did not laugh. Laughing alone at nothing was the kind of thing that got a man noticed, and being noticed was the one debt Ivo had spent thirty-one years refusing to carry.

He dipped his pen. Column by column, the winter audit balanced itself under his hand: tallow, salt, iron nails, lamp-oil, and — in the locked annex no auditor ever saw — forty-one entries marked only as *specie, uncounted*. The Guild of Provisioners of Vessany traded in everything that kept a country alive, and Ivo had learned, over eleven years of careful silence, exactly how much of it was real.

Most of it was not.

*Cold coming,* observed a raven on the sill outside, in the flat tone ravens used for weather and funerals alike. *Cold like the old cold. Cold before your grandmothers.*

Ivo wrote *lamp-oil, 12 casks* and told himself the bird was being dramatic. Ravens were always being dramatic. It was practically their trade.

The counting room smelled of ink and tallow and the particular dust of paper that had been handled too long. Beyond its shutters, Corvane — capital city, pride of Vessany, home to forty thousand souls who would burn a man for hearing what he heard — went about its evening: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the bell of the Wardens' tower ringing the hour with its usual dull authority. Ordinary sounds. The sound of a world that intended to keep existing.

Ivo balanced the last line, blotted it, and allowed himself one breath of satisfaction. Tomorrow the auditors from the southern chapter would arrive, and Odile Vess would stand before them with these ledgers, and the guild's impossible arithmetic would pass inspection for another year. Forty-one uncounted entries. Eleven years, and he had never once asked what they were.

He had never once needed to. That was the arrangement. He counted; he didn't look.

*Ivo.* The rat behind the pantry again, but different now. All the rats, actually — he realized the argument had stopped, and every small voice in the walls had gone quiet in the way animals go quiet when something crosses their ground. *Something's wrong with the edges.*

"The edges," Ivo murmured to his ledger, pen moving. "Of course."

*The edges of everything,* the rat said, and there was no cleverness in it now, only the animal fact of fear. *The edges are forgetting us.*

He should have gone home. He knew that even then, with the pen still moving and the audit finished and the sensible thing sitting right there in front of him like an open door. But the raven on the sill had begun making a sound he'd never heard from a raven — a low, continuous note, like a struck bowl — and when Ivo looked up through the shutter slats at the sky over Corvane, the stars were going out.

Not clouding. Not dimming. Going out, one by one, west to east, as if someone were walking across heaven pinching wicks. And where each star had been, there was nothing. Not darkness — darkness is something, darkness has weight and company. This was absence, clean-edged, and Ivo's eyes watered trying to look at it, the way they watered looking into the vault annex too long.

The bell in the Wardens' tower rang the hour again. It had rung the hour four minutes ago.

"Ah," said Ivo Nystrom, to no one, in a voice he kept very steady. "That's not good."

* * *

Guildmaster Odile Vess received the news standing at the window of her office on the top floor, which meant she had already seen it, which meant she had already known what to do with it, because Odile had been born knowing what to do with things. She was sixty, silver-haired, built like a door, and she had hired Ivo eleven years ago off a street where he'd been talking to pigeons without noticing he was doing it aloud — and then, instead of reporting him, she had said, *You'll work nights. You'll count. You'll never tell me what the animals say unless I ask.* She had never asked.

Until tonight.

"Tell me what they're saying," she said, without turning around.

Ivo's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop. "Guildmaster—"

"I'm not asking as a confession, Ivo. I'm asking as a woman who pays your wage. The wards on the border just failed. All of them, all at once, from the Salt Marches to the Thornline. The Warden-Captain sent a runner twenty minutes ago and the runner hasn't come back, and the sky is eating itself, and my senior counter is standing in my office making the face of a man doing sums he doesn't like." She turned. Her eyes were pale grey and entirely without mercy or comfort. "What are the animals saying?"

He could lie. He had lied beautifully for eleven years. But the rats had said *the edges are forgetting us*, and the raven was still ringing its one struck note, and somewhere below them the vault annex — the forty-one uncounted entries — had begun, very faintly, to hum.

"They're saying the country is unwriting itself," Ivo said. "From the borders inward. They're saying the edges have forgotten them." He swallowed. "They're saying it's the old cold. Before our grandmothers."

Odile looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once, briskly, filing it away the way she filed everything. "Then we're past pretending this is a supply problem."

"What is it?"

"It's a payment problem." She crossed to the wall behind her desk and set her palm against a panel Ivo had passed ten thousand times and never once seen open. Stone ground against stone. Stairs went down, breathing out air that smelled of deep earth and lamp oil and — underneath, unmistakably — of animal. Warm fur. Musk. Something alive, down in the dark, breathing slow.

"The guild doesn't hoard gold, Ivo. Gold is a fiction we invented so people wouldn't ask what we really keep." She lifted a lantern from the hook by the stairs. "Forty-one entries. Forty-one payments Vessany made, over four hundred years, to keep the border wards fed. We're the custodians of the last one."

"Of a—" Ivo stopped. Started again. "It's alive."

"It's very old, and it's very tired, and until tonight the wards drew on it slowly enough that it could rest between winters." Odile started down the stairs. After a moment, hating himself, Ivo followed. "Tonight the wards didn't fail, Ivo. Tonight the wards were *drained*. Someone took everything at once. And when the land runs out of magic, it starts spending other things. Memory. Names. Stars."

Below them, in the dark, the humming resolved into something almost like speech — not words, not beast-tongue, something older than either, and Ivo's gift, unbidden, reached toward it the way a hand reaches toward a flame.

And the thing in the vault reached back.

He came to himself at the bottom of the stairs with Odile gripping his collar and the lantern swinging wild shadows across a vault he couldn't properly see — only a shape, vast and curled, fur the colour of dusk, one eye opening like a door opening.

"It spoke to you," Odile said. Not a question. Her face, for the first time in eleven years, was afraid. "Ivo. What did it say?"

Behind him, up the stairs, the counting room bell began to ring — not the hour, not any hour, just ringing — and beneath it he could hear the rats in the walls of the guildhouse saying, all together, all at once, in the smallest voices in the world:

*It's starting. It's starting. It's starting.*

Ivo opened his mouth to answer his guildmaster, and found that for the first time in his life, he had absolutely nothing to say.
