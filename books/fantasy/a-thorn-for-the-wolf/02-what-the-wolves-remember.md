## 2. What the Wolves Remember

The road out of Corvess was full of people who had forgotten where they were going.

Ivo passed them in the dark—carts standing empty in the middle of the causeway, horses grazing calmly with their bridles still knotted, a woman walking in tight circles and asking everyone, politely, if they had seen her street. Not her house. Her street. She couldn't remember the name of the road she'd lived on for forty years, and every time she said *street* the word came out thinner.

He kept his eyes down. He kept his ledger satchel tight against his ribs. He did not listen to what the abandoned horses were thinking, because he had learned at fourteen that some thoughts you can't unhear, and tonight he needed his whole mind to walk.

Sefa walked beside him with her ward-keeper's staff cut down to a walking stick, which she'd done with an axe in the guild courtyard while Odile watched from a window. It had been, Ivo suspected, a message. Ward-keepers were property of the realm; disgraced ones walking out of the capital in the night were nobody's problem.

"The eastern gate will be unwritten by now," Sefa said. "We go north through the fens."

"You know the fens?"

"I know the door at the end of them." She said it flatly, like reporting weather. "The last true door out of Vessany. The wolves hold it."

"Wolves," Ivo said.

"The pack. They've held it for three hundred years. Longer than the guild, longer than the crown." She glanced at him sideways. "You'll want to keep your ledger hidden up there. Wolves don't trust men who carry numbers."

*Wolves don't trust men who carry anything,* said a voice from the ditch, low and amused, and Ivo's whole body went rigid before his mind caught up: a fox, half-hidden in reeds, thinking lazily at the world. *But that one smells like more than numbers.*

He didn't answer. He had spent twenty-nine years not answering. But his heart hammered, and he felt the fox notice the hammering, feel it the way you'd feel thunder through floorboards, and he walked faster.

They reached the fen road at dawn—or what should have been dawn. Above the treeline there was only a smooth grey ceiling where the sun ought to have broken the horizon, light without a source, shadows pointing nowhere. The country had stopped pretending to turn.

* * *

The wolves took them in the third hour of the fens, and they did it so quietly Ivo never heard it coming—which shamed him later, because he could hear the thoughts of things a mile off when he bothered to listen. He hadn't bothered. He'd been listening to Sefa describe the ward-lattice instead, grateful for anything that wasn't a voice.

Then six grey shapes simply existed around them, close enough to touch, silent as snowfall.

The largest stood on a hummock ahead, and she was wrong in the way the stars' absence was wrong: too large, too deliberate, her coat marked with pale streaks like marrow showing through. She looked at Sefa first, then at Ivo, and her regard had weight, the weight of something deciding.

"Grey-Marrow," Sefa said, and bowed—not deep, but real. "Keeper Sefa of the Vessany lattice. Unmade this morning, for what it matters."

*It matters,* Grey-Marrow thought, and Ivo heard it plainly, the thought arriving in his skull like a stone dropped in still water. *It matters enormously, small broken keeper. Everything matters now. That is the problem.*

"She asks our business," Ivo said quickly, because Sefa couldn't hear what he heard, and silence was starting to look like rudeness.

Sefa stared at him. "How do you—"

"Trade secret," Ivo said. "Guildmaster's shorthand. Body language. Very advanced."

One of the younger wolves circled behind them, and Ivo felt her curiosity brush against his mind—bright, quick, young. She sniffed at his satchel, then at his hands, then sat back on her haunches and thought, distinctly: *He's strange. His smell is frightened but his insides are talking.*

And because he was exhausted, and terrified, and because the polite thing to do when someone speaks to you is to answer—Ivo answered. Out loud. In wolf.

"Nothing inside me is talking about you," he said—a rough, rising syllable-cluster, accurate enough to make the young wolf's ears shoot forward—and then heard himself, heard the fen go utterly still, heard Sefa stop breathing beside him.

The gift. Out of his mouth, in open air, where anyone with ears and one year of border service would recognize beast-speech for what it was.

Grey-Marrow's eyes fixed on him. *Say it again.*

"Ivo," Sefa said loudly, stepping between him and the alpha, "has a stutter. A terrible stutter. Sounds like animals sometimes—tragic condition, runs in his family, we're very sensitive about it."

*A stutter,* Grey-Marrow thought, and her amusement rolled over him like cold water. *Three hundred years I have guarded this door, and no man has ever stuttered at me in my own tongue.*

She rose from the hummock and crossed to them, close enough that Ivo could see the pale streaks were not markings but scars, old and deliberate, laid in patterns he almost recognized from the ward-lattice diagrams Sefa had sketched in the dirt.

"Kill them or question them," she said aloud, in the trade-tongue, perfectly, and Ivo understood that the pretense was a courtesy offered to Sefa alone. "My pack is divided. My young one says your fox-voiced friend is honest. My old ones say honesty that hides for thirty years is just a longer lie." Her muzzle came near Ivo's face. "Which are you?"

"I'm a coward who's tired of it," Ivo said. In trade-tongue. Plainly. His voice shook and he let it.

Grey-Marrow held his gaze a long moment. Then she turned to the pack, and whatever passed among them was silent and fast, a conversation of ear and shoulder and scent, and Ivo caught fragments of it—*keep them, use them, the door is failing, the keeper knows the lattice, the strange one hears*—and one voice, older, colder, from a scarred male at the rear: *Odile's hunters are two days behind them. Bring the debt closer.*

That, Ivo decided, he would think very carefully about sharing.

"Walk with us," Grey-Marrow said. "Provisionally."

* * *

**Sefa**

The trouble with lying well, Sefa had learned, was that people believed you, and then you had to live in the world your lie built.

She'd lied for the guild for eleven years—fudged lattice readings, buried reports of ward-drain, told herself each small falsehood bought time for someone to fix the great machine quietly. Nobody fixed it. The machine ate. Now the country was being eaten back, and the only person who knew her worst secret was a guild clerk who spoke wolf and pretended it was a stutter.

She watched him now across the fen campfire—the wolves permitted fire, grudgingly, as a concession to human frailty—as he talked with the young wolf, Halven, in that impossible rough tongue. He laughed at something the wolf thought. The wolf laughed back, in her way.

He'd hidden it his whole life, she realized. The way she'd hidden her falsified ledgers. Two liars, sitting in a drowning country, each certain their lie was the unforgivable one.

"They'll want something from you," she said quietly, when Halven trotted off. "The pack. Nothing is provisional forever."

"I know." Ivo fed a stick to the fire. "The scarred one—Old-Bone, I think they call him—he wants to give us to Odile's hunters. Trade us for something."

"For what?"

"That's the part he's keeping quiet." Ivo looked up, and his face in the firelight was older than twenty-nine. "Sefa. When the vault opened, Odile said it held something alive. You were a keeper. What lives in the guild vault?"

Sefa looked into the fire for a long time.

"A wolf," she said finally. "The first wolf. The one the door was built around."

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Grey-Marrow went very still—and Ivo, who could not help hearing what beasts thought even when he wished to be deaf, heard the alpha think one word, in a voice three hundred years deep:

*Mother.*
