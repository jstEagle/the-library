## 3. A Country Unwriting Itself

They came to Hallowmere on the second morning, and it was already forgetting itself.

Ivo knew the village first by its silence. Not the silence of empty places — he'd walked through plague-towns and abandoned mines, and those still had a hum of what they'd been, rats in the rafters muttering old names, crows keeping inventories. Hallowmere had nothing. The wells stood open like unstrung mouths. Laundry hung frozen on lines no one would claim. And in the doorways of the houses, chalk marks — ward-marks, Sefa said, though half-faded — that no one alive remembered drawing.

"The land's eating them," Sefa murmured, pulling her hood lower. She hadn't slept. Her hands, when she thought Ivo wasn't looking, kept tracing ward-sigils on her knee, over and over, a scribe with no page. "Not killing. *Eating.* A village dies, you get corpses and ghosts and crows. This is different. Look at the well-rope. Someone cut it yesterday. I can almost remember them doing it."

Ivo could hear it worse than she could. The beasts could too. Grey-Marrow's pack moved through Hallowmere in a tight wedge, hackles up, and the alpha herself kept stopping to press her muzzle against doorposts as if reading letters written in rot.

*Nothing here,* she thought — broadcast, unguarded, the way wolves thought around their own, and Ivo was learning he counted now, provisionally, as *their own.* *Nothing here remembers being here. Even the lice have gone quiet.*

A cat followed them out of the village. That was the worst part. An orange tom, ribs like a washboard, trotting at Ivo's heel and thinking one thought on a loop, worn smooth as a river stone: *someone is coming, someone is coming, someone is coming.* Not a memory. A hope. The last thing Hallowmere had left behind.

"Someone is coming," Ivo said aloud before he could stop himself.

Sefa looked at him. The tom looked at him. Grey-Marrow turned her great grey head, and her eyes were the color of thaw-water over ice.

"Yes," she said, in the way wolves say things, which is not words but is understood anyway. "And we are not enough."

* * *

GREY-MARROW smelled the country dying the way humans smelled smoke.

It did not smell of death. Death had a grammar she knew — blood, bowels, cold meat, the sweetish panic-smell of things that knew they were prey. This was the absence of grammar. They crossed a river whose fish had forgotten how to be fish and lay belly-up in the shallows, not rotting, just *stopped*, and the water tasted of nothing at all, which frightened her more than any poison. Poison had an author.

Her pack felt it too. Fen-Shoulder, youngest, kept pressing close to her flank, and she let him, because even an alpha needed to pretend there was a shape ahead worth walking toward. Behind them walked the two soft-skinned ones — the woman who drew marks in the air like she was stitching the world shut, and the man who heard.

That one troubled Grey-Marrow. The man heard the way she smelled: everything, all the time, without wanting to. He flinched when the ravens spoke. He apologized to horses under his breath. And twice now he had answered thoughts meant for no one, aloud, his face going white after, like a boy caught stealing bread.

The pack's law was simple: the door chose. The door needed a voice the land trusted, and the land had been withdrawing its trust from Vessany for a season, village by village, name by name. If the man's voice was what the door wanted, then the man was pack, whatever the soft-skins' burning-priests said about such gifts. Grey-Marrow had watched men burn other men for hearing. She had no use for gods that burned their own ears.

But something else hunted them, and it did not smell like the land's hunger. It smelled of ink and iron and fear kept in ledgers. Men on horses, many, moving fast along the old north road.

She told the pack: *We go into the fells tonight. The soft ones walk or we carry them.*

The man heard her decide before she'd finished deciding. He looked up sharply, met her eyes across the fireless camp, and did not look away.

Grey-Marrow held his gaze and thought, deliberately, clearly, so there could be no mistake:

*Run with us, listener. Or be run down.*

He nodded once. Good. Now they only had to outrun the thing that wrote people down and made them less.

* * *

They ran for three days through a country losing its nouns.

Bridges existed that no road led to. A shrine to a harvest god stood in a field where no crop had ever grown, its god's name worn off the stone, its offerings — Ivo checked, couldn't help himself — fresh within the week. Whoever left them no longer knew why. On the fourth day they passed a shepherd standing motionless in the rain, staring at his own crook, turning it over and over, and when Ivo asked if he was well the man looked at him with polite blankness and asked, "Well? Who's asking?" and went back to turning the crook.

"That's the pattern," Sefa said that night, in the hollow beneath a fallen watchtower where the wolves let them shelter. Her voice had gone flat, the way it did when she was reciting rather than feeling. "The wards fail outward-in. First the border, then the roads, then the names. My order taught that the wards were walls. They were never walls. They were *sentences*. Somebody wrote Vessany safe, centuries ago, in a language the land speaks, and somebody has been unpicking the stitches ever since." She fed a stick to the small fire she'd allowed herself. "The guild's vault. Odile keeps calling it an asset. Assets don't need feeding, Ivo. Living things do."

"You think the vault-beast is the author. Or the pen."

"I think the guild has been spending it for three hundred years to keep the ledger balanced, and the bill has come due." She looked at him across the fire, and for once there was no ward-keeper's composure in her face, only a tired woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Odile will offer them a trade. She always trades. She'll give them us, if it balances."

Ivo wanted to argue. He had spent nineteen years inside the guild's warmth — the lamplight, the ledger-paper smell, Odile's dry voice teaching him double-entry as a kindness, *everything must be accounted for, Ivo, especially yourself.* But he could hear the tom-cat, still following them, still thinking *someone is coming,* and he found he could not construct the defense.

"They're two days behind," he said instead. "Ravens told me. Odile travels with twelve hunters and a warded cage."

Grey-Marrow, at the mouth of the hollow, lifted her head.

*I know,* she thought. *The cage smells of the vault. Of home.*

Ivo went very still. "Home?"

The alpha looked at him for a long moment, and what came through was not words but an image, ancient and wolf-simple: a door. A door under a mountain of roots and stone, older than the wards, older than the writing. Something had been kept on this side of it so long it had forgotten there was another side.

*The land does not hate the beast,* Grey-Marrow thought. *The land misses it. You cannot unwrite a country while its heart still beats somewhere. So they starve it. They keep it in a box and call the starving profit.*

Sefa had gone grey. "Then the wards were never protection. They were a *hutch*."

"And the door opens," Ivo said slowly, the shape of it assembling in him like dawn, "when someone gives the heart back."

*When a voice the land trusts asks it to,* Grey-Marrow corrected. *The door is deaf to kings. It listens for—*

The raven hit the watchtower stones above them screaming, and the night filled with horses.

* * *

They came with lanterns that ate color — warded light, guild-made, the kind Ivo had carried on audits himself — and they came in a crescent, cutting off the fells, and at their center rode Odile's huntsmaster, a narrow woman called Vess with frostbite scars on both cheeks, holding a document out before her like a relic.

"Ward-keeper Sefa," she called into the dark. "Guildmaster Odile extends terms. Return the asset's key-bearer and the ward-thief, and the guild forgives your desertion. Refuse—" she consulted the paper, theatrical, "—and the guild declares you both *unwritten.* There are forms for it. We've filed them already."

Ivo's stomach dropped. *Unwritten.* He hadn't known the word until Vessany started doing it to its villages. He hadn't known the guild had learned to do it to *people.*

Sefa rose slowly beside him. "And the vault-beast?"

"The guild keeps what the guild keeps," Vess said. "The country ends. Fine. Countries end. The guild *persists.* We have stores. We have wards. We survive the winter, and we rebuild, and in a hundred years nobody living will remember there was ever anything more to want." She lowered the paper. "It's generous, Sefa. Odile is being generous."

"She's being a bookkeeper," Sefa said.

"She's being *right.*"

Grey-Marrow flowed out of the dark to stand between the horses and the hollow, and eleven more shapes rose around her on the rocks, silent, patient, the way winter is patient.

Vess looked at the wolves and then, deliberately, at Ivo. "There it is. The listener. We've known since the audit, boy. Since before the stars went out. Odile protected you for years — did you think that was kindness? A gift like yours is *guild property.* Come home, and we'll never speak of the fires."

And that was the trap in it, the hook set deep: not the threat, the *home.* Lamplight. Ledgers. The found family of paper and rules that had raised him and hidden him and used him, and would keep using him, forever, while the world outside went quietly nameless.

Ivo looked at Sefa. Sefa looked back and gave him nothing — no signal, no absolution. *Choose,* her face said. *I chose exile; you haven't earned your choice yet.*

He looked at Grey-Marrow. The alpha didn't beg. She only thought, calm as snowfall: *The door is two days north. It is listening already. It wants to know who will speak.*

Ivo opened his mouth to answer —

and every bird in the fells went silent at once, and out of the south, faint as a heartbeat, he heard the vault-beast wake.
