## 1. Entries in a Dying Hand

The inventory of impossible things, as of the third frost of autumn:

One jar of borrowed years, vintage uncertain, contents evaporating at a rate I have stopped measuring because measuring it only made me sad.

Two unspoken names, sealed in wax that sweats when it rains. The names belong to people who are almost certainly dead by now, which raises a philosophical question about whether an unspoken name spoken to no one is still unspoken, and whether we should discount them accordingly. Master Corvane says yes. Mistress Sable Vane says the ledger does not deal in discounts, it deals in debts, and debts do not care how you feel about them.

Three griefs, sold by weight. This is my favorite shelf in the whole guildhouse: griefs in little clay pots, labeled like preserves. *Widow's grief, first year, half measure.* *Father's grief for a daughter lost at sea, full jar, heavy.* Grief weighs more than you'd think. A grown man's grief for his mother can run four pounds, easy, and it never lightens on its own. That's why they sell it. Not because anyone else wants to carry it — though some do, God help them, some do — but because the act of selling it makes it weigh less in the pot and less in the chest, both at once. Nobody knows why. It was in the founding ledgers before anyone thought to ask.

Half a bottle of courage. Someone spilled the rest in 1147 and nobody has had the heart to write it off.

And one door, which is not inventory exactly, but which I count anyway, because everything else in this building is leaving and the door stays.

I am Noor Kavanagh, junior keeper of the Guild of the Ledgered Veil, and I am writing this entry in the dying hand of a dying institution, which is to say: in ink, on paper, with no one left alive who will ever read it unless they come looking for trouble.

That last part is the job description, actually. *No one reads these entries.* It is written into our charter, right under the seal, in letters so old the script has fallen out of fashion twice over. We keep accounts no one can read, of transactions no one remembers, for clients who paid us in things that were never theirs to give. If you find that confusing, good. Confusion is the first symptom of understanding how the Veil works. The second symptom is headaches. The third is employment.

* * *

The argument started, as arguments in this guild always start, with Master Corvane saying something true at a volume inappropriate to its truth.

"We have eleven members," he said, standing in the common room with the ledger open across both arms like a priest presenting scripture. "Eleven. When I took my vows there were two hundred and forty. There were waiting lists. There were *applicants*. Now look at us."

We looked. There were six of us in the room, which tells you something about attendance at emergency meetings. Old Fen sat by the fire doing what he always did, which was pretending to be asleep while missing nothing. Sable Vane stood at the window with her back to all of us, which is her version of sitting down but louder. The twins, Pell and Perrot, occupied opposite ends of the same bench, as they did everything in life, including breathing, which they managed in perfect alternation so that between them they constituted one continuous set of lungs.

"Look at us," Corvane repeated, gesturing at the room with the ledger, "and tell me this is a guild. Tell me this isn't just a house where old people keep jars."

"It is a house where old people keep jars," said Fen, eyes closed. "It has been a house where old people keep jars since before your grandfather's grandfather mispronounced his own name. The jars are not the point, Corvane."

"Then what is the point? Because I have read the charter, such as any of us can read it, and I will tell you what I found. I found obligations without counterparties. I found debts held against names that no longer exist. I found a founding bargain so thoroughly redacted that the redactions have redactions." He let the ledger fall shut with a sound like a coffin lid. "We don't know what we owe or to whom. We don't know what we're keeping the veil *for*. Eleven members, and by winter we'll be nine, because Pell's cough sounds like a landslide and everyone here knows it even if nobody's said it."

Nobody said it. Pell examined his fingernails with tremendous interest. Perrot, three feet away and sharing none of his brother's air, watched the fire.

"The motion," Corvane said, quieter now, which was worse, "is dissolution. Wind down the inventory. Return what can be returned, bury what cannot, and let the last of us die as people instead of keepers. I put it to the vote."

And then everyone looked at me, because I am the junior keeper, and in this guild the junior keeper speaks last and is heard least, but also — and I think this is the actual reason — because I am the only one among us who still loves this place without irony, and love, in a room like this, is embarrassing. Like watching someone dance alone.

"I'll need to finish the inventory first," I said.

Corvane closed his eyes. "Noor."

"If we dissolve, fine. But the ledger requires a complete final accounting. That's not sentiment, that's procedure. You taught me procedure yourself, Master, third year, when you made me recopy the salt-road receipts because I'd used the wrong marginalia symbols. 'An institution is only as honest as its worst page,' you said. So let me make our last pages honest ones. Give me until the frost locks the pass. Then call your vote."

He stared at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh it — the part of him that wanted to be rid of the whole rotten, holy business, against the part that had once been young enough to say things about honesty's worst pages. The second part lost. It usually does in him. But he nodded, once, sharp as a blade going back in its sheath.

"Until the frost locks the pass," he agreed. "Then we vote, and whatever the number says, we obey it." He pointed the rolled ledger at me like a weapon he'd decided not to use. "And Kavanagh? Count the door."

I blinked. "Count the—"

"You count it every year in the autumn inventory. Don't think I haven't noticed. An asset that appears on no other page in the history of the guild, and you enter it faithfully every single time, valued at—" he consulted memory, or perhaps contempt, "—one door."

"One door," I confirmed.

"Why?"

Because, Master, because the door is the only thing in this building that has ever opened onto anything, because every impossible thing we trade walks through it eventually, because I have watched it admit grief-stricken widows and nameless kings and one extremely apologetic ghost, and because when everything else on the shelves is gone the door will still be a door, and someone will still be on the other side of it, needing.

What I said was: "Sentiment, Master. You may dock my wages."

He snorted and left, taking the ledger's twin volumes under his arm. Fen opened one eye, considered me with the mild menace of a cat deciding whether the mouse is worth the effort, and closed it again. At the window, Sable Vane hadn't moved through any of it. Her reflection in the glass watched me with an expression I couldn't parse and wouldn't forget, though I didn't know yet why I'd need to remember it.

* * *

Here is what I know about how the Veil works, which is more than most of our own members know and less than the ledger knows, and the difference between those two quantities is where I live, professionally speaking.

People come to us when they need to get rid of something that cannot be carried and cannot be dropped. A year they cannot afford to live. A name that would kill them if spoken. A grief too heavy to survive intact. We take these things — we buy them, technically, though the currency varies — and we hold them behind the veil, and the veil holds them out of the world. And in exchange for holding them, the veil grants the guild its other property, the one Corvane didn't mention because he doesn't know it exists, or knows and can't bear to say:

Nobody sees us.

Not invisibility like a spell. Invisibility like furniture. Kings have signed treaties in our front room and walked out unable to say where they'd been. Tax assessors have knocked on our door, stood inside it, and left having concluded the building was a wall. For as long as there has been a Guild of the Ledgered Veil, the world has looked straight at us and recorded nothing. We are the margin note the eye skips. We are the page turned past.

I used to think that was simply what the veil did — that holding the impossible out of sight required the holders to share the condition. It seemed tidy. Symmetrical. The kind of bargain old magic likes.

But I have been reading the deep ledgers lately — the ones chained in the cellar, the ones whose script predates the kingdoms whose histories mention us not at all — and I have begun to notice something in the margins. Little entries. Bookkeeping entries, the kind any keeper would recognize: debits, credits, carryovers. Except the entries aren't ours. The handwriting isn't any keeper's hand I know. And the dates go back further than the guild's official founding by a margin I initially assumed was clerical error, and then assumed was fraud, and have finally, reluctantly, begun to assume is neither.

Something paid for our invisibility. Something pays for it still, installment by installment, century by century. And the account, whatever it is, is drawing down. You can see it in the numbers the way you can see winter in the way birds sit on wires: hunched, waiting, counting.

Our inventory shrinks. Our membership shrinks. And I have a theory — junior keeper's theory, worth exactly what you paid for it — that those two facts are the same fact.

* * *

I worked through the evening and most of the night, because griefs must be weighed before first light (they gain weight after dawn; nobody knows why that either) and because the alternative to working was lying in my cot listening to Pell cough two rooms away and thinking about votes.

The storeroom at midnight is my favorite place in the world, and I am aware of how that reflects on me. Shelves to the ceiling, each one labeled in the hands of dead keepers. Jars of years with their corks waxed and dated. Names in boxes that whisper if you hold them near your ear, which is forbidden, which is why I have done it exactly once, years ago, and heard nothing except the sound a name makes when nobody is saying it: a small, patient pressure, like a knock from inside a door.

I cataloged. I weighed. I wrote.

*Borrowed years: 1 jar (evaporating). Unspoken names: 2 (wax sweating; recommend re-sealing). Griefs, assorted: 3. Courage: ½ bottle. Door: 1.*

And then, because the frost was coming and the vote was coming and because honest pages are the only inheritance I'll ever be able to leave this place, I added a line I have never added before, in the smallest hand I could manage, at the very bottom of the final column, where auditors never look and where, tonight, I needed something true:

*Liabilities, outstanding: unknown. Counterparty: unknown. Term: exceeded. Note — the veil is thinning. Recommend inquiry.*

There. Let the record show somebody asked.

I was blotting the entry when I heard it. Not from the street. Not from the common room, where the embers had gone gray and Fen had finally achieved genuine sleep. From outside. From beyond the door.

A knock.

Two knocks, precisely — and every keeper learns the code before they learn the alphabet, because the door only opens for the right pattern, and the right pattern has not changed since the founding, and no living person outside this guild knows it.

I stood very still. The candles guttered in a draft that had no source. On the shelf above me, the two sealed names began, very softly, to sweat.

I went to the door, put my hand on the iron ring, and told myself the sensible things: that it was a trick, a test, Corvane checking my discipline before the vote. That no stranger knew the knock. That no stranger had known it for three hundred years.

I opened it anyway. Of course I opened it. I have already confessed I love this place, and love means opening doors.

On the step, mud to the knees and shivering in a coat that fit someone else's life, stood a child of maybe eight years, looking up at me with eyes far too old for the face they were wearing. Behind the child, the fog lay along the street like something waiting.

"Please," the child said — and then stopped, frowned, and tried again, in a language I did not know and yet understood the way you understand a bell: not the words, but what they mean. The words were older than the street. Older than the city. Older, I would learn, than the kingdoms themselves.

The child spoke. Then knelt, wetted one finger in the gutter muck, and wrote something on our doorstep stone, in careful strokes, in that same impossible tongue.

I looked down at it, and the cold went through me like a coin through a torn pocket, because I could not read the language —

but I knew the name.

It was mine. My secret name, the one given to me at my vows, the one I have never spoken aloud, the one that exists nowhere in any ledger, any register, any memory but my own.

The child looked up at me, and said, in the old tongue that meant itself, a word that translated somewhere below language, straight into the marrow:

*Found you.*
