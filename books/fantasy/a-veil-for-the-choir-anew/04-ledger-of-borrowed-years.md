## 4. Ledger of Borrowed Years

The oldest ledgers live in the deepest room of the guildhouse, which is also the coldest and the dampest and the one most likely to be haunted, because of course it is. Nothing in this institution can simply be old. It has to be *old in a way that sighs*.

Sable Vane had given me the key the night before, pressing it into my palm with a look I'd never seen on her before—something between apology and indigestion.

"Don't read past the red thread," she'd said.

"What happens if I do?"

"Nothing you can undo."

Which is the guild's version of *goodnight, sleep well*, so naturally by dawn I had a candle, a pot of cold tea, and every intention of reading past the red thread.

To understand why, you have to understand what the child did to us. Three days since he arrived mud-caked at our door, speaking a language that made our wards sit up like dogs hearing their names. Two days since he wrote my secret name in the dust—a name I have never spoken aloud, never written, never told anyone living or dead. A name I am not entirely certain I knew until I saw it.

Since then, Sable had locked the archive, doubled the wards, and started drinking her tea with something stronger in it. Since then, the senior members argued dissolution again, louder, and I had sat in the corner taking minutes nobody would read, feeling the particular ache of loving an institution that has never once loved me back.

And since then, the child had slept in the east room and talked in his sleep in a voice too deep for his chest, reciting things like a man dictating letters from a great distance. Last night I heard him say, clearly, in plain Common: *"The fourth ledger is not what they think it is."*

I am invisible. It is my single greatest professional skill. Nobody sees me take the key, nobody sees me go down, nobody sees me light the candle in a room where the shadows lean away from the flame as if they've been warned about it.

* * *

The archive is a long vault of stone shelving, and the ledgers are arranged by age the way sediment arranges itself—heaviest and darkest at the bottom. The newest volumes are mine: neat columns of griefs bought and sold, years borrowed against names, all the small impossible commerce that keeps the free cities' hidden economy humming. Above them, generations of keepers' hands, each more cramped than the last, as though handwriting itself grew tired over the centuries.

At the very bottom, behind the red thread Sable had tied like a tourniquet, sat four ledgers.

They were bound in something that wasn't leather. I've handled enough strange goods in this trade to know vellum from dragonhide from the pressed skin of things better left unnamed, and this was none of them. It was smooth and grey and slightly warm, and when I set my palm on the first cover, I felt a pulse—not a heartbeat, exactly. More like the memory of one.

"Right," I said to the empty room. Empty rooms are my best conversationalists. "Let's see what we've done."

The first ledger opened to entries dated four hundred and eleven years ago. The ink was brown with age but the hand was fresh somehow, unweathered, as though the words had been written yesterday and merely *waited*. And here is where I should describe the entries, and I will, but first let me tell you what struck me, which was not the content but the *handwriting itself*.

It was round. Childlike, almost—but not clumsy. Each letter formed with the careful precision of someone learning to write for the first time and doing it perfectly, the way children sometimes draw faces that adults cannot manage. Loops like little birds. Crossbars placed exactly where crossbars belong.

I knew that hand.

I had spent three days cataloguing everything the child touched—the mud on the doorstep, the cup he drank from, the dust where he'd written my name. His hand was unsteady with exhaustion, but the shapes were the same. The loops were birds. The crossbars sat exactly right.

The entries in the first ledger were dated centuries before the child was born. If children could be said to be born at all, in his case, which I was beginning to doubt.

* * *

Here is what the entries said. I copy them now from memory, because I copied them that night into my own notebook, because some part of me understood even then that I would need proof of what I saw, and that paper remembers better than people do.

*Borrowed: forty years of rain, deferred. Repaid: one summer of the king's boyhood.*

*Borrowed: the plague's turning, south quarter. Repaid: the king's memory of his mother's face.*

*Borrowed: nine years of peace. Repaid: the king's name, true form, spoken once and withheld thereafter.*

On and on. Four hundred years of entries, and every repayment column held the same currency. Rain for memories. Peace for childhood. Plague stayed, and in exchange, another piece of Aldric—because it was Aldric, there was no longer any pretending otherwise; the sleeping child two floors above had been dictating these terms in his own round bird-looped hand while kings came and went and kingdoms forgot there had ever been anything under the crown but crown.

I sat down on the cold floor without meaning to. My tea had gone completely over to the cold side and no longer deserved the name.

We talk, in the guild, about the founding bargain the way sailors talk about the sea. Reverent. Vague. Sable had begun, the night before, to tell me pieces of it—invisibility purchased at a price paid by a king—and I had nodded along with the comfortable numbness of a junior member being handed institutional mythology. Oh, yes, the bargain. Yes, the veil. Every guild has its ghost story.

Except this wasn't a story. This was an account book. And accounts, unlike stories, have balances.

I turned pages faster now, the candle guttering, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. Every impossible thing the guild had ever sold—every borrowed year, every unsaid name, every ounce of weighed grief—had been paid for out of one wallet. One soul, drawn down in installments. We hadn't been merchants. We'd been *spending him*. Four centuries of miracles, funded by the slow demolition of King Aldric, who sat in his palace missing pieces he couldn't name, signing decrees with gaps in his hours, hollowed out entry by entry by entry.

And the thing wearing his crown—whatever it was, whatever Sable feared—it had moved in the way rot moves into a house whose owner keeps selling rooms.

* * *

Somewhere around the third ledger I found the war entries, and I stopped sitting comfortably and started sitting like a person ready to run.

*Borrowed: the Veil entire. Effect: the Guild unseen, unrecorded, unminded by crown and commons alike. Term: perpetual. Collateral: [entry continues in smaller hand].*

The collateral line went on for nearly a full page. It listed, with the same patient bird-looped letters, everything the guild had ever promised as surety against its own invisibility. The founding members' names—I recognized three of them from portraits in the front hall, painted younger and uglier than they'd have liked. The guild's charter. The archive itself. And then, at the bottom, appended in fresher ink, dated ninety-one years ago:

*Collateral renewed and expanded per standing agreement. Added: one keeper's apprentice, enrolled this day. Name recorded. Value assessed: negligible now; appreciable later.*

Ninety-one years ago. There had been no apprentices ninety-one years ago. I checked—I turned back through the third ledger, through the second, looking for enrollment records, because the guild records everything, we are constitutionally incapable of not recording things.

There was one apprentice listed. Enrolled ninety-one years ago. One line.

*Kavanagh, Noor. Assigned: keeper's apprentice, indefinite term. Note: enrollee arrives early.*

I read it eleven times. I am not exaggerating; I counted, because counting kept me from screaming, and screaming in the archive is frowned upon mostly because the archives scream back.

Ninety-one years ago, a ledger written in the hand of a child who appeared at our door last week recorded my apprenticeship. My name. Not just my name—*arrives early*, which is a joke, a joke, in a book four centuries old, about me, the invisible girl who catalogs other people's impossibilities and has never once been considered an impossibility herself.

Except apparently I had been. Assessed. Valued. Filed as *collateral* against a debt incurred before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk.

The candle had burned down to a stub. The shadows in the corners had crept back to their normal distance, which I chose to find reassuring and not at all like they'd gotten bored of watching me.

* * *

Here is the thing about vertigo that nobody tells you: it isn't the falling. Falling is easy; people do it constantly. Vertigo is the sudden certainty that the floor was never where you thought it was—that you have spent your whole life standing on nothing and calling it ground.

I had built my whole self around this guild. That is an embarrassing sentence to write and a truer one than any I've penned in the official minutes. My parents died of the grey cough when I was six; the guild took me in—not warmly, not cruelly, just *took* me, the way a ledger takes an entry. I grew up between these shelves. I know which floorboard creaks near the stillroom and which senior member waters down the wine and how Sable Vane's face moves when she's lying (it doesn't; that's how you tell). I loved this place with the dumb, stubborn love of a stray for the hand that feeds it, and I told myself the hand didn't need to pet me for the arrangement to be worthwhile.

And the whole time—before I was even born—the guild had been spending a king's soul to keep its doors invisible, and had filed *me* among the assets.

I did the only reasonable thing. I sat on the cold stone floor of a haunted archive at four in the morning and laughed until it turned into the other thing, and then I wiped my face with my sleeve, picked up my candle, and kept reading. Because if there is one thing this institution taught me, it's that you finish the entry. You don't get to stop mid-ledger. Whatever the numbers say, you follow them to the bottom line.

The fourth ledger was the smallest. It was also, I realized as I lifted it, warm—warmer than the others, warm like a sleeping animal, and beneath my hands its pulse quickened faintly, as if it noticed me noticing it.

There was only one entry.

It was undated. The hand was the child's, but older somehow—not the careful loops of a learner but a settled script, the writing of someone who had been doing this a very long time, which raised questions about the word "someone" that I was not prepared to examine by candlelight.

It said:

*Final entry pending. When the fragment returns to the whole, all accounts settle. The keeper who reads this owes nothing yet. She will. Bring the fragment home, and bring the pen.*

Then, beneath, smaller, almost an afterthought, in plain Common:

*She always does. I'm sorry. —A.*

I closed the ledger. The room was very quiet. Two floors above, I heard—or imagined I heard, though my imagination has never once been that kind—a child's voice, talking in his sleep.

* * *

I don't remember climbing the stairs. I remember standing in the east room doorway with candle-ends for fingers, watching Wren sleep—Sable had taken to calling him Wren, small brown bird, nothing to do with kings—and his lips were moving. Shaping words. And the words, when I leaned close enough, were my name. Not the secret one. Just *Noor*, over and over, gentle as a lullaby, in a mouth that had signed my life into a ledger before my parents ever met.

Behind me, a floorboard creaked—the one near the stillroom, the traitorous one—and I turned to find Sable Vane in the corridor holding a lamp, and her face did something it never did.

It moved.

"I tied a red thread on that door," she said quietly.

"I know," I said. "I read past it."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then past me, at the sleeping boy who had been paying our bills for four hundred years, and she sighed the sigh of a woman whose worst ledger line had finally come due.

"Then you know what we owe," she said. "And Noor—" her voice dropped, and outside, far off toward the salt road, something rumbled that might have been thunder and might have been armies, "—you should know the collection has already begun."

She turned and walked toward the map room, and after a moment, because I always do, because I finish the entry, I followed.
