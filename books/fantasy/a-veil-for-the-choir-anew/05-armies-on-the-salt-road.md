## 5. Armies on the Salt Road

I had been a soldier for nineteen years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things worth teaching: dig your latrines uphill from your water, never trust an officer who volunteers for the forage detail, and when the sky over a town turns the wrong color at dawn, it is already too late to be useful.

Brass Hollow was burning by the time we crested the ridge, and the sky above it was the color of a bruise someone had pressed too long. Orange at the heart. Black at the edges. And threaded through the smoke, faint as a vein of ore in dull stone, a shimmer I recognized and wished to every god I didn't believe in that I didn't.

Guild-light. The glow of impossible goods burning.

"Gods below," said Sergeant Tam Voss beside me, spitting into the heather. "That's not a raid. That's a *harvest*."

"That's a message," I said.

The Salt Road ran from the free cities east toward the old kingdom like a scar across the world's back, and everything along it lived or died by what traveled it. Salt, mostly, in the honest years. In the dishonest years—the ones nobody wrote down—other things moved in sealed wagons with guild seals waxed over their locks: borrowed years in stoppered jars, unspoken names folded into paper that whispered if you held it to your ear, griefs weighed out on scales fine enough to measure a widow's Tuesday against her Sunday.

Brass Hollow had been one of the road's quiet waystations. A town of maybe four hundred souls, two inns, a shrine to a god whose name had worn off the lintel, and beneath the floor of the second inn—a cellar where a ledgered factor kept inventory no customs officer could have read without going quietly mad.

Now it was a torch.

We watched from the ridge because there was nothing else to do. That is the part they don't put in the songs, the part about watching. Nineteen years, and most of my war has been watching things burn from ridges while I counted enemy banners and did arithmetic I hated.

"Count them," I told Voss.

He counted. He was good at counting; it was nearly all he was good at, which is why I kept him. "Banners of the Sun-and-Chain. Six, seven... eight standards visible. Pikes—I'd say three thousand on the road alone. Cavalry wheeling on the far side, can't get a clean number. Siege train behind. Two trebuchets, maybe three."

"And the town?"

"Four hundred people, Captain."

"Three hundred and eighty," I corrected. "The census taker got drunk there last spring and counted the same family twice. They were very proud of their twins." I said it flatly, because if I didn't say something flat, I was going to say something else entirely. "Three hundred and eighty people, and a host of six thousand came down on them before dawn. You don't spend six thousand men on three hundred and eighty people unless you're trying to tell somebody something."

Voss squinted at the smoke. "Telling who?"

"Us." I turned my horse south along the ridge line. "Come on. We've seen what we came to see, and I'd rather not die somewhere so educational."

* * *

Here is what you should know about the free cities, since you'll be hearing a great deal about them and most of it will be flattering nonsense.

There are five of us—Cinderport, Latch, Greywater, the Twins, and Marrowgate—and we are bound together by nothing sturdier than mutual irritation and shared shipping lanes. We have no king. We are insufferably proud of this. We elect councils, the councils elect speakers, the speakers argue until everyone involved wishes fervently for a king just so someone could make a decision, and then we go back to being proud of having none.

For two hundred years this arrangement survived because the kingdom to our east was ruled by a man named Aldric, who was, by all accounts, a reasonable king. Reasonable kings are worth more than good weather. Aldric honored treaties, kept his lords leashed, and once—this is documented, look it up—personally paid to rebuild a Cinderport warehouse district after a fire his own tax collectors had accidentally started. A king who pays for his own mistakes. The free cities could have had worse neighbors. We have had worse neighbors. We are looking at worse neighbors right now.

Because eighteen months ago, something changed in Aldric. The histories will call it madness, probably, because histories prefer madness to the alternative, which is that sometimes a crown is not a hat but a mouth, and sometimes the thing it eats first is the man underneath.

The decrees started small. New tariffs dressed up as tolls. Then garrisons in towns that had never asked for them. Then edicts written in a style no chancery clerk would own up to—sentences that circled back on themselves, orders signed in a hand that looked like Aldric's the way a mask looks like a face. And then, this spring, the levies. Whole villages emptied into the host now marching down the Salt Road, carrying banners of the Sun-and-Chain that hadn't flown in living memory.

The council speakers met for eleven days and produced a resolution, a committee, and a strongly worded letter. I commanded the militia's eastern company, which meant the letter was addressed to me, which meant the committee's work was done and mine was starting.

"You can't fight six thousand with nine hundred," Voss said as we rode south. It wasn't a question. It was the kind of statement soldiers make to hear whether their captain has gone soft in the head.

"No," I agreed. "I can't fight six thousand with nine hundred. But I'm not planning to fight six thousand. I'm planning to survive six thousand, which is a completely different profession."

"And how's that profession pay?"

"Poorly. Mostly in scenery." I nodded back at the smoke. "But there's a wrinkle."

"There's always a wrinkle."

"The wrinkle is that Brass Hollow didn't just burn. Look at the reports again tonight. Every building torched, yes—but the cellar under the Drowned Bell inn? The one with the factor's stock? Burned hottest. Melted stone, Voss. Stone doesn't melt in an ordinary fire, and it certainly doesn't melt in the hour and a half that host spent in that town before marching on. They went in specifically for the guild goods. They knew what was there. They knew *where* it was."

Voss was quiet for a while. Horses are good listeners during silences; mine put her ears back sympathetically.

"Somebody talked," he said finally.

"Somebody always talks. The question is who knows enough to talk about *that*, and the answer is: almost nobody. Which means whoever gave up Brass Hollow either sits very high in the palace or..." I let it hang.

"Or?"

"Or used to sit very high in something else." I thought of the guild then—everyone thinks of the guild eventually, usually around the time they need something impossible—and felt the particular unease I always felt thinking of them, the sense of a debt being tallied somewhere in a book I couldn't see. "There are powers in this world older than the free cities' pride, Voss, and lately I've been getting the feeling the ground under all of us is shifting. The guilds have been invisible for centuries. Invisible things stay invisible by staying out of wars. If the usurper is hunting them now..."

"Then the rules changed."

"The rules changed," I said. "And nobody sent us the new ones."

* * *

We made Greywater by nightfall, and I spent the evening doing what captains actually do, which is not glory but paperwork with occasional stabbing.

Greywater sat where the Salt Road crossed its namesake river, and it was the next sensible target after Brass Hollow, which made it my problem. The town had walls older than anyone's grandmother and a garrison of sixty, plus my nine hundred scattered through the surrounding villages. Behind me, two days' march, lay the marsh country where a smaller force could make a larger one bleed. Ahead of me, three days east, the host would come.

I stood on the wall after midnight with a cup of wine I wasn't drinking and did the arithmetic again. It hadn't improved. Arithmetic rarely takes criticism well.

"You're doing the face," said a voice below me.

Corporal Isbet Sarn climbed the ladder with the ease of a woman who had been climbing things since before she could walk, which in Greywater meant since birth. She was twenty-six, sharp as a fishhook, and the best scout I had, which is a sentence I could only say because my previous best scout was dead.

"What face?"

"The face where you've decided to do something stupid and you're waiting for the wine to agree with you."

"The wine abstains." I handed her the cup anyway. "Tell me what you found."

Isbet drank, grimaced at the vintage—it was terrible; I keep terrible wine on walls because good wine makes sentries sentimental—and leaned on the parapet beside me.

"They're not looting," she said. "That's the strange part. I got close enough to smell the cookfires. An army that size should strip a burned town to the nails, and they left the nails. Left the wells. Left the shrine standing, even, and the shrine's the only building with a roof left. They took the guild stock and nothing else. Not grain, not iron, not coin. There were bodies—" she paused, and her voice flattened into the tone soldiers use to carry bad news without dropping it, "—and they weren't pretty, but they weren't massacred either. The host killed the people who fought and the people who ran the factor's cellar, and marched everyone else out on the road behind them."

"Took hostages?"

"Worse. Took *witnesses*. Marching them east, fed and watered, guarded but not chained. Ede—they want people to see this. They want the story walking ahead of the army, town by town. 'This is what happens to places that trade with the hidden guilds.'" She looked at me sideways. "You already knew that."

"I suspected. Confirmation's nicer." I took the cup back and still didn't drink. "Anything else?"

"One thing." Isbet hesitated, and Isbet doesn't hesitate, which frightened me more than the army did. "On the road east of the burn, there's a milestone. Old one, pre-Founding carving on the base, some script nobody reads. This morning it read plain as print, in trade-tongue: *THE LEDGER CLOSES. ALL DEBTS COME DUE.*"

I stared at her. "Written on stone."

"Written on stone that was blank yesterday. My runner swears it was blank yesterday. And Ede—" she dropped her voice, though we were alone on the wall with the wind, "—the letters weren't carved. They were grown. Like frost. Like the stone remembered the words and decided to show them."

Below us, Greywater slept, four thousand souls who trusted the wall and the militia and the general soundness of the world. East of us, six thousand men slept in ranks, dreaming whatever the Sun-and-Chain allowed them to dream. Somewhere between those two facts, a milestone was growing words.

"All debts come due," I repeated. It sounded like a creditor's phrase. It sounded, in fact, exactly like something I'd heard once, years ago, in a room I couldn't quite remember, from a person whose face slid away whenever I reached for it—which happens more often than you'd think, in the free cities, near anything the guilds have touched. Memory is slippery around their wares. Everyone knows that. Nobody says it.

"Captain?" Isbet said. "What do we do?"

What we did, I told her, was send riders. Three of them, fast horses, different roads, all bearing the same message to the same address—an address that wasn't an address, a door that wasn't on any street, carried in a cipher the militia had inherited from somebody's grandfather and never understood. *The host burns guild waystations. It comes west within the week. Whatever bargain your people struck two hundred years ago, the other party to it has stopped honoring it. We would speak.*

Whether the guild would answer, I didn't know. Whether the guild could answer—whether there was enough guild left to answer with—I knew even less. I'd heard the rumors same as everyone: the ledgers thinning, the members dwindling, the impossible goods running scarce as honesty in a tax season. Institutions die the way empires do, slowly and then all at once, and I had a bad feeling the guild was somewhere in the *all at once*.

But the message cost three riders and a day, and the alternative was facing six thousand men with nine hundred and a strong worded letter.

"Send them tonight," I said. "Before dawn. And Isbet—if any of them asks why the militia is writing to the guild, tell them the truth."

"Which is?"

I looked east, where the horizon still glowed faintly over what remained of Brass Hollow, and where, further on, a king who was not a king was gathering an army around a purpose none of his generals could name aloud.

"Tell them the debts are coming due," I said, "and we'd rather settle them standing than kneeling."

She went down the ladder fast. I stayed on the wall until the riders slipped out the postern gate one by one, small dark shapes swallowed by the dark country, carrying our question toward a door I half-remembered and couldn't find again if you paid me.

Behind me, Greywater's bells rang the third watch. Ahead of me, the Salt Road waited, patient as a ledger line, for whatever we would write on it next.

And far to the east, in a palace I had never seen, something wearing a king's face turned its attention west—and I felt it, the way you feel weather change in an old wound.
