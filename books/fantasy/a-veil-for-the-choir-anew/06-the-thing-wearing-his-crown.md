## 6. The Thing Wearing His Crown

There are three hours missing from yesterday. I know this the way a man knows he has been robbed—not by witnessing the theft, but by the shape of the empty space left behind.

I went to bed after the third bell, as I have every night for eleven years of this reign, and I woke at dawn with ink on my right hand and wax under my fingernails and a decree on my desk bearing my seal, my signature, and my full and terrible authority. *Levy the winter granaries of Callowmere,* it read. *Disperse the Second and Third Banners to the Salt Road.* The hand was mine. The flourish on the final *r* was mine—I have always made too much of that letter, curled it like a cat's tail, a habit my old tutor beat at for two years before surrendering. The words were not.

I do not think in those sentences. I have never in my life called soldiers "banners," as though men were cloth to be hung from walls. Whoever wrote that decree thinks of armies the way a carpenter thinks of nails: by function, by count, without tenderness.

I burned it. Then I sat on the cold floor of my study with my back against the hearth—unlit; I had not thought to light it; I had not thought anything, which is its own kind of horror—and I waited to feel afraid.

The fear came late, the way it does. First there is only the strangeness, the wrongness, the sense that the world has been very slightly rehung on its wall and everything is half an inch lower than you remember. Fear arrives when you realize the picture was moved by someone else's hands.

* * *

Let me be honest, since honesty is all I have left that hasn't been tampered with: I am not a great king. I want that on the record, wherever records go when they burn. I am a moderately competent man who inherited a moderately troubled kingdom and has done his best to keep the wolf from the door by feeding it occasionally from the neighbors' flocks. I have signed foolish treaties and wise ones, promoted two good chancellors and one embezzler, wept exactly twice in public and blamed the dust both times. My father would have called me soft. My mother would have agreed, fondly.

But I knew, every morning of my reign until four months ago, what I had done the night before. That is the whole of kingship, really—not wisdom, not courage, just continuity. A king who cannot remember his own decrees is a kingdom with a hole in it, and things move through holes.

Four months ago the gaps began. Small at first. An hour lost here, an evening gone there. I told myself it was the wine. I cut back the wine. The gaps grew anyway, politely, like ivy. They never took anything I would notice immediately—it was never the day of a coronation or a funeral that vanished, never the obvious hours. It was the dull ones. The gray ones. As if whatever was taking them had studied me first and learned precisely which memories I would miss least.

That is what frightens me most, when I let myself think about it, and I try not to let myself, and then I think about nothing else for a week: whoever is doing this has been *attentive*. You cannot steal from a man so carefully unless you have watched him a long time. Unless you know him.

Unless, some voice in the cellar of my mind whispers, you *are* him.

I pour the second glass of wine after all. For medicinal purposes.

* * *

This morning Chancellor Orrey came to me with the muster rolls, beaming like a man presenting a grandson. "Magnificent, sire," he said. "The speed of it. The precision. Fourteen thousand marched within three days of your order—the lords complain, of course, but they complain the way they complain about weather. One almost believes you *want* the free cities."

"One almost," I said, and smiled, because a king's face is crown property and does not belong to him.

Orrey is sixty and loyal and has served me since my father's time, and I could not ask him the question sitting in my throat, because the question was: *Orrey, when did I give this order? Describe the room. Describe my mood. What did I say with my mouth while my hand was signing away the autumn?* And if he answered—if he said, *Why, last Tuesday, sire, you were in fine spirits, you joked about the hunt*—then either he was lying, or he had spoken to a thing wearing my face and never known the difference, and either answer would break something in me that I need intact.

So instead I asked mild questions, kingly questions, and Orrey answered them, and I nodded, and through the whole interview a thought kept circling me like a gull after a fishing boat: *fourteen thousand men are walking toward the free cities because of an order I have never seen until this morning.*

They will burn towns. I know how this goes; my grandfather wrote a memoir about it that survives mainly as a doorstop. Towns burn, and the burning is done by boys with pikes who believe—in the honest, simple way boys believe things—that their king willed it. And their king did will it. His hand signed it. His seal closed it. And yet some remainder of their king, the part currently holding a wineglass and trying not to shake, never consented to any of it.

Where does responsibility live, in such a case? In the hand? In the hollow where the man used to be? I would ask the priests, but the priests answer to the crown, and I no longer trust the crown to relay my questions faithfully.

* * *

I have begun leaving myself notes.

This sounds mad. I have considered the madness hypothesis carefully and rejected it on the grounds that madmen rarely worry so methodically about being mad. So: notes. Slips of paper in my own hand, hidden in places only I know—the false bottom of my mother's jewelry case, the spine of the atlas in my private study, inside the left boot I never wear because it pinches. Short notes. *You are Aldric. You were born in the spring, you hate the taste of anise, you loved Maren Corvey when you were nineteen and she married a shipwright and you were glad, genuinely glad, when her children thrived.* Small proofs of self. If the thief can take hours, perhaps he can take tastes and loves as well, and I want evidence preserved somewhere outside my skull, which has proven to be a building with unreliable locks.

Last night I found a note I do not remember writing.

It was in the atlas, wedged between the maps of the southern coasts. My hand, unmistakably—but hurried, the letters leaning as though pushed. It said:

*He comes when the candles gutter. Do not fight him in front of the mirror. He is stronger there.*

I sat with that for a long time. Then I checked every candle in the study, found three burning low, replaced them all with fresh ones, and turned the mirror to face the wall.

I am aware of how this reads. I am aware that if I ever speak of it aloud, the court physicians will be summoned, and after them the quieter men with the quieter solutions. Kings who report intruders in their own skins do not long keep their thrones, whatever the truth of the intrusion. So I say nothing. I sign what is put before me when it seems safe to refuse and unsafe to sign, and I have gotten quite good at the headache excuse, and I go through my days like a man walking on river ice, listening all the time for the crack beneath the ordinary.

* * *

Tonight the gap came early.

I was reading dispatches at the ninth bell—a tedious petition about bridge tolls from a baron whose name I now cannot recall, which may mean nothing or may mean the thief has begun taking names—and the candle nearest my hand guttered, though there was no draft, and then—

Then it was the first bell after midnight, and the bridge-toll petition was gone, and in its place lay a war council's summary in my handwriting. Troop rotations. Supply lines. A schedule of advance down the Salt Road with dates attached, dates falling in the coming weeks like teeth. At the bottom, beneath my signature, a marginal note in my own hand: *The guild cities first. Their ledgers must burn.*

Guilds. I know of the hidden ones the way everyone knows of them—as rumor, as grandmother's warnings, as the reason you never bargain twice with the same stranger. Borrowed years. Sold griefs. Nonsense, I had always assumed, the sort of nonsense kingdoms run comfortably on. But my hand had written *their ledgers must burn* with the underline of a man giving instructions to himself, or being instructed, and the pressure of the pen had torn the paper slightly at *burn*, as if the word had been written hard, hungrily.

Whoever holds my hand hates the hidden guilds. That is worth knowing. That is the first true thing I have learned about the tenant in my house: he has enemies, and they are not mine—or perhaps they are, and I simply cannot remember making them.

I stood at the window a while. The palace sleeps badly around me; somewhere below, a sentry coughed the same cough three times, and a dog gave up on barking halfway through. The capital looked beautiful, the way it always does from above at night—lamps like spilled coins, smoke standing straight up in the windless dark. Fourteen thousand of my subjects are camped along a road I chose for them in hours I will never get back, marching toward people who have done me no harm, on behalf of a will that is not mine.

And underneath the fear, if I am honest—and I promised myself honesty, even alone, especially alone—there is curiosity. There is even, God forgive me, a kind of grim fellowship. Whatever wears me in the lost hours is *busy*. It has plans. It moves through my life with purpose while I drift through it signing papers and remembering my one love at nineteen. Some traitor corner of me watches the efficiency of the thing and feels, absurdly, outworked.

I tell that corner to shut up. It mostly does.

* * *

The mirror again. I know I said I'd leave it facing the wall. But a note written to yourself by a self you don't remember is a strange kind of bait, and I am, whatever else has been taken from me, still curious enough to be dangerous to myself.

I turned it around at moonrise. Sat before it with all six candles lit—new ones, tall ones, a small forest of flame between me and the glass—and made myself look.

A tired man of middle years looked back. Gray coming in at the temples ahead of schedule. Good bones wasted on a slouch. My mother's eyes, or what my mother's eyes had become in me: watchful, a little amused, easily hurt. Nothing wrong. Nothing hollow. I laughed at myself, quietly, the laugh echoing oddly in the stone room—

and the candles guttered. All six at once, bent sideways like grass under wind, though the air did not move, and in the glass my reflection stayed upright while the flames leaned, and for one heartbeat—one, no more, I counted afterward the way a drowning man counts waves—the reflection was not tired. It sat straight. Its eyes held none of my mother in them. It looked at me the way a landlord looks at a tenant whose lease has quietly expired, and its mouth moved, and though no sound crossed over, I understood it perfectly, the way you understand thunder.

It said: *Soon.*

Then the candles stood up straight, and the reflection was only a tired man again, and I discovered I had moved backward across the room without deciding to, and that my hand ached from gripping the poker I did not remember picking up.

I turned the mirror to the wall. I sat down. I wrote this—all of this—in the ledger I keep now beneath the floorboard, in code of my own devising, poor protection against a thief who evidently reads me fluently, but a man must do what a man can.

Here is what I know, laid out plainly, so that if the next me reads this he will not have to learn it trembling at midnight as I have:

Something lives in my lost hours. It signs with my hand and commands with my crown. It is marching fourteen thousand souls to war against the free cities, and it wants the hidden guilds destroyed first, and it is patient, and it is *soon*, and I am the only man in this kingdom positioned to oppose it, which would be comforting if I were not also the territory on which the opposition is fought.

Tomorrow the court convenes. Tomorrow Orrey will bring more papers, and some of them will bear orders already signed in my absent hours, and I will smile the crown's smile and search each document for the curl in the final *r*, my one small signature within my signature, my watermark. And tonight, before sleep—which is to say, before whatever waits to use me while I'm gone—I will write one more note and hide it in the boot that pinches.

It says only: *Hold on. Find out what he fears. Every thief fears the owner coming home.*

Somewhere beyond these walls, I do not doubt, the free cities are lighting their lamps and counting their defenses, and in some hidden shop a guild of impossible merchants is weighing griefs on little brass scales, unaware—or perhaps horribly aware—that a king's stolen hours are marching toward them wearing my face.

I blew out the candles myself, one by one, before anything else could.

The dark was total, and in it, faint as a coin dropped in another room, I heard my own voice say: *Thank you.*

I did not sleep at all.
