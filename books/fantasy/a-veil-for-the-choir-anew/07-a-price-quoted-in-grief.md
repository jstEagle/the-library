## 7. A Price Quoted in Grief

The Guild of the Ledgered Veil had not held a full vote in forty-one years, and it showed. The voting chamber sat above the counting room, and it had been used for storage so long that the ballots were kept in a crate labeled WINTER ONIONS, three chairs were missing legs, and someone—Sable, I suspected, though she denied it with the particular vigor that confirms a thing—had been curing hams in the alcove where the Abstentions used to hang.

There were nine of us left. Nine members of an order that once numbered four hundred, gathered on mismatched chairs in a room that smelled faintly of brine and old paper, to decide whether we would march into the capital and commit what the Crown's law called treason and what our own founding charter called, in pre-Founding script I could now partially read, *the repayment of the first debt*.

I want to say the debate was dignified. I want to report that the last keepers of the Ledgered Veil met their possible deaths with the grave eloquence of an older, wiser age. What actually happened was this: Ossian Pel fell asleep twice, Harrow and Quennel got into a shouting match about whether the wards would hold if we all left at once or needed to be let down gently like a patient lowering off a fever, and Mother Tansy knitted through the entire proceedings, only looking up to say "mind the wool" when Harrow knocked her basket off a chair with an expansive gesture.

Wren sat beside me with his feet not quite touching the floor. He had been scrubbed, fed, and given clothes that mostly fit, and he looked like any boy you might find stealing apples off a barge—except that when he grew bored he mouthed words in a language that made the candle flames lean toward him, listening.

And except for the dreams. Every night since he arrived, Wren had slept in the cot next to mine and spoken aloud the memories of a king: coronations, betrayals, a queen's face, the taste of a specific pear on a specific afternoon thirty years gone. I wrote them down each morning before they could fade, filling ledger after ledger with Aldric's scattered self. My handwriting had developed a cramp I'd named after him.

"—which is why," Quennel was finishing, red-faced, "we dissolve. Formally, cleanly, while we still have assets to distribute. The veil is failing anyway. Sable said so herself."

"She said the debt has come due," Harrow corrected. "That's different from failing. A debt coming due means—" he groped for it—"means somebody intends to pay."

"Nobody's paid anything in four hundred years! That's rather the point of the arrangement!"

"Quiet," said Sable Vane.

She hadn't spoken since the vote was called. She sat at the head of the broken chairs with the master ledger open in front of her, and when she said *quiet*, even Mother Tansy's needles stopped.

"The vote isn't whether we dissolve," Sable said. "Dissolution requires closing the ledgers, and closing the ledgers requires settling balances, and our largest outstanding balance is sitting on that chair with clean fingernails and a king's name under his tongue." She nodded at Wren. "The vote is whether we pay."

Silence did its work on the room. Outside, rain came down the gutters of Veil Street in ropes.

"Pay how?" Ossian Pel asked, awake now, which for Ossian was saying something. "We've nothing left. Borrowed years, unspoken names, griefs by weight—we sold the last of the griefs in spring. Our inventory page reads like a beggar's cup."

"Not inventory." Sable turned the master ledger around on its lectern so we could all see it. "Principal."

I should explain something about the master ledger, because everything that followed turned on it. Most guild books record transactions: who borrowed what, who owed whom, which grief weighed four ounces and went home in whose pocket. But the first book—the one bound in hide no tanner will admit to having worked—is different. It doesn't record the guild's business. It records the guild's *terms*. And terms, unlike transactions, can speak back, if you know how to ask them properly.

Sable knew. She laid both hands flat on the open pages and said, in the formal cadence of assessment: *"Ledger of the Veil. State the cost of restoring to King Aldric of the Emberline his whole and rightful memory, against the debt incurred at Founding."*

The pages moved.

I have seen the ledger do small things—shuffling entries, blotting liars' ink, once, memorably, biting Harrow. But I had never heard it speak. It didn't speak in sound. It spoke in pressure, the way a change in weather speaks to your teeth, and simultaneously every character on every visible page rearranged itself into a single line of text, and the line read:

**THE COST IS ONE KEEPER'S EVERY MEMORY OF LOVE.**

Nobody moved. Rain. Candleflame. Somewhere below, the counting room clock ticked with what I decided was insolence.

"That's not a sum," Quennel said finally. His voice had gone thin. "That's not a *price*. That's a person."

"It's a category of person," Sable said quietly. "A keeper. One of us."

"And every memory of love—" Harrow stopped. He was an old man built like a door, and I watched the arithmetic arrive in his face one grim number at a time. "Every memory of love is everyone. Mothers. Brothers. Wives. The friend who pulled you out of the canal when you were nine. It's not forgetting facts. It's—" he swallowed— "it's being emptied of everyone who ever mattered, and keeping the shape of the hole."

"Yes," said Sable.

Mother Tansy set down her knitting. She looked, for the first time all evening, entirely present, and very old, and very calm. "Then the question is who," she said. "And I'll tell you now it isn't me, because I'm ninety-one and most of mine are already on the other side of the grass. The ledger would be robbing a house that's mostly empty. It needs a full one." Her eyes went around the table, and rested, briefly, on me, and moved on, and I understood later that she was already grieving, and hiding it the way she hid everything, behind wool.

They began to argue again. Who had the least to lose, who had the most, whether it could be split, whether it could be cheated, whether the ledger could be lied to (it couldn't; the ledger had eaten a liar once, or so Ossian claimed, and Ossian's lies were at least well researched). I barely heard them. I was watching Wren.

He had gone still in the way he went still when a memory rose in him that wasn't a boy's—a king's memory, surfacing like a body in a pond. His eyes had fixed on the middle distance, on something thirty years and two hundred miles away.

"He signs them himself," Wren whispered. It wasn't to me, exactly. It was to the air, to the dead pear-tasting afternoon, to whatever fragment of Aldric lived in his sleep. "The marching orders. His hand shakes at the end of every signature and he doesn't know why it shakes. He asks the crown why it shakes and the crown says *the cold, majesty, the winter comes early this year*." Wren blinked and came back to the room, to the candles, to my hand which had somehow found his shoulder. "Noor. They're going to make someone forget everybody."

"I know," I said.

"There has to be another way. There's always—guilds have rules. Escapes. Small print."

"This guild *is* the small print," I said, "and there's no escape clause. Believe me. I've read it more times than anyone alive, mostly while avoiding parties."

He laughed, wet-eyed, and something in my chest performed an operation I did not authorize.

Here is what you should understand about me, and I will say it plainly because the moment deserves plainness: I am nobody's favorite. I am the youngest member of a dying order, the one who catalogs the stock and sweeps the ash and is sent to the market for fish. The guild forgot to celebrate my twentieth year entirely—not out of malice but out of habit; I am easy to overlook, quiet as a marginal note. I loved them anyway. All of them. The snoring Ossian and the knitting and Harrow's terrible jokes and Sable's hams and the creak of the stairs I have climbed ten thousand times. I loved the guild itself, the idea of it, doors that open for the impossible and keep it safe. If you had asked me an hour earlier what I had of value, I would have said: almost nothing, except that I belong somewhere, and somewhere belongs to me.

If you had asked me what it would cost to keep that belonging whole—to pay the principal, not the interest—I would have told you, without understanding, without *any* understanding, the way you tell a child about death:

Anything.

So perhaps I should have understood. Perhaps some part of me did, and simply declined to inform the rest of me, the way the body declines to inform you of the knife until after.

"—and in any case it must be someone who can walk afterward," Quennel was saying. "Someone young enough to rebuild a life around the gap. Not that anyone here—"

"I'll do it."

Four words. I said them at a normal speaking volume, which in that room, after hours of argument, landed like dropped crockery.

Everyone turned. Nine faces, and one small boy's, and the ledger's open pages, patient as water.

"Noor." Sable's voice was careful. Careful the way a surgeon is careful. "Sit down, child."

"It should be me." I stood, which surprised me; I hadn't decided to stand, my body had simply gotten ahead of my sense. "You're all going to say it should be someone with less to lose, but think about it. Tansy's right—the ledger wants a full account. Mine is full. And I'm the one who can read the old hand, I'm the one Wren trusts, I'm the one who's been transcribing the king's memories for a fortnight—if anyone carries Aldric's self back to him, it has to be someone who knows where all the pieces are filed." I was talking fast now, riding the momentum downhill. "Besides. You'd all be terrible at being forgotten. Harrow would haunt us. Actually haunt us, out of spite."

Harrow opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again and produced nothing, which may have been a first.

"Noor," Mother Tansy said, very gently, "do you understand what the ledger asked?"

"Every memory of love," I recited. "Yes."

"Say what that means. Out loud. To this room."

I opened my mouth to say *mothers, brothers, friends, the ordinary harvest*, and found that the words had gone somewhere else. Because the truth arrived all at once, uninvited, wearing its actual face: it meant Tansy's needles and Ossian's snoring and Harrow's laugh and Sable's hand on my head the night I passed my vows, and Wren—Wren's weight against my shoulder on the cot, Wren's hand in mine on the stairs, Wren laughing at my terrible impression of Quennel—it meant the smell of the counting room and the fish market and every single morning I had ever woken glad, and it meant that I would stand in an empty space where all of it used to be and not even know there was a space, because you cannot miss what you cannot remember existing—

I understood it for perhaps three seconds. Fully. The way you understand drowning fully, once.

And then I said, "Yes. I know what it means."

Liar. Keeper of ledgers, keeper of secrets, liar. But my voice didn't shake, and I have been grateful ever since that at least my voice kept faith with me.

Sable looked at me for a long time. Behind her eyes some calculation ran—not about whether, I realized. About *when*. She had known, I think, from the moment the ledger spoke. Possibly from the moment the child knocked twice. Possibly, God help her, from the day she took in a quiet orphan who was good at reading hands no one else could read. The oldest members exchanged glances over my head—Tansy, Harrow, Ossian, Quennel—and those glances contained a silence with a whole language in it, and I was not permitted to be fluent in it yet.

What was in that silence, I know now, was this: *they always volunteer before they understand. We did too. Say yes quickly, before the understanding catches up, or she'll live the rest of her life dreading it.*

"Then it's moved and seconded," Sable said, and her voice cracked exactly once, on *moved*, and recovered. "All in favor of restoring King Aldric at the quoted cost, keeper Noor Kavanagh paying, say aye."

Seven voices said aye. The eighth was mine, and it was steady, and Wren's wasn't—he said aye in a voice like tearing cloth, and then put his face in his hands, and I patted his shoulder like a fool, comforting him for my funeral.

"So carried," said Sable Vane. She closed the master ledger with a sound like a coffin lid, gently done. "We leave for the capital in three days. Pack light. Take nothing you aren't prepared to have mean nothing to you later."

Nobody laughed at that. It wasn't a joke. Or it was the worst kind, the kind that's also true.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I went down to the counting room and did what I always do when the world gets too large: I catalogued. Shelf by shelf, bin by bin. Fourteen borrowed years, mostly partial, mostly autumn. Sixty-one unspoken names in wax, cool to the touch. Two ounces of somebody's grief, improperly sealed, leaking regret into the drawer.

Wren found me there past midnight. He didn't say anything at first, just climbed onto the stool across the bench from me and watched my pen move.

"You'll forget me," he said finally.

"Probably," I said. "Unless the ledger's feeling generous, and I've read its terms, and it is not."

"I won't forget you."

"You don't know that. When Aldric's whole again, your part goes back in the—"

"I won't forget you," he said again, with the flat absolute certainty of a boy who is also a king's cast-off soul and has seen the inside of centuries. "Some things don't go back in the book. They get written in deeper."

I let that sit. Then I slid a blank ledger across the bench to him and dipped a second pen.

"Well then," I said. "Help me inventory. Start with the pears. Kings remember strange things, and I want the pear in the file before we march, in case the new me needs a laugh."

He smiled through it and took the pen, and we worked until the candle guttered—two keepers, one full ledger, one nearly empty, the rain easing off toward a dawn we were both, in our different ways, trying not to count.

Three days later we would take the south road to the capital: a guildmaster, seven aging conspirators, a boy who dreamed a kingdom's memories, and me, carrying everything I loved in the only vault that couldn't be robbed.

But that night, all I knew was this: the ink was still wet, the boy was humming in pre-Founding, and for the first time in my life I had volunteered for something that mattered.

I just wished somebody would tell me why none of the others would look me in the eye.
