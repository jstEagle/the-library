## 8. Teaching the Child to Be a Boy

The first thing you should know about traveling with a fragment of a king is that he does not know how to chew.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that on the second morning out of the guildhouse, at a roadside inn where the porridge was gray but honest, Wren held a spoon the way you'd hold a knife you'd found in a body, stared at the bowl as though it had insulted him, and then attempted to drink it whole. He choked. I thumped his back. He looked up at me with enormous offended eyes and said, in that language older than kingdoms, something I have chosen to translate as *this vessel has betrayed me.*

"You have to chew," I said.

"I know what chewing *is*," he said, in trade-tongue now, which he'd begun picking up with unsettling speed. "I have watched ten thousand meals. I watched your King Aldric eat pheasant every feast-day for nineteen years. I simply have never done it."

"Well." I pushed the bowl back toward him. "Watching isn't living. That's rather the whole trouble, isn't it?"

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he took up the spoon again and chewed, deliberately, ostentatiously, like a scholar performing an experiment, and pronounced porridge "an indignity that kings endure for appearances."

"Good," I said. "That's your first joke. You'll want to ration them."

* * *

We were four days out from the guildhouse, walking the long pilgrim road east toward the capital, disguised — if you could call it disguise — as an apprentice clerk escorting a foundling to be registered with the city orphan rolls. Sable Vane had forged the papers herself, which meant they were better than real ones; she'd once told me that a forgery done properly outlives the original document's memory of itself. The guild's remaining members had scattered ahead of us by separate roads, carrying what inventory couldn't be left behind: three vials of borrowed courage, a jar of unspoken names gone cloudy with age, and the master ledger, which rode against my chest in a sling like a colicky infant.

Wren walked beside me in a coat two sizes too big, because children grow and fragments of kings apparently do not think about such things when manifesting themselves in the world. His hair was the color of wet slate. When he slept, he recited.

That was the part nobody had warned me about.

* * *

The first night, I woke because the fire had guttered and the cold had teeth. Wren lay curled in his blanket, small and ordinary-looking, and from his mouth came a voice that was not his — deeper, wearier, a man's voice worn smooth by decades of saying things he didn't mean.

"—the levy on salt will stand, though it breaks the eastern towns, because the treasury must not show weakness before the autumn court—"

I sat very still.

"—tell the Queen Mother I regret her illness and cannot visit. Tell her I said it with warmth."

The voice drifted, changed register, became younger: "Father, why does the crown weigh more in winter? Father? Father—" And then it was a child's voice entirely, Wren's own, thin and frightened, saying words in the pre-Founding tongue that I did not understand but that made my chest ache anyway, the way certain griefs do when they belong to someone else.

I fed the fire and sat watch until dawn, listening to a king's scattered life pour out of a boy who was one-eighth of him, maybe less, maybe only the part that remembered how to be afraid.

In the morning I asked him what he dreamed.

"Nothing," he said, around a mouthful of bread he was chewing with tremendous concentration. "Why?"

"No reason. Chew on the left side too."

* * *

Here is what I learned about teaching a child to be a boy: it is mostly teaching him to be bad at things.

Kings — or the idea of kings, which is all Wren had ever been — are not permitted incompetence. A king's errors are policy; a king's silences are strategy. But a boy is allowed to spill things. A boy is allowed to laugh at the wrong moment and sing badly and lose at cards and lie about it transparently. The whole architecture of boyhood is built on glorious, survivable failure, and Wren had never been granted a single load-bearing mistake.

So we practiced.

At the inn in Marrow's Ford, I taught him dice. He calculated odds instantly and won eleven coppers off a drover before I kicked him under the table. "You have to *lose sometimes*," I hissed afterward. "Losing is how people trust you."

"That is the most corrupt thing anyone has ever taught me."

"Yes. Isn't it wonderful? Now lose the next three throws and look disappointed about it."

His disappointed face was a masterpiece of theater — too much lower lip, eyes narrowed like a tax assessor — and the drover laughed and clapped him on the shoulder and bought him a cider, and Wren sat there afterward holding the cider with both hands as though it might hatch.

"What?" I asked.

"The drover touched my head," Wren said. "Kings are not touched. Kings touch."

"And how did it feel?"

He thought about it honestly, which was the most kingly thing about him. "Like being a place where weather happens," he said. "Warm weather."

I had to look at the fire for a while after that.

* * *

Jokes took longer. His instinct was satire, which is a king's weapon, honed for court — dry, precise, aimed at power. It cleared rooms. I had to teach him the low arts: puns, exaggeration, the joke whose entire purpose is to make one specific person snort into their soup.

"Why," he demanded, "would anyone tell a joke that is not *good*?"

"Because the person you love laughs at bad jokes harder than good ones. A bad joke says, I am not trying to impress you. A bad joke says, I would rather be foolish with you than clever alone."

He turned this over for a full mile of road. Then he said, "Tell me a bad joke, then. So I can learn the shape of it."

I told him the one about the ferryman who charges ghosts double because they never tip. He didn't laugh. He filed it away somewhere behind his eyes, and three days later, at a crossing where the ferryman was indeed a ghost and did indeed try to charge us double, Wren looked at me sideways and said, "We should tip him. On principle."

It wasn't funny. I laughed until my ribs hurt, partly at the joke and partly at the sight of his face — the careful, hopeful tilt of it, a boy checking whether he'd done the thing right — and he laughed too, uncertainly at first and then helplessly, both of us wheezing on the riverbank while a dead man poling a flatboat watched us with the weary contempt of the eternally underpaid.

"There," I gasped. "That. Do more of that."

"Laughing?"

"Loving things badly."

* * *

Lying was the hardest lesson, and the strangest, because you would think a fragment cast out of a usurped king would take to falsehood naturally. But Wren lied the way the crown-thing governed: totally, structurally, without seam. Ask him his name and he said "Wren" without a flicker, because it was true and also armor. Ask him anything else and he either told the truth with devastating precision or went silent as a locked room. There was no middle country in him, no artful fog.

"A lie," I explained, "is a small kindness you pay for later. You say *I'm fine* when you're not, so the person asking can sleep tonight. You say *the soup was lovely* so the cook keeps cooking. Every lie is a debt. Good liars keep their accounts current."

"That sounds like guild doctrine."

"It is. Everything is guild doctrine if you squint."

He practiced on me. *Noor, your hat looks fine.* (It did not.) *Noor, I slept soundly.* (He had recited the founding of the harbor fleet between midnight and two.) *Noor, I am not afraid of the capital.* 

That last one he delivered perfectly, seamlessly, and then ruined completely by adding, "Was that a good lie? I kept the account current. You needed to hear it. I will pay the debt later, when I am afraid and can admit it."

I stopped on the road. Somewhere behind us a lark was making an idiot of itself in the hedgerow, and the capital lay three days east, a smudge of smoke and ambition on the horizon, and this impossible child stood looking up at me waiting to be graded on his homework.

"That one," I said, when I could speak steadily, "you should retire. Save it for someone who needs it more than I do."

* * *

But here is the ledger-entry I have not yet written, the one that sits in me like a stone in a shoe:

Every night he recited, and every night I listened, and I began — without meaning to, without any professional justification whatsoever — to know Aldric. Not the king on the coinage. The man. The boy who'd asked his father why the crown weighed more in winter. The young prince who'd once smuggled bread to a kitchen girl fined for burning tarts, and been caught, and taken the fine himself rather than let her be whipped. The man who'd wept at his sister's wedding and blamed the wine. The man who, in the last clear year before the crown-thing settled into him like frost into mortar, had stood at a window and said to no one, to the glass, to himself: *if I forget everything else, let me remember the smell of the orchard at Harrowmas.*

I knew the smell of the orchard at Harrowmas. I had never been there. I knew it through a sleeping boy's borrowed mouth, green apples and woodsmoke and cold coming down off the hills, and I loved it, the way you love a country you've only seen in someone else's remembering.

And every night I loved it a little more, I understood what I had volunteered for in that drafty guildhall when I raised my hand before Sable finished quoting the price. One keeper's every memory of love. I had thought — I had *told myself* — that I understood. Memories of the guild. Of Sable's dry voice, of old Corvin's cough, of the ledger-room's candle-smell. A price paid in institutional affection. Manageable. Bookkeepable.

But love is not an institution. Love is a boy learning to lose at dice. Love is the smell of an orchard you've never visited. Love is porridge betrayed, a drover's warm hand, a bad joke landing on a riverbank. The ledger doesn't care where the memories come from. It weighs them, and takes them, and leaves you standing in your own life like a house after a flood — walls standing, everything soft gone.

I caught myself, on the fifth evening, memorizing him. The exact pitch of his laugh. The way he held the spoon now, correctly, carelessly, like a boy instead of a coronation. The slate-colored cowlick that refused all governance. I was doing inventory. I knew exactly what I was doing, because inventory is the one thing I have always been able to do, and some traitor corner of my heart had opened a fresh page and started writing: *one child, condition used but sound, provenance royal, value —*

Value incalculable. Value beyond recovery. Value that will be collected.

I closed the page. I burned no candles over it. I just sat there in the inn's common room while Wren cheated badly at solitaire across the table, letting myself want what I was going to pay for, because refusing to want it in advance seemed like the one theft even I couldn't square in the books.

* * *

On the sixth night, the recitations changed.

I woke to the deep voice again, but wrong — frayed, urgent, a man talking fast because he could feel himself being edited in real time.

"—whoever finds this, whoever hears it, the crown is not mine, the crown was never — it speaks through my mouth and I watch from inside like a guest at my own — the orchard, remember the orchard, if any of me survives, tell him the orchard at Harrowmas, tell him he was loved before he was a king, tell him—"

The voice broke apart into static, into the pre-Founding tongue, into Wren's own voice crying out — and then silence, and then, very quietly, awake-voiced, in the dark:

"Noor. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"He's still in there." Wren sat up. His eyes were wet and furious. "The rest of him. The crown-thing didn't eat him, it *shelved* him, it keeps him the way your guild keeps griefs — labeled, dusted, intact enough to use. I heard him tonight. He's been leaving messages in me. In the dreams. He's been leaving messages for *years*, and no one came."

"We came," I said. "We're slow, but we came."

He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, a gesture I had absolutely taught him, and something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there.

"Noor," he said. "When we do this. When you give the ledger whatever it asks. Will it hurt you?"

And because I am, by his own assessment, the finest liar in the guild:

"No," I said. "Now go back to sleep. We reach the capital tomorrow, and you'll want to be rested for lying to a palace."

He believed me. That was the worst of it. He believed me completely — and as he drifted off, his breathing evening out into something almost like peace, I sat watching the fire and understood that I had finally learned to lie like a living boy.

The lesson had cost more than I'd quoted.

Eastward, past the dark fields, the capital's watch-fires had multiplied overnight — more of them than a peaceful city needs, arranged in rings, like a throat preparing to swallow.
