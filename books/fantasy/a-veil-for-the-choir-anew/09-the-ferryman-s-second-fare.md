## 9. The Ferryman's Second Fare

I have drowned twice in my life, and both times the river was the Vell, and both times I came back up owing it something.

The first drowning was an accident of youth, a dare gone wrong at the ford below Harrow's Bend. The second was a bargain, struck forty years ago when I paid the ferryman to let me walk across his water without wetting my boots, and he took as his fare the memory of my mother's face. I have regretted that payment every day since, which is to say I have regretted it without knowing precisely what I was missing—a grief shaped like a hole, aching in the outline of something no longer there.

So understand me when I say: I know this river. I know its moods, its fords, its lies. And on the third night of our flight from the guildhouse, standing on its southern bank with a sleeping boy against Noor Kavanagh's shoulder and the mist coming off the water like breath off a blade, I knew we were being watched before I saw a single watcher.

"You feel it too," Noor said. They had not slept properly in three days; their voice had the frayed quality of a rope near its breaking strain. Wren's head lolled against them, mud-streaked and hollow-eyed even in sleep, his lips moving through some dream in the old tongue—the king's memories leaking out of him like light through a cracked jar.

"I feel it," I said. "Don't cross yet."

The ferry was there. That was the first wrongness. The Vell at this crossing—Caul's Ford, the maps call it, though no map worth ink has marked it in a century—was served by a ferryman who came only when called, and only for those who knew the call, and the call was a guild secret older than my grandmother's grandmother. We had not called. And still the flat black boat sat at its mooring, lamp lit, as patient as a debt.

"Someone summoned it," Noor said.

"Someone did." I looked at the lamp. Its flame burned without flickering, which is not how fire behaves on open water, and not how fire behaves at all when the thing burning it has been dead for sixty years. "And I think I know who."

* * *

His name had been Oskar Vey, once. He had been a keeper of the second rank, a man who could hold a lie so steadily in his mouth that it ripened into truth if you left it there long enough. He had been my friend, in the way that guild members are friends—which is to say we had traded memories of each other so often that neither of us could say anymore what we actually remembered versus what we had purchased—and then he had defected, thirty-one years ago, sold what he knew to the palace, and walked into the capital wearing a new name and a pension.

We had crossed him out of the ledger. We had taken back everything he'd bought from us over the years: his sister's forgiveness, his courage at funerals, his ability to weep. It should have hollowed him. Instead he had thrived, because Oskar Vey had discovered early what I have known all my life and hated knowing: that a man can be built entirely out of other people's castoffs and stand up straight anyway.

He died eleven years ago. I attended the funeral by proxy—one of our agents in the capital sent back a memory of the service, and I keep it in a locked drawer because I cannot decide whether watching your enemy buried counts as pleasure or loss.

And yet here was his summons on the ferry-lamp, written in the old hand-code we had shared as apprentices: *Sable. Come alone. I have something of yours.*

"He's dead," Noor said, when I told them. They shifted Wren's weight and frowned. "Dead men don't send messages."

"Dead men are the most reliable correspondents in the world," I said. "They never change their minds." I looked out at the boat, at the lamp with its unblinking flame. "But they can be made to carry them. The usurper's people have wrights who can stitch a ghost to its habits. Oskar loved this crossing. If anyone's ghost would be set to wait at a mooring with a lamp lit, it would be his."

"What do we do?"

What we did, in the end, was what the guild has always done when cornered between two prices: we looked for the third option, and it cost more than either.

* * *

I went down to the bank alone, because the message asked it and because refusing the shape of a trap is sometimes more dangerous than walking into it. Noor stayed back among the reeds with Wren, and I felt their eyes on my spine the whole way down, young and fierce and frightened for me in a way that I found—I will admit it here, in whatever ledger records these things—deeply inconvenient. One does not want to be someone's reason for fear. Fear is a currency, and I had spent my whole life making sure I was never spent.

The ghost stood at the bow when I stepped aboard. It wore Oskar's face the way a glove wears a hand: correctly, but without warmth. Sixty years of habit had stiffened around him like ice. His lamp hung from the pole above him, and in its light I could see the stitching—the fine silver threads running up through his jaw, behind his eyes, anchored somewhere in the air itself, in whatever far room held the wright who had made him into a messenger.

"Sable Vane," the ghost said. My name in Oskar's voice. He had always pronounced the V too carefully, as if apologizing to it.

"Oskar." I kept my hands visible and empty. "You look well. Death agrees with you—it's the first honest thing you've ever worn."

The ghost's mouth moved into something adjacent to a smile. "Still sharpening words on stone, I see. Some things don't wear out." He gestured with the pole, and the boat slid from the bank without a ripple. "I have a message. You'll want to hear it before you decide anything."

"I rarely decide anything before hearing a price. It's the deciding after that ruins people."

"Then hear the offer." The ghost leaned forward, and the silver threads sang faintly, a sound like a wet finger on glass. "The Regent knows you've taken the boy. He knows where you were three nights ago. He knows you're short-handed, half-starved, and carrying the single most dangerous object in seven kingdoms asleep on a child's shoulders." A pause. "He also knows what you lost, Sable. All of you. The veil is thinning. Your invisibility is failing—you've felt it, haven't you? Doors opening that used to stay shut. Eyes finding you in crowds."

I had felt it. For years now, small failures: a landlord squinting at a guild safehouse as if seeing it for the first time; a tax clerk asking one question too many about our front businesses. I had told myself it was age, decay, the ordinary rot of institutions. Hearing it named aloud by a dead traitor's mouth was different. Hearing it named meant the enemy had measured it.

"The Regent offers restoration," the ghost said. "Full renewal of the founding bargain. The veil rewoven, stronger than before. The guild goes back to being nothing, unseen, untouchable—safe for another three hundred years. In exchange—" and here the ghost's borrowed voice softened into something almost gentle, almost Oskar, almost the friend I had drunk bad wine with while we argued about whether names could be stolen or only lent, "—in exchange, you leave the boy at the next crossing. Walk away. Let the river take him where rivers take things that are owed."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the veil fails entirely. Within the season. Every name in your ledgers becomes readable. Every safehouse becomes findable. Every member becomes a line in someone else's book." The ghost tilted its head. "You've read the histories, Sable. You know what happened to the guilds that were found."

I did know. There is a reason the Ledgered Veil has no graveyard. Guilds that are found do not die; they are *unwritten*—and unwritten things leave holes in the world that ache, that draw the eye, that make dogs bark at empty corners for generations.

The mist closed around us. Midstream now. The water beneath the hull was black and slow and full of the reflections of stars that were not in the sky above us—the Vell has always kept a second sky down there, and I have never trusted it.

"May I ask you something, Oskar?" I said. "Not part of the negotiation. Just curiosity between old colleagues."

"If you like."

"When they stitched you—did it hurt?"

For a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—the careful pronunciation dropped away, and the thing wearing my dead friend's face looked at me with an expression I recognized from thirty-one years ago, from the last night I ever saw him alive, when he stood in the guildhouse doorway with his coat already on and said, *It's not betrayal if they never valued you rightly.* It was the expression of a man who has discovered the exact weight of his own soul and found it wanting.

"Yes," the ghost said. "It hurts. It hurts all the time. They tell you ghosts don't feel, but the stitching pulls every time I remember something they didn't put there." His hand rose halfway toward his own throat and stopped. "Sable. Take the offer. Not for them—for yourself. You're seventy-one years old. You've given them everything. What's left that they haven't already taken?"

And there it was. The second fare. Not the Regent's offer—that was merely coin—but this: the true temptation, arriving as temptation always arrives, dressed not in threat but in sympathy. *Rest,* it said. *You have earned rest. Let the young ones carry it.*

I thought about my mother's face. Forty years I had carried the hole where it should be, and I had built my life around the hole the way a tree builds around a nail, and I had told myself the building was the living. And I thought about Noor in the reeds, holding a child who was a king's discarded wholeness, keeping watch with those tired, stubborn, invisible eyes. And I thought about what I would say to the guild's founders, if the founders could be said to exist anywhere anymore except as entries in books, if they asked me: *What did you preserve?*

Everything, I would say. We preserved everything. That was the whole of the work.

"No," I said.

The ghost sighed. "I hoped you'd be wiser. You were always the wisest of us. That was the tragedy of you, Sable—you were wise enough to see the cage and too loyal to leave it."

"It wasn't loyalty." I reached up, slowly, and touched the lamp. The flame bent around my fingers without heat. "It was arithmetic. The Regent offers three hundred years of safety bought with one child. But I've read the ledgers, Oskar. I know what the veil was woven from. I know whose memory holds it together. And I know that the moment the crown-thing finishes digesting Aldric, it won't need bargains anymore—it'll take veils the way it takes kingdoms. Your masters aren't offering to renew our invisibility. They're offering to collect it early." I leaned close, close enough to see the silver threads trembling. "Tell your wright the guild declines. Tell them the debt runs the other way now."

The threads snapped taut. The gentleness went out of the ghost's face all at once, like a lamp snuffed, and what remained was only mechanism and distance—a puppet remembering it was a puppet.

"Then the Regent sends his regards," it said, "and asks me to inform you that the crossing is now closed to you. Permanently. And that the boy will be collected regardless. The river keeps accounts too, Sable. Everyone does, in the end."

"Yes," I agreed. "Everyone does."

And I killed him.

Understand: it was mercy and tactics in equal measure, and I decline to pretend otherwise. A ghost stitched to a wright's will is a listening ear and a speaking mouth, and so long as Oskar Vey's remnant floated on this water, everything said or done at Caul's Ford belonged to the palace. The crossing had to become secret again, and secrets are made of silence, and silence is made the hard way.

I did it with the lamp. I pulled the flame out of the glass—flames are only memories of fire, and memories can be taken—and I pressed it into the ghost's chest, into the knot where all the silver threads gathered, and I spoke the unbinding. Three words in the pre-Founding speech, taught to me fifty years ago by a woman whose name I traded away long before I understood what I'd given up. The words mean, roughly, *the account is settled.*

The threads burned. The ghost did not scream, which I am grateful for; it made a sound like a page turning, like a ledger closing, like a long-held breath finally let go. And for one instant before it came apart, the stitching fell away and what looked out at me through Oskar's face was not a puppet and not quite a man—just the remnant, the true remainder, the bit of him that had waited sixty years under all the purchases and sales. It looked at me with something that might have been thanks.

Then it was ash on the water, and the lamp went dark, and the boat drifted to the southern bank and grounded itself like a thing that had finally been permitted to stop working.

* * *

Noor met me at the reed-line with Wren awake now and clinging to their hand, his eyes enormous in the dark. They had heard none of it—the mist carries sound strangely on the Vell—but they read my face the way guild members learn to read faces, entry by entry.

"We can't use the ferry," I said. "We can't use any of the old ways. Every road the guild owns is a road the Regent has measured. From here on, we travel like ordinary people—badly, slowly, paying full price for everything."

"That's suicide," Noor said flatly. "We'll be seen."

"Yes," I said. "We will." I looked down at Wren, who was staring past me at the dark river, at the place where the ash had gone. His lips were moving. I caught only a fragment of it, in that old tongue that predates kingdoms, but I have spent my life learning to translate impossible things, and I translated this one despite myself.

*The ferryman is free now,* the boy said to the water. *Thank you.*

And the river, which keeps a second sky beneath its skin, showed us for a moment a reflection of a lamp relighting—somewhere far downstream, somewhere no boat was—before it went out.

Noor shivered. "What did he say?"

I started to answer, and stopped. Because downstream, on the far bank, a rider had appeared out of the mist—not moving, just sitting his horse, facing us—and beside him another, and beyond them the faint orange stain of torches multiplying in the fog like a fever coming up.

"They've closed the crossing," I said instead, and reached for the children, and turned us all toward the hills. "Run."
