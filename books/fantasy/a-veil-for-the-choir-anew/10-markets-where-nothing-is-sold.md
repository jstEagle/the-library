## 10. Markets Where Nothing Is Sold

The city of Vellamere had a rule older than its walls: nothing here may be owned.

You could rent a room, lease a horse, borrow a name for an afternoon. You could hold a thing in trust, in escrow, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. But the moment you tried to *own* something—outright, forever, no takebacks—the city's magistrates would appear from whatever shadows magistrates keep when they aren't being smug, and they would explain, gently and at length, that you had misunderstood where you were.

I used to think it was an affectation. A merchant city dressing up greed in philosophy, the way rich men dress up cruelty in tailoring.

Then I spent three days in its markets, trading favors with people who dealt in impossible things, and I understood: Vellamere wasn't pretending. It was *quarantining*. The whole city was built like a lockbox around a truth nobody wanted to say out loud—that some things rot faster when you grip them too hard, and the guilds of the world had been gripping for centuries.

Wren walked beside me through the Saltglass Market with his hood up and his hand in mine, which was half protection and half because he kept trying to buy things.

"That man is selling weather," he said, pointing.

"He's selling *memories* of weather," I said. "There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"About four coppers, usually."

He laughed—the bright, unguarded laugh I'd spent two weeks on the road teaching him, one stolen joke at a time—and something in my chest did the thing it had been doing more and more lately, which was ache in a way I couldn't file anywhere. Ledgers have columns for everything except the entries you don't want to read twice.

Sable Vane walked ahead of us, gray as her name, counting doorways. She'd been counting since we entered the city. In Vellamere, she'd told us on the road, the hidden guilds didn't hide behind veils and wards the way ours did. They hid behind *ordinariness*. A debt-broker's office above a bakery. A memory-lender working out of a bathhouse. The Guild of the Ledgered Veil had spent so long being invisible that we'd forgotten there were other ways to be unseen.

"Third archway past the fountain," Sable said, without turning around. "Don't look at it directly. Look at what it's *next to*."

I looked at what it was next to—a fruit stall, a beggar with a very professional-looking limp, a shrine to some god of small mercies—and only then did the archway resolve itself: a doorway of black wood, unmarked, breathing faintly, the way old doors do when they've been listening a long time.

Above it, carved so shallowly you'd swear it wasn't there at all: a set of scales, and beneath the scales, a single word in pre-Founding script.

I felt Wren go still beside me.

"I can read that," he whispered.

"Don't," said Sable. "Not yet. Not here." She put her hand on the boy's shoulder, and I saw her fingers tremble slightly before they steadied. "In this city, names are currency, and you, little king, are a mint."

* * *

The guild behind the black door called itself the Concordance of Held Things, which sounded respectable until you learned what they held: promises too heavy for their makers to carry. Oaths that had outlived their contexts. Vows made by dying men to gods who'd stopped listening. They stored them the way banks store gold—in vaults, under wards, accruing interest.

Their factor was a woman named Odile Marchetti, who received us in a room with no corners and served tea that tasted like whichever childhood drink you missed most. Mine tasted like the barley water the guild kitchens made on feast days, and I had to set the cup down before it could do any more archaeology.

"You're Vane's people," Odile said. "The Ledgered Veil. We thought you were a story."

"We get that a lot," I said. "It's sort of our business model."

"It was," Sable corrected quietly. "Past tense. Our veil is failing."

Odile's eyebrows performed a small, elegant catastrophe. "Failing? The Aldric Veil?" She laughed, but it was the wrong kind of laugh, the kind that's holding a door shut against something. "Guildmistress, do you know how many guilds are standing inside your shadow? Your bargain bought invisibility for *all* of us. If your veil goes down—"

"Then everyone gets to see us," Sable said. "All of us. Everything we've done. Every favor traded, every debt held, every impossible thing moved through the dark." She folded her hands. "So before you ask me what I want, ask yourself what happens to the Concordance of Held Things when the world remembers that oaths can be *stored*. When kings learn that their grandfathers' promises are sitting in a vault in Vellamere, gathering interest against their heirs."

The silence went on long enough that I heard the tea cooling.

"What do you want?" Odile asked finally.

"Information," said Sable. "And passage. And for you to tell me about the other six."

I watched Odile Marchetti, who had not blinked once during the entire conversation, blink seven times in rapid succession.

"The other six what?"

Sable just looked at her.

"You know," Odile said, "for a guild that's supposed to be a story, you're remarkably well informed."

"We keep accounts," Sable said. "It's what we're for."

* * *

Here is what I knew going into that room, and what I'd told no one—not Sable, not Wren, certainly not myself out loud:

For weeks, ever since the child wrote my secret name in the dust outside our door, I'd been reading the oldest ledgers. The ones in the sealed case, the ones written in hands that predated our founding charter. And in the margins of those ledgers, in a cipher I'd finally cracked somewhere on the third night of the road, while Wren slept against my shoulder reciting a dead king's memories—I'd found references. Cross-references. Entries marked not with our guild's seal but with six others.

Seven seals total. Seven marks. Seven *veils*, if the word in the margin meant what I thought it meant.

I hadn't told Sable because I wanted to be sure. I hadn't told Wren because he was eight years old and already carrying a kingdom in his sleep. And I hadn't told myself because the alternative explanation—that the guilds of the world had done something together, something enormous and terrible, and then each gone home and agreed to forget—was a load-bearing wall in my understanding of my own life, and I wasn't ready to find out what the house looked like without it.

Odile poured herself more tea. Hers smelled like nothing at all, which told me something about her childhood I decided not to pursue.

"There were seven founding bargains," she said. "Everyone in the trade knows the shape of it, even if nobody says it plainly. Seven guilds, seven veils, seven... holdings. Each guild took custody of one piece of something that couldn't be allowed to exist whole." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Your guild took the king's memory. That's the famous one—or it would be, if anyone remembered it, which was rather the point."

"And the others?" I asked.

Odile looked at me for the first time. Really looked, the way appraisers look, weighing and pricing.

"You're the keeper," she said. "The one who reads what others can't. Yes. I'd heard there was one." She sighed, and for a moment the broker's polish dropped and underneath was just a tired woman in a cornerless room. "The Guild of the Ninefold Key holds a king's *name*. The Tallow Order holds his reflection—they keep it in a well, don't ask. The Cartographers of the Unwritten Road hold his future, or a version of it. The Sisters of the Quiet Hour hold his death, scheduled and shelved like a returned book. The Meridian Table holds his—" she paused, choosing, "—his *want*. His appetite. What he desired, removed and preserved, so that the remainder would be governable."

My mouth had gone dry. "And the seventh?"

"The seventh," Odile said, "holds his shadow. Or held." She set down the cup. "The seventh guild is gone, keeper. Burned, scattered, or eaten—we don't know. But about forty years ago, the holdings started to *drift*. Pieces migrating. A king's want turning up where his reflection should be. His death slipping loose of its shelf. And in every case, the drift pointed the same direction. Toward one throne. Toward one crown."

"Toward Aldric," I said.

"Toward whatever is sitting on Aldric's throne wearing his face," Odile said. "Because here is the thing nobody wanted to know, keeper, the thing we all agreed not to know, because knowing it would mean the whole system was rotten from the foundation stones up." She leaned forward. "The seven pieces were never meant to stay separate. He was never meant to be *dismembered* that way—memory, name, reflection, future, death, want, shadow. Seven guilds, seven cuts, and every one of us told ourselves we were keeping the world safe from a tyrant. But a man cut into seven pieces doesn't stop existing. He starts *looking for himself*. And something found him first. Something crawled into the hollow where a king used to be and pulled the crown down over its own eyes, and it has been wearing him ever since, and every guild that ever held a piece of that man has been feeding it without meaning to. Because the pieces *want* to go home. And the crown-thing knows how to ask."

The room's lack of corners suddenly felt less like architecture and more like denial.

"How long has it been wearing him?" I asked.

"Forty years," said Odile. "Give or take the drift."

Forty years. Forty years of decrees signed by something cold. Forty years of a kingdom slowly being bent around a hollow center, and armies now marching on the free cities because a hollow thing wants what hollow things want—to be filled, at any cost, filled with anything, filled with *everything*.

Beside me, Wren had gone very quiet. He was staring at his hands.

"That's why I'm broken into pieces," he said. Not a question. The pre-Founding words came out of him soft and level, and then translated themselves into boy-speech. "That's why I remember things that haven't happened to me. I'm not a ghost. I'm a *piece*."

"Wren—"

"Six other children," he said. "Or six other somethings. Aren't there? Somewhere. A piece that's a name. A piece that's a shadow." He looked up at me, and his eyes were too old, the way they got sometimes, and I hated it every time. "Is anyone else looking for them?"

Nobody answered him. In the trade, I was learning, silence was the most honest currency there was.

* * *

We left the Concordance with what we'd come for: passage east through the neutral quarter, a letter of credit good at three other hidden doors, and a promise from Odile that she would send word to the surviving five guilds that the Aldric Veil was failing—which, translated from broker-speak, meant she would warn them, and translated further, meant she was afraid of what we'd started.

Outside, the Saltglass Market was closing for the evening. The weather-seller was packing away his jars of remembered rain. The beggar with the professional limp was walking home with a perfectly symmetrical gait, off duty. Somewhere a bell rang the hour in a language of pure pitch, no numbers, because Vellamere didn't believe in owning time either.

Wren walked between us, subdued, chewing on the questions he was too young to chew safely.

"Noor," he said finally. "If the other pieces are children like me. And if someone finds them first—the crown-thing, or the wardens, or anyone—" He worked at it. "Do the pieces get to say no?"

I opened my mouth to give him the kind of answer adults give children, the kind that's mostly padding over a hole.

What came out instead was the truth, because apparently this boy had ruined me for anything else.

"I don't know," I said. "But I know someone who might. Someone who keeps records of everything the guilds ever did—who they cut, what they took, where they shelved it." I glanced at Sable's gray back, marching ahead of us toward the inn. "Our oldest ledger isn't just an account of debts, Wren. I think it's a map. Of all seven pieces. Including the ones that went missing."

"Then we read it," Wren said simply, the way children decide enormous things. "Tonight."

"Yes," I said. "Tonight."

And then, because the market was emptying and the light was going amber and he was, for one more evening, just a boy walking home:

"Also, tomorrow I'm teaching you to haggle. You paid that weather-man full price for a jar of rain that never actually fell."

"It *felt* like rain," Wren protested.

"It was somebody's *memory* of rain. Probably a fisherman's. You bought a stranger's nostalgia for four coppers."

"...Can I get my money back?"

"No. Consider it tuition."

He laughed again, and I filed the sound away in whatever column holds the things you refuse to price because pricing them would mean admitting they can be taken.

Behind us, unnoticed by either of us, a figure detached itself from the doorway of the fruit stall and followed—wearing the livery of no guild at all, carrying a message sealed in wax the color of dried blood, addressed to the capital, concerning a child seen in the company of the Ledgered Veil.

But that, as they say, is another ledger's entry.
