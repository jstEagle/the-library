## 11. Ede's Impossible Bargain

I have counted men before. It is a captain's oldest arithmetic: theirs against ours, and then the honest sum underneath, which is how many of ours will still be standing when the counting stops.

From the ridge above Harrow Fen I did the sums again, because I kept hoping the numbers would feel ashamed of themselves and change.

Nine thousand, give or take the dust. That was the usurper's host on the salt road, banners of white ash on black, siege engines crawling like beetles too big for the world. Against them: four hundred militia of the free cities, which is a polite way of saying farmers with spears, dockhands with crossbows, and a smith named Olla who had never held anything sharper than a horseshoe until three weeks ago.

"Captain," said Bren, my second, who had the gift of stating the obvious in a voice like wet gravel. "We're going to die."

"Not yet," I said. "First we're going to lose."

He looked at me. I let him look. There are moments when a commander has to let her people see the shape of the truth, because if you dress it up too fine they'll spend the battle looking for the ribbons instead of fighting.

"We fall back to the fen," I said. "We make them come to us in ground where nine thousand counts for less. And before that—" I turned south, toward the reed-beds and the old drover's track nobody used since the toll wars, "—before that, I'm going to buy something."

"With what?" Bren asked. "The cities' treasury sent us prayers and pickled herring."

"With something better than coin." I swung down from the ridge. "Come on. And Bren—if I come back from this different, don't pretend you didn't notice."

* * *

The guild house at Marrow Cross was not a guild house. It was a tannery that had never once, in living memory, produced leather, run by a woman named Mistress Corvane who smelled of vinegar and had eyes the color of a ledger's margins. The free cities' militia had dealt with her kind twice in my lifetime—once for weather, once for plague—and both times the price had been strange and the goods had been stranger, and both times the accounts had balanced exactly, which frightened me more than either.

She received me in a back room where the only furniture was a table, a chair, and a set of scales large enough to weigh a dog.

"Captain Rusk." She didn't ask how she knew my name. People like her never do; it's part of the merchandise. "You want courage."

"I want one hour," I said. "One hour in which four hundred ordinary people fight like the last four hundred people alive, because for one hour they will believe it, and belief is the only reinforcement I'm getting."

"One hour of perfect courage." She wrote nothing down, which somehow meant she was writing everything down. "For four hundred. You understand what you're asking. Courage isn't a thing we keep in jars. We borrow it forward—from the person each fighter might have become if they'd ever been tested. It comes back changed."

"It doesn't need to come back at all," I said. "They won't survive to miss it."

Corvane studied me the way an assayer studies ore. "And your payment?"

"The treasury—"

"Will send prayers and pickled herring. Yes. The Ledger does not accept herring." She folded her hands. "We accept value. Memory is value. Grief is value. Years, names, the weight of a promise unkept. What do you have, Captain?"

I had prepared for this. I had ridden two days knowing this question was coming, and I had prepared an answer the way you prepare a speech for a funeral: carefully, and hoping it would never leave your mouth.

"My brother," I said.

* * *

His name was Tam. He was older than me by two years and smaller than me by half a head, which he resented with his whole heart, and he laughed like a door falling off its hinges. When our father drowned, Tam was the one who taught me to swim in the same river, out of spite, so the river wouldn't get to keep being right about us. When I enlisted, Tam marched me to the gate and told the recruiting sergeant I snored like a collapsing barn, and the sergeant took me anyway, and Tam stood at the fence every market day for a year until I made corporal and could walk out to meet him myself.

Tam died eight years ago. Fever, not glory. The kind of death that leaves no one to blame, which is the cruelest kind, because grief needs somewhere to put itself and there was nowhere, so it just lived in me, taking up a room.

I remembered everything. That was the point. Other people's dead fade—they blur at the edges, the voice goes first—but I had kept Tam sharp. Every year on the anniversary I would sit down and go through him like a drill sergeant going through kit inspection: the gap in his teeth, the way he said *Edekins* like an insult and a blessing at once, the joke about the miller's donkey that I cannot repeat here and have never once managed to think of without laughing. Eight years of maintenance. I had kept him the way you keep a blade: oiled, honed, ready.

And I had told myself, riding those two days, that offering him up was simple. That grief hoarded is grief wasted. That Tam would have laughed himself sick at the idea of being spent on four hundred strangers, and then he'd have done it, because that was Tam all over—generous past the point of sense.

Mistress Corvane listened to all of it. I gave her the details the way you pay out rope, hand over hand, and with each detail I felt the room tilt slightly, as though the scales were already weighing.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"You understand," she said, "that we do not take the grief. Anyone can take grief; grief is cheap, the world manufactures it by the ton. We take the *memory*. The whole of him. Not the fact that you had a brother—you'll keep the fact, the way you keep a scar's history without the pain. But the boy himself. The laugh. The name on your tongue. The joke about the donkey." She leaned forward. "You will know that someone mattered once, and you will not know who, and you will not know why your chest hurts on the anniversary of nothing. Is that a trade you can sign?"

I thought about saying yes quickly, the way you pull a bandage. Instead I sat with it, because I owed Tam that much—the sitting-with-it.

"Will it hurt the fighting?" I asked finally. "Me. Will I be less?"

"No," Corvane said. "That's the obscenity of it. You'll be exactly as good a captain. Perhaps better. Grief is heavy, Captain Rusk. Soldiers march faster without it." She said it without cruelty, which was worse. "Well?"

Outside, far off, I could smell smoke on the wind. Harrow Fen again, or the next town after it. The usurper's army did not wait for anyone's grief to be settled.

"Yes," I said. "One condition. The hour goes to all of them. Every man and woman on that line, even Olla, especially Olla. And one more hour besides—not courage. Just time. One hour where the light holds, where their arms don't tire, where the wall holds one hour longer than walls hold. I've heard your guild sells borrowed years. Sell me a borrowed hour."

Something moved behind Corvane's face—surprise, or respect, or the professional pleasure of an unusual transaction.

"That's not militia work," she said. "That's ledger work. Deep entries. I'd have to write to the mother house."

"Then write fast."

* * *

It took three days. Three days while I drilled the fen positions and watched the dust cloud on the salt road grow from smudge to storm, and every night I sat by the fire doing my inspection of Tam, top to bottom, because some stubborn sergeant-part of me wanted to be sure the kit was all accounted for before I handed it over. The gap in his teeth. *Edekins.* The donkey. The way he cried at our mother's funeral and pretended it was the wind. The last thing he ever said to me, which was *"Bring back something stupid from the capital,"* and I never went to the capital, and now I never would, and I found I could live with that if the four hundred did.

On the third night, Corvane came to the camp herself, which I gathered she never did, carrying a book bound in grey leather and a small brass instrument like a tuning fork crossed with a pair of scissors.

"The mother house agrees," she said. "Your hour of courage, and the hour of held time. Signed against your account." She opened the book. "There is a formality. You must speak the payment aloud. The ledger requires consent in the debtor's own voice. Say what you're giving."

I stood up straight, the way I'd stood at our father's grave, the way I stood when any hard thing needed doing, because posture is half of soldiering and most of grieving.

"I give the memory of my brother," I said. "All of it. His name was Tam."

Even as I said it, I felt the words begin to slip, like trying to grip a fish with wet hands—*his name was*—and I panicked, one hot instant, and grabbed: *Tam, Tam, Tam,* the way you'd grab a drowning man.

Corvane touched the fork to the page. Something sang, very high, below hearing.

"Now," she said gently. "Hold still. This part takes as long as it takes."

It did not hurt. That was the worst discovery. It felt like warm water, like setting down a pack after a long march, like sleep coming at last after a night watch. I felt pieces of myself go soft and lift away, and I knew—I *knew*, in the last seconds—that they were the best pieces, the load-bearing ones, and I tried to hold on and couldn't remember what I was holding.

Then it was done, and I was standing in a firelit camp with a hollow in my chest shaped like a person, and I could not tell you whose.

"Captain?" Bren was beside me. "You alright? You look like you've seen a ghost."

"No," I said, and my voice worked fine, which seemed like fraud. "No ghosts. Get the line formed. Dawn, the east causeway. Tonight we fight like the last four hundred people alive."

Bren stared. Around us, the camp was already changing—men and women standing straighter, checking straps with steady hands, an ease settling over them like armor fitting properly for the first time. Olla the smith was sharpening a billhook and humming.

"What happened to them?" Bren whispered.

"They were reinforced," I said.

* * *

We fought at dawn, and it was everything I had bought.

I will not dress it up. War is mud and screaming and the specific embarrassment of watching a boy try to hold his own arm on. But for one hour, my four hundred were magnificent. They held the causeway when the causeway should have folded. They rallied when rallied-from was a kindness. Olla killed six men with a billhook and a kind of terrible serenity, and when the hour of held time came—the light refusing to fail, our arms refusing to tire—we broke their first assault, and their second, and made the third so expensive that the white-ash banners pulled back to regroup, and nine thousand men learned to fear a fen.

Afterward I walked the line among the wounded and the dead, doing the captain's arithmetic again. Two hundred and eleven of mine still breathing. Better than any sum I'd dared. I passed the body of a young spearman with freckles and closed his eyes, and felt my chest ache in a way I couldn't source, an ache with no address, grief returned to sender unknown.

"Captain." A runner, mud to the eyebrows, holding out a scrap of paper. "Rider came through from the south road. Marked urgent. Guild seal."

I took it. The handwriting was cramped and hurried, a keeper's hand:

*To Capt. Rusk — The debt moves tonight. Whatever is to be done in the capital must be done before moonset. Hold your wall one night longer. Help is bought and paid. — V.*

I read it twice. Somewhere in my chest, the hollow shifted, the way a house shifts when someone walks through it.

"Bren," I said. "Get me every torch, every lantern, every bit of pitch we have. If they think we're ten thousand, they'll wait out the night. And wake the fastest rider we have left."

"Where's he going?"

"The capital," I said. "Tell whoever's listening there: whatever happens in the palace, it happens tonight—or there won't be a free city left to thank them."

The rider went out under a sky just beginning to bruise toward morning, and I turned back to my wall, my two hundred and eleven, my hour already spent, and waited for the dark to earn its keep.
