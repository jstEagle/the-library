## 13. Audience with a Hollow Man

There is a particular cruelty in being a king who cannot remember his own mornings. I do not mean the ordinary forgetting that comes with age or wine—I remember plenty of wine, more than my physicians would like, and every glass of it. I mean the other kind. The kind where you wake with ink on your fingers and a decree on your desk in your own hand, ordering the levying of troops from provinces whose names you have to look up, and the last thing you can recall with any certainty is breakfast three days ago.

My mornings have been stolen. I have decided this is the most accurate way to put it, though I have told no one, because a king who says *someone is stealing my time* is not a king for long. He is a patient. And I have met my physicians.

So instead I keep what I privately call the true ledger—a little book of cheap paper hidden behind a loose stone in my bedchamber, where I write down everything I can still hold onto. What I ate. Who spoke to me. Whether the sky was grey. Small proofs of ownership over my own life. Some nights I fill six pages. Some mornings I find entries I don't remember making, in handwriting that is mine but somehow *not* mine—the letters too neat, like a forger who studied me carefully and got everything right except the tiredness.

This morning there was such an entry. It said: *The boy arrives today.*

I did not know what boy.

* * *

Let me describe my court, since I am apparently its only honest witness. The throne room of Aldermere was built by men who believed that height was a moral argument. Its windows are tall enough to make petitioners feel like insects; its banners hang so far overhead they might be clouds. In summer the light falls through them in great golden columns, and everyone below pretends this is holiness rather than architecture.

I sat the throne—cold thing, iron under velvet, it never warms no matter how long you sit—and listened to the day's business. A grain dispute between two river towns. A petition about wolves. A delegation from the salt road merchants asking, very politely, why their tolls had doubled, and receiving from my chancellor an answer so smooth it could have greased an axle. Lord Casserly is good at answers. He has had practice answering for me.

That is the arrangement, as best I understand it. Casserly speaks when I am... elsewhere. He signs when my hand hesitates. He calls it efficiency, and the court calls him indispensable, and I call him nothing at all, because when I try to think clearly about Casserly—about his pale eyes, his habit of standing slightly behind my right shoulder, the way conversations bend around him like water around a post—my thoughts go soft and slide away, like trying to grip a fish with buttered hands.

Today he stood behind my right shoulder.

"The foundling singers," he murmured, "from the eastern villages. Displaced by the border actions." The border actions. That is what we call it now, the burning of towns that used to trade freely with the free cities. I signed something about the border actions last week. My hand knew the signature better than I knew the reason.

"Send them in," I said, because a king's options are usually limited to *yes*, *later*, and *send them in*.

They came in single file, these displaced children, clutching instruments too big for them—fiddles, a drum, one small harp with a broken string. They were meant to perform, I gathered. Music for the court while their villages smoldered somewhere east. There is a word for a kingdom that entertains itself with the orphans of its own wars, and I suspect the word is not flattering, and I am ashamed to say the thought arrived without any feeling attached to it, the way so many of my thoughts arrive now: correct, complete, and cold as the throne.

Then the last child came through the doors, and the cold went out of me all at once.

He was smaller than the others, mud still dried on his knees, carrying no instrument at all. His hair was dark and uncombed. He walked like someone who had learned recently that floors exist and wasn't entirely convinced. And when his eyes found mine across that vast insulting distance—from door to dais, forty feet of polished stone designed to make every subject a stranger—he looked at me the way no one has looked at me in years I cannot count.

He looked *sorry* for me.

Not afraid. Not awed. Not calculating, the way even children learn to calculate before a crown. Sorry. As if I were the foundling. As if he recognized something missing in me the way you recognize a missing tooth in an old friend's smile.

"What's your name?" I heard myself ask. My voice carried oddly in the high room, thinner than I intended.

The chamberlain started forward to answer for the boy—of course he did, no one here expects anyone to speak for themselves—but the child answered first, in a clear voice pitched to carry:

"Wren."

And something happened in my chest. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. It was not memory. I do not remember a Wren. It was not recognition, exactly, because recognition requires a referent and mine appeared to have been repossessed. It was the feeling you get when a name of a person you loved is spoken years after their death—that sudden drop, that *oh*, that grief arriving ahead of its explanation. Grief with no corpse. Mourning for nothing.

My eyes were wet.

I felt them go wet and I felt the entire court pretend not to notice, which is the highest courtesy Aldermere knows how to pay, and I sat there on my cold iron throne with tears running down my face in front of two hundred courtiers and a line of refugee children, grieving something I could not name, and the strangest part—the part I will set down in the true ledger tonight in my worst handwriting—was that it did not feel like sorrow at all.

It felt like *returning*. Like a room in me, long locked, hearing a key turn in the hall.

"Sing something," said Casserly softly, at my shoulder. It was an order dressed as a suggestion. Everything he says is. "The court enjoys a song."

"I didn't ask—" I began.

But the boy stepped forward anyway, out of the line, into the center of that enormous floor, alone, mud-kneed and instrumentless, and he looked up at me again with those sorry eyes, and he sang.

* * *

I will tell you what he sang, and then I will tell you what it did, and you will understand why I am writing this down as though my life depends on it, because I have begun to suspect that it does.

He sang a song with no words I knew. The language was old—not the Old Tongue they teach scholars, all declensions and dead kings, but something older, older than grammar almost, vowels that seemed to come up from underneath language rather than through it. It sounded the way I imagine rivers sounded before anyone named them. The court fell silent in that particular aristocratic way, the silence of people deciding whether to be bored or impressed, and then, gradually, it stopped being either.

Because the song was familiar.

Not the tune—I'd swear I'd never heard the tune. But the *shape* of it. The way a melody moves through a phrase, rises, doubles back, lands somewhere unexpected and true—it moved the way my own thoughts move. Or used to move. Before they went soft and slid away. Listening to it was like hearing someone hum along with a song inside my own head, one I'd forgotten I was humming, and getting it right.

Halfway through the second verse I understood, with no evidence whatsoever, that the song was about a garden. A walled garden. Someone tending it. Someone leaving it. And I wept.

Not decorously, as kings are trained to weep—one tear, well-placed, signifying magnanimity. I wept like a man at a funeral for a friend whose name he has lost. My hands gripped the arms of the throne hard enough to hurt. Somewhere in the third verse I realized I was mouthing along, shaping sounds in that impossible old language, words I had no right to know, and I stopped, frightened, and the boy saw me stop, and his eyes filled too, and he kept singing, softer, the way you sing to a frightened animal.

The court did not laugh. That should have warned me. Two hundred bored courtiers watching their king weep at a mud-caked orphan, and not one smirk, not one whisper—only a stillness that spread outward like frost, like the whole room was listening to something behind the song. Even the light through the high windows seemed to lean in.

When it ended, the silence held for a long moment, and then broke all at once into applause, loud and relieved, the sound of a room exhaling. The chamberlain began shooing the children back toward the doors. The grain dispute resumed where it had left off, as if the world had only paused to let a ghost pass through it.

But the boy—Wren—did not go with the others. He stood in the middle of the floor looking at me, and mouthed something. No sound. Just the shape of words, in the old tongue, aimed at me across forty feet of stone.

I could not read them. I want to be honest about that too: I watched his lips move and understood nothing, and it nearly broke me, because every instinct I have left insisted that whatever he said was meant for me alone, was perhaps the single most important sentence ever shaped in my direction, and I stood there illiterate in my own mother tongue—or whatever that tongue was, whatever *I* was, once.

Casserly's hand came to rest on my shoulder. Cold through the silk. It always feels cold through the silk.

"A charming performance," he said. "Though I wonder where a village foundling learned pre-Founding speech."

"You know the language?" I asked, too quickly.

"I know *of* it," he said, and there was something in his voice I had never heard there before, and it took me a moment to identify it, because it was so wildly out of place on him.

It was hunger.

"The guilds deal in old things," he went on, mild again, the pale eyes fixed on the boy being ushered out. "Old words surface sometimes. Usually nothing. Still—an unregistered speaker of pre-Founding speech is a curiosity. Curiosities should be examined." He paused. "Thoroughly."

"They're children," I said. "Refugees. They'll be housed and fed, not *examined*."

"Of course, Majesty." The bow was perfect. Every bow of his is perfect. I have begun to collect them in memory, the way a man collects evidence against a business partner—this bow, given at this moment, after these words. My private archive of Casserly grows daily, and means nothing, and proves less, because whenever I sit down to reason from it, the reasoning slides away like the fish and the buttered hands.

The audience ended. The court emptied. And I remained on the throne longer than protocol allowed, doing sums I couldn't finish.

A boy who looks at me with pity. A song that fits the inside of my skull. An old language rising off my own tongue unbidden. Hours missing from my life like teeth pulled from a jaw. Decrees in my hand that I never wrote. A chancellor who stands just behind my right shoulder, always, like a shadow that learned manners.

These facts will not join together. I lay them side by side every night in the true ledger and they lie there like bones from different animals, refusing to make a skeleton. But today, for the length of one song, I felt the shape of the whole animal. I felt it the way you feel weather coming—in the ache of old wounds, in places that no longer quite exist.

Something of mine is walking around outside me. Something was taken, and taken recently enough that the wound hasn't scarred, and it stood in my throne room today with mud on its knees and sang my grief back to me in a language older than kingdoms.

And the crown—

Here is the strangest entry yet, and I save it for last because I hardly believe it myself. When the boy finished singing, when the applause crashed down, I reached up—one motion, half a gesture, nothing—to touch my crown. Just to touch it. Kings do it without thinking; it is our worry-stone, our rosary, our proof.

I could not.

My hand rose and stopped, a hand's width short, as if the air above my brow were solid. As if something around my head had extended a boundary and said: *not that.* And in that same instant, for less than a heartbeat, I felt—attention. Not sight, not sound. Attention, vast and patient and cold, turning toward me the way a lighthouse turns toward a boat that has drifted too near the rocks.

It noticed me noticing.

I lowered my hand. I smiled the royal smile. I said the royal nothing. And beneath the smile, in the locked room where I keep what remains of myself, I wrote the only sentence that has ever made the fish hold still:

*Whatever wears this crown is not me, and it knows that I know, and one of us is running out of time.*

Tonight I will find where they've housed the foundling singers. Tonight, if it costs me the last of my missing hours, I will walk the servants' corridors myself, past Casserly's sleeping vigil, past whatever boundary the crown has drawn around my head, and I will stand in front of a mud-kneed boy who pities me, and I will say to him the only words I trust:

*I don't know who you are. But I think you know who I'm supposed to be.*

And if the walls have ears—and lately I suspect the walls are mostly ear—if the pale-eyed thing behind my shoulder hears me, if the attention turns and the price comes due—

Well. Then at least I will finally learn what I am worth.
