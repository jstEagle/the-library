## 14. Sable's Last Entry

I have been Guildmistress of the Ledgered Veil for thirty-one years, and in all that time I have learned exactly one thing worth teaching: every bargain has a second page. The one they show you is always the pretty one.

The crown-thing showed me its pretty page at the hour past midnight, in the warden's hall beneath the palace, where the candles burn without flickering because even fire here has been told to behave.

It came to me as a voice first. Of course it did. Things like that never bother with faces unless they want something you can look at while they take it.

"You are Sable Vane," it said, from everywhere, from the mortar between the stones. "Keeper of the Second Ledger. Sworn under the old seal. You have carried your guild's debt for longer than most of your members have been alive."

"I've carried worse," I said. "I raised a son."

The silence that followed had texture to it—the particular quality of an ancient intelligence consulting its files. I imagined it flipping through me the way Noor flips through ledgers: quickly, hungrily, looking for the loose thread.

"Your guild was founded on a simple transaction," it said. "A king's memory, sold to purchase invisibility. The debt accrues still. Interest compounds. In another generation the veil fails entirely, and every member of your order becomes visible to every enemy they have ever made. I can erase the debt."

"Generously," I said.

"I can erase the debt," it repeated, with the patience of a thing that has never once needed to hurry, "if you deliver the boy."

*The boy.* Wren. The fragment of Aldric that the crown-thing had cut out of him and cast into the world like a bad coin, and which had come knocking twice on our door with mud on his knees and a language older than sin in his mouth.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take the debt out of you personally. I am told grief weighs differently when it is measured by weight rather than by year. Your guild taught the free cities that method, Guildmistress. It seems fitting we should use it on their teachers."

There it was. The second page.

I sat in the warden's chair—they had left me one, a courtesy, or perhaps a joke—and I thought about how I had gotten here, which is what old women do instead of praying.

* * *

We had entered the capital through the gate that remembers names, and I had known, walking through it, that the gate would remember us too. Gates like that are not doors; they are clerks. And clerks report to somebody. I had told Noor as much, in my way, which is to say I had said nothing and let them draw the wrong conclusion, because a Guildmistress who explains herself is a Guildmistress who has stopped being useful.

Noor thinks I don't trust them. That's not true. I trust Noor more than I have trusted anyone since my son died, which is precisely why I keep it off the books. Trust that gets written down becomes collateral. Collateral gets collected. I have spent three decades making sure nothing about the people I love appears anywhere in any ledger, including the ones in my own head.

So when the wardens took me—quietly, in the archive corridor, while Noor and Wren were being fitted for choir robes two floors up—I went without a fight, because fighting would have made noise, and noise travels upward.

They brought me down here, to the hall where the candles obey, and they left me alone with the dark for six hours, which is standard practice. Fear ferments best in the dark. Then the voice began.

* * *

"Consider the arithmetic," the crown-thing said, when I had finished not answering it. "You are sixty-eight years old. Your guild numbers nine living members, four of whom are past seventy. Your youngest keeper is twenty-two and sentimental beyond all repair. Against this: myself. Seven veils, of which yours is the last functioning. Armies enough to salt every road from here to the coast. Deliver the fragment, and I restore what your founders stole. The king's memory returns to the king. The veil dissolves cleanly. Your people walk away whole."

"Whole," I said. "That's a word with a lot of room in it."

"Your memories remain intact. Your guild remains intact. Only the debt is erased."

"And the boy?"

"The boy is not a person. He is a severed portion of a man I am currently wearing. Returning him is not cruelty. It is bookkeeping."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Thirty-one years of keeping accounts of impossible things, and here was the largest impossible thing of all trying to convince me that a child was a line item.

"You know," I said, "you sound almost reasonable. That's the frightening part. My husband sounded reasonable too, right up until he sold our daughter's laughter to pay a gambling debt. Reasonable men and reasonable monsters both keep excellent records."

"Then let us keep a record together." Something moved in the dark—not approaching, but *arriving*, the way winter arrives, all at once and everywhere. A table stood before me that had not stood there before, and on it lay a ledger I knew better than my own hands: the master book, the First Ledger, the one we had smuggled into the capital sewn into the lining of Wren's traveling cloak because no searcher thinks to strip a child.

Wait. No. This was a copy. A very good copy—the wards hummed along its spine convincingly—but the true book was still with Noor, hidden where only a keeper could find it. Which meant the crown-thing wanted me to believe it had the original, or wanted me to act as though it did, or—

Or it didn't know where the original was, and it was gambling that I would trade the truth for my skin.

Every bargain has a second page. And sometimes the second page belongs to *you*.

"That's a forgery," I said pleasantly.

The dark went very still.

"The spine-warding is elegant," I continued, standing, because an old woman stands when she wants to be underestimated, and I have built a career on being underestimated. "But the Founders bound the First Ledger with grief-weight, not ink-weight. A real copy would ache. This one just hums. Whoever made it has read about our work but never done any. Which tells me you haven't found the book. Which tells me you need me."

"Sable Vane—"

"You need me," I said, "and I am going to die in this room. We both know it. Wardens' knives, or whatever passes for knives in a place where the candles mind their manners. So here is my counteroffer, monster, and mark it well, because it is the last honest transaction this city will see for some time: I will tell you nothing, I will give you nothing, and everything you think you know about where that book is will be wrong, because starting tonight, I am going to lie to you in writing."

* * *

Here is what the crown-thing does not understand about keepers, and what no usurper, no army, no god of small cruelties has ever understood:

We do not keep ledgers to record what happened. We keep ledgers to decide what happened. An entry written in a Founder's hand outweighs an event written in blood. Memory is currency, yes—but forgery is minting, and minting is the oldest magic there is.

They gave me back my satchel. That was their mistake, though to be fair, they had little choice; a Guildmistress separated from her tools stops being a hostage and starts being luggage. Inside the satchel: three pens, a vial of borrowed ink (ink that remembers being written), a stub of candle-wax for sealing, and my personal day-book, forty years of entries in a hand so cramped that only Noor and I can read it.

And one more thing they did not catalog, because it looks like nothing: a blank quire of paper pressed from reeds grown on the grave of the guild's first founder. Paper that believes whatever is written on it, provided the writer believes it harder than the reader doubts it.

I have been saving that paper for thirty years. I had always assumed I'd save it for something grander than this. But grandeur is for the young.

* * *

The wardens checked on me at intervals. I counted them by their footsteps: heavy, heavier, light-and-limping, and one who walked like a man reading while he moved. Four wardens, rotating, every third hour. Between visits, the voice returned and made its offers again, each one sweeter and each one rotten at the center, like fruit from a tree planted over something dead.

Erase the debt. Restore the veil. Return your youth—I could feel it probing for the shape of my wants, finding the usual ones: my son alive, my hands steady, the smell of the guild's kitchen on a winter morning. It offered me my son. It offered me thirty extra years. It offered me, at the end, the one thing it must have dug out of some deep record of my heart: it offered to let me forget that I had ever betrayed anyone.

"Forget?" I said. "Monster, forgetting is your trade, not mine. I deal in remembering. It's the family business."

On the fourth night—or what I judged was the fourth night, time being slippery below the palace—I began to write.

* * *

If you are reading this, Noor, and I pray to no god in particular that you are, then you have done what I trained you to do, which is to distrust every clean surface and check behind every tidy entry. Good. Now listen, because I have very little time and less ink.

The First Ledger is where it has always been: in the false bottom of the inventory chest in the guildhouse cellar, beneath the crate marked *SPOILED GRIEF — DO NOT OPEN*. Yes, really. The Founders had a sense of humor; it's the only part of them that survived intact. But you already knew that, because you're the one who found the child, and children always find what's buried.

What you don't know is what I'm writing now, in the copies I'm leaving for the wardens to find, in the entries I'm forging into the historical record of this city's archives, in the false trail I am laying across four decades of the guild's history like a bride scattering flowers:

The crown-thing has a name. Of course it does. Everything that bargains has a name; names are how bargains bind. The Founders wrote it down once, in the first book, in the first hand, in the first tongue—the pre-Founding speech, the language that predates kingdoms, the language that no living member of my guild can speak.

Except one.

The child speaks it in his sleep, Noor. I heard him, that last night on the road, reciting the founding entries like a psalm. He wasn't dreaming. He was *reading*. He is a fragment of Aldric, yes, but Aldric's line goes back through the Founders, and the Founders wrote the crown-thing's name into the First Ledger in the boy's own future hand. Time, for things like us, is a circle with a very good accountant.

Do not look for the name yourself. You'll find the entry—you'll know it by the seal—but the words will be dust to you. They were dust to me for thirty years, and I am the finest reader this guild ever produced. It must come from him. Freely spoken, fully meant, in the throne room, with the crown listening. That is the shape of the unbinding. The Founders designed it that way: the king's own severed self, speaking the jailer's name, is the only key that fits.

Which means, Noor, that you cannot protect him from what comes next. You can only stand beside him while he does it. I know how that sits in your chest. It sat in mine for thirty-one years, the whole weight of every young keeper I ever sent into harm's way. It doesn't lighten. You just get stronger shoulders.

One more thing, and then I must put down the pen, because the footsteps are coming early tonight, and they are all four walking together.

The price quoted for restoring the king—one keeper's every memory of love—is real, and it is not negotiable, and I watched you volunteer before you understood it, and I said nothing, and I will carry that silence into whatever dark takes me with more regret than anything else I have ever done. Forgive me. Or better: don't forgive me. Get angry. Anger keeps better than forgiveness, and you'll need something that keeps.

You asked me once why I never wrote anything kind about you in the day-books. Here is your answer, in the last entry I will ever make, in a hand going shaky, by a candle that has finally begun to flicker because somewhere above us the crown-thing's attention has slipped, just slightly, just enough:

Noor Kavanagh is the best of us. Not the cleverest—that was Merrin, dead these ten years—and not the bravest—that was Ede Rusk, who isn't even ours—but the best. The guild forgot you for twenty years, child, because forgetting was the price of keeping you safe, and I signed that entry myself, and it cost me more than any debt the crown ever levied.

Remember that I remembered. Someone has to.

The door is opening. The knives are patient things, and so am I.

Final entry, Sable Vane, Guildmistress, Keeper of the Second L

* * *

They came for me at what the candles claimed was dawn.

Four wardens, and behind them the voice wearing a shape at last—a tall figure in the king's colors with a face like a page someone had tried to erase. It looked at the papers scattered around my chair, at the forty years of forged history I had spread across the floor of its perfect obedient hall, and for one moment—for one glorious, unforgivable moment—it hesitated.

"What have you done?" it asked.

"My job," I said.

The knives were quick. I'll grant them that. The last thing I saw was not the blades but the day-book, open on my knee to the final page, my last line trailing off unfinished—*Keeper of the Second L*—and I thought, with the last of the wit I have hoarded like a dragon hoards gold:

Let them try to collect *that* debt.

Somewhere above me, in a choir loft, a boy was singing in a language older than kingdoms, and a keeper with ink-stained fingers was about to start reading.

Good. Read closely, Noor. Check behind every tidy entry.

I left you everything.
