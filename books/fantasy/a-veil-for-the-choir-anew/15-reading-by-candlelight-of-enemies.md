## 15. Reading by Candlelight of Enemies

Grief, it turns out, is also an entry in the ledger. Nobody warned me about that. There's a column for it—there's a column for everything, that's the guild's whole problem and its whole genius—and when Sable Vane died, the ledger recorded the loss the way it records a sale of borrowed years or a grief sold by weight: *one Guildmistress, aged, unrepentant. Condition: used hard. Provenance: herself.* The ink dried before I stopped shaking.

I did not cry at the funeral, mostly because we don't have funerals. We have *deaccessions*. Old Pell said a few words over her body in the guild's back room—the one with the good chairs, which she never let anyone sit in while she was alive—and then we burned her with rosemary and a page from her own hand, because that's what she'd written into her own file decades ago, in the flat administrative tone she used for everything including her own death. *Disposal: burn with rosemary and one page of my handwriting. Any page will do. I was always saying something.*

Any page will do. I chose the one where she'd scolded me for misfiling a shipment of unspoken names, back in my second year. It seemed like the most honest thing I had of hers.

Then the others went to bed, or to drink, or to whatever senior keepers do at two in the morning after burying their mistress, and I took the master ledger to my desk and lit three candles, because two felt insufficient and four felt like an accusation.

Here is what I knew, sitting there with wax already starting to pool:

Sable had died buying us time. That much was clear from how she died—under warden knives in the archive corridor, with the crown-thing's agents standing over her, offering her the deal they must have offered everyone: betray the girl who carries the king's missing self, and walk free with your debt erased. Sable had said no in the only language she truly spoke. She'd falsified entries.

I didn't know which ones yet. That was the work of the night.

* * *

You have to understand how forgery works in a ledgered house. Anyone can write a lie; children do it constantly, beautifully, without effort. But our ledgers are warded against falsehood the way other buildings are warded against weather. A false entry doesn't just sit wrong—it *itches*. The ink crawls. The margins grow cold. Junior keepers are trained to feel for it with their fingertips before their eyes ever confirm it, and I was, by every measure anyone had ever bothered to apply, still a junior keeper. Invisible even within an invisible guild. Which meant that when the wardens came through our books looking for what Sable had done, they would read the surface and move on.

But Sable hadn't hidden her lies from them. She'd hidden them from *the thing wearing Aldric's crown*, and it had no fingertips. It had decrees and armies and a hollow king, but it could not touch paper with its own hands. It needed wardens, and wardens read like wardens.

So she'd left the lies in plain sight, dressed as errors.

I found the first one an hour in, on a page dated eleven days ago: an entry recording the sale of "three measures of silence" to a buyer marked only by a sigil I didn't recognize—a circle with a vertical stroke through it, like an eye being closed. The price column was wrong. Not obviously wrong. Wrong the way Sable made things wrong on purpose, the way she'd undercharge a widow by exactly the amount that would make the widow argue, because she believed people should fight for what they were given. Three measures of silence, priced at seven coppers each. Silence ran at nine. Always had. The discrepancy was small enough to be fatigue, deliberate enough to be a message.

Seven minus three is four. Four days ago. Something had happened four days ago—or would happen four days from the entry, if you read backward, which is the direction you read anything Sable wrote when she wanted to be understood by exactly one person.

I turned pages. My fingers learned her rhythm faster than my head did. A misdated consignment of borrowed years—off by six. An inventory count off by two. A buyer's mark repeated where it should have been unique. She was leaving me numbers, and the numbers were counting down, or counting toward, or spelling something, and around candle number two burning down to a stub I realized I wasn't decoding an accounting error at all.

I was reading a letter. The longest letter she'd ever written me, and she'd written it in the only dialect we shared, and she'd known—I am certain now she'd known, walking down that corridor with the knives waiting—that I'd be the one holding the candle.

* * *

What the letter said, once I assembled it:

The crown-thing has a name.

That shouldn't have surprised me. Everything in this trade has a name; names are the raw material we traffic in, the grain and salt of our economy. Unspoken names, stolen names, names sold by weight. Of course the thing in the palace had one. What surprised me was *where* Sable said it was buried—not in some vault, not in a warden's memory, not in the usurper's own black heart, but in the guild's first book.

The First Ledger. The one chained under the reading room floor since before anyone currently alive joined. The one nobody opens because opening it requires a key that was lost, or spent, or eaten—we genuinely weren't sure which—and because the last person who tried came back unable to remember his mother's face, though he insisted he'd never had a mother, and retired to the coast very confused and very calm.

Sable's forged entries triangulated it. Six, four, two—the shelf positions, I finally saw, in the old cataloguing system she'd abandoned thirty years ago precisely so that someday a system existed that only she remembered. And then a final entry, dated tomorrow, which was either a joke about her own death or the truest thing she'd ever written: *Inventory adjustment pending. One item to be recovered from deep storage. Value: incalculable. Risk: total.*

Deep storage. Under the floor. Where the First Ledger slept with all the guild's original sins.

I sat back and watched the third candle gutter and thought, with the strange clarity that arrives around hour four of any vigil, *she planned this.* Not her death—maybe not her death—but this night. This exact posture of mine, hunched over her arithmetic, following her down into the dark she'd kept for both of us.

And underneath the purpose, quiet as a second pulse, the sorrow sat and refused to move. I kept trying to set it aside the way you set aside a heavy coat indoors, and it kept staying on. Grief is an entry, I thought again. Fine. Then let it be entered. Let it be carried forward. Sable would have hated a messy book more than she'd have hated tears.

* * *

Wren woke when I pried up the floorboard. Of course he did. The boy sleeps like a cat in a house full of dogs—hears everything, claims to hear nothing.

"It's the middle of the night," he said from the doorway of the reading room, wrapped in a blanket that had belonged to three dead keepers before him. He'd taken to wearing the guild's castoffs like armor. "People who sneak around at midnight in this place end up in the inventory."

"So do people who stand in doorways eavesdropping," I said. "Come hold a candle."

He came. He held it. He watched me work the chains off the trapdoor with a burglar's patience I refuse to explain the origins of, and when the First Ledger came up out of its dark wrapped in oilcloth and forty years of dust, he sneezed with tremendous authority.

It was smaller than I expected. That's the thing they never tell you about founding documents—they're usually disappointingly human-sized, because giants don't start institutions; tired people do. The binding was cracked leather gone soft as skin, and the first page was written in a hand so old the letters looked less like writing than like weather.

Except.

Except the first page wasn't in any script I knew, and I know scripts the way sailors know tides. It was dense, looping, older than the Founding, older than kingdoms, older than the idea of writing things down to keep them—and I recognized it anyway, because I'd heard it spoken in a mud-caked child's voice on the worst night of the guild's recent history.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out careful, the way you handle something already broken. "Read this to me."

He went pale in the candlelight. Not the theatrical pale of a boy performing reluctance. The real kind, the kind that starts at the mouth and drains outward. He looked at the page the way you look at a room you dreamed about once and hoped never to enter awake.

"I know it," he whispered. "I've always known it. It's—" He stopped. Swallowed. Tried again. "It's the language from before. Before the veils. It's what things were called when calling them still *worked*."

"And can you say it?"

"Do you understand what you're asking? Names in this language aren't labels. They're *handles*. You say a true name right and you're not describing the thing, you're *holding* it." His knuckles had gone white around the candlestick. "Noor, what's on this page?"

I turned the First Ledger gently toward him, to the line Sable's numbers had pointed me to, buried in the founding entries like a splinter in a palm. The line where the guild's invisibility bargain was recorded. The price paid. The seller's mark.

"That," I said, "is the name of the thing wearing the king's crown. Sable died making sure we'd know where to find it. It's been in our first book since the day the guild was founded. We've been sleeping on top of our enemy's name for two hundred years." I heard my own voice steady itself into something harder. "We bought our safety with his memory. He wants it back. And the only way to give it back—to give *Aldric* back—is for you to stand in front of that thing and call it what it is, in the tongue it was born in, before kingdoms, before crowns, before it learned to wear people instead of being one."

Wren stared at the page. Somewhere behind his eyes, a king's scattered memories shifted like furniture in a locked house.

"If I say it wrong," he said quietly, "it takes me. That's what names like this do. They go both ways."

"Then we make sure you say it right," I told him, and blew out the last candle, because dawn was coming up gray through the shutters and because I couldn't bear to watch the light run out on Sable's letter one more time. "We practice until your voice doesn't shake. We practice until it's boring. And then we take it to the palace."

He nodded. Small, scared, resolute—the whole boy in miniature.

It was Ede Rusk's messenger who reached us at noon, half-dead in the saddle, salt-crusted and smoke-smelling, carrying a scrap of militia paper with four words scrawled in a captain's blunt hand:

*They breach tonight. Hurry.*
