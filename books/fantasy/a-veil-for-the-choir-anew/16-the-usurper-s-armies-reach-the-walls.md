## 16. The Usurper's Armies Reach the Walls

The hour I bought began at dusk, and it was worth every memory of my brother.

I want that on the record, in case anyone finds this journal and thinks me a fool for the trade. The Guild of the Ledgered Veil quoted a fair price—fair by their standards, which is to say ruinous—and I paid it without haggling, because haggling over Tam's face felt like selling him twice. What I got in return was sixty minutes of perfect courage. Not bravery, which is fear with good posture. Courage. The kind where your hands don't shake, where the arithmetic of odds stops mattering, where you look at ten thousand men coming over the salt flats and think, *well, they'll have to work for it.*

That hour is nearly spent now. I can feel it going the way you feel a fever break—the cold clarity thinning, ordinary dread seeping back in around the edges like water through a cracked hull.

But first, let me tell you about the walls.

* * *

The free cities had never needed walls before. That was our whole identity, wasn't it? Open gates, open markets, open hands. We traded in the guilds' impossible wares—borrowed years sold by the season, griefs weighed on brass scales, names kept safe in vaults no thief could find because the vaults didn't exist until you needed them. Nobody conquered a city that could sell you your enemy's grandmother's recipe for forgiveness. It was bad for business all around.

Then the usurper's armies came up the Salt Road burning every town that had ever done business with a ledger-keeper, and the free cities discovered, rather late, that openness is only a virtue when nobody wants what's inside.

We built walls in nine months. Nine months! Masons working double shifts, paying themselves in borrowed stamina from the guild stores—which, I'll note, we were stripping bare anyway, since the guilds were fleeing east or going underground or, in one memorable case, turning into a flock of pigeons and departing over the harbor. The walls went up ugly and fast, limestone over old brick over desperation, and every engineer who looked at them said some version of *they'll hold, probably, against anything short of a determined effort.*

The usurper sent a determined effort.

* * *

They arrived three days ago and made camp in a crescent around the eastern wall, close enough that at night we could hear their cookfires popping and their soldiers singing—a low, droning hymn that none of us recognized and all of us hated on principle. Their banners showed a crown with no head under it, which the priests among us found blasphemous and the pragmatists found honest advertising.

Their commander sent a herald on the second day. A tall woman with a voice like a bell rung underwater, who stood outside bowshot and read us terms: surrender the guild-folk sheltering in the city, surrender the ledgers, and the usurper would permit the free cities to continue existing as tributaries. She said "permit" the way a butcher says "humane."

I gave her the reply the council had voted on, which was a great deal more diplomatic than what I'd have said myself. Then I climbed back up the wall and told my militia the truth: relief isn't coming. The other cities are besieged too, or cowed, or both. Whatever happens next happens here, with us, with what we've got.

What we've got is four hundred militia, six hundred volunteers, two retired siege engineers who argue like married couples, and one borrowed hour of perfect courage currently burning a hole in my chest like a coal wrapped in silk.

And we've got the message from Noor Kavanagh.

* * *

It came by pigeon, which surprised me—I'd expected something stranger from the Ledgered Veil, maybe a letter delivered by its own ghost, maybe words appearing in the frost on my morning glass. But no. A pigeon, mud-footed and indignant, with a scrap of paper tied to its leg in handwriting so small and precise it looked typeset:

*Captain Rusk — The debt comes due tonight. When the king is whole, the armies stop. Hold until moonset. Then hold anyway. — N.K.*

Underneath, smaller still: *Thank you for what you paid. It was not wasted.*

I read that last line four times and then put the paper in my breast pocket, over the heart, which is where I keep things I'm not ready to think about. Whoever this Noor was—and Sable Vane had spoken of them once, briefly, as "our youngest, who sees everything and says nothing"—they knew what I'd given up in that market stall in Vell. They knew I'd traded my brother's laugh, his terrible singing, the way he used to fall asleep sitting up during harvest with an apple still in his hand. They knew, and they'd taken a moment, mid-catastrophe, to tell me it mattered.

Guild-folk. Impossible people trading in impossible things. And somehow that was the kindest sentence anyone had written me in eleven years.

Tonight, then. Moonset. Whatever the boy and the keeper meant to do in the palace, they'd do it while the usurper's attention was fixed here, on my wall, on my four hundred.

I went down to the armory and checked the oil stores for the ninth time.

* * *

The assault came an hour after midnight, which flattered me—I'd guessed they'd wait for the moon to wester, same as I'd have done. Horns along the whole crescent of their camp, deep and brazen, and then the dark slope below the wall sprouted ladders the way a corpse sprouts mold: everywhere at once, silent, wrong.

"Bows," I said, and my voice didn't shake, because the hour was still holding. Around me, four hundred throats repeated it down the line, and the night filled with the creak of drawn yew.

Here is what perfect courage does not do: it does not make you cruel. I'd feared, buying it, that I'd become some serene butcher, ordering deaths with a smile. Instead it did the opposite. In that hour I saw every man on those ladders as somebody's Tam. Somebody's little brother who sang badly and slept sitting up. I shot them anyway, with a steadiness that felt almost tender, and I grieved each one in the half-second between loosing and nocking, and there was room in me for all of it because courage, real courage, turns out to be mostly a bigger container for sorrow.

"Oil," I said, when the first ladders bit stone. "Now."

The eastern wall caught fire along its whole face, and for twenty minutes the world was orange and screaming and the smell of pork roasting, which I will never eat again, and which I am aware is the least of what I'll never do again.

* * *

They breached at the sally gate. Of course they did. Old Hesk the mason had warned us—the joints there were rushed, the mortar mixed with river sand because we'd run out of the good stuff in month five. The usurper's engineers must have known it too; they brought a ram shaped like a serpent's skull and worked it with a patience that told me professionals were running this army, not fanatics. Fanatics are easier. Fanatics tire.

The gate went at the third hour past midnight with a sound like the world's largest knuckle cracking, and through the gap came the vanguard in black-lacquered scale, shields locked, moving with the horrible economy of men who have done this many times and enjoyed it approximately never.

"Squares!" I shouted, and took the stairs down two at a time, because this was the part I'd bought the hour for. Not the wall. The wall was arithmetic. This—meeting armored professionals in a narrow street with farm boys and dockhands and a baker's guild that had volunteered as a unit—this was where ordinary commanders broke, where the fear got into your voice and your voice got into your men and the whole thing curdled into rout.

My hour held. I walked into that street ahead of my banner feeling nothing but a vast, level calm, and I put myself where the line would bend first, and it didn't bend.

I won't give you the whole of it. Street fighting is not glorious; it is plumbing problems solved with steel. We funneled them, we flanked them off the tannery row, we dropped a loaded cart of quarry stone across the mouth of Cooper's Lane and turned their column into two disconnected arguments. Dara Voss, seventeen, held a doorway alone for six minutes with a billhook and came out of it gray-faced and vomiting and asking, in a small voice, if she'd done right. I told her she had. I'd have told her that regardless of the hour; the hour just let me say it steady.

By the fourth hour we'd sealed the breach with rubble and bodies and burning wagons, and the vanguard pulled back to reform, and the street belonged to us again, in the way that a battlefield belongs to whoever is standing on less of it than yesterday.

And then the hour ended.

* * *

I felt it go at moonset, exactly as the ledger had promised. One moment the calm; the next, all the fear arriving at once like creditors, like everything I'd deferred coming due with interest. My hands shook. My knees informed me of the day's invoice. I sat down hard against a doorframe in an alley full of sleeping wounded and shook like a wet dog, and Sergeant Imre found me there and had the grace to pretend I was checking on the injured.

"Wall's holding," he said. "Gate's plugged. They're regrouping for dawn."

"Dawn," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone tired and mortal. "How many have we got?"

"Two hundred and change fit to fight. Maybe a hundred and fifty by light, once the walking wounded stop lying about themselves."

"And them?"

"More than that." He said it flatly, the way you report weather. "A lot more."

I looked up at the sky. The moon was a fingernail paring above the western rooftops, going down. *Hold until moonset,* Noor had written. *Then hold anyway.* Well. Moonset had come and gone, and I was holding, and the holding was about to become the kind that costs everything per minute.

"Imre. Paper and ink. And find me a pigeon that's still speaking to us."

* * *

I wrote the message by lantern-light in the triage tent, kneeling over a stretcher-table while a surgeon two feet away argued with a man's insides. I kept it short, because pigeons have opinions about payload and because the truth doesn't need padding:

*Walls breached at sally gate. Sealed. Half my strength spent. They come again at dawn and we cannot stop a third effort. Whatever you mean to do in that palace, do it TONIGHT. Every hour you buy, we buy with blood. — E.R.*

I hesitated, then added, because I am apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone:

*The hour you thanked me for ran out at moonset. I want you to know I'd buy it again. Tell the boy the king owes me nothing. Tell him he owes the ones who died on this wall, and the way to pay them is to be worth saving.*

I tied the scroll to the pigeon's leg myself. It regarded me with the flat contempt of its species, and I told it, quietly, "East. Find the mud-footed girl. Don't die stupidly," and threw it up into the dark, and watched it beat away toward the capital until the stars ate it.

Then I went back to the wall.

* * *

Dawn found us still there. That's the whole of my report, really, and someday, if anyone writes the history of the free cities, I hope that's the line they use: *Dawn found them still there.* Not victorious—we weren't, we'd lost the outer quarter and the granary and poor Hesk the mason, who died defending the gate he'd been ashamed of. Not unbroken—there wasn't a man or woman on that wall without a bandage or a burn or a friend's name newly added to the list. But there. Lined up along the smoke-blackened stone with whatever weapons remained, watching the usurper's host form ranks on the plain for the third effort, the one we couldn't stop.

I stood at the center of the eastern wall with my sword out and my brother nowhere in my memory except as a shape where something loved used to be, and I waited for the horns.

They blew.

And then—they stopped. Mid-note. All of them, along the whole crescent, cut off at once, as if a hand had closed over the army's throat. The ranks on the plain wavered. Banners dipped. And from somewhere far to the east, beyond the hills, beyond the capital, came a sound I have no better word for than *unraveling*—a long, low note like a struck bell heard through the earth itself, and every soldier on that plain felt it in their teeth. I saw men drop their spears. I saw the serpent-skinned officers shouting orders that no longer landed. I saw, at the center of the host, the crownless-crown banners begin, one by one, to fall—not lowered, *falling*, sliding down their poles like the cloth had forgotten how to hang.

Imre grabbed my arm. "Captain? Captain, what is that?"

I didn't know. I know now, or I know enough: somewhere in a throne room in the capital, a mud-footed boy spoke a name older than kingdoms, and a keeper named Noor paid a price I cannot imagine and will spend the rest of my life trying to repay, and the thing wearing Aldric's crown came apart like a lie at noon.

But standing on that wall at dawn, I knew only this: the horns had stopped, the army was dissolving into argument and terror, and the sun was coming up gold on the salt flats.

"Stand down the archers," I said. My voice shook, and I let it. "Somebody's paid the fare."

Then I sat down on the bloodied stones of the wall my city had built in nine months out of desperation, and I laughed, and I cried, and I did both at once, which I recommend to no one, and far to the east the bell-note faded like a tide going out—

—leaving behind, on the horizon, a single rider coming north along the Salt Road at a gallop, carrying something white that caught the new light.

I stood up to see who'd survived to bring us news, and the wind off the flats carried the first warm breath of a morning that, against every wager I'd ever placed, had actually arrived.
