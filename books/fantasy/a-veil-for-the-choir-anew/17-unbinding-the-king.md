## 17. Unbinding the King

### Noor

There is a particular quality to a throne room at night, and that quality is *lie*. By day, the banners and the gilded columns and the dais all conspire to convince you that power is a solid thing, load-bearing, architectural. By candlelight—or in our case, by the single guttering lamp Sable's forged entries had bought us passage past three wardens to carry—you see the truth, which is that a throne room is a very large empty box with one expensive chair in it, and everything else is upholstery for fear.

Wren's hand was cold in mine. Small hands, chapped from the road, with a healing scrape across one knuckle from the night he'd learned to skip stones on the Vell and had been, in his words, *terrible at it*. I found myself cataloguing these details the way I catalogue inventory, which is what I do when I am frightened, which is always.

"The wardens change at the third bell," I whispered. "After that, the throne room stands empty except for the king."

"Except for the king," Wren repeated. He said it the way you'd say *except for the fire*.

We had come through the Gate That Remembers Names, across the palace's service corridors, past a guard who was asleep in a way that Sable's last forgery had purchased and that I intended never to think about too hard because thinking about it meant thinking about Sable, and I could not afford Sable yet. Grief, in the guild's terms, is inventory. You do not open the strongbox in the middle of a job.

The doors to the throne room were twice my height and carved with the history of a kingdom that had, if our ledgers were honest, been purchased on credit.

"You remember the words?" I asked, and immediately hated myself for it, because Wren had been reciting Aldric's scattered memories in his sleep for a hundred miles, and the first name—the true first name, the one buried in the guild's first book in language older than kingdoms—was not something he could forget. It was something he *was*.

"You remember the words," Wren said, with a boy's merciless ear for a foolish question. Then, softer: "You'll be right there?"

"Right there. Ledger open. Collateral ready."

He nodded. He squared his small shoulders the way I'd taught him to square them for lying to innkeepers, and I thought, absurdly, *I taught him that, I taught him everything, I taught him to eat stew without wearing half of it, and tomorrow the ledger will take all of it back,* and I put the thought in the strongbox with Sable and shut the lid.

We pushed the doors.

They opened without sound, which was somehow worse than groaning. A throne room should announce you. This one simply *admitted* us, like a mouth.

King Aldric sat on his throne in the dark, and he was weeping.

Not the weeping of a man in sorrow. The weeping of a house with a broken pipe—automatic, unattended, the king's face slack and empty above it, his eyes fixed on nothing, tears running down into the collar of a nightrobe he must have been sitting in for hours. The crown sat on his head and it did not gleam. It *absorbed*. The candlelight went into it and did not come out, and looking at it too long made my teeth ache, the way the oldest ledgers made my teeth ache, the way things that were older than accounting made everything ache.

"Your Majesty," Wren said.

And the king looked up.

I have seen a great many impossible transactions. I have watched a woman sell her shadow and live to complain about the damp. I have seen borrowed years change hands and the borrower's hair go grey at the temples in real time. None of it prepared me for the look on Aldric's face when he saw Wren, which was the look of a man finding a room in his own house he had forgotten he owned—a room he had walled up himself, in an emergency, a lifetime ago.

"You," the king said. His voice was ruined, rusted from disuse. "I know you. I know you the way I know—" He pressed a hand to his chest. "There's a hole. There has been a hole. And you are the shape of it."

"I'm the part they cut out," Wren said. "The part that remembers being you."

The crown moved.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all the guild has ever had, and because no one will believe me otherwise. The crown did not turn. It *leaned*, the way a predator leans, and the darkness in it gathered itself, and a voice came out of the king's own mouth—not from the crown, from the *mouth*, wearing the king's lips like a puppeteer wearing a glove—and it said:

"Little piece. Little scrap of the man I ate. You've come such a long way to be digested."

Wren did not flinch. I had taught him to lie to innkeepers, but no one had taught him this, this stillness in the presence of the thing that had unmade him. He had brought it with him out of whatever he was before he was a boy.

"Say it," I whispered.

And Wren, in a language older than kingdoms, older than the ledger, older than the lie of thrones, spoke the first name of the thing wearing the crown.

### Wren

The name was not a word. I want whoever reads this—I want *Noor*, though Noor will not remember reading it—to understand that. It was not a word. It was a *shape*, and my mouth was the only shape that could hold it, because I was made at the same time the crown-thing was made, cut from the same king, the true king, on the night the bargain was struck and the veil was drawn.

The guild thinks it bought its invisibility with the king's memory. The guild is half right. The guild's founders bought their veil with a king's memory, and the crown-thing was the *knife*, and the knife has been wearing the hand ever since.

When I spoke the name, three things happened.

The candles went out. All of them, everywhere in the palace, I think, though I only know our room. The dark that came was not the dark of a place with no light. It was the dark of a place with no *witness*, which is worse.

The king screamed. Not the crown-thing—the *king*, Aldric, the true and ruined remainder of him, screaming as something that had coiled through his marrow for his whole reign let go all at once, like a root system torn from soil, like a debt called in.

And the crown-thing *unmade*.

I do not have better words. It came off the king's head the way frost comes off a window when the sun takes it—not falling, not breaking, but ceasing, in long bright runnels, and in its ceasing it screamed too, in the pre-Founding speech, in my language, cursing me in the grammar of before-names. It had been a veil once. One of seven. It had been meant to hide a kingdom and instead it had hidden a king from himself, and it had gotten *fat* on the hiding, and now the hiding was over and it did not want to stop existing, and nothing that has existed for a hundred years wants to stop existing.

It reached for me.

Not with hands. With *accounting*. I felt it begin to itemize me—the way the guild itemizes, the way Noor itemizes when frightened, grief by weight and year by year—and I understood, in the last clear moment, that it was going to take me as its final entry. I was the fragment. I was the loose page. If the book was burning, it would take the loose page into the fire, and the king would be whole again but for one piece, forever, and the piece would be ash, and no one would ever know there had been a boy who learned to skip stones and was terrible at it.

I was not afraid. That is the strange part. I was made from a king's courage, or what the king used to have of it, and it held.

What I felt instead was *regret*, and the regret had a specific address. It was: Noor will be alone in the dark with the ledger, and Noor will have done all of this, and I will not be there to be terrible at stones with.

Then something was between me and the unmaking.

It was a book.

### Noor

The ledger.

Our ledger—the guild's master ledger, the one I had carried from the guildhall through the Gate That Remembers Names, the one with Sable's forged entries in her dying hand and my own entries in my careful invisible one. I had it open against my chest. I had said, *collateral ready*, and I had meant: if the price comes due tonight, let it come, let it take what it takes.

But when the crown-thing reached for Wren, I did not think. Thinking is for people with time.

I stepped between them and I held up the book, and I said—the words came from somewhere older than my training, from the first book's first page, which I had read by candlelight with Sable's blood still on my cuffs—I said:

"*Take the account instead.*"

Everything stopped. The dark without witness stopped. The unmaking stopped, hung mid-frost, and the crown-thing's voice came from everywhere, amused and starving:

"A keeper. A little keeper. You offer me a *book*."

"I offer you a *debt*," I said, and my voice did not shake, which I will be smug about for as long as I have a self to be smug with. "You are a veil gone wrong. You are an account that would not close. And every account that will not close can be closed by collateral. The guild's founders knew it. They wrote it into the first page. *The ledger stands surety for the ledger.*"

"That is not what that line means."

"It is what it means *tonight*."

I felt the thing consider me. I felt it the way you feel a creditor read your name. And I understood, in that held moment of terrible luminous dark, what it saw: not a girl with a book. A girl with a *capacity*. A girl who could hold memory the way a vault holds gold, who had spent her whole invisible life keeping accounts no one else could read—and therefore a girl who could be read *from*.

It wanted Wren because Wren was a fragment of a king. But I was a keeper, and a keeper's mind is a ledger, and a ledger can be collected.

"Then it's a trade," the crown-thing said, in the king's mouth, in the king's ruined voice, and for one obscene moment it sounded almost gentle. "The scrap for the scribe. The piece for the page. Do we have terms?"

"Noor," Wren said behind me. "Noor, *don't*—"

"Terms," I said, "are accepted," and I pressed the ledger into the dark, and the dark took it.

What happened next happened fast and slow at once, the way the worst and best moments do. The ledger burned—not with fire but with *reading*, every page illuminating itself as it was consumed, and I saw our whole history flare past: the borrowed years, the unspoken names, the griefs sold by weight, Sable's forgeries, my apprenticeship listed as collateral, the founding bargain, the veil, the king. The crown-thing drank it in, and as it drank, it finished unmaking, because an account, once collected, is *closed*, and it had been open for a hundred years and it was so tired, I think, so unspeakably tired of being open.

The last thing it did—because even a thing older than kingdoms can be petty—was reach back through the closing account and mark its collection.

It marked *me*.

I felt the first page of myself turn. It was a memory of the guildhall kitchen, and the smell of Sable's terrible tea, and it peeled away like a leaf from a wet window, and I watched it go and could not, even then, remember what I was losing.

The candles came back.

The king was on the floor of his own throne room, whole, gasping, his hands pressed to a bare head that had not been bare in a lifetime, weeping now with a face that was *his*, every tear accounted for. And Wren was running to him—no, Wren was running to *me*, both, the boy torn between the king he was cut from and the keeper who had stepped in front of him, and he caught me as my knees went, and he was saying my name, my name, my name, and it was already beginning to sound like a word in a language I was forgetting.

"Noor. Noor, look at me. The ledger's closed. It's over. You paid it, you paid, you—"

"Who," I said, "is Noor?"

### Wren

The candles came back and the crown was gone and the king was whole, and none of it mattered, because Noor was looking at me with a stranger's eyes.

"Who is Noor?"

My name for them. The name I had given them on the road, the name they had let me keep, the name they had answered to through six chapters of country and every one of my terrible stone-skips. It was the first page of them to go.

"It's you," I said. "It's—you're Noor. You're the guild's keeper. You taught me to lie to innkeepers. You're—"

They smiled at me. That was the worst part. They smiled the polite, careful smile you give a child in a market who has mistaken you for their mother.

"I'm sorry," they said kindly. "I think you've got me confused with someone. Are you lost? Is that the king on the floor?"

Behind me, Aldric had risen, unsteady, one hand on his throne like a man leaning on a doorframe after a long illness. He was looking at Noor with an expression I recognized because it was my expression: the ache of a debt you cannot aim.

"They paid for me," he said hoarsely. "Boy. Whoever they were. They paid for me, didn't they."

"They paid for everyone," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle, because I was a fragment of a king made whole and I still could not fix this, I could speak the language older than kingdoms and there was no word in it for *come back*.

Noor's eyes were wet. They noticed this themselves, with visible puzzlement, raising a hand to their own cheek.

"I'm crying," they said. "I don't—why am I crying? I don't know why I'm crying."

And they looked down at the book still clutched against their chest—the ledger, our ledger, empty now, every page white as first snow, the whole history of the Guild of the Ledgered Veil collected and closed—and they frowned at it the way you frown at a word on the tip of your tongue.

"There's something I'm supposed to do with this," they said slowly. "There's something—someone is waiting for me to—"

They stopped. They looked up at me, and for one heartbeat—less, a fraction, a page not yet fully turned—something moved behind their eyes, something that knew me, something that was already peeling away even as it surfaced, and they said, in a whisper, in a voice that was theirs and mine and ours:

"Your name means something. Why does your name mean something?"

And then it was gone, and they were a stranger holding a blank book in a throne room, crying without knowing why, and the bells of the palace began to ring the hour, and somewhere beyond the walls the free cities were burning, and I understood that the account was closed but the story was not, because the ledger was empty, and an empty ledger is not an ending.

It is a first page.

I took Noor's hand. They let me, the way you let a lost child hold yours.

"Come with me," I said, because it was all I had. "Please. Just—come with me."

They came. It was the only word in any language, old or new, that I had left to give them.
