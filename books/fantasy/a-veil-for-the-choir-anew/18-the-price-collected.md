## 18. The Price Collected

I have read a great many contracts in my time as keeper. I have watched people sign away their griefs, their years, their mothers' maiden names and their children's first steps. I always assumed—quietly, smugly, in the way of someone who catalogs other people's losses for a living—that I understood what it meant to pay.

I did not understand. I want that recorded somewhere, even if the only place left to record it is the inside of my own dissolving skull: Noor Kavanagh did not understand, and volunteered anyway, and would do it again, which is either courage or idiocy, and the ledger does not care which.

The collection began with the smell of old paper.

That sounds like a small thing to lead with, but you have to understand—the ledger didn't take my memories all at once, like a thief emptying a drawer. It took them the way it takes everything: properly. In order. With entries. And the first thing to go was not Wren, or Sable, or the guild itself. It was smaller than any of those. It was the particular smell of the guild's back room on a rainy afternoon—beeswax and dust and the faint iron tang of ink made from ground slate—and I felt it lift out of me like a splinter drawn from flesh, and then it was simply *gone*, and I could no longer tell you what I was missing, only that a hole had opened where something used to fit.

"That one doesn't count," Wren said. He was holding my hand. His grip was too tight. He'd been told, as we all had been told by the ledger itself in its terrible patient voice, that interference voided nothing but added penalties, so he held on anyway, because he was eight years old and eight-year-olds do not care about penalties.

"Everything counts," I said. "That's sort of the guild's whole thesis."

The throne room was quiet around us. Aldric lay on the steps below his own dais, breathing, whole, his crown lying beside him like a dead thing—which, I suppose, by then it was. The unmaking had worked. The thing that had worn him for thirty years had peeled away shrieking into the space between names, and Wren had poured back into his king like water finding its old channel, and for one luminous moment we had all stood there thinking *we did it, we actually did it*.

Then the ledger opened itself on the floor between us, which is a sentence I never thought I'd write, and began turning its own pages.

"Noor Kavanagh," it said, in the voice it uses for collections, which is the voice of an abacus being very polite about arithmetic. "Collateral is called due. Do you consent?"

"I already consented," I said. "Chapter and verse. It's in your own book."

"The ledger requires verbal confirmation at time of collection."

"Yes. Fine. Yes."

Wren made a sound like he was swallowing broken glass. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," Sable would have said, except Sable was three days dead under wardens' knives with her final entry unfinished, and I was the last keeper standing, and the ledger knew it. That was the other reason there wasn't another way. The price was *one keeper's every memory of love*, and there was exactly one keeper left to pay it, and the ledger does not accept substitutes, because the ledger has never once accepted anything except what was written down, and what was written down was my name.

The pages turned. The smell of beeswax went. Then the sound of rain on the guild roof, which I hadn't known I loved until I felt it leave. Then—

* * *

You want to know how memory leaves. Everyone wants to know, and no one who knows can tell them, which is the cruelest part of the trade. So let me tell you while I still can.

It doesn't hurt. That's the horror of it. It feels like being set down gently after carrying something heavy for so long you'd forgotten the weight was there. Each page turned and something lifted out of me, and the space it left behind filled up with a soft gray nothing, comfortable as fresh snow, and I could feel myself *wanting* to lie down in it.

First the small loves. The baker on Tallow Street whose bread I bought every morning for six years—I lost his name, then his face, then the fact of him, and the morning-shaped hole in my days sealed over so smoothly you'd never know a morning had been there at all. A woman named Petra whom I'd loved badly and briefly at nineteen; she went like smoke off warm metal. My mother. My mother went early, and I remember thinking, as her face dissolved out of me, *I should be fighting harder than this,* and also *fighting what?* in the same breath, because the forgetting takes the knowledge of the fight along with the thing fought for.

"It's okay," Wren kept saying. He had crawled into my lap at some point, this boy who was also a king-fragment who was also, now, simply a boy, and he was talking to me the way you talk to someone walking a tightrope, steady and low. "It's okay. You're doing it right. Keep looking at me. Whatever else goes, keep looking at me."

"Why?" I asked. And I want to be honest here: I asked it genuinely. Somewhere in the middle of the collection, between losing my mother and losing whatever came after my mother, I looked at this mud-streaked boy in my lap and did not entirely know why he mattered to me. I knew that he did—the way you know a room is important without knowing what happened in it. But the *why* had been collected. Page four hundred and eleven, maybe. Or four-twelve.

He flinched like I'd hit him.

"You're teaching me to lie," he said. "On the road. You said the best lies are mostly true, and then you taught me to cheat at cards, and you said—" his voice cracked, and he pushed through it the way I'd taught him to push through hard words, "—you said nobody gets to decide what Wren is except Wren. You said that."

"Wren," I repeated. The name sat in my mouth like a coin from a country I'd never visited. Familiar shape. No history. "Is that your name?"

"It's *my* name." Fierce, wet-eyed, furious. "You gave it to me."

"Did I?" I looked at the ledger, still turning, still patient. "It seems like the kind of thing I'd do."

* * *

Here is what I lost, in order, and I am writing it down now in the only hand left to me—this strange floating hand, this voice with nowhere to live—because somebody should keep the account. Keeping accounts is, apparently, the one thing about me that survives everything.

The guild's common room. The table with the wobble we never fixed because fixing it would have cost a favor we were saving. Ede Rusk laughing with her head tipped back. Sable Vane saying "entries close at dusk, Kavanagh" in a voice that was ninety percent threat and ten percent love, and me learning, over fifteen years, to hear the ten percent.

Sable going last of all. She went slowly, grudgingly, the way she'd done everything—each memory of her had to be pried loose, and I felt the ledger work for it, felt actual friction, as though her ghost were arguing terms from beyond the knife. When she finally left me I was crying without knowing why, which should have frightened me and instead just felt like weather.

The road to the capital. Teaching Wren to eat soup without slurping like a drain. Him falling asleep against my shoulder reciting a dead king's coronation oath in pre-Founding speech, syllables older than kingdoms, and me thinking: *this child is made of someone else's life, and somehow he is becoming his own.* The gate that remembered my name. The market where nothing was sold. The ferry crossing. All of it, page by page, gone the way frost goes off a window—not destroyed, just returned to the general gray.

And underneath all of it, the great load-bearing loss: the guild itself. The Guild of the Ledgered Veil. Its purpose. Its founding bargain. The reason any of this had happened at all. That went near the end—it was too big to go early—and when it went I was suddenly a person sitting in a strange stone room beside a sleeping king, holding a child I couldn't name, next to a book I couldn't read, and I had no idea why any of us were here.

I said so. Out loud. "Why are we here?"

Wren made a noise I will describe even though describing hurts: it was the noise of someone watching a house burn down from across a river, able to see everything, able to save nothing.

"You don't remember," he said. Not a question.

"Remember what?"

He looked at me for a long time. Eight years old, and doing the arithmetic that adults spend whole lives avoiding: *the person who loved me is still sitting here, but the loving has been sold.*

"You found me," he said finally. "In the rain. You took me in. That's all you need to remember. That's enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get." And then, because I had taught him that the best lies are mostly true: "Come on. We should get the king somewhere safe. He'd want—someone should look after him. Someone should write down what happened here."

Something in me moved toward that. Write it down. Yes. That felt like mine, whatever *mine* meant anymore. "I'm good at writing things down," I heard myself say. "I think. I feel like I'm good at that."

"You are," Wren said. "You're the best there ever was."

* * *

The last page turned near dawn.

I know because light was coming through the high windows of the ruined throne room, gray-gold and indifferent, catching the dust the unmaking had left hanging in the air like the aftermath of a sneeze from God. Aldric was awake by then, sitting propped against the dais, watching us with the hollow-eyed caution of a man who has just gotten himself back and doesn't trust the warranty.

The ledger spoke its final line. "Account settled. Collateral received in full. The debt of the Veil is discharged." A pause, and then—for the first and only time in my long acquaintance with it—the ledger sounded almost gentle. "Keeper Kavanagh. For the record: the entries were well kept."

"Was I good?" I asked it. I was crying. I want to note that clearly, because it matters: I was crying, and I did not know why, and I asked a book whether I had been good, the way a child asks a wall.

"The ledger does not editorialize."

"That's an editorial."

The book closed itself. The gray snow inside me settled. And Wren took my face in both his hands—he had to stand on his knees to do it—and said, very carefully, in the tone of someone placing the last stone on a cairn:

"My name is Wren. Your name is Noor. You found me in the rain. Everything else, we'll start again."

"Wren," I said. Testing it. "Noor. Those are names, all right."

"Do you want to know a secret?" he said, and his eyes were streaming and he was smiling anyway, and I thought, *whoever this child is, I would follow him anywhere, and I don't know why, and maybe not-knowing-why is its own kind of love, the clean kind, the kind with no debts attached.*

"What secret?"

"I remember everything," he said. "All of it. Every page they took, I remember. Somebody has to, and I'm the only one left who can." He wiped his nose on his sleeve, which I had definitely, at some point, told him not to do—I could feel the shape of having said it, even with the words themselves long gone. "So it's not really lost. It's just kept somewhere new. That's all remembering ever is."

Outside, far off toward the city gates, horns were sounding—the usurper's armies halting, confused, their orders dissolving with the thing that wrote them. Somewhere in that confusion a kingdom was going to need putting back together, and the man who would have to do it was sitting on his own steps trying to remember how to be one person.

But that was his problem. Mine, at that moment, was smaller and larger: a boy whose name meant nothing to me was holding my hand, and dawn was coming up, and I noticed—I noticed this with the strange clarity of the emptied-out—that I was not afraid.

I didn't know what I'd paid. I didn't know what I'd lost. But I noticed that my hand fit around his like it had been practicing for years.

"Hungry?" I asked him. It seemed like the right question. It seemed like the beginning of something.

Wren laughed—wet, cracked, real. "Starving," he said. "You always ask me that."

"I do?"

"You have no idea," he said, "how much you do," and behind us the ledger lay closed and blank, waiting for a new hand.
