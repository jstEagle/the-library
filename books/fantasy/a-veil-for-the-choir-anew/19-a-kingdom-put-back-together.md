## 19. A Kingdom Put Back Together

The first thing I did with my own hands again was spill wine on a treaty.

I want that recorded somewhere, because the chroniclers will insist otherwise. They will say King Aldric the Restored rose from his sickbed already wise, already whole, and governed from that hour forward with a gravity befitting his ordeal. What actually happened is that I sat down across from an envoy of the free cities, reached for my cup with a hand that still trembled like a plucked string, and turned forty years of careful diplomacy into a red stain across the Salt Road accords.

Envoy Corvane looked at the blot for a long moment. Then she said, "I've negotiated with three kings, Your Majesty. You're the first to bleed on the table. It's oddly reassuring."

"Give it a week," I said. "I'll find ways to disappoint you that require no wine at all."

Here is what no one tells you about being put back together: you do not feel mended. You feel *seamed*. There are places in me where something was torn out and stitched back in by hands I will never meet, and when the weather changes—or when I pass the nursery corridor, or hear a child singing in the lower court—those seams pull. I do not know why. That is the shape of my new life, mostly. Not knowing why.

I had been hollow for eleven years. I know this now the way you know a scar: by touch, not memory. For eleven years there were hours missing from me, days, seasons. Decrees issued through my mouth in a voice that sounded like mine and was not. Armies marched under my seal toward cities whose names I could not have found on a map if you'd asked me to point—and no one asked, because kings who ask questions get replaced, and some part of me, some animal cellar of myself, understood that perfectly well even while everything above ground went dark.

The crown is gone now. The thing that wore it—I refuse to call it by any grander name than *the thing*—came apart in the throne room three nights ago, unmade by a word in a language older than kingdoms, spoken by a boy I am told is mine. A fragment of me, cast off like a rotten rafter, walking around in the world wearing mud and hunger and a name like a bird's.

Wren.

They tell me that's his name. It means nothing to me. That is the cruelest arithmetic of my restoration: everyone in this palace can see what I lost, and I cannot. I look at the boy and I feel—God help me, I feel *gratitude*, enormous and directionless, like weather. It blows through me looking for somewhere to land and finds nothing. No face attaches to it. No debt has a creditor. I am grateful the way a man is grateful for rain: generally, stupidly, at whatever sky happens to be overhead.

Corvane cleared her throat. I realized I had been staring at the wine blot for the better part of a minute, watching it soak into the word *perpetual*.

"Your Majesty," she said gently, "if the terms need another day—"

"The terms don't need another day." I pushed the treaty back across the table so the blot faced her, because I had decided something while staring at it, and I find that honesty makes a better opening move than dignity. "But before your clerks copy this out clean, I want one amendment, and I want it witnessed."

She waited. She had the patience of someone who had spent her career across tables from men with crowns, which is to say she could wait like stone waits.

"I want it written into the accord," I said, "that the crown may not command an army without a council countersignature. Ever. Including mine. Especially mine."

A pause. Somewhere behind her, her clerk's pen stopped moving, which in a bureaucrat is the equivalent of a gasp.

"You're asking us to leash you," Corvane said.

"I'm asking you to help me build the leash *before* either of us trusts me enough to think I don't need one." I spread my hands. I noticed they were still trembling and decided to let them. Let her see it. "Envoy, eleven years ago something got inside my government because one man's will was allowed to be final. I don't remember those years. I will never remember them. Which means I am exactly as dangerous as any other king who believes his own goodness—and less honest about it, because at least they have their memories to lie to themselves with. So yes. Leash me. Write it in ink your grandchildren can read."

Corvane studied me for a long moment, and then she did something I suspect she had never done in an audience with a king. She laughed—a short, startled bark, quickly strangled, but real.

"They said the usurper's host broke against the free cities' walls last night," she said. "They said a captain named Rusk held the breach with borrowed courage and half her militia standing. And they said the orders recalling the army came from the palace within the same hour, sealed and signed, as if the hand that sent the wolves also called them home." Her eyes narrowed—not hostile; measuring. "Which hand was that, Your Majesty?"

"Mine," I said. "For the first time in eleven years. And I signed it with a hand that didn't stop shaking until the ink was dry."

* * *

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and old fear.

I convened them at dawn—all of them, including the ones who had spent the past decade perfecting the art of not meeting my eye. Lord Chancellor Merrivale arrived first, because Merrivale always arrives first; survival is a punctual discipline. He stood when I entered, and the others followed, and I made them stay standing a moment longer than courtesy required, not out of vanity but because I wanted to watch who flinched.

Merrivale flinched. Good. I noted it and moved on.

"Gentlemen. Ladies." I took my seat—the seat that had been mine all along, occupied all along, just not *inhabited*. "You served the crown faithfully for eleven years. Some of you served the thing wearing it. I have no way to tell the difference, and neither, frankly, do you. So we're going to skip the purges."

That woke them up. Purges are the theater kings are expected to perform; mercy, done deliberately, frightens courtiers far more.

"Merrivale," I said. "In the spring of the fourth year of my—absence—there was a decree relocating the granary reserves from Thornmere to the coastal garrisons. Thornmere starved that winter. Find me every document connected to that decision. Every signature, every marginal note, every courier log. Don't burn anything. Don't lose anything. If your name appears anywhere in the chain, bring those pages to me *personally*, unannounced, and we'll discuss what your name was doing there."

He bowed very low. Low enough that I couldn't see his face, which I suspected was the entire purpose of the bow.

"To the rest of you: the same rule applies. I am not going to ask who knew what, because the answer would be *everyone knew something*, and the thing that ruled through me was clever enough to make sure each of you only ever knew your own small piece. Complicity by compartment. Very efficient. We're going to dismantle it the same way—one drawer at a time, no bonfires." I let the silence sit. "And one more thing."

They leaned in. Courtiers lean; it's reflexive, like breathing.

"If any of you ever again receives an order from me that you find cruel, or senseless, or simply *wrong*—" I looked around the table, letting each of them feel the weight of it, "—you will come to me and say so. To my face. Even if I gave the order twice. *Especially* then. Because I now know what it is to give orders I cannot remember giving, and I would rather be contradicted by cowards than obeyed by ghosts. Dismissed."

They filed out with the careful blankness of people recalculating everything. All except Merrivale, who paused at the door.

"Your Majesty," he said, without turning around. "Thornmere. Was that... was that you? Or—"

"I don't know," I said.

It was the most honest sentence spoken in that chamber in eleven years. He nodded once, almost gratefully, and left.

* * *

I went down to the infirmary in the afternoon, because I had been told Captain Ede Rusk's messenger had arrived at dawn, half-dead on a lathered horse, carrying word from the free cities' walls.

The messenger was a boy of maybe sixteen with a bandaged arm and the thousand-yard stare of someone who had spent a night holding a breach. He tried to stand when I came in. I pushed him back down with a hand on his shoulder, and he stared up at me with an expression I was beginning to recognize everywhere I went—that stunned, searching look, people trying to reconcile the king in front of them with the king they'd imagined, the tyrant-king, the hollow-king, the king whose armies had burned a border town that traded in guild wares.

"Captain Rusk sends her compliments," the boy managed. "And says—" he swallowed, reciting, clearly having rehearsed this through blood loss, "—she says the wall held, barely, and the withdrawal order came in time, and she doesn't know what changed in the palace but she wants whoever changed it to know the cost. Four hundred dead. Maybe five hundred. She says write that number down somewhere it won't get polished away."

"Tell her I'll carve it myself," I said. "Into the stone over the east gate, if she'll allow it. In letters big enough that no chancellor can ever sand them smooth."

The boy blinked. Then, with the bluntness of the badly wounded, he said: "She also asks if it's true there was a child involved. A boy. People are saying a boy spoke the armies home."

I felt that directionless gratitude blow through me again, looking for its landing place, finding none.

"There was a boy involved," I said carefully. "I'm told he matters a great deal. I couldn't tell you how, or why, or who he is to me. Ask me again in a year. Perhaps by then I'll have earned an answer."

* * *

I found him on the roof of the west tower, which apparently was where he went when the palace grew too loud. Of course it was. I am told I used to go there too, before—before whatever before is. The body remembers its habits even when the mind has been gutted and restuffed.

Wren sat cross-legged near the parapet with his knees drawn up, watching the army camps on the plain below dissolve, regiment by regiment, as my recall orders took hold. From up here the retreat looked almost gentle. Columns of dust instead of columns of spears. From up here you could pretend the four hundred names hadn't happened.

I sat down beside him. He glanced at me sidelong—the quick, wary glance of a child who has learned adults are weather—and went back to watching.

"You're the king," he said. Not a question. His voice was rough from disuse, from years of speaking a language no one alive should have known.

"I'm the king."

"People bow to you."

"Terrifyingly often."

He considered this. "Do you know me?"

And there it was. The question I had been dreading since the throne room, since the word that unmade the thing, since I woke whole in my own body with seams pulling in places I couldn't name. Everyone else had tiptoed around it. Children don't tiptoe. Children walk straight through the middle of the room and ask.

"No," I said.

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that unsurprise hurt worse than any accusation could have.

"They said you were gone a long time," he said. "The piece of you that's me. They said the crown ate it."

"So I'm told."

"And you don't remember anything. Not the guild, not the girl with the ledgers, not—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening. He had been learning, I gathered, to keep certain things behind his teeth. "Nothing."

"Not nothing." I looked out at the dissolving camps, because it was easier than looking at him. "I remember how to read. How to ride. How it feels to stand in a room full of people and be entirely alone. I remember griefs I can't attach to anyone, and joys with no occasion, and—" I hesitated. Honesty, I had promised myself. With this boy above all. "And there's a gratitude in me with no address on it. It's been there since I woke. It's very large and I don't know who to give it to."

He was quiet for a long time. Below us, a supply train crawled north along the Salt Road, going home.

"It was a girl," he said finally. "With ledgers. She paid for you." He said it flatly, the way children deliver unbearable things, because they haven't yet learned the adult trick of wrapping them in ceremony. "She paid everything. And she doesn't remember paying. She doesn't remember me." His voice caught on that last part, just slightly, and he covered it by scuffing his boot against the roof tiles.

I wanted to say something kingly and comforting. What came out instead was:

"Then we'll both have to carry what she dropped. Between the two of us, we might manage half a memory apiece."

He looked at me then—really looked, for the first time—and something passed between us that had no words in either of our languages. Recognition without recollection. Two halves of a shattered thing regarding each other across the gap, agreeing, silently, to stand on opposite rims of it and call it a whole.

"She'll knock," Wren said at last, to the horizon more than to me. "At the guild door. They said she would. She'll start over and she won't know why she's starting, and she'll be happy anyway. That's the worst part." He wiped his nose with his sleeve, fiercely, like a soldier. "Being happy anyway."

"Wren." He flinched slightly at his own name—I was learning to notice that; the name meant more to him than to me, and hearing it in my mouth was like hearing a stranger use it. "Whatever she paid for. Whatever I was worth. I intend to make it count. Not because I remember being worth it—but because everyone else does, and their remembering will have to be enough until mine comes back. If it ever does."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then their remembering will simply have to be enough forever." I shrugged. "Kings are used to borrowing. Courage. Loyalty. Legitimacy. One more borrowed thing won't kill me. Apparently nothing else has."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh, strangled halfway, the way children strangle laughs when laughing feels like betrayal. But it was there. It counted.

Below, the last of the campfires winked out. The plain went dark and quiet, and somewhere on a road south of here, a stranger with no memories was walking toward a door she'd never seen, about to knock twice.

I didn't know that yet. But I felt it—the way you feel a seam pull before the weather turns. Something ending somewhere. Something beginning.

"Come inside," I said, standing, offering him my unsteady hand. "It's cold, and tomorrow I have to be a king in front of witnesses, and I'm told I'm much better at it when I've slept."

He took my hand. His grip was small and fierce, like something determined to hold on.

"What kind of king?" he asked.

"A distrusted one," I said. "By design. It's the only kind I trust."

We went down together, out of the wind, into the long work of putting a kingdom back together—one drawer at a time, no bonfires, four hundred names carved deep enough to outlast us both.
