## 21. A Stranger at the Threshold

The city of Corvess has three hundred gates, by the count of anyone who counts such things, and I have walked through all of them in the past year without once knowing why.

That's not quite true. I know *why* I walk. Everyone walks for a reason — bread, work, love, escape from creditors. What I don't know is why my feet choose the streets they choose. There is a pull in me, faint and constant, like a fishhook set so long ago the flesh has grown around it. It doesn't hurt. It just *points*. And this morning it pointed down a crooked lane behind the tanneries, where the smell is honest if nothing else is, and stopped me in front of a door I have passed — or failed to pass — a hundred times without ever seeing it.

I see it now. That's the strange part. The door was always there; I can feel that the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. Grey wood, iron hinges gone green with age, a knocker shaped like an open book with its pages worn smooth by centuries of hands. Above it, carved shallow enough that rain had nearly erased it, two words:

*THE VEIL.*

I stood there a long while, letting the tannery reek wash over me, feeling like a man who has come home to a house he has never entered. My chest did something complicated. Grief, maybe, though grief for what? For whom? You cannot mourn a door. And yet my eyes were wet, and I wiped them on my sleeve and told myself it was the smoke from the curing yards, because I am a practical person and practical people do not weep at architecture.

Here is what I know about myself, which is to say here is everything:

My name is Noor Kavanagh. I am twenty-two years old. I arrived in Corvess a year ago with no money, no family, no history worth the telling, and a head full of nothing but fog where a life should be. The physickers called it a wasting of memory. The priests called it a trial. A kind innkeeper called it Tuesday and gave me soup, and I have been paying off that soup ever since by washing dishes in her kitchen, which suits me fine, because dishes are simple. Dishes do not ask where you came from. Dishes do not look at you with pity when you say "I don't remember" for the four-hundredth time.

But I am good with accounts. That survived whatever took the rest. Numbers sit in my head like tame birds. Give me a ledger and a candle and I am content until dawn. The innkeeper says I tally in my sleep sometimes, muttering figures she swears are too large for any honest trade — sums in the thousands, in units she doesn't recognize. Years, I think, though I couldn't say why. Once I woke myself up whispering a word over and over, a word like a key turning in a lock I couldn't find.

*Ledgered.* Whatever that means.

And once — only once — I dreamed of a child. Mud-caked, small, solemn as a magistrate, looking up at me across some dim room full of shelves. In the dream I knew him the way you know your own hands. In the dream I would have burned down the world for him. I woke with tears on my face and a name dissolving on my lips, and I sat in the dark of my rented room and cried for a boy I could not name and had never met, and then I got up and washed my face and went to work, because that is what practical people do.

So. This morning the hook in my chest pulled, and I followed it, and now here I stand before a door that says THE VEIL, and my heart is hammering like a debtor at a creditor's own gate.

There is a second knocker-mark beside the first, I notice. Fainter, older, half-filled with grime. Two knocks, pressed into the wood by repetition, like a ritual worn into stone by kneeling pilgrims. I don't know how I know this, but I know it the way I know numbers: absolutely, without evidence, without permission.

Knock twice.

The thought arrives in my own voice, which is somehow worse than if it had arrived in someone else's. I look left down the lane. Right. A tanner's boy stares at me, bored. Nobody cares about the madwoman talking to doors.

"Right," I tell the door. "You'd better be worth it."

I knock twice.

* * *

The sound goes through me like cold water. Not because it's loud — it isn't. Because it's *familiar*, in the way my own heartbeat is familiar, and I realize I have knocked on this door before. Not today. Not in this year. Somewhere in the fog, in the drowned country of whatever I lost, these two knocks are written, and hearing them again is like hearing your childhood lullaby sung in a language you've forgotten you speak.

A long silence. Then footsteps — unhurried, measured, the steps of someone who has answered this door ten thousand times and expects to answer it ten thousand more.

The door opens.

An old woman stands there. Silver hair cropped short, eyes grey as ledger-paper, a smudge of ink on one knuckle that no amount of living will ever fully remove. She looks at me, and I watch something move through her face — quick, swallowed whole, gone before it finishes arriving. Recognition, I'd call it, if I believed in it. But she's never seen me. Her face settles into polite inquiry, the mask of a professional.

"Yes?" she says.

"I—" And here is the humiliating part: I prepared a speech all the way down the lane, and it has evaporated. "I don't know," I finish honestly. "I was walking, and I stopped here, and I knocked. I'm sorry. I don't know why."

Most people would close the door on that. Most people would have closed it on the knocking itself. But this woman tilts her head, and her eyes do a thing I recognize without understanding — they flick down, briefly, as if consulting a page only she can see.

"Twice," she says. "You knocked twice."

"It seemed right."

"It usually does." She steps back and holds the door wide. "Come in out of the tannery stink, Noor Kavanagh. The soup's on and the accounts won't keep forever."

I freeze on the threshold. The cold water feeling again, all the way through.

"I didn't tell you my name."

"No," she agrees, unbothered, already turning down the hall. "You really didn't."

* * *

The inside of the guildhouse is dim and smells of candlewax, old paper, and ink — a smell that hits me like a fist made entirely of homesickness. Shelves climb every wall, crammed with books whose spines are lettered in scripts I half-recognize, the way you half-recognize a face from a dream. There's a long table under a skylight furred with dust, scattered with ledgers, and a cold hearth, and a staircase vanishing upward into shadow, and everywhere — everywhere — the sense of a place built to hold things that shouldn't fit anywhere.

"You may as well stare," the woman says. "Everyone does. I'm Maren. Keeper of the outer accounts, which sounds grander than it is. Sit." She pushes a bowl of soup across the table at me without asking whether I want it. She has correctly guessed that I want it. I decide immediately that I would follow this woman into a fire.

"Maren," I say, around a mouthful of barley and grace. "Is this a guild? The carving said—"

"The Veil, yes." She sits opposite me, folding her hands, studying me with the frank patience of someone who has done this many times and expects to do it perhaps once more. "It's a guild. We deal in — well. The guildmaster likes to explain it properly. I'll spare you the lecture. We keep accounts of things other people think can't be counted. Borrowed time. Sold sorrows. Names people would rather forget. That sort of nonsense." She smiles thinly. "You'll think it's nonsense. Most do, right up until it isn't."

"And you're telling a stranger all this because...?"

"Because you knocked twice." She shrugs. "Nobody finds this door by accident, and nobody who finds it on purpose knocks twice unless the door wants them to. The door wanted you, Noor Kavanagh. I'm just the hinge."

"That's not an explanation."

"No," she says cheerfully. "It isn't."

I should leave. Any sensible person would leave. Instead I find myself asking, "Have I been here before?"

Maren goes very still. It lasts only a moment — a held breath, a blink held too long — and then she's moving again, ladling more soup into my bowl as if I'd asked for it, which I hadn't, and which I am nonetheless grateful for.

"What makes you ask that?"

"The smell. It feels like remembering something. Only there's nothing there when I reach for it." I turn my spoon in circles. "I lost my memory. A year ago. Before that, nothing. I woke up on a barge on the Vell with a purse I don't remember filling and a headache I still have. The physickers say the mind sometimes sheds itself like a coat." I laugh, and hate how thin it comes out. "Mine shed everything. So when I say this place feels familiar, I want to know if that's madness or if—"

"It's neither." Maren sets down the ladle with great care, as though it might go off. "Noor. Do you dream? Since the loss. Do you dream of particular things?"

"A child," I say, before I can stop myself, and then the words come out in a rush, years of solitude breaking like a dam. "A mud-caked child, in a room like this one. I don't know his name but I know I loved him. I wake up crying and I don't know why and everyone tells me grief needs an object and mine doesn't seem to have one, it just floats around my chest looking for somewhere to land, and I—"

I stop, because Maren has put her hand over mine on the table, and her eyes are bright, and she is smiling in a way that hurts worse than anything I can remember, which is everything.

"There's someone you should meet," she says softly. "He's been waiting a long time. He keeps saying he'll know you when he sees you. He's been wrong twice." Her grip tightens. "Third time's the tradition."

"Who—"

"He'll want to tell you himself. He's rehearsed it." She rises, wipes her hands on her apron, and pauses at the foot of the stairs. "Fair warning, Noor. He's insufferable now. Whatever else he was — whoever he was to you — he's a seventeen-year-old boy with a title and opinions, and titles make boys worse, not better."

"A title?"

Maren is already climbing the stairs. Over her shoulder, with terrible casualness: "Heir to the kingdom. Don't let it put you off. He hates it too."

She disappears above. I sit alone in the candle-smelling dark with my cooling soup and my hammering heart, and I take stock, the way I always do, because numbers are safe and this moment is anything but.

One: I am sitting in a guild of impossible things, invited by name I never gave.

Two: a child I love without knowing exists upstairs, wearing a crown's shadow, rehearsing words for me.

Three: the hook in my chest has gone quiet for the first time in a year. Not gone — quiet. Like a debt acknowledged. Like a ledger balanced, awaiting only a signature.

Four: I am crying again, and this time I don't blame the tanneries.

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow ones, deliberate, the steps of someone counting each tread to steady himself. And a voice from the shadows above — young, careful, cracked at the edges with something it's trying very hard not to be — saying my name.

"Noor?"

I look up. I don't know him. I have never seen him in my life.

I know him better than I have ever known anyone.

"Wren," I say, and the name comes out of the fog like a lamp, like a hand, like the first entry in a book I haven't opened yet, and the boy stops dead on the stairs with his rehearsed speech dying on his lips, because apparently I was not supposed to know that either.

Neither of us moves. Neither of us breathes. Upstairs, Maren very tactfully closes a door.

"So," Wren says at last, wet-eyed and furious about it, gripping the banister like a drowning man. "You're going to have a lot of questions."

"Yes," I say.

"And I only know about half the answers, and half of those are probably wrong."

"Also yes."

"And the rest is in the ledgers." His chin comes up, and there — beneath the title and the grief and the terrible teenage dignity — is something stubborn and bright that I would burn cities for, I am suddenly, completely sure of it. "The ledgers remember everything, Noor. Even when we can't."

I look at the long table, at the ledgers lying open under their skin of dust, at the blank book at the end of the row with its cover uncreased and its first page white as snow. Drawn by nothing I can name — drawn by everything — I rise from my chair and cross the room and pick up the pen lying beside it, and it fits my hand like it was cast from it.

"Well then," I say. "Someone should start writing."
