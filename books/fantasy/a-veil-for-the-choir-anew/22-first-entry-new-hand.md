## 22. First Entry, New Hand

The pen is heavier than I expected.

That's a foolish thing to think—pens weigh what pens weigh—but this one has been in other hands before mine, and hands leave something behind the way footsteps do on stairs. The nib is silver, worn to a slight lean to the left, as if every previous keeper had been hurrying. I hold it the way you'd hold a bird that might still be alive.

The ledger open before me is new. Not new the way a thing is new from a shop—new the way morning is new, which is to say it has been here all along and has only just decided to begin. Its pages are cream-colored and faintly warm, like bread. When I breathe on them, they smell of candle smoke and river water and, very faintly, of salt. I don't know why salt. I have decided not to be afraid of not knowing why. That decision took me most of the night, so I'd appreciate if no one tested it too soon.

My name is Noor Kavanagh. I know this because it's written on the card pinned to my coat, in handwriting that isn't mine but feels like it should be: *Noor Kavanagh — keeper, first class, returned.* Underneath, someone has added, in smaller letters, *do not overwhelm.*

I have been overwhelmed twice already today. Once by the staircase, which has more landings than floors and seems to rearrange itself when it thinks I'm not looking. Once by a woman with iron-gray hair who came down the hall, saw me sitting in the counting room, stopped dead, put her hand over her mouth, and then said, "You must be the new one," in a voice that cracked exactly once, in the middle, like ice on a pond.

"Yes," I said. "I suppose I am."

"You'll want tea," she said, and fled, and I heard her crying in the corridor for a good minute before she composed herself and brought back a tray with far too many things on it, as though feeding me were the only grief she was permitted to express.

I don't know her name. I don't know anyone's name. This is apparently by design, though no one will tell me the design, and I've learned—in three days, which is the whole of my remembered life—to stop asking directly. People's faces go strange when I ask. They look at me the way you look at a house where someone died, kindly and carefully, keeping their voices low.

Here is what I know:

I woke four days ago in a bed in this building, in a room with a window overlooking a courtyard where a single tree grows at an angle, leaning east like it's listening for something. There was a card with my name. There were clothes that fit. There was a letter, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, addressed simply to *The New Keeper*, and the letter said: *You have been hired. You have always been hired. Begin at the beginning, which is an entry, which is a door, which is a knock.*

And there was a boy.

He wasn't in the room. He was standing outside the door when I opened it, a boy of perhaps ten or eleven with mud-colored hair and eyes the exact gray of a sky deciding about rain, and when he saw me he did not introduce himself or ask how I was feeling, which everyone else in this building insists on doing. He just looked at me for a long moment and said, "You're shorter than I remembered."

"I'm sorry?" I said.

"Don't be sorry," he said. "It's not your fault." And then he smiled, and the smile was wrong—not wrong like a lie, wrong like a key that almost fits, and my chest did something painful and inexplicable, and I said, "Do I know you?"

"No," he said, too quickly, and then, watching my face, more slowly: "Not yet."

His name, I have since learned, is Wren. He lives here, in the room across from mine. He is, according to the iron-haired woman—who is called Maren, and who is either the guildmistress or pretending very hard to be one—the guild's "ward and warder both," whatever that means. He follows me through the halls at a polite distance. He watches me take my meals. When I drop something—and I drop a great many things, because my hands haven't decided yet whether they belong to me—he is always the first to pick it up, and he always holds it out with both hands, formally, like returning a sword.

Yesterday I caught him watching me write. I was practicing, because the letter said to begin with an entry and I didn't know what an entry was, so I was copying words out of the old ledgers stacked in the corner of the counting room—*borrowed, one year, repaid in kind; grief, sold by weight, buyer wept correctly; name, unspoken, held in trust*—and Wren stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and his jaw tight, and when I looked up he said, "Your hand is different."

"Is it?"

"It used to slant the other way." Then he went pale, as if he'd said something forbidden, and left, and I sat there for a long time looking at my own handwriting, which slants to the right, unlike the pen, which remembers a leftward lean.

I should explain what this place is, except I can't, entirely, because I only half understand it myself. It is called the Guild of the Ledgered Veil—I found the name carved above the door, worn nearly smooth—and it trades in impossible things. That part I understood immediately, the way you understand water when you're drowning in it. The ledgers in the counting room are full of transactions that cannot be true and are nevertheless recorded in meticulous, beautiful hands: years borrowed against futures; silences bought and sold; a man's shadow, pawned twice. The older volumes are written in scripts I shouldn't be able to read and can, the way I shouldn't be able to find my way through these shifting corridors and do, the way I knew which floorboard in my room creaks before I'd ever stepped on it.

Maren says I was "trained elsewhere." She says it smoothly. She says it the way people say things they have rehearsed, and she looks at Wren when she says it, and Wren looks at the floor, and nobody meets anybody's eyes, and the whole building hums with a politeness so dense it's nearly a sound.

There are others here too. An old man called Tam who repairs clocks that don't keep time but keep something else. A pair of twins who never speak aloud and communicate with me exclusively through notes that say things like *the third stair misses you* and *don't let them give you the green ink.* A young woman named Odile who showed me the wards on the doors—lines of script burned into the wood, ancient and patient—and told me, "They like you. That's unusual. They usually tolerate us."

"Tolerate?"

"We're staff," she said, with a small shrug. "You're—" and then she stopped herself, pressed her lips together, and changed the subject to lunch.

Everyone keeps stopping themselves. Everyone keeps almost saying something. The building is full of sentences with their ends bitten off, and I have begun to collect them, secretly, the way some children collect stones: *you used to*, *before the*, *when we lost*. I write them down in the margins of my practice pages. If I am ever going to understand this place, I suspect it will be through its unfinished sentences.

But that isn't why I'm here tonight, alone in the counting room with a candle burning down and the fresh ledger open in front of me.

I'm here because of the dream.

I dreamed of a throne room. I have never seen a throne room—I'm fairly confident of that; my entire confident past is four days long—but I dreamed it in perfect detail: pillars like frozen fountains, banners gone gray with dust, and a king on a throne with his head in his hands, weeping, and I was walking toward him through a crowd that couldn't see me, carrying a book under my arm, and beside me walked a boy whose hand fit inside mine exactly, as if the shape of my hand had been made to order around his.

In the dream I knew everything. I knew what the book was for. I knew what it would cost. I knew the boy's laugh and the guildmistress's scolding voice and the taste of the tea Maren makes with too much honey, and I knew that I loved all of it, and I knew that knowing was the price, and I paid it anyway, gladly, page by page—

and I woke up crying, in a bed in a room in a building I've lived in for four days, grieving people whose names I have never learned, and the strange thing, the thing I keep turning over like a coin I can't spend, is that the grief doesn't feel like a wound. It feels like a scar. It hurts, but it's healed. Whatever happened to me happened, and ended, and ended well—or ended as well as anything ends—and what's left is not loss exactly. What's left is capacity. Room. The way a field is empty after harvest but still a field.

Wren was sitting in the hallway when I came out of my room, wrapped in a blanket, awake. Of course he was. I'm beginning to suspect the boy doesn't sleep so much as keep watch lying down.

"You dreamed it," he said. Not a question.

"I dreamed something."

"The throne room." His voice was careful, flat, the voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "The king. The book."

"Maybe."

"Noor." He said my name like it mattered, like it was a small fire he was trying to keep lit in wind. "Do you remember why you're crying?"

I thought about lying to him. I don't know why—I've known him four days—but I felt that lying to Wren would be a serious crime, possibly the only crime this building takes seriously. So I said, "No. But I remember that it was worth it."

Something moved across his face then, quick as a fish under ice—grief and relief fighting for the same window—and he nodded, once, and said, "Okay," and got up, and paused, and said, "The tea Maren makes has too much honey in it. You always complained. You should complain tomorrow. She'll like that."

"I always complained?"

"It's one of your best qualities," he said, and went into his room, and closed the door, and I stood in the hallway for a while with wet cheeks and dry eyes, which is a trick I didn't know I could do.

So. Tonight. The ledger.

Maren gave it to me at supper without ceremony—a plain black volume, unmarked, its spine stiff with newness—and said, "Every keeper begins with a first entry. Yours is overdue." And Wren, across the table, went very still, the way he does, and Maren looked at him, and he looked at his plate, and the ends of sentences were bitten off all around, and I took the book and thanked her and pretended not to notice any of it, which is apparently also one of my qualities.

Now the candle is short and the house is quiet and the pen is in my hand, and I understand—without being told, the way I understand the corridors—that the first entry matters. That somewhere in the rules of this place, unwritten or written in languages I can read without learning, the first entry is a kind of keystone. Everything built after it leans on it. So it should be true. It should be simple. It should be mine.

I dip the pen. The ink is black, not green—the twins will be relieved—and it takes the light like still water.

I think about what to write. I think about the throne room, and the king, and the book I carried, and the hand in mine. I think about a guildmistress whose face I can't recall but whose absence has a specific shape, like a chair kept by a window. I think about a captain somewhere east, maybe, whom I never met and somehow miss. I think about the word *salt*, which surfaced yesterday while I was washing my hands and made me stand at the basin until the water went cold.

None of that goes in the entry. That's the old ledger's business, whoever kept it, whatever it cost. This is a new book. This is a new hand. The rules say begin at the beginning, and the beginning, the letter said, is an entry, is a door, is a knock.

So I write what I know. Four days of it. All of it true.

*A child arrived today who seems to know me.*

Then I sit back, and look at the line, and find that it isn't finished—not because the sentence lacks words, but because the day lacked ending. Because even now, as I write, I can hear, faintly, from the direction of the front hall, the sound I have somehow been waiting four days to hear without knowing it: a knock. Two knocks. Polite. Certain. The knock of someone who knows the door will open.

The candle gutters. Down the corridor, a floorboard creaks—third stair, the twins would say, the one that misses people—and I hear small feet hit the floor in the room across from mine, and a door open, and Wren's voice, low and wondering, saying to someone I cannot see: "You came back. You actually—wait here. Wait *here*. I'll get her."

And I am already standing. I am already smiling. My hand knows the way to the door before I do.

Some things, it turns out, survive even forgetting.

I pick up the pen, and beneath the first entry, in a hand that slants—newly, hopefully—to the right, I add the only other thing I am sure of:

*Send them in.*
