## 2. Prayers with Teeth

The candle in the Hollow Chapel had burned down to a thumb's width, and Wren Calloway still could not make herself snuff it.

Below her, in the flooded cellar that the faithful called the chapel and the city called nothing at all, forty-one people knelt on wet stone. Forty-one. She counted them the way she counted everything now — obsessively, the way a woman counts the cracks spreading through glass. Last month there had been twelve. The week before that, four. Before the Answering, before the gods woke like something stirring under floorboards, she had been lucky to fill the room with the smell of tallow and the sound of her own voice reciting liturgies no one believed anymore.

Now they came every night, and they brought their prayers with them like knives.

"Please," whispered Marisol Vane, a laundress whose hands were cracked white from lye, "please let my son's fever break."

Wren had heard that prayer eleven nights running. On the twelfth morning, Tam Vane's fever broke so suddenly that his skin went cold as riverstone, and he did not wake again. The fever was gone. The boy was gone. The prayer had been answered *precisely*.

That was the thing no one understood yet — no one but Wren, who stood each night at the lip of the cellar steps and watched hope move through her congregation like a tide carrying broken things to shore. The gods of old Vellamere had returned. The candles guttered toward the ceiling when certain names were spoken. Water in the font moved against its own stillness. And prayers were answered — truly answered, for the first time in seven years, since the Ban had driven the pantheon into silence and the Inquisition had driven the faithful into cellars.

But the answers had teeth.

Old Tomasin prayed for work, and found his employer dead three days later, leaving him the shop — and the shop's debts, and the widow's grief to carry past his door every morning. A girl named Effie prayed to be beautiful, and woke with a face so luminous that men followed her home, and worse than men. The cooper Aldous prayed for rain for his dying orchard, and got a flood that took his neighbor's house and two of the neighbor's children, and Aldous had not spoken since, only sat in the third pew with his hands open in his lap like a man waiting for someone to return what they'd borrowed.

*Be careful what you ask for.* It was an old proverb, a grandmother's proverb, meant to be funny. Now it was scripture.

"Mother Calloway." Marisol had come up the stairs without Wren hearing, her shawl dark with cellar damp. Her eyes were red-rimmed and terribly bright. "Tomasin says you can teach us how to pray *safely*. How to ask for small things. He says the old rites had — protections. Guardrails."

Wren looked at this woman who had lost her son to the precision of heaven, and felt the lie rise in her throat and die there.

"There are no guardrails," she said gently. "There never were. The old priests told us there were because the truth would have emptied the temples. The gods hear everything, Marisol. They always did. What's changed is—" She stopped. What *had* changed? That was the question that kept her kneeling past midnight, palms pressed to cold stone, asking nothing, absolutely nothing, because she had seen what happened to those who asked.

What had changed was that something down in the deep places of the world had opened its eyes after seven years of sleep, and it was hungry, or eager, or lonely — she couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing was its own kind of prayer, the kind that answered itself in nightmares.

"They're listening," Marisol said. It wasn't a question. "Aren't they. Really listening."

"Yes."

"Then why does it feel like being *eaten*?"

Wren had no answer. She put her hand on the woman's shoulder and gave her the only sacrament left: presence without promise. Below them, the congregation murmured its dangerous litany, and Wren listened to the sound of forty-one people begging a god with teeth to be gentle.

* * *

The Inquisition came on a Thursday, which surprised her; she had expected them on a holy day, when the cellars would be full.

They came at dawn, when the fog off the Vell lay thick as wool over the Warren district, and they came quietly — no drums, no heralds, just six gray-cloaked figures moving through the streets with the unhurried certainty of people who already knew where everything was. Wren watched from the shuttered window of the chandler's loft where she slept, and her stomach turned to stone.

They started with Effie.

Wren saw the beautiful girl pulled from her doorway into the fog, saw the gray cloak close around her like a hand closing around a moth. Effie didn't scream. That was the worst part. She went the way people go when they have been expecting it — shoulders bowed, feet shuffling, one glance back at the street where men had once followed her just to look at her face. One of the Inquisitors carried a ledger. Wren watched him open it, watch him run a gloved finger down a column, watch him nod.

*A list.* They had a list of the answered.

By noon the bells of the Cathedral of the Lawful Light were ringing the pattern that meant *quarantine of souls*, a sound Vellamere hadn't heard since the Ban, since the last time the city had decided that faith itself was a contagion. By dusk, Wren had walked the length of the Warren and counted nine empty beds, nine families sitting silent behind their doors, nine stories that all ended the same way: *gray cloaks, a ledger, gone.*

They were calling them *the answered*. She heard the word in the market, spat like a diagnosis. The crier at Tanner's Gate read the proclamation in a voice polished smooth by repetition: *"All persons who have received supernatural intercession are required to present themselves for examination. Concealment of the afflicted constitutes heresy against the Lawful Light."*

*Afflicted.* As if miracles were a disease. Wren stood in the crowd with her hood up and her heart hammering, and understood with terrible clarity that the Inquisition was right about exactly one thing: something was spreading through her congregation. Not affliction. Attention. The gods had noticed Vellamere, and everything the gods noticed began to change shape, and the Inquisition — which had spent seven years building a city with no room in it for divinity — had noticed *that.*

She thought of her forty-one souls in the flooded cellar. Forty-one names on some clerk's ledger by week's end, if she did nothing.

She had to do something. But every road she knew led somewhere watched.

Except one.

* * *

The rumor came to her secondhand, then third, wearing different clothes each time until she almost didn't recognize it. But the bones of it stayed the same: there was a charm-seller in the night market who sold luck and love-knots and little folded paper wards, harmless things, legal-adjacent things, the kind of magic the Ban permitted because it was too small to bother outlawing. And the rumor said — whispered, always whispered — that this charm-seller had been a cartographer once. Before the Ban. That they had charted the unmapped realms, the shifting roads outside the world, back when such charting was a sacred science instead of a memory. That they knew ways in and out of Vellamere that appeared on no map the Inquisition possessed.

*Old roads,* the rumor said. *Roads the gray cloaks can't follow.*

Wren found the stall on her third night of searching, in the crook of the night market where Lamplighters' Row bent around the old customs house, and she knew it before she saw the wares, because the seller stood apart from their trade the way a lighthouse stands apart from the sea.

Sebastian Hartwell. She would have known that silhouette anywhere — the slight forward lean, as if perpetually about to set out on a journey, the hands that were always moving, always folding, always making something out of nothing. Seven years since she'd last seen those hands, and they were doing now what they had always done: working. Folding a paper charm along creases worn soft with repetition, sealing it with a breath, setting it among dozens of its brothers on the striped cloth of the stall.

She stood in the shadow of the customs house and let herself look, because looking cost nothing and she had missed it more than she would ever say aloud. The lantern light caught the same face she had loved and left and mourned in equal measure — older now, harder at the edges, with a new wariness around the eyes that seven years of selling small magics in a hostile city would carve into anyone. A child stood at the stall's edge, coins clutched in her fist, and Wren watched Sebastian crouch to the girl's height, watch them speak softly, watch them press a charm into the small palm and fold the small fingers over it and add, quietly, words Wren couldn't hear but recognized anyway: a blessing. An old one. Pre-Ban phrasing, the kind that hadn't been spoken openly in this city since the temples fell.

*You never stopped believing either,* Wren thought, and her chest ached with it. *You just learned to sell your faith in pieces so no one would take the whole.*

She waited until the market thinned, until the child had gone and the last customers drifted away trailing smells of fried dough and lamp oil, until Sebastian began packing the striped cloth with the economy of long practice. Then she stepped out of the shadows, and watched the whole body of them go still — that particular stillness, total and instantaneous, of a person who has survived by knowing when they've been seen.

"Wren." Her name in that voice, low and careful, stripped of everything it used to carry. "Seven years."

"Seven years," she agreed. "And I need to be quick, because there are gray cloaks on my street and a ledger with my congregation's names in it, and I'm told you know roads that aren't on any map."

Something moved behind Sebastian's eyes — she saw it clearly even across the distance between stalls, saw the flinch and the calculation and, beneath both, briefly, unbearably, the tenderness they had both agreed to bury. Then the wariness won, as she'd known it would.

"I sell charms," Sebastian said. "Paper and thread and wishful thinking. Whatever you've heard—"

"I've heard you charted the unmapped realms." She came closer, close enough to see the lantern flame reflected twice in their eyes. "I've heard you drew maps of places that don't hold still. And I'm telling you, Sebastian, as a priestess of a pantheon that has just woken up hungry — the gods are answering prayers in Vellamere, and the answers are killing people, and the Inquisition is taking the answered away in gray cloaks, and I have forty-one souls in a cellar who will be next unless I can find a road out of this city that doesn't exist."

The market noise seemed to fall away between them. Somewhere a fiddle played; somewhere a fishmonger shouted his last prices of the night. Sebastian looked at her for a long moment, and Wren saw the exact instant the decision was made — not in the eyes but in the hands, which went quiet at last, stilling against the folded paper charms as though holding them down.

"You'd better come inside," Sebastian said. "And Wren — the answer to your prayers is going to be worse than you think."

Behind them, unnoticed, unremarked, the paper charms on the striped cloth had begun, very faintly, to glow.
