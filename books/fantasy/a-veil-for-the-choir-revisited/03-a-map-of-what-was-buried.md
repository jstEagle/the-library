## 3. A Map of What Was Buried

The knock came at the hour when Vellamere's night market began to fold itself away, lantern by lantern, like a flower deciding it had made a mistake about the sun.

Sebastian was counting coppers into a tin that smelled of beeswax and old smoke — the day's earnings, pitiful as always, three charms sold and two returned by customers who claimed the luck had gone sour. The knock on the shutter of their stall was not a customer's knock. Customers knocked twice, apologetic, half-embarrassed to be buying superstition in a city that had outlawed believing in anything. This knock came once, hard, with the authority of someone who had knocked on many doors and expected all of them to open.

Sebastian knew who it was before they lifted the latch. Some part of them had known for days, ever since the child's prayer at this very stall had been answered by a voice like a bell rung underwater. Wren Calloway had always been able to find Sebastian when she wanted something. It had been one of the things Sebastian loved about her, seven years ago, before love became another thing the Ban had outlawed by extension — too dangerous, too visible, too much like lighting a candle in a room full of spilled lamp oil.

She stood in the lamplight wearing a gray traveling cloak with the hood pushed back, and her hair was shorter than Sebastian remembered, cropped close at the temples the way priestesses of the Choir wore it when they were in mourning or in hiding. Her eyes did the thing they had always done: looked at Sebastian as though reading a map of them, noting where the roads had changed.

"You're harder to find than you used to be," she said.

"I'm exactly where I've always been." Sebastian set down the tin. "You're the one who left."

* * *

They did not speak for a long moment after that, because there was no honest way to continue and neither of them had ever been good at dishonesty with each other. That had been the problem, near the end. Honesty, between a charm-seller and a priestess, in the first year after the Ban, was a kind of slow suicide.

Wren stepped past the counter uninvited — she had never once waited to be invited anywhere in her life — and looked at the charms laid out on the velvet: knotted cords, iron nails wrapped in red thread, little glass vials of river water blessed by nobody in particular. Harmless things. Things that worked only because people needed them to work.

"These are beautiful lies," she said softly.

"They're honest ones. I tell every buyer they do nothing." Sebastian crossed their arms. "Which is more than your gods ever did, back when anyone bothered asking."

That landed the way Sebastian had meant it to land, and immediately regretted it. Wren's face didn't change, but something behind her eyes went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Come inside," Sebastian said, quieter. "Not here."

* * *

The room above the stall was small and smelled of ink, which was the one truth Sebastian's life contained that no customer ever saw. Maps covered every wall — but not maps of Vellamere. Those would have been confiscated years ago, along with everything else. These were maps of elsewhere: the unmapped realms Sebastian had charted in the years before the Ban, when cartography was a holy profession and Sebastian Hartwell had been the youngest journeyman ever licensed by the Cartographers' Guild. The Glasswood with its trees that recorded sound in rings instead of seasons. The Salt Cathedral. The Long Stair, descending into country that appeared on no other map because no other mapmaker had survived the descent.

Wren stood in the middle of the room and turned slowly, and Sebastian watched her take inventory of a life she had been absent from.

"You kept drawing," she said.

"I kept breathing. Same reflex."

"Sebastian." She turned to face them fully. "Three nights ago, a dyer named Osla Vetch prayed for rain on her crops. Her street flooded to the second story. Only her street. Two days ago, a dockhand prayed his brother home from the war barges. The brother walked out of the harbor, alive, dry, with no memory of how he got there — except he keeps saying the water 'handed him back.' Last night a woman prayed her husband's cough away, and he woke up without lungs scarred from thirty years of kiln smoke, healed so completely it frightened the physickers." She paused. "These aren't rumors. I've spoken with all of them. I'm a priestess, Sebastian. Or I was. We kept the old prayers alive underground, quietly, the way you keep your maps. And now the prayers are being *answered*, and the answers have teeth, and the Inquisition is dragging away everyone whose prayer came true."

"The answered," Sebastian said.

"You've heard."

"I've heard the wagons at night." Sebastian sat down heavily on the stool by the desk. "I've also heard something else. A child at my stall prayed for her mother to stop crying. And something answered her. Something I could hear, Wren, and I shouldn't be able to hear anything. My blood has been as quiet as everyone else's since the Ban." They looked up. "Why are you really here? You didn't cross the city at night to trade gossip about miracles."

"No." Wren drew something from inside her cloak and set it on the desk between them. It was a scrap of parchment, old, cracked along its folds — and Sebastian's breath stopped, because they recognized the hand. The compass rose in the corner drawn as a choir of singing mouths. The marginal notation in Cartographers' Cipher, the old tongue of the guild, which fewer than five living people could read.

"This is yours," Wren said. "Or your family's. It was in our archive. It's been in our archive for a hundred years, and three weeks ago the ink started moving."

Sebastian reached for it and stopped, hand hovering. "What does it say?"

"That's why I came to you." Wren's voice dropped. "It says the gods didn't leave, Sebastian. When the Veil went up, when the Ban was written — someone didn't seal the gods away from the world. Someone sealed them away from each other, beneath the city, behind a door that can only be opened from *this* side. And the cipher names the bloodline of the one who holds the key."

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"And?" Sebastian's mouth was dry.

"And the name is Hartwell."

* * *

They went out through the market's skeleton, past stalls folded dark, past the Inquisition's notice boards with their fresh lists of the answered, down streets Sebastian had walked ten thousand times and never once thought of as a map. But that was the sickness of the old craft: once a mapmaker, always a mapmaker. Every gutter was a river line. Every alley a contour. Sebastian had spent seven years pretending otherwise, and now, walking beside Wren with her sleeve brushing theirs, the pretense crumbled like wet paper.

The temple entrance was under the tannery district, behind a wall that Sebastian's grandfather's generation had bricked over — Sebastian knew this because their grandfather had told them, drunk, one winter night: *There's a door in this city, child, and our family is the reason it stays shut.* Sebastian had filed it away as an old man's fable. Old men's fables, it turned out, were just prophecies that hadn't ripened yet.

Wren pried loose the first brick; Sebastian worked the rest free in silence, hands remembering masonry the way hands remember lovers. Behind the wall was a stair, and the stair breathed cold air that smelled of stone and myrrh and something older underneath, something like a forest floor a thousand years drowned.

"You've known where this was your whole life," Wren said. It wasn't quite an accusation.

"I've known where everything in this city is. It's what I do." Sebastian wiped mortar dust on their coat. "Knowing isn't opening."

"No. But drawing might be." She looked back at them, and in the dark her eyes caught light that wasn't there. "Your guild didn't just make maps, Sebastian. Your guild made *doors*. Why do you think the Ban came for cartographers first?"

Because Sebastian had asked themselves that question every night for seven years, they had no answer to give her that wasn't a confession. So they said nothing, and descended.

* * *

The temple was small, which surprised Sebastian — they had imagined vastness, a cathedral of the buried gods. Instead it was a chamber barely larger than their room above the stall, round, its walls covered floor to ceiling in maps. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Parchment and vellum and sheets of hammered gold, layered over each other like feathers, every one drawn in the same hand — the choir-mouth compass rose repeated in every corner.

And at the center of the floor, a door. Not a grand one. A plain iron-banded door, low enough that a person must stoop to pass through it, its wood black with age. Across it, in fresh paint that gleamed wetly as though applied an hour ago, ran a line of Cartographers' Cipher.

"It rewrites itself," Wren whispered. "Every night. The priests who sealed this place wrote a lock, and the lock is a sentence, and the sentence is changing."

Sebastian knelt before it, and their hand rose without permission, fingers tracing the cipher — and the moment skin touched paint, every map on every wall lit up.

Not firelight. Not lamplight. The lines themselves glowed, ink waking like veins remembering blood, and the whole round chamber became a lantern, and Sebastian felt the glow answer something deep in their chest, some door inside them swinging open onto a room they had boarded shut seven years ago.

"Sebastian." Wren's hand found their shoulder. "Your arms."

Sebastian looked down. Beneath their rolled sleeves, faint as watermarks, lines were rising along their forearms — thin, branching, precise as survey marks. Roads. Their own body, mapping itself.

The cipher on the door finished rewriting itself, and Sebastian read it, because of course they could read it, because they had been able to read it their whole life and had simply never looked:

*The thirteenth moon shall find the key. The key shall choose: choir, or crown, or ash.*

Behind the door, very softly, something began to sing.

Wren's grip tightened. "Tell me you hear that too."

Sebastian could not speak. The singing was in the language of the old maps, and it was calling their name — not *Sebastian*, but the older name, the guild-name, the one written in the margin of every map their ancestors had ever made, the name of the bloodline that held the key.

And beneath the singing, closer than the door, closer than the walls, a voice that Sebastian had last heard answering a child's prayer in the night market said, gently, the way one wakes a sleeper:

*You took your time, little cartographer. Come and see what we've buried.*
