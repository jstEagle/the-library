## 4. The Godseed

The stairs went down farther than the city went up.

Sebastian counted them out of habit — a cartographer's tic, measuring the world even when the world refused to be measured. Forty steps, then sixty, then a number past counting, the stone beneath their boots worn into shallow bowls by feet that had climbed here before the Ban, before Vellamere, before perhaps anything at all. Wren's lamp threw their shadows long against walls that were not walls, that were in places carved with processions of figures whose faces had been deliberately smoothed away, and in other places simply *absent*, as if the rock itself had declined to exist where the builders hadn't asked it to.

"You knew this was here," Sebastian said. Not an accusation. Their voice came out strange in the dark, flattened by stone.

"I knew it was *under* here," Wren said. "There's a difference. My order keeps maps too, Sebastian. Ours just aren't drawn in ink." She paused on a landing where the stair split three ways, and the lamplight caught the fine bones of her face, the new lines around her eyes that seven years had put there. "The middle way. Always the middle way. That's what the litany says when you descend to the choir."

"Your litany sounds like it was written by someone who got lost."

"It was. That's rather the point."

Sebastian's satchel pressed warm against their hip. That was the thing they could not stop noticing, the thing that had been wrong since the moment they'd crossed the temple's threshold three streets and a lifetime above: their maps were warm. All of them. The old charts of the unmapped realms, the ones the Inquisition would burn them for owning, the ones they'd kept rolled in oilcloth through seven years of selling luck-knots and fever-charms — every sheet of them had begun to glow faintly, like paper held near a banked fire, and the glow grew stronger with every step down.

They did not tell Wren. It was the old habit, the seven-years habit: keep the true maps hidden, keep the true self hidden, survive.

But the maps, it seemed, were done hiding.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was round, and it was not empty.

Sebastian had expected ruin — fallen pillars, silt, the patient rot of the buried. Instead they stepped into a space that felt *held*, the way a breath is held. A dome overhead painted with constellations no astronomer had ever named, their gold still bright. A floor of inlaid stone forming a wheel with thirteen spokes. And at the center, rising from the hub of the wheel like a blade from a wound, a single pillar of pale stone that hummed.

Not hummed. Sebastian corrected themselves, standing very still while Wren moved to the chamber's edge and set her lamp in a niche, where it caught and multiplied in a dozen hidden mirrors. Not hummed. *Sang.* A note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the soft meat behind the eyes.

"I've been here once before," Wren said quietly. "When I was noviced. They brought us down to see it and told us it was the Anchor, and that the gods were asleep behind it, and that our whole faith was the act of keeping watch over something that would never wake." She turned to look at Sebastian, and her expression was one Sebastian had never seen on her — not the fierce joy of the night market, not the priestess's calm. Something rawer. "It never sang like this before."

Sebastian approached the pillar the way one approaches a sleeping animal. Up close, the pale stone was not stone at all but something smoother, veined with faint gold that moved — *moved*, slowly, like light through water — and the warmth of it reached them before they touched it, soaking through their coat, through their shirt, into the skin of their outstretched hand.

The moment their fingers met the pillar's surface, the maps in their satchel blazed.

Light spilled through the oiled canvas, gold as the veins in the pillar, and Sebastian heard Wren's sharp intake of breath and did not turn around, because they were looking at the pillar's surface, where their own handprint had appeared — pressed into the pale material like a seal into wax — and around the handprint, rising through the stone like ink through water, words in a script Sebastian knew better than their own heartbeat.

Cartographer's script. The old notation. The hand of Aldous Hartwell.

They knew it because they had spent a childhood tracing it. Because their grandmother had kept Aldous's field journals under lock and made young Sebastian copy the letterforms as penance and as inheritance both. Because Sebastian had built a whole secret self out of a dead ancestor's alphabet, and now that alphabet was rising out of a pillar a hundred feet beneath the city to greet them.

*Read it,* the pillar did not say, but the warmth said it, the hum said it, the gold light said it, and Sebastian — who had never once in their life obeyed a voice — read it.

*To whoever of my blood stands here: I bound them, and the binding is a seed, and the seed is you.*

The chamber tilted. Or Sebastian did. Wren's hands were on their shoulders suddenly, steadying them, and her voice came from very far away asking something — *Sebastian, what is it, what does it say* — and Sebastian heard themselves answer in a voice that did not sound like theirs:

"It says I'm the reason."

* * *

They sat against the base of the pillar, because standing had stopped being possible, and Wren sat beside them, and the lamplight made a small gold island in the vast dark of the dome. Sebastian talked, because if they stopped talking they would have to feel what they were saying.

"Aldous Hartwell. My — I don't know. Great-something-grandfather. The first cartographer of the unmapped realms. They teach children that he *discovered* them." Sebastian's laugh came out broken. "He didn't discover them. He *closed* them. He was the one who bound the gods out of the world, Wren. Everyone thinks the Ban was the Inquisition, the Council, the laws. It wasn't. It was him. He made a — a seal, a living seal, and the seal was his own blood, and it passed down, generation to generation, dormant, and I—" Their hands were shaking. They watched them shake with the detached interest of a surveyor noting a fault line. "I sold charms for seven years. Little knots and prayers, harmless, I told myself. And the whole time I was a *key* walking around the city with a bell around its neck."

"The prayers started being answered seven months ago," Wren said slowly. Sebastian could see her priestess's mind working, arranging the liturgical calendar against the mundane one. "The first answered prayer in Vellamere. The cooper's daughter. The fever that broke."

"Seven months ago I finished copying Aldous's journals. All of them. The last one, the one he wrote dying, the one my grandmother said never to read aloud." Sebastian pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "I read it aloud. In my rooms. Alone. Like an idiot. Like a man reading a map out loud in country he doesn't know is listening."

*The seed is you.* The words would not stop repeating, would not settle into sense. Every prayer answered in Vellamere — the fever broken, the drowning man coughing up river water and living, the child at Sebastian's own stall weeks ago whose whispered wish had been met by a voice no one else heard — every one of them had been answered not because the gods had woken, but because Sebastian's blood had *called*, and the gods, asleep behind their binding, had heard the call the way the drowning hear a rope thrown into dark water.

They had not returned. They had been *summoned*. By the one person in the city who wanted, more than anything, for them to stay buried.

"The Inquisition is rounding up the answered," Wren said. Her hand had found Sebastian's, and she did not let go. "My order thinks it's the dawn of the return. There's a prophecy — thirteen turns of the moon — they'll want to know what I've found down here. They'll want to know about *you*." She paused, and when she spoke again her voice was very careful. "Sebastian. The seed. Can it be — is there a way to—"

"To unseed it?" Sebastian lowered their hands. The pillar hummed beside them, patient, warm, terrible. Up close they could see that the gold veins were not random. They were *routes*. Ley lines, the old lines Sebastian had spent seven years mapping in secret as they faded from the city above — except here they were not faded. Here they blazed, converging on the pillar, converging on the handprint, converging on Sebastian as if Sebastian were the wellhead and the whole city's buried magic were a river running backward toward them.

"I don't know," they said. "Aldous bound them with his life and it still wasn't enough to kill the binding — it just went to sleep and waited for blood. Maybe it can't be unmade. Maybe it can only be *claimed*." The word came out of them cold. "That's what the voice at my stall said. *Claimed.*"

Wren went very still.

"What voice at your stall?"

"The child's prayer. Weeks ago. Something answered her, and then it—" Sebastian's throat closed around it, the memory they had been refusing for weeks, the words in the dark behind their own skull, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "It said *thank you, vessel*. It said the choir was almost ready to sing."

The lamplight guttered, though there was no draft this deep. On the pillar, the gold veins brightened, and the hum rose — half a tone, no more, the way a singer leans into a note — and Sebastian felt it pass through the stone into their spine and knew, with the awful certainty of a man reading a map and recognizing the place he is already standing, that the pillar knew they were there. That it had been waiting. That seven months of answered prayers had not been the gods reaching for the world at all.

They had been the world's gods, reaching — through the seed, through Sebastian's sleeping blood — for *Sebastian*.

"Thirteen turns of the moon," Sebastian said. "That's what your prophecy says. How many are left?"

Wren's face, in the lamplight, gave the answer before her voice did.

"Four," she said. "Sebastian. Four."

And above them, through a hundred feet of stone, faint as memory, the bells of Vellamere began to ring — all of them at once, every tower, though it was not any hour the city kept.
