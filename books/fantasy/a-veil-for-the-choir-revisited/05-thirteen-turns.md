## 5. Thirteen Turns

The crypt beneath the Candlemakers' Row had no windows, but Wren had learned to read the moon anyway — in the way the tide of rats moved through the walls, in the ache behind her eyes that came three nights out of every seven when the old light pulled at whatever the gods had left in her blood. Tonight the ache was a hammer. The moon was full, and it was not the only thing turning.

She knelt with the rest of her order on cold flagstones while Mother Vesper held up the Reliquary Bone — a finger's length of something pale and honeycombed, older than the city above them, older than the Ban that had driven forty faithful souls into cellars to worship by tallow-light.

"The Choir sang again last night," Mother Vesper said. Her voice was dry paper over stone. "In the Saltmarket. In the Tanner's Quarter. Three answered prayers in one night, and one of them *bled*."

Wren kept her face still. She had been the one to see it — a cooper's widow who had prayed for her dying son and woken to find him whole, and the boy's fever standing in the corner of the room like a second child, gray and weeping, until it dissolved at dawn. The Inquisition had taken the widow by noon. Wren had watched from across the square, hood drawn, hands folded like any honest woman buying bread.

"Thirteen turns," Mother Vesper said, and the words went through the crypt like a nail through a palm. "That is what the Bone says. Count them." She turned it in the candlelight, and Wren saw what she had been refusing to see for two days: thirteen shallow notches spiraling its length, and the thirteenth notch worn nearly smooth, as though something had been worrying at it for centuries, patient as water.

"When the moon completes its thirteenth turn since the first prayer was answered — ninety-one nights from the Saltmarket widow — the godseed must be claimed. By the Choir, or by the city that outlawed it. There is no third door." Mother Vesper lowered the Bone. "And if neither claims it, the seed will claim itself. It will open. And what comes through an opened seed is not godhood, children. It is the war that made the Ban necessary, come home to roost."

Someone gasped. Someone began to pray. Wren counted her own heartbeats and found them traitorous — not quick with fear but with a name. *Sebastian.*

"You all know the stories of the Hartwell line," Mother Vesper continued, and now her gaze found Wren across the crypt the way a hawk finds the one rabbit in the meadow that has moved. "Cartographers. Mapmakers of the unmapped realms. The ancestor, Aldous Hartwell, who walked into the deep places with the gods' own inheritance folded into his blood and bound it there, sealed it, carried it out again like a thief carrying stolen fire in his veins. It has passed down, quiet and dormant, generation to generation." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Until one of them woke it."

The candles guttered, though there was no draft.

"Brother Anselm has confirmed it," she said. "The charm-seller of the night market. Sebastian Hartwell. The seed sleeps in their blood like an ember under snow — and every prayer that has been answered in this city since the thaw began was answered *through them*, whether they know it or not." She let that settle over the kneeling congregation like a shroud. "The order requires the vessel brought to us before the thirteenth turn completes. Alive. Willing, if grace permits. Restrained, if it does not."

Wren's knees were stones. Her hands, folded in her lap, would not stop trembling, so she pressed them flat against her thighs where no one could see.

"And Sister Calloway." Mother Vesper's voice did not rise, which was how you knew to be afraid. "You have already sought out the charm-seller once. The order thanks you for opening that road." A thin, terrible smile. "You will walk it again. You will deliver them to us. We could ask no one better — they trust you."

*They trusted me,* Wren thought, and the thought had teeth.

* * *

She did not go straight back to her cell. She climbed instead — up the rotting ladder, through the coal hatch, out onto the roof of the tallow-chandler's shop where the city spread below her like a map she couldn't read anymore. The moon hung fat and white over Vellamere, and she hated it, suddenly, ferociously, this great blind eye counting down its thirteen turns over people who had never asked to be pieces on anyone's board.

*Ninety-one nights.* She did the arithmetic the way she'd once done devotions, mechanically, and the answer was always the same: not enough time to become the person the order wanted her to be.

Because here was the ruin of it, the thing she could not say in the crypt: she knew Sebastian Hartwell's hands. She knew the particular way they went still over a map, the ink-stain in the web of the left thumb, the scar from the glass vial they'd broken the winter they were both twenty-two and poor and foolishly happy. She knew that Sebastian talked to their maps when they thought no one listened, apologizing to the blank spaces like they were old friends they'd neglected. She knew the sound they made in sleep — a small exhale, almost a word — and she knew that if she led chained Hartwells into the crypt, Mother Vesper would use that blood like a key, and everything Sebastian was would be spent to buy the gods a door.

And if she refused? The Choir would find the seed themselves. The gods were awake now; she felt them the way one feels weather. They had waited seven years behind the Ban and they would not wait politely for the moon. Or the Inquisition would take Sebastian first — Inquisitor Hale had been asking questions in the night market for a week; even Wren had heard that much — and the city would do exactly what the city had done seven years ago, which was burn whatever it could not bind.

Every road led through Sebastian's blood. The only question was whose hand held the knife.

"You're on my roof, priestess."

Wren turned. Old Maren the chandler stood in the hatchway, half in shadow, unbothered. She had been hiding clergy on this roof since before the Ban and had opinions about none of them.

"I'm sorry," Wren said. "I needed sky."

Maren grunted, lowered herself onto the roof edge with the groan of her knees, and looked out at the moon. "Bad night for sky," she said. "Sky's full of teeth tonight." Then, after a silence: "You've got the look of a woman doing sums that won't come out right."

"No," Wren admitted. "They don't."

"Then don't do them up here." Maren tapped the slates between them with one cracked fingernail. "My husband was a chandler before me, and his mother before him, and I'll tell you what I told every frightened soul who ever sat where you're sitting. Sums done out of fear always come out wrong. Do your sums out of love, and even when they're wrong, you can live with them." She heaved herself back up. At the hatchway she paused. "There's bread on the sill if you want it. There usually is."

When she was gone, Wren sat a long while with the moon on her face, and slowly, the arithmetic changed shape. Not *whom do I betray* — but *what do I protect?* Not *the order or Sebastian* — but *Vellamere itself*, all of it, the cooper's widow and the tallow-chandler and the bread on the sill and yes, the frightened congregation in the crypt too, who deserved better than to be spent as someone else's soldiers.

The order wanted a vessel delivered. The gods wanted a vessel claimed. The city wanted a threat extinguished. None of them, Wren realized, had asked the simplest question in the world:

What did *Sebastian* want?

She didn't know. Seven years, and she had never once asked. She had come to their stall with suspicion and then with need and then with something older than either, and she had never stopped talking long enough to hear what Sebastian Hartwell wanted to do with the fire sleeping in their veins. Maybe they wanted to be rid of it. Maybe they wanted to be feared. Maybe — and this was the thought that made her stand, made her heart slam — maybe they had already figured out what it was, alone in their little shop of harmless charms, and were lying awake nights trying to decide, exactly as she was, whom to save and how.

Or maybe they were deciding whether to run.

She went down the ladder fast, took the bread because refusing kindness felt like bad luck, and slipped into the city's arteries with her hood up. The night market was still breathing at this hour — lanterns, fish-smoke, the murmur of small illegal comforts changing hands. She knew the route to Sebastian's stall by her feet now, which should have frightened her more than it did.

She smelled the trouble before she saw it. Snuff and hot metal. Inquisition scent.

Around the mouth of Cooper's Lane, three figures in gray stood at the edge of the market's glow, and one of them was tall and narrow and patient, and even at this distance Wren knew the set of Inquisitor Hale's shoulders. They weren't moving. They were watching the stall. Waiting for it to close. Waiting for the crowd to thin.

Wren pressed into a doorway, heart hammering, bread crushed against her ribs. She could walk away. Warn no one. Let the law do the order's work and wash her hands of the choosing entirely — wasn't that, in its coward's way, a kind of obedience?

But Sebastian had once sat up all night with her when she had the marsh-fever, reading maps aloud in the dark because the sound of their voice kept her from sliding under. That was the sum she kept arriving at, no matter how she arranged the numbers.

Hale shifted. One of the gray figures peeled off toward the alley behind the stall — the back way, Sebastian's way.

Wren moved.
