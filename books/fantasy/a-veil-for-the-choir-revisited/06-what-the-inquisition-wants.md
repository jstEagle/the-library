## 6. What the Inquisition Wants

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded into the change a customer paid for a luck-charm — a slip of grey vellum bearing the seal of the Vellamere Inquisition, pressed in wax the color of old bruises.

Sebastian read it twice by lamplight behind the stall, then once more in full daylight to be sure they hadn't imagined it. *Master Hartwell. Your presence is requested at the Bastion of Inquiry, third bell. Come alone. Come willing.* No threat attached. That was the worst part. The Inquisition never wasted ink on threats; threats were for people who still had choices.

They spent the morning doing something almost absurd in its smallness: selling charms. A fisherman bought a knot against storm-luck. A widow bought a charm against dreams. Sebastian's hands moved through the old motions — twist the copper, whisper the harmless nothing into it, smile the smile that had kept them fed for seven years — while their mind ran elsewhere entirely, running ahead like water finding cracks.

*Come alone. Come willing.*

Wren had not come back to the stall since the night beneath the temple. Three days now. Three days of silence from the priesthood, silence from Wren, and the ley lines on their hidden maps brightening each dawn like embers coaxed back to life. And now this.

At noon, Sebastian did what any sensible criminal would do before walking into the Bastion: they prepared to run.

* * *

The workshop above the stall looked, to any searcher, like the home of an unremarkable charm-monger. Dried herbs. Copper wire. Ledgers of modest sales. But Sebastian had spent seven years building this room the way a squirrel builds a winter — with caches, and false floors, and exits nobody would think to look for.

The real work was under the floorboards: the master maps. Not the city charts everyone assumed were their livelihood, but the other ones. The *unmapped* realms — the shifting territories beyond Vellamere's walls where distance obeyed older rules and roads existed only if you believed in them hard enough. Sebastian's ancestor Aldous Hartwell had charted them first, three hundred years ago, in the days when cartographers carried compasses that pointed at longing instead of north. Sebastian had inherited the talent and, until seven years ago, the license to use it.

Now they spread the oldest map across the workbench and began, with a steadiness that surprised them, to plan.

The realms had changed since the gods began answering. Sebastian could feel it in the way the ink behaved — certain routes that had been stable for years now shimmered faintly, as though the paper itself remembered being holy ground. The Hollow Way, which ran beneath the river and surfaced wherever grief was thickest, still held. The Stair of Small Hours remained passable, though its lower steps had begun to glow at moonrise, which was new, and not comforting. The Glasswood was lost entirely; the trees there had gone luminous, and luminous trees in the Glasswood meant the gods had put down roots.

Sebastian marked three viable routes out of the city, two viable routes *back in*, and one route that led nowhere at all — a road that ended in a fold of blank vellum, which was either a dead end or a door, depending on how desperate you were when you reached it.

"You're planning your escape," they said aloud to the empty room, "while agreeing to walk into the Bastion." A pause. "That's not cowardice. That's *cartography*."

It sounded less convincing spoken than it had in their head. They rolled the maps anyway, wrapped them in oilcloth, and hid them where only a person who already knew the realms would think to look — inside the frame of a map of ordinary Vellamere, hanging in plain view above the hearth.

Then they buttoned their coat, and went to answer the Inquisition.

* * *

The Bastion of Inquiry had been a cathedral once, before the Ban. Sebastian recognized the bones of it — the rose window bricked over but still bleeding colored light through its mortar seams, the nave converted to offices, the confessional booths repurposed as interrogation rooms. Someone had made a joke of it, or a point. With the Inquisition it was always hard to tell which.

Inquisitor Hale received them in a chamber at the top of the bell tower, which had no bell anymore, just an open arch looking out over the city. Hale was smaller than Sebastian expected — a compact woman with iron-grey hair cut short and the patient stillness of someone who had learned that silence made people confess faster than shouting did. She sat at a plain table with a pot of tea and two cups, and she poured before she spoke.

"Master Hartwell. Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit anyway. You'll want your legs under you in about ten minutes." She pushed a cup across. "Chamomile. I'm not going to poison you; poisoned guests can't answer questions."

Sebastian sat. The tea smelled genuinely of chamomile. They didn't drink it.

"Seven years," Hale said, settling back. "That's how long you've been selling charms without a license. Knots against storms. Coins against fever. Little coppers twisted just so, carrying little nothings." Her eyes were pale grey, unhurried. "I've known about the stall for five of those years."

The floor of Sebastian's stomach opened quietly, like a trapdoor.

"Then why—"

"Why haven't I arrested you?" Hale sipped her own tea. "Because your charms are worthless, Master Hartwell. Beautifully, expertly worthless. You sell people comfort at a copper apiece and call it magic, and there isn't a drop of true craft in any of it — which is, I've come to believe, rather the point. You could work real charms. Anyone who's seen your family's records knows that. Instead you chose to be harmless." She set down the cup. "In another life, I might have found that admirable."

"And in this one?"

"In this one, the world has changed around us." She rose and walked to the open arch, hands clasped behind her back. Below, Vellamere sprawled in the afternoon haze — the market, the river, the temple district with its shuttered churches. Somewhere among all those rooftops, prayers were coming true. "Three weeks ago, a baker prayed for rain and got a flood that drowned his ovens. Two weeks ago, a mother prayed for her son's fever to break, and it broke — along with every bone in his left hand. Last week a man prayed to win back his wife, and she came home. Walking. Smiling. Not blinking enough." Hale turned. "The old gods are answering, Master Hartwell. Badly. And my office needs to know why."

"Perhaps ask them."

"The gods don't come when *we* call." Something flickered across her face — fatigue, genuine and human. "But something called *them*. And our scholars believe the calling has a source. A living source. An inheritance passed down through bloodlines, dormant for centuries." She let the words sit in the tower air between them. "The godseed, the texts call it."

Sebastian kept their face still. It was the hardest thing they had ever done, harder than any exam at the Cartographers' Guild, harder than burying their magic seven years ago. Inside their chest, beneath the ribs, something *turned over* — slow and warm and horribly awake, like a sleeper disturbed by voices.

"I've heard rumors," they said. "Tavern talk. Priests hiding in cellars, miracles gone wrong. Sad business."

"Mm." Hale studied them with the expression of a woman reading a map whose terrain had shifted. "Here is what I'm offering. Full clemency. Seven years of illegal commerce — forgiven. Your name struck from the watchlists. Your Guild credentials restored. You'd be a licensed cartographer again, Hartwell. Free to chart anything within the Ban's limits. A pardon in writing, signed by the Provost herself." She walked back to the table and laid a document upon it, cream-colored, official, heavy with seals. "All of it. For one thing."

"Which is?"

"Find the godseed's bearer. Lead us to them." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle. "We don't intend harm. We intend *containment*. Whatever is waking the gods, we mean to put it somewhere the gods can't reach it — or it can't reach the gods. You of all people understand containment. You've been containing yourself for seven years."

Sebastian looked at the pardon. Cream paper. Black ink. A whole future, pre-written, waiting only for their signature.

Every instinct they owned screamed at once. *She knows. She's always known. This is not an offer; it's a leash with a very long rope.* Because if Hale had watched the stall for five years, she knew more than the stall. She might know about Wren's visits. About the sealed temple. About the maps that glowed. The offer wasn't a door out — it was a corridor with doors on both sides, both of them locked, and Hale holding the keys.

And yet. Refuse, and what then? Arrest? Or worse — the quiet kind of attention the Inquisition paid to problems it hadn't solved yet? Say yes, and lead soldiers to Wren. To her congregation, praying in cellars. To whatever slept in Sebastian's own blood.

"No," Sebastian said.

Hale raised an eyebrow.

"Not today." Sebastian stood, keeping their voice as level as hers. "You want me to find someone who may not exist, based on tavern rumors, and hand them to an office that drowned a baker's ovens last month. I need time to consider whether I believe in your containment."

"Time." Hale smiled thinly. "How much?"

"A week."

"You have three days." She slid the pardon across the table anyway, leaving it there. "Keep that. Read it. Imagine your old life." At the stair, she added, without turning: "One thing more, Master Hartwell. Your associate — the priestess. Calloway. Her order has been asking after her too. Everyone wants the same person these days, it seems. Do think about who you'd rather she belong to."

Sebastian walked down four hundred steps with their heart hammering like a trapped bird, past confessionals that smelled of dust and old fear, out into streets that suddenly felt mapped by everyone except them.

* * *

Night. The workshop. Candlelight. The pardon lay on the workbench where Sebastian had thrown it, and beside it, unrolled, the master map of the unmapped realms — because three days was not a week, and three days meant deciding *now*.

Sebastian worked through the dark hours with red and black ink. Red for routes out. Black for routes that might be traps. The Hollow Way, still good. The Stair, risky after moonrise. The road that ended in blankness — they stared at that one longest, tracing the place where their own confident lines simply stopped, the fold in the world they had never dared test.

If it came to running, they wouldn't run alone. That was the thought that frightened most. Because Wren had appeared tonight at last — a knock at the shutters near midnight, her hood wet with river fog, her face lit by candle-flame and something worse than fear.

"The Choir sings again," she'd said, breathless. "Not in temples. In the *streets*. Sebastian — they're manifesting. All of them. Tonight. And they're saying—" her voice had cracked on it— "they're saying they've heard their vessel's heart. They're coming to claim what's theirs."

Sebastian had looked at the pardon on the table. At the maps. At Wren.

And far below the floorboards, far below the city, deep in the blood and bone of them, the godseed had answered — a single pulse of light so bright Sebastian saw it through closed eyes, warm and vast and unmistakably *glad*, like a child hearing its mother's footsteps on the stairs.

Outside, over the rooftops of Vellamere, the moon was rising. And rising with it, thin as smoke, a sound that was not sound — a thousand voices singing one note, growing louder.

The thirteenth turn had begun.
