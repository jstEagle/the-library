## 7. The Choir Sings Again

The sky over Vellamere cracked open at dusk, and Wren Calloway was on her knees in the undercroft when it happened.

Not thunder — she knew thunder. This was a sound like a great door unbolting after centuries of rust, a groan that traveled down through stone and soil and into the marrow of everyone in the city. The candles guttered. The chalk circle at her feet — drawn for a prayer to Mereth of the Quiet Hands, for a dying child whose mother had come weeping three nights past — flared white.

*We are awake,* said the sky. *We are hungry. Bring us our vessel.*

Wren's hands were still pressed flat against the floor. Around her, the others in the undercroft — the answered and the unanswered alike — lifted their faces as one, listening. Old Tam had gone gray as ash. Sister Ilse was mouthing the words of a liturgy that hadn't been spoken aloud since before the Ban, her lips moving faster than breath could carry them.

"Wren." Ilse seized her wrist. "The choir. Do you hear it? After all these years — the choir sings again."

*I hear it,* Wren almost said. *That's what frightens me.*

Because this was not the voice she had prayed to all those years in secret, the small warm presence that had felt like her grandmother's kitchen, like candlelight, like being known. This was vast and cold and certain, a voice with teeth behind its music. *Bring us our vessel.* It did not ask. Asking was for creatures that feared refusal.

She pulled free of Ilse's grip and ran up the stairs into the street.

Vellamere's evening sky had turned the color of an old bruise, violet shot through with veins of gold, and above the rooftops hung shapes that might have been clouds if clouds had faces. She saw the silhouette of the Weaver, many-armed and patient. She saw the Hollow King, crowned in absence. She saw Mereth, gentle Mereth, and even her expression had changed: the kindness carved into her marble face now looked like hunger wearing kindness as a mask.

People stood frozen in the lanes below, market baskets dropped, children lifted onto shoulders so they could see the gods their grandparents had been taught to deny. Some wept. Some cheered. A man near the fountain fell to his knees and began reciting the old confession, tears streaming, and where his tears struck the cobblestones, small golden flowers opened, blooming and wilting and blooming again.

"Bring us our vessel," the sky said again, and every voice in Vellamere heard it differently — in a mother tongue, in a dead father's timbre, in the voices of everyone they had ever lost. Wren heard it in her own.

She was running before she finished hearing it.

* * *

Sebastian Hartwell was packing when the gods came to town.

They had felt it coming for two days — the ley lines beneath the city thrumming like plucked strings, their hand-drawn maps refusing to lie flat, corners curling upward toward some distant summons. They had told themselves it was weather. Cartographers who had walked the unmapped realms did not get to tell themselves much else.

Now they threw charm-bottles into a satchel without sorting — luck charms next to fever wards next to the little glass vials of nothing-at-all they'd been selling honest folk for seven years — and their hands shook only slightly, which they counted as a triumph.

The godseed sat behind their sternum like a swallowed star. It had been quiet since the temple, or quiet-ish, humming to itself the way a kettle hums before it screams. Tonight it was not humming. Tonight it was singing, harmonizing with the impossible chorus overhead, and Sebastian understood with sickening clarity that the sky was singing *to them.*

*Vessel,* sang the stars. *Ours.*

"No," Sebastian said out loud, to the ceiling, to the sky, to the ancestor whose blood had done this. "Absolutely not. Find someone else's lineage to ruin."

Someone hammered on the door. Sebastian's heart performed a complicated maneuver, because Inquisitors knocked like that — three sharp blows, then silence, then three more — and they grabbed the iron poker by the hearth before crossing the room.

"It's me." Wren's voice, low and ragged. "Sebastian, open the door, please, please—"

They opened it and she fell half into their arms, hood thrown back, face streaked with soot and something that might have been gold dust. Her priestly tattoos — the ones she usually kept hidden under sleeves and misdirection — were glowing faintly, all of them, like embers breathing.

"They've named you," she said without preamble. "The order received a vision at sundown. All of us, the same vision. Your face, your blood, the seed. The Choir wants its vessel, and the Synod—" She stopped, swallowed. "The Synod wants to deliver you. There was a vote."

"And you voted?"

"To deliver you." She met their eyes, and her own were wet and furious. "I abstained. I couldn't — I raised my hand halfway and then I heard myself, and I walked out. Sebastian, I walked out of my own conclave. Ilse will have me declared apostate by morning."

Sebastian set down the poker. Very carefully, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown, they took her by the shoulders. "You chose wrong, then. You should have voted to give me to them and saved yourself."

"I've made worse choices for better reasons," Wren said. "I'm not doing it again. Not with you."

Seven years of silence, and she could still dismantle them with a sentence. Sebastian leaned their forehead against hers, just for a moment, just long enough to feel the godseed settle — not quiet, exactly, but quieter, like a dog lying down at its master's feet.

"The Inquisition knows too," Wren whispered. "Hale's writ came through the cathedral district while I was running here. They're sweeping the night market first. Sebastian, they're already there."

* * *

The night market was burning.

Not all of it — not yet. But the lantern-strung lane where Sebastian had sold charms for seven years was an avenue of orange light and black smoke, stalls overturned, awnings ablaze, and moving through the chaos came the Inquisition in their ash-gray coats, iron censers swinging, chanting the Rite of Severance in flat unison. And against them, descending from the bruised sky on wings of golden smoke, came the answered — ordinary citizens lit from within, eyes shining, wielding miracles like knives.

Sebastian and Wren arrived at the mouth of the lane in time to see a baker — a woman Sebastian knew by name, Marta, who bought a fertility charm every spring and never once asked if it worked — rise six feet off the cobbles on a column of golden light and hurl a wagon at three Inquisitors.

"We should not be here," Wren breathed.

"We're always here," Sebastian said. "This is my market. I know every bolt-hole in this lane."

Which was fortunate, because at that moment Inquisitor Hale stepped out of the smoke directly into their path, flanked by four severants, and smiled the thin smile of a man who has stopped pretending to negotiate.

"Hartwell." Hale's voice carried strangely through the din, unnaturally clear, and Sebastian realized he was wearing a speaker's amulet — the kind used for pronouncements, for sentences read over crowds. "You will come with us. The city extends its clemency one final hour. The vessel comes quietly, or Vellamere burns as heresy fuel."

Above them, the sky pulsed. *OURS,* it said, and the word knocked Sebastian to one knee, the godseed flaring so bright they could feel it glowing through their shirt.

"You see?" Hale spread his arms, magnanimous amid the ruin. "Even your body votes against you."

Sebastian got one knee under themselves. Then, slowly, deliberately, they reached into their coat and drew out a map.

It was not one of their careful sale-maps. It was older — inked in the years before the Ban, when they had still walked the unmapped realms, when the roads between worlds had bent to their compass. They had kept it folded against their heart for seven years, telling themselves it was sentiment. It was not sentiment. It was insurance.

"Hale," Sebastian said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect, "do you know what happens when you burn a map while standing on the territory it describes?"

Hale frowned. "What nonsense—"

Sebastian struck a spark from their flint and touched it to the corner of the paper, right where they had once drawn the night market lane, and the world hiccupped.

Fire raced along the ink lines — not consuming the paper but the *street*, flame leaping from drawing to reality, and where it passed, the lane itself became uncertain. Cobbles rippled like water. The fountain drifted ten feet left. Hale's severants cried out as the ground beneath them tilted gently, horribly, in directions ground does not go. Somewhere above, the gods' golden shapes recoiled, their song stumbling over a note.

"That," Sebastian said, hauling Wren toward the alley mouth they had drawn a trapdoor beside eleven years ago and finding, blessedly, that it was still there, "is nonsense with a professional certification. Come on!"

They plunged down the stairs together as the market roared behind them — Hale shouting orders, Marta the baker laughing a terrible golden laugh, the sky demanding and demanding and demanding. Wren yanked the trapdoor shut and threw the bolt, and darkness folded around them, cool and total, smelling of clay and old rain.

Their breathing was the loudest thing in the world.

"Well," Wren said finally, her voice shaking with adrenaline and something close to laughter. "You burned your own map."

"I burned a copy of my own map."

"Liar."

"A little bit of a liar." Sebastian's eyes had adjusted enough to make out her face, soot-streaked and luminous-eyed, inches away. The godseed had gone quiet in their chest — utterly quiet, for the first time in weeks, as if even it understood there were moments that belonged to no prophecy. "Wren. You'll be excommunicated. I'm the most wanted person in Vellamere. The sky itself is hunting me. There is nowhere in this city, in any city, that—"

"Then we don't stay in any city." She found their hand in the dark. Her palm was warm; her grip was certain. "You once told me about roads that don't appear on any honest chart. Roads only you can walk. Take me there."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong to Vellamere's sewers or cellars: the slow, patient lap of water against stones in a place far larger than any cistern. The unmapped realms, feeling for their old cartographer like a tongue probing an empty socket.

And somewhere overhead, muffled through earth and timber, the choir changed its song. No longer *bring us our vessel.* Now, sweeter and far more terrible:

*We see where you are going.*
