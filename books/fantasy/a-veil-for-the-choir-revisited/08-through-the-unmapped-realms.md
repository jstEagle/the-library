## 8. Through the Unmapped Realms

The door in the cellar wall had not been a door seven years ago.

Sebastian knew this the way they knew their own heartbeat — by the ache of it. When they had charted the unmapped realms as a young cartographer of the Guild of Veils, this threshold had been a seam in the world, a fold of shadow behind the temple's wine racks that opened only for those who carried ink and intent. Now it stood open like a wound, breathing out air that smelled of rain on hot stone and something sweeter beneath — myrrh, perhaps, or the memory of myrrh. The returning gods had pried the realms apart the way a child pries open a locked box, careless of what bent.

"Tell me that's not where you're taking me," Wren said. She was pressed against Sebastian's back in the dark, her breath ragged from running, one hand fisted in their coat. Above them, muffled through three floors of stone, the night market burned. They could hear the choir of it — the gods' voices braided with screaming, with the Inquisition's horns, with the wet percussion of collapsing stalls.

"It's the only road they can't follow."

"They're *gods*, Sebastian."

"The realms aren't theirs." Sebastian pulled the last of their chalk from their pocket — a stub worn round as a knuckle — and drew the opening sigil on the doorframe with hands that shook only a little. "They were never theirs. That's rather the whole problem, isn't it?"

The seam swallowed them.

* * *

Falling through the unmapped realms was not like falling. It was like being *remembered* — every step forward also a step into some earlier version of yourself, so that Sebastian crossed the threshold already grieving things that hadn't happened yet. The passage around them unfolded in ribbons of color that had no names outside old guild lexicons: vespertine blue, the green of drowned bells, a red that tasted of copper and confession. Wren made a small sound beside them, and Sebastian caught her hand without thinking, the way they once had across a chapel pew, seven years and a lifetime ago.

"Don't look at anything too long," they said. "The realms take attention as payment."

"What does that mean?"

"It means if you stare at that—" they nodded at a floating archway hung with lanterns that burned without light, "—it will show you something you want, and then it will keep it."

Wren looked away. Her hand tightened around theirs. "You've been here before."

"I mapped here." The words came out flat, and they heard what lived under the flatness: shame. "Before the Ban. Before I sold my first charm to a woman who just wanted her bread to stop molding. I walked every fold of these places and wrote them down, and the Guild bound the maps in lead and locked them under Vellamere, because knowledge of the roads is knowledge of the gods, and the city decided it would rather be blind than be tempted."

"And now the gods are back anyway."

"Now the gods are back anyway." Sebastian laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Because of me, Wren. Not despite everything. Because of me."

She stopped walking. In the ribboned light her face was half-shadow, half-something fiercer. "Say that again."

* * *

Sebastian had rehearsed the confession for six days — since the temple beneath the city, since the moment the godseed had flared in their blood like a second heart and the ancestor's voice had poured through them, ancient and tired: *You are the bell. We are only the echo.* Six days of composing sentences while selling charms and dodging Inquisitor Hale's polite, patient letters. Six days of deciding, over and over, never to say any of it aloud.

The realms took the choice away. There was no stalling here; the passages shifted according to truth, and every lie Sebastian told made the road fork wrong, doubled back, grew teeth. So they walked and talked, and the truth came out of them the way blood leaves a wound — in pulses, unstoppable.

"My great-grandmother was Meridian Hartwell. The cartographer who bound the pantheon behind the first veil." They kept their eyes on the path, on the chalk-marks of their own past glowing faintly along the stones like old scars. "Everyone thinks she did it to save the city. The histories say she was a hero. But she didn't bind them out of virtue. She bound them because they offered her godhood, and she refused, and refusing meant caging them or being caged. And the binding — it needed an anchor. A living bloodline. A seed planted in her descendants, dormant, passed down, waiting." Sebastian's voice thinned. "The godseed isn't a treasure, Wren. It's a *lock*. It's the hinge of her cage, grown into our family like a bone. And when it woke in me—"

"When it woke, it called to them." Wren's voice was very quiet. "The prayers started answering because the lock started singing."

"The prayers started answering because *I* started dreaming about mapping again." Sebastian finally turned to face her. "Every night for a month before the first miracle, I dreamed of these roads. I thought it was nostalgia. It wasn't. The seed was waking, and it reached for its makers, and they heard it across the veil, and they began to lean against the bars." They spread their hands. "Seven years of quiet. Seven years of harmless charms and honest work. And the moment my blood remembered what it was, the whole sky started tearing open. Every answered prayer, every person the Inquisition has dragged away, every fire in the night market tonight — that's me. That's all me."

The silence between them stretched until the realms themselves seemed to lean in.

Wren did not flinch. Sebastian watched for it — watched for the recoil, the recalculation, the priestess doing arithmetic with her faith — and it did not come. Instead she stepped closer, over the glowing chalk-line of a road Sebastian had drawn at nineteen, and took their face in both hands.

"You've been carrying this alone for six days."

"I've been carrying it alone for thirty years. You just didn't know there was anything to carry."

"I know." Her thumb traced their cheekbone, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "And here is what your guilt won't let you see, Sebastian Hartwell: the seed didn't choose you. Meridian didn't ask you. You were *born* into someone else's war, and you have spent your whole life trying to be small enough that no one could use you. I watched you do it. I loved you while you did it." Her breath caught. "I'm not going to stop loving you because the world finally noticed you exist."

"Wren—"

"No. Listen." Her forehead came to rest against theirs. "My order wants to deliver you to the gods. Hale wants to deliver you to the city. The gods want to wear you like a coat. And you want to disappear, because disappearing is the only mercy you've ever believed you deserve. But I am done letting institutions decide what happens to the people I love. My congregation prayed for rain and got a flood. They prayed for a sick mother's recovery and got a mother who cannot die. Miracles with teeth, Sebastian. Someone has to close the mouth." She pulled back just far enough to look at them fully. "So we walk to the end of this road together, and whatever the thirteenth turn demands, we decide it *ourselves*. Not them. Us."

Something in Sebastian's chest — older than the godseed, older than the Ban, older than the first map they'd ever drawn — unclenched for the first time in living memory.

"You know," they managed, "the last time you held my face like that, I'd just told you I was leaving for the northern folds and wouldn't write."

"And you didn't." A tear slipped free and she laughed at it, furious and fond. "Seven years, you absolute coward, and the realms had to burn down to bring you back to me."

"I brought you a charm instead. Once. A little glass bead that was supposed to keep nightmares away."

"It never worked."

"It worked beautifully. I enchanted it myself, badly, and I spent the whole next year terrified you'd dream of me and know."

Wren kissed them then, there on the shifting road between unnamed colors, and the realms — capricious, hungry, alive — did something they had never done in all Sebastian's years of mapping. They went still. Every ribbon of light hung motionless, every archway paused mid-bloom, as if the unmapped places themselves were holding their breath around two people who had finally stopped running in opposite directions.

When they broke apart, the road ahead had changed. The chalk-lines of Sebastian's old maps had gone dark, and in their place new lines glowed — not drawn but *grown*, silver and sure, winding toward a distant point of white.

"That wasn't there before," Wren whispered.

"No." Sebastian stared at it, and felt the godseed turn over in their blood like a sleeper rolling toward dawn. "That's the way to the temple. To the anchor-point. To the place where Meridian made her choice." They swallowed. "It's showing us the end of the road."

"Then we walk it."

They walked. And as the white point swelled ahead of them, bright as the moon that would reach its zenith tomorrow night, Sebastian understood, with a cartographer's terrible clarity, that the realms were not guiding them.

They were counting down.
