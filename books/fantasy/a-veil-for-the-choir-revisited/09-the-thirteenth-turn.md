## 9. The Thirteenth Turn

**Sebastian**

The temple beneath Vellamere had no ceiling that Sebastian had ever been able to chart.

Their ink had refused it seven years ago, back when they were young and arrogant enough to believe every place could be pinned to paper. The lines simply slid away from the vaulted dark overhead, the way water slides from oiled cloth, and Sebastian had learned — the hard way, in a hundred unmapped realms since — that some spaces decline to be known.

Tonight the refusal was gone. Tonight the ceiling was there, and it was *moving*, and through its shifting stone poured moonlight that had no business existing forty fathoms below the streets of a city that had outlawed wonder.

The thirteenth turn. The moon hung full and fat directly above the broken dome, close enough that Sebastian could see the pale scars of its craters, close enough to feel it pulling at their blood like a tide dragging at something small and stranded on a shore.

In their cupped hands, the godseed beat like a second heart.

It was not what they had imagined when Wren first said the word. Not a jewel, not an ember, nothing so simple. It was a seed the way grief is a seed: small, warm, patient, and capable of becoming anything at all. It pulsed against their palms with the rhythm they had carried all their lives without knowing — the rhythm their ancestor, old Alaric Hartwell, cartographer and binder of gods, had pressed into the family bloodline like a seal pressed into wax. *Sleep*, he had told them. *And I will keep you safe until someone worthy wakes you.*

Seven years of harmless charms. Seven years of selling luck to children and courage to thieves, and all the while the thing in Sebastian's blood had been calling across the veil like a lighthouse keeper who'd forgotten he was lit.

Below them, arranged in the great ring of the temple floor, the world had come to collect.

The Inquisition stood in ranks of grey steel, Inquisitor Hale at their head, his lantern held high and his face carved into the expression of a man who believed mercy was a debt to be repaid in obedience. Behind him, two hundred soldiers. Behind them, the city, sleeping or screaming — Sebastian could no longer tell which sounds drifted down were prayers and which were panic.

To the east, the priesthood: Wren's order in their patched vestments, Mother Sablevein at their fore with her crozier raised, her voice already hoarse from the demands she had shouted up the temple stairs. *Deliver the vessel. Deliver the seed. Let the gods be whole.* Forty faces Sebastian knew by name now, after three weeks underground, and not one of them would look at Wren where she stood apart from them, excommunicate in everything but heart.

And everywhere else — in the walls, in the moonlight, in the space between one heartbeat and the next — the gods.

They did not stand. They did not need to. They were pressure and perfume and the taste of lightning on the back of the tongue. Sebastian felt them the way a mapmaker feels a coastline: as certainty. *Here* was Vellamere's drowned river-god, weeping salt into the stone. *Here* was the twin who kept thresholds, leaning against the doorway of the air. Here were thirteen others, and behind them shapes too vast to name, pressing against the thinned veil like faces against frosted glass.

*Vessel,* they said, in a chorus that made the bones of the temple hum. All of them at once. All of them patient. That was the worst of it — the patience. They had waited under the ice of the world for three hundred years. They could wait out a single mortal's hesitation.

But the moon could not wait. Sebastian watched it climb the last handspan toward zenith, and felt the godseed answer, swelling warm against their fingers, and understood that patience was about to run out for everyone.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had prayed for miracles her whole life, and she had finally learned the lesson too late: never pray without specifying the terms.

She stood between the Inquisition's line and the priesthood's ring, in the exact center of the temple floor, in the exact place a person stands when they have burned every bridge behind them and decided to call it a road. Her order's crozier-light fell on her shoulders like a sentence. Hale's lantern fell on her like a question. And above it all the moon poured down its terrible abundance, filling the temple with light so thick it seemed Wren could have waded through it.

Three weeks ago she had led a congregation of sixty frightened souls in a cellar beneath the Tallow District. They had prayed for bread, and bread had come. They had prayed for a sick child's fever to break, and it had. They had prayed for the landlord who beat his wife to suffer, and he had fallen down his stairs and died, and the wife had wept at the funeral and thanked the gods, and Wren had lain awake all night understanding that something had answered her people with teeth.

Now she understood the rest of it. Now she stood beneath the moving ceiling and looked up at Sebastian Hartwell — the charm-seller, the mapmaker, the lover she had lost to the Ban and found again in the dark — holding the end of the world in their hands, and she understood that the miracle had a name, and the name was *Hartwell*, and the miracle had been sleeping beside her all along.

*Wren.*

The gods spoke to her too now. They had ever since the night market, since the battle, since she had chosen Sebastian over her vows with her whole body and not regretted it once. Their voices were honey and iron. *You served us faithfully. Tell your vessel what is asked. One life offered freely, and we return, and no child of Vellamere ever kneels to hunger again.*

"And what happens to the vessel?" Wren asked aloud, because she had learned that the gods preferred you didn't ask, and asking anyway was the only prayer left worth saying.

Silence, sweet and terrible. The silence of an answer everyone already knows.

Mother Sablevein's voice cracked across the temple like a whip. "Daughter Calloway. You stand in the house of your gods wearing the face of a traitor. Step aside."

"I was never your daughter," Wren said, and found it was true, and found it cost less than she feared. "I was theirs." She lifted her chin toward the dais, toward Sebastian, silhouetted against the descending moon. "And I am theirs."

"Sentiment," Hale said. His voice carried the way cold carries. He stepped forward from his line, and the soldiers moved with him, a tide of grey. "Priestess. You've seen what the answered become when the seed stays unclaimed another season. Half the Tallow District is growing things in their lungs. Miracles with teeth, eating the faithful from inside. Every day this goes unclaimed, the city bleeds." He raised his lantern, and in its light his eyes were almost kind, which was how Wren knew he had already decided everything. "Master Hartwell. Last offer. Give the Inquisition the seed, walk free with full clemency, marry your priestess, live a long dull life. Or refuse, and I take it from your corpse, and my men are not gentle with witnesses."

Wren turned, and looked up, and found Sebastian already looking down at her.

They had crossed the unmapped realms together. She had seen them confess the whole rotten truth of it — the ancestor, the binding, the seed in the blood, seven years of guilt worn like a coat — and she had not flinched, and they had kissed her in a forest that grew upside-down, and somewhere between one warped horizon and the next she had stopped believing that love was a thing you had to choose against your duty.

So she gave them the only thing she had left to give. Not an argument. Not a plea. Just her face, open, certain, saying what her mouth could not say in front of gods and Inquisitors: *Whatever you choose, I chose you first.*

Sebastian smiled. It was the smile from the night market, from before everything — wry, tired, a little bit wicked — and Wren felt her heart break cleanly, like a branch, the way wood breaks when it has finally grown strong enough to hold weight.

* * *

**Sebastian**

"Everyone wants a sacrifice tonight," Sebastian said, and their voice rang off the moving ceiling and came back to them multiplied, thirteenfold, thirty-fold, the temple itself a choir rehearsing their words. "The gods want mine. The Inquisition wants my corpse's. The priesthood wants my surrender." They laughed, and heard how thin it sounded, and let it sound thin. Honesty, at the end. They owed the room honesty. "I've spent seven years learning what happens to cities when powerful things get exactly what they ask for. I mapped the aftermath. There isn't a scale fine enough to draw it."

"The seed must be claimed," the gods thundered, gentle as an avalanche. "It cannot remain. It will burn the vessel or the vessel will burn the world."

"You're right," Sebastian said. "It can't remain."

They opened their hands.

The godseed rose — slowly, the way a soap bubble rises, catching the moonlight and breaking it into colors that had no names before magic was outlawed — and the whole temple inhaled at once. Two hundred soldiers. Forty priests. Thirteen gods pressing at the veil. One excommunicate priestess with her fists clenched white at her sides, trusting him, trusting *them*, in front of everyone, which was braver than any martyrdom Sebastian had ever charted the ruins of.

They thought of their grandfather teaching them to draw coastlines. They thought of the child at their stall whose prayer had started all of this, whose voice no one else had heard. They thought of Alaric Hartwell, three hundred years dead, kneeling in this same temple with the same light in his hands, choosing to bind instead of to burn, believing some future descendant might find a better answer.

*I'm sorry,* Sebastian told him silently. *There wasn't one. But here's mine.*

They had spent three weeks, in the unmapped realms, doing the only thing they had ever truly been good at. They had mapped the godseed. Not its power — its *shape*. Every vein of it, every root of it, every hidden channel where it threaded through Sebastian's blood like rivers threading a delta. And every map, if you know how to read it, contains the means of its own unmaking: fold it right, cut along the crease, and the territory comes apart along lines no one drew but you.

Sebastian folded themselves along the crease.

It did not feel like dying. It felt like the last page of an atlas being torn out — a clean rip, a small grief, a sudden openness where a border used to be. The godseed screamed, a sound like a choir losing its note mid-verse, and Sebastian screamed with it, and drove the fold home, and *shattered* it.

Light went everywhere. It went into the soldiers and made them drop their swords and remember their mothers' kitchens. It went into the priests and made Sablevein fall to her knees weeping for reasons she would spend years refusing to name. It went into the gods — and the gods *receded*, not destroyed, not banished, only veiled, folded away behind a new sky of Sebastian's making, their patience finally answered with a door gently closed. The moonlight thinned to ordinary silver. The ceiling stilled. Somewhere far above, real clouds were crossing a real moon, indifferent and beautiful, and the pressure that had lived in Vellamere's bones for three weeks simply — lifted.

Sebastian fell.

Wren caught them. Of course she caught them; she had been positioned to catch them since the night market, since the beginning of the world. They lay together on the cold temple stones while the factions around them stood frozen in the wreckage of their demands, each holding a sacrifice that no longer existed, each staring at the one choice no prophecy had predicted: *neither*. Not god, not weapon, not martyr. Just a mapmaker with empty hands and a priestess with empty arms full of them anyway.

"Your magic," Wren whispered, touching their face. "Sebastian, your—"

"Gone," Sebastian rasped, and grinned up at her through the blood and tears and ordinary moonlight. "All of it. I'm just a person now."

Wren laughed — wet, disbelieving, the best sound in the newly quiet world. "Just a person," she repeated. "You absolute idiot. You brilliant, impossible—"

The temple doors burst open above, spilling torchlight down the stairs, and boots began to hammer downward — Hale's survivors, or the priesthood's hardliners, or both, converging on the one thing in Vellamere that still mattered to anyone: the two people lying in the ruin of the miracle, alive.

Sebastian tightened their grip on Wren's hand.

"Can you still run?" they asked.

Wren pulled them upright, kissed them once, fierce and quick, and said, "I know a way out that isn't on any map."

"Those are," Sebastian said, finding their feet, finding her, finding the dark stairwell and the dawn beyond it, "the only kind worth taking."
