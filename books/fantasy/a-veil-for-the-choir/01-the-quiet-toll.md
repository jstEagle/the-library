## 1. The Quiet Toll

The mule spoke first.

*Cold coming down the wrong side of the ridge,* she said, in the way mules say things—which is to say not in words at all, but in a pressure behind Isandro's eyes, a taste like iron and pine sap at the back of the throat. *Cold, and men under it.*

Isandro stopped on the switchback and pretended to adjust a strap, the way they had pretended a thousand times before. Behind them, the smuggling party strung itself along the trail like beads on a fraying cord: six clients, two hired blades, forty crates of untaxed brandy and one crate of something that had made even the hired blades nervous. Above them, Thornfell's peaks wore their first white of the season like a crown that had been set down carelessly. Below, the valley floor was already drowning in blue dusk.

*Where,* Isandro asked the mule, without moving their lips.

*Behind the split rock. Four. Bows.* A flicker of the mule's own opinion came with it, sour as old hay: *Stupid men. They smell of fear and cheap tobacco.*

Isandro had spent eleven years learning to make their gift small. Not gone—gifts like theirs didn't go, they only waited, coiled in the marrow like a second spine—but small. A whisper instead of a voice. They listened to the beasts the way a man might listen to a river: attentively, gratefully, and never letting on that the river spoke back. The smugglers who hired them knew Isandro Reyes could read a trail the way priests read scripture. They did not know the trail read back, nor that Isandro could ask it questions, nor that somewhere in the dark behind the split rock, four men with crossbows were already known to them by name, temper, and the state of their boots.

That was the arrangement. That was the veil. Silence traded for safety, year over year, like a toll paid at a gate no one else could see.

*Four men,* Isandro thought now, *and six clients, and the first snow coming down the wrong side of the ridge.* The wrong side. That was the part that itched. Snow came down the north faces in Thornfell, predictable as tax collectors. Snow coming up from the south meant weather that had been pushed, and weather that had been pushed meant someone had paid a storm-caller, and storm-callers did not work for men who wanted brandy.

They wanted something else.

Isandro turned and raised one hand, the old smuggler's signal for *hold*, and watched it travel down the line: Hesk the blade passing it to the fat wool-merchant Veyra, Veyra to her nervous clerk, the clerk to the last client in the line—a thin figure in a scholar's grey coat, hood up, carrying a satchel the way a man carries a child through a plague town. Clutched. Guarded. The scholar had paid triple rate and asked no questions, which in Isandro's experience meant he had answers he didn't want shared.

*Everyone behind the rock shelf,* Isandro signed. *Now. Quietly.*

Hesk's eyes narrowed. Twenty years of knife-work had given him a face like a closed door, but he knew Isandro's instincts had a reputation that outpaced any explanation, and reputation was the only currency Isandro had ever allowed themselves. He moved. The clients moved. The mules moved, grumbling in Isandro's skull about the indignity of hurrying.

The split rock passed on their left, a granite fist the size of a chapel, furred with lichen the color of old bruises. And as the last mule's tail cleared it, Isandro felt the ambush break—not heard, *felt*, four heartbeats spiking in the dark like dropped stones, and then the whistle of a bowstring being drawn by a man who had just realized his quarry had stopped exactly where he least wanted it to.

The bolt took the mule in the shoulder.

*

She screamed, and the scream went through Isandro like a hook through the soft part of the hand. Not the flesh-scream—flesh healed, flesh was honest—but the other thing, the thing no one else on that trail could hear: her voice tearing, her terror flooding the link between them, *betrayed, betrayed, the cold men, the cold men—*

The veil tore with her.

Isandro did not decide it. That was the truth they would worry at for years afterward, the way a tongue worries a cracked tooth: there was no decision, only the bolt, and the blood steaming on the frost-dusted stone, and the four men rising from behind the rocks with their crossbows, and the smallness Isandro had built over eleven years simply—shattering. Like ice under a boot. Like a held breath finally let go.

They stood up straight in the middle of the trail, in front of six strangers and two killers, opened their mouth, and *spoke*.

Not words. Not the way people speak. Isandro's gift, unhooded, was a voice that lived underneath language, in the register where the mountain itself kept its old thoughts. It went out of them like a bell being struck inside the chest, and every living thing on that trail heard it.

The mules stopped screaming and turned, all of them together, toward the split rock.

The wolves came down off the high shoulder of the pass—three of them, grey as the coming snow, the pack Isandro had traded meat and news with for a decade—and hit the ambush line before the second bolt could be loosed. Isandro felt the men's courage break against the wolves' certainty, felt it snap like green wood. *Run,* Isandro told them, in that under-voice, in the voice that was not supposed to exist, that was a hanging offense in two provinces and a burning offense in the third. *Run down-valley and do not stop and do not come back to this pass while you draw breath.*

They ran. Men with crossbows, running, stumbling, one of them weeping, and the wolves pacing them to the treeline like black thoughts at the edge of a mind, and then gone.

Silence came back to the pass the way water comes back into a footprint.

Isandro stood very still, chest heaving, and made the mistake of turning around.

Six clients. Two blades. All of them staring. Hesk's hand was still on his knife—not pointed at Isandro, not yet, but *awake*, the way a dog wakes. Veyra the wool-merchant had gone the color of tallow. Her clerk had sat down in the snow without appearing to notice he'd done it.

And the scholar in the grey coat had his hood back.

He was younger than his coat suggested, sharp-faced, with the wind-burned look of a man who had traveled hard and fast and recently. His eyes were not afraid. That was the thing Isandro would remember longest, the thing that would keep them awake in the nights after: everyone else on that trail wore fear, but the scholar wore something hungrier. Recognition. He was looking at Isandro the way a man looks at a door he has been searching a wall for.

"You spoke," the scholar said. Not a question. His voice was hoarse, reverent, ruined with something that was not quite awe. "I *knew* it. The texts said—the old texts said there would be—"

"Pack it up," Hesk said loudly, to the trail in general, to the world. "Mule's dead. Cut the load off, redistribute, we move in ten minutes. We move *fast*." His eyes found Isandro's, and what was in them was not fear either. It was arithmetic. Hesk was calculating what a beast-speaker who could call wolves out of a winter sky was worth, and to whom, and what it would cost him to be the man who sold the answer.

Isandro had spent eleven years being nobody. A good guide. A quiet guide. A man whose gift was a rumor too faint to repeat. And in the space of one mis-thrown bolt, the veil was gone, and they could feel it settling over the trail like ash: the scholar's hungry eyes, the blade's arithmetic, the clients already rearranging their memories of the evening into stories they would tell in low rooms in low towns.

*Invisibility is a debt,* Isandro's grandmother used to say, back when Isandro had believed the old woman was only being grim in the way mountain women were allowed to be grim. *You pay it every day in small coins. And one day the collector comes for the whole sum at once.*

The collector had come. The snow was starting to fall—softly, gently, down the *wrong* side of the ridge—and the dead mule's blood was already crusting pink on the stone, and Isandro knelt beside her and put a hand on her cooling neck and let her feel, one last time, that she had not been betrayed by the one human who knew her name.

*I'm sorry,* they told her. *You warned me and I was too slow.*

*You spoke,* she said, in the fading pressure behind Isandro's eyes, and there was no accusation in it, only wonder, the way there is wonder in animals for everything large and inevitable. *After so long. You spoke.*

* * *

They made camp two miles on, in a hollow the wolves had once shown Isandro, walled on three sides by stone. The clients slept badly and close together. Hesk took first watch and did not look at Isandro once, which was worse than looking.

The scholar did not sleep at all.

Isandro found him sitting at the hollow's mouth with his satchel open on his knees, watching the snow come down. Up close he smelled of ink and road dust and something older underneath, like a book that had been rained on and dried a hundred times. He didn't startle when Isandro sat down beside him. He had been waiting.

"You'll want to know my name," he said. "Before you decide what to do with me. It's Wren. Wren Halloran. Late of the Collegium at Aurelian, formerly of good standing, currently of none." He smiled, and it was a strange smile, crooked and terrified and lit from inside. "I've been looking for you for two years, Isandro Reyes. And I have a book you need to see before the snow gets deep enough to seal the passes."

The snow, Isandro noticed, was still falling on the wrong side of the ridge. Falling harder now. Falling like it had somewhere to be.

"Tomorrow," Isandro said.

Wren shook his head slowly, and pulled something from the satchel—something wrapped in oilcloth, small and heavy and bound, even through the wrapping, with a smell like birchbark and old smoke.

"Tonight," he said. "I've read the ending. The long winter finishes falling *tonight*."
