## 2. A Book Bound in Birchbark

The fire in the safehouse had burned down to a red memory by the time Wren could no longer stand her own silence.

She had been a scholar of the Aurelian Archive for eleven years. She knew how to wait out a difficult text, how to sit with a passage until it yielded, and she had told herself that this was the same—that Isandro Reyes was simply a difficult text, closed and dense and written in a hand she hadn't yet learned to read. But the analogy kept breaking down, because texts did not watch her back when she moved toward her pack.

"You've been staring at that satchel since dusk," Isandro said from the doorway, where they'd been mending a strap with the particular economy of someone who expected their tools to outlive them. "Whatever's in it, you've been deciding whether to show me for two days."

Wren's hands went still on the buckle. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters of the abandoned shepherd's hut, and somewhere down the slope a fox screamed—short, sharp, cut off. She thought of all the things she had carried across half the country to keep them from burning: three notebooks, a seal of office she no longer had the right to wear, and the book.

"It's not mine," she said. "That's the first thing you should know. I didn't write it. I don't even think a person wrote it—not one person, not with one life to work in."

Isandro set down the strap. They did not ask what are you talking about; they only waited, which was somehow worse, and Wren understood suddenly why smugglers paid this person so well and asked so little. Silence, here, was a currency. Everyone in the room was rich with it.

She unbuckled the satchel and drew out the book.

It was bound in birchbark, pale as bone, stitched with sinew gone dark as old tea. The cover had been peeled from a living tree in a single unbroken sheet—she'd checked, years ago, with a magnifier and a guilty heart—and along its edges ran a script that predated the Archive's founding by centuries. The pages inside were not paper. They were vellum so fine it was translucent, and when she tilted it toward the firelight, faint veins showed through, like something that had once been alive and remembered it.

"The Catalogue of Falling," she said. "That's what the marginalists called it. There were copies once—nine, maybe twelve. The Archive burned six of them deliberately, in 1801, and called the fires accidents." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the lecturing tone she'd sworn off when they struck her name from the rolls. "I stole this one. That's why I'm here. That's why I'm anywhere."

"Stealing gets people hanged," Isandro said. "In the capital it gets them hanged politely."

"In the capital it gets them *quietly*, which is worse." Wren turned the book over in her lap. Her palms had sweated through to the bark. "I was a reader in the Prophecy Division. Third rank. Do you know what we actually did, most days? We reconciled dates. We argued about commas. Real prophecy is rare—it has to be *given*, not guessed—and the Catalogue is real. Every prediction in it that can be checked has come true. The famine of 1823, down to the month. The collapse of the Marrow Bridge. The year the wolves left Thornfell entirely." She looked up. "And one entry that isn't a prediction at all. It's an address. A naming."

The wind rose against the hut. In the hearth the embers shifted with a sound like a held breath.

Isandro crossed the room and sat, finally, on the far side of the fire—the side closest to the door, Wren noticed, the side with the sightline to the pass. Even now. Even listening.

"Show me," they said.

* * *

The page was numbered in a cipher Wren had spent four years breaking, and she turned to it without looking, the way a musician turns to a well-worn measure. The script was old High Thornean, brushed in iron-gall ink that had oxidized to a soft sepia. Above the entry, someone long dead had drawn a mountain in section—cut away like a cake—to show the chambers beneath, and around those chambers, small patient circles like lamps.

"*When the debt walks down the blood again,*" Wren read, translating as she went, keeping her voice steady the way one keeps a lantern steady in wind, "*the Reyes who speaks will come under the stone. What was sold shall be taken back, and the court beneath the mountain shall be unmade by its own hunger.*"

The words sat in the air between them. Isandro's face did nothing at all. It was the finest performance of blankness Wren had ever seen, and she had studied under masters of dissemblance.

"There are other Reyeses," Isandro said.

"Not with that surname in that valley. Not in a century." Wren tapped the margin, where a later hand—a copyist, or the original giver—had added a gloss in a rougher script: *the speaker of beasts.* "And there's more. Listen to the last line. This is the part the Archive couldn't reconcile, the part that got me laughed out of the colloquium when I presented it, because they thought it was corruption in the text. But it isn't. It's a date. Prophecy doesn't give dates—except when it's already too late to matter."

She read it slowly, letting each word land:

"*And the long winter finishes falling tonight.*"

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them snow was beginning somewhere else, and neither of them knew it yet.

"It means nothing," Isandro said. "It's a bad translation of a bad copy. Your colleagues were right to laugh."

"They weren't laughing at the translation. They were laughing because a third-rank reader stood up and said the book named a living person, and named a date, and both were verifiable." Wren closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "So I verified. Four months in the archives, cross-checking parish records and toll ledgers. There is exactly one line of Reyes in these mountains, Isandro. And I found you."

"You found a smuggler who talks to goats."

"Do you?" Wren asked quietly. "Talk to them? Or do they talk to you?"

For a moment—one breath, two—something moved behind Isandro's eyes, quick and feral, like a shape crossing a lit window in a house everyone believed empty. Then it was gone, shuttered, and the blankness came back down like a visor.

"I guide animals," Isandro said. "It's a knack. Like having a good whistle."

"A knack doesn't make a wolf pack hold a kill for you. A knack doesn't get ravens following your route for days, waiting to be told things." Wren leaned forward. She knew she should stop; she heard the zealotry climbing into her own voice and hated it and could not stop it, because four years of ridicule had been leading to this fire, this night, this face across from hers. "The beasts on the passes know you, Isandro. Not fear you—*know* you. That's not a whistle. That's the gift, and the book says the gift has a price attached to it, and the price is coming due, and the date is—"

"Stop."

The word cracked across the hut like a whip over livestock. Wren stopped.

Isandro had gone very still, in the way of prey animals and soldiers, and when they spoke again their voice was low and stripped of everything. "You don't know what you're asking me to be. You have a book. Books are paper. I have spent my whole life being *no one*—do you understand? No one asks a nobody for blood. No one follows a nobody home. Whatever my grandmother was, whatever some bark-bound madman wrote a hundred years before I was born, I am not going to die for a metaphor."

"Then don't die for it," Wren said. "Live for it. Come see what's under the mountain, and take back whatever was sold, and prove me wrong. I would *love* to be wrong. Do you know what they do to scholars who are right about things like this?" She laughed, and it came out ragged. "They burn the books first. Then they burn the readers."

Outside, the fox screamed again—or something that used to be a fox—and this time it did not stop quickly.

* * *

They did not sleep.

Wren dozed sitting up around the smallest hours and woke to a cold so precise it felt deliberate. The fire had died to white ash. Her breath hung in the air and did not move, and when she lifted her head, she saw frost on the *inside* of the shutters—in patterns like ferns, like feathers, like the branching lungs of something vast drawing its first slow breath beneath the world.

Isandro was already awake, standing at the window, looking out through a gap in the boards.

"What time is it?" Wren whispered.

"Past the middle of the night." Isandro's voice was strange. Flat, but with something underneath it, the way ice has water underneath. "It shouldn't be cold yet. First snows don't come to this valley till the Feast of Chains. We're three weeks early."

Wren rose and joined them at the gap. Below the hut, the valley lay under a thin silver rime that had not been there at dusk. It crept as she watched—actually crept, she saw it happen, frost advancing down the slope in a slow tide, grass blades stiffening one by one, the stream at the valley's bottom going quiet and glassy from its edges inward. And beyond, up-slope, the high pass where the peaks of Thornfell stood black against the stars: snow was falling there. Only there. A column of white descending out of a clear sky, straight down, unhurried, like ash from a fire no one could see.

"That's not weather," Wren said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Weather doesn't fall in one place."

Isandro said nothing. Their breath fogged the gap in the boards, and in the fog, for just a moment, Wren watched their hand rise halfway—as if to touch the glass, or the frost, or the thing on the other side of both—and then drop.

"The book," Isandro said at last, and their voice was barely there. "Read me the last line again."

"*And the long winter finishes falling tonight.*"

The frost reached the door of the hut with a sound like tiny teeth. Down in the dark of the yard, something moved between the shadows—not the fox, Wren realized, never the fox—and every hair on her arms stood up as Isandro went to the door and opened it, and the cold poured in around them like a tide finding a breach.

Out on the frozen grass, in the blue dark, the beasts were waiting. Dozens of them. Wolves, goats, a lean red fox standing calm as a dog—all facing the hut, all facing Isandro, silent, their breath rising in columns, their eyes catching the starlight.

As if they had come to say goodbye.

Or as if they had come because the mountain had begun, at last, to call its debts home—and they alone knew the roads down.

"Get the book," Isandro said, not turning around. Their voice had changed. It was the voice of someone stepping off a ledge. "We leave before dawn."
