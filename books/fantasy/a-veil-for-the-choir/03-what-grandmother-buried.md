## 3. What Grandmother Buried

The cabin had not been touched in three years, and the mountain had been filling it with itself the whole time: pine needles sifted through the gaps in the shutters, a fine gray drift of them along the windowsill like snow that had forgotten how to melt. Mouse droppings in the crockery. A spider's city strung between the rafters, its silk gone dull as old tallow. The air inside was the smell of every winter at once—woodsmoke soaked into the beams, dried thyme hanging brown from a nail, and beneath it something older, sweeter, like apples rotting in a cellar no one had opened since before Isandro was born.

They stood in the doorway a long time without going in.

It was not grief, exactly, or not only grief. It was the particular reluctance of a person about to open a door they suspected had been waiting for them. Grandmother Serafina had died in this room, in her chair by the cold hearth, so quietly that the neighbors found her two days later with frost on the window glass and a look on her face that the priest called peace and Isandro privately called *satisfaction*. She had been a woman who finished things.

*She knew,* Isandro thought now, crossing the threshold at last, their boots loud on boards that used to creak under smaller feet. *She knew what she was doing when she left me nothing but silence.*

Wren waited outside. The scholar had wanted to come in—of course she had; Wren's curiosity had the manners of a starving dog—but Isandro had told her this was family ground, and to her credit she had only nodded and settled against the fence post with the birchbark book hugged to her chest like a child she wasn't sure she trusted. From the yard came the sound of the beasts: Marrow the mule shifting his weight, the dogs Vesper and Tally circling the perimeter of the clearing with their noses down, reading the ground the way scholars read texts. All of them uneasy. All of them, Isandro realized, facing away from the cabin, toward the low dark notch of the passes where the trees grew wrong—too straight, too close together, as if standing at attention for something underground.

Isandro began with the kitchen because beginning anywhere else would have felt like trespassing on purpose.

Grandmother's things were where she had left them. That was the strange mercy of it. A spoon still in the sugar jar. A darning needle threaded with gray wool, abandoned mid-sock, the sock itself folded over the chair arm with the patience of someone who assumed there would be time. On the table, a ring stain where she had set her cup ten thousand mornings running, worn into the wood dark as a wound. Isandro put their palm flat against it, briefly, and then took their hand back before it could become a ritual.

*You're looking for paper,* they told themselves. *Look for paper.*

The contract, whatever it was—the thing Wren's stolen book had whispered of, the debt that had followed a name down a century of bloodline—would be written somewhere. Grandmother Serafina did nothing unwritten. She kept accounts of everything: eggs, debts owed at the mill, insults received and repaid with interest. Her ledger shelf stood beside the hearth, a row of cracked spines, and Isandro went through them one by one in the failing light, blowing dust off pages dense with a small, upright hand.

1823. The famine year. Everyone in Thornfell still spoke of it the way people speak of a storm that killed someone they loved—the Year the River Lied, the old-timers called it, when the fish vanished and the barley came up black and the valley ate its seed corn and then began to consider eating other things. Isandro had grown up on those stories the way children grow on ghost tales: half-believed, half-felt under the bed. They had never connected them to their grandmother, who had been a girl of seventeen that year, and who never once in Isandro's memory spoke of being hungry.

But here, in a ledger dated the spring of 1824, the entries changed character. No more eggs. No more flour. Instead:

*Paid in full. Paid in full. Paid in full. The valley eats again. Let no Reyes after me go looking under the hill, for what is buried there is mine to bury.*

And beneath that, in letters smaller than any she ever wrote, in a script that seemed almost to crawl off the page when Isandro looked directly at it:

*Transferable by blood. Payable at the court's choosing. The voice or the vein.*

Isandro sat down in grandmother's chair without deciding to. The wood knew their shape better than it should have.

*The voice or the vein.* Wren had said nearly the same words three nights ago, huddled by the fire with the birchbark book open between them, her finger trembling on a line of prophecy: *the Reyes who speaks shall give the buried court either breath or blood, and the long winter finishes falling tonight.* Isandro had laughed at her. It had been an ugly laugh, defensive, the kind you use to scare off your own heartbeat. They had told her the Reyes were stonemasons and smugglers and one famously unlucky fiddler, and that none of them spoke anything worth prophesying about.

Now they sat in the dead woman's chair with the dead woman's handwriting under their thumb and understood that they had lied to Wren out of habit, the way they breathed out of habit, and that the lie had been the last wall between them and a door already open behind them.

There was more. There was always more with grandmothers.

Under the false bottom of the ledger shelf—a bottom Isandro knew about because they had hidden contraband there themselves as a child, and apparently talent ran in the family—lay a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with red thread gone pink with age. Isandro's hands were steady. They noted that with distant professional interest, the way they noted weather. Inside the oilcloth: a sheet of heavy paper the color of old teeth, covered in writing that was not ink.

It was ash. Ground fine, mixed with something that made it cling—spit, maybe, or blood—and brushed onto the page in careful, ceremonial strokes. The ash of what, Isandro could not say. It smelled faintly of burned hair and burnt bread, of offerings, of every small fire a desperate village lights when the real fires won't take.

The words, in that crawling script and in a language Isandro should not have been able to read but found sliding into their understanding like water into sand:

*Let it be known beneath the stone and above it, on this day of the black barley, that Serafina Reyes, daughter of the valley, sells her soul entire to the Court Under the Mountain, that her people may eat. Payment rendered upon her death, or transferable down her bloodline should the Court find the harvest sweeter young. Sealed in ash, which remembers what fire tried to forget.*

Below the signature—her signature, her upright schoolroom hand, *S. Reyes*, with the little flourish she used on receipts—was a mark that made Isandro's skin try to leave their body. Not a seal. A print. Long and narrow, four toes, each tipped with a claw that had pressed hard enough to dent the page, as if something enormous and patient had leaned its weight down through the floor of the world to make certain the terms were understood.

The cabin got very quiet. Quiet the way the woods go quiet, which is not silence but attention.

Outside, Marrow screamed.

Not brayed—screamed, the high human sound a mule only makes when death has a shape, and then the dogs joined in, not barking but that low continuous roaring howl that means *we will not go there, do not ask us*, and Isandro was through the door with the ash-contract crushed against their chest before they remembered having moved.

The clearing had gone blue with dusk. Snow was starting—not falling so much as *arriving*, sifting down through the pines with the unhurried confidence of something that intended to stay until spring. And the beasts had all turned to face the low passes, the notch in the ridge where the smuggler roads dipped down toward the deep valleys, and they stood in a line without knowing they stood in a line, shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, hackles up, ears flat, staring at the dark between the trees.

Vesper broke first. The big shepherd spun and pressed herself against Isandro's legs, shaking, whining—that thin rising sound, over and over, the sound dogs make at funerals. Tally wouldn't come closer to the tree line at all; he'd planted himself by the gate like a creature nailed there. Even Marrow, who had carried Isandro through rockslides and border patrols and one memorable hailstorm without complaint, stood with his head low and his eyes rolled white, pulling at the hitching rail until the wood groaned.

"They won't go," Wren said. She had come off the fence post and stood with the birchbark book clutched to her, snow gathering in her wild hair, and her face in the dusk was the face of someone watching a theory become true. "The beasts. They know the roads better than we do—they've always known—but they won't go down there." She swallowed. "Animals remember longer than archives do, Isandro."

"I know." Their own voice sounded foreign to them. Flat. Steady. Grandmother's voice, actually, the one she'd used for prices. "I know they won't."

"Then how—" Wren stopped. Looked at the contract wrapped in Isandro's arms, at the way Isandro held it, not like evidence but like a wound. "You found it. You found *her* in it."

"She sold her soul." Saying it aloud was like stepping off a ledge; the fall went on longer than expected. "In 1823. To end the famine. And the court let her people eat, and then it let her die quiet in her chair with frost on the window, and then—" Isandro looked down at Vesper trembling against their leg, at the snow thickening over the low passes, at the whole patient dark of the mountain that had been, all their life, just scenery. "Then it kept the receipt. Because the fine print says the debt runs down the bloodline. Me, Wren. I'm the bloodline."

The wind came down off the ridge carrying snow and something else, something underneath the cold—a smell like candle smoke and turned earth, like a cellar opening somewhere far below, and every animal in the clearing shuddered at once.

Isandro should have been afraid. Some part of them registered, distantly, that fear was the appropriate response and would presumably arrive shortly. But what filled them instead was stranger and harder: a heat starting under the breastbone, the same heat that had lived in them since childhood whenever they opened their mouth to the beasts and let the forbidden words come out. Grief, curdling. Fear, setting like cement. Grandmother Serafina had finished things. So would they.

"Tally," Isandro said, and their voice came out low and even and carrying, the smuggler's voice, the voice that had talked tolls out of trolls and bargains out of worse. "Vesper. Marrow. Listen to me."

Three pairs of eyes turned. In the blue snowlight, Isandro held the ash-sealed contract up where the beasts could see it, and let them smell the truth of it, and spoke the way they spoke to animals when no human ears were near—which was to say, honestly, and with the gift riding plain and unhidden under every word.

"We are going down," they said. "I know you don't want to. I don't want to either. But the debt is mine, and I am done letting it grow interest in the dark."

Marrow blew out a long shuddering breath. Vesper's whining faltered, changed key. And Tally—Tally lifted his head and stared past Isandro, past the cabin, down into the notch of the low passes, and gave one single bark, sharp as a struck flint, into the gathering snow.

From somewhere below the tree line, faint as a memory, something answered.
