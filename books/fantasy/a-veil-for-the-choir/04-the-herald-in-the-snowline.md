## 4. The Herald in the Snowline

I had carried a thousand death warrants, and I remembered every one.

That was the cruelty of my office: the court made its herald unforgetting. Each summons I bore was pressed into me like a seal into wax—the name, the debt, the hour of reckoning—and it stayed there, ink-bright, long after the named one had gone to ash or to the lantern-racks below. I could recite the famine-dead of 1823 by heart if anyone asked, though no one ever asked, because the only ones who remembered them were hanging in the cathedral dark, singing.

So when the magister set this latest summons in my hands and said, *You will find them at the snowline*, I already knew how the finding would go. It always went the same way. A name spoken into a cold morning. A face going white as the frost. And then the long walk down, with someone behind me who would never come back up.

Except that this time, the summons burned a little differently against my palms. This time the parchment did not say *come and be paid*. It said something stranger, something the court had not offered in three hundred years:

*The Reyes who speaks may give voice instead of blood.*

*Voice instead of blood.* As if a voice were a small thing. As if it were a coin one kept in a pocket and might part with lightly, the way a merchant pays a toll. The court had grown hungry enough to flatter itself that what it took was painless. Or perhaps—this thought came later, much later—it had grown hungry enough simply to lie.

I climbed out of the deep roads three nights before the first snow, because the mountain told me to. That is not poetry; it is mechanics. Thornfell speaks to those of us bound to it, a low grinding language of settling stone and freezing water, and for the past season it had been saying *soon, soon, soon* the way a clock says the hour before midnight. The gates would seal with the long winter. Whatever happened at the snowline had to happen before the sky finished falling.

I stood where the tree line broke—pines giving way to wind-scoured rock and the first dirty drifts—and I waited, wrapped in a grey cloak the color of weather, wearing the shape I wore among the sunlit world: tall, narrow, wrong only in ways humans rarely noticed but always felt. They would feel it now too, whoever came up that switchback trail. I had been exile long enough to forget how it felt to be on this side of a summons, holding the paper instead of receiving it. My hands shook. Centuries of practice, and they still shook.

They came up at dusk, four of them if you counted the beasts, and I counted the beasts, because the beasts counted me first.

Wolves—no, not wolves, something older and leaner, with frost-colored eyes—came around the bend ahead of the humans and stopped dead in the snow. Their hackles rose. Then the lead one put her nose to the wind, tasted it, and did something I had not seen a beast do in all my years beneath the mountain.

She sat down. Deliberately, facing me. Waiting.

Behind her came the scholar—Wren, the runaway from the capital, recognizable from a hundred wanted-notices I had never delivered because the capital's warrants were not mine to carry—a thin figure buried under satchels, clutching a birchbark-bound book like a shield. Behind Wren, two hired guides with the flat wary eyes of people paid to walk toward danger. And behind them, last, half-hidden in the failing light—

There. The one the summons named.

Isandro Reyes moved like someone who had spent a lifetime learning not to be seen and had gotten so good at it that they had nearly vanished even here, even now, in an empty snowfield with nowhere left to hide. Small corrections of posture. Eyes that touched everything and settled on nothing. But the beasts knew. The beasts had arranged themselves around Isandro the way water arranges itself around a stone it has decided to keep.

I stepped out of the shadow of the rocks and let them see me.

The effect was immediate and satisfying in a grim, familiar way. One guide swore and reached for iron. Wren made a sound like a hinge. The lead beast growled low, then hushed herself, watching Isandro rather than me—which told me more than any interrogation could have about where authority actually lay in this ragged company.

"Isandro Reyes." I said the name the way the summons required, full and formal, letting the old cadences carry it. "Hear me."

"Don't," Isandro said.

It was barely a whisper, but it stopped me mid-breath. Not fear—I had heard fear, I had catalogued every species of it across centuries of cold mornings. This was something else. This was the voice of someone begging a storm not to notice them.

"You know why I'm here," I said, gentler.

"I don't want to know."

"The court knows your name." The parchment warmed in my hands, eager, and I hated it. "The debt of 1823 stands unpaid. Your grandmother's signature is in the book, sealed in her own ash. By the old terms, the bloodline owes—"

"I know what it owes." Isandro's jaw was tight. Snow had begun to fall between us, fine and dry, the first grains of the long winter, and each flake seemed to land on their shoulders with weight. "I found the contract. I've read it. I've read it until the words stopped meaning anything."

"Then hear the amendment." I unfolded the summons. My breath fogged. "The magister offers terms. Come down before the gates seal, stand before the court, and give your voice freely given—and all debts end. Yours. Your line's. Every soul tied to the Reyes name walks free of it."

Silence, except the wind and the soft hiss of falling snow.

Then Wren pushed forward, eyes blazing behind cracked spectacles. "Freely given," the scholar repeated. "That phrase. In the corpus of fae law, *freely given* has—" Wren flipped open the birchbark book one-handed, pages riffling. "Here. *What is freely surrendered cannot be freely reclaimed.* Do you understand what they're asking? Not your life. Your gift. Permanently. The speaking, the listening, all of it, cut out at the root."

"I understand the offer," I said quietly.

"Do you?" Wren rounded on me, and there was fury in it, but underneath the fury, fear—the specific fear of a scholar who has read too many endings. "You're their herald. You've carried how many of these? How many times have you stood on a mountainside and told someone the price, and smiled while you did it?"

"I have never once smiled." The words came out of me flat and cold and true, and Wren blinked. "I have carried nine hundred and forty-one summonses. I remember every name. I remember the smell of every morning I spoke them in. I remember a woman in this very valley, sixty years ago, who asked me if she could bring her dog." My voice did something I did not authorize. It roughened. "The dog wasn't permitted. She went anyway. I heard her singing under the mountain for eleven years before her lantern dimmed."

No one moved. The snow fell.

"So no," I said. "I am not here because I love the work. I am here because this time—this once—the summons in my hands is not a death warrant. It is the closest thing to mercy the court has offered since before I was born. And I thought—" I stopped. Started again, lower. "I thought they should hear it from someone who remembers what refusal costs."

Isandro looked at me then. Really looked, for the first time—the veil of their invisibility slipping just enough for me to see through to the person underneath, exhausted and furious and grieving, a century of inherited debt sitting on shoulders built for carrying packs, not dynasties.

"And if I refuse?" they said. "If I run?"

"The gates seal with the winter. Nothing passes—not you, not the debt, not the beasts. The court can wait. The court is very good at waiting. And the snow lasts as long as the court decides it lasts." I glanced up. The flakes were thickening, swirling, beginning to fall with intent. "You have perhaps two days. Three if the mountain loves you. It doesn't. It's bound to your family same as I am, and it resents it."

One of the hired guides spat, muttered something about pay and madness, and started back down the trail. His companion hesitated, looked at the beasts, looked at the snow, and followed. Neither of them would be missed. Wren watched them go with the resignation of someone whose companions had a short average lifespan.

Isandro turned away from me. Stood facing the valley, where the frost was creeping down from the peaks like a tide coming in, silvering the pines one by one. When they finally spoke, they didn't turn around.

"Why are you really here?"

The question hung in the cold. I could have given the official answer. I was good at official answers; I had delivered nine hundred and forty-one of them.

"Because I guarded those gates for four hundred years," I said. "And in four hundred years, I watched the court take and take and take, and I told myself it was law, it was order, it was the old bargain that keeps the world balanced. And then they exiled me—for a mercy. For opening a gate one hour early for a dying man so he could see the sun once more before his lantern was lit. One hour. They stripped my office for one hour of kindness." I stepped closer, snow crunching under my boots, close enough that Isandro finally turned. "So yes. I carry the summons. But I'm offering something the summons doesn't include. I know the deep roads. I know which gates are watched and which have rotted from neglect. I know the court's rhythms, when the magister sleeps, when the lantern-hall goes dark. If you mean to go down there—if you mean to do more than kneel and pay—"

"We're going to steal her back," Isandro said.

Not a question. Not a plan either, exactly. Just a fact laid bare on the snow between us, plain as a drawn knife.

Something in my chest that had been packed in ice for four hundred years shifted, groaned, and began—very slowly—to thaw.

"Then you'll need a guide who knows the dark," I said.

The lead beast rose from the snow, shook herself, and trotted over to press her shoulder briefly against my leg—an endorsement, cool and provisional, the way beasts grant trust. Isandro watched this with an expression I couldn't read, and then held out one hand, gloved, chapped, steady.

"Cael," I said, taking it. "My name is Cael. It seemed you should have it, if we're going to die together."

A flicker at the corner of their mouth. Almost a smile. The first warmth of anything, struck like flint in a wet wood.

"Astrid's grandmother used to tell stories about the herald of the gates," Isandro said. "Said he was the only beautiful thing the mountain ever grew."

Above us, the long winter gathered itself, and began, in earnest, to fall.
