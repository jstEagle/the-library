## 5. Toll Roads and Old Debts

The mountain kept its smuggler roads the way old women kept grudges: close, and in the dark.

Isandro went first with a shuttered lantern held low, so the light spilled only across their own boots and the next three paces of the Hollow Stair—a goat-track the Thornfell cartels had widened just enough for mules, then deliberately let the scree swallow again. Behind them came Cael, moving like he'd been poured down the mountain rather than walking it, and behind him Wren, who descended the way scholars did everything: narrating her own missteps under her breath. The beasts brought up the rear—Marrow the gray wolf-dog at Wren's flank, the two shaggy fell-ponies with their hooves wrapped in sacking so the stone wouldn't hear them coming.

The stone heard everything. That was the first rule of the deep roads, and Isandro had never once broken it aloud: *the stone hears, and some of what it hears, it keeps.*

They smelled the river before they saw it—that cold iron smell of snowmelt forced through limestone—and then the passage opened into the Vaulted Narrows, where the water ran black and fast beneath ceilings hung with pale root-like growths that weren't roots at all. Isandro raised two fingers and stopped the column with nothing but the gesture. The beasts sat. Wren, to her credit, actually shut up.

Three flat stones broke the current's surface, spaced too far apart for any honest traveler's stride.

"Trolls," Cael murmured. It wasn't a question. His eyes had gone silver at the edges, catching light that wasn't there.

"River-trolls. The Gault sisters." Isandro set the lantern down on the bank, unhooded it, and let the flame stand tall. "There are courtesies. Watch my back, don't stare at their hands, and whatever they offer you to eat—"

"I've served a fae court for three hundred years," Cael said dryly. "I know not to eat."

"You've served *their* court. This is a toll road." Isandro crouched at the waterline and knocked twice on a stone, then once more. The ripples answered wrong—moving upstream, gathering shape.

The Gault sisters rose out of the river like the river deciding to stand up. The eldest came first, moss-green and dripping, her face all knuckle and jaw, her voice a wet gravel sound. "Little whisperer." She said it almost fondly. "You haven't come this way since the spring thaw. What do you carry?"

"Passage for five and silence besides," Isandro said. "And I carry payment, not cargo." They unwrapped the bundle from their pack: three smoked trout from the valley, salt-cured with rowan ash—which trolls couldn't abide but respected as an honest insult—and a jar of beeswax for the middle sister's cracked hide, and for the youngest, who was watching from the water with enormous yellow eyes, a ribbon. Red. Nothing else. Just a ribbon.

The youngest made a sound like water going over a long fall and snatched it with a hand like a bundle of drowned reeds.

The eldest counted the fish with one finger, sniffed them, found the rowan ash, and laughed—a horrible boulder-tumbling laugh. "Still cheeky. Still paying insults wrapped in courtesy." Her gaze slid past Isandro to Cael, and everything about her changed; the humor drained out of her face and left something older. "You bring a *herald* down my stairs."

"He's exiled," Isandro said evenly. "He carries no warrants here."

"Exiled things are still what they are." But she stepped aside, gesturing at the crossing stones with a dripping arm. "Go, then. Pay your other debts lower down. And little whisperer—" her yellow eyes found Isandro's and held them, and Isandro felt the weight of being *seen*, which they had spent thirty years learning to avoid, "—the river's been tasting strange since the frost came. Sweet, like something remembering. Whatever sleeps under this mountain is waking hungry."

Isandro thanked her, because you always thanked a troll, and crossed.

* * *

Wren didn't speak until they were an hour deeper, camped in a bell-shaped chamber where an old charcoal-burner's vent still drew air from somewhere far above. Then she couldn't hold it anymore. She spread the birchbark book on her knees like a priest laying out relics, firelight making the old glyphs swim.

"I worked it out on the stair," she said. "I should have seen it before. I *did* see it—I just didn't understand what I was reading. Everyone assumed the court *imprisoned* souls. That's how the capital tells it. Souls stolen, souls chained, the whole melodrama of it." She shook her head. "It's worse than that. Look—the glyph here, the one I translated as 'kept'? It has a second reading. '*Remembered*.' Same sign. The court doesn't keep souls the way a jailer keeps prisoners. It feeds on them the way a candle feeds on wax."

Isandro fed a stick to the fire and didn't answer.

"It eats *being remembered*," Wren pressed on. "That's why it takes souls instead of lives. A dead soul in the world above fades—one generation, maybe two, and no one living remembers your face or your name, and whatever's left of you gutters out on its own. But hang a soul in a lantern in the deep dark, where time runs slow and it can't be forgotten because it's *right there*, burning—then you can feed on its light for centuries." She looked up, and her eyes were bright with the awful joy of a scholar whose theory has come true. "The famine of 1823 wasn't the court's cruelty. It was its *harvest*. Three hundred souls sold by desperate families, and each sale bought a season of grain. It ate their remembrance, and the valley forgot them so thoroughly that no one even asks anymore why there are graves older than any names on them."

Cael had gone very still across the fire. When he spoke, his voice was careful, stripped of everything herald-smooth. "That's how it was in my grandmother's time too. Bad winters. Bargains. We called it the Tithe and pretended it was honorable." He turned his silver-rimmed eyes on Isandro. "Your grandmother's lantern is still lit down there. Which means someone still remembers her."

"You," Isandro said quietly.

"And you. You've carried her all your life without knowing her name meant anything." He hesitated, and then said the thing Isandro had been circling for two days. "If we take her lantern back—if she passes on, free—she'll be forgotten properly. That's what passing on *is*. The court will have eaten everything except what she chooses to keep. Are you ready for that? To lose her completely in order to save her?"

The fire popped. Somewhere far off through the stone, water dripped with the patience of mountains.

"I never had her to lose," Isandro said at last. "I had a cabin that smelled of pine resin and a woman who taught me which beasts lie and which ones only gossip. If freeing her means she gets to be *someone's* memory instead of the court's meal—then that's not losing her. That's burying her. Properly, this time."

Marrow laid his heavy head across Isandro's boot, and stayed there until the watch changed.

* * *

They reached the Wither Bridge at dawn-that-wasn't-dawn, deep enough now that the sky was a rumor and the only morning was the one Isandro invented by lighting the lanterns. The bridge was a single span of black stone over a gorge that breathed—actual breath, slow and cold, air rising from depths no map admitted to. And on the bridge's far footing sat the wight.

It looked like a heap of road-moss until it looked back. Eyes like coins left in a well. It had guarded this crossing since before the smugglers' grandmothers were born, and it took exactly one kind of payment, and it took it seriously.

Isandro approached alone, as the rules required, and stood at the near end of the bridge.

"A toll," said the wight, in a voice like pebbles settling. "A true thing, truly named. Names only. Nothing else weighs."

Two days ago, Isandro would have paid with a dead man's name from a parish ledger, purchased in the valley for exactly this purpose. They still had three such names folded in oilcloth in their breast pocket. Cheap coin. Coin that cost them nothing.

But the wight's coin-eyes were fixed on them, and the gorge breathed, and somewhere above—Isandro could feel it now, a pressure like a hand laid flat against the world—the snow was falling.

They drew out the oilcloth. Unfolded it. Readied the first borrowed name on their tongue.

And then thought of Wren's book lying open in the firelight. *A true thing, truly named.* All day the words had been turning in them like a swallowed stone: the court eats remembrance; the debt is paid in blood or voice; *the Reyes who speaks*. Everything they owed, everything anyone owed, traced back to names—sold, hidden, hoarded, forgotten. Their whole life was a purse of counterfeit coin. Silence passed off as safety. Anonymity spent like it would never run out.

Invisibility, Isandro had learned on the worst night of last autumn, was also a debt. And debts came due.

They let the borrowed names fall into the gorge, paper fluttering down into the breathing dark, and gave the wight the truth instead.

"My family name is Reyes," they said, and their voice cracked on it like ice on a stream, the first time it had been spoken aloud in this mountain in a hundred years. "My grandmother was Sela Reyes, who sang to goats and cheated at cards. My name is Isandro. I am the one who speaks to beasts, and I am going down to take back what my blood owes."

Silence. The gorge breathed in. The gorge breathed out.

The wight rose—all of it, unfolding taller than any man, moss streaming off shoulders of weathered stone—and inclined its head with terrible ceremony. "Paid in full," it said. "And witnessed." It stepped aside from the bridge. "Go swiftly, Isandro Reyes. The winter is sealing the high passes even now. By tomorrow's dusk, every gate but the court's own will be closed until spring."

Behind Isandro, Wren whispered, "*Witnessed,*" in the hollow voice of a scholar watching a prophecy sharpen its knife.

They crossed. And when Isandro looked back from the far side, the snow had begun—not above them, where no sky remained, but filtering down through the mountain's high vents, fine and glittering and endless, sifting into the deep roads they had just walked. Sealing them, flake by patient flake, like a door being quietly closed behind a guest who would not be permitted to leave the same way they came in.
