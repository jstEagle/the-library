## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Cael had walked this road before, but never from below.

The last time he descended toward the cathedral cavern, he had come down as a herald, silver-braided, carrying a summons written on vellum cured in moonlight. He had known every turning, every threshold-chant, every name of the doorwardens who bowed as he passed. That was three hundred years ago, before his exile, before the magister's voice had stripped his titles like bark off a wet branch. Now he came down as what the court would see: a traitor, leading thieves.

He felt it in the stone first. Thornfell's deep roads had always hummed beneath his feet—a low chord the mountain held the way a throat holds a note. But here, near the heart, the chord changed. It thickened. It *sang*.

And Cael knew that song. He had heard it at every harvest, every feeding, drifting up through the flagstones of the court while the lanterns burned low and the magister counted what was owed.

"Stop," Isandro said softly.

They all stopped. Ahead, the tunnel opened its mouth into darkness so complete it seemed to drink the light of Wren's lamp rather than merely swallow it. The beasts pressed close to Isandro's legs—the grey wolf-bitch with her torn ear, and the old mule-shaped thing that was no mule at all—and their hackles rose as one animal.

"It knows we're here," Isandro said. Not a question.

"The court has known since you crossed the river-troll's ford." Cael kept his voice level. "It has been listening to you the whole way down. Listening the way a starving man listens to a kitchen."

Wren clutched the birchbark book to her chest. In the lamplight her face was pale and stubborn, the face of a scholar who had already decided to be damned for what she knew. "Then there's no sense in stealth."

"There never was," Cael said. "Only manners. Come. Walk slowly. Speak little. And whatever you hear singing—"

He didn't finish. Because they heard it then, all of them, even the beasts.

It came up out of the dark ahead of them: a thread of sound, thin and high and patient. A woman's voice, singing something old—old enough that the words had worn smooth, old enough that Cael remembered it being sung in the valley when he was young in his duties, when the famine still gnawed the roots of Thornfell and the people above burned their furniture for warmth.

A harvest song. A famine song. Sung by a soul who had been singing it for a century.

Isandro made a small sound. Cael turned and saw them standing very straight, both hands buried in the grey wolf's fur, knuckles white. Their face in the lamplight had gone the color of old bone.

"That's—" Wren began.

"Don't," Isandro said. One word, cracked down the middle.

Cael wanted to say something—wanted it badly, which frightened him more than the dark did—but there was nothing true to say. So he turned and led them into the cavern.

* * *

The cathedral of the court beneath the mountain had been carved by no chisel. It had been *grown*—stone flowering outward in ribs and vaults, pillars twisted like the trunks of trees that had learned architecture from nightmares. When Cael had served here, he had thought it beautiful. He had stood beneath the great vault and believed, sincerely, that the court's hungers were justice: debts collected, balances kept, the old arithmetic of the world maintained.

Now he entered behind three thieves and two beasts, and saw the cathedral for what it was.

A granary.

The souls hung from the vaulted dark like lanterns in a festival hall. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one a soft gold light wrapped in a cage of woven shadow, swaying gently though no air moved, and each one burning with the particular warmth that only remembered lives can give. Cael had seen them his whole long service. He had lit new ones himself. He had carried the warrants that fed them into the dark.

But he had never heard them sing.

They were singing now. All of them. Not words—not quite—a slow braided harmony that rose and fell with the breathing of the mountain, and underneath it, threading through, clear as a needle through cloth:

*...the winter will pass, love, the winter will pass...*

"There." Wren's whisper shook. She raised the lamp. "Third tier. The one that flickers blue at the base—isn't that—"

"I know," Isandro said.

They walked forward. Cael let them lead; it was their debt, their blood, their grandmother. He watched Isandro cross the cathedral floor like someone walking into deep water—shoulders braced, breath shallow, eyes fixed upward on a single golden light among thousands.

Up close, the lantern was small. That was the cruelty of it. A whole human life, a century of hunger and patience and song, and it fit inside a cage of woven shadow no bigger than a turnip. It pulsed. And each pulse carried the voice out into the great harmony: *the winter will pass, love.*

"Grandmother," Isandro said.

The lantern flared. Blue at the base, gold at the crown, like a candle catching wind. And the voice within it bent toward them, and Cael—who had heard ten thousand souls sing in that hall and never once heard one *answer*—felt the hair rise along his arms as the old woman's voice came down like snowfall:

*"Oh," she sang. "Oh, look how tall. Look how tall you grew, little speaker."*

Isandro's hand rose toward the cage. Stopped an inch away. The heat coming off the lantern was not warmth—it was memory, pressing against the skin, a century of winters and songs and a bargain struck in ash.

"You're still in there," Isandro whispered. "You're still *her*."

*"Part of me,"* the soul sang, gentle as a lullaby. *"The part that remembers. The rest went where sold things go. Don't grieve, little one. It isn't so bad, being remembered forever. Everyone should be so lucky as to be a lantern."*

"Liar," Cael said quietly.

The cavern went silent. Not gradually—all at once, the whole braided harmony cutting off like a throat closed by a hand. Thousands of lanterns swayed. The darkness leaned in.

And the magister stepped out from between two pillars.

Cael had forgotten—or had forced himself to forget—how the magister looked. Tall past reason, robed in a grey that was not grey but the absence of color, its face a smooth oval with features that arrived only when it wished to be seen. Tonight it wished. Tonight it wore the face of a kind old teacher, warm-eyed, faintly smiling, and that choice was worse than any monster's mask could have been.

"Herald," it said. Its voice filled the cathedral without effort, the way cold fills a cellar. "You bring thieves into my hall. Or perhaps I should thank you. Deliveries are so much easier when they walk themselves down."

"They're not deliveries," Cael said. "They're my guests."

"Guests." The magister tasted the word. Its gaze slid from Cael as if he were a smudge on a window, and settled on Isandro. Everything in the cavern seemed to settle too—the lanterns' light bending, the shadows pooling toward the place where Isandro stood. "And here is the child of the child of the woman who saved your valley. Here is the Reyes who speaks. Do you know, I have waited a hundred years to look upon that face?"

Isandro did not answer. The beasts had gone flat to the stone, ears back, trembling—but they did not flee. Cael understood why. Fleeing was impossible. The whole cavern had become the palm of a closing hand.

"You've come for her lantern," the magister said, mild as a tutor. "Of course. They always do, eventually. Blood calls to blood." It drifted closer, robes making no sound, and Cael's stomach clenched because he knew this part—he had watched it from the herald's dais a hundred times, the gentle approach, the reasonable offer, the moment the mark began to believe the arithmetic was fair. "So let us speak plainly, speaker, as one honest creature to another. Your grandmother sold her soul to end a famine. A noble sale. The valley lived. But contracts are contracts, and the debt rolls down the bloodline like rain rolls downhill. It has reached you."

"I know what I owe," Isandro said.

"Do you?" The magister smiled wider. "Blood or voice. Those were the terms. Blood is so final, and so messy, and frankly—" it glanced around the cathedral with theatrical distaste— "so little use to me now. Blood is just meat. But a *voice*, child. A voice that speaks to beasts. A gift like yours, freely given, willingly surrendered? Do you know what I could do with such a voice? What songs I could hang from my ceiling?"

Cael moved before he decided to. "Don't," he said—to the magister, to Isandro, to the whole listening dark. "It lies. It always—"

"Herald." The magister did not raise its voice, and the single word stopped him like a wall of ice. Its eyes never left Isandro. "Let the child decide. Here is my offer, speaker, and I make it once: Give me your voice—freely, gladly, with no trickery and no regret—and every debt ends. Yours. Your grandmother's. Every soul in this hall goes free tonight. Every lantern unlit, every spirit released to whatever waits beyond the mountain. One voice against ten thousand. Surely even a smuggler can do that arithmetic."

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the mountain's hum stopped.

Cael watched Isandro's face, and what he saw there terrified him more than the magister ever had.

Because Isandro wasn't refusing. Their jaw worked. Their eyes went up—past the magister, past the tiers of swaying lights—to their grandmother's lantern, burning blue and gold, still pulsing out its patient song. *The winter will pass, love.* A hundred years of that. A hundred years of hanging in the dark, remembering, so that a valley full of strangers could eat bread.

One voice against ten thousand.

"They'd really go free," Isandro said. It was barely a whisper. "All of them. If I—"

"Yes," said the magister, soft as snow beginning to fall.

"And the debt. Mine. Gone."

"Gone. You would walk out of here lighter than you have ever been. No contract. No prophecy. No hunters, no scholars, no names." The magister's smile was kindness itself. "You could go back to being nobody, child. Isn't that what you wanted all along? Isn't that the shape of your whole small careful life? Give me the one thing you've spent years strangling silent, and I will wrap your invisibility around you again like a warm coat. Nobody will ever see you. Nobody will ever need you. You will be safe."

Cael's chest hurt. He realized he was gripping the strap of his old herald's satchel hard enough to bruise, and that some treacherous part of him—the part that had served this court for centuries, the part that still woke some nights believing the arithmetic was fair—was waiting to see Isandro say yes. Waiting the way the magister was waiting. Patient. Hungry.

"No," Cael said. Quietly. Not to the magister this time. To Isandro, across the widening dark between them. "Isandro. Listen to me. There is no version of this where you win. I carried these bargains for three hundred years. I wrote the terms. I know where the fine print sleeps. A voice given freely to this court does not hang as a lantern—it becomes the *bell*. It rings the harvests. It calls the debts. You would spend eternity summoning others into these cages, and the magister would wear your gift like a stolen coat, and somewhere above, the valley would starve again someday, and your voice would be the one calling it down."

"Cael," Wren breathed, warning.

"And another thing." His voice cracked, and he let it. "You think nobody seeing you is safety. I had a throne, Isandro. I had a title and a hall and every eye in the court on me, and I traded it all to stop carrying death warrants. Freedom isn't being unseen. I learned that too late, and it cost me everything, and I am standing here telling you—" he stopped, because the next word was too large, too soon, too true. He swallowed it. "Don't do the arithmetic it wants. That's all it is. Arithmetic."

The magister laughed. It was a terrible sound, warm and grandfatherly, filling the vaulted dark. "Herald. You were always sentimental. It's why we exiled you instead of caging you." Its attention returned to Isandro, and its voice dropped, intimate, almost tender. "Take your time, child. Touch her lantern. Feel what a hundred years of remembering is like. Then decide whether you would leave your own grandmother hanging in the dark to keep a stranger's opinion of you."

Isandro reached up.

Their fingertips brushed the woven cage of shadow, and the lantern flared so bright that the whole cathedral caught the light—thousands of lanterns answering, gold rippling outward in waves—and the old woman's voice poured down over her grandchild like thaw-water after a hard frost:

*"Little speaker,"* she sang. *"Whatever you choose—I chose this for you. Not for the valley. For you. Don't you dare waste my one good bargain on guilt."*

Isandro's hand closed around the cage.

And Cael saw it happen—the exact moment temptation crested, the moment the yes gathered itself behind Isandro's teeth like a held breath. Their eyes had gone distant. Their shoulders had loosened, the way a body loosens when it finally sets down a weight it has carried too long. The magister leaned in, patient, radiant, certain.

"Yes," Isandro whispered. "I'll—"

The grey wolf bit them.

Not hard—just enough, teeth closing on Isandro's wrist above the glove, a sharp animal pressure, and Isandro gasped and looked down, and the beast looked up, and something passed between them that Cael could not hear but could feel in the humming stone: the old wordless conversation of the beast-speaker and the spoken-to.

Isandro's eyes refocused. Distant no longer. Terrified no longer—or terrified differently, awake, the fear of someone who has just seen the edge they were about to step off.

Their hand fell away from the lantern.

"No," Isandro said, louder, and the word rang off the vaulted stone. "I don't accept."

The magister's kind face did not change. But the temperature of the cathedral dropped twenty degrees in a heartbeat, and every lantern in the hall guttered at once, and when the magister spoke again, the grandfatherly warmth was gone like a mask lifted, and what remained was older and colder than the mountain above them.

"A pity," it said. "Then we proceed to collection."

Behind it, far above, the great gates of the cathedral began to grind shut—and with them, distantly, Cael felt it through the soles of his boots: snow falling on the high passes. The first snow. The long winter, finishing its fall.

Sealing everyone in.
