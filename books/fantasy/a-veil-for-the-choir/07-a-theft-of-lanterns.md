## 7. A Theft of Lanterns

Wren had spent her whole life believing that scholarship was a form of courage—that to sit with a dangerous book and read it honestly was the bravest thing a person could do. Standing now in the throat of a smuggler's tunnel beneath Thornfell, ash-smeared to the elbows, a stolen prophecy pressed against her sternum like a second heart, she understood how young that belief had been.

The tunnel breathed. That was the first thing no ballad ever mentioned about the deep places: they breathed, slow and mineral, in and out, and every exhalation carried the smell of wet slate and old smoke and something sweeter beneath it, like lilies left too long in a closed room. The beasts moved ahead of them without light—two of Isandro's mountain foxes and the grey mule called Sorrel who had, Wren was nearly certain, been speaking to her in images since the second switchback down, showing her tunnels before her eyes found them.

*Here*, said the pressure against the back of her skull. *Now.*

"Isandro." Wren's whisper barely qualified as sound. "The wall."

Isandro knelt where Sorrel had stopped, pressing one palm flat to the stone. Wren watched them listen—not with their ears but somewhere deeper, the way she herself listened to a text for the argument under the argument. Whatever the stone said, it took a long moment to say it.

"The beast-road comes up behind the lantern-hall," Isandro murmured. "The beasts dug it themselves. Years of it." A pause, and something moved through their voice that Wren couldn't name—wonder, maybe, or guilt at being loved so patiently by creatures who asked nothing back. "They've been making us a door since the snowline."

*Since the book*, Wren thought. Since the night frost came down the valley and the prophecy began keeping its promises.

She checked her satchel for the tenth time: chalk, birchbark pages copied in her own shaking hand, the list of names she'd translated from the contract's margins—the ledger of the sold. Grandmother Reyes's name sat third from the top. *Mireia Reyes, who sang.* Above them, beyond forty feet of living rock, the court was singing still.

* * *

Cael went first, because Cael had insisted, and because there was no arguing with a man who had spent three centuries delivering death warrants and had decided this would be the last one he witnessed rather than carried.

They watched him go through a crack in the cavern wall—a seam thin as a knife's edge, through which the light of the lantern-hall spilled in long amber bars. Wren pressed her eye to it and saw the cathedral cavern entire for the first time.

It was worse than she had prepared for. She had imagined a vault, a treasury, something with the clean cruelty of an archive. Instead it was beautiful. Columns of flowstone rose into dark the eye couldn't finish; the floor was polished obsidian that held every reflection like a promise; and hanging from hooks of pale bone along the great walls, hundreds of lanterns burned—not with flame but with a slow interior gold, each one pulsing gently, rhythmically, and Wren realized with a lurch that what she was watching was not light at all.

It was heartbeat. It was memory, banked and burning. Each lantern was a soul the court had harvested, and they hung there in their hundreds like a orchard of fruit no god had planted.

And in the center of the hall, singing—the song came through the stone itself, through Wren's teeth, through the fluid of her eyes—hung a lantern that burned a different color. Not gold. Rose. Stubborn, hearth-colored rose, and its pulse was stronger than all the others, and it was singing a harvest song in a language three villages dead.

*Mireia Reyes, who sold her soul to feed the hungry.*

"She's still in there," Wren whispered. It wasn't meant to be spoken aloud. Beside her, Isandro made a sound like a man being quietly unmade, and put their hand against the stone as if they could reach through it.

Then Cael walked into the light, and everything began.

* * *

He had dressed for it, gods help him—in the herald's regalia Wren hadn't known he still possessed, hauled in oilcloth through half a mountain: a long coat stitched with silver thread that caught the lantern-light and threw it back in cold geometry, and a collar of black feathers that made his bare throat look like an offering. He crossed the obsidian floor alone, unarmed, and when he reached the dais where the magister sat coiled in robes the color of riverbed shadow, he struck the floor once with a herald's iron-shod staff, and the sound went through the cavern like a crack through ice.

"Herald," the magister said. Its voice was dry paper. "Exiled."

"Cael of the Ninth Gate," he answered, and his voice did not shake, though Wren saw his knuckles whiten on the staff. "Returned, with a formal challenge under the Old Concord. I claim the right of address. The court is bound to hear me until the challenge is answered or withdrawn."

The silence that followed was the most terrible thing Wren had ever heard, because in it she could hear the lanterns—hundreds of souls, all listening.

"Speak, then," said the magister.

"Not here," Cael said, smooth as a smuggled lie. "Under the Old Concord, a challenge is heard at the Threshold Gate, where exiles were cast out. You know the law. You *wrote* the law." And there—there was the spark of the man beneath the herald, the gallows humor of someone who had finally found a warrant worth forging. "Come and hear me walk the distance you made me walk out."

The magister rose. Its robes moved wrong, the way water moves wrong when something lives under it. Behind it, the court began to stir—shapes unfolding from alcoves, tall and pale, drawn by protocol older than their hunger. The magister could not refuse. That was the whole architecture of the plan: courts run on law starve more obediently than courts run on appetite.

The magister gestured, and the great doors at the far end ground open onto the dark of the approach corridor, and the entire luminous horror of the court began to process toward Cael and his beautiful, suicidal legal fiction.

Wren turned from the crack in the wall. "Now."

* * *

The beast-door opened where Sorrel pushed it—a slab of stone dressed with moss and moved on some mechanism of root and patience—and then they were inside the lantern-hall, and the smell hit Wren like a hand over her mouth: lilies and candle-tallow and underneath it, faintly, the smell of every winter she had ever survived.
