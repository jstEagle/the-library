## 8. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The first snow came down through the mountain itself.

That was the wrongness of it—snow should fall from sky, not rise from stone—but there it was, sifting from the cathedral cavern's distant ceiling in slow white spirals, catching the glow of a hundred soul-lanterns as it fell. Each flake passed through those captive lights and came out changed, faintly singing, landing on Isandro's shoulders with the weight of a held breath.

*The long winter finishes falling tonight.* Wren had read the line aloud three days ago in a voice gone thin with dread. Now Isandro understood that the prophecy had never been a warning about weather. It had been a schedule.

They were running. Wren ahead, arms full of stolen lanterns, her scholar's robe torn at every seam and trailing threads like a comet's tail. Cael behind her, silver-haired and terrible, his herald's sigil raised high enough to make the court's lesser servitors flinch back into the dark. And behind all of them, the beasts—a gray wolf-shape and something larger and older that wore the shape of shadows given hooves—their eyes reflecting lantern-light like coals banked for morning.

Isandro carried their grandmother.

That was what they could not stop thinking, even now, even with the alarm rolling through the cavern in waves of pressure that made teeth ache. The lantern in their left hand burned steady and warm, and inside it a voice still sang—low, cracked, patient. A famine-ending song. A soul-sold song. Grandmother Sefa, who had walked into this dark a century ago with her chin up and her hands empty, and who had been hanging here ever since, a light for a court that fed on remembering.

*Not tonight,* Isandro told her silently. *Tonight you are stolen.*

The gates were sealing.

They heard it before they saw it: a groan in the mountain's bones, deep as tectonic prayer. Ahead, where the smuggler's road met the great gate of the undercourt—a gate carved with faces, each one a name some living person still spoke aloud—the snow was gathering into walls. Not drifting. *Building.* Flake clinging to flake in impossible architecture, closing a passage wide enough to drive cattle through down to a hand's width, then to nothing.

"The beasts' tunnel," Isandro gasped. "Where the wolves broke through—we can—"

"It's behind us," Cael said, and his voice cracked on it. "The magister closed it when the alarm sounded."

Then Wren stumbled.

It was such a small thing. Her boot caught a root of stone that had no business growing roots, and she went down with a cry, lanterns scattering across the frozen floor like dropped stars. Two rolled toward the gate. One guttered. And out of the dark on every side came the court—not the grand ones, not yet, but the small hungry things that served them, pale as grubs, moving with the terrible patience of things that have never once needed to hurry.

Wren scrambled backward, snatching at lanterns, and got two. The third was already in a grub-thing's grip.

"Leave it!" Cael shouted. "Wren, leave it, come *on*—"

But Wren had spent her career, her reputation, her whole exile on those souls. She lunged for the lantern, and the dark closed over her like water.

What happened next happened slowly, in the way of nightmares. The small things did not hurt her. They simply *held* her, dozens of pale hands on her arms and throat and hair, pinning her with a gentleness worse than violence. And through the crowd came the magister.

It had worn a courtier's beauty when they entered. It did not bother now. What walked toward Wren was tall and wrong, its face an arrangement rather than a feature, its voice the sound of a ledger being opened.

"A theft," it said, conversationally. "A debt, then. One soul for the balance." It considered Wren the way a man considers an apple. "This one is ripe. Old griefs. Good harvest."

"Take me instead." Cael stepped forward, sigil blazing. "I am still herald. I invoke the right of substitution—"

"You are exiled." The magister did not look at him. "Your rights died with your oath. But you may watch. You have always been good at watching, Cael, son of no one."

And Isandro felt it happen—that moment the whole descent had been building toward, the moment the prophecy had been circling like a hawk. The fear rose, the old familiar fear, the one they had carried since childhood: *be nobody, say nothing, hide the gift deeper than your name and you will live.* Their grandmother's law. Their mother's law. The law that had kept them alive through twenty years of smuggling runs and silent bargains.

*The Reyes who speaks will unmake the court.*

They had thought it meant dying. All this way, through troll-tolls and wight-bribes and the singing dark, they had thought the prophecy promised their death, and they had come anyway because Grandmother Sefa's song deserved better than eternity in glass.

Standing here, watching Wren's breath fog faster, watching Cael's face break open with a horror he was too proud to hide—they finally understood.

*Unmake* did not mean die.

It meant *stop hiding.*

The court fed on remembered souls, Wren had deciphered. On names held in memory. And Isandro had spent a lifetime making themselves unrememberable—no true name spoken, no gift acknowledged, a ghost walking the mountain's skin. The veil had protected them the way darkness protects a thief.

But a veil, lifted, becomes something else entirely. It becomes a *voice.*

The snow was falling harder now. The gate was nearly shut. And Isandro Reyes, who had not spoken their gift aloud since they were nine years old, opened their mouth.

"*Stop.*"

One word, and it was not a word. It was everything they had swallowed for twenty years, all of it leaving at once, and the mountain heard.

Sound has a texture, when a beast-speaker truly speaks. Isandro had always felt it as a touch—a suggestion to a mule, a calming pressure against a spooked goat. This was not suggestion. This was command, delivered in the oldest language there is, the one before words: *I am speaking to you. You are heard.*

And the mountain was not a beast. But the mountain was also not *not* one. Stone is only very slow flesh. Every smuggler knows mountains shift, settle, breathe over centuries. Isandro seized that slow breath and gave it urgency.

"*I speak to the stone,*" they said, and their voice rang off the cathedral vaults, and every lantern flickered, and the court's small horrors went still. "*I speak to the snow that is not snow. I speak to the gates your masters built on bones they stole. You have been made a jail. I am unmaking you.*"

The magister turned. For the first time, its arranged face achieved something real: alarm.

"Reyes," it said. "The one who does not—"

"*Speaks.*" Isandro walked forward, and the floor moved with them. That was the truth of it, the wonder of it—the stone rising gently beneath their boots like a beast leaning into a trusted hand. "*You bought my grandmother's voice when she sold her soul. You have been drinking this mountain's patience for a thousand years. You feed on being remembered? Then remember me.*"

They raised both hands—Grandmother's lantern in the left, nothing but open palm in the right—and spoke the way they had spoken to frightened animals all their life, except now at the volume of a soul.

"*Gate. Open. These are my people, and I am taking them home.*"

The gate of faces screamed. It was a horrible sound, a hundred carved mouths opening at once, and then it was a joyful sound, because the faces were souls too, remembered into captivity, and Isandro's voice had reached them like dawn reaches a dark room. The snow-wall shuddered. Cracked. Melted—not into water but into light, flakes unraveling into motes that swirled upward and vanished through the stone.

A passage opened, wide and bright.

The small pale things released Wren so fast she fell. She caught herself, stared at Isandro with an expression that would have been funny under any other circumstances—the scholar who had studied the prophecy for years, meeting it wearing torn robes and soot—and gasped: "That's not— the texts said nothing about *stone*—"

"The texts weren't written by anyone who talked to rocks," Isandro said. Their voice was raw. Speaking at that depth had scraped something, they could feel it; they suspected they would be hoarse for days, maybe longer. It was worth it. Every word worth it.

Cael had not moved. He was looking at Isandro the way men look at the first sunrise after a long polar night—with relief and terror in equal measure, because light reveals as much as it warms. There was no hiding now. Every creature in this cavern knew Isandro's gift, knew their nature, knew their name in the old bloodline sense. The anonymity that had kept them alive for twenty years lay on the cavern floor, spent.

Isandro found they could not mourn it.

"Go," they said to Wren, to the beasts. "Through. Take the lanterns."

"And you?" Wren was already moving, souls cradled to her chest like infants.

Isandro looked back at the magister. At the court beyond it—the grand ones now, emerging from their veils of shadow, beautiful and starving. At the thousands of lanterns still hanging in the dark, souls that belonged to no prophecy, whose names appeared in no contract. If Isandro walked through that gate, the court survived, wounded but alive, and simply waited for the next famine, the next desperate grandmother, the next bloodline to bleed.

The debt had transferred down Isandro's family for a century. It ended tonight or it ended never.

"I'm not finished speaking," Isandro said.

The magister drew itself tall. Around it, the court gathered, and the temperature fell, and Isandro felt the full weight of ancient attention settle on them like snowload on a roof. Behind them, Cael's hand found their shoulder—brief, burning, a promise or a farewell.

The magister smiled with a mouth it had borrowed.

"Little speaker," it said. "You have made yourself *very* memorable."

* * *

The mountain began to sing.

It started low, in the stones underfoot, a vibration more felt than heard—and Isandro realized, with a lurch, that it was not the mountain at all. It was the lanterns. Thousands of them, every soul the court had hung in its dark, and they were answering the voice that had finally, after centuries, spoken *for* them.

Grandmother's lantern blazed in Isandro's hand like a struck match, and inside it, the famine-song changed key—from lament to something with an edge, something that had waited a hundred years to become a battle hymn.

The court took one step back.

Isandro stood in the falling snow that was no longer obeying the court, lifted their grandmother's light, and let the whole buried dark hear exactly who they were.

"My name," they said, "is Isandro Reyes. And I have come to collect."
