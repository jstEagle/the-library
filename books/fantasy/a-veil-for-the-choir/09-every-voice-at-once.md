## 9. Every Voice at Once

Cael had heard the court sing for six hundred years.

He had heard it in its hunger and its splendor, in the centuries when the lantern-hall blazed with a thousand stolen souls and the magister's voice rolled through the cathedral cavern like harvest thunder. He had thought he knew every register of it — the lullaby it sang to lull the dying, the psalm it sang to itself, the thin whine it made when the feeding was poor. He had believed there was nothing left in that music that could surprise him.

Then Isandro Reyes opened their mouth, and spoke.

Not shouted. That was the terrible thing, the beautiful thing. The whole cavern held its breath — stone and soul-lantern and fae alike — and Isandro stood in the center of the shattered heist, Wren caught in the magister's root-grip, snow sealing the gates behind them like a closing eye, and they did not shout. They simply *spoke*, the way one speaks to a frightened horse on a dark road, low and certain and unbearably kind.

"I hear you," they said. "All of you. I have always heard you."

And the mountain listened.

Cael felt it pass through him first, through his herald's blood, which was older than his exile and truer to the deep places than he had ever wanted to admit: a vibration like the first thaw after a killing winter, running up through the soles of his boots, through the roots of his teeth, into the hollow where his heart had once been certain of its loyalties. The gift of the beast-speakers — the gift the Reyes line had buried under silence for three generations — poured out of Isandro not as a whisper to animals but as an *address*. To everything that could hear.

The stone heard.

The whole mountain heard.

* * *

The lanterns went up like a sky remembering stars.

Cael had watched souls hang in that hall for centuries — dim, swaying, singing their small captive songs, harvested and hoarded and slowly drunk down by a court that called it stewardship. Now, one by one, they flared. Not the sickly amber of the court's keeping but a clean white-gold, each lantern straining against its chain like a bird testing a cage it has only just understood is a cage.

The magister's voice cracked across the cavern. "Seal them! Seal the vessels — the claim is ours, the contract is *ours*—"

But contracts are words, and words are breath, and breath belongs to whoever speaks last.

Isandro turned in a slow circle, and Cael watched them do the thing no speaker had ever dared: they named what they heard. "The miller who drowned in 1841 — your wife kept your coat by the door until she died." A lantern shuddered. "The shepherd girl from the high passes — your dog waited eleven winters at the fold." Another flared white. "Grandmother—" Their voice broke, and mended. "Abuela. I found your name in the ash. I know what you bought with yourself. I know how many people ate."

The oldest lantern in the hall — the one that had flickered toward Isandro's hand as the alarm rang, the one Cael had watched with a herald's dread because he knew what it meant when a soul reached for its blood — rose off its hook.

The chain did not break. It simply *ended*, unravelling into ash the way the contract had been written, the way the famine had been ended: paid, finished, free.

* * *

She came down out of the light like snow falling upward.

Cael had carried death warrants for four hundred years. He knew the shape of the dead better than the living — knew how they clung, how they raged, how they begged. Luz Reyes did none of these things. She descended slowly, her form gathering out of the lantern-light into a woman of perhaps sixty, silver-streaked hair, hands rough as bark, and she looked at her grandchild with an expression Cael had no word for in any of the court's nine tongues.

It looked like morning.

"Niño," she said, and her voice was thaw — river-ice letting go all at once, the groan and rush of a valley remembering spring. "You sound like me."

Isandro made a sound Cael would carry for the rest of his long life. Not a sob. Something smaller and more broken-open than that — the noise a person makes when a door they had sealed shut for decades swings wide and the air behind it is warm.

"You're free," Isandro said. "You can go. All of you can go — I've spoken it, the mountain heard me, the claim is—"

"Hush." Her hand, when it touched Isandro's cheek, left no coldness behind. That was the miracle of it; Cael felt the warmth of it from ten paces away, felt the whole hall warming, frost retreating up the walls like a tide going out. "A century I hung here, singing. Do you know what I sang? Not laments. Lullabies. For the ones who came after me, the frightened ones, the newly dead. Someone had to keep them company in the dark." Her eyes crinkled. "I told them stories about my granddaughter who talked to beasts. They never believed me. They will now."

"Abuela—"

"I chose this," she said, firm as a fencepost driven into frozen ground. "In 1823 I walked down that road with my eyes open, because children were eating dirt, and I could pay, and no one else could. I have never regretted it. But I am *tired*, niño. And I would very much like to see the sun again before I go wherever it is that old women go." She smiled, and it was Isandro's smile, the crooked one, the one they hid. "Thank you for speaking. Thank you for being loud enough to reach me. Now let an old woman finish falling."

Around her, the other lanterns were rising too — dozens, then hundreds, the whole hoard of the court's long harvest lifting free of their hooks, chains dissolving, souls gathering themselves into shapes half-remembered from life. The cavern filled with voices, all of them speaking at once, and yet Cael heard, beneath the roar, a single note underneath it all: gratitude. Every voice at once, and every voice saying *thank you*.

The magister screamed.

* * *

Cael had served under three magisters. He had watched the court decline from a nation to a pantry, from a pantry to a trap, from a trap to a hunger wearing a crown. He had told himself, in his exile, that he had stopped loving it. Standing in the storm of freed souls, watching the current magister claw at the emptying air where its harvest had hung, he learned the truth: he had not stopped loving it. He had stopped being able to save it. Those were different griefs, and he had spent two centuries confusing them.

The magister turned from the vanishing light and fixed its gaze — vast, ancient, threaded through with root and mineral and want — on Cael.

"Herald," it said, and the old title fell on him like a mantle of lead. "Herald of the Deep Court. You turned your back on us, and look what your mercy has wrought. We are starving. Without the harvest we gutter out within a season — you know this. You have always known this." Its voice softened into something almost gentle, which was worse. "But the throne stands empty. It has stood empty since you left. Take it. Rule us. Feed us *wisely* — you alone among us ever understood restraint. Save the court, Cael. Be the king who ends the hunger instead of the exile who watched it die."

And oh, the temptation was real. That was the obscenity of it. Cael felt it open under him like a stairway of black glass: the throne he had been shaped for since before human kingdoms had names, the chance to hold the leash instead of cutting throats, to ration the horror, to be *kind* inside the machine. Six hundred years of duty whispered that this was redemption. That a good jailer was better than an empty cell.

Then he looked at Isandro.

They stood with their grandmother's last light fading around them like snowfall in reverse, their hand still raised toward the ascending souls, their face wet and unhidden — utterly unhidden, for the first time in their guarded, silent, careful life. They had just given away the only thing they owned: the veil. The invisibility that had kept them alive. They had traded safety for truth in front of a court of monsters, and they were standing straighter for it.

What kind of fool would Cael be, to take a throne after watching that?

"No," he said.

The word came out quiet, and the mountain — Isandro's mountain now, listening still — carried it to every corner of the cavern anyway.

"I carried your death warrants for four hundred years," Cael said, and his herald's voice, unused at full strength since his exile, filled the hall like a struck bell. "I told myself I was serving something older than cruelty. I was wrong. There is nothing left to serve here but appetite, and appetite does not deserve a king. It deserves to end." He drew himself up, wings mantling, the exile's ragged cloak falling away from shoulders that remembered their old bearing. "Keep your throne. Freeze on it. When the last of you gutters out, I will stand at your gates and speak your names aloud, so that even your ending is *heard* — because that is what heralds do. We announce. We do not rule."

The magister stared at him. Around it, the court — what remained of it, the lesser fae, the root-things, the pale choristers — stared too, and Cael saw understanding move through them like wind through wheat. Not agreement. Not yet. But comprehension: that the harvest was gone, that the bargain was broken, that the world above had sent them a speaker and a thief and a traitor, and the old order was over.

Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the long winter creaked — and began, very faintly, to loosen.

* * *

Luz Reyes was the last to go.

She had lingered while the others streamed upward through seams in the rock that had not existed an hour ago, paths of thaw opening in the mountain's bones as if the stone itself were making way. Now she stood before Isandro, growing translucent at the edges, and took both their hands in hers.

"The beasts," she said. "You'll keep talking to them. Promise me. Out loud. No more hiding in that head of yours — hiding nearly got you killed, and it would have broken my heart twice."

"I promise," Isandro said, wrecked and laughing at once.

"And the pretty herald." Her eyes slid sideways to Cael, wicked as any grandmother's. "He watches you when you're not looking. Watched you the whole time you were stealing my lantern, actually, like a man watching the sun come up over a country he'd given up on. Don't you dare hide from that either."

"Abuela!"

"Good." She pressed their knuckles to her lips, and where her mouth touched, Cael saw — actually saw — a faint gold mark settle into Isandro's skin like a coal going to sleep. "A blessing. Old-fashioned. It'll keep the frost off you. Now watch."

She stepped back, lifted her arms, and let herself come apart into light — not sadly, not dragged, but the way a traveler sets down a pack at the end of a very long road. For one heartbeat she was a woman. Then she was a lantern-song. Then she was a warmth in the air, moving upward through the mountain's new-open veins, toward a sky that had finally stopped snowing.

The last of her voice drifted back down like the smell of bread:

"*Tell the valley I'm sorry about the turnips.*"

And she was gone.

* * *

The silence afterward was not empty. It was full — full of stone settling, of frost retreating, of a thousand departed souls' echo still ringing in the walls the way a bell rings after the striking stops. Cael stood in it with ash on his boots and his old name burning in his throat, and felt lighter than he had in two hundred years, and more unmoored.

Isandro turned to him. Behind them, Wren was already scrambling up, ink-stained and furious and alive, demanding to know if anyone had seen her satchel; the great grey wolf-beast was pressing its nose to Isandro's palm, huffing warm breath; the gates at the far end of the cavern had gone from sealed iron to merely heavy, and beyond them, impossibly, daylight was leaking through the thinning snow.

"So," Isandro said. Their voice was raw. They were smiling anyway — the crooked smile, the hidden one, hidden no longer. "You turned down a throne."

"It was ugly furniture," Cael said. "Terrible acoustics."

Isandro laughed — really laughed, the sound bouncing off the cathedral vaults, and the mountain seemed to laugh with them, a low rolling chuckle in the bedrock. Then they crossed the distance between them and took Cael's hand, ash and all, in front of the ruins of the court, in front of the scholar and the wolves and the waking stone, and did not hide it.

"Come on, herald," they said. "Walk me out. The valley should meet us both."

Above them, through a hundred fathoms of loosening stone, the long winter was finishing its fall — and somewhere past it, spring was already waiting to learn their names.
