## 10. A Veil, Lifted

The snow broke on the third day of our climb, and it did not melt so much as surrender.

I felt it happen before I saw it—a loosening in the stone under my boots, a long exhale running up through the mountain's ribs like a held breath finally released. The beasts felt it too. Marrow, the old grey wolf who had walked beside me since I was fourteen, stopped at the mouth of the last tunnel and lifted her muzzle, tasting the air, and what she tasted made her tail move in slow, astonished sweeps.

*Warm,* she said, in the way wolves say things, which is not words but a pressure against the mind. *The world is warm.*

We came up out of the mountain into a morning that should not have existed. The valley below us lay green where it should have been white—the long winter that was to have sealed Thornfell until spring, the winter Wren's book had promised would finish falling the night we descended, broken open like an egg. Snow clung only to the highest crags, retreating upward as we watched, thinning to lace, to memory. Rivers ran loud with meltwater that had no business moving yet. And everywhere, everywhere, birdsong—thick and shameless, the whole valley singing at once as if it had been waiting years for permission.

Wren stood beside me with the birchbark book hugged to their chest, its pages warped from the cavern's damp, and laughed until they had to sit down on a warm rock.

"It's early," they kept saying. "It's early by two months. Do you understand what this does to every calendar of the court's influence? Every harvest record? Isandro, I could write three treatises—" They stopped, pressed the book tighter, and looked at me with wet eyes. "You broke a prophecy."

"I didn't break anything," I said. "It just turned out to mean something kinder than it sounded."

That was the truth of it, though I was still learning to hold it without flinching. *The Reyes who speaks shall unmake the veil.* I had read those words as a death sentence, and they had been one—but not mine. The veil that fell was the one I had built around myself, brick by silent brick, over thirty years of being nobody. The court beneath the mountain had fed on hidden souls, on names swallowed and debts unpaid, and when I stood in that cathedral dark and spoke my gift aloud—commanded the living stone before the whole assembled hunger of it—I had not only shattered their claim on the lantern-souls. I had shown them, and myself, what a voice was actually for.

Cael came up out of the tunnel last, because Cael always moved like someone who expected doors to close behind him. He carried his herald's staff still, out of habit more than duty; the silver bell at its head had cracked in the final collapse and gave only a dull, broken chime when it swung. He looked wrong in daylight. Too pale, too fine-boned, his ears' faint points catching the sun like a confession he'd forgotten he was making. A century underground had taught him to be beautiful in the way caves are beautiful—all shadow and suggestion. In the open air he squinted like a man seeing color for the first time.

He was. In a sense.

"You're staring," he said, without looking at me.

"You're burning," I said. "Fae don't do well in sun. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows many wrong things." He tipped his face up anyway, reckless, letting the light land on him. His eyes were watering. He didn't blink it away. "I spent four hundred years carrying death warrants down into the dark. Do you know what I never once did? Stood in a field. Just—stood in one." His voice did something small and ruined at the edges. "I think I'd like to burn a little."

So we stood in a field, or near enough—a shelf of meadow above the valley with the meltwater roaring somewhere below—and the beasts spread out around us and flopped in the grass like creatures who had never known duty, and Wren cried quietly into the birchbark book, and nobody said anything about what waited below.

What waited below was the village of Hollowmere, first hearth-smoke on the valley road, and the question underneath all questions: *what happens now that they know your name.*

* * *

They knew my name.

I heard it before I saw anyone—carried up the road on the smell of woodsmoke and bread, in the voice of a child who pointed at the ragged, ash-streaked company descending the switchbacks and shouted, not in fear but in the bright flat way children announce weather: *"That's them! That's the beast-speaker who opened the mountain!"*

My whole body braced for the old arithmetic. Fear, then stones, then torches, then the long cold work of becoming nobody again in some new valley further on. Thirty years of practice. My hand found Marrow's ruff without my deciding it.

But the village did not throw stones.

They lined the road instead. Farmers with thaw-mud on their boots, the miller's wife with flour to her elbows, the old ferryman who had once paid me, years ago, to guide him past a bridge-wight and never learned my face. They stared—at the wolf, at the fox-troop trotting at my heel like an honor guard, at Cael with his cracked bell—and they did not step back.

An old woman pushed through to the front. I knew her distantly: Marta Ostrow, who kept bees and buried grudges. She looked at me a long moment with eyes gone milky at the rim.

"My mother starved in the famine year," she said. "Eighteen twenty-three. She was nine." Her chin trembled. "You went down and you brought them back. The ones the mountain took. Didn't you."

"Some of them," I said. My throat was tight. "Some chose to stay passed on. But they're not hanging in the dark anymore. None of them."

Marta Ostrow took my hand—my scarred, rope-burned, unwashed hand—in both of hers, in front of the entire village, and pressed it to her forehead like a blessing.

"Well then," she said, thickly. "You'll take bread. And you'll stop sneaking through my bee-pastures at night like a thief, Isandro Reyes, because I've known what you were since you were nine years old and stealing apples, and I never said a word, and neither did anyone else worth salt in this valley. We were waiting for you to say it yourself."

I stood there with bread in my hands and thirty years of dread dissolving like the snow on the crags, and I could not speak, which—for once in my life—was not the same as hiding.

* * *

We stayed a week, then a month. Wren commandeered the miller's back room and filled it with paper, writing the true account of the court—not the sanitized chronicle the capital would want, but the whole ugly, grieving thing, lantern-souls and stolen centuries and a grandmother who sold herself to save a valley that never learned her name. "Serafina Reyes," Wren said one evening, tapping the page. "She should be in the histories. Not 'a woman of the famine year.' Her name."

"She'd have hated that," I said. "She hid too. It ran in the blood."

"Then let the histories annoy her," Wren said, and wrote on.

Cael healed slowly, the way deep things heal. The sun stopped hurting him by midwinter-that-wasn't. The court's last messenger had come up before the gates closed forever—a single moth-winged courier with a sealed offer: the magister's throne, vacant, starving, his. Come back and rule what remains. Rule the remnant honestly, feed them on something other than souls, be the change from inside.

He read the letter twice by candlelight and then fed it to the candle.

"They offered me everything I was exiled for wanting," he told me later, on the porch of the cabin I'd inherited from my grandmother, the one I'd spent a decade avoiding and now slept in. Snowmelt dripped off the eaves in the dark, a sound like slow applause. "A throne. A purpose. Four hundred years of exile undone with a signature."

"What did you want, back then?" I asked. "When you were young enough to want thrones?"

"To matter," he said simply. "To be seen. To walk into a room and have the room know my name." He turned, and the candlelight caught the points of his ears, the old grief, the new softness around it. "And then I got it, and it meant carrying death warrants, and I learned that being seen is worthless if what they see is a function. A tool." He reached across the space between us and took my hand—the second person in a week to do it, and the first to make my pulse misbehave. "You were invisible your whole life, and I was nothing but visible, and we both learned the same lesson from opposite ends. So tell me, Isandro Reyes, beast-speaker, mountain-breaker: what do you want now?"

The honest answer sat in my chest like a coal. I had spent a month not saying it, telling myself the old lies—that he was fae and I was human and it would end badly, that the valley would turn, that safety lay in keeping even love half-hidden, half-voiced, a contract with a clause for escape.

But I had signed away clauses in a cathedral of hung souls. I had learned what debt really cost.

"I want you to stay," I said. "Not as a herald. Not as an exile killing time. As—" My voice caught on the word, the last locked door, and I thought of my grandmother's lantern flickering toward my hand, choosing, at the end, to go free. Everything ends. Everything passes. The only sin was hiding while it did. "As mine. If you'll have a smuggler with wolf-hair on all her clothes and a talent she's only just started using."

Cael smiled, and it was not the court's polished smile, the herald's mask. It was crooked and disbelieving and entirely his.

"I guarded gates for four hundred years," he said. "Yours is the first door I've ever wanted to be on the inside of."

* * *

Spring came properly at last, and with it, work.

Word travels fast in mountains. By the time the passes were fully clear, letters had begun arriving at Hollowmere—traders wanting routes through Thornfell, pilgrims wanting safe passage to the shrines the court's fall had uncovered, a surveyor from the capital who wanted, of all things, a map of the deep roads and had heard there was finally a guide who would give her real answers. They came asking for Isandro Reyes by name, and I met them at my own gate, in daylight, with Marrow at my side and the fox-troop draped over the woodpile like royalty, and I named my price out loud and shook hands on it.

No more silence traded for safety. No more watching clients from the treeline, pretending to be hired muscle with a knack for weather. When a mule shied at something in the dark, I asked it what it had seen, aloud, in front of everyone, and told the party the answer, and watched their fear curdle into wonder and settle, usually, into trust. Once, a merchant's son called me witch with hate in his mouth, and his father cuffed him and apologized to me twice, and Marrow growled once, softly, and that was the whole of it. The world, it turned out, had been waiting for me the way the valley had waited for spring.

Some evenings, Cael walked the high meadows with me and the beasts, learning the names of flowers he'd only ever seen pressed in books, and the cracked bell on his old staff chimed its broken note, and I thought: *this is what the gift was always for.* Not hiding. Not surviving. This—speaking, and being answered.

One such evening, late in the thaw, Marrow went stiff at the meadow's edge, ears forward, nose working the wind off the eastern peaks. Cael noticed a heartbeat after I did.

"What is it?" he asked.

I listened the way I had learned to listen with my whole self, no longer rationing the gift, and what came back down the wind was strange enough that I sat down hard in the grass.

"Wolves," I said slowly. "From beyond Thornfell. A pack I've never scented. And they're not hunting." Marrow looked back at me, and her amber eyes held something I had only ever seen in mirrors on bad nights, long ago: recognition, and appeal. "They're asking for me. By name." I swallowed. "They say there are other courts. Older ones. Deeper than the mountain ever went. And one of them has woken hungry, and it has heard what happened here—and it knows exactly what I am."

Cael's hand found mine in the dark, steady as a gate-bar.

"Then we'd better introduce ourselves properly," he said.
