## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

There are three rules to keeping a ledger for the Veiled Guild, and I learned all of them before I learned to write my own name properly.

One: every debt is real, even the ones that look like kindness. Two: nothing enters the books that cannot be paid, and nothing leaves them that has not been. Three—and this is the rule they teach last because it's the one that gets keepers killed—*the ledger does not lie, but it can be made silent.*

I was thinking about rule three on the morning the candle appeared at our door, because rule three had been bothering me for a week.

But let me start with the arithmetic, since arithmetic is what I do best. It was the fourth day of the Dwindling, which meant the guild had been lean for eleven months and would be lean for at least three more, and I sat in the counting room with my sleeves pinned back and my ink pot wrapped in wool against the cold, balancing a column of small debts that would have made my predecessors weep.

A widow in Copperlane owed us a favor: one favor, unspecified, redeemable at need. A cooper owed us the memory of his father's face, traded years ago for a winter without fever. A midwife owed us her second sight for as long as she lived, though she'd never once used it and probably didn't remember giving it. Small things. Small debts. The kind the old ledgers wouldn't have bothered recording, because once upon a time the Veiled Guild dealt in impossible things—a kingdom's luck, a storm bottled and sold, a dead man's shadow returned to his grieving house—and now we dealt in the dregs of the impossible, the crumbs, the favors nobody else wanted to hold.

I wrote *received, in full, one (1) blessing of safe passage, minor, from the ferryman of the Graywater, in exchange for the mending of his daughter's crooked spine,* and then I sat back and looked at what I'd written, because that entry was the whole guild in miniature. We had taken something enormous from a man who loved his daughter and given him something worth less than a copper coin, and he had thanked us. He had *thanked* us. That's what decline looks like when you're inside it: not a collapse, but a slow settling, like a house sinking into soft ground, everything still standing, everything slightly wrong.

The guildhall itself told the same story if you knew how to read it. The great vault door in the cellar hadn't opened in nine years; the seals on it were intact but the mechanisms beneath had rusted into ornament. Half the private rooms upstairs stood empty, their doors locked, their keys lost or sold. We kept only the ground floor warm—the counting room, the meeting hall, Master Ellery's study—and let the rest of the building go dark around us like a body drawing warmth inward toward its heart. There were eleven of us when I arrived as an apprentice at fourteen. There were four now. Me, Cassian, Master Ellery, and old Sefa, who mostly slept by the kitchen fire and claimed, when pressed, that she was dreaming the guild's future.

"I dreamed of coins," she told me last week. "A great many coins."

"That's just hunger," I said.

"Dreams are always hunger," she agreed placidly, and went back to sleep.

Cassian found me at midday with two cups of chicory that was trying very hard to be coffee. He set one beside my elbow without asking, which was his way; Cassian never asked for anything, he simply arranged the world so that the thing he wanted happened to be within your reach. It made him an excellent negotiator and a terrible patient.

"Where are we?" he asked, nodding at the ledger.

"Balanced," I said. "Barely. If the cooper defaults on his memory we're solvent through spring. If the ferryman's blessing appreciates—which it might, river blessings always appreciate—we could see autumn."

"And if neither?"

"Then we sell the hall." I said it lightly, the way you say terrible things when you've said them often enough in your head. "There's a wool merchant in Lowbridge who's been circling the property for two years. He doesn't want the building. He wants the *address*. A guildhall on your street is good for business, even a dying one."

Cassian drank his chicory and looked out the frost-furred window at the street below, where the market was setting up in the gray light. "Ellery won't hear it."

"Ellery won't hear anything. That's rather the problem."

"Odile." He turned, and there was something in his face I couldn't read—not worry exactly, more like a man listening to a sound from another room. "Has anything come in this week? Anything odd? Anything you can't place?"

I almost laughed. "You've seen the ledger. Nothing comes in. That's the season we're having."

He held my eyes a moment longer than the joke deserved, then nodded and took both cups away, including mine, which was also his way. I went back to my columns and thought no more about it until evening, when I went down to bar the front doors and found the candle.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the door of the Veiled Guild: it is not a door people knock on. It is a door people leave things at. Once, supplicants came nightly—lovers wanting their beloved's heart unhardened, merchants wanting rivals ruined, mothers wanting sons home from wars that hadn't started yet. They left offerings on the step: bread, salt, silver, hair, teeth, letters written in blood or tears or both. The guild took what it wanted and left the rest, and the city learned to give us a wide berth, half in reverence and half in the way you avoid a house where something was once done badly.

Now the step stayed bare for weeks at a time. Now the offerings, when they came, were mistakes—an apple core, a child's lost button—and I swept them away each morning without ceremony.

So when I saw the candle, my first thought was *litter.* My second thought, arriving a heartbeat later and considerably colder, was *that candle is burning.*

It stood on the middle of the step, a stub of tallow no bigger than my thumb, and it burned with a flame that did not move. Not steady—*still*, the way water goes still before it freezes. No wind touched it, though wind came off the mountain every evening at dusk and rattled our shutters like a creditor at a door. No wax pooled at its base. And it had not been lit. I looked for the wick's char, the smell of tallow smoke, the little dark history a flame leaves behind, and found none. The wick was white. The flame simply sat upon it, the way a bird sits on a wire, as though it had been burning forever and intended to continue.

I am, by nature and training, a person who counts things. I counted the flame's stillness—one hundred heartbeats, two hundred—while the sky went from iron to black above me. I counted the distance between myself and the door. I counted, uselessly, the number of explanations available, and arrived at zero, which is a number I dislike more than any other.

In fifteen years of keeping impossible accounts, I had seen many strange entries. But I had never once seen a prayer answered. That was the whole architecture of our trade: the gods were gone, had been gone for centuries, gone so thoroughly that their absence had become an industry. People prayed into silence, and the Veiled Guild sold them the next best thing—bargains, trades, substitutions, sleight of hand dressed up as miracle. We were the echo where the voice used to be. Everyone knew it. It was practically our motto, though Master Ellery preferred the older one, embroidered in tarnished thread over the meeting hall hearth: *All Debts Are Sacred.*

And yet.

A candle, lit by no one, burning on our step like an answer to a question nobody here had asked.

I should have swept it away. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the other thing I'm practicing—I should have swept it into the gutter, barred the door, and entered *one (1) anomaly, unresolved* in the margin, which is what a careful keeper does with things that don't fit the books. Instead I crouched down on the cold stone, close enough to feel that the flame gave off no heat at all, and I looked at it the way you look at a ledger line that refuses to balance: patiently, thoroughly, with the growing suspicion that the error is not in the numbers but in what you've been told the numbers mean.

There was something written on the candle. Tiny, in a script I didn't recognize—old, angular, the letters leaning like people walking into wind. I tilted it toward the light of my lamp, careful not to touch the flame, and made out perhaps a third of it before the characters seemed to slide away from my reading, the way a dream slides away from waking.

What I could read was this: *...for the thirteenth turn...*

I don't know how long I knelt there. Long enough for my knees to ache. Long enough for the market noise to die and the street to empty and the mountain's wind to arrive on schedule and bend every tree on our lane—every tree except the flame on our step, which did not so much as flicker.

When I finally reached out to pick the candle up, it went out. Not blown out. Not pinched. It simply ended, between one heartbeat and the next, leaving the wick white and unburnt and the tallow cool under my fingers, as though the fire had never been there at all—as though it had only been *lent*.

I carried it inside. I know I shouldn't have. Rule two says nothing enters the books that cannot be paid, and though the candle wasn't in the books, some part of me understood, with the cold clarity of a keeper staring at a column that won't sum, that taking it was an entry of a kind. That somewhere, something was noting the transaction.

I put it on my desk, in the exact center, weighted down with the guild's oldest seal, and I opened the current ledger to a fresh page.

*Received,* I wrote, *one (1) prayer candle, source unknown, lit by no hand. Terms unstated. Payment pending.*

My pen hovered over the entry. In eleven years I had never once written *payment pending* on an item whose terms I didn't know. It was the accounting equivalent of standing in an open field during a storm.

I wrote it anyway. Then, because the alternative was lying awake listening to the building settle, I climbed to the archive with a lamp, meaning to check the historical records for any precedent—candles, unlit flames, thirteenth turns, any of it.

That was when I noticed, for the first time, that the oldest ledger in the guild's keeping—the one chained to its shelf since before Master Ellery was born—was missing pages. Not torn recently. Torn carefully, long ago, by someone who knew exactly which pages mattered.

Someone had cut the past out of our books. And I had just written an entry that the past was going to come collect.
