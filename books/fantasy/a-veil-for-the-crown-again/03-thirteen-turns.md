## 3. Thirteen Turns

There is a particular art to confessing a sin you've kept warm for fifteen years. You don't do it standing up. You don't do it with good light. And above all, you don't do it until the person asking has already found the ledger page herself—because if she hasn't, there's still a version of the evening where you lie, and lying has always been easier on my knees.

Odile found me in the counting room after midnight, which was where I deserved to be found. The candle she carried was one of ours, guild-stamped wax, and its flame did that thing flames have been doing lately—the little lean toward her, like a dog that knows its name. She set it on my desk without ceremony and stood over me the way a very small person can stand over a much larger one when she has arithmetic on her side.

"Page two hundred eleven," she said.

I had known this moment was coming for fifteen years, and it still arrived like a dropped anvil.

"The deep archive," she went on, "has a ledger older than the guildmaster's chair. Someone tore pages from it. Someone marked names *paid* in a hand I now realize is yours." She turned the book around so I could see my own confession written in my own tidy script. "Cassian. Whose souls did we sell?"

I want it noted, by whatever gods are currently awake and taking notes, that my first instinct was to check the door. Fifteen years of keeping a secret teaches you habits deeper than honesty. The door was shut. The guildmaster was three floors up pretending he didn't hear anything, which was his own art form. There was no one left to protect except the girl in front of me, and protecting her was precisely what the secret had been for.

So I pulled out the second chair, the one nobody sat in anymore, and I said, "Sit down. This will take as long as it takes, and your legs should be under you."

She didn't sit. Odile never sits when sitting would be wise. But she leaned against the shelf, arms crossed, and I took that as the concession it was.

"You know the guild's history," I began. "The official one. Founded to trade in impossible things. Mirrors for the blind, songs for the deaf, second chances at fair prices."

"I know it," she said. "I've recited it at every induction since I was nine."

"Now learn the true one. We were never merchants, Odile. Not originally. We were *brokers*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. A merchant sells what he owns. A broker arranges exchanges between parties who cannot or will not meet. In the beginning, the parties were gods and mortals. A farmer wanted rain; the river-god wanted worship; we drew up the terms and took our commission. Clean work. Honest work, if such a thing exists."

"And then?"

"And then the gods stopped answering." I spread my hands. "You've seen the ledgers. Lean season after lean season. Prayers going unanswered, commissions drying up. What you don't know is how bad it got. Forty years ago we were down to eleven members. Thirty years ago, six. The Veiled Guild was dying, Odile—not fading, *dying*—and then a visitor came up from beneath the mountain wearing a suit cut too well for any living tailor, and he made us an offer."

Her face did something complicated. "The court."

"The court beneath the mountain," I confirmed. "They'd been watching us fail with great interest. They are creditors, child. All of them, every god down there—they don't want worship anymore, they want *obligation*. Worship is voluntary and unreliable. Debt is forever. And here was a guild full of desperate people who knew exactly how to structure an exchange." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughter does when it's been buried too long. "We thought we'd be clever. We thought we'd broker for *them*, arrange their collections, skim a percentage. It took us a decade to understand we hadn't been hired. We'd been *acquired*."

"Sell souls," she said flatly. "That's the business model."

"When a client couldn't pay in coin—and most couldn't, near the end—we offered alternatives. A memory. A year of life. And when even those weren't enough..." I looked at the ledger between us. "Yes. Souls. Not all at once. Never all at once. A trickle, so the books stayed balanced and no one downstairs grew suspicious of abundance. The court doesn't want a flood of souls, Odile. It wants a steady income. We became their collection agency, and in exchange, the guild lived."

"How many?"

I told her. She made me tell her twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misheard.

"Ninety-one," she repeated. Her voice had gone very quiet, which anyone who works with Odile Sandoval knows is worse than shouting. "Ninety-one people walked through our door asking for impossible things, and we sold what they didn't know they were offering."

"They knew," I said, because I owed her the whole ugly truth, not the comfortable version. "The contracts were explicit. That's the worst part. Every one of them signed. Desperation makes excellent penmanship. And the court honors its agreements to the letter—it's the only virtue it has. Nobody was cheated. Everybody was *paid*. That's what the column means. Paid in full, delivered in full."

"And the torn pages?"

"Mine." I met her eyes. "The oldest ledger records the founding debts of the guild itself. Debts older than everyone in this building, including me. When I understood what we'd become, I started removing the pages that explained *why* the court had approached us in the first place. Selfishness, mostly. If the founding debt surfaced, the guild would be liable. Everything would unravel. I chose the institution over the truth, and I'd choose it again, and I hate myself in a way that has become quite boring to me."

Odile was silent for a long time. Then: "Your name is in the paid column, Cassian."

"Yes."

"You sold *your own* soul?"

"No." I closed my eyes. Here it was. The part I'd rehearsed a thousand times in the dark and still could not say smoothly. "My name marks collateral. There's a difference between payment and security, and the court is very fond of the distinction. My soul isn't theirs yet. It's *pledged*—held against a debt that predates me. If the guild defaults on the founding obligation, they collect me along with everything else."

"What founding obligation? What could a debt from before you possibly—"

"Odile." I said her name the way you say a word that's about to break something. "Fifteen years ago, a woman came to this guild. Pregnant, alone, sick with something the physicians called incurable because none of them wanted to admit they were simply poor. She asked for the impossible thing. She asked to live long enough to see her daughter born."

The candle flame leaned hard toward Odile, as if it too wanted to watch her face.

"We granted it," I said. "We brokered with the court. The mother's life was extended—she survived the birth, she recovered, she had years she wasn't promised. And the price..." I pressed my palms flat against the desk, because my hands wanted to shake and shaking would have been unforgivable. "The price was her soul. Payable upon natural death. Standard terms. She signed knowing exactly what she traded. She told me—I remember her exact words—*a lifetime bought with something I'll be finished using anyway. That's the best bargain anyone will ever offer me.*"

"She died," Odile whispered. "Fifteen years ago. You told me she died."

"She died in body. Her soul went below. To the vaults. Where it has been ever since, held as surety against—" I stopped. Swallowed. Some truths have teeth and bite the tongue that carries them. "Against a related debt. One I am not permitted to name tonight. Ask me again when you're somewhere safer than this building, because walls down here have learned to listen."

"Cassian." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "What was her name?"

"Maren," I said. "Maren Sandoval. Your mother didn't die, Odile. Your mother is a lamp-flame in a vault beneath the mountain, and she has been burning there since you were nine years old."

*

Afterward—for some value of afterward, some stretch of time that might have been minutes or might have been a season—she stood at the narrow window of the counting room, looking up at the moon.

I let her look. Grief needs somewhere to put itself, and Odile had always been too proud to cry in front of furniture.

"It's changing," she said finally.

I joined her at the glass. And there it was, plain as a signature: the moon, which should have been a clean silver coin tonight, wore instead a faint seam across its face, a hairline fracture like the crack in old porcelain. The thirteenth turn. Every nineteen years, the moon completes a cycle that astronomers pretend doesn't exist and accountants cannot ignore. Thirteen turns, and all outstanding obligations come due at once. The court's grand settlement. The night every debt in every ledger everywhere is called simultaneously.

"It began last night," I said. "When the unlit candles started burning. The gods waking up—that's not separate news, Odile. That's the *opening bell*. Something is stirring below, getting its accounts in order, preparing for the reckoning. When the moon finishes turning—thirteen nights from now—the court calls in everything. Every soul in the vaults gets processed. Finalized. Filed beyond retrieval."

"Can they be released before then?"

"Debts can be discharged early. It's been done. Once. Maybe twice." I hesitated. "It requires either payment in kind or proof the contract was void from the start, and the court sits as judge over its own cases, so you may imagine how often that succeeds."

"Then we have thirteen nights."

"*We*?" I turned to her. "No. Odile, no. Listen to me. You are a ledger-keeper. You are the best of us, which is a low bar built on a rotten foundation, but still—you balance numbers. You do not walk into the court beneath the mountain. The things down there eat auditors for amusement. I have seen—"

"You've seen what happens to people who go down there and come back," she said. "Yes. I know. You're going to tell me all of it, Cassian. Every detail. Every rule, every trap, every name worth knowing below. Because I'm going whether you help me or not, and the difference between those two outcomes is whether I arrive prepared or arrive as a story someone else tells later."

I looked at her—at Maren's stubborn jaw set into Maren's stubborn expression on a face I'd helped raise—and felt fifteen years of careful lies finish collapsing, quietly, like a building whose last wall finally admits what it's holding up.

"There's one more thing," I heard myself say, before cowardice could catch me. "The founding debt. The one my soul is pledged against. Before you go down there, you need to understand it, because I think—God forgive me, I think it may have your name in it too."

Outside, the cracked moon climbed another degree, and somewhere far below us, something vast turned a page.
