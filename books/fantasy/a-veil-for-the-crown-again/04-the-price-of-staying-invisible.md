## 4. The Price of Staying Invisible

The guildmaster's study smelled of beeswax and old paper and, faintly, of something burnt that had no business being burnt—like a candle that remembered being lit by someone who wasn't there.

I stood in the doorway for a long moment, the torn ledger page folded against my ribs, and watched Maren Voss pretend I wasn't standing in it. She had that particular stillness of hers, the one she wore the way other guildmasters wore rings: a sign of office, a warning. Her ink-stained fingers kept moving over a ledger of her own, small and steady, as though arithmetic could hold the world together if you did enough of it.

I understood the impulse. I'd built my whole life on it.

"You found the archive door open," she said, without looking up.

"The archive door was open."

"Was it." She turned a page. "Curious. Doors in this house have been doing strange things lately. Burning candles. Torn pages. I expect we'll have mice next, singing hymns."

"Maren." I never called her that. The word came out of me like a coin I'd been keeping under my tongue for fifteen years, and it landed on her desk between us, bright and unmistakable. She looked up.

For a heartbeat—just one—something moved behind her face. Not guilt. Guilt would have been easier. It was more like the look of a woman doing sums she already knew the answer to, and hating the answer, and doing them anyway.

"Odile." She set down her pen. "Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit anyway. You'll want to be sitting."

I sat. The chair was the one Cassian used when he came to report, and it still held the shape of him, or I imagined it did, which amounted to the same thing.

"Tell me what you found," Maren said.

"You know what I found."

"Tell me anyway. I want to hear how you say it."

So I said it. Flat, precise, the way I'd say any entry: *Thirteen names in the deep ledger, column marked paid. Payment received by the court beneath the mountain. Cassian Reyes, entered as collateral against a debt incurred before my registration with this guild.* I kept my voice level. I had practiced level. Level was the first thing they taught us in the counting room, before ciphers, before law: *a ledger-keeper's voice must be a locked box; nothing rattles inside it.*

Maren listened with her eyes on my hands, which were not entirely steady, and I hated her a little for noticing, and hated myself more for letting her.

"And the other name," she said quietly. "The one you haven't said yet."

My mouth went dry. "You know that name too."

"I asked to hear you say it."

"Sera Sandoval." My mother. Dead fifteen years, or so the guild's records said—records kept, I now understood, by the same hands that kept every other lie in this house. "Entered as paid. Fifteen years ago. The same year I was registered."

*The same year I was registered.* I hadn't meant to say that part aloud. It slipped out the way water slips through a cracked jar, and once it was out, the whole room seemed to tilt around it.

Maren closed her ledger. Slowly. Like a woman shutting a door on a room where something was sleeping.

"You want to know what it cost," she said. "The guild. Its survival. You want the number."

"I want the truth."

"The truth *is* a number, Odile. That's the first lesson and the last one. Everything else is decoration." She leaned back. "Very well. You're the best ledger-keeper this house has produced in a century, and you've earned a ledger-keeper's answer. The guild was dying. Not fading—dying. The old gods had gone silent, the prayers had stopped coming, and a guild that trades in impossible things cannot survive on possible ones. We had assets. We had names. And the court beneath the mountain was—hungry. So we sold. A soul here. A soul there. Enough to keep the doors open and the wards fed and the ledgers balanced."

"Cassian."

"Cassian volunteered." Her voice didn't change at all, and that was the most frightening thing in the room. "He knew what he was doing. He'd have told you himself, eventually, in his own time, with his own reasons."

"And my mother?"

The stillness in Maren deepened. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, I heard the house settle—or didn't settle; I couldn't tell anymore which sounds in this building were the building's.

"Your mother's entry predates my guildmastership," she said. "It was made by my predecessor. I inherited it the way one inherits a roof that leaks and a debt that doesn't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have that won't insult both of us." She stood, and went to the window, and stood with her back to me, which I recognized as the posture of a woman who did not want her face read. "The moon has begun its thirteenth turn. When it completes, every debt in that ledger comes due at once. The court will collect. Cassian. The others. All of them, all at once, and the guild will not survive the collection, and neither, I suspect, will the city above us, because the wards that keep this city's luck from souring are paid for with exactly the coin you found in the deep ledger."

"Then we renegotiate."

She turned. For the first time that morning, she looked genuinely at me, and what was in her face was almost—almost—kind. "Renegotiate."

"Offer them something else. That's what the guild *does*. We trade in impossible things. So let's trade." I heard my own voice quicken, and I let it, because for the first time in days the fear had a shape, and a thing with a shape can be bargained with. "The court wants payment. Fine. I'm offering myself—not my soul, my *labor*. A lifetime of it. I'm the finest ledger-keeper the Veiled Guild has ever registered, and I'm not saying that out of pride, I'm saying it because it's *true*, and true things are the only currency worth anything down there. Give me to the court as a servant. A clerk. Let me keep *their* books. Fifteen names in that ledger, Maren—fifteen souls—and I'm offering to buy them all back with one lifetime of honest work. That's not even a bad rate. That's a *steal*. They'd be fools to—"

"Odile."

"—and I've already drafted the terms, I did it last night, they're in my pocket, the consideration is enumerated, the duration is fixed, there's a severance clause—"

"*Odile.*"

I stopped. My hands were shaking. I put them flat on my knees, the way you steady a table.

Maren came back from the window and stood over me, and when she spoke, her voice was gentle in the way of someone setting down something fragile that they intend, eventually, to break.

"They don't want your labor, child. They don't want your skill, your years, your cleverness. I know it feels like those things are worth everything—they are, *here*, among people who run on paper. But the court beneath the mountain runs on a different ledger entirely." She crouched, so we were eye to eye, and I saw that she was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Souls, Odile. They want souls. That is the whole of their appetite and the whole of their law. You cannot pay a debt of souls with service any more than you can pay a debt of blood with water. I have sat across from those creditors. I have watched them listen to offers exactly like yours—better than yours, from guildmasters cleverer than either of us—and I have watched them smile the way a ledger smiles when the numbers finally balance. They will take your offer, and they will take your labor, and they will take your soul too, because your labor was never the payment. It was the *bait*. That's how they've always fed."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below the floor, below the cellar, below the archive, below everything, I thought I heard—very far off—a sound like a page turning.

"So that's it," I said. "We do nothing. We wait for the moon to finish turning, and we lose Cassian, and we lose the wards, and we lose the city, and we file the loss under *unavoidable*."

"We survive," Maren said. "It's what this guild does. It's the only thing it has ever done well."

"By feeding people to the dark and calling it bookkeeping."

"Yes." She didn't flinch. I almost wished she had. "And you will do it too, one day, if you live long enough and love this house enough. That's the price of staying, Odile. That's what the invisibility costs. You keep the books, you keep the secrets, you keep your head down, and once every generation or so, you hand over a name, and you tell yourself the arithmetic was unavoidable." She straightened. "I'm offering you the same thing I offered Cassian: stay. Keep the ledgers. Grieve quietly. In thirty years you'll be guildmaster, and you'll hate yourself in exactly the proportion that you're good at it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll do something reckless and noble and doomed, and I will be required to expel you from the guild for it, formally, with the seal, so that when the court comes asking who sent you, my answer will be true." She held my eyes. "I would rather expel you alive than bury you honest. Think about which of us that costs more."

I thought about it. I thought about it the whole walk back to my cell, and the whole time I packed—three candles, a stylus with a lead core, the folded page, a change of clothes rolled tight the way they teach you when you might need to run. I thought about it while I ate the supper I wasn't hungry for, and while I listened to the guild settle around me, forty souls breathing in the dark, each of them warm and each of them, I now knew, warm on borrowed credit.

And somewhere in the middle of that long grey thinking, the fear went out of me.

Not all at once. It went the way a candle goes—not blown out, but *used up*, burned down to the socket, and what was left where the fear had been was something harder and colder and much, much quieter.

I had spent fifteen years being invisible. It was a skill, like any other: the downcast eyes, the unremarkable face, the way of standing in doorways so that people's gazes slid off you like rain off oiled cloth. The guild had taught it to me as a survival art, and I had mastered it, and it had kept me alive, and I had told myself that was enough.

But invisibility, I understood now, was never neutral. It was a *price*. Maren had said so herself, without meaning to. The price of staying invisible was every name in the deep ledger. It was Cassian's soul, marked *paid* in a hand I recognized. It was my mother, burning somewhere in the dark as a lamp for creatures who had never once needed light.

I was done paying it.

* * *

The guild seal lived in the warden's alcove off the counting room, in a box of black oak with three locks, and I had been opening those locks since I was nine years old, because that was the year I learned that locks are just ledgers with hinges—they keep an account of who is allowed, and every account can be balanced by someone patient enough.

I went at the hour between hours, when the house's breathing is deepest. I wore the soft-soled shoes. I took no light, because I had spent fifteen years learning the dark of this building the way other people learn scripture.

At the alcove, I paused with my hand on the black oak box, and I let myself feel the weight of what I was about to do—not to talk myself out of it, but the way you test ice before you commit your whole weight to it. Stealing the seal was theft. Stealing the seal was exile. Stealing the seal was, if Maren's face that morning had told me true, exactly what she expected of me, and had already begun drafting the expulsion writ for, and I found, to my surprise, that this comforted me. Let her write it. Let it be true. Truth, I was learning, was the only coin the dark respected.

The locks opened under my fingers like old men standing up.

Inside, the seal lay on its bed of velvet, a disc of grey metal no bigger than a communion wafer, stamped with the guild's mark: a closed eye above an open ledger. It was warm. It should not have been warm. Nothing in that cold alcove had any business being warm, and I stood there holding it in my palm and feeling its small heartbeat against my skin, and I understood that the seal had been waiting, in whatever way metal waits, for someone to pick it up and take it somewhere it had always been meant to go.

"Odile."

I didn't jump. I was proud of that, later. I turned, slowly, and Cassian stood in the doorway of the alcove with a candle in his hand—the only light in the room, its flame bending toward me as though it were listening.

He looked terrible. He had looked terrible for three days, since I'd found his name, and he had known that I'd found it, and we had orbited each other through the halls of the guild like two stars that couldn't bear to collide. But now here we were, colliding, in a cold room, over a stolen seal.

"You're going down there," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going down there."

"Alone."

"Alone."

"You don't even know the way."

"I know the way the seal knows the way. It's older than I am. It's been down before." I closed my hand around it. "Why are you here, Cassian? To stop me?"

He came closer, and the candle flame leaned with him, and in its light I saw that his eyes were wet and furious and proud, all three at once, which is a combination I have since learned is the truest thing a face can hold.

"To stop you," he said, "I'd have to want to. And I—" His voice caught. He mastered it the way we were both taught to master things, quickly and privately and with cost. "The court keeps its vaults in the deep galleries past the Weeping Stair. The wards there read intent, not words. If you go in wanting to *steal*, they'll take the wanting out of you and keep it, and you'll walk out with empty hands and no memory of why you came. You have to go in wanting to *file a correction*. Do you understand me? You're not a thief. You're a ledger-keeper. You are the only person to walk into that court in three hundred years who has a right to be there."

I stared at him. "You're helping me."

"I sold my soul to keep this guild alive," Cassian said, "and the guild used it to buy silence, and I have been dead on paper for eleven years and walking around anyway, and I would like, before the moon finishes turning, for one thing in my life to be *true*." He pressed something into my free hand, small and cold: a key, black iron, worn smooth. "The Weeping Stair. Third door past the cistern. And Odile—"

"Yes."

"When the road asks you your name—and it will ask—don't give it the one in the ledger." His hand closed over mine, around the key, around the seal, around everything. "Give it the one your mother used."

I stood in the dark with a stolen seal and a borrowed key and a name I hadn't spoken aloud in fifteen years, and the candle flame between us bent low, the way a flame bends when something far below has begun, very quietly, to inhale.

"Go," Cassian whispered. "Before I find my courage and take it back."
