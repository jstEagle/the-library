## 5. The Road That Remembers Names

The road to the mountain began, as all bad ideas do, with a favor.

Rook was waiting for me at the eastern gate before dawn, leaning against the milestone like she'd been carved there by a sculptor with a grudge. She was smaller than I remembered—grief does that, wears people down like water on stone—and her god-talker's bells were gone from her wrists, replaced by plain iron bands that didn't ring at all.

"You're late," she said, though I wasn't.

"I'm punctual," I said. "You're early. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of us has been standing here dreading this."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished, embarrassed to be seen. "You called in the favor."

"I did."

"You could have asked me for coin. Or a horse. Or literally anything less suicidal than walking into the court beneath the mountain."

"I needed a god-talker," I said. "And you're the only one left who isn't dead, defrocked, or bought."

"Flattery." She pushed off the milestone and shouldered her pack. "The last refuge of ledger-keepers."

We walked.

* * *

I should explain what Rook was, or had been. The guild traded in impossible things—bottled storms, second chances, the last words of the dying—but even we needed intermediaries for the oldest transactions. God-talkers were that: people born with the knack of hearing when something vast turned over in its sleep. They carried bells because the old gods answered to sound the way creditors answer to knockings on doors. Rook had been the best of them, once. Then she'd told a client the truth about what his prayer would cost, instead of what he wanted to hear, and the Guild of Bells—our sister order, richer and crueler than ours—had stripped her name from their rolls and her voice from their rites.

She'd come to us afterward, broke and furious, and I'd falsified an entry to hide her from their collectors. One line in a ledger. It had seemed like nothing at the time. It had kept a woman alive. I'd thought about that entry a great deal since learning what other lines in other ledgers had done.

"So," Rook said as the city fell away behind us and the fields took over, brown and stubbled, waiting for a spring that felt late this year. "Tell me what we're actually doing. You said 'retrieve an asset.' That's guild-speak. I want the ugly version."

"The asset is my mother's soul."

She stopped walking. A crow lifted off a fencepost, offended.

"Fifteen years ago they told you she died."

"They lied. Cassian confessed last night. The guild sold souls to the court beneath the mountain to survive the lean years. Hers was among them. And the moon—" I looked up at it, a pale thumbnail in the brightening sky, already visibly fatter than it had any right to be. "It's begun its thirteenth turn. When it completes, every debt comes due at once. Everything they hold gets... consumed. Spent. Whatever gods do with souls when they finally cash them in."

"And you're going to steal it back."

"I'm going to *amend* it back. There's a difference. Theft is crude. Amendment is legal."

"That's the most horrifying sentence anyone has ever said to me."

"I've been practicing it in a mirror."

* * *

The road itself was the first test, and I don't mean the weather or the wolves or the bandits that supposedly haunted the pass, though we saw signs of all three. I mean the road proper—the old pilgrim way that ran up the mountain's flank like a scar. It was older than the guild, older than the city, maybe older than the names of things, and it had a habit that every child learned as a warning and every adult learned as a rumor:

The road remembered names.

Not the names you went by. Not the name your mother used when she called you in for supper, or the name inked into guild rolls, or the name you'd chosen for yourself at fourteen because the original one didn't fit. The road knew the name underneath all those—a true name, the one thing in the world that was only yours—and when you walked it, sooner or later, the road would say it out loud.

Nobody knew why. Nobody knew what happened if something heard.

"We can avoid it," Rook said, studying the way where it split from the cart track and climbed toward the tree line. "There's a shepherd's path around the western shoulder. Adds four days."

"Four days against a moon that's already turning." I shook my head. "We take the road."

"Odile." She said my use-name carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "When the road speaks your true name, everything within earshot learns it. Everything *below* earshot too, if the wind is wrong. The court listens. It has always listened. That's half of why the road exists—to sort the travelers worth collecting from the ones who aren't."

"Then we make sure the wind is right."

"You can't control the wind."

"No," I agreed. "But I can read a ledger, and roads are just ledgers made of dirt. Every record has a pattern. Every pattern has a weakness. We walk at midday, when the air is still and heavy and sound doesn't carry. We talk loudly about boring things. And if the road says my name—" I swallowed. "If it says my name, we run."

Rook looked at me for a long moment. Fifteen years of knowing each other, and she still looked at me like that sometimes, like she was trying to find the girl she'd hidden behind a forged entry.

"You've changed," she said finally. "You used to be afraid of everything."

"I still am." I started up the road. "I've just gotten better at being afraid while walking."

* * *

Midday found us three miles up, where the trees thinned and the mountain showed its bones through the grass. The air was exactly as I'd hoped—still, warm, dense as wool. Sound fell dead a few feet from your lips. We talked loudly about grain prices. We discussed, at length and with genuine passion, the comparative merits of two breeds of mule. Rook delivered a fifteen-minute lecture on tanning methods that I will remember until I die, mostly because I couldn't escape it.

The road let us go almost the whole day.

I know now that it was letting us. That it was listening the whole time, patient as interest accruing, and that our mule conversation amused it not at all. But at the time, as the sun leaned west and the stillness held and the tree line of the upper slopes came into view, I allowed myself the dangerous luxury of believing we'd won.

That was when the stones began to speak.

Not all at once. First a single flagstone under my boot hummed, a note like a wet finger on a rim. Then the next one. Then the next, a ripple running backward down the road behind us, each stone passing the sound along like clerks passing a summons, until the whole visible length of the way was murmuring in a language that predated language.

"It's sorting," Rook breathed. "It's reading us. Walk. Don't stop. Don't answer."

"I wasn't going to answer."

"You were going to answer. You have a face that answers."

The murmuring rose. It wasn't threatening, exactly—that was the worst part. It sounded almost fond, the way our oldest ledger sounded when you opened it, creaking with the weight of everything written there. The road was reading us the way I read accounts: patiently, thoroughly, looking for the true figure beneath the entered one.

Then it found mine.

I felt it happen. There was no other word for it—a sensation like a hook slipping gently under my sternum and lifting, just slightly, just enough that I understood I had been weighed and found *recordable*. The murmur crested, gathered itself, and spoke.

My true name. Out loud. In a voice like gravel remembering water.

I won't write it here. Some entries you keep sealed. But I heard it—I heard *myself*, the self under the self, spoken by a road—and the sound of it went through me like cold water through cloth, and then it rolled down the mountainside, down the switchbacks, down into the valleys below, carried farther than any midday stillness should have permitted, because the wind had changed without asking my permission.

Rook grabbed my arm. "Run."

We ran.

Behind us the road's murmur deepened, spread, sank downward through stone the way water sinks through sand, and far below—farther than any sensible distance, deeper than the roots of the mountain—I felt something turn its attention upward. Not eyes. Not a sound. Just a *weight*, sudden and total, the way a page feels heavier when someone begins to read it.

Something below had heard my name.

And it had recognized it.

Because as we ran, gasping, scrambling over stones, the weight below did not press down on both of us equally. It settled on me alone, curious and pleased, like a creditor who has just spotted a signature he's been looking for—one that appears, if memory serves, in a very old contract.

"*Ledger-keeper,*" the mountain said, in no voice at all, everywhere at once. "*Little keeper of little books. We have an account open in your family's name.*"

Rook's iron bands rang, all of them at once, though nothing had touched them.

The gates of the court were still a full day's climb away.

They opened anyway. Somewhere beneath our feet, with a groan of stone older than sin, something unbarred—and the road tilted gently, helpfully, downhill-toward-the-dark, like a hand offering a chair.

"Odile," Rook whispered. "What did your family sign?"

I stared down the slope, at the dark seam opening in the mountainside ahead, and did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since Cassian's confession. My mother's soul, sold fifteen years ago. My life, saved the same year. Two entries. One date.

"I think," I said, "we're about to find out."
