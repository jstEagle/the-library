## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The seal opened the mountain the way a key opens a throat.

I had expected a door, or at least the suggestion of one—a crack, a seam, something I could pretend was ordinary while I talked myself into it. Instead the rock simply stopped being closed. One moment Rook and I were standing in a hollow of black stone that smelled of cold iron, and the next there was a stairway where the wall had been, descending in long shallow flights into a warmth that had no business existing under a mountain.

"That's not ominous at all," Rook said.

"Ledgers don't open for ominous," I said. "They open for authorized parties." I held up the guild's seal, which had grown warm in my hand somewhere around the third flight of stairs and now sat against my palm like a sleeping animal. "We're authorized."

"We're thieves with paperwork."

"Thieves *with* paperwork are called clerks, Rook. It's the whole difference between us and common criminals."

He snorted, but he went first down the stairs, which I chose to interpret as chivalry rather than a desire to have someone behind him. The light came from nowhere and everywhere—the walls themselves gave off a faint amber glow, like paper held too close to a candle without quite catching. As we descended, the glow resolved into writing. The walls were covered in it. Columns of figures, names, dates, terms of agreement, all of it flowing downward like water finding its level, all of it in a hand I recognized.

My hand. Or rather, the hand every ledger-keeper of the Veiled Guild is trained into: the narrow, upright script they call clerk's-clear, designed to be legible for two hundred years. The mountain was written in our handwriting.

"Odile," Rook said quietly. "Tell me you see this."

"I see it."

"Is that normal?"

"Nothing about this is normal. But yes—it means the court keeps books. That's good news. Courts that keep books can be bargained with. Courts that don't keep books just eat you."

"You say the most comforting things."

"I'm a comfort professional."

* * *

The court itself announced its presence by sound before we saw it: a low murmur, rhythmic as rain on a roof, which turned out to be hundreds of voices reading aloud. Not together—each voice going at its own pace, some whispering, some droning, one laughing softly at intervals like a leak in the ceiling of the dark. We came out of the stairway onto a gallery of black stone, and below us spread a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, filled with tier upon tier of desks, and at every desk a figure bent over a book.

They were beautiful the way frost is beautiful, which is to say: precisely, and without any interest in your survival. Tall, gray-robed, their faces smooth as unwritten pages. Some had too many joints in their fingers. Some had no faces at all, only a faint impression of features, like a name remembered wrong. They read, and read, and read, and the murmur of them rose up through the cavern like heat.

At the far end, raised on a dais of green stone, sat three thrones. On the middle throne sat something old.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because later—when everything went wrong and then right and then differently wrong—I will need to remember that my first feeling was awe. Not fear. Awe. The thing on the middle throne was vast in the way a debt is vast: not large, exactly, but heavy, present everywhere at once, folded into every transaction in the room. It wore the shape of a woman with her hair bound up in a clerk's knot, and when it looked at me—and it did look at me; the whole room's attention swung toward me like a compass needle—I felt known. Catalogued. Entered into a column.

"*A visitor,*" it said, and its voice was the sound of a page turning. "*How rare. How expensive.*"

Everything is expensive here, I would learn. Breathing has a rate. Standing still accrues interest.

I made myself bow—not deep, not shallow, the exact bow a junior ledger-keeper gives a senior one, which communicates respect while reserving the right to correct their arithmetic. "Honored creditors of the deep court. I come on guild business."

"*The guild,*" said the thing on the left throne, and several hundred readers below paused mid-sentence, which was somehow worse than if they'd all turned to look at me. "*The guild has not sent an envoy in forty years.*"

"The guild has not had business worth an envoy in forty years." I kept my voice level. Wryness is armor; I've worn it since I was nine years old and learned that the guildmaster laughs when he's pleased and I wanted him pleased. "But debts mature regardless of whether anyone wishes to discuss them. Thirteen turns of the moon. Your own law, honored ones. I've read it."

"*You have read it,*" said the middle god, and there was something in its tone—not warmth, nothing so human—but interest, the way a lender is interested in a borrower who knows the terms. "*Where?*"

"In your ledgers. Which are, I notice, kept in clerk's-clear. Guild script. Which raises certain questions about provenance that I'd be happy to discuss—" I let the pause stretch, because a pause is a hook and gods bite hooks same as fish, "—after I've completed my business."

"*And your business?*"

"Access to the vaults. Specifically to the collateral holdings under the ninth seal. I'm here regarding a named account."

Rook made a small sound beside me, the sound of a man watching someone juggle knives. I didn't blame him. Named accounts were how souls got sold in the first place; walking into a creditor's hall and saying *named account* was like walking into a wolf den announcing you were made of meat.

The middle god leaned forward. The motion took a long time, the way a shadow takes a long time to cross a valley.

"*Named accounts,*" it said, "*are sealed until maturity. You know this.*"

"I know this," I agreed. "And I know that sealed accounts may be inspected by any party holding a valid claim of kinship, suretyship, or successorship—clause eleven of the Concord of Ashes, which I believe you wrote, honored one, back when you were still answering prayers instead of invoicing them."

The silence that followed had texture. Below us, the readers had gone entirely still, and I understood suddenly that I had said something either very clever or very fatal, and that from the outside these look identical.

Then the middle god laughed. It was not a comforting sound. It sounded like a strongbox opening.

"*Oh,*" it said. "*Oh, they still make them like you down there. Clause eleven. Do you know how long it has been since a mortal cited clause eleven in this hall?*"

"How long?"

"*Longer than you have been alive, little keeper. Perhaps longer than your guild has been dying.*" It lifted one long hand, and the air above the dais unfolded like a ledger being opened. "*You wish to inspect a named account. Very well. State the account.*"

Here it was. The door I'd walked a hundred miles to reach, standing open, and beyond it either my mother or the proof she was past saving.

"The account of Maren Sandoval," I said. "Collateral, entered fifteen years ago. Paid status."

The god's face did not change, but the room did. Every reader below resumed reading at once, all together, a single vast intake of breath through paper.

"*Maren Sandoval,*" the god repeated, tasting it. "*And your claim?*"

"She's my mother."

Three thrones, silent. Somewhere far off in the dark, water dripped, and even the drip sounded like it was being charged for the privilege.

"*Kinship acknowledged,*" the middle god said at last. "*Inspection granted. One hour, standard terms. Note well, little keeper: inspection is not redemption. You may look. Looking costs nothing.*" A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "*Touching is another matter.*"

* * *

They led us down through the vaults themselves, corridors lined with shelves, and the shelves held lamps.

That's what I had expected—Cassian's confession had prepared me for lamps, soul-flames burning in glass—and knowing it did not help. Nothing could have helped. There were thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Whole galleries of flame, each lamp tagged with a lead seal stamped in clerk's-clear, each flame a different color, a different rhythm of burning. Some burned steady as candles. Some guttered, restless, like people talking in their sleep. In one niche a flame flickered in patterns I recognized after a moment with a lurch of horror: it was keeping time. It was counting. Even in here, some soul was doing sums.

"Odile." Rook's voice, low. "Don't look at them too long. They notice being noticed."

"They can't notice. They're flames."

"Everything down here notices. That's rather the point of the place."

I walked faster. The guide—one of the faceless ones, gliding ahead of us like a bookmark sliding through a book—turned down a narrower corridor, and the lamps grew older here, their seals green with verdigris, their flames smaller, dimmer, patient in the way of things that have waited a long time and expect to wait longer.

And then I saw hers.

I knew it was hers before I read the tag. I don't know how to explain that except to say that I have spent my whole life around flames—candles, hearths, the prayer-lights the desperate leave at our door—and a flame is just combustion, chemistry, indifferent physics. This was not that. This flame burned gold shot through with green, and it moved the way a person hums when they think no one is listening, swaying side to side, and something in my chest recognized it the way you recognize a song from childhood: not the notes, but the feeling of being small and safe and sung to.

The tag read: *SANDOVAL, MAREN. COLLATERAL. PAID.*

Paid. Fifteen years of grief, and the word they'd chosen was *paid*, as if she were an invoice settled, a line item closed. My mother, who sang off-key on purpose to make me laugh. My mother, whose hands smelled of bread and ink. Reduced to four words and a green flame in a jar, filed under S.

The awe I had carried into this place—the trembling, stupid awe, the part of me that had thought *these are gods, these are real, I am standing inside a miracle*—curdled in my chest and came out the other side as something harder and much more useful.

Fury.

"Odile." Rook caught my arm. His grip was gentle but his eyes were urgent. "Your hour. Look with your eyes, not your hands. Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," I said, "that clause eleven grants inspection rights to kinship claims. And clause fourteen—" I was reciting from memory, from the Concord I'd read three times on the road while Rook slept, "—grants a kin-holder the right of *contestation*. A formal challenge to the validity of the underlying contract."

"Contesting a contract with the old gods."

"With creditors, Rook. Not gods. Gods forgive. Creditors follow procedure." I stepped closer to the lamp. Up close, the flame leaned toward me, the way a plant leans toward light, and I felt it lean, felt it the way you feel a hand rest on your shoulder. "And procedure," I said quietly, "has loopholes. Procedure always has loopholes. That's why they invented it."

The flame flared—just once, just slightly, gold-green and bright as a struck match.

And behind us, at the end of the corridor, the faceless guide turned its blank head toward the lamp, then toward me, and said in a voice like dry paper:

"*The account holder requests an amendment.*"
