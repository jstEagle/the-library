## 8. The Theft of a Soul

The vault beneath the court smelled of cold iron and old paper, which is to say it smelled like home, if home had been designed by something that hated you.

I had been in the Guild's deep archive since I was six years old. I knew the way ledgers breathed—the faint exhale of vellum in dry air, the way ink settled into paper like a secret settling into a conscience. The vault of the court beneath the mountain was the same animal, only older and better fed. Shelves rose into darkness past the reach of my lamp. Somewhere above me, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental. Counting. Everything down here counted.

I had three hours before the changing of the vault-wardens, and Rook's voice in my ear—well, not literally; he was two galleries over with the original contracts, doing whatever disgraced god-talkers do when left alone with the founding documents of a divine bureaucracy. Probably apologizing to them. He apologized to everything. Doors. Rivers. Once, memorably, a soup.

*Focus,* I told myself, and pulled the ledger toward me across the reading table.

It was the master ledger. Not a copy, not an abstract—the thing itself, the book in which every soul sold to the court was written in the court's own hand, in ink mixed with something I chose not to think about too closely. The cover was black leather gone soft as skin. When I opened it, the pages did not rustle. They *turned*, slowly, of their own accord, presenting themselves for inspection like prisoners at muster.

"Ledger-law," I whispered, because saying it aloud made it real, and making it real made me brave, or at least made me functional. "Article One: a debt is a thing written, and a thing written may be amended by one who understands what is written."

I had spent my whole life learning what was written. That had to count for something. That had to be worth more than blood or courage or any of the currencies the gods actually respected.

* * *

Finding my mother took eleven minutes.

Fifteen years of grief, and it took eleven minutes, because the court kept immaculate books. I found that funny in a way I knew would hollow me out later. Sandoval, Mireille. Entered in a fair hand forty years prior—no. Thirty-nine. She had been sold before I was born, or around the time I was born, the entry amended once, the amendment itself amended, until the truth of her was buried under corrections like a face under veils.

But there she was. A line item. *One soul, female, taken in payment.* And beside the entry, in the margin, a small notation in the court's shorthand: *held as flame, third gallery, western niche.*

A lamp. They were keeping my mother as a *lamp*.

My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because no one who saw me that night would have believed it: my hands did not shake. Ledger-keepers' hands don't shake. We are trained out of it early, trained to hold a pen through anything—a lean season, a funeral, the discovery that your entire life has been purchased with someone you loved. The pen is the point. The pen must not waver.

I uncapped mine.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about altering a ledger, because almost no one has ever been stupid enough to try: the ink remembers. Every entry in the master ledger was written by the court, and the court's ink does not accept correction the way ordinary ink accepts it. You cannot simply cross out. You cannot simply write over. An amendment requires a counterweight—an equal weight entered against the change, so that the books stay balanced. That was the first law I ever learned, back when I thought balance was just arithmetic. Nothing is struck from the record. Something is always given.

I had come prepared to give labor. Years of it. My skill, my service, the only things I owned outright. I had drafted the amendment in my head a hundred times on the road down: *ownership of the soul of Mireille Sandoval transferred from the Court Beneath the Mountain to Odile Sandoval, in exchange for the lifetime labor of Odile Sandoval, valued per guild schedule.*

I wrote the transfer clause first. My pen moved through the court's ink like a knife through silk that wanted to be cut, and the ledger shivered under my hand—not resistance exactly, but attention. The whole book paying attention to me. Somewhere far above, I imagined, the drips stopped counting.

Then I wrote the counterweight clause, laid down my pen, and waited for the ledger to weigh my offer.

The ledger laughed.

There is no other word for it. A sound moved up through the table, through my bones, dry and vast and amused, and the fresh ink of my counterweight clause darkened, thickened, and slid off the page entirely, pooling into a black bead that rolled to the edge of the parchment and dropped into the dark beneath the table like a coin into a well.

*Service,* said the ledger, in a voice that came from everywhere the ink was. *You offer us service. Child. We are eternal. What is your service to us? A candle offers the sun a warm room.*

"I'm the best ledger-keeper the Veiled Guild has produced in a century," I said. My voice sounded very small in all that dark. "That's not nothing."

*It is less than nothing,* the ledger said, almost gently, which was worse. *It is a rounding error.*

I sat with that. I am good at sitting with terrible arithmetic; it is most of my job.

"What do you want, then?"

The pages turned themselves, backward, faster and faster, entries blurring past—souls and souls and souls, forty years of them, a century, three centuries—and stopped at the first page. The founding page. And there, where I expected the court's name, was a blank. A space ruled off neatly, waiting, the way a form waits. The one entry the court had never been able to complete.

*We bought this mountain,* the ledger said, *and paid for it in names. But our own name was taken from us long ago, by the ones who built the guilds, who wrote the laws, who decided that gods should keep books instead of being worshipped freely. We are owed a name. We have been owed a name since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to fear the dark. Bring us a true name, freely given, and your mother walks out of here with her flame in her own hands.*

I understood then, with the particular clarity that arrives only when it is least welcome, exactly what they were asking. Not my life. Not my years. Names are worse. Your name is the handle by which everything else can be picked up—your luck, your love, your memory, your place in other people's mouths. Give a god your true name and you become, in some real and permanent sense, theirs to call.

"That's not a trade," I said. "That's just selling myself a different way. That's what the guild did. That's what I'm here to undo."

*Yes,* said the ledger, and I could have sworn the ink sounded tired. *And we will take it however it comes, because we are owed, and being owed is the only shape eternity has left us. You may refuse. The moon completes its turn in four days, and your mother burns on as she has burned for thirty-nine years. Choose.*

* * *

I want to say I thought of Cassian. I want to say I weighed it, the way I'd been taught to weigh everything, costs against costs, column against column. And I did, a little. I thought about how he'd carried the knowledge of my mother's sale for fifteen years without his spine breaking, and how that must have cost him more than any soul-price. I thought about Rook, two galleries away, probably mid-apology to a contract, who had followed me down here for no reason except that I'd once fed him soup when he was dying in an alley, and favors between the broken are the only currency that never devalues.

But mostly—mostly—I thought about a woman I didn't remember, whose hands I had never held, who had been a lamp in a niche for longer than I had been alive, and who would go on being a lamp until the moon finished its thirteenth turn and the court called in everything at once and there was no one left to bargain for anyone.

Four days. Or now.

I thought about the prayer candle that had burned unlit on the guild's doorstep, the night the old gods woke. Someone had prayed. Someone had hoped hard enough to light a fire that needed no match. I had spent fifteen years keeping my head down, keeping my name small, being invisible because invisible was safe—and safe had gotten my mother thirty-nine years in a niche.

I dipped my pen.

"My name," I said, "is Odile Sandoval. Daughter of Mireille. Keeper of the Veiled Guild's ledgers, youngest of the order, and I give it freely—" my voice caught, and I let it, because a freely given thing should show its cost, "—freely, knowing what it is, in exchange for the soul of Mireille Sandoval, entire and unencumbered, released from the third gallery, western niche, this hour."

I wrote it. My own name, in the counterweight column, in my own hand, and the moment the last letter closed, the ledger drank the ink down and went still as a held breath.

Then, softly, in the margin beside my entry, the court's own hand appeared, writing itself: *Received. Named. Owed.*

The lamps along the gallery walls flared all at once, every flame leaning westward like grass in wind, and I felt it settle onto me—not heavy, exactly. *Attentive.* Like being held. Like being *known*, which is the same thing, and infinitely worse.

Somewhere below, a door I hadn't known existed opened with a sound like a sigh three centuries overdue.

And in my ear, faint and fraying, came Rook's voice, stripped of all its usual apology:

"Odile. Whatever you've done—they're reading the charter. They're reading the guild's founding charter, and Odile—it's *screaming*."
