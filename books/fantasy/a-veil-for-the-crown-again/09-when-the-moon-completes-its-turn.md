## 9. When the Moon Completes Its Turn

The thirteenth moon did not set so much as it was *collected*.

I felt it happen the way a ledger-keeper feels a page turn—something in the world's arithmetic shifting from one column to another. One moment the moon hung above the mountain's broken crown, swollen and wrong, thirteen days of it stacked like coins someone had counted twice. The next, the light beneath the mountain changed. It went from gold to white, the way candlelight goes when the candle is being snuffed and something colder takes its place.

Then every debt came due at once.

I heard it before I understood it—a sound like a thousand quills scratching vellum, rising up through the vault floors, through the bone-white corridors of the court beneath the mountain. The lamps made of souls flickered in their niches, all together, like a congregation drawing breath. Somewhere far above us, I imagined doors slamming open across the world as the old gods' collectors stepped out to gather what they were owed: promises kept too long, prayers answered on credit, names sold for bread.

Rook grabbed my arm. His fingers were cold, which was strange, because Rook's hands were always warm—he talked with them the way other people talked with their mouths, and warmth came off him like heat off a forge. But now they were cold as the marble we stood on, and his voice, when it came, was stripped of all its usual theater.

"That's the calling," he said. "Every debt. Every contract. Everything this court has ever held gets called to account tonight, or nothing does ever again."

"Then we're out of time." My own voice sounded far away. In my hands I still held the ledger I'd been altering—the one with my mother's soul recorded as a lamp-flame in the west gallery, entry eleven thousand four hundred and six, ownership pending transfer to Odile Sandoval. Pending. Because the counterweight had never been weighed. Because the gods had wanted my name, and I had run instead of paying.

"I have to get to the Hall of Instruments," I said. "Before the calling finishes."

"The *what*?"

"You'll see."

* * *

We ran.

The court beneath the mountain is built like a ledger itself—I'd understood that by my third day down here, and hated how much I admired it. Every corridor is a column. Every hall an entry. The gods who ruled this place did not think in stories; they thought in balances, in sums carried forward, in debts honored and defaulted. It was the most beautiful and most monstrous thing I had ever seen, because it was my own craft, perfected past the point of cruelty.

The Hall of Instruments sat at the court's heart, and we reached its threshold just as the last of the moonlight drained from the high shaft that pierced the ceiling—no window, no sky, just a vertical wound in the stone that let the thirteenth moon look down on everything the gods owned.

Inside, they were already assembled.

I had seen individual gods before, in these weeks below: a collector with a mouth full of counting-beads; a thing called the Assessor whose face was a scale that weighed whatever it looked at. But I had never seen them all together, and I will not pretend the sight did not unmake me a little. There were dozens of them. Hundreds. They filled the Hall like weather fills a valley, tall as pillars, some of them barely more than shadows wearing obligations, and every one of them turned toward me when I crossed the threshold.

Not toward Rook. Toward me.

"*Debtor,*" said the one nearest the door, and its voice was the sound a coin makes when it stops spinning.

"I'm not here to default," I said, and was quietly astonished that my voice didn't shake. "I'm here to file."

The scratching sound paused. All those quills, hanging mid-stroke. Even the lamps in their distant galleries seemed to lean in.

The Assessor drifted forward, its scale-face tilting. "*File.*"

"A claim against the estate." I lifted the ledger in my arms like an offering, which, in a sense, it was. "Under your own law. Article the first of the Concord of Weights—you don't get to be offended by that; you wrote it, or rather, you *are* it. Any party may contest a debt if they can show the collateral was never lawfully possessed."

A murmur went through the Hall—not sound exactly, but pressure, the way air moves before a storm decides. The Assessor's scale-face tipped slowly toward me.

"*Show it.*"

So I opened the ledger to the page I had spent three nights preparing, and I told them the truth.

Not the whole truth—I didn't have the whole truth yet; that was still sitting in Rook's chest like a swallowed coal, though he didn't know I knew—but enough truth to kill a guild.

"The Veiled Guild," I said, "was founded two hundred and eleven years ago by a woman named Maren Sandoval. No relation. Her charter is recorded in your own archive, sealed in iron, entry nine. I've read it. I've read what it says the guild paid for its founding."

The pressure in the room sharpened. Gods do not like being reminded of their own paperwork. It is, I suspect, the mortal equivalent of being shown photographs of yourself naked.

"And what did she pay?" I asked the Hall. "What did the first ledger-keeper trade for the right to trade? Not gold. Not souls. She traded *light*. She took the divinity of a dying god—the god of small mercies, the one whose shrine used to stand where the Guildhall's front step is now—and she bound it into the charter itself. That's why the guild could bargain with impossible things. That's why our contracts held. We weren't clever. We were running on stolen fuel."

I let the silence stretch. Ledger-keepers know the power of a silence entered correctly into the record.

"Every contract the guild has ever signed," I said, "every soul sold into this court—including entry eleven thousand four hundred and six, including Cassian Vale, including a lamp burning in the west gallery that has a name none of you bothered to record—was executed under a seal powered by divinity that did not belong to the guild. Stolen collateral. Void consideration. Under Article the first—" my voice cracked, finally, and I let it, because grief entered honestly carries more weight than grief hidden— "*the debts are void. All of them. You must release the souls.*

"Not because I'm asking. Because you're bound. You are, all of you, creatures of law, and law is the only prayer you've ever answered."

* * *

They deliberated.

Gods do not deliberate the way people do, with muttering and walking and cups of terrible tea. They *weighed*. I felt it happening—the Assessor turning its scale-face over the ledger, over the iron-sealed charter Rook had fetched from the archive at a dead sprint, over me, standing in the middle of the Hall of Instruments with ink on my fingers and no name left to lose.

Because that was the other thing. The alteration I'd begun three nights ago had never been completed, and when I'd fled the vault rather than pay my name as counterweight, the entry had half-closed behind me like a door left ajar. The court considered me, I think, less a debtor now than a rounding error. A discrepancy to be reconciled.

Let them reconcile *this*, then.

"They'll refuse," Rook whispered beside me. "Odile, they'll find a way. They always find a way. That's what they *are*."

"Then they'll break their own law to do it," I said, "and the law will stop holding them together, and they'll come apart like a badly kept book. I've seen it happen to institutions. It's the only thing institutions do well."

The weighing went on long enough for me to count my heartbeats and give up somewhere past four hundred. Long enough for the last white light of the thirteenth moon to fade from the shaft overhead, leaving only the lamps, only the souls, only the small warm fires of everyone the guild had ever sold.

And then the Assessor spoke, and its voice was not the sound of a coin settling.

It was the sound of a page turning.

"*Consideration found void. Collateral found stolen. Contracts executed under false seal: null from inception.*" The scale-face swung level. "*The court is bound. Release is ordered.*"

The scratching resumed—all those quills, all at once, but the sound had changed. It wasn't collection anymore. It was *correction*. And down in the west gallery, I knew without seeing it, without anything so kind as knowing, a lamp-flame with my mother's eyes was being carefully, formally, irreversibly unclenched from the hand that had held it for fifteen years.

I sat down on the floor of the Hall of Instruments, because my legs had resigned without notice, and I laughed until it turned into something else entirely.

Rook crouched next to me. He looked like he wanted to say something—the coal-shaped thing, the truth about entry one, about a life traded for a life, about why my mother's soul had been the price of mine. I watched him decide not to. Not yet. Not here.

"Later," he said instead.

"Later," I agreed, and meant: *when my legs work, when my mother has a body again, when there is a sky above us that isn't counting anything.*

The gods began to disperse around us like fog surrendering to morning. And at the door of the Hall, pausing on her way out into whatever gods do with freedom, the Assessor turned its scale-face back toward me one final time.

"*One entry remains unreconciled, debtor,*" it said. "*Yours.*"

I looked at it. I looked at the ledger lying open on the floor between us, at the blank page waiting after the last corrected line, at the space where a name should go and didn't.

"I know," I said. "Give me a pen."

It did. Of course it did. It was, after all, bound by its own law.

And as I dipped the pen and set it to the page—as somewhere far above us the sun came up on a world where every debt had just been forgiven, and somewhere below us a woman fifteen years dead began to remember having lungs—I wrote the only entry I had left in me, the one I'd been carrying since I was a child balancing other people's accounts in a house built on theft:

*Paid in full. By whom, and for what, remains to be recorded.*

I signed it with no name at all.

Which, under Article the first, made it the most binding contract in the history of the court beneath the mountain—and left the question of who Odile Sandoval would be tomorrow, in a world with no Veiled Guild and no debts and no walls to hide behind, wide open on the table like a fresh, terrifying, magnificent page.
