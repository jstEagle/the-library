## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The storm came down off the high crags an hour before dusk, the way storms always did in Thornfell, as if the mountain itself had decided to shrug off its cloak and shake out all its old grievances at once. Noor Quintana stood at the mouth of the Hollowmere Pass with her collar up and her hood low, counting the rain where it struck the stone, and listening.

Not to the rain. Anyone could listen to rain. She listened beneath it — to the deep, grinding patience of the rock, to the water threading through cracks older than any kingdom that claimed this land, and to the small, sour voice of something that lived in the scree slope above the third switchback and hated travelers with a passion that had outlasted empires.

*They come again,* said the goat spirit, whose name Noor had never learned because it had never offered one. Its voice was like gravel poured slowly into a wooden pail. *Soft things. Wet things. They climb my slope and their hooves — their feet — knock loose my stones. My stones.*

"Your stones are safe," Noor murmured into her scarf, so quietly that the words barely disturbed the air in front of her lips. Behind her, the smuggler caravan was still making camp in the lee of the pass gate, mules stamping, canvas snapping, men laughing too loudly the way men did when they wanted the storm to know it didn't frighten them. None of them looked at the thin woman at the edge of the light who spoke to nothing at all. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement. "I'll keep them off your slope. I'll take them by the river shelf instead."

*The river shelf is mine too.*

"It's yours," she agreed. "All of Thornfell is yours. I only ask passage."

A long silence, filled with rain and the mutter of distant thunder rolling through the peaks like a barrel being dragged across a floor. Then, grudgingly: *Payment.*

Noor had expected this. She reached into the inner pocket of her coat and drew out the twist of waxed paper she'd carried up from Duskmarket three days ago: dried rowan berries, a handful of salt from the southern flats, and — the true prize, bought at a price that would have made the caravan master weep — a lump of honeycomb, dark amber, smelling of summer meadows a thousand miles from this cold. She set it on a flat stone at the path's edge, tucked beneath an overhang where the rain couldn't reach it.

"For you," she said. "And I'll sing the old verse at the cairn, if you like. The one about the grey goat of Hollowmere who was old when the kings were young."

*I am not old,* the spirit snapped. *I am enduring. There is a difference.*

"There is," Noor said solemnly. "Forgive me."

Another silence. Rain drummed on the flat stone above the offering. When the gravel-voice came again, it had softened by perhaps a single degree, which for the goat spirit of Hollowmere Pass constituted outright affection.

*Take them by the river shelf. Keep them from my scree. And tell the loud one with the red beard that if he strikes flint near my birches, I will drop a boulder on him and not feel sorry.*

"I'll tell him," Noor said, and meant it, and knew the spirit knew she meant it, and that was the whole secret of her life folded up small enough to fit between two sentences: she kept her word to the things in the mountains, and the things in the mountains kept theirs to her, and no human being alive needed to know why the caravans she guided never lost anyone to rockfall while other guides buried their dead every season.

She turned back toward the campfire glow. The rain streamed down her hood. Somewhere above, half-hidden in the murk, she felt the goat spirit settle itself along the ridgeline like a long grey shadow with too many joints, watching her with eyes like chips of quartz, and she felt — not comforted, exactly. Acknowledged. Which in twenty-nine years of living was the closest thing to company she'd learned to trust.

* * *

Verrin Doss, caravan master of the Salt Road Company, was a broad man built like a door and about as talkative, which was why Noor had guided for him four seasons running. He paid on time, asked no questions, and held the superstition — common among smugglers, and the only kind of religion Noor had ever found useful — that a guide who brought you through alive should be fed first, thanked second, and questioned never.

"River shelf?" he said now, squinting at her through the fire-smoke as she laid out the route on the oilcloth spread between them. His red beard was soaked flat against his jaw. "In this rain? The ford'll be up."

"The ford will be up past the crossing stones by midnight, which is why we cross at moonrise instead, while it's only knee-deep." She tapped the map. "Then the shelf stays above the waterline the whole way to the Narrows. We lose four hours against the scree route but we keep our feet dry and our necks unbroken."

Doss grunted, which from him was a speech. Around the fire, his men were drying socks and arguing about dice, and one of them — a wiry youth named Pell, three runs into his first season — glanced over at Noor with the particular expression she'd learned to read in a hundred variations: curiosity strangled by fear. He'd heard something, maybe. A rumor about the Quintana woman. About her mother.

He looked away first. They always did.

"Eat," Doss said, shoving a bowl of barley and mutton at her. "You'll want it. Moonrise crossing means no sleep till the Narrows."

Noor took the bowl and ate with her back against a supply crate, knees drawn up, hood still dripping. This was the part of guiding she was best at, she sometimes thought — not the passes or the weather or the bargaining, but this: sitting at the edge of human warmth without quite entering it. Close enough to smell the mutton and hear the jokes. Far enough that when someone finally did look at her too long, when someone finally asked the wrong question in the right tone of voice, she could be gone before the answer mattered.

Her mother had burned for less than what Noor did every night in the dark, murmuring to spirits under her breath. Eleven years ago — Noor had been eighteen, and had watched from the crowd because running would have been a confession — they'd stacked the faggots around Maren Quintana in the market square at Highmoor, and the warden reading the charges hadn't even used the word *beast-speech*. He'd used *consorting*, and *unnatural commerce*, and *traffic with the unquiet wild*, a string of legal silk wound around a simple truth: Maren Quintana talked to animals, and animals talked back, and somewhere in the capital that was considered worse than murder because murder at least stayed within the proper order of things.

Noor had inherited the gift whole, along with her mother's green eyes and her habit of humming when she was frightened. She had also inherited the lesson, purchased at the price of a life: *never let them see, never let them hear, never give them a reason.* So she spoke to spirits in whispers and in silence, in the space between heartbeats, and she guided smugglers because smugglers were the only people in Thornfell who made a profession of not asking questions, and she told herself — every night, lying under the stars with her bedroll a careful distance from everyone else's — that this was enough. Solitude wasn't loneliness. Solitude was safety. There was a difference.

There was a difference.

Pell drifted over near midnight, when the fires were banked and the mules were loaded. He fell into step beside her at the head of the column, boots squelching, and for a while said nothing, which she appreciated. The pass rose around them, black walls wet and gleaming, the river roaring somewhere below in the dark like a beast pacing its cage.

"They say you can read weather," he offered at last. "Better than the almanacs."

"I pay attention," Noor said.

"And the rockfalls. They say the Quintana woman walks under falling mountains and nothing ever falls on her."

"Careful footing," Noor said. "And luck."

"Luck." Pell laughed, a little breathlessly. On the switchback above them, loose stone clattered — just the mountain settling, just water finding new paths — but both of them flinched, and Noor felt, rather than heard, the goat spirit shift its weight along the ridge, attentive, proprietary. *My stones,* it thought at her, wordlessly, and she sent back a thread of calm like a hand laid flat on a spooked animal's neck. *Mine. Safe. Walking the river shelf. Yours, all yours, the whole grey kingdom, nothing taken, nothing touched.*

"That's a lot of luck," Pell said quietly.

Ahead, Doss raised a fist, and the column halted at the top of the descent to the ford. The river ran black and swollen below, flecked with white, and the moon was fighting through torn cloud just well enough to show the crossing stones — six granite humps breaking the current, slick and close together, the water already licking their shoulders.

"Noor." Doss's voice, low. "You sure?"

She looked at the water. She listened past it — to the river's own voice, cold and vain and half-asleep, boasting to the stones about how much it carried, how far it had come, how little anything could stand against it. Rivers lied the way children lied, hugely and transparently, and this one was lying about its depth on the far side of the third stone, where the current had quietly dug out a hole deeper than a man was tall.

"Third stone's sound," she said. "But don't stop on it. Keep moving, one mule at a time, ten paces apart. If anyone stops on the third stone they'll go down, and I won't fish them out."

Nobody stopped on the third stone. It took an hour, mule by mule, man by man, the water clawing at their thighs and the wind driving sleet sideways off the crags, and Noor stood at the crossing with her hands bare in the freezing spray, whispering to the river the whole time — *not tonight, not these ones, carry your ice elsewhere, there are deer carcasses rotting two miles downstream, go gorge yourself on the easy dead* — until the last man stumbled up the far bank and Pell turned and looked at her across the water with an expression that was no longer curiosity at all.

It was awe. And awe, in Noor's experience, was more dangerous than suspicion. Suspicion you could laugh off. Awe made people follow you. Awe made people watch.

* * *

They made the Narrows two hours before dawn, a slot canyon where the walls leaned so close together that the sky was a ragged ribbon of cloud overhead, and here Doss called the halt because there was no choice — the shelf beyond was washed by a waterfall in full spate, impassable until the rain slackened, and the rain showed no sign of slackening. The men wedged themselves into a hollow with the mules and lit a single shielded lantern, and Noor climbed the shelf alone to check the waterfall's temper.

Up there, with the water thundering down the rock face close enough to mist her face, she let herself speak aloud for the first time in hours. It was a luxury she allowed herself only when the noise would drown her — a guilty indulgence, her mother's habit of talking to everything, surfacing in her like blood from an old scar.

"You're loud tonight," she told the falls.

*Loud, loud, loud,* the water agreed gleefully, tumbling over itself. *Everything comes down, everything goes down, down to the sea, down down—*

"When will you be tired?"

*Tired when the sky is empty. Not yet. Not yet, not yet.* A pause, and then, with the sudden slyness water sometimes had: *Something else is coming up the pass behind you, little speaker. Something walking soft. Something with iron.*

Noor went still, rain streaming off her chin. "Travelers?"

*Not travelers. Travelers clatter. This one walks like the owls walk. Quiet-quiet. And it smells of—* the water churned, searching its vast shallow memory — *of ash. Old ash. And paper. And grief.*

Behind her, down in the hollow, the lantern glowed small and warm among the sleeping shapes of men and mules. Ahead, the pass climbed on toward the high country and the waystation at Grey Saddle, where she'd planned to leave the caravan and turn home. Behind her, something was climbing. Something quiet, that smelled of ash and paper and grief.

Wardens rode horses. Wardens clattered. Whatever this was moved alone, on foot, through a storm, in the dark — and Noor had lived long enough in Thornfell to know that the mountain's own creatures feared very few things, but the water, chattering mindless and honest as only water could be, had named the smell of the one thing that had killed her mother.

Fire. And documents. And sorrow.

She stood a moment longer in the roar of the falls, letting the cold soak through her, doing the arithmetic of her life. She could wake Doss, break camp, push through the flooded shelf with ropes and lose two men to the water — or she could finish the run as planned and put the pass between herself and whatever followed. Or she could simply vanish, now, into the side ravines she knew better than her own hands, and let the caravan find its own way to Grey Saddle.

She looked down at the lantern. At Pell, asleep with his head on a crate, young and awed and dangerous. At Doss, who paid on time and asked nothing, which was the nearest thing to kindness her trade afforded.

"Thank you for telling me," she said to the falls.

*Little speaker,* the water sang after her, delighted and terrible, as she climbed down. *Little speaker, the ash-walker isn't following you. It's following the road. Everyone who walks the road tonight walks toward the same thing. Down, down, everything goes down—*

Noor dropped the last few feet to the shelf, landed in the wet gravel, and stood looking north up the pass, where the storm had torn open just far enough to show a smear of stars above Grey Saddle — and, faint on the wind beneath the rain, a smell that did not belong to the mountain at all.

Smoke. Not her campfire's smoke. Older. Colder. Like a fire that had been put out a long time ago and never quite forgiven.

Somewhere ahead, at the waystation where she meant to sleep tomorrow night, something was burning. Or something had burned. And the road, as the water said, ran straight toward it.
