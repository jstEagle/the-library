## 2. A Stranger at the Waystation

The storm had been building all afternoon the way storms did in Thornfell — not arriving so much as assembling itself out of spite, cloud by cloud, until the whole sky above the pass looked like a bruise going bad. Noor Quintana watched it gather from the doorway of Hollowpine Waystation while she split kindling, and she did not like what she saw.

Not the weather itself. Weather was honest. Weather wanted things and said so plainly: *shelter me or drown*. It was everything else that came with weather that worried her. Storms closed the high passes, and closed passes meant travelers who should not be traveling — couriers running late, soldiers cutting corners, smugglers with cargo too precious to wait out three days of rain in some lowland inn. And every one of them would end up here, at her door, wanting fire and stew and silence.

She could sell them the first two. The third she took for herself.

The waystation sat where the Thornfell road narrowed between two shoulders of granite, a squat stone building with a slate roof weighted down against the wind and a stable that smelled of hay and old horse sweat. It had been hers for six years now, ever since she'd scraped together enough coin from guiding to buy it from a man who'd been only too glad to leave the mountains behind. Officially, she kept it as a rest stop for licensed caravans. Unofficially, everyone on the wrong side of a border knew that if you could pay, Noor Quintana asked no questions, checked no seals, and remembered no faces.

It was a good life, if you didn't look at it too closely. Warm in winter, quiet in summer, far enough from any town that nobody noticed how she talked to the goats.

The goats were restless tonight. That was the first sign.

She'd turned them into the lower paddock before the rain started, and instead of huddling under the rock overhang like sensible animals, they stood facing up-valley, ears swiveled toward the pass, making a sound low in their throats that wasn't quite bleating. Noor leaned on the paddock fence and listened with the part of her that wasn't supposed to exist.

*Something coming,* the eldest nanny told her, in the way goats communicated — less words than weights, meanings pressed flat and passed mouth to mouth. *Something hurt. Something afraid.*

"Bandits?" Noor murmured, too quietly for any human ear.

*Wrong shape,* the goat answered, which meant nothing and something. Goats had no gift for precision. But the fear in the meaning was real enough that Noor went back inside, banked the fire high, loaded her crossbow, and set it within reach of the door. Then she put the kettle on, because whatever came through that pass tonight, it would come cold and wet, and even dread deserved a cup of tea.

The second sign came an hour after full dark, when the storm finally broke and the rain came down in ropes. She heard the horses first — three sets of hooves moving fast along the switchbacks, unshod or muffled, which no honest rider bothered with. Then voices, thin and torn by wind, shouting to each other over the thunder. Royal cadence. She'd guided enough tax convoys to know it.

Noor snuffed the lamp by the window, took up the crossbow, and waited.

They passed without stopping. Three riders in oilskin cloaks, moving hard down-valley toward Duskmarket, and even in the lightning-flashes she caught the grey beneath the wet — not smuggler's grey, not merchant's grey, but the particular blue-tinged grey of the Crown's wardens. They rode like men following a scent rather than a road, heads down, one of them holding something up against the rain that glimmered faintly even in that light.

A lodestone compass. She'd seen wardens use them once, years ago, hunting a debt-fleeing jeweler through the eastern passes. They didn't follow roads. They followed *traces* — blood, mostly, or the residue of certain kinds of magic that the Crown preferred not to name aloud.

The hoofbeats faded. The rain filled the silence back in. Noor stood in the dark of her own kitchen with her heart going harder than it had any right to, and told herself it was none of her business. Wardens hunted who they hunted. The mountains were full of people running from something. Her job was the waystation, the passes, the tolls, and nothing else.

The third sign came at moonrise, or where the moon would have been if the clouds had left any sky for it.

A knock. Not a knock, really — a collapse against wood, repeated twice, the sound of someone spending their last strength on the simple act of standing upright. Noor set down the crossbow, picked it up again, then made herself leave it on the table. A wounded man at her door was a wounded man. She could be careful and still be human. Her mother had managed both, once.

She opened the door on a stranger who fell across the threshold like a bundle of wet sticks.

He was young — younger than her, maybe twenty-five, though it was hard to tell under the blood and the mud and the grey stubble of a man who hadn't slept in days. Courier's leathers, travel-scuffed, the royal courier's badge ripped from his shoulder but the stitching still there, a pale ghost of it. His left side was soaked dark from hip to ribs, and when he tried to push himself up from her floorboards his arm folded and he made a sound he clearly hated himself for making.

"Door," he rasped. "Bar the door."

Noor looked past him at the empty, roaring dark of the pass, then shut it and slid the bar home. "Who's hunting you?"

"Wardens." He laughed, and it turned into a cough that brought up something wet. "Three of them. Maybe four by now." His eyes found hers, and even half-blind with pain they were sharp, searching, doing arithmetic. "You're the guide. Quintana. The one who takes caravans over Sorrowback in winter."

"I'm the woman who runs this waystation," Noor said. "That's all anyone needs to know I am."

"No." He caught her wrist. His grip was fever-hot and shockingly strong. "I've asked in three valleys. They all say the same thing. *If the mountain talks to you, find Quintana.*"

The words landed in her chest like a dropped stone into a well. Noor went very still, the way she'd learned to go still as a child, the stillness that had kept her mother's fate from becoming her own. "People say a lot of foolish things about mountain guides."

"They do." He sagged back against the wall, breathing in short, careful pulls, the way men breathed when their ribs were broken. "And then they say them anyway. Because they're true." He fumbled at his belt pouch with shaking hands. "I don't have time to convince you. They'll double back when they lose my trail at the river. An hour. Less."

"Then let me stitch you and put you in the cellar and we'll—"

"There's no stitching this." He said it simply, without drama, the way he might have reported the weather, and somehow that was worse than screaming would have been. "Warden steel. Poisoned, I think, or cursed, or both — I stopped being able to tell the difference around noon." He got the pouch open. "Listen to me. My name is Ferren Vale. I carry dispatches for the Crown. Have carried them for nine years. And eleven days ago I was given one that I was ordered never to read, sealed with the heir's own signet, and I read it, because I'm a coward and a curious man and those are the same thing."

Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like a barrel of stones.

"The prince," Ferren said, "sold his soul."

Noor had spent her whole life learning to keep her face closed. She kept it closed now. But inside, something old and cold turned over, because her mother had used those exact words once, drunk and weeping, years before the pyre: *the crown beneath the crown, the court beneath the mountain, and what the boy-king paid them.*

"That's a mad story," she said.

"It's a true one, and it's ending, and I'm the last man carrying the proof." He pressed the pouch into her hands. It was heavier than it looked. "There's a map in there. Blood-marked — my blood, warden-bonded, so they can track it, so listen carefully. You have to burn the tracking sigil off it. Salt and silver, quick flame, don't let it scream. Then it's just ink and paper, and it becomes the most dangerous document in the realm."

"Ferren—"

"The solstice." His voice cracked on the word, and his eyes were bright now, feverish, fixed on her face with terrible urgency. "Nineteen days. The court beneath Thornfell comes to collect what was promised, and the Crown has agreed to hand him over — do you understand? Not rescue him. *Hand him over.* There are envoys already traveling. I was carrying the schedule. That's why they can't let me live."

Noor's hands had gone numb around the pouch. "Why me? I'm nobody. I guide smugglers. I—"

"You're a beast-speaker."

The words hung in the smoky air of the kitchen, and Noor felt six years of careful walls develop hairline fractures all at once. Nobody knew. Nobody living knew. She had buried that word with her mother's ashes and built a life on top of the grave.

"You flinch when the goats make noise," Ferren said, and there was something almost gentle in his wrecked voice. "But you answer them. I watched you through the window for ten minutes before I knocked. You tilt your head when they talk. Like my grandmother did. She was one too, in the old country, before the purges." He coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed there was blood on his lips. "The map needs more than a guide. It needs someone the deep places will *listen* to. My grandmother said the mountain courts respect the old tongue. Said they remember it, even when the surface world burns it out."

"My mother burned for that tongue," Noor said, and her voice came out flat and dead. "In Ashmarket square. I was nine. So you'll forgive me if I don't run off to visit the thing that killed her."

Ferren smiled. It was a terrible smile, gray-lipped, but there was real humor in it somewhere underneath. "Then don't. Burn the map. Forget me. Live your quiet life." He closed her fingers tighter around the pouch. "But you opened the door. Beast-speakers always open the door. My grandmother said that too."

The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof. And Noor stood there holding a dead man's secret and hating him for the accuracy of his dying.

"Tell me what's actually on the map," she said. "Before you tell me anything else."

"Tunnels." He slid down the wall until he was sitting, conserving what was left of him. "Old ones, older than the kingdom. Ways under Thornfell that don't appear on any survey — because the surveyors who found them didn't come back, or came back wrong. The map shows the way down to the Weeping Stair, and past it, to the sealed gate of the court beneath. My orders were to deliver it to the warden-captain at Duskmarket before solstice." His mouth twisted. "Which means delivering the prince's death warrant into the hands of his executioners. I decided I'd rather die a traitor to the Crown than a faithful servant of this particular lie."

"And the blood-marks?"

"Her handwriting." For the first time, Ferren hesitated. "The map's original cartographer signed her work. A woman named Maerin Quintana. I assumed kin of yours. The name's not common."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way it does when the ground reminds you it was never as solid as you believed. Noor sat down on the bench across from him without deciding to. Her mother's name. Her mother's *handwriting*, on a map of tunnels beneath the mountain, carried by a dying courier, hunted by wardens, nineteen days before a solstice her mother had also died close to — though Noor had never learned the details of that bargain, only that her mother had gone to the mountain in the winter before the pyre and come back changed, quieter, watching the northern peaks like a woman waiting for a bill to come due.

"She drew maps," Noor heard herself say. "For the mining guilds. Before I was born."

"Then maybe she drew this one for the guilds too." Ferren's kindness was a deliberate thing; she could see him choosing it. "Or maybe she drew it for reasons of her own. Either way — it's yours now. I have no one else. There is no one else."

He taught her then, in a voice growing thinner by the minute: the salt-and-silver burning, the sigil's location in the map's corner margin, the passphrase stitched into the lining of the pouch in courier's cipher — *the veil holds what the crown cannot* — and the name of a contact in Duskmarket, a minstrel called Tamrin Voss who owed the courier service a favor and would deny it loudly if asked directly. "Ask him badly," Ferren said. "He responds better to insults than gratitude."

Outside, far down the valley, a horse screamed. Not one of theirs. One of the wardens', spooked by something in the treeline — or by something that had found *them*.

Ferren's head came up. "How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." Noor was already moving, pulling salt from the pantry, the silver candle-snuffer from its hook, dragging the brazier away from the hearth. "Cellar's behind the flour shelf. There's a crawl-tunnel out the back into the scree. You could—"

"No." He said it firmly enough that she stopped. "I'm done running. And if they find me here, they find me here, and you were never involved — a sick traveler stumbled in, you fed him, he died. That's a true story. Keep it true." He watched her lay the map's corner over the coals, watched the salt hiss, watched the hidden sigil begin to glow a sullen red. "Burn it clean, Quintana. Don't stop when it screams."

It screamed. Of course it screamed — a thin, whistling, almost-human shriek as the tracking magic died, and Noor held the tongs steady with her jaw clenched and her eyes watering and thought, absurdly, of her mother's hands, of the way Maerin Quintana had held a pen, small and precise, drawing lines through the dark under the world. The sigil blackened, curled, and crumbled to ash. The map lay there afterward looking like nothing at all: old paper, faded ink, a labyrinth of tunnels drawn in a sure and loving hand.

*Maerin Q.*, in the corner, in letters Noor had seen a thousand times on shopping lists and birthday notes and the margins of her childhood books.

The knocking started again — but this time it was boots, many boots, and a voice like a filed blade: *"Royal wardens! Open in the name of the Crown!"*

Ferren met her eyes across the kitchen. All the urgency had drained out of him, leaving something calm and almost young. "Back door," he whispered. "Take the map and go. Now. Don't watch."

Noor did not go. She blew out the lamps, pulled him into the shadow behind the woodpile, and opened the front door with her crossbow held loose and visible and her face arranged into the blank, weary hospitality of a mountain innkeeper roused at midnight.

Four wardens in royal grey, rain streaming off their cloaks, swords drawn but lowered. Their captain was a broad woman with a scarred jaw and eyes that catalogued the room in one sweep — the fire, the brazier, the smell of burnt salt.

"Traveler came through," Noor said. "Bleeding. I gave him water. He's dead or near it — behind the woodpile, if you want him."

The captain looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, Noor felt Ferren's breathing stop.

"Dead," the captain repeated.

"Dying when he arrived. Couriers shouldn't ride the Sorrowback switchbacks in a storm." She shrugged, letting weariness do the work of innocence. "Take him, if he's yours. I'll want compensation for the mess."

The captain stepped inside. Knelt by Ferren. And Noor, moving on instinct older than caution, slipped out into the rain and the dark with the map dry against her skin, and did not look back — except once, at the tree line, where something with wings too large for a crow lifted off a branch and followed her into the storm, silent as a held breath.

Behind her, muffled by rain and stone, she heard the captain's voice say, softly, almost sadly:

"So the solstice stands. Pity."

And the last thing Ferren Vale ever said, so quietly that only the waystation walls and one fleeing woman heard it, was:

"*Nineteen days.*"
