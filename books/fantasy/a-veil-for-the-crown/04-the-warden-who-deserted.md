## 4. The Warden Who Deserted

The rain had been falling for three days when Serevan Ashvale finally caught her trail, and by then it had washed most of the mountain into the valley below.

He crouched at the edge of the shepherd's path above Hollowmere, gloved fingers pressing into mud that gave up its secrets reluctantly. A bootprint — narrow, deep at the toe, the stride of someone climbing hard with a pack weighted wrong. Not a smuggler's load, then. Smugglers balanced their burdens like priests balancing sin: evenly, reverently, so nothing clinked and nothing dragged. This print said a person carrying something they kept shifting from side to side, unable to find a way to hold it that didn't hurt.

A map, he thought. Folded small. Carried against the body.

He had been following the courier's blood for two days before that — spoor dropped along the high road like a dying man's rosary, each dark fleck a prayer he couldn't read. The wardens had lost it in the storm. Serevan had not, because Serevan had learned in eleven years of service that the Crown's hounds ran fastest when they were running away from something, and he had made an art of running slowly.

"Trail's cold here." Captain Veyra's voice came from behind him, flat as slate. She stood with rain sheeting off her grey cloak, three of her people fanned out along the ridge in the practiced geometry of a sweep that had already decided what it would find. "Courier went over the edge at Widow's Bend. Body'll wash down to the millpond by week's end. Nothing left to carry."

Serevan rose slowly. Mud slid from his knee. "Nothing left to carry," he agreed.

Veyra studied him a moment too long. She had eyes the color of old ice and the patience of a woman who had buried four warden-captains before her and intended to die at a desk instead, which made her more dangerous than any of them. "You've been strange since Duskmarket orders came through, Ashvale."

"I'm always strange. It's why you keep me on the far ridges."

Something almost like humor moved behind her face and died there. "Solstice protocols," she said. "Sealed writs. You know what that means."

*It means soul-bargainers,* Serevan did not say. *It means the thing I saw at Karst Hold, the thing I deserted rather than carry another order for.* What he said was: "I know what sealed writs mean, Captain."

"Then find me whatever the courier was carrying before the solstice, or confirm there's nothing to find." She turned, cloak snapping wet. "And Ashvale — if you find it first, you bring it to me. Not to the archive. Not to the reliquary men. To me." Her jaw worked once. "There are things in this commission I'd rather keep out of certain hands."

She whistled her team off the ridge, and the rain swallowed them, and Serevan stood alone on the shepherd's path with a bootprint full of water and a choice he had already made without admitting it.

Because the trail wasn't cold. The trail went down.

* * *

He followed it because following trails was the only prayer he knew how to say anymore.

Three years ago — no. He counted. Two years, seven months, and some days he did not count at all. Two years and seven months ago, Serevan Ashvale had been a model of the Warden Commission: decorated, promoted twice, trusted with the sealed roads and the quiet arrests. He had believed, in the uncomplicated way of good soldiers, that the realm's ugly work was at least honest work — that someone had to hold the line between the light of the Crown and the dark under the mountains, and that holding it meant getting your hands dirty so that other hands stayed clean.

Then came Karst Hold.

They had brought him a woman there. Old, bent, with flour still in the creases of her knuckles, arrested for the crime of having been heard talking to rats. Not commanding them. *Talking* to them — the witnesses swore she'd only been chatting, telling the cellar-rats where she'd hidden the cheese so they'd stop chewing through her walls. Beast-speech, the magistrates called it. First mark of the deep court's corruption. The writs were clear. The writs had always been clear.

What the writs did not say — what Serevan learned standing in that stone room with his hand on his sword hilt and his stomach turning slowly over — was what happened after. The reliquary men had come with their instruments of glass and silver, and they had not questioned the woman, and they had not executed her. They had *harvested* her. They had drawn the beast-speech out of her the way you draw poison from a wound, slow and careful, catching it in vials, and they had told her, in gentle voices, that it wouldn't hurt much longer, while she screamed in a language none of them spoke but all of them understood — because somewhere beneath the words, in the register where beasts and stones and rivers listen, everyone could hear what she was saying.

She had been saying *please*. And then she had been saying names. And then, near the end, she had stopped saying anything at all, and the reliquary men had capped their last vial and thanked the garrison for their cooperation, and one of them had remarked to another, mildly, as if discussing weather, that the material quality was excellent this quarter, much better than the last batch.

Serevan had reported it. Of course he had. He had written the report himself, in his precise warden's hand, and delivered it up the chain, and watched it travel upward and vanish the way a stone vanishes into deep water — no splash, no ripple, nothing. Three weeks later his transfer orders came through. To the far ridges. To Thornfell. Where the soul-bargainers came through, and where the sealed writs waited, and where a man who asked questions could be stationed conveniently close to the things he wasn't supposed to ask about.

He had lasted four months after that. Then, one grey morning, he had taken off his commission ring, left it on the shrine-stone at the pass head with a copper coin beside it — payment for the road, the old custom, the last honest transaction of his career — and walked down into the valley a deserter.

Except he hadn't walked away. That was the joke of it, the bitter private joke he carried like a stone in his boot. He had deserted the *commission*, but not the work. He still tracked the couriers. He still read the sealed traffic moving between the palace and the mountain. He still knew every safehouse, every relay, every name the Crown used for the thing it would not name aloud. He had simply stopped believing that the line he held divided light from dark. Somewhere in the last two years he had come to suspect the line ran straight through the middle of the throne itself.

So when Veyra said *find what the courier was carrying*, Serevan saluted and turned south with the squad, and then, when the rain thickened and the ridge curved, he turned north instead, alone, toward a bootprint going down.

* * *

The trail led him to the waystation at Hollowmere by dusk of the second day, and by then he had pieced together enough to be afraid.

The courier had come from the palace. Serevan knew the man's gait from the prints — long-strided, right-heavier, a soldier's walk trained into him young. Palace couriers didn't run the mountain roads except on sealed business, and sealed business in autumn, this close to solstice, meant one thing in the whole world. It meant the collection.

Everyone in the wardens knew the story, though nobody said it plainly. How Prince Aldric, fifteen years old and heir to a bleeding border, had gone up the Weeping Stair with a delegation of white-faced ministers and come back down having ended the Border War in a single night. How the enemy lines had simply — stopped. How the deep court's nobility had withdrawn beneath Thornfell like a tide going out, and how the peace that followed had been so complete, so effortless, that the ballads wrote themselves. *The Boy Who Bought the Dawn*, they called him. There were festivals. There was a statue in the capital square, the prince cast in bronze with his hand extended in blessing.

What the ballads did not mention was the price. What the ballads could not mention, because the Crown had burned the ledgers and silenced the negotiators and quietly retired every official who had touched the treaty, was that the dawn had been bought with the only coin the deep court truly valued. Not gold. Not territory. A soul — the heir's own, signed away in vow and memory and marrow, held in trust beneath the mountain until the debt came due.

And debts, Serevan knew, always came due at solstice.

He had seen the internal correspondence. He had spent two years making it his business to see it. The court beneath the mountain did not consume what it collected — that was the horror of it, the part that kept him awake. It *held*. It kept souls the way misers kept coins, gleaming and intact and forever out of reach, and the person who sold theirs went on living, went on smiling at festivals, went on being bronze-cast and ballad-sung, hollow as a bell and just as resonant. Serevan had passed through the capital eight months ago and seen the prince across a courtyard, and what had stayed with him was not the famous beauty or the famous melancholy but the *stillness* — the way the man had stood in wind and rain without seeming to feel either, like a portrait of a person rather than a person.

The courier had been carrying the collection terms. Or the route to them. Or — Serevan's gut tightened as he crouched outside the waystation's yellow window, watching lamplight move across frost-furred glass — something else entirely, something worth killing a palace courier over and worth sending royal wardens hunting through a storm.

Inside, shadows moved. Two figures. One big and slow-moving, tending a fire — the waystation keeper, old Marta's boy, probably. The other smaller, quicker, pacing the length of the room in restless turns. A guide, then. Someone who'd taken shelter off the high passes.

Someone whose bootprints matched the ones going down from Hollowmere ridge.

Serevan settled into the shadow of the woodpile, rain dripping off his hood, and did what he had gotten frighteningly good at: he waited, and he doubted.

* * *

Doubt, he had learned, was not the opposite of duty. Doubt was what duty became when you looked at it too long in certain lights.

His father had been a warden before him, and his grandmother before that, back to the founding companies that built the first beacon-towers on Thornfell's shoulders. Duty was the family inheritance, older than any land they'd owned, and Serevan had worn it like a second skeleton. It had held him upright through Karst Hold. It had held him upright through the report that vanished, the transfer, the grey morning at the shrine-stone. Even desertion, he had told himself, was duty — duty to the truth of what he'd seen, which the commission would not carry, so he had to carry it instead.

But tonight, watching the lamplit window with his sword-hand aching from the cold, he found the old skeleton had developed cracks, and through the cracks he could hear something he'd been refusing to hear for two years and seven months.

*You let her go.*

That was the thought. Plain as a bell struck. Because he had already seen her face — earlier, at the ridge crossing, half a mile off through the rain, a slim figure in oiled wool leading a mule down the switchbacks with the sure, economical movement of someone born to these slopes. He could have signaled. Could have blown the warden's whistle that carried six miles in wet air. Instead he had stood behind a boulder and watched her descend, and something in her posture — the constant low readiness of a person who has spent a lifetime listening for boots behind her — had snagged in him like a hook.

She moved like the woman at Karst Hold had moved, in those last minutes before the reliquary men came. Like someone who knew exactly what the Crown did to people like her, and had organized her entire existence around never being found.

And he had let her go. Had stood in the rain and *chosen* it, the way he had chosen the shrine-stone, the way he kept choosing, over and over, some quieter treason that felt less like betrayal than honesty.

The trouble was that letting her go wasn't enough anymore. Because if the courier's burden was what Serevan feared — if it was the collection terms, or worse, a key to the deep court itself — then it wasn't going to stay lost. Veyra's sweep would widen. The reliquary men would come up from the capital with their glass and silver, and they would find whoever held the map, and they would do to that person what they had done to the flour-handed woman, gently, professionally, in the name of the realm.

Unless someone got there first. Unless someone who knew both sides of the line — who had held it, and crossed it, and knew every seam where it frayed — got to the truth before the machines did.

Serevan looked at his reflection in the black ice of the horse trough: a gaunt man in a warden's oilskin with no insignia, thirty-one years old and already carrying more ghosts than a graveyard. His father would have called what he was about to do desertion upon desertion. His grandmother, who had actually fought the deep court's things at the Beacon War's end, might have called it something else. He liked to think she'd have called it the family trade.

The door of the waystation opened. Lamplight spilled out, and with it a voice — low, roughened, female, pitched to carry no farther than the yard: "...road's washed out past the bend, I told you. Nobody's crossing Widow's Gap till the rain quits, and anyone who tries is trying to die."

A pause. The keeper's deeper voice, rumbling.

"No," the woman said, and there was something in the single word — a tightness, a flinch — that made Serevan go absolutely still against the woodpile. "No riders came through. No one came through. You have my word."

Her voice lied beautifully. That was the thing that decided him, more than the bootprints, more than the timing, more than the shape of the burden she shifted from hip to hip inside her coat. She lied the way guilty people lied — and guilty, in Serevan's long experience, almost always meant *hunted*, and hunted meant she had the thing, and having the thing meant the reliquary men were already drawing a circle around her life and tightening it.

He should walk away. He had walked away once; he knew the road.

Instead Serevan Ashvale rose from the woodpile, silent as the snowline, and began working his way around the waystation toward the high track — the one path south that a hunted guide would take at first light, the one he could reach before she did if he pushed through the night.

Not to arrest her. He was done arresting.

To follow. To learn what she carried and who she was and whether the truth she was running with could be used as a shield instead of a weapon — because somewhere between the shrine-stone and tonight, Serevan had stopped believing the Crown deserved to win, without ever quite finding anything else to believe in.

Maybe she was it. Maybe she was just a smuggler with a stolen map. Either way, the rain was easing, and the solstice was nine days off, and doubt had curdled at last into something he could act on.

Behind him, in the waystation yard, a crow called once from the eaves — a harsh, deliberate sound, almost like a word — and Serevan glanced up at the small black shape silhouetted against the dying firelight, and thought nothing of it at all.

He should have. But that, as his grandmother used to say, was tomorrow's mistake.
