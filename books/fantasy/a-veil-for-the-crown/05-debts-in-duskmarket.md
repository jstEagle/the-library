## 5. Debts in Duskmarket

Duskmarket earned its name honestly. It lived in the hour between legal and illegal, in the seam of a valley where the sun gave up early and the lanterns never quite agreed on what color the dark should be. Noor came down out of Thornfell with the map sewn into her coat lining and three days of fear riding between her shoulder blades like a second spine.

The town smelled of wet wool, frying onions, and tallow smoke — the smell of people who had all, at one point or another, decided that the Crown's roads were more suggestion than law. She knew it well. She'd been coming here since she was sixteen, trading safe passage for coin, keeping her face forgettable and her gift buried so deep even she sometimes pretended it wasn't there.

Tonight she couldn't afford to be forgettable. Tonight she needed something from people who only sold to those who asked loudly enough.

She went first to Vessa's ferry-house at the town's drowned edge, where the river ran slow and black under pilings furred with moss. Vessa herself sat behind a counter scarred by knife-marks and candle-wax, a broad woman with silver rings on every finger and eyes that weighed customers like cargo.

"Quintana." Vessa said her name the way other people spat. Not with hatred — with accounting. "Heard you lost a caravan on the high pass last month."

"Heard wrong. I lost *one mule* and gained a reputation for honesty about it." Noor set two coppers on the counter, an old courtesy. "I need passage south. Quiet. Small boat, small crew, no questions."

Vessa turned the coppers over with one finger but didn't pocket them. "South's bad business this season. Wardens in the valley like flies on a wound. Three patrols through Duskmarket since the new moon, asking after a courier." Her eyes came up, flat and patient as deep water. "Asking after a *map*."

Noor kept her face still through long practice. Inside, everything clenched. "I don't carry maps."

"No," Vessa agreed pleasantly. "You carry secrets, which is worse, and which is also why you're still breathing and half my other guides aren't." She leaned forward, rings clicking against the wood. "I'll get you south, Quintana. But not tonight, and not free. The river's watched till the moon turns. You want quiet passage, you wait four days and pay double, and you keep your head down in my town while you do it. There are men in grey walking these streets who make *me* nervous, and I've been robbed by professionals."

Four days. The word *solstice* sat in Noor's chest like a swallowed coal. Four days might be too many. But a dead courier's map was not worth anything if she floated downriver inside a warden's net, so she nodded once and left the second copper where it lay.

* * *

She took a room at the Gilded Eel, which was neither gilded nor, as far as anyone could prove, an eel, and spent the evening doing what she always did when the world grew teeth: she listened to the animals.

Duskmarket was rich with them. Goats tied outside the butcher's, muttering their dim grievances. Dogs along the lanes narrating territory to one another in short, blunt sentences. Rats in the Eel's walls holding a running commentary on the kitchen that would have turned Noor's stomach if she'd let herself translate all of it. And above the rooftops — crows. Always crows in Duskmarket, fat and insolent, arguing over chimney territory and dropped fish heads.

She did not speak back. That was the code, the iron rule her mother had died for breaking: *listen if you must, but never answer.* A voice answered in kind is a voice that can be identified. A woman who talks to beasts is a woman on a pyre; everyone in Thornfell remembered that, even the ones who claimed they didn't.

But listening cost nothing. Listening was how she learned, within an hour of taking her room, that the dogs feared the men in grey because the men in grey smelled of iron and old blood. That the butcher's boy had seen riders questioning ferrymen at dawn. That a crow perched on the Eel's rotten signboard kept saying, to itself, in its dry scrap-voice, a word that made Noor go cold where she sat.

"*Solstice.*"

Crows repeated things. That was all it was. Crows were magpies of sound; some traveler had said the word on the street below and the bird had picked it up like a shiny button. It meant nothing.

It meant nothing, and still Noor stood at the shuttered window for a long time, watching the crow watch the street, feeling the map press against her ribs like a second heart.

* * *

She heard the music before she understood what she was hearing.

It drifted up from the taproom — a lute, played beautifully, playing the same eight bars over and over. Not badly. Beautifully, and then stopping, and starting again, each repetition a little tighter than the last, like a man trying to thread a needle with shaking hands.

Noor went down mostly because staying upstairs felt like hiding, and hiding felt like the beginning of being hunted.

The taproom was half-full of smugglers and river-rats pretending not to look at the man on the corner bench. He was youngish, maybe twenty-five, with the kind of handsome that had gone slightly feral — dark curls in need of cutting, a jaw shadowed past fashionable, a fine coat gone shabby at the cuffs. His lute was better than he was. Someone had loved that instrument once, someone with money.

And around him, unhurried as tide, sat three men with the specific patience of creditors.

"—and again from the top, minstrel," said the largest of the three, a slab of a man with a voice like gravel poured slow. "Since you keep *almost* getting it right."

"It's a difficult bridge, Orlan, it's structurally ambitious—"

"You owe us forty crowns, Tamwell Bright. Forty crowns, and your fingers are the collateral. Play the song right and we knock ten off. Play it wrong—" Orlan smiled with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse, "—we start with the pinky. Left hand. You won't miss it for lute work."

Tam — Tamwell — looked down at his hands. They were, Noor noticed, remarkable hands, quick and expressive, and currently trembling in a way he was trying to disguise as tuning.

"The thing is," Tam said, with the bright, doomed cheer of a man stepping off a cliff, "the song is *about* the debt. It's a ballad. 'The Ballad of Tamwell Bright, Who Owes Forty Crowns.' If I play it correctly, I'm confessing. If I play it incorrectly, I'm lying musically, and my art cannot abide—"

Orlan's hand closed around the lute's neck.

Noor hadn't decided to move. Her body simply arrived, the way it did on a collapsing scree slope — decision made somewhere below thought. She set her palm flat on the table beside the lute, casual as ordering ale, and looked at Orlan until he looked at her.

"Forty crowns," she said.

"Mind your supper, girl."

"I'm buying the debt. Forty crowns, and the minstrel works it off guiding for me. I need a fourth pair of hands going south, and his are attached to a mouth that talks, which is useful when you're trying not to be noticed. Nobody notices a chatty musician." She put a purse on the table. It held eleven crowns and she knew exactly how much next month's food was going to hurt. "Eleven now. Twenty-nine when the job's done. Your paper says he owes *you*, doesn't it? Then it transfers."

Orlan looked at the purse. At Tam. At her. Something moved behind his eyes — the flicker of a man recalculating, because in Duskmarket a stranger who bought debts was either a fool or a fence, and fools were rare.

"Twenty-nine," he said, "plus interest. Thirty-four."

"Thirty-one, and I walk out of here knowing your name and your crew, which means I know where to send people who hold grudges louder than mine."

A pause. Then Orlan laughed, short and real, and swept up the purse. "Done. Bright, you're her problem now. Congratulations on your freedom." He stood, and paused beside Noor, and lowered his voice to something almost kind. "Whatever you're running from, it caught up with a courier in the north pass last week. Just so we're honest with each other."

They left together, the three of them, unhurried.

Tam stared at her across the ruined silence of the taproom. "I have," he said finally, "so many questions, and I'm aware that gratitude requires me to ask none of them."

"Good. Save them for the road." Noor picked up the lute, checked it for damage with a professional's quickness, and handed it back. "Can you fight?"

"No. Can you?"

"Yes. Don't make me prove it. We leave in four days."

"That's — hm. That's a suspiciously specific number of days." But he was slinging the lute across his back, following her toward the stairs, because a man who has just been sold like a mule apparently develops attachments quickly. "Where are we going, exactly? Since I'm contractually obligated to walk there."

"South. Through the mountain."

Something crossed his face — not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. "Through," he repeated. "Not around."

"Through."

He was quiet for three whole steps. Then, softly, almost to himself, in the tone of a man composing: "*The road goes under, the road goes under, and none who sing come back the same—*"

"Don't," Noor said, "write that one yet."

* * *

The crow found them the next morning.

Noor woke to a tapping at the shutters — precise, rhythmic, deliberate. Not wind. Wind didn't count in fours.

She opened the shutters onto grey light and the crow from the signboard, perched on the sill with the confidence of a landlord. It was a big female, feathers blue-black with an oil-sheen of purple, one wing carrying a scar that had healed crooked. Its eyes were the unsettling amber-black of all crows' eyes, except that this one's eyes were looking at Noor the way no crow had ever looked at her: steadily, knowingly, like a person waiting for another person to admit something.

"Shoo," Noor whispered.

The crow did not shoo. It hopped closer, tilted its head, and said, in its dry gravel voice:

"*Solstice.*"

Ice walked down Noor's spine. "Who taught you that word?"

The crow regarded her. Somewhere below, Tam was singing tunelessly in the yard, and the town was waking, and the world was ordinary. Only this bird was not ordinary. Only this bird leaned forward and said, very clearly, with the careful diction of someone using a language learned late:

"*Run.*"

Noor's hand went to the windowsill and gripped it until the wood creaked. Every instinct she owned was screaming the old code — *never answer, never answer* — but the code assumed the voice belonged to a beast. This voice belonged to something wearing a beast like a coat.

"Who are you?" she breathed.

The crow ruffled its feathers, and for a moment Noor could have sworn it looked embarrassed. It glanced away, out at the street, then back. When it spoke again, the word came out cracked and reluctant, dragged up from wherever it kept its human sounds:

"*Kess.*"

Then it launched off the sill and was gone into the grey morning, leaving Noor standing in the cold air of the open window with her heart hammering and every certainty she had built her life on developing hairline fractures.

A talking crow. A dead courier. A map in her mother's handwriting leading beneath the mountain. *The solstice.*

She dressed, went down, and found Tam in the common room attempting to charm breakfast out of the cook with two chords and a smile that wasn't working.

"We have a third companion," Noor said.

"Already? I haven't even finished composing our theme song. Is it a fighter? Please say it's a fighter."

"It's a crow."

Tam blinked. "A... crow."

"A crow named Kess, who speaks, and who told me to run." She sat down across from him and lowered her voice until it was barely sound. "And here's what I know that you don't, minstrel, since you're mine now whether either of us likes it. There's a court under Thornfell. There's a prince who sold something to it. There's a map showing the way down, drawn by my mother before she burned. And there are men in grey hunting all of it, and they killed a courier to get to me, and in four days I am walking straight toward them instead of away, because the alternative is letting something happen at the solstice that I don't fully understand yet."

The taproom noise seemed to recede around them. Tam had stopped smiling. For the first time, Noor saw what lived underneath the performance — something steadier, and sadder, and older than his years.

"My grandmother used to tell stories about the deep court," he said quietly. "I thought they were stories."

"So did my mother's neighbors. Right up until they lit the fire."

Tam turned his cup slowly on the table. "Why take me with you? You could've just paid Orlan and walked away clean. One less mouth. One less risk."

Because, Noor thought, watching him — because the courier died alone pressing a map into a stranger's hands, and I was the stranger, and I am so tired of being the only one who knows. Because a man who composes ballads about his own debts is a man who tells the truth sideways, and sideways truth might be the only kind that survives what's coming. Because the crow said *run* and instead I stayed, and I don't know why, and having witnesses to my own stupidity seems suddenly important.

"Because you play well," she said aloud. "And because people underestimate musicians, and I need underestimated people."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Don't make me regret it."

Outside the window, on the gutter opposite, the crow landed. It fixed them both with its amber stare through the glass, and tapped the leaded pane once, twice, three times — impatient, insistent, like a friend knocking at a door that had been closed too long.

Tam looked at it. Looked at her. "Your crow is staring at us."

"She's not my crow."

"Then whose is she?"

Noor didn't answer, because she didn't know — and because at that moment the door of the Gilded Eel swung open and let in the cold, and a man stepped through it who made every animal-sense Noor possessed stand up and scream.

He wore travelling leathers gone soft with use, and a sword worn like a habit rather than a threat, and he had the build and bearing of a warden — she knew it the way she knew weather. But his cloak bore no badge, and his eyes, sweeping the room with a hunter's methodical calm, were the eyes of a man looking for something he dreaded finding.

His gaze passed over Tam. Passed over her.

Stopped.

For one heartbeat, two, the stranger and the guide looked at each other across the smoky room, and Noor saw him recognize her — not her name, not her face, but something else. Something in the way she sat, or the way the crow outside had gone utterly silent, or perhaps something he'd been tracking for days across the high passes.

Then he turned, deliberately, and ordered breakfast from the far end of the room, as if he hadn't seen her at all.

Noor's hand found her knife under the table.

"Tam," she murmured, without moving her lips. "How fast can you pack?"
