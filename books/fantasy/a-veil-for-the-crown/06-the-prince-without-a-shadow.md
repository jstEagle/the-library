## 6. The Prince Without a Shadow

The rain had followed Serevan Ashvale out of the capital for three days, as though the sky itself had been assigned to his detail and could not be dismissed.

He crouched at the edge of a shepherd's track above the Thornfell passes, watching a thin thread of woodsmoke rise from a waystation below, and tried to remember when he had last slept under a roof he trusted. Not since Vaelmoor. Not since the night he had learned what the Crown kept beneath its seal of state.

The tracker's report lay in his mind like a splinter: a woman, alone, guiding contraband through the high passes. Ordinary. The kind of quarry wardens ran down a dozen times a season. But the courier they'd pulled from the river at Greyford had died clutching nothing, and the map he'd carried had vanished, and Captain Ilvane did not believe in vanished things. *Find the woman,* she had said. *Find what he gave her.* And so here he was, three days into the fells, chasing smoke.

Below him, the waystation door opened, and a figure stepped out into the rain to check the sky — a woman, dark-haired, moving with the particular economy of someone who had learned that wasted motion on a mountain was wasted life. She scanned the ridgelines the way a warden would. Then she looked up, directly toward the rocks where Serevan crouched, and held there for one long breath, as if she could see him through the rain.

She went back inside.

Serevan did not move for an hour after that. When he finally descended, the waystation stood empty, its hearth still warm, a bowl of half-eaten porridge abandoned on the table as though she had simply evaporated between one heartbeat and the next. On the table's scarred surface, pressed flat by the bowl, was a single black feather.

He had seen feathers left as messages before. He had never seen one left as a warning.

* * *

Three years ago, almost to the day, Serevan had stood in the antechamber of the Sunhold palace, armor polished, oath fresh on his tongue, waiting to be presented to the prince who had ended the war.

Everyone remembered where they had been when the Peace of Aldric was signed. The Border War had ground on for eleven years — eleven years of levies and burned granaries and boys coming home in boxes too small for their ambitions — and then, in a single winter, it had simply stopped. The eastern lords had laid down their arms. The treaties had been drafted in a fortnight. The realm called it a miracle, and miracles, the Crown understood, required management.

Serevan had expected a hero. What stepped into the antechamber was a young man of perhaps twenty, tall and correct, dressed in white and silver, with a face that might have been handsome if anything behind it had bothered to arrive. His eyes were the problem. Serevan had spent his career reading eyes — guilty eyes, lying eyes, brave ones — and Prince Aldric's eyes were none of these. They were vacant in a way that was not emptiness but absence, like windows in a house from which the furniture has been sold off room by room.

"Your Highness." Serevan knelt.

"You're the one who found the traitor in the Third Company," the prince said. His voice was pleasant, modulated, entirely without texture. "They say you have a gift for seeing what people hide."

"I try to be thorough, Your Highness."

"Yes." The prince looked at him then, and for a moment something moved behind the vacancy — not light, exactly. More like the memory of light, the way a wall holds the sun after sundown. "Thoroughness is a dangerous virtue in this palace, Warden Ashvale. I hope you never have cause to regret it."

He had thought, then, that it was a joke. Princes made jokes. It took him two more years, and one sealed order he should never have read, to understand it had been a plea.

* * *

Now he crouched in the lee of a granite shoulder while the rain turned to sleet, and let himself think about the sealed order, because the map in the dead courier's hands had been headed somewhere, and every road in this part of Thornfell eventually led to the same place.

The order had come through the warden-captaincy in his third year, marked with the privy seal, addressed to the commander of the Vale garrison. Serevan had been carrying dispatches; Commander Brase had been drunk; and the seal had been broken before Serevan realized what he was holding.

*Regarding the solstice obligation. The collection proceeds as contracted. No warden is to approach the Weeping Stair or the approaches thereto. Any soldier who witnesses the rite is to be remanded to the Crown's physicians for treatment. This order supersedes all others.*

Collection. Contracted. He had asked Brase about it, once, carefully, over wine. The old commander had gone grey around the mouth and said, "Some doors you don't knock on, boy. Some doors, you stand guard facing away from them, and you thank the Crown for the privilege."

Two weeks later, Serevan had requested transfer to the archive division, and in the archive division, because thoroughness was indeed a dangerous virtue, he had found the rest.

There was no single document. That was the genius of it. There was a grain requisition for a village that no longer appeared on any census. A payment ledger entry — *to the mountain court, per the old covenant* — repeated annually for fifteen years. A physician's report, redacted to ribbons, concerning a member of the royal household whose condition was described only as *progressive diminution*. And a letter, unsigned, in a clerk's careful hand, summarizing what the treaty delegations had been told: that the eastern lords had received terms too generous to refuse because something had been given in exchange that made refusal impossible. Something that had been *promised to powers older than the crown*, in return for the war's end.

And beside that letter, in a different hand — younger, hurried, the ink blotted as though written by someone whose fingers no longer fully obeyed him — a single line that Serevan had copied out and burned and still knew by heart:

*I was fifteen. I didn't know what else to do. Please tell no one it was me.*

* * *

The sleet thickened. Serevan pulled his cloak tighter and made himself review the facts as if they belonged to someone else's case, because the alternative was to feel them.

Prince Aldric had been fifteen years old when the Border War entered its worst winter. Fifteen, when the eastern lords broke through at Cael Marsh and the capital panicked and the king — Aldric's father — lay dying of the wasting sickness that would kill him before spring. Fifteen, when someone in the palace — a tutor, a priest, a frightened regent — had remembered the old stories. The court beneath the mountain. The ones who traded in vow and memory, who had been paid off and sealed away generations ago, whose bargains always held and never healed.

A child had gone down into the dark. Or something had gone down in his name. And the war had ended by midwinter, and the realm had prospered ever since, and every year at the solstice the Crown paid its quiet installments, and every year the prince grew a little more transparent, a little more absent, until now he walked through his own palace like a guest who had forgotten which rooms were his.

Serevan had seen him twice more since that first presentation. Once at a review, where the prince had saluted the troops with perfect form and been heard to ask an aide afterward how many men had attended. Once at a funeral, where he had stood beside the widow of a man he had never met and wept — genuinely wept, Serevan was certain of it — though when questioned later he could not say whom he had been mourning. Grief without an owner. A soul paying out its contents in drips, like a barrel with a stave sprung.

The realm prospers. That was the sentence the chancellors used, in council, whenever anyone asked too loudly why the prince did not marry, did not speak unscripted, did not appear at his own birthday feast. *The realm prospers.* As if prosperity were a coin that could only be minted from one metal. As if a kingdom could be built on a child's ransom and remain clean because the ransom was paid in something invisible.

Horror, dressed in statecraft, walking around in broad daylight with a herald's trumpet before it.

* * *

He found her trail again at dusk, two days later, and it was wrong in ways that made the hair on his arms rise.

Not wrong as in hidden — she was a skilled traveler, but he was a warden of twelve years' experience, and she left sign like anyone: bent grass, a fire ring swept but not perfectly, the print of a boot with a patched sole. Wrong as in accompanied. At the edge of a stream crossing, pressed into the mud beside her prints, were the marks of a crow that had walked — *walked*, for twenty paces, wing-prints folded neat at its sides like a person strolling with hands clasped — before taking flight again.

Crows did not walk beside smugglers. Crows did not leave deliberate sign. And yet the prints were unmistakably deliberate, placed where a tracker could not fail to see them, as though the bird wanted him to know it knew he was following.

He should have reported it. He had a signal whistle in his pocket and Captain Ilvane's outriders within a day's ride, and every rule he had sworn to told him to blow it.

Instead he sat on a wet stone in the gathering dark and thought about the courier at Greyford. The man had been royal service, fast-courier class, carrying something the captain wanted badly enough to spend a full company on. He had drowned in four feet of water — drowned, with no wound on him, in water he could have stood up in. The river surgeon had called it heart failure. Serevan had seen the body. The man's face had been fixed in an expression he had only seen once before, on the corpse of a soldier pulled from the Weeping Stair's upper reaches thirty years ago, preserved in the archive photographs: terror of a very specific kind, the terror of someone who had heard something speak to him in a voice that wore his own mother's tones.

The deep court did not like its mail intercepted. The deep court did not like its roads watched. Which meant the courier had been carrying word *of* the collection — the solstice obligation, due in less than a month now — and someone below the mountain had decided the Crown's correspondence traveled better unsupervised.

And the map had fallen into the hands of a smuggler woman who spoke to things in the dark, and the Crown wanted it back, and the mountain wanted it lost, and somewhere between those two hungers, a warden was supposed to do his duty.

Serevan took out the signal whistle. Turned it over in his fingers. It was brass, standard issue, stamped with the crowned eye of the Wardens Royal. He had carried it for twelve years. It had never once felt heavy until tonight.

He thought of the prince at the funeral, weeping for a stranger. He thought of the line in the blotted hand: *I was fifteen.* He thought of the annual ledger entries, fifteen years of them, each one a small quiet theft of a human being conducted with the full machinery of state, and every minister who signed off on them going home to warm houses and children they had not sold.

He put the whistle back in his pocket.

Then, because desertion and treason apparently came naturally to him now, he stood up, checked the wind, and started down the trail after her — not to arrest her. To warn her. Because whatever she carried, both the Crown and the thing beneath the mountain wanted it, and no lone smuggler survived being wanted by two such masters.

The crow found him an hour into the descent. It landed on a branch ahead of him at eye level, feathers slicked dark with sleet, and regarded him with an attention no bird should possess.

"I'm not your enemy," Serevan told it, feeling immediately foolish, and then less foolish, because the crow tilted its head with what could only be called consideration.

It hopped once along the branch. Then it flew — not away, but onward down the trail, alighting again fifty paces ahead, waiting, in the manner of a guide.

"Or you're leading me into an ambush," he added. "That's also possible."

The crow made a sound that was almost, almost, a laugh.

* * *

Near midnight, sheltering in a hollow beneath an overhang, Serevan allowed himself the memory he usually kept locked in the deepest drawer, because tomorrow he would need all his reasons and this was the truest one.

The archive, seven years ago. A restricted folio he had no clearance for, opened anyway, because thoroughness. Inside: the original terms of the Peace of Aldric, or a fragment of them, in a cipher the archivists had never fully broken. But one passage had been rendered into plain script by some earlier scholar, and it was this passage Serevan recited now to the dark, like a prayer said backwards:

*The debtor offers that which he is, entire, unto the court beneath, in pledge perpetual, against the peace of the realms. The court shall hold and keep the same until such time as the debt is discharged or claimed in whole.*

*Claimed in whole.* The solstice. Less than a month away. And the collection site — he had worked it out over years of cross-referencing, and the geography confirmed itself now with every mile he descended — lay beneath Thornfell, past the Weeping Stair, in the sealed dark where the old covenant was honored.

The same direction the smuggler woman was traveling. The same direction her dead courier's map pointed, if the captain's intelligence held.

She wasn't running from the Crown. She was running *toward* the mountain, straight at the one place in the world the Crown forbade its own soldiers to look at, carrying a map drawn — the courier's last words, garbled, recorded by a ferryman — *in a dead woman's hand*.

Serevan stared into the fire he dared not make and felt the shape of it close around him at last, terrible and complete: the hollow prince, the annual payments, the solstice approaching, and a smuggler with a dead woman's map walking willingly into the dark to meet whatever had been eating the heir of the realm for fifteen years.

He did not know what she intended. He did not know if she could be stopped, or helped, or saved.

He only knew that in the morning he would follow her down, and that somewhere below the Weeping Stair, a fifteen-year-old boy grown into a hollow man was waiting to learn whether his debt had come due — and that Serevan Ashvale, for the first time in twelve years of service, intended to be standing on the wrong side of his oath when it happened.

Above him, on the rim of the hollow, the crow settled its wings and kept watch, and if birds could smile, Serevan would have sworn it did.
