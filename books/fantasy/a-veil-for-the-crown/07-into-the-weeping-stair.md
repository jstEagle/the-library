## 7. Into the Weeping Stair

The entrance to the Weeping Stair did not look like a door. It looked like a wound.

Noor stood at its lip in the grey hour before dawn, breath fogging, and felt the mountain breathe back at her — a slow, wet exhalation from the dark below, carrying the smell of stone and old water and something sweeter underneath, like flowers rotting in a cellar. The stair itself was a seam in the cliff face above the drowned village of Hollowmere, a descending spiral of steps cut into living rock by hands that had not cared whether the cuts were kind.

"You're certain this is the way," Serevan said behind her. Not quite a question. He had the tone of a man who wanted it to be one and knew better.

"The map is certain." She did not turn around. Her mother's handwriting was certain; her mother's handwriting had been the last thing Noor had trusted without reservation, and she was not ready to examine what that meant now that the woman who wrote it had been ash for fifteen years. "The Stair runs under Thornfell's spine. Three days down, if we don't stop. It comes out above the sealed gates."

"And if the wardens find the entrance?"

"Then they'll have to climb down after us." She allowed herself a thin smile. "And I know how to make stairs disagreeable."

Kess shifted on her shoulder, feathers ruffling against Noor's neck, and made a sound low in her throat that Noor had learned to read as *I don't like this place*. The crow had not liked anything since they'd turned off the high passes toward Hollowmere. Crows remembered things, Tam said. Old things. Things buried.

"Tam," Noor called. "You can still turn back."

Behind her, the minstrel stood at the edge of the light with his lute strapped across his back like a shield he didn't believe in. His face was pale in the pre-dawn — pale, and set. He'd been gambling with his life since Duskmarket; the difference was that until now the stakes had been someone else's coin.

"I owe money to people who sell information to royal wardens," Tam said. "Down there, at least, I only owe money to you." He swallowed. "Also I've written three ballads about doomed descents into the underworld and I'd very much like to see if I got any of the details right."

"That's the worst reason to come along I've ever heard."

"It's the only honest one you'll get out of me today."

Noor laughed despite herself — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered. She hadn't laughed in company since she couldn't remember when, and the fact of it sat strangely in her chest, warm and unwelcome, like a coal carried too long in bare hands.

She stepped onto the first stair, and the Weeping Stair began to sing.

* * *

It started as a whisper in the walls, or so it seemed at first — the ordinary complaint of water through stone. But within a hundred steps the whisper found its shape, and the shape was a name.

*Maren.* A woman's voice, thin as thread, rising from the wet rock on their left. *Maren, Maren, come home, the bread is burning.*

Another voice answered from below, deeper, male: *Odran. Odran of the Nine Fingers. Odran, your daughter waits.*

And another, and another, dozens of them, hundreds, the water threading through the mountain carrying names the way a river carries silt — the dead of Thornfell, every soul the mountain had swallowed in its long slow hunger, miners and smugglers and lost children and soldiers, all of them still being called home by voices that loved them once.

Tam stopped walking. His hand had gone white on his lute strap. "What is that? What *is* that?"

"The Stair weeps," Noor said quietly. "That's why it's named so. The water here passes through the deep places where the dead are kept, and it carries their names up. Some say it's mourning. Some say it's an invitation."

"An invitation to what?"

"To stay."

Serevan had gone very still. In the dim glow of Noor's shuttered lantern his face looked carved, older than his years. "I've heard reports of this place," he said. "Warden records. They call it a hazard. Men go down sane and come up answering to other men's names."

"Then don't answer," Noor said. "Keep your own name in your mouth. Say it often. That's the old rule, my mother taught me—" She caught herself. Too much. Always too much, with these two. She could feel them looking at her, filing away each small confession, and she hated how little she seemed able to stop making them.

"Your mother taught you a lot about the deep places," Serevan said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — curious.

"My mother taught me a lot of things." Noor shouldered past him and took the next stair down. "Walk. Don't drink the water. And whatever you hear, however much it sounds like someone you've lost—" she glanced at him, and saw the flinch she'd expected, the name of some dead person moving behind his eyes like a fish under ice, "—it isn't them. It's only the mountain, wearing their voices. Remember that."

They walked. The names sang around them.

By the second hour Noor had counted forty-seven distinct voices, and had begun, helplessly, to answer them.

Not aloud. Never aloud. But inside her chest, where the gift lived — the thing she had spent her whole life strangling down to a whisper, the thing that had burned her mother on a pyre in the market square at Vael's Crossing while the crowd sang hymns — the gift woke and stretched and *listened*. Because the names in the water were not only human names. Beneath the human voices, down in the registers no human ear could follow, the water carried other calls. Older ones. The goat-spirits of the high scree. The blind pale worms that chewed the roots of the mountain. The thing that lived in the flooded gallery two levels below and had been asleep for six hundred years and dreamed, even sleeping, of being spoken to.

No one had spoken to it in six hundred years.

*Soon,* the water promised it, in Noor's own voice, because her mind had slipped without permission into the speech-that-was-not-speech, the grammar of pressure and warmth and attention that beasts understood. *Soon, soon.*

"Noor." Kess's beak pressed hard against her ear. Sharp. Deliberate. The crow had never used her name before — crows didn't, not properly — and the shock of it cut through the drowning sweetness of the listening like cold water.

Noor stumbled, caught herself on the wet wall, and realized she had been walking with her eyes half-closed, leaning toward the sound, toward the *depths*, for gods knew how many steps. Her lantern had drifted in her grip until its shutter gaped fully open, spilling light everywhere.

"Thank you," she breathed to the crow.

Kess ruffled, unimpressed, and said nothing more. But she stayed pressed close to Noor's neck the rest of that stretch, a small warm weight, an anchor of feather and heartbeat, and Noor held onto it the way a drowning sailor holds a spar.

This was the danger her mother had died for knowing. Not the gift itself — the gift was only a door. But doors underground opened both ways, and the things beneath Thornfell had been waiting a long time for anyone to leave theirs unlatched.

* * *

They camped on the ninth landing, a shelf of rock wide enough for four bodies and a fire the size of two cupped hands. The Stair sang them to sleep in shifts. Noor took first watch, then Serevan, then Tam, who fell asleep on his own watch and was forgiven for it because none of them were sleeping well anyway.

It was during Noor's second watch, in the dead hours, that the water changed.

The names stopped.

All of them, at once, mid-syllable — *Maren, come ho—* — and the silence that followed was worse than any song, a silence with weight and intent, the silence of a room full of people turning to look at you.

Noor was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, hand on Kess's back, feeling the crow's feathers rise.

"What is it?" Serevan whispered. He was awake instantly — warden-trained, she reminded herself, whatever else he was — knife already in his hand.

"They've stopped singing."

"Why would they—"

"Because something told them to." Noor closed her eyes and let herself listen downward, carefully, carefully, holding the door of herself open only a crack. Far below — far, but coming closer — she heard footsteps. Many. Boots on stone, climbing. And threaded through the boot-rhythms, the smell-signatures, the heat-prints that her gift read as easily as print on a page: iron. Dogs. And oil.

"Wardens," she said. "Outriders. Six of them, maybe eight. They're on the Stair below us, maybe two hours' climb. They brought dogs."

"Dogs can't track us through water-song."

"Dogs aren't tracking scent." She opened her eyes. "They're tracking *me*. Someone told them what I am, Serevan. Someone who knows what the gift sounds like when it's listening." The courier's blood on her hands rose up in memory, hot and accusing. *The solstice.* Someone had known what he carried. Someone had known who might carry it next.

Serevan's face went through several expressions in quick succession — she watched them like weather — and settled on grim acceptance. "Then we run. There's a branch tunnel ahead, the map showed—"

"A branch tunnel that floods at snowmelt. I saw it."

"Then we fight."

"With what? You have a knife. I have a lantern and a crow. Tam has—" She glanced at the sleeping minstrel, curled around his lute like a child around a doll. "Tam has debts."

As if summoned by the insult, Tam stirred. "I heard that," he mumbled, sitting up, hair wild. "Heard all of it. Wardens below, dogs, no route, we're going to die in a hole full of singing corpses. Lovely. Truly, my ballads undersold it."

"Go back to sleep."

"Not likely." He was already on his feet, fumbling for his lute, and Noor stared at him because surely — surely — he wasn't going to do what she thought he was going to do. "Six wardens, you said? Maybe eight?"

"Tam—"

"They're climbing in the dark, in a tunnel where the dead sing, and they're frightened. Frightened men hear things." He began tuning, fingers finding the strings by touch alone. "My mother always said my music was wasted on tavern crowds. Let's find out what it does to a warden's nerve."

"You'll bring them straight down on us—"

"No." Kess spoke.

Everyone froze. The crow had never spoken a full sentence in human words before — a word here, a stolen phrase there, enough to unsettle, never enough to explain. But now she lifted her head from Noor's shoulder and said, in a voice like dry leaves and distant bells, "No. I will bring them. You will make them afraid to follow the sound."

"Kess," Noor said slowly, "what are you?"

The crow looked at her with one black eye, and in that eye was something old and patient and terribly sad. "Later," Kess said. "When we are deeper. When it is safe. Now — the minstrel plays, the crow flies, and the warden's dog-handler hears his dead wife calling from a side passage. Yes?"

Tam's mouth worked. "You can — you actually talk."

"*Yes,*" said Kess, with unmistakable exasperation, and launched herself off Noor's shoulder into the dark.

* * *

What followed, Noor would remember all her life in fragments, the way one remembers a storm.

Tam played. Softly at first — a lullaby, of all things, something with a wandering minor melody that slid along the stone like water itself — and Noor understood suddenly what the minstrel was: not a fool, not a debtor, but a man who had spent his whole life learning exactly how sound moved through a room, and could aim it. He played grief. He played the specific grief of a woman's voice calling a name, and he bent it down the side passage Kess had chosen, and somewhere below, a dog-handler heard his dead wife and broke formation.

Then Kess screamed.

Crows can mimic anything. Noor had known that. But she had never heard a crow produce, in perfect fidelity, the shriek of a cave-in — the groan of stressed stone, the crack, the roar — echoing from the darkness ahead of the climbing wardens, followed by the sound of falling rock that did not exist. Boots scrambled. Someone shouted orders. Someone else shouted prayers.

And then, because fear is contagious and the dark is generous, the wardens' own dogs began to answer the water-song. Noor felt it happen through the stone — felt the animals' minds, simple and honest, seize on the names in the water and *believe*, and begin to howl at voices only they could hear, dragging their handlers, tangling their leads, turning discipline into chaos in the space of thirty heartbeats.

"Move," Serevan breathed. "Now, while they're blind."

They ran. Down the spiral, past the ninth landing, past the tenth, the eleventh, the water-song resuming around them in a flood of relieved voices as whatever had silenced it released its hold — and Noor did not let herself wonder what that thing had been, or why it had helped, or what it might want in return. Later. All of it later.

They ran until Tam's lungs gave out, and then Serevan simply picked the minstrel up — lute and all, slung over one shoulder like a sack of grain — and kept running, and Tam, upside-down and wheezing, said, "This is the single most romantic thing anyone has ever done for me and I resent it completely," and Noor, running, laughing again, that same startled coal-warm laugh, thought: *oh no.*

Oh no. This was how it happened. This was how it always happened, in the stories her mother had told, in the lives of everyone who had ever been burned or betrayed or broken open — you ran from the fire and you tripped over love in the dark and you didn't even notice until it had already gotten into your blood.

She ran faster.

* * *

They stopped at last in a chamber where the stair widened into a vaulted gallery, its ceiling lost in blackness, its floor crossed by a stream that sang in a hundred gentle voices. Here Noor finally let them halt, and they collapsed against the walls, four creatures breathing hard in the lantern-light.

Tam slid down the wall to a seat, lute clutched to his chest. "I want it noted," he gasped, "that I fought bravely."

"You played a lullaby and hid behind a rock."

"I *artistically directed* a military engagement. There's a difference. Ask any general." He looked up at her, and beneath the flippancy his eyes were shaking. "I've never — I've never done anything like that. Anything that mattered. My whole life I've been running from the people I owe, and tonight I pointed myself *at* the danger, and I keep waiting to feel like a different person and I just feel like me, but scared, and — did it matter? Did any of it matter?"

Noor crouched in front of him. She was not good at this. She had never been good at this; comfort was a language she'd never been permitted to learn. But she reached out and put her hand flat on his chest, over the lute, over his hammering heart.

"It mattered," she said. "They're two hours behind us instead of twenty minutes. That's your doing. Yours and hers." She nodded upward, to where Kess had appeared silently on a ledge overhead, preening, insufferably modest.

"Then I'm keeping the fear," Tam said. "The fear means it mattered. I'm going to write a song about a talking crow and a cowardly minstrel and a woman who talks to goats, and it's going to be terrible, and it's going to be true, and someday when we're all dead someone will sing it wrong in a tavern and I won't care, because—"

"Because it will have mattered," Serevan finished quietly.

They looked at each other across the little circle of light — the deserter, the debtor, the witch, and the bird — and something passed between them that none of them named, because naming it would have made it fragile, and it was not fragile. It was the hardest thing any of them had ever built and they had built it in a single night, out of terror and lullabies and a screaming crow, and it would hold.

Noor felt it settle into her like a second heartbeat, and was afraid of it, and did not push it away.

"We rest one hour," she said, standing, because someone had to say practical things or the moment would swallow them all. "Then down. Two days to the gates."

"One question," Serevan said. He had not relaxed. His knife was still out, resting across his knee, and he was looking at the stream. At the water. "The singing stopped earlier. Before the wardens came. Something down there silenced the dead, and then let them start again the moment we needed cover. That's not accident. That's *intent*."

Noor looked at the water, and the water looked back, in the way it had begun to look at her ever since she'd first slipped into beast-speech on this stair. Patient. Expectant. Full of names.

"I know," she said. "Something down here knows I'm coming. And I think—" She hesitated, hearing her mother's handwriting rustle in her memory, the map's final annotation, the one line she had not yet shown the others, written small and fierce in the margin beside the sealed gates:

*It remembers my voice. God forgive me, it will remember yours.*

"I think it's been waiting for me," Noor said, "my whole life."
