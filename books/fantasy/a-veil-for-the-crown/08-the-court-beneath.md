## 8. The Court Beneath

The map stopped making sense at the edge of the lamplight.

Noor had studied it every night since the waystation, tracing her mother's handwriting through tunnels that should not have existed beneath Thornfell, and every night the ink seemed to have shifted a little — as though the mountain itself were redrawing its veins while she slept. But here, in the cold throat of the world where the Weeping Stair finally leveled out, the map and the mountain agreed at last. A corridor of black glass. A door marked in her mother's careful hand with a single word: *Listen.*

So she listened.

At first there was only the drip of water, and the soft clink of Tam's borrowed mail, and Serevan's breathing — controlled, warden-trained, too quiet to be honest. Then, beneath all of it, something else. A murmur. A rustle like a thousand pages turning in a room she could not see. It was not beast-speech. It was older than that, older than the shapes her gift took, and it pressed against the inside of her skull the way a moth presses against a window.

"They're singing," Kess said from her perch on Tam's shoulder. The crow's voice came out low and strange, stripped of her usual sardonic crackle. "Not singing. Counting."

"Counting what?" Tam whispered.

"Days," Kess said. "Yours. Mine. His." She tipped her head toward Serevan. "The court keeps excellent ledgers."

Noor's hand found the map in her coat, the blood-marked vellum warm against her palm as if it remembered whose blood had written it. "Then let's go ruin their arithmetic."

The corridor opened without warning. One moment black glass, the next a vista that stole the air from her lungs and left it hanging somewhere far above.

They stood on a ledge at the rim of a cavern so vast the far wall was a rumor. Stalactites the length of cathedral spires hung down into a city of pale light — light with no flame behind it, welling up out of the stone itself like milk pressed from a bruise. Terraces spiraled down the cavern walls, and on them moved figures. Even at this distance Noor could see they were beautiful, and even at this distance she could see that the beauty was a trap laid for the eye the way a spider's web is a trap laid for the wing.

They were tall. They were slender past the point of comfort, their limbs too long, their joints bending at angles that made her teeth ache to look at. Their skin held the faint nacreous sheen of a beetle's wing-case, and from their brows and cheeks swept crests of chitin that caught the pale light and scattered it in rainbows. They wore robes that moved like living things, and when they turned their heads, their eyes were compound and depthless, each one a thousand small mirrors reflecting a thousand small fires.

"They're gorgeous," Tam breathed. Then, with the fatal honesty that had nearly gotten him killed in Duskmarket: "I hate that they're gorgeous."

"Don't stare," Kess said. "Staring is a question. Questions are contracts."

"Everything here is a contract," Serevan said grimly. He had gone the color of old tallow, and his hand rested on his sword hilt not like a man reaching for a weapon but like a man reaching for something familiar in a house that had burned down. "I saw envoys from this place once. In the palace. They came to collect the first installment of—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They came to collect. That's all you need to know."

Noor barely heard him. She was looking at the terraces, at the figures moving between them, and at the things the figures carried.

Some bore trays. On the trays, glowing softly, were objects she had no name for — small, luminous shapes like drops of light given weight, like tears that had decided to become gems. And the court moved among them with the unhurried grace of merchants in a market, lifting them, weighing them, trading them back and forth, and every so often one of the tall figures would lift a glowing drop to what might have been lips and *drink*, and shudder, and laugh — a sound that reached the ledge a heartbeat later, thin as wire, sweet as a bell rung over an open grave.

"They're eating them," Tam whispered. "Those are— those are *people*, aren't they. Memories. That's what the vials in Duskmarket were. Little sisters of whatever those are."

"Memories. Vows. Names." Kess preened her wing, a gesture that did not quite hide the tremor in it. "The court beneath trades in everything a soul is made of. It's how they stayed rich after the war. It's how they stay rich now."

Noor's gift stirred in her chest, uncoiling like a waking animal. And then, for the first time in her life, it *answered*.

It was not the goat spirit's grumbling, nor the mules' simple wants, nor the crows' sharp gossip. This was a chorus — a vast, layered chorus of voices rising from the depths of the city, from places below the terraces, below the light, and every one of them was *chained*. She felt it the way she felt the pressure of deep water: the weight of countless bound things, beasts and worse than beasts, creatures the court kept the way a miser keeps coins, and every one of them crying out in the old language, in *her* language, and her gift rising to answer them before she could stop it—

"Noor." Serevan's hand closed on her shoulder. Hard. Deliberately painful. "Your eyes. They've gone silver. Whatever you're hearing, *stop hearing it*."

She wrenched herself back. The chorus dimmed to a distant keening, and she realized she had taken three steps toward the edge of the ledge, toward the light, toward the voices, and that Kess had her cloak gripped in her beak like a mother bird hauling a chick back from the nest.

"They felt you," Kess said quietly. "Something down there just turned around."

* * *

Serevan had served the Crown for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned that the realm's peace was a curtain, and that behind every curtain there was a room, and that some rooms you did not enter even when the screaming started.

He had entered one anyway. That was why he was here, disgraced, following a smuggler and a minstrel and a crow toward the thing he had sworn an oath to protect.

He watched the deep court from the ledge and made himself catalog it like a warden — threat, threat, unknown, threat — because cataloging was the only thing keeping his hands steady. The nobility moved on their terraces like figures in a clockwork orrery, and every one of them was a treaty violation, and every one of them was untouchable, because the treaties had been signed by men who signed first and read never.

He had been in the room, eleven years ago. He had been a fresh-sworn warden, standing post outside a council chamber, and through the door he had heard a voice like silk dragged over glass say: *The boy's soul, in surety, until the debt is paid. The war ends tonight.* And he had heard the old king weep. And he had heard the old king agree.

The realm's peace. Fifteen years of full granaries and empty border cemeteries, bought with a child.

And now he stood above the creditors' city, watching them drink memories like wine, and he understood at last that the war had never ended at all. It had only changed what was being fought over.

"The map," he said. "Noor. Show me the map."

She hesitated — he saw the old reflex, the smuggler's reflex, the *never show anything to a warden* reflex — and then she drew it out and spread it on the stone between them.

In the pale light of the cavern, Serevan saw what the firelight at the waystation had hidden. The map was not drawn in ink. The lines were too fine, too certain, and where the light touched them they gleamed faintly reddish-brown, and he had seen enough wounds in eleven years to know what color dried blood kept.

And the handwriting — he was no expert, but he had spent three days watching Noor trace those tunnels with her fingertip, and he knew the way her whole face changed when she touched the letters. He knew it the way you know a person's grief before they confess it.

"This is your mother's hand," he said.

It was not a question. He watched her arrive at the answer anyway, watched it land on her like a rockfall, because there is a difference between carrying a suspicion and hearing it spoken in a warden's flat, certain voice.

"Yes," Noor said. Her voice was very steady, which was how he knew she was holding herself together with both hands. "My mother drew this. My mother *came here*."

"Your mother was burned for beast-speech," Tam said softly. "You told us. In the pass."

"She was burned for beast-speech," Noor agreed. "But that wasn't all she was. That wasn't even—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she lowered it, she was looking at the map as if it were a face. "She knew the tunnels. She knew the cipher on the inner door — chapter seven, the one I haven't been able to read yet, she wrote it in a code I *almost* recognize. She didn't learn that from the surface. Nobody on the surface knows the deep court's door-codes."

"Then she learned it down here," Serevan said.

"Then she learned it down here," Noor echoed. "Which means she came. Which means she *bargained*." Her hand closed over the map, crumpling it, and Kess made a sharp warning sound and Noor made herself let go, finger by finger. "The court doesn't teach you its doors for free. Nothing here is free. I watched them drink someone's childhood five minutes ago."

The silence that followed had weight. Below them, the city glittered on, indifferent, its pale light rising like something blooming in the dark.

"She came down here," Noor said at last, "and she bargained, and she lost. And whatever she lost — whatever she *paid* — she came home and she never told me. She taught me to hide. She taught me the passes. She taught me to keep my heart shut like a strongbox. And all the while she was carrying—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and Serevan watched her hate it. "I spent my whole life thinking my mother's secret was that she spoke to beasts. That's not the secret. The secret is that she'd been *here*."

* * *

Tam had faced down a lot of terrifying things in his twenty-six years: his father's ledger, Madame Vesh's enforcers, an audience in Rell's Ford that threw turnips. None of it had prepared him for the simple, grinding fact of the deep court's beauty, which was worse than ugliness the way a forged coin is worse than no coin at all — because someone had *made* it to deceive you.

He sat with his back against the black glass wall, his lute across his knees, not playing it. Playing it felt dangerous. Everything here felt dangerous, and the strange thing — the thing he would never admit to the others — was how much of him wanted to go *down*. The music of the court drifted up in fragments, and it was the most beautiful music he had ever heard, and it was made of nothing, he could hear that now, hear it the way you can taste watered wine: melodies built out of other people's stolen joys, worn smooth by a thousand mouths, with no composer anywhere in them at all.

"I could write something better than that," he murmured.

"You could," Kess said, from his shoulder. "That's why you're dangerous to them."

He snorted. "I'm a debtor with a lute."

"You're a man who makes things that are *his*." The crow's eye, black and ancient, fixed on him. "The court cannot abide that. Everything down there belongs to someone else. Everything down there has been traded at least twice. A song that is only yours, that you'd never sell—" She clicked her beak. "They'd pay anything to own it. And once they owned it, you'd be empty, and empties are so much easier to govern."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. "You talk like someone who's read the ledgers."

"I *am* someone who's read the ledgers," Kess said, and turned her head away, and did not say anything else, and Tam had learned enough about crows — about *this* crow — to know when a door had been closed on purpose.

Below, the court's music swelled. On the highest terrace, directly beneath the great spire of stone at the cavern's center, the nobility had begun to gather in numbers. Tam counted thirty, forty, sixty of the tall figures, their wing-case robes shimmering, arranging themselves in patterns that hurt to follow, and at the center of them a dais was being built out of what looked, even at this distance, like bone.

"Solstice," Serevan said quietly. He had a spyglass out, a warden's glass, brass and honest. "That's a rite-ground. They're building it early. They always build early when the payment is large."

"The prince," Noor said. She was standing at the ledge's edge, the map pressed flat against her chest, and the pale light of the court rose on her face and made her look, for a moment, like something the cavern had grown itself. "They're building a stage for a soul."

"And we're going to walk into it," Tam said, "with one sword, one lute, one criminal crow, and a map drawn by a dead woman."

"Three of those things have gotten me out of worse," Noor said.

"Which three?"

"Ask me after."

But when she turned back to the map, Tam saw her finger stop — on the inner door, the cipher, chapter seven — and saw her lips move, shaping letters in a language that was not the beast-speech he'd heard her use on mules and goats and crows, but something older, something that made the black glass under his hands hum faintly, like a struck bell remembering its note.

And down in the city, the music stopped.

Every compound eye on every terrace turned upward at once — a thousand small mirrors, a thousand small fires, all of them fixed on the ledge where four outcasts stood in the dark.

"Kess," Tam whispered. "What did she just say?"

The crow's feathers rose slowly along her neck. "*Open*," she said. "She just said *open*. In the court's own tongue. And they heard her."

The pale light of the city began, very softly, to rise toward them like floodwater.
