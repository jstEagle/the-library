## 9. A Bargain Half-Remembered

The memory-trader kept her stall in the lowest gallery of the deep court, where the light came down in ropes and the air tasted of old rain.

Noor had expected something grotesque. She had spent the descent imagining the traders of the under-mountain as the warden pamphlets painted them — things with too many mouths, merchants of nightmare who weighed souls like fishmongers weighing herring. What she found instead was a small, neat woman with grey threading through her dark braid, hands stained to the wrist with ink the color of dusk, seated behind a table of black glass on which no dust would ever dare to settle. Around her, arranged with a curator's tenderness, hung hundreds of small glass vials, each one stoppered with wax, each one holding a curl of light that moved when you looked away from it.

Memories. Shelves and shelves of them, humming faintly, like bees asleep in winter comb.

"You're staring," the trader said, without looking up from the ledger she was writing. Her voice was dry and pleasant, the voice of someone who had made peace with her own strangeness a long time ago. "They all stare. The living always do."

"I'm not—"

"Dead? No. But you've been walking among my neighbors' collections since you came through the Doors of Bone and Salt, child, and everything down here collects. Including impressions." She set down her pen at last and raised her eyes. They were ordinary brown eyes, which somehow made them worse. "You want something. Everyone who comes this far wants something. Sit."

Noor did not sit. Behind her, Tam shifted his weight, his hand hovering near the knife he'd started carrying after the Weeping Stair, and Kess ruffled her feathers on Noor's shoulder in a way that meant *careful* in whatever language crows used for warnings. Serevan had insisted on scouting the gallery's other entrances; she could feel him at the edge of the lamplight, still as stone, doing warden things with shadows.

"Everyone keeps telling me what I want," Noor said. "I'd like to hear it from you."

Something flickered across the trader's face that might have been approval. "Good. The ones who already know their own wanting are easier to bargain with, and harder to cheat." She turned one vial idly on its stand, and the light inside it coiled like a sleeping eel. "You came down here following a map drawn by a dead woman. You speak to beasts in a voice your mother was burned for teaching you to hide. And you have been asking every creature in these tunnels — politely, so politely — about a bargain struck thirty years ago." The trader smiled thinly. "I sell memories, beast-speaker. Do you imagine for one moment that the deep court doesn't know exactly whose daughter you are?"

The word landed in Noor's chest like a dropped stone. *Mother.*

"My mother's name," Noor said quietly, "was Maren Quintana."

"I know," said the trader. "She sat where you're standing. Thirty-one years ago, in the spring, when the meltwater ran loud through the Stair. She bargained with me for an hour and left owing nothing, which is rarer than you'd think." The trader folded her ink-stained hands. "And I hold the last thing she ever gave this court: her final bargain, whole and unspent, sealed and shelved. I can give it to you. Every word she spoke here. Every term. Every price. You could watch her face as she made it. You could hear her voice say your name."

The world narrowed to the size of that single vial.

Noor had spent her whole life building herself around an absence. She knew the shape of it the way a river knows the shape of its banks — her mother's laugh, unrecorded; her mother's reasons, unwritten; the eleven days between the soldiers coming and the pyre being lit, which no survivor would ever describe to a frightened girl of seven. She had made a fortress of not-knowing. It had kept her alive. It had also kept her alone.

"How much?" she asked, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

The trader's smile gentled into something almost kind. "There it is. The question." She rose, unhurried, and drifted along her shelves, trailing one finger near — never touching — the vials. "Understand what you're asking. A memory is not a coin, child. It is a room in the house of yourself. When I take one, the room does not empty. The room is *walled up*. You'll know something is behind the wall — you'll feel the draft of it, sometimes, on cold nights — but you will never open the door again. Not by wishing. Not by weeping. Perhaps, if you're very lucky and very loved, someone else remembers it for you. That is the whole of the trade. There is no clause for regret."

"I understand bargains," Noor said. "I've been making them since I was nine years old. With things far older and hungrier than you."

"Mm. But those bargains were about goats and passes and storms. This one is about *you*." The trader selected a vial from a high shelf — a plain thing, its wax seal stamped with a spiral — and set it on the black glass between them. The light inside it stirred. For half a heartbeat Noor would have sworn she saw a woman's silhouette within it, dark-haired, arms crossed, wearing the particular stubborn stance of someone mid-argument.

"The price," the trader said, "is one memory of equal weight. And I warn you honestly: hers is heavy. A mother's final hour, offered freely, given in full knowledge — the court values such things above gold. You cannot pay with the taste of bread or the color of some childhood afternoon. You must pay with something that matters."

*Choose,* Noor thought, and the choosing tore at her even before she began. A morning with her mother, the last one, before the soldiers — the memory she returned to more than any other, the hearth-smell and the humming and the way Maren had braided her hair with trembling fingers, as if she'd known. Or the pass at Grey Hollow, the first storm she'd ever talked down alone, the moment she'd understood her gift was not a curse but a craft. Or—

Or Serevan's face in the firelight at the waystation, the first time he'd looked at her like she wasn't a fugitive but a person. Or Tam's terrible singing on the Stair, the moment the fear had cracked and become something else. Or Kess settling onto her shoulder for the very first time, years ago, uninvited, permanent.

No. Not those. Those were load-bearing walls. She could not pull them out and expect the house to keep standing.

But there was one memory she had guarded so fiercely, so long, that guarding had become its own kind of wound. The night the soldiers took her mother. Seven years old, hiding in the root cellar, listening through the floorboards. She had never told anyone the whole of it — not what she'd heard, not what she'd promised herself in the dark with her fists in her mouth so she wouldn't scream. It was the coldest room in her house of self. She had built her entire life on top of it: the closed heart, the hidden gift, the lonely competence. Everything she was had grown around that night like a tree growing around a nail.

If any memory could weigh against a mother's final bargain, it was that one.

"This," Noor said. "The night they took her. All of it. The cellar, the voices, what I swore in the dark."

The trader went very still. Then, slowly, she bowed her head — the first courtesy she had shown. "That will do. That is more than do. Are you certain? Once the wax breaks, there is no returning it to the shelf."

Behind Noor, Tam made a small sound — "Noor, wait, maybe—" — and Kess's talons tightened gently on her shoulder, and somewhere in the dark Serevan shifted, and she loved all three of them suddenly, fiercely, for trying to stop her, and it changed nothing.

"She spent her last hour buying something for me," Noor said. "The least I can do is pay for it myself."

* * *

The trader worked with a ritual precision that was almost tender.

She broke the wax on her mother's vial first and tipped the light into a shallow basin of black water, where it spread like smoke and held. "Drink with your eyes, not your mouth," she instructed. "Look into the water and remember that none of this is happening to you now. It happened long ago, to a woman who loved you. Grief will try to convince you otherwise. Let it try."

Then she came around the table and stood before Noor, and produced a second vessel — no vial this time, but a slender needle of pale glass, humming with Noor's own borrowed light. "This will not hurt," the trader said. "That is the cruelest part."

She touched the needle just below Noor's ear, where the skull met the neck, and Noor felt — nothing. A coolness. A sensation like stepping out of sunlight into shade. And then the root cellar was gone.

Not faded. Not dimmed. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the jaw — a absence with edges, a place where something used to be. She knew, abstractly, that she had once been seven and terrified beneath the floorboards. She could recite the facts like a smuggler recites toll schedules: soldiers, night, cellar, oath. But the cold was gone. The sound of boots overhead. Her mother's muffled voice saying her name through the boards one last time. All of it walled up behind a door that would never open again, and she could already feel the draft of it, exactly as the trader had promised — a coldness breathing through the seams of her, forever.

*So this is what it costs,* she thought, dizzy. *This is what I just paid.*

Then she looked into the basin, and forgot her own loss entirely.

* * *

Spring. Water running loud. A cave lit by candles that burned without heat, and her mother — young, younger than Noor was now — standing where Noor stood, arguing with the same dry-voiced trader across the same black table.

Maren Quintana did not look like a woman who would be burned. She looked like a woman who was tired of being careful. Her dark hair was loose, her sleeves rolled, and she gestured at the map spread on the black glass — *her* map, Noor realized, the blood-marked map that had crossed half a kingdom to find her daughter — with the fierce authority of someone correcting a ledger error.

"—the terms are mine to set, or there is no bargain," her mother was saying. "You'll hold the prince's soul in trust, unspent, unconsumed. You'll release it to whoever comes bearing my mark and speaking the counter-oath. And in exchange—"

"In exchange," the trader's voice said, patient as dripping stone, "you give us the one thing the court truly covets. Not your memories, Maren. Not your years. Your *line*. Your gift, passed to your heirs, bound to our service when called upon. The beast-speech has run in your family since before Thornfell had a name. We want what it will become."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the boy dies at solstice, thirty years hence, and the war goes on, and thousands die in the passes instead of one soul going hungry underground. You know how these arithmetic work. You've done the Crown's smuggling arithmetic your whole life."

Silence. In the basin-light, Noor watched her mother close her eyes, and watched her open them again, and watched her look — impossibly, directly out of thirty-one drowned years — at the place where her unborn daughter would one day stand.

"The counter-oath," Maren said. "Whoever comes. Swear it binds the court, not the comer. If my child pays for my promise, the payment ends with the debt. Nothing compounds. Nothing follows her home. Nothing *eats* her."

"It binds the court," the trader agreed. "We are creatures of law, Maren. It is our one virtue and our entire cage."

"Then witness it." And her mother spoke words in a language Noor had never learned but somehow, bone-deep, understood — the counter-oath, the release-terms, the mark of the blood-map burning itself into existence as she spoke, seared into vellum by the force of the vow alone. Noor watched her mother's hands shake as she rolled the finished map. Watched her tuck it into a courier's satchel with instructions whispered too soft for even the basin to carry.

And then the vision shifted — forward, to the end, because the memory was her mother's *final* bargain, and the court kept what was final.

A cell. Torchlight through bars. Maren Quintana kneeling, wrists bound, while a figure in the deep court's insectile beauty leaned close through a crack in the world that only prisoners and priests ever see, and said: *The terms have been challenged. The court wishes to renegotiate. Give us the girl instead — the child, the heir of your line — and walk free tonight.*

And her mother laughed. Actually laughed, ragged and unafraid, and said: "Take the bargain I made or take nothing. My daughter owes you *nothing.*"

"They will burn you."

"They were always going to burn me. That was never the question." Her mother lifted her chin, and through the bars of that distant cell she seemed to look straight at Noor, straight through the water and the years. "The question was always what I'd leave behind. And I leave behind a map, and an oath, and a girl who speaks to beasts — and *none* of it belongs to you."

The memory ended. The basin went dark.

* * *

For a long moment no one in the gallery moved.

Tam had both hands over his mouth. Kess had gone utterly rigid on Noor's shoulder, crow-still in the way that meant she was anything but calm. And Noor stood over the black water, tears sliding down her face without permission, feeling something in her chest break and re-set at a new angle.

Her mother hadn't died for hiding. Hadn't died for her gift, not really, not in the end. She had died *refusing to sell her daughter* — refusing a renegotiation, refusing to trade the girl's future for her own skin, holding the line of a bargain struck to save a fifteen-year-old prince she'd never met because it was right, because someone had to, because arithmetic done in human lives was the one sum she'd never balance.

Thirty years her mother had been a wound. Now, suddenly, she was a *legacy*. A plan, laid decades deep, with her daughter's name written in the terms of it. Noor hadn't stumbled into this. She had been *sent for* — by blood, by map, by a dead woman's stubbornness reaching across three decades to put the counter-oath in her child's hands.

"She bought back a soul before me," Noor whispered. "She tried. She got as far as the terms, and then they burned her rather than honor them, because the court wanted—" Her voice caught. "Because they always want more. Because they tested her, and she passed, and they killed her anyway."

"No," the trader said quietly. "They killed her because she *won*. Look again at the terms, beast-speaker. The counter-oath stands. The claim can be broken — by you, at the rite, before witnesses, in the old forms. Her death didn't void the bargain. Her death *sealed* it. They needed thirty years and a solstice and a soul ripening to its collection-weight before the claim could even be tested — and they hoped, all this while, that no one would come bearing her mark." The trader's brown eyes held Noor's steadily. "You are not too late. You are precisely on time. That is what your mother purchased with her life: your punctuality."

Noor wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, the way she did everything. Above, through leagues of mountain, the solstice was two days away. Somewhere in the dark of the deep court, a hollow prince was being prepared for consumption, and nobility like beautiful insects were rehearsing their rites, and royal wardens were closing in through tunnels that sang with the names of the dead.

Two days. One counter-oath. One blood-marked map. And a court that had killed her mother once and believed, with all the confidence of immortality, that no one would ever be fool enough to come back for the sequel.

Kess croaked softly — and then, in her cracked, rusted, once-in-a-season human voice, said a full sentence: *"She waits for you."*

Noor looked at the crow. At Tam, white-faced and resolute. At Serevan emerging from the shadowed archway, having heard enough, his warden's composure cracking around the edges.

"No," Noor said, and picked up the blood-marked map, and felt her mother's oath hum against her palms like a live coal. "She isn't waiting. She's *marching*. And we leave for the inner court within the hour."

Far off in the dark of the under-mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep — and every chained creature in the deep court, all at once, began to sing.
