## 10. Wardens at the Gate

The storm had followed them down from the Weeping Stair, and Serevan had learned to read its moods the way he'd once read a battlefield: by the weight of the air, the taste of iron on his tongue, the way the horses — and in their company, there were no horses, only mules and one ill-tempered pony named Grudge — pressed close against the cliff face when something on the wind displeased them.

Something on the wind displeased them now.

He stopped at the mouth of the ravine where the old shepherd's path split, one branch climbing toward the high pastures of Thornfell's southern shoulder, the other dropping into a fold of grey stone that the maps called Hollowmere Cut. Noor stood beside him, hood back despite the drizzle, her dark hair plastered to her forehead. She was listening. Not with her ears — he'd learned that much about her in the weeks since Duskmarket — but with some deeper instrument she kept hidden behind her ribs.

"Grudge doesn't like it," Tam said from behind, hugging the pony's neck as if the animal might shield him from whatever came next. "And I'll be honest, I don't care for it much myself. That ridge." He pointed with his chin at the eastern crag. "I've been watching it for an hour. It's watching us back."

"You've been watching it for an hour because you're avoiding tuning your lute," Kess said. The crow sat on Tam's pack, feathers slicked dark with rain, eyes like wet obsidian.

"I've been *strategically resting* my hands."

"Quiet," Noor said. Not sharp — she was never sharp, not with them — but low, the voice she used when the mountain was speaking through her and she needed silence to hear it. Her eyes had gone distant, unfocused, tracking something none of them could see. "There are riders below. In the cut. Six. Maybe eight."

Serevan's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. He knew what six to eight riders meant. He'd commanded companies of that size. He knew their spacing, their discipline, the way they'd fan out along a ravine to close a trap with the patience of men who had done this a hundred times before.

"Grey cloaks?" he asked, though he already knew.

"Grey cloaks." She turned to him, and in her eyes was the question she'd been carrying since the waystation, since the first night she'd seen him and understood exactly what he was. "How long have they known?"

"Since the Stair, probably. The outriders we fought — they weren't hunting blind. They were herding." He said it flatly, because saying it any other way would mean admitting what he'd been refusing to admit for three days: that his desertion hadn't bought them the time he'd hoped. That Captain Veyra Draymoor did not herd prey. She closed doors. "She knows where we're going. She's letting us go there so she can take us all at once, at the gate, with witnesses."

"Then we don't go to the gate," Tam said quickly. "We go around. There's always an around. There's always a smuggler's around, Noor, tell him there's an around—"

"There isn't." Noor's jaw tightened. "The cut is the only approach that doesn't cross open scree. If wardens hold the lower mouth, we either fight or we climb the southern face in this weather, and the southern face kills goats in good conditions."

"So we fight," Serevan said.

The words came out of him with a strange calm, the calm he remembered from the Border War, from the last stand at Cael Ford, from every moment when the choosing was over and only the doing remained. But this was different. At Cael Ford he'd fought for the Crown. Now he would raise steel against it, against men whose boots he'd polished and whose funerals he'd attended, and there would be no coming back from it. Treason wasn't a door you walked through. It was a door that closed behind you, forever.

He looked at each of them in turn. Noor, who carried her mother's map and her mother's grief and a gift that could unmake kingdoms. Tam, who had traded his life's songs for a single night of concealment and hadn't once complained, which frightened Serevan more than any blade. Kess, who was not a crow, who had folded her wings and gone very still in the way of creatures deciding whether to spend themselves.

"We can't win a stand-up fight with eight wardens," he said. "But we don't need to win. We need a hole, and we need thirty seconds to pour through it."

"And how do we make a hole?" Tam asked.

Serevan reached over his shoulder and drew his sword — the warden's blade, crown-stamped, that he should have surrendered at his desertion and hadn't, because some part of him had known even then that he would need it again. The rain ran down the fuller and dripped from the point.

"I make it," he said. "Personally."

* * *

They came down into Hollowmere Cut an hour later, moving fast and low, and the trap sprang exactly as Serevan knew it would spring, because he had designed traps like it himself.

The wardens held the narrow throat of the ravine where the walls pinched to less than ten paces wide. Two crossbowmen on the ledges above, four swords below, and behind them, seated on a boulder as if she'd been waiting for tea rather than treason, Captain Veyra Draymoor.

She looked older than Serevan remembered. Or perhaps he simply remembered her wrong — remembered the woman who had taught him to read tracks in fresh snow, who had once carried him three miles on her back after a rockslide shattered his leg, who had sworn the warden's oath beside him under the same winter sky. Grief had carved new lines into her face, and disappointment had deepened them.

"Serevan Ashvale." Her voice carried up the cut without effort, trained to carry across battlefields. "Eleven years I wore the grey beside you. Eleven years, and you throw it away for a smuggler and a songbird."

"For the truth," Serevan said. His own voice sounded strange to him, stripped of everything he'd used to fill it before. "You've seen what the Crown does to soul-bargainers, Veyra. You signed the orders yourself. You know what waits beneath this mountain, and you know what they intend to do at solstice, and still you ride for them."

"I ride for the realm." She rose from the boulder, and her hand found her sword hilt with the easy familiarity of thirty years' practice. "The realm is not yours to save, Ashvale. It is not theirs." A gesture at Noor, at Tam, at the crow. "Hand over the map. Hand over the girl. Come home. The tribunal will be lenient — desertion, obstruction. You'll serve five years in the northern garrisons and die an old man with a pension."

"And if I refuse?"

Veyra's face did something complicated then — something that might have been sorrow, if sorrow were allowed to wear a uniform. "Then I bring you in dead or alive, and I will grieve you either way, and I will never forgive you for making me choose."

Behind Serevan, Noor made a small sound. Not fear. Recognition. He'd heard it before, in the Weeping Stair, when the water sang the names of the dead and she'd whispered that her mother's name was among them. It was the sound of someone hearing their own future spoken aloud.

"Veyra." He stepped forward, alone, putting his body between the wardens and his people. "Last chance. Walk away. Take your company south. Tell the Crown you lost us in the storm. I'll believe you tried."

"I know you'll believe it," she said quietly. "That's what makes this unbearable."

She drew.

* * *

Later — much later, in the safe dark of a smuggler's cache two miles north, with his side bound and his hands still shaking — Serevan would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find that memory had scattered them like dropped coins.

He remembered the crossbows firing first, because that was doctrine, and Veyra never broke doctrine. He remembered shouting "*Down!*" and Noor dropping without looking, trusting him absolutely, and the bolt meant for her chest burying itself in the rock where her head had been. He remembered Tam — useless, debt-ridden, lute-carrying Tam — hurling a fist-sized stone with a slinger's accuracy and taking one crossbowman off the ledge in a shower of gravel and cursing.

He remembered Kess becoming something else. Wings like black sails, a shriek that was not a bird's shriek, and the second crossbowman clutching his face and falling backward off his perch screaming about *eyes, too many eyes*.

And he remembered Veyra.

She fought the way she'd taught him to fight: no flourish, no anger, economy itself. Every stroke intended to end him. And he met her stroke for stroke, and it was like fighting his own reflection, his own conscience given steel, and somewhere in the middle of it — between the third parry and the seventh, between the rain and the blood — he understood that she was not trying to kill him. She was trying to stop him. There was a difference, and the difference was mercy, and mercy was the one thing he could not afford to give her or accept from her.

"*Yield,*" she snarled, blades locked, faces inches apart. Rain ran between them. "Yield, boy, and live. I buried your father. I will not bury you."

"My father died for the Crown," Serevan said, "and the Crown let him. I won't."

He broke the lock, gave ground, gave more ground, let her press him back against the cut wall — and in the half-second where her balance committed forward, where eleven years of training told her the duel was won, he did the thing no warden was ever taught to do.

He struck to wound, not to kill. A short, vicious cut across her sword arm, opening her forearm from wrist to elbow, and as her blade fell from nerveless fingers he reversed his grip and laid his own crown-stamped edge against her throat.

The cut went silent.

Four wardens frozen mid-charge. Two crossbowmen down. Their captain on her knees in the mud with a traitor's steel at her pulse, and above them all, the crow circling with too many eyes.

"Order them back," Serevan said. His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated himself for it, and he said it anyway. "Order them back, Veyra, or I swear by every god we swore to together, I will do what has to be done."

For a long moment, the only sounds were rain and breathing and the distant complaint of Grudge the pony, who had opinions about all of this.

Then Veyra Draymoor laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and broken, and Serevan realized with a lurch that she was crying, that she had perhaps been crying since the moment she'd recognized his stride on the mountain trail three days ago and had ridden toward this anyway, because duty was duty.

"You always were the best of them," she said. "That was always the problem with you."

She raised her voice. "Wardens! Sheathe. *Sheathe!* Fall back to the treeline and hold." And when they hesitated, staring at their kneeling captain: "That is a lawful order from your captain. Move."

They moved. They withdrew twenty paces and stood in the rain, confused, armed, waiting.

Serevan lowered his sword. He stepped back. And Veyra got slowly to her feet, cradling her ruined arm, and looked at him with an expression he would carry to his grave — the look of a woman watching the last door close on someone she loved.

"Go," she said. "Run. Because I'm going to bind this arm, and I'm going to report that I was overpowered by superior numbers, and I'm going to buy you until sundown. And at sundown, Serevan Ashvale, I send a raven to the Citadel, and every warden in Thornfell comes for you, and there will be no more mercy. Not from me. Not from anyone. Do you understand what you've become?"

He did. He understood it completely, standing in the rain with his former captain's blood washing off his blade, listening to her offer him the last gift she would ever give him.

"Treason," he said quietly. "I've become treason."

"No." Veyra shook her head, and for one unguarded moment she was just a tired woman in the rain, older than her years, holding a wound she couldn't explain to her superiors. "You've become *free*. Gods help you. I never managed it."

She turned and limped toward her company, and did not look back.

* * *

They didn't speak for the first mile. They moved through the trees in the grey light, Noor leading now, reading the land the way she read everything — deeply, silently, with her whole self — and Serevan walked at the rear because that was where rearguards walked, and because he could not yet bear to look at any of them.

It was Tam who finally broke, because it was always Tam who finally broke.

"You could have killed her." His voice was thin. "You had her. Cold steel at the throat, game over, one less warden captain hunting us. Why didn't you?"

"Because she carried me three miles on her back when I was sixteen and my leg was broken," Serevan said. "Because she taught me everything I know about being a warden, including the parts worth keeping. Because killing her wouldn't have bought us anything except a company that fights to the death instead of a company that hesitates." He paused. "And because I've seen enough good people burned for the Crown's sins. I wasn't going to add her to the pile."

Noor had stopped walking. She turned, and the rain had stopped sometime in the last few minutes, and the light through the pines caught her face in a way that made his chest hurt.

"You're an outlaw now," she said. "Truly. There's no going back. No pardon, no tribunal, no northern garrison and a pension. Everything you were —"

"Was built on a lie I told myself," he finished. "That I could serve a thing and look away from what it did. I can't. I never could. That's why I deserted in the first place." He sheathed his sword, and the motion felt strange, final, like closing a book he'd never finish reading. "I chose this the day I saw what they did to the soul-bargainers, Noor. Today I just... paid for it."

Kess fluttered down from the pines and landed on his shoulder — an honor she extended to almost no one — and said, in her cracked human voice, the word she saved for moments that mattered:

"*Stay.*"

"I'm staying," Serevan told her.

Tam made a noise that might have been a laugh or a sob. "Well. That's it, then. We're all criminals. A smuggler, a deserter, a minstrel who owes money to people who sell information to insect nobility, and a crow who isn't a crow. The Crown trembles."

"The Crown doesn't tremble," Noor said. She was looking east, toward the mountain, toward the sealed court beneath it, and her hand had gone unconsciously to the map at her breast. "But something under it does. I felt it just now, when the wardens fell back. Something down there noticed us. Really noticed us." She turned to Serevan, and her eyes were steady, and afraid, and brave, all at once. "Sundown, Veyra said. Then the ravens fly. So we have until sundown to reach the Doors of Bone and Salt."

"Then we walk," Serevan said.

And they walked — four outlaws and a pony, into the shadow of the mountain, while behind them the sun climbed toward the hour when the first raven would rise from Hollowmere Cut carrying his name, his crime, and the promise that nothing in his life would ever be simple, or safe, or his own again.

He found, to his surprise, that he did not regret it.

What he regretted was how little that surprised him.
