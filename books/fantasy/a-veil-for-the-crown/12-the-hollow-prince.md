## 12. The Hollow Prince

The palace at Veymarch had a hundred clocks, and Prince Aldric heard none of them.

That was the first thing he had lost — not his name, not his face, but time itself, the way it used to press against him like weather, the way an hour could be heavy or light depending on what it carried. Now all the hours poured through him like water through a cracked bell. He walked the long gallery where the portraits hung — his grandfather, his grandmother, the regent-uncle who had died smiling and been painted smiling for eternity — and he felt about them the way one feels about furniture. Familiar. Inanimate. His.

A chambermaid was polishing the rail of the great stair when he passed. She dropped into a curtsy so fast she nearly spilled her beeswax, cheeks flushing.

"Your Highness."

"Yes," he said, because that seemed to be the correct response to whatever she had said, and kept walking.

He did this often now. The servants called it his melancholy walks, though melancholy was not the word. Melancholy was a weight, and weights required substance, and he was running short of that particular currency. He had counted once — truly counted, in the dark of his rooms, taking inventory the way a merchant counts coin before a bankruptcy — and found remaining: his love of rain on glass. A fragment of a song whose words had gone but whose tune clung like burrs. The memory of cold water on a summer day when he was nine. And one thing more, which he guarded like a coal cupped in bare hands: the *feeling* of having once been whole. Not the memory of it. The ache where it had been.

An ache, at least, was proof of a body. Proof that something had been taken from a shape that still remembered its own outline.

He descended the stair. No one stopped him. That was the strangest cruelty of it — that no one ever stopped him, that the heir to the Thornfell throne could wander his own palace like a ghost wearing his own face, and the guards saluted, and the stewards bowed, and not one of them looked at him long enough to see what was missing. Or perhaps they saw. Perhaps they had always seen, since the war ended, since the treaties were signed with ink that dried too dark, and they simply agreed among themselves never to speak of it, the way families agree never to mention the empty chair.

*The war is over,* they told each other in the taverns of the lower city. *The war is over, and the prince bought it for us.*

They said it like gratitude. They meant it like gratitude. And every winter, when the snows came easy and no border villages burned, the gratitude grew another year fatter and the prince grew another year thinner, though no physician could find the cause and none dared look too closely.

* * *

His study still smelled of cedar and old vellum, though he could no longer smell it. He knew it smelled of those things because the ledgers said so, because a steward had once written *the cedar oil has been refreshed, Your Highness* in a hand so careful it looked apologetic. He sat at the desk beneath the high window, where afternoon came through colored glass and lay across the blotter in bands of red and gold, and he did the thing he did most evenings now.

He wrote letters.

To no one.

There was a drawer full of them. Forty-one letters, the last count. None addressed, none sealed, none sent. He wrote them the way other men prayed — not expecting answer, only needing the motion of it, the scratch of nib on paper, proof passing out of him and onto a surface that could hold what he no longer could.

*Dear —*

He always began that way. *Dear*, and then nothing, because there was no one left to fill the space. His mother had died before the war's end; his father followed within the year, and some said of grief, though Aldric suspected grief would have required a soul, and his father had given most of his own to the same court his son had bargained with, in a different generation, over a different war, in a bargain the histories recorded as *a wise and prudent accord.* The Hollow Kings, someone had whispered once, drunk, in a corridor — and then gone white as salt and begged forgiveness, and Aldric had smiled and said *it's quite all right,* and meant it, because how could it wound him, a man with nothing left to wound?

*Dear —*

*I dreamed again last night. I say dreamed, though I am told I do not sleep deeply enough to dream. But something comes to me in the dark hours, a sensation rather than a scene: that I am standing in a doorway, and on the other side of it someone is calling my name. Not Aldric. My true name — the one I had before I was a prince, the one my mother used when I had skinned a knee or lied about it. I cannot retrieve it. I only know the door exists, and that the voice behind it is patient, and that patience frightens me more than anger ever could.*

*Today I reviewed the grain levies for the northern valleys. The numbers are good. The numbers have been good for eleven years. I signed them with a hand that does not shake, and I thought: somewhere under this mountain there is a ledger too, and my name is written in it, and the entry is coming due.*

*Solstice. Eleven days.*

He set down the pen. His hand *was* shaking now, faintly, the way a plucked string shivers after the music stops. He watched it with detached interest, the way he watched everything — from a slight remove, as if his life were a play performed in a theater he'd wandered into by mistake and couldn't find the exit of.

Eleven years ago he had been fifteen years old, and the Border War had eaten three harvests and both his elder brothers, and the ambassadors of the deep court had come up through a door in the roots of Thornfell that the Crown pretended did not exist, and they had offered peace the way a spider offers silk: generously, beautifully, wrapped around and around until nothing moved. He remembered almost none of it now. That was the design. He remembered the *terms* — those had been carved into him deeper than memory, into whatever substrate remained — but not the room, not the voices, not his own face when he said yes. He had said yes. Somewhere inside the hollow of him there was a boy who had said yes to save a kingdom, and the kingdom had accepted, and the boy had been spent like coin, and everyone had agreed the price was fair.

Fair. He turned the word over. It made no sound.

* * *

He took the letters out that evening, all forty-one of them, and laid them across the floor of his study by candlelight, reading them in order, watching the handwriting decay year by year the way a shoreline erodes. The earliest ones were dense, urgent, crammed to the margins — a drowning man writing. The recent ones were sparse. Airy. Gaps between sentences wide enough to walk a cart through.

*Dear — Today I watched a hawk above the east tower for two hours. I do not know why I am telling you this. I think it may be the only thing that happened.*

*Dear — The gardener has planted winter roses. I remember liking roses. I have written that sentence so that future me will know it too.*

*Dear — If you are reading these, then either I am dead or I am finished, and I find I cannot tell which would be worse.*

He gathered them up, squared their edges, returned them to the drawer. Ritual completed. Another day banked against the dark.

And then — as he rose, as the candle guttered in a draft from the window seam — it happened.

It came first as warmth. A single degree of warmth, blooming behind his sternum like a drop of dye in water. Aldric froze with his hand on the drawer. Nothing warmed, in here. Nothing in him had warmed in eleven years; the deep court's collection ran cold by nature, a slow siphon, and what remained in a hollowed man did not respond to anything. He had tested it. Joy did not move him. Grief did not move him. His brother's funeral had passed through him like wind through a ruined house.

But this was warm, and growing warmer, and it had a *direction*.

He stood very still in the red-gold light of the dying candles and let himself feel it — really feel it, the way a man starved lets himself taste the first bite, slowly, guiltily, certain it will be taken back. South and west. Toward the mountains. Toward Thornfell, where the door in the roots lay sealed behind wards older than the dynasty, where the court beneath kept its accounts and waited for solstice with the patience of stone.

Something was moving toward the mountain. Something small, and swift, and alive, and it burned in the direction-map of him like a spark carried in a closed fist.

Not the wardens. He knew the flavor of the Crown's instruments; they registered against his emptiness like frost against glass, dutiful and chill. This was different. This was *hot*. It felt — and he groped for the word, dredging the shallows of himself — it felt the way the doorway in his dreams felt. The one with the voice calling his true name.

"Hello," Aldric said aloud, to the empty study, feeling foolish and not caring, because caring also required substance. "I don't know what you are."

The warmth pulsed, once, like an answer. Or like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

He went to the window. Below, Veymarch glittered — ten thousand lamps, a capital fat and safe on a peace purchased with a child's ransom, its people sleeping soundly in beds they did not know were paid for. Beyond the city walls, far off, the white shoulders of Thornfell caught the moonrise. Somewhere under all that stone, something ancient sharpened its appetite for the eleventh day from now.

And somewhere on a road between here and there, a spark was traveling toward it. Carrying — he was suddenly, unreasonably certain — carrying something that belonged to him. Not gold. Not armies. Something smaller and infinitely heavier. The kind of thing that could be pressed into a dying man's hands. The kind of thing drawn in blood.

Hope, he thought. Is this hope? I had forgotten the word's weight.

It hurt. That was the miracle of it — that anything could hurt him anymore. It hurt like frostbite burning, like a limb waking after too long asleep, pins and needles and agony and *alive*, and he pressed his palm flat against the cold glass and laughed, a rusty, terrible sound that startled a nightbird off the sill.

"Eleven days," he told the mountain.

Then, because the ache demanded it, he went back to the desk, pulled fresh paper close, and wrote the forty-second letter — faster than any before it, the nib biting:

*Dear whoever you are — I felt you tonight. Come quickly. Whatever it costs, come quickly. I have been waiting eleven years to owe someone something again.*

He did not seal it. He did not know the address. Instead he folded it small and held it a long moment over the candle flame, watching the edges brown — and at the last instant, laughing again, breathless, he snatched it back and tucked it into his breast pocket, over the place where the warmth lived.

Some letters, he understood suddenly, you deliver yourself.

Far to the south and west, under storm-wrack and starlight, the spark moved on toward the mountain — and Aldric, hollow prince of a prosperous realm, stood at his window until dawn with his hand over his heart, keeping vigil over the first fire it had held in eleven years.
