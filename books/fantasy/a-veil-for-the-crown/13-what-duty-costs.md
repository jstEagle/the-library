## 13. What Duty Costs

The safehouse was an old shrine to a saint whose name the valley had forgotten, tucked into a fold of Thornfell where the pines grew so thick that even starlight had to negotiate entry. Rain came down in silver ropes outside the broken roof, drumming on the stone flags, and inside, the four of them arranged themselves around a fire like people who had learned — recently and painfully — that warmth was not guaranteed.

Kess perched on the saint's headless statue, feathers slicked dark with wet, one eye closed and one eye open, because Kess never fully trusted sleep. Tam lay flat on his back with his lute cradled on his chest like a sleeping child, fingers twitching through chord-shapes even as he dozed. And across the fire from Noor sat Serevan Ashvale, sharpening a sword he no longer had any legal right to carry, the whetstone whispering against steel in long, patient strokes.

He'd been doing it for an hour.

Noor knew the sound of a man avoiding conversation when she heard it. She'd grown up around smugglers, after all, and they were the world's foremost experts on silence used as armor. But Serevan's silence had a different grain to it tonight — tighter, guiltier — and she was tired enough, raw enough, close enough to the mountain's heart that she didn't want to let it sit.

"You've been circling something since we left the Stair," she said. "Say it or put the sword down."

The whetstone stopped. He looked at her across the flames, and the light moved in his face — the strong jaw, the scar splitting one eyebrow, the eyes that were warden-grey to their roots no matter what colors he wore over them.

"The realm," he said.

"What about it?"

"When you give Aldric his soul back." He turned the blade in the firelight, watching the edge. "You understand what happens then. The Border War doesn't stay ended out of gratitude. It stays ended because the deep court holds his vow. Break the vow, break the peace. The northern clans will smell blood by midwinter. There will be another war, and this one won't have a bargain at the end of it, because there'll be nothing left of him to sell."

Tam's fingers stilled on his lute strings. Even Kess opened both eyes.

Noor fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "I know what you think happens."

"Then tell me why I'm wrong."

"I'm not sure you are," she said quietly. "That's the worst part. Maybe the war comes back. Maybe the north burns. I've run that road in my head every night since the Weeping Stair, and every night it ends the same way — with me choosing anyway." She looked up. "Because the realm wasn't bought fairly, Serevan. You keep talking about it like it's a ledger that balances. It doesn't balance. It never balanced."

"It ended a war." His voice stayed level, but she could hear the effort holding it there, the way a bridge holds weight — fine until it isn't. "Ten years of peace. Harvests that got planted. Kids who grew up not knowing what a raiding horn sounds like. I served on the border before the armistice, Noor. I buried children. Whatever the cost was—" he stopped himself.

"Whatever the cost was, someone else paid it," Noor finished. "That's exactly my point. Say the rest of it. You're a man who follows rules down to the marrow; say the whole accounting, not just your half of it."

Serevan set the sword down across his knees. For a long moment the rain filled the silence, and the fire cracked, and Tam very carefully did not pretend he wasn't listening.

"Aldric made the bargain," Serevan said at last. "Freely. Heir to the throne, of age by law, sworn and signed before witnesses. That's what the histories will say if anyone ever writes them. A sovereign act, freely given."

"And how old was he?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped knife.

Noor hadn't meant to say it yet. She'd been carrying it since chapter upon chapter of her mother's cipher had finally yielded its dates — since the map's margins, in that handwriting she knew better than her own name, had laid out the whole shape of the thing. But exhaustion had stripped away her tact along with everything else, and now it was out, hanging in the smoke between them.

"Fifteen," she said, because there was no taking it back. "He was fifteen years old, Serevan. The treaty documents list him as sovereign signatory. The court beneath recorded him as willing. But he was a boy whose father was dying, whose generals were losing, whose councilors put a pen in his hand and told him the mountain would take his soul instead of ten thousand soldiers'. What kind of freedom is that? What fifteen-year-old can weigh a kingdom against himself and be called *willing*?"

"That's not—" Serevan began.

"They dressed it up," she said, and her voice was rising now, and some distant part of her noted it with alarm and ignored it. "They gave it ceremony. They gave it *legality*. They wrote it into the accord between crown and mountain so that no one would ever have to call it what it was. It was ransom. A child's ransom, paid to monsters, and everyone who benefited decided not to look too closely at the coin."

"The realm benefited—"

"*We* benefited!" She was on her feet without remembering standing. "Every town that slept safe, every merchant whose roads stayed open, every warden who collected wages guarding a peace built on a boy's hollowed-out chest — we all spent that money, Serevan. I did too! Don't you dare stand there and tell me about duty while you spend the interest on stolen goods!"

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, water found a new path through the ruined roof and ticked against stone.

Serevan rose slowly, the way he did everything — deliberately, like a man who had learned that speed was the enemy of judgment. He was half a head taller than her and broad through the shoulders, and none of it mattered at all, because Noor Quintana had stared down rockslide spirits and royal wardens, and she was not going to flinch from a deserter in a borrowed coat.

"He chose," Serevan said, low. "However badly the choice was framed, he made it. And it worked. You want to tear up the one contract keeping the peace together because the signature was young? Then own what comes after. Own the burned villages. Own the conscription notices. Own every mother in the borderlands burying what's left of a son, and tell yourself the soul of one prince was worth it."

"Don't you think I have?" Her voice cracked on it. "You think I sleep? Every mile closer to that mountain, I do the arithmetic you're doing, and mine comes out worse, because I know what it is to be sold young."

The words escaped before she could cage them.

Serevan went still. Not the stillness of a man regrouping — the stillness of a man who has just heard a door open that he thought was bricked shut.

"What do you mean," he said, "sold young."

* *

The rain kept falling. Noor stood with her arms wrapped around herself and hated, with a clean and total hatred, the part of her that had spoken.

But there was no unhearing it now. She could see him waiting — not pushing, never pushing, that was the maddening decency of the man — just present, solid, giving her the space like ground he'd cleared of his own feet.

"My mother," Noor said. "Everyone in Thornfell knows the story. The witch of the high passes, burned at Ashcross when I was nine. Beast-speaker. Devil-tongued. They tied her to a cart and made her ride through three villages so people could throw things, and then they burned her in the market square, and I watched from a rooftop because she told me to run and I couldn't make my legs obey." The firelight blurred. She blinked it clear. "What nobody knows is why. It wasn't just the gift. She'd bargained with the deep court. Years before — before I was born. And the bargain went wrong, or right, depending on which side of it you stood."

She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged.

"She sold something to buy my life. I was a sick infant. Fever that wouldn't break. The healers washed their hands of me, and my mother — who swore off the mountain's court forever, who hid her gift harder than I ever have — walked into the dark under Thornfell and made a deal. And the fever broke. And I lived. And eight years later the Crown's wardens came for her anyway, because someone talked, because a debt leaves traces even when the memory doesn't."

"Noor—"

"So don't tell me about choice," she said. "She chose. Freely, by any law you care to name. She chose to buy her daughter's life with whatever the court took from her, and she spent nine years after that teaching me to hide, to lie, to be small, so that her choice wouldn't cost me the way it cost her. And it cost her everything, and it bought me nothing but survival, and survival is *not enough*, Serevan. I am so tired of survival being held up as the price well spent."

Tam had stopped pretending to sleep. He sat propped on one elbow, lute forgotten, watching her with an expression Noor couldn't afford to examine. Above them, Kess had gone utterly still, head cocked, one black eye fixed on her with an attention that felt older than birds.

Serevan crossed the space between them slowly. He didn't touch her — she registered that, the discipline of it, his hands staying loose at his sides — but he stood close enough that she could feel the heat coming off him, human heat, ordinary and stubborn.

"You said sold," he said gently. "Not saved. Sold."

"Same coin," Noor whispered. "Different hand spending it."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the warden's polish worn through to something underneath.

"I arrested a soul-bargainer once."

The words seemed to surprise him as much as her. He looked past her, toward the fire, toward some middle distance only he could see.

"Her name was Maren Voss. Fisherwoman, down the coast from Greyharbor. Daughter sick with the lung-rot. She went to a river-court — lesser branch of the deep nobility, the ones who live in drowned places — and traded for the girl's life. Sound familiar?" His mouth twisted. "I was twenty-three. Captain Halvard's protégé. Best young blade in the eastern company, they said. I brought Maren Voss in myself. Proud of it. Did it *gently*, even — walked her the whole way, let her say goodbye to her daughter, told her the tribunal would show mercy if she cooperated."

"What happened?"

"The tribunal showed mercy." He said it flatly, each word laid down like a stone on a cairn. "They commuted the burning. Took her memories instead — all of them, back to childhood, the whole continuous thread of who she was. Left her alive. Breathing. Standing on the dock in Greyharbor with her cured daughter running up to hug her, and no idea who the girl was. No idea who *she* was. She looked straight through her own child and asked the little one why she was crying."

Noor's breath caught. "That's not mercy. That's worse."

"Yes," Serevan said. "That's what I told the captain. In front of the tribunal liaison. I asked him how we called that justice, and he said the alternative was fire, and I said then maybe we should ask why either was ours to give, and—" he exhaled, slow, controlled, the story dragged up from somewhere deep where he kept it locked. "Three weeks later I stood watch on the transfer of another one. A man who'd bargained with the mountain itself. And the transfer wasn't a prison convoy, Noor. It was a delivery. Wardens as couriers. The Crown has been feeding bargainers to the deep court for decades — settling accounts, smoothing treaties, paying old debts with other people's souls. I saw the manifest. I saw the seals. And I understood that everything I'd sworn to uphold was a machine for doing politely, in the dark, what we burn women in squares for doing desperately, in the light."

He finally looked at her again, and his grey eyes were terrible and honest.

"So when you say the realm was built on a child's ransom — I know. I've known for six years. I deserted rather than deliver one more soul, and I've hated myself for the cowardice of it ever since, because walking away isn't the same as stopping it. I've been following your trail for weeks telling myself it was duty, that I'd bring you in, that the law would sort it — and the whole time I was hoping you'd get far enough to do what I never had the spine to attempt."

The rain drummed. The fire settled. Noor found that her anger had nowhere left to stand; the ground beneath it had shifted, and what remained was something more dangerous — recognition.

"We're the same," she said quietly. "You ran from the machine. I hid from the gift. Both of us calling it wisdom."

"Is it?"

"No." She almost smiled. "It's fear wearing duty's coat. My mother taught me that hiding keeps you safe. She died proving hiding keeps no one safe. And you—" she studied his face, the scar, the lines carved by six hard years, "you put on a warden's coat every morning of your life until the coat fit so well you couldn't find yourself under it. And the day you finally took it off, you picked up a sword and kept swinging at ghosts of orders."

A breath of laughter escaped him — startled, rusty, real. "That's unfair."

"Completely. I learned unfairness from the best — the Crown, the court, and my mother's ghost."

Tam made a small sound that might have been a suppressed cheer. Serevan glanced over. "How much of that were you awake for, minstrel?"

"All of it, and I intend to set it to music." Tam yawned hugely and resettled against his pack. "Verse one: the warden and the witch argue by firelight. Verse two: they discover they're the same person. It writes itself. I'll take royalties in bread."

"Go to sleep, Tam."

"Gladly. Some of us sacrificed our entire artistic legacy to save your hides last week, you know. I mention it hourly, as is my right."

But his voice was warm, and his breathing slowed quickly after, and soon only the rain and the fire kept time in the shrine.

* *

Noor didn't move back to her bedroll. Neither did Serevan. They stood by the dying fire, closer than argument required, and the silence between them had changed texture entirely — no longer a wall but a shared country, strange and unmapped.

"Why does it matter to you?" she asked at last. "The prince. Really. You could walk away. Let the court collect, keep the peace, go be anonymous somewhere the Crown's writ doesn't reach. Why follow a smuggler and a debt-ridden minstrel into the dark under the world for a man neither of us has ever met?"

Serevan was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Because I've seen what the Crown does with souls," he said finally, "and I've never once seen it give one back. Everything in that machine moves one direction — down, into the dark, into the ledgers. Ten years of statecraft built on a fifteen-year-old's terror, and not one soul in all that time returned, not one debt forgiven, not one account reopened. If we pull Aldric free — if it's even possible — then it proves something the Crown has spent generations making unthinkable. That the debts can be undone. That Maren Voss's daughter could have had her mother. That the machine isn't a law of nature." His jaw tightened. "It's not really about him. It's about whether the ledger only ever runs one way."

Noor looked at him — this man who had crossed a kingdom chasing a hope he couldn't name, who argued for duty because duty was the only language the powerful had ever allowed him, and who had, underneath all of it, been carrying the same wound she carried: the certainty that love, properly practiced, means paying for other people.

"My mother used to say something," she murmured. "In beast-speech, when she thought I was asleep. *Duty is what love looks like when it's frightened.*" She shook her head. "I thought she meant it as a warning. Keep your heart closed and your duty clean. But I think she meant it the other way around. That the duty she did — the bargain, the burning, all of it — wasn't separate from loving me. It *was* loving me. Ugly and enormous and priced beyond sense."

"Then maybe," Serevan said, and his voice had gone quiet in a way that made the hair rise on her arms, "we stop arguing about whether the price was fair. Maybe the fair price was never the point. Maybe the point is that we pay it together this time, and we make the ones who set the price watch us do it."

Something in Noor's chest unlocked — a door she'd barred at nine years old, watching smoke rise over Ashcross market square.

"Serevan Ashvale," she said, "if we survive the next few days, I may have to stop being angry at you, and I genuinely don't know who I am without it."

"I could teach you. It's mostly walking and complaining about rations."

Despite everything — despite the mountain waiting below with its beautiful insectile hunger, despite her mother's handwriting on a dead courier's map, despite the solstice bearing down on them like a closing fist — Noor laughed. It came out cracked and surprised and true, and Serevan smiled like a man seeing sunlight through a window he'd sealed shut years ago.

Above them, Kess spread her wings, silent as falling snow, and dropped from the saint's statue to land on Noor's shoulder. Her beak parted, and the voice that came out was thin and rusted and absolutely certain:

"*Below.*"

Noor went cold. "What is it?"

The crow's black eye reflected the fire, and behind the reflection, deeper, something vast shifted in the stone beneath them — a tremor too faint for the others, but Noor's gift flared open like a wound, and she heard it clearly, in a hundred chained voices singing one note:

The deep court had felt them thinking about mercy.

And the mountain had begun, softly, to sing back.
