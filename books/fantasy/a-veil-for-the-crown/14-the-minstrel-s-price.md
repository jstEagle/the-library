## 14. The Minstrel's Price

Tam of Duskmarket had learned long ago that debt had a texture. His mother's debts had been coarse wool — scratchy, constant, ignorable if you kept moving. The debts he'd run up himself, in the years since, were finer things: silk debts, silver debts, the kind that slid against the skin so smooth you forgot they were there until they drew blood.

The one he carried now was velvet over a knife.

He sat in the back alcove of the Sallow Lamp — the last honest-adjacent tavern on the descent road where Thornfell's roots cracked through the earth like old bones — and he tuned a lute he no longer deserved to own. Three tables away, a woman in moth-grey drank nothing and watched everyone. He'd noticed her an hour ago. He'd been noticing her for two days, in one shape or another: at the ferry crossing, at the shrine where Noor had stopped to leave an offering she pretended was for luck, at the edge of the firelight when they'd camped beneath the leaning stone.

Moth-grey. The color of Duskmarket's ledger-houses. The color of the Verrin family's collection agents, who did not carry ledgers at all anymore, only memories of them, which were far harder to argue with.

"Your G string is flat," Kess said from the rafter above him.

"It's always flat. It gives the song character."

"It gives the song a headache." She dropped soundlessly to the bench beside him, folded her wings, and regarded him with one black eye. In crow-shape she could say perhaps forty human words, most of them insults, all of them delivered like verdicts. "Woman. Watching."

"I know."

"Noor doesn't know."

"Noor has enough to carry." He plucked the string, bent it true. "I'll handle it."

Kess tilted her head, feathers ruffling in what he had come to understand was her version of a sigh. "You," she said, with devastating accuracy, "handle badly."

* * *

He wanted to tell himself it wasn't his fault. That was the whole liturgy of a gambler's life, the morning prayer and evening vespers of it: *it wasn't my fault, the dice were crooked, the wine was watered, the hand I lost was never mine to begin with.* Tam had sung that hymn in every key the realm possessed.

But this time the words wouldn't fit the tune.

Because he knew exactly how it had happened. Six days ago, in Duskmarket, with Noor haggling for passage and Serevan standing watch with that coiled stillness of his, Tam had done the single stupidest thing a man in debt could do: he'd gone home. Back to the Verrin counting-house, back to Master Ferrin Verrin himself, with a story about settling accounts once the mountain business concluded. He'd even smiled. He'd made it charming. Charm was the only currency he'd ever minted honestly.

And Verrin had listened, hands folded, eyes soft as a spider's abdomen, and Tam had walked out believing he'd bought himself patience — not realizing until three days later, watching a moth-grey figure shadow their trail, that he'd purchased something else entirely. That in the counting-house, with his fine silk voice, he had told Verrin *where they were going*. The Weeping Stair. The deep roads. The sealed court beneath Thornfell.

He hadn't said it plainly. He'd said it in songs, in half-lines, in the oblique poetry that was his native tongue — but Verrin hadn't grown rich on plain speech either. And now the route was sold. Not to wardens, who at least wanted prisoners breathing. Sold *downward*, to buyers who paid in years and favors and the small pale memories of children, buyers whose nobility wore faces like wet jade and folded wings like a mantis at prayer.

His companions' lives, pawned to settle his tab.

He tested the thought the way he tested a new song, singing it silently to himself in the dark of the alcove, and it held every ugly note.

"You're doing the face," Kess observed. "The face where you decide to be brave alone."

"I don't have a face for that."

"Everyone has a face for that. Yours is the worst one. Very dramatic. Good for ballads. Bad for living."

He laughed despite everything — a short, cracked sound — and Kess sidled closer along the bench until her warm weight pressed his hip, which was as close as a crow came to an embrace.

"Noor," he started.

"—would forgive you," Kess finished. "That is the problem. Forgiveness is cheap. You need something else."

What he needed was a night. One night of concealment between here and the Doors of Bone and Salt — because the moth-grey woman would report by dusk, and whatever the deep court sent in answer would not be riders in royal grey. It would be quieter than that. Hungrier.

And there was exactly one merchant on this stretch of road who sold concealment, and Tam knew precisely what coin she took.

* * *

The memory-trader kept shop in a hollowed-out waystone half a mile off the road, a place so old the pilgrim carvings on its lintel had worn into suggestions of faces. Tam had passed it twice before in his traveling years and twice before had told himself he'd never go in. Debt taught you where all the doors were. That was debt's cruelest gift: it made a mapmaker of you.

Inside, the air smelled of cold candlewax and rain on stone that had never seen rain. Shelves lined the walls, and on the shelves sat vials — hundreds of them, thousands, each holding a curl of light the color of whatever it contained. Gold-white for joys. Blue for griefs. One near the door glowed an embarrassed pink that Tam decided he did not want explained.

The trader herself sat behind a table of driftwood, a small woman with white streaks in her hair like frost finding a window, and she looked up at him with the mild interest of someone reading a menu.

"The minstrel," she said. "You were louder last time you were in my town."

"I've matured."

"Mm." She turned a vial in her fingers; it shed a warm amber glow. "You want passage-concealment. A night's veil over four travelers and one bird, strong enough to blind the eyes the mountain lends to the deep court's servants." Her mouth curved. "News travels down faster than people think."

"That's rather the point, isn't it." His voice held. He was proud of his voice; it always held. That was its whole tragedy — it held when nothing else did. "Name the price."

"For a veil of that weave?" She set down the amber vial and laced her fingers. "A year of your future craft. Your hands' knowledge of the next twelve months of music you'd have made."

It was a fair price, as these things went. It even had a certain elegance — she wasn't taking anything he had, only everything he was going to be. Tam stood there in the candle-smell and the cold and thought about haggling, because haggling was instinct, because somewhere under the fear the performer in him was already composing the scene: *the minstrel who bargained with Memory herself.*

"No," he heard himself say. "Not enough. It has to hurt more than that, or I'll find a way to weasel out of it later, and you know it, and I know it."

The trader's eyebrows rose a fraction — genuine surprise, or the memory of it.

"There's a vial on your third shelf," Tam said, and his heart was going like a drummer who'd missed the count and decided to solo anyway. "Blue-green light. Small. Older than the rest — the wax seal's been redone twice. I've seen it every time I've passed this place for six years and every time I've pretended it wasn't there."

"And why is that, minstrel?"

"Because it's mine." His mouth had gone dry as chalk. "Because I sold it to you six years ago, the night after the Fever Winter, when my sister was dying and the physickers' fees had eaten everything, and I needed coin that didn't ask questions. Because it holds every song I ever wrote. Every one. Nine years of compositions, from the first terrible thing I scrawled at fourteen to—" His voice finally cracked, right on schedule, and he let it. "To the lullaby I wrote for Wren the week before she died. Which she never heard, because she was past hearing, and I sang it anyway at the graveside because it was the only way it would ever exist anywhere except inside me. And then I dug it out of inside me and I sold it to you, and I have spent six years telling myself it was a kindness, that those songs are better off asleep, that a man who can't remember writing a thing was never really its father."

The silence rang. Somewhere in the shelves, a vial hummed faintly, sympathetic frequencies.

"That is quite a speech," the trader said softly.

"I write speeches. It's most of what I do." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Here is my offer. The vial of my songs, plus the year of future craft. Both. In exchange for the veil — and one thing more."

"Which is?"

"When you hold my songs again, you'll know every melody in them. Every lyric. You traders can't help listening; it's in the touch." He leaned across the driftwood table. "So listen well, and then go sell word of them downward, or sideways, wherever you sell things. Sing one bar of my sister's lullaby into the deep court's fine jade ears. Make them hear what a soul sounds like before it's spent. Maybe it won't change anything. But maybe some creature down there that's ten thousand years old and eats children's futures will hear a dead girl's lullaby and feel something it hasn't felt since the mountains were young. Call it a bonus. Call it spite. Call it art."

The trader looked at him for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the third shelf, and brought down the little blue-green vial, and the sight of it undid him more thoroughly than any argument could have. It was so *small*. Nine years, and it fit in her palm.

"You understand what you're buying," she said. "Not the veil. The veil is my side of the trade. I mean afterward. You will sit at fires for the rest of your life and reach for a song and find the shape of it gone — the hole where it was, clean as a tooth pulled. You'll remember that you were someone who wrote music. You will never again remember being that music. Most men couldn't pay that price. Most men would rather die."

"Yes," Tam said. "Funny thing. I asked around."

* * *

He almost didn't do it.

That should be recorded somewhere, he thought later — carved into the waystone above her door: *he almost didn't do it.* Because heroism as told in taverns was a straight line, a drawn blade, a clean decision struck like a note. What actually happened was that he stood in the cold candlelight with his hand outstretched and his whole body staging a mutiny, and he thought about the first song he'd ever written — *The Ferryman's Daughter*, God help him, fourteen years old and rhyming *heart* with *apart* — and how he'd never get it back, none of it, and how Wren's face was already going soft at the edges in his memory like wet paper, and how her lullaby was the last hard outline of her left in the world and it lived in glass on a shelf and he was about to hand it to a stranger and ask her to weaponize it.

He thought about Noor, who had spent her whole life locking up the truest thing in her, and what it had cost her, and how she was walking toward a court that ate souls to give back a stranger's because duty demanded it, and she had never once, not once, asked anyone to watch her do it.

He thought: *somebody should at least see the price being paid. Somebody should be a witness, even if it's just me.*

"Wait," Tam said, as the trader reached for his hand. "One moment."

He took out his lute. Right there, in the shop of vials, among a thousand sleeping memories, he tuned the flat G string properly — truly properly, the way he never bothered, the way he'd been saving for occasions that never seemed to arrive — and he played.

He couldn't play what was in the vial; it was locked away from him even now, six years of melodies sitting right there on the shelf, unreachable. So he played the other thing, the thing nobody had ever put in a bottle: the songs he'd learned, stolen, mangled, and made his own. The ferryman's reel his father had danced to. The border ballad Serevan had hummed, thinking no one heard, three nights ago. The drinking song Kess liked, though she'd deny it. Noor's walking rhythm, the long stride, the mountain pace — he built a tune around it while she slept thirty miles behind him, unaware, and if it wasn't good, it was at least *true*, and truth was the only instrument he'd ever really mastered.

Then he set the lute down, and gave the trader his hand, and said, "All right. Take it."

* * *

The taking was gentle, which was somehow the worst part.

Her fingers closed around his, cool and dry, and she drew from him the way you draw thread from a spool — a soft continuous pull behind his eyes, a sensation like a tide receding from a shore he hadn't known he owned. The year of future craft went first; he felt it leave as a strange lightness in his hands, as if his fingers had forgotten an appointment they'd been keeping. Then deeper. Then the shelf.

Nine years. It took nine seconds.

He came back to himself on his knees on the cold stone floor, the lute still lying where he'd set it, and the trader was standing over him holding two vials now instead of one — the old blue-green one and a new one, still warm, glowing the uncertain gold of work unfinished. Her expression had changed. There was something in it he'd never seen on a merchant's face: reluctance.

"For what it's worth," she said quietly, "the lullaby is lovely. I listened. You asked me to."

"Is it?" His voice came out sanded flat. He couldn't remember. He tried to reach for it and found the hole, clean as a pulled tooth, exactly as promised. Wren's face swam up at him, soft-edged, and this time there was nothing behind it holding the line. "Was she — in it? Could you tell it was for someone?"

"It was for someone," the trader said. "Anyone who hears it will know that. I'll make sure of it."

She worked fast after that, whether out of respect or the simple professional desire to be rid of a crying man, he never learned. She unstoppered a vial of grey-silver light and spoke words that tasted of slate and snowmelt, and the veil settled over the waystone, over the road, over the night itself — Tam felt it go out like a held breath released across miles, wrapping four sleepers in a camp by the leaning stone and one crow roosting above them in a soft cocoon of *unnoticed*, a night sewn shut against hungry eyes.

"Done," the trader said. "Until dawn, the mountain cannot find them, and the mountain's masters cannot lend it sharper sight than that. Go back to your friends, minstrel. Sleep. It helps."

"Will it?" He gathered up the lute. Its weight was wrong now, or he was wrong for it; the strings looked like arguments in a language he used to speak. "Help?"

"No," she said, with the honesty of her profession. "But it passes. Everything does. That's rather my entire industry."

* * *

He walked back along the moonlit road with the lute slung on his back like something borrowed, and he let himself feel all of it — the grief, the hollow, the obscene lightness — because he'd learned tonight that feelings you refuse to perform only charge interest.

At the camp, Noor woke the instant his foot touched the trampled grass, knife already half-drawn, then easing when she saw his face. Even half-asleep she moved like the mountains themselves: patient, watchful, slow to anger and slower to trust.

"You were gone," she said.

"Had an errand."

"At this hour."

"I'm a musician. Our hours are famously disgraceful." He sat down heavily by the embers, and she studied him — she had a way of studying a person like weather coming over a ridge — and he watched her decide, visibly, not to press. She carried enough. They all carried enough. That was the covenant of the broken: nobody audits anybody's pockets.

Serevan slept like a man keeping one ear on duty even in dreams, which he was. Kess had tucked her head under her wing. The veil lay over them all, invisible and total, and Tam felt it like a second blanket and hated how much comfort it gave him, knowing what it cost.

Noor shifted, making room. "The watch changes at moonset," she murmured. "Try to sleep."

"Aye," Tam said. "Goodnight, Noor."

She closed her eyes. Within moments her breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of someone who trusted the person on watch, and Tam sat by the embers with his useless beautiful lute and thought about holes shaped like songs.

Then, from the darkness beyond the fire, a voice began to sing.

It was thin, and high, and wordless, and it came from everywhere and nowhere at once — from the stones, from the wellshaft of the old waystation behind them, from the deep roads below. It was not the deep court's hunting cry. It was smaller than that, stranger than that: a single bar of melody, repeated, wandering, learning itself.

Wren's lullaby. Sung down in the dark by something ten thousand years old, feeling its way through a dead girl's tune like a child learning to whistle.

Tam went very still. Beside him, Noor's eyes snapped open, and in their depths he saw the beast-speech waking, answering, reaching toward the sound.

"Well," he whispered, "that was fast."

And far below them, through a hundred leagues of singing stone, something in the sealed court *stopped* — a vast attention turning upward, toward the surface, toward the veil, toward the four sleepers and one crow who had until dawn to disappear.
