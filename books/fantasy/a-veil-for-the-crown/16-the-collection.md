## 16. The Collection

The mountain swallowed them whole.

Noor had descended the Weeping Stair with the taste of fear on her tongue, but nothing had prepared her for this — the last gallery before the rite-chamber, where the walls were not stone at all but something older, veined with light that pulsed like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of centuries. The air tasted of iron and honey. Of vows. Of everything ever promised and never kept.

She pressed her palm against the wall and flinched. Beneath her skin, the beast-speech rose like floodwater. Every chain in this place sang to her — and there were so many chains. She could feel them the way she felt weather coming, a pressure in the bones: creatures bound in alcoves, spirits nailed to thresholds, small gods kept in jars like preserves. They had been crying out for so long that most of them had forgotten the words for anything else.

*Hush,* she told them, not aloud, not yet. *Not yet. But soon.*

Kess landed on her shoulder, feathers cold as river stones. "They are gathering," the crow said, in that voice that was not quite a bird's and never had been. "The Crown's envoys came through the eastern gate an hour past. Velvet and gold and fear. The court is delighted. The court is *always* delighted, before."

"Before what?"

"Before it eats."

Tam shifted beside her, his lute-case strapped across his back like a shield, his face pale in the vein-light. He had traded his songs for one night's concealment, and the loss sat on him like a coat two sizes too small — she could see it in the way he kept reaching for melodies that weren't there anymore, his fingers twitching against his thigh, playing nothing. "How many?" he asked. "Envoys, I mean. How many do we have to scandalize?"

"All of them," Noor said.

"Good." A ghost of his old grin. "I never could do anything by halves."

Serevan said nothing. He stood at the edge of the gallery with his warden's blade drawn and turned point-down, the way a man holds a sword when he has already decided what it will be used for and is only waiting for the moment to arrive. He had turned it on his own captain three days ago. He was a traitor now, branded and hunted, and he had not once looked back.

He looked at Noor instead. He had been looking at her, she realized, the way he looked at her now — steady, unflinching, as if she were something worth guarding — for longer than she had let herself notice.

"When you walk in there," he said quietly, "they will offer you things. The court always offers. That's how it hunts. It doesn't chase. It *invites*."

"I know."

"Whatever they offer — your mother, your past, a life where none of this ever happened — it will feel real. It will feel like the truest thing you've ever been shown. That's the trick of it. The truer it feels, the deeper the hook."

Noor met his eyes. "And if what they offer is real? If they can actually give it?"

Serevan's jaw tightened. "Then it will cost you the only thing they actually want. And it won't be your coin they take. It'll be everyone standing behind you."

The words landed like stones in still water. She thought of her mother's handwriting on the map, the careful loops of a woman who had bargained here and lost. She thought of the memory she had already paid — a whole year of her childhood, traded for the truth of what her mother had died trying to do. She was not walking into this court clean. She was walking in already bleeding.

"Then I'll bleed in the open," Noor said. "Where everyone can see what it costs."

* * *

The rite-chamber had no ceiling. Or rather, its ceiling was the inside of the mountain's heart — a vaulted dark shot through with slow-moving lights, like the aurora drowned and buried. The floor was a single disc of polished obsidian, and upon it, in concentric rings, the deep court had assembled.

They were beautiful. That was the first obscenity of them. Insectile and elegant, their bodies chitin and gossamer, their faces arranged into expressions that were almost human — *almost*, the way a mask is almost a face. Their voices, when they spoke, were music. Their movements, when they moved, were dance. Everything about them had been refined across ten thousand years into the perfect shape of a thing that takes.

At the chamber's center stood a dais of black salt and white bone, and upon the dais stood the Crown's envoys — six of them, in grey and gold, holding a prince who did not know he was a prince.

Noor had never seen Aldric before. She had expected hollow, but hollow was a word, and this was a wound. He stood among his handlers like a man standing in his own house after a fire, touching things, looking at them with a polite, distant wonder. He was tall and fair and there was something in his face — some last stubborn ember behind the glass of his eyes — that made her chest ache. He kept glancing toward the chamber's entrances. Searching. For what, he could not have said. For something he had lost so long ago he had forgotten its shape.

For himself.

The court's herald rose — a towering figure whose body was a cathedral of translucent wing-cases, whose voice was the sound of a harp strung with hair.

"The solstice turns," the herald sang. "The vow comes due. Let the Crown's debt be paid, as it was sworn, as it was sealed, as it was *signed in the blood of the heir of the realm*."

The envoys bowed. That was the second obscenity — how easily they bowed. These were the Crown's chosen, the realm's finest, and they had come to watch a man be eaten and called it statecraft. Noor saw one of them, an old woman with a chancellor's chain, look away at the last moment. Look away, and keep bowing.

*That one,* Noor thought. *Remember that one. Even the guilty have a seam.*

The court's nobility began to move, circling the dais, and the lights overhead dimmed toward the color of a closing eye. Aldric did not struggle. That was the worst of it. He simply stood, and tilted his head, as if listening for something just out of hearing — and Noor understood, with a certainty that felt like falling, that some part of him knew. Some buried, starving part of him had been listening for rescue for twenty years.

Her terror rose like a tide — and she let it.

She had spent her whole life drowning that voice, the beast-speech, the inheritance that had burned her mother. She had kept it small and hidden and safe. But her mother had not hidden. Her mother had walked into this same court, with this same gift, and died trying to unmake a bargain. The gift was not the shame. The hiding was.

Noor stepped out of the shadow of the gallery into the rite-chamber's open light.

The effect was instantaneous. A hundred inhuman heads turned. The music of the court faltered, a harp-string snapping somewhere in the dark. The Crown envoys stared — a beast-speaker, a smuggler's daughter, a woman in road-dirt with a crow on her shoulder, a disgraced warden and a debt-ridden minstrel flanking her like an honor guard of the impossible.

"Who walks uninvited?" the herald demanded, and its song curdled into thunder.

Noor lifted her chin. Her heart was a war-drum. Her hands, she noted distantly, had gone completely steady.

"Noor Quintana," she said, and her voice — her human voice, the small careful voice she had used all her life — cracked once, and then did not crack again. "Daughter of Maren Quintana, who drew your maps and paid your prices. Beast-speaker. Guide of Thornfell. And I demand audience of this court — under its own law, which binds it as surely as it binds the chained."

A ripple went through the assembled nobility. Somewhere in the rings, a voice like wind through a bone flute hissed: *A Quintana. Again.* The word *again* carried a weight of old grievance, old fear — and old memory. The court remembered her mother. The court had not expected to remember her mother tonight.

"The rite is sealed," the herald said. "Audience is closed."

"Then unseal it." Noor walked forward — one step, two — into the outermost ring of the court, and the nobility parted before her like water before a keel, not from courtesy but from something very like unease. "Or are your laws like the Crown's, binding only on the small?"

That struck home. She saw it land, saw the ripple become a tremor. The deep court was old, and what was old was proud, and what was proud could be *wounded*. Kess shifted on her shoulder and said, clearly, in the silence, in the human tongue: "Answer her."

A hundred heads turned to the crow. A hundred ancient minds recalculated. Kess — exile, watcher, crow — sat on a smuggler's shoulder in the heart of the court that had cast her out, and dared them.

The largest of the nobility descended from the inner ring. It was tall as a gallows and moved like silk poured over a blade, and when it spoke, its voice was gentle, which was the most frightening thing Noor had heard all night.

"Little speaker of beasts," it said. "You are owed your audience. The law is the law, even here. *Especially* here." It gestured, and the rite paused — the circling nobility stilled, the dimming lights held at the color of a half-lidded eye. "Speak, then. You have until the solstice turns. And the solstice is *turning*."

Noor looked past it, to the dais. To Aldric. The prince was looking at her now — truly looking — and behind the glass of his eyes, that buried ember had turned toward her like a flower toward sun.

"I know why you've gathered," Noor said, and her gift rose in her voice now, the beast-speech threading through the human words like gold through dark cloth, until every chained thing in the chamber lifted its head, until every bound spirit strained toward the sound of a voice that could hear them. "I know what was taken. And I know what a boy of fifteen is, and what he is not — and I have come to tell you that your claim is *rotten at the root*."

The chamber went utterly, perfectly silent.

The tall one smiled. It had too many teeth for the smile to be anything but a threat, and its eyes glittered with something that was not quite amusement and not quite hunger.

"Bold," it said. "Foolish. *Inherited.*" It inclined its head, courteous as an executioner. "Very well, daughter of Maren. The court hears you. Speak your challenge — and know that we will answer it. We will answer it with everything you have ever wanted."

The lights above began to move again. The solstice was turning. And Noor Quintana, who had spent her whole life hiding, stood in the open heart of the deep court with her gift blazing like a torch, and did not look away.

* * *

Later — though *later* was a strange word in a place where the dark itself leaned in to listen — she would try to remember the exact moment her fear became something else. She never could. There was no clean line, no single breath where terror ended and defiance began. There was only the walking: one foot and then the other, across the obsidian floor, through the parted rings of beautiful hungry things, with her family at her back and her mother's name in her mouth.

Serevan walked half a step behind her, blade bare. Tam had his hand on his lute-case, though there were no songs left inside it — only the shape of them, only the sacrifice. Kess rode her shoulder with her feathers lifted, a small black banner of war.

And ahead, on the dais of bone and salt, the court's herald raised its voice to sing the solstice in, and the Crown's envoys bowed their heads, and the prince without a shadow watched her come with the first hope he had felt in twenty years written across a face that had almost forgotten how to hold it.

Noor Quintana drew breath — and the mountain, every chained thing within it, drew breath with her.
