## 17. Speak, Beast-Speaker

The rite had a heartbeat.

Noor felt it before she saw it — a slow, subterranean pulse rising through the obsidian floor of the great vaulted hollow the deep court called its Hall of Vow. It came up through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her shins, into the cage of her ribs, where it settled and kept time with something that was not her own heart. The mountain's heart. Or the court's. After this long beneath the earth, she could no longer tell where one ended and the other began.

She stood at the edge of everything.

Before her, the hall opened like the throat of some vast and patient creature: walls of veined black stone hung with banners that were not cloth but membrane, translucent as dragonfly wing, each one shivering with the memory of a bargain struck upon it. Chandeliers of pale crystal dripped light that had no source — cold, honey-colored, the color of things preserved in jars. And at the center, ringed by tiers of seated nobility, rose the Rite-Stone: a spire of white salt and braided bone, taller than three men, humming with the pulse she felt in her teeth.

Upon it, bound in threads of silver light at wrist and throat and heart, stood Prince Aldric of Thornfell's crown.

He did not look like a king-to-be. He looked like a man made of smoke holding the shape of a person because no one had told him yet he could stop. His eyes were open. That was the worst of it. He was watching the ceremony happen to him the way a man watches weather — present, powerless, already half-gone — and when his gaze drifted across the assembled court and snagged, for half a heartbeat, on the small figure standing uninvited at the hall's far door, something flickered in his face. Not recognition. Not hope. Something older than either. The look of a drowning man who feels, without understanding why, that the water has grown slightly warmer.

Noor walked forward.

Every step rang against the stone, and every ring went ahead of her like a rumor. She heard the whisper-silk sound of a thousand insectile heads turning, the dry rustle of wings folding and unfolding beneath gossamer robes, the click of chitin fingers on chitin palms. She had imagined, on the long walk down from the breach, a hundred ways this could go. In none of them had she imagined the court would simply *watch*. But that was what it did. That, she understood suddenly, was all it had ever done. It watched. It weighed. It waited for mortals to hang themselves on their own words.

So she would give it words worth hanging on.

"I am Noor Quintana," she said, and her voice carried farther than it had any right to, because the hall *wanted* to hear her, the way a spider wants to hear the fly describe the web it's caught in. "Daughter of Marisol Quintana. Guide of Thornfell. Beast-speaker."

The last two words she said loudly, plainly, without flinching — and it was like unbuckling armor she'd worn so long she'd forgotten its weight. Her whole life she had swallowed that phrase before it reached her teeth. Her mother had died for it. And here, in the deepest and most dangerous room in the world, she gave it away freely, and found she was still standing.

A murmur moved through the tiers like wind through wheat.

At the foot of the Rite-Stone, on a dais of fused glass, sat the Sovereign of the court beneath the mountain — though *sat* was generous; it *pooled*, a tall shape of jointured shadow and pale luminous joints, crowned with antler-like filaments that forked and refracted the crystal light. Its face, if it had one, was a suggestion behind a veil of drifting membrane. When it spoke, its voice did not come from its body but from everywhere at once, including, horribly, from inside Noor's own skull.

"A beast-speaker walks into the Hall of Vow on the night of collection," the Sovereign said, "unbidden, uninvited, and unarmed." A pause with the texture of amusement. "Speak, then. You have our attention. It is a more expensive gift than you know."

Noor lifted her chin toward the figure on the Rite-Stone.

"That," she said, "is not a bargain being collected. That is a boy being eaten."

The murmur again — sharper now, edged with something that might have been offense or might have been delight. The Sovereign's filaments tilted, all of them, toward her at once.

"The vow was sworn," it said. "Freely spoken, sealed in blood, witnessed by our law. The Border War ended the hour it was made. Two kingdoms breathe in peace because of it. Do you dispute the peace, guide?"

"No." Her voice did not shake. She made certain of that, made it flat and hard as river ice. "I dispute nothing of the kind. I dispute the *teller* of the tale your law is built on."

She turned, deliberately, slowly, so the whole court could watch her do it, and faced the tiers.

"Your law says a vow binds the will that spoke it. So ask yourselves what will stood on this stone twenty years ago. Ask what manner of sovereign signs away his soul." She pointed at Aldric, at the hollow face and the open eyes. "He was fifteen years old."

Silence. Total, mineral silence. Even the pulse of the Rite-Stone seemed to hold its breath.

"He was a child," Noor said, quieter now, and let the quiet carry, the way her mother had taught her to speak to frightened creatures — low, steady, no false comfort, only truth offered gently to something braced for cruelty. "His father was dying. His generals were feeding him casualty counts with his supper. Men he trusted told him it was the only way, and he believed them, because fifteen-year-olds believe the adults who tell them terrible things are necessary. He came down here weeping. He swore your vow crying. And your law took the tears as sealant and the fear as sovereign intent, and you have been dining on a child's terror for twenty years and calling it statecraft."

She turned back to the Sovereign.

"You bind *wills*. Your own law says so. I've read it in the margins of a map drawn by a woman who knew your rules better than any of you wanted her to. A vow made under coercion of circumstance, by an unsealed minor, in grief and terror, is not sovereign will. It is a wound wearing a crown. And wounds," she said, "*can be closed.*"

The hall erupted.

Not with roars — the deep court did not roar — but with a sound like ten thousand pages tearing, wings snapping open, membranes rippling, voices layering over voices in a shrieking susurrus that made the crystal chandeliers shiver and shed motes of pale light like snow. Noor stood in the middle of it with her heart slamming against her sternum and her hands open at her sides, and she thought, absurdly, *Mother, look at me now.*

Behind her, somewhere near the breached doors, she heard Serevan's boots scrape stone — moving to stand beside her, not behind her. Tam's lute case thumped down on the obsidian. The beat of crow wings, close overhead. Her strange, impossible family, arranging itself around her like a wall made of loyalty instead of stone.

The Sovereign raised one filament-tipped limb, and the hall fell silent so fast it left Noor's ears ringing.

"You argue well," it said. "For a thing that lives seventy years and calls that forever." It shifted, and the pooled shadow gathered itself upward into something more nearly a standing figure, more nearly a face, which was somehow worse. "But you bring us a technicality, little guide, and technicalities are our native tongue. Yes — the law binds wills. Yes — a child's vow is a wound. But the law also says a claim may only be voided by the claimant's consent or a superior binding. The boy cannot consent; his will is already ours, sold and shelved. And you—" the filaments fanned wide, almost gracious, "—have no binding greater than ours."

"Don't I?" Noor said.

And she let her gift off its leash.

It poured out of her the way floodwater pours through a broken dam — twenty-eight years of swallowing it down, of pressing her lips shut in barns and passes and market squares while horses screamed warnings and goats grumbled prophecies and crows recited the secrets of the dead. Here, in the deep places, the flood was oceanic. Every chained creature in the court heard her open her gates. From the dark galleries above came a answering cry — something vast and winged thrashing against bindings older than the war — and from the pits below the floor, a groan of stone and hunger, and all around her the court's own beasts, its drudge-things and gate-wardens and the pale hounds of the outer tunnels, turned their heads toward her as one, and *listened.*

"*Free,*" she said to them, in the speech that has no words. "*Hold. Speak.*"

The Sovereign's veil rippled. For the first time, something in it hesitated.

"Clever," it said softly. "You would set our law against us with our own creatures as witnesses. A binding of testimony. Crude. Dangerous. Possibly valid." The filaments folded inward, one by one, like a hand closing. "We could contest it. We could spend a century doing so, and your prince would be ash and your realm a memory by the end. Or—"

It stopped. And Noor felt the temperature of the entire hall change, the way a room changes when a predator stops stalking and starts savoring.

"Or," the Sovereign said, "we may offer a counter-vow. To *you.*"

"Noor." Serevan's voice, low and urgent at her shoulder. "Don't listen—"

"Daughter of Marisol." The name rolled through the hall, and with it came the crystal light bending, gathering, coalescing before her into a shimmering pane like frozen water — and inside it, an image.

A woman stood in a sunlit kitchen Noor had not seen since she was six years old. Dark hair going silver at the temples. Flour on her forearms. She was laughing at something outside the frame, head thrown back, and the laugh was so vivid Noor could hear it, could feel it in her own chest, could remember — no, could *re-experience* — the exact weight of being lifted into those flour-dusted arms.

Her knees nearly buckled.

"Marisol Quintana," the Sovereign said, each syllable laid down like a coin on a scale. "Not resurrected. Not puppeted. *Restored.* Soul, memory, will, laughter — the full treasury of her, returned from the deep ledgers where we keep such things. Alive. Herself. Standing in your sunlight within a season."

The image held. Her mother turned, in it, as if sensing her daughter watching across impossible distance, and smiled — that specific smile, crooked on the left, that Noor had spent two decades failing to forget and being unable to recall.

"We require only one thing in exchange," the Sovereign continued, silk over stone. "Withdraw your testimony. Release your witnesses. Walk out of this hall and let the rite conclude as sworn. The boy completes his transaction, as his regents intended, as his realm has profited by for twenty years. And you receive the only thing you have ever truly wanted."

The pane brightened. Her mother stepped closer to the surface of it, close enough that Noor could see the small scar on her brow, the gray strand that always escaped her braid, and her mother's mouth opened and she said —

"*Mi cielo.* Come home."

Noor's whole body moved toward it. She couldn't stop it. Twenty years of grief leaned on her like a landslide, and the voice was real — not trickery, not mimicry, she knew the difference, her gift told her plainly that this was her mother's true soul speaking from true keeping — and every animal instinct she had ever owned said *take it, take it, take it.*

Somewhere behind her, Kess made a sound. Not a crow's caw. A small, wounded, human sound.

And Noor thought: *they fed her. They fed a lost exile, and she stayed.*

She thought: *Tam traded his songs for one night of keeping me safe. Serevan burned his own name to stand next to mine. They gave away pieces of themselves, and not one of them asked if I was worth it.*

Her mother's soul was real. Her mother's voice was real. But the hand offering them belonged to the same court that had taken a weeping child's vow and called it sovereignty, that had let Marisol Quintana die in the attempt to undo what they profited from — and Noor understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that her mother had not been captured by these creatures. Her mother had *defied* them. Had come down here, alone, to buy back a stranger's son, and had paid everything, and had never once regretted it — Noor knew that now the way she knew stone underfoot, knew it in the ledger-page of her mother's recovered bargain, knew it in the handwriting on the map.

Her mother had chosen a stranger's child over her own return once already.

What kind of daughter would it make her, to choose otherwise now?

"No," Noor said.

The word cracked across the hall like breaking ice.

"No," she said again, louder, and turned to face the shimmering pane with her eyes streaming and her spine straight. "Mama. Listen to me. I love you. I have loved you every day you were gone and I will love you every day after, whatever happens in this room tonight." Her voice broke, and she let it. "But if I buy you with his soul, then I'm not your daughter anymore. Because you didn't teach me to save what's mine. You taught me to speak for the ones who can't speak for themselves." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, fierce and clumsy. "He can't. He's been trying to say something for twenty years and nobody would listen. So I'm listening. And I'm saying it for him. *Void the claim.*"

The pane trembled. Her mother's image pressed both hands against the inside of it, and her mouth was moving — *no, mi cielo, no, don't you dare* — and even in defiance her mother was proud of her, Noor could see it, could see it shining through the tears on the image's face, and somehow that was the cruelest cut of all.

"Then the offer dies unanswered," the Sovereign said, and there was no silk in it now, only the stone beneath the silk. "And the claim stands, unless you possess a superior binding. Which you do not. Your testimony requires witnesses willing to share the weight of voidance — and voidance, little guide, is not free. Undoing a vow means unmaking its surety. Its price must be paid by those who testify. Memory. Self. Everything the testifier *is*." The filaments spread wide, triumphant. "You stand alone with your clever words. Words are cheap. Who will pay with you?"

And that — that was the moment.

Serevan Ashvale stepped forward, drew his warden's blade, and drove it point-down into the obsidian at Noor's side.

"I deserted the Crown once to follow my conscience," he said, loud enough for the tiers. "I'll pay what it costs. Count me."

Tam shouldered past him, lute abandoned, chin up, hands shaking visibly and voice only slightly less so. "Whatever she's paying, put me down for it. I've got terrible credit anyway."

Kess dropped from the rafters and landed on Noor's shoulder in a flurry of black feathers, and when she spoke, she spoke in the clear, layered voice of the court's own exiles, and every creature in the hall went still to hear it. "Count the crow," she said. "Our laws bind us all."

The Sovereign's veil rippled. The filaments retracted, slowly, one by one, folding down into stillness — and in that stillness Noor heard, very clearly, the sound of a spider discovering that the fly had brought its own web.

"So be it," the Sovereign said. "Then let the voidance begin. Let the weight fall where you have chosen it to fall — and let the Hall of Vow witness whether love is a currency or a delusion."

The Rite-Stone flared white.

Beneath Noor's feet, the obsidian split in a widening seam of light, and the pulse she had felt since entering — the mountain's slow heartbeat — lurched, stuttered, and began to race. Above the dais, the crystal chandeliers blazed and started, one by one, to shatter.

On the Rite-Stone, Prince Aldric turned his hollow eyes toward her and whispered, with the first voice he had used in twenty years:

"What did you just do?"

* * *

The light climbed.

It came up through the fissure in waves, and each wave carried a sound like a page being torn from an enormous book, and with each tear Noor felt something leave her — gently, almost kindly, the way sleep takes a waking child. The smell of rain on Thornfell granite. The name of her favorite pass. The sound of her mother's laugh, going secondhand now, then secondhand to someone else's telling, then gone. She gripped Serevan's arm and felt him gripping hers and understood they were both doing the same arithmetic: how much of ourselves is left, how much is still owed, and neither of them could finish the sum.

The court's law unfurled around them like a storm given legal form — and Noor, sinking slowly to her knees on the splitting stone with a crow clinging to her shoulder and two men holding her hands, had just enough time to wonder whether the Sovereign had known all along that this was the only ending its law permitted—

—and whether it had simply been waiting, all along, for someone stubborn enough to choose it.

Then her mother's face rose before her one final time, smiling crooked on the left, and Noor opened her mouth to say *I'll find you, I'll remember, I promise* —

and forgot, mid-word, who she was promising.
