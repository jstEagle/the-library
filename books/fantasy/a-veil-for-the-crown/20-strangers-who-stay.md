## 20. Strangers Who Stay

### Serevan

The waystation smelled of pine smoke and burnt porridge, and Serevan Ashvale had never in his life been so grateful for the smell of anything.

Noor sat by the hearth with a blanket around her shoulders that she kept pulling tighter, as though the wool were a stranger's arms and she hadn't decided yet whether to permit them. She had been awake for two days now. Two days of asking questions in a voice that was hers and not hers — *Who are you? Why are you here? Whose handwriting is this?* — questions that landed on him like arrows loosed from a bow he'd once sworn to stand in front of.

She did not know his name meant anything to her. She did not know his name at all.

"Serevan," he'd told her, the first morning, when she looked at him with those grey eyes gone empty of everything but wariness. "Serevan Ashvale. I was — " he had stopped there, because *I was your* — would not fit through his throat. "I was a warden," he said instead. "I'm not anymore."

She nodded slowly, the way a person nods at weather. Then: "Was I your prisoner?"

"No," he said, and the word came out cracked down the middle, and she studied him for a long moment before turning back to the fire.

Now he watched her from the doorway of the waystation's little kitchen, where he was failing to make bread. Flour to his elbows. The dough sat under his palms like something surrendered rather than made. He had killed a man eleven days ago — his captain, twenty years of shared campaigns, one stroke of steel in the dark beneath the mountain — and since then his hands had wanted work. They did not know how to want anything else.

"You're kneading it wrong," Noor said behind him.

He turned. She stood in the doorway, blanket still caped over her shoulders, watching his hands with an expression that was not quite recognition but was adjacent to it — a cousin of remembering. His heart performed a small, treacherous leap.

"Is that so?" he said carefully.

"You're strangling it. Bread wants firm hands and a light heart." She frowned, as if surprised by her own words. "I don't know why I said that."

"Maybe you knew someone who baked."

"Maybe." She came closer, hesitated, then took the dough from him with hands still scarred from rope-work he could have described in his sleep. Her fingers moved without hesitation — fold, turn, press — and Serevan watched her body remember what her mind had released and felt something in his chest tear quietly along a seam.

"There," she said. "Like that."

"Thank you." He meant more than the bread. She couldn't know that. That was the whole unbearable architecture of it: every kindness he owed her, every debt of love, and no way to pay it that didn't sound like the ravings of a madman. *You walked into a court of living shadows for me. You gave up my face from your memory so a prince could have his soul.* How did you say that to a woman who was trying to decide whether to trust you with the porridge?

She wiped her hands on her apron — his apron, technically; hers were ash somewhere under Thornfell — and looked at him sideways.

"You watch me," she said. "When you think I won't notice. Like you're waiting for me to break, or vanish."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just tell me what you're waiting for."

He thought about lying. He had been trained in lying; it was half of warden-craft. But her eyes held his the way they used to hold his across campfires, and the lie died before it drew breath.

"For you to remember," he said. "Or — if you don't — for you to decide you don't need to, and stay anyway."

The dough rose between them in its bowl, patient as neither of them felt. Noor looked at him for a long time.

"I don't know you," she said at last, not unkindly. "But when you say things like that, something behind my ribs sits up. Like a dog hearing its name in another room." She turned toward the hearth-room. "It's very strange, warden. You should stop doing it or start explaining it."

Then she was gone, and Serevan stood in the flour-dusted kitchen with his useless hands and his whole heart, laughing at nothing, until the laugh turned into something else entirely and he let it, alone, where no one could see a disgraced warden weep into bread.

### Tam

Tam Vessic had played for duchesses and cutthroats, sometimes in the same evening, and he knew exactly how to read a room. This room — this smoky little waystation full of ghosts wearing familiar faces — was the hardest booking of his career.

Because his lute was empty.

Not literally. The instrument hung on his back same as ever, strings bright, wood oiled. But the songs — his songs, the ones he'd traded to a memory-vial in Duskmarket to buy the company one night of concealment, the ones that had lived in his fingers since he was fourteen and poor and furious — were gone. When he reached for *The Cartwright's Daughter*, he found a hole shaped like it. When he tried to hum *Winter, Willingly*, nothing came. He owned a hundred borrowed ballads now, other men's griefs, and not one note of his own.

And Noor — who had written half of them with him, who had sung harmony in passes where singing could kill you — didn't miss them at all. She couldn't. She didn't know they existed.

So Tam did what Tam did. He filled the silence anyway.

Every evening after supper he played for her. Old songs, safe songs, drinking songs and shepherd tunes and the long slow mountain laments that everyone in Thornfell absorbed with their mother's milk. And every evening he watched her face while he played, because here was the thing, here was the miracle he clung to like a rope over a drop:

Her foot tapped. Her breath caught at the sad turns. Once — only once — she hummed a phrase back at him, three notes rising, and Tam nearly dropped the instrument, because those three notes were the opening of a song called *Quintana's Road* that she had written for him two years ago in exchange for a pair of dry socks, and she did not know it, and her mouth knew it anyway.

"What?" she said, catching his stare. "Did I do it wrong?"

"No," Tam said, around the thing in his throat. "Nobody could do it wrong. It's — it's an old tune. Nobody knows who wrote it."

"That's sad," Noor said. "Someone should get credit."

"I'll tell them you said so."

He began, that night, to write her a new song. It came out crooked and plain because his best melodies had been sold and he was working with whatever scraps his heart still owned, but it came. He wrote it on the backs of kindling bundles and the flyleaf of Serevan's old campaign psalter, which the warden pretended not to notice being vandalized. It had no title yet. It was about a woman who guided people through the dark and thought that keeping them alive was the same as loving them, until she learned better, all at once, at terrible cost, and forgot, and was loved anyway.

He wasn't going to tell her that last part. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Some songs you play to people; some you keep tuned and ready for years, waiting for the night they can finally be heard.

On the ninth evening, Noor asked, "Can you teach me?"

"Teach you?"

"To play. Or sing, at least. I seem to know how songs go even if I don't know any." She said it lightly, but her hands were knotted in her blanket, and Tam understood — oh, he understood like a scholar — that she was hunting herself in every corner of this life, and music was a corner she hadn't checked.

"It's the worst bargain in the world," he said gravely, tuning a peg. "You'll be terrible for months. Your fingers will hate you. Everyone within earshot will file complaints."

"Sold," said Noor.

He put the lute in her lap and folded her fingers around the neck of it, and if his hands shook a little, well. Minstrels were permitted trembling. It came with the trade.

"Now," he said. "This chord is called G. It is the friendliest chord in existence. It has never hurt anyone. Press here — gentler, gentler, you're strangling it — there. Like bread."

"Like bread," Noor echoed, frowning, and strummed, and produced a sound like a door falling off a cart, and laughed — actually laughed, the first one Serevan dropped a spoon over, the first one Kess sidled closer for — and Tam Vessic sat in the smoke-warm room and thought: *they can take the vials and the verses and the names. They cannot take this. Whatever this is, it doesn't live in memory.*

### Kess

The crow considered herself a patient creature. Eternity taught patience. Still, there were limits.

The woman had forgotten her. Forgotten her name, forgotten the way she said *Kess, you menace* while hiding cheese in her sleeve, forgotten eleven years of shared roads and shared silences and the particular quality of Noor's breathing when she was pretending to sleep while a storm argued with the pass. Forgotten that Kess had once been something courtly and cold and winged in other ways, and had chosen — *chosen*, freely, the first free thing she had ever done — to stay.

Kess had watched the rite shatter. Had seen the solstice break apart like ice in spring thaw, had felt her mother-court recoil beneath the stone. And she had carried Noor's body out of the shaking dark herself, feather to skin, while the mountain screamed and the prince woke weeping on the floor of both courts at once. She did not mention this. Crows did not bill themselves.

Instead she did her work.

She brought Noor things. A blue river-stone worn with a hole, set on the windowsill without comment. A sprig of rowan from the high pass, laid across her bowl. A single crowfeather, black and silvered, tucked under the strap of the pack Noor no longer remembered owning. Small archaeology. Seeds planted in a field that had been burned clean.

Noor noticed, of course. Noor-who-forgot still had Noor's eyes.

"Why does the crow keep giving me things?" she asked the room at large, on the eleventh day, holding the river-stone up to the light.

"Gifts mean loyalty," Serevan said from his corner, mending a strap. "In crow terms."

"And whose loyalty is it? Yours? Mine?"

Neither of them answered, because none of them knew — whether a bond survived when one end of it was emptied out, whether a crow's devotion could land on ground that no longer remembered receiving it. Kess hopped along the table's edge and looked at Noor directly, one amber eye to one grey eye, and put every gram of eleven years into the look.

Noor looked back.

"I know you," she said slowly. "Not — not your name, not where from. But when you look at me like that, my chest goes tight, like there's a word stuck sideways in it." She extended her hand, flat, palm up — an old gesture, an offering of perch, done without knowing she had ever learned it. "Say your name again. All of it."

"*Kess,*" said the crow. And then, because she had never once in her long strange life managed to be only what was expected: "*Yours.*"

The room went very quiet. Tam's hand froze on the strings. Serevan set down the strap.

Noor's fingers curled slowly closed around nothing, around the shape of a weight that should have been there. "That's a strange word for a bird to know," she whispered.

"I am a strange bird," said Kess, and stepped onto her wrist, claws gentle as an apology, and began — softly, in the creaking voice humans found unsettling and Noor had once found hilarious — to preen the hair back from Noor's temple the way she had done ten thousand times on ten thousand cold mornings.

Noor did not pull away. She sat very still, tears standing unshed in her eyes, and said, "I don't remember you. But I think I must have loved you. Things that aren't loved don't ache like this."

"*Stay,*" said Kess, which meant, in crow, everything else.

### Noor

They would not leave.

She had told them to, twice. Politely the first time — *you've done enough, you've more than done enough, I can't repay strangers for this much care* — and less politely the second, when the politeness failed and the fear underneath it showed through, fear of owing what she could not account, fear of being a stranger in her own debt.

Serevan had taken the second telling standing very straight, the way soldiers took bad news, and had said only: "We're not here to be repaid."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because the road's bad this season," said Tam, too fast, grinning his minstrel's grin.

"Because the roof leaks elsewhere," said the crow.

"Because," Serevan started, and stopped, and looked at her with those grey eyes that made something behind her ribs sit up like a dog hearing its name in another room, and finished, quietly, "because we said we'd see you home."

She could find no crack in it. Three impossible people — a warden with outlaw's eyes and flour on his sleeves, a minstrel who flinched at his own reflection in the polished pot but played every night like the world depended on it, a crow who spoke and gave gifts and slept curled against her neck like a coal of warmth — and none of them would leave her alone with her own emptiness.

So she stayed too. She learned bread. She learned G and its treacherous cousins. She learned that the warden's tea needed honey and he would deny it; that the minstrel hummed in his sleep, always the same three rising notes; that the crow watched over her like a sentry and lied beautifully about it.

And at night, sometimes, the mountain spoke.

Not in words she understood — she kept checking, pressing her palm to the stones of the waystation wall, listening for whatever it was these people clearly believed she had once heard — but in a low continuous murmur, like a hall full of guests speaking just below the threshold of sense. Goats on the ridge above the station paused their grazing and stared at her through the window with wet amber eyes, and did not run away when she approached. A fox sat in the yard one dawn and watched her for an entire hour, head tilted, as though waiting for her to finish a sentence.

"They do that," Serevan said carefully, when she mentioned it. "Around you. They always did."

"*Did*," Noor repeated. "You keep saying that. Did. Always did. Were." She hugged her knees on the bench outside, the fox's tracks printing the frost below, the crow warm on her shoulder, the warden's shadow falling long beside her in the early light. "Tell me again. The story. From the beginning — the storm, the pass, the courier. Tell me the parts you always skip, too. I can tell you skip parts. Your voice goes careful."

Serevan was quiet for a while. Somewhere under the earth, faint as a pulse, the mountain murmured on.

"The parts I skip," he said at last, "are the parts about you. What you did. What you gave." His jaw worked. "I keep thinking there's a right order to give them to you in. That if I find it, they'll come back."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I'll tell you new ones," he said simply. "Better ones. We'll fill you up with those instead."

Below them, at the tree line, the fox reappeared — and it was not alone. Along the frost-rimed edge of the pines, still and patient as a congregation, stood goats and a marten, two ravens that were not-Kess, a lean grey wolf, and a hind with her fawn, every eye fixed on the waystation, on Noor, on her hands, as though they were listening for a voice the rest of the world could not hear.

The crow went rigid on her shoulder.

"Noor," Kess said, and her voice had a shake in it that Noor had never heard there before. "Ask them what they want."

Noor looked out at the assembled dark eyes of the mountain. Her chest ached, that sideways-word ache, that name-in-another-room ache — and when she opened her mouth, not knowing what she meant to say, what came out was not any language she owned.

It was a low, ringing note, animal and glad, and every creature at the tree line answered at once.
