## 22. A Veil Lifted

The first snow of the season came down the mountain in long silver veils, and Noor Quintana walked out into it with her hands bare and her voice open.

That was the miracle she still tested each morning, the way a tongue returns to the gap where a tooth was: that she could stand before strangers and speak to the world's small listening things without shame. A mule train waited at the waystation gate, six traders from the lowlands with frost in their beards and suspicion in their eyes, and when their lead mule laid its ears back at the narrow bridge ahead — *ice, ice, ice under the snow* — Noor did not pretend she hadn't heard.

"The bridge is iced," she said aloud. "We'll rope the loads and go single file."

The traders exchanged glances. One of them, an old woman with a warden's faded brand half-scraped from her wrist, said quietly, "You speak it out loud now."

"I do." Noor smiled, and felt the smile reach all the way down to whatever place inside her had once been a locked room. "It's in the accord. Beast-speech is a trade on Thornfell now, like smithing or midwifery. I'm licensed by the Crown." She paused. "And by the mountain. The mountain's paperwork is more complicated."

The old woman laughed, short and disbelieving, and after that the crossing went easily. The mules trusted Noor; the traders learned to. By the time they came down into the valley where the waystation's chimney smoke stood up straight against the grey sky, they were telling her about their daughters, their debts, their dead — the way people talk to someone who has listened to the world for them all day. She took their coin at the door and their stories at the fire, and both felt honest.

Months. It had been months since she had woken on a pallet in this same room with no memory of the faces bent over her, and the strangeness of it had worn smooth as river stone. She knew herself only as a series of facts now, told to her by others the way you'd tell a traveler about a road: *You are Noor Quintana. You saved a prince. You lost everything you remembered to pay for it.* And then, softer, told less often because it hurt less often now: *But you built something here anyway.*

She lit the lamps. She counted the stores. She wrote in the ledger in a hand that was, everyone agreed, exactly the hand her mother must have had — which was one of the few gifts the deep court had returned along with her mother's recovered bargain, sealed in a vial of dark glass that sat on the mantel like an urn. Sometimes she held it and felt nothing, which was its own kind of grief. Sometimes she held it and heard, faintly, water singing names, and then she set it down and went to feed the goats.

* * *

Kess arrived first, as Kess always arrived first, dropping through the doorway snow with a shake and an air of having been there all along.

"You're late," Noor told her.

*I am precisely on time,* said the crow, in the layered voice that was not quite speech and not quite thought, that had taken Noor weeks after waking to stop flinching from. *Time bends around my dignity.*

"Your dignity is soaked in snow."

*My dignity is waterproof.* Kess hopped onto the ledger desk, considered the ink pot with criminal intent, and thought better of it — mostly. She had not left since the solstice. Noor understood why, though she could not remember being given the reason: a court exile who had finally found creatures worth watching did not fly home. "Home" had been the problem in the first place.

"You know they're coming tonight," Noor said. "Both of them. Serevan sent word from Duskmarket."

*I know,* Kess said. *I have been composing my greeting for three days.*

"It's going to be 'caw,' isn't it."

*It is going to be devastating,* Kess said, and flew up to her beam above the hearth, where a nest of stolen ribbon and one royal warden's epaulette marked the exact spot where loyalty had decided to roost permanently.

Noor banked the fire and tried not to count the minutes. She was bad at it. She had been bad at it since autumn, since the night Serevan Ashvale had come back from escorting Aldric's envoys to the lowland capitals and stood in this doorway with snow in his dark hair, and she had looked at him — a stranger, a tall stranger with careful hands and a scar across his jaw — and her heart had done something entirely unauthorized.

They had told her the story since, in fragments, each of them keeping a piece so no single person carried the whole weight: Tam singing the ballad version, which was ninety percent invented and somehow emotionally accurate; Serevan giving the plain soldier's account, dates and passes and the moment he turned his blade on his own captain; Kess providing corrections no one asked for. And always, woven through every telling, the thing they never said outright but which lived in the pauses like a coal under ash:

*You loved him. You love him still, somewhere below the waterline where the court's price cut. He loves you. Neither of you has made it strange for you by saying it too hard.*

So instead he brought her honey from Duskmarket and she mended his cloak and they argued about bridge tolls and both of them pretended that the space between their shoulders at the hearth was not exactly the width of a leaning person.

Footsteps crunched in the yard — two sets, uneven, one of them limping cheerfully.

* * *

Tam Vess came through the door like weather.

"Noor!" He shed snow, satchel, and lute case in three directions. "You will not believe what the lowlands have done to my ballad. They've given it *verses*, Noor. Verses I didn't write. There's a verse where I slay a drake—"

"You did not slay a drake."

"There's a verse where I *slay a drake*, and the Duskmarket garrison sings it every payday, and I have decided that correcting it would be an act of vandalism against art." He grinned, and the grin had changed in the months since — or perhaps she was seeing it fresh, without the old weight of knowing him. It was a good grin. It was the grin of a man who had traded his songs to buy his friends one safe night, and gotten most of them back note by note from the memory-vial, and kept the one song the vial couldn't hold because it had never been written down: the sound of his friends laughing around a fire while the mountain shook.

Behind him came Serevan, stamping the snow off his boots with the thoroughness of a man buying himself three extra seconds.

He was broader than winter allowed anyone to look graceful, grey-eyed, quiet in the way of people who had once been very loud inside their own duty and had emptied that room out. He wore no warden grey anymore. He wore a guide's oiled coat with a Crown accord-pin at the collar — the new kind, the kind Aldric had struck himself, beast-speech and warden-law reforged together — and when his eyes found Noor across the firelit room, the whole conversation of his face changed.

"There she is," Tam announced, oblivious, unwrapping cheese. "The famous veil-lifter herself."

"Hello, Serevan," Noor said, and hated how carefully casual it came out.

"Noor." His voice did the thing it did — low, even, and underneath it, faintly, a tremor like a struck string still ringing. He crossed the room and stopped at the polite distance and offered her the cloth-wrapped bundle he'd been carrying. "Honey. From Marta's hives. And—" he hesitated, then produced a second, smaller bundle, "—a book. Found it in a Duskmarket stall. It's about mountain birds. I thought... you might like it. Or not. It's a book."

"A book," Noor repeated gravely. "For me. Who lives on a mountain."

"I panic in markets," Serevan said.

Above the hearth, Kess descended like judgment.

*Caw,* she said, devastatingly.

Tam howled with laughter. Serevan flinched on principle. And Noor — standing in the waystation where a dying courier had once pressed a blood-marked map into her hands, surrounded by the family she had bought with her own memories and who had refused, absolutely refused, to let the payment leave them orphaned — Noor laughed until her ribs ached, and thought: *I don't remember choosing these people. But I choose them again. Every day. That counts. That has to count.*

* * *

Later, when Tam had fallen asleep mid-anecdote with his boots on and Kess had claimed the best corner of the hearth, Noor and Serevan ended up outside, as they always ended up outside, checking the goat pen in the dark because neither of them would admit they were avoiding going to bed in rooms at opposite ends of the same building.

Snow fell soft. The goats regarded them with ancient contempt. Beyond the fence, Thornfell rose black against the stars, and somewhere beneath its roots the deep court slept behind doors of bone and salt, its claim voided, its hunger leashed by a treaty written in Noor's mother's cipher and signed by a prince who had come back from hollow with his soul restored and his eyes, witnesses said, wet for a week.

"Do you ever miss it?" Serevan asked suddenly. "Not the memories. Just... the shape of them. Knowing your own story."

Noor considered the question honestly, because honesty was another thing she practiced daily now, like scales on an instrument.

"Every morning," she said. "And then I feed the goats, and Tam plays something he swears I wrote, and Kess steals my ribbon, and you bring me honey like a man courting a ghost—" she saw him start, and pressed on before either of them could retreat, "—and I think: whoever I was, she spent her whole life hiding. Locked gift, closed heart, one rule: never let anyone close enough to burn. And then she broke every rule she had, for a boy she'd never met and three people she met in the worst week of her life. That woman sounds exhausting, Serevan. I don't miss her. I'm trying to *be worthy* of her."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked on the pen rails. Somewhere upslope, a rockslide-prone goat spirit murmured drowsily in its sleep — *steady, steady, steady* — and Noor answered it without thinking, a low wordless hum, the way you'd hum to a child.

"She loved you," Serevan said quietly.

"Don't."

"She loved you. I watched it happen. I watched you fight it for two hundred miles and lose gracefully and then lose badly and then stop fighting at all, and it was—" his breath fogged, caught, restarted, "—it was the bravest thing I saw in the whole war's worth of brave things I've seen. Braver than the rite. So I won't pretend otherwise just because it's easier for you. You deserve the truth even if you can't hold it yet. *Especially* if you can't hold it yet."

"Serevan—"

"And I'm not asking anything," he said. "I want that clear. I'm not waiting for you to become someone who remembers me. I'm—" the careful soldier's cadence cracked, and what came through was just a man, cold and hopeful and tired of being careful, "—I'm asking if I can stop pretending the width of a hearth between us means anything except cowardice. Because I've been a coward about one thing my whole life and it isn't the crown, and I'm tired."

Noor looked at him. Snow in his hair again, the same as the night she'd first seen him — no, the first time *she'd* seen him, which was different from the first time, which she did not remember but which Kess described, mercilessly, as *"the longest anyone has stared at a map."*

Here was the truth she had been building toward for months, the one the beasts had known all along because beasts do not complicate things: she did not remember falling in love with him. But love, she had learned from watching these three stubborn people refuse to leave her, was not an archive. It was not a vault to be robbed or a debt to be collected. It was a fire that had to be fed daily, and she had hands, and there was wood right there.

So she stepped across the distance — the whole width of the hearth, all of it, cowardice and caution and courtesy together — and took his face in her bare hands, and kissed him under the falling snow while the goats watched with vindication.

When she pulled back, his breath had gone entirely ragged, which pleased her disproportionately.

"For the record," she said, "I'm falling again. Right now. On purpose. And when I fall the second time, I expect you to catch me the way you apparently caught me the first time, which according to Tam involved a drake—"

"There was no drake."

"—a *drake*, Serevan, and also a bridge collapse and a declaration of love delivered during a landslide, which is extremely on-brand for both of us and I resent not remembering it."

His laugh came out of him like something unlocking. He pulled her in against his chest, snow and all, and above them on the waystation roof a crow began — loudly, tunelessly, with tremendous feeling — to sing the ballad of the veil-lifted queen, including the verse with the drake.

Inside, Tam woke briefly, heard Kess murdering his melody, and smiled into his collar without opening his eyes. The fire burned low and steady. On the mantel, the dark vial of her mother's bargain caught the light, and if you had been listening — really listening, the way Noor could listen, the way she was teaching the valley to listen — you might have heard, from far beneath the mountain, the weeping stair singing its names.

One of the names was new. It was hers. It sounded, the water seemed to think, like a beginning.

Far away in the palace at Cairnlow, Prince Aldric woke from a dream of snow and laughter, wrote a letter that began *Dear friends at the waystation — I've finished the accord's last page, and I find I have a question that requires a crow's opinion* — and smiled at the window, toward the mountains, toward home.
