## 1. The Last Tollbooth

The snow came down over Thornfell like ash from a fire no one had put out.

Ines Oyelaran crouched at the lip of the pass with her hood drawn low and her breath steaming through the wool wrapped over her mouth, and she counted the lanterns below her the way other people counted their sins: carefully, and with a certain grim familiarity. Seven of them this time, strung along the switchback road like a rosary of yellow eyes. Crown patrols had never bothered with the high passes before—not in her mother's day, not even five years ago—but the Ashcarts had changed that. The Ashcarts changed everything they touched, and they touched everything with a burning hand.

Seven lanterns meant three men apiece, if the Crown was paying its soldiers properly, which it wasn't. So call it eighteen or twenty, spread thin along a road that wound four miles down into the valley, and somewhere among them, if the Guild's information still held any truth at all, a wagon carrying six families out of the river provinces before the winter levies came to take their sons.

Six families. Once—Maren said once—the Veilwrights had moved whole villages. Once they had carried kings' daughters and cobblers' sons across borders that did not officially exist, through doors that were not doors, and the roads had sung under their feet. Now Ines squatted in the dark counting lanterns for six families and one mule cart, and her knees ached, and the snow kept falling like something being buried.

She checked the waymark at her left hand without touching it. A casual eye would have seen only a lichen-crusted boulder leaning against its neighbor, the kind of stone every mountain grows by the thousand. But the lean of it mattered. The angle of the crack running down its face mattered. The way the snow sat on it—thicker on the north shoulder, as though the stone itself turned slightly away from the wind—mattered more than anything. *The road remembers*, her first master used to say, tapping his pipe against such stones until they rang. *Your job is only to remember it back.*

Master Oduya had been dead eleven years now. The Crown had burned him in a market square in Lowmarsh with a paper pinned to his coat listing his crimes, and half of them weren't even true. The true ones would have been enough.

Ines waited for the patrol lanterns to make their turn at the third switchback—that took them eleven minutes, always eleven minutes, because soldiers walked to a drumbeat even when there was no drum—and then she moved.

* * *

The smuggler's road was not a road. That was the first thing they taught you, and the last thing you ever truly learned. It was a permission. The mountain permitted you here, along this shelf of scree where the rockfall had made a stair of broken slabs; permitted you there, beneath the overhang where the ice grew in curtains too thin to bear a goat but somehow bearing a woman with a pack; permitted you through the notch in the ridge that looked, from ten feet away, like solid wall. Every step of it was written down nowhere. Every step of it lived in the bodies of people who had walked it, and in the songs they sang while walking, because a song is harder to torture out of a man than a map, and easier to carry.

Ines sang under her breath as she went, an old counting-song about a miller's daughter and seven black birds, and the nonsense syllables in the third verse—which every child in the river provinces thought were just nonsense—told her exactly where the shelf narrowed and where she needed to swing her weight left. Her boots found the holds the way a tongue finds its own teeth. She had been walking these passes since she was small enough to ride in a smuggling pack herself, back when the Guild still had the numbers to run children through in relays, passing them hand to hand like buckets at a fire.

The pack on her back held medicine, mostly. Willowbark and feverfew, a jar of honey, needles and gut thread, a phial of poppy syrup worth more than the mule pulling the families' cart. The Veilwrights didn't move contraband anymore. There was no profit in it worth the risk, and besides, the Guild had decided long ago—if a brotherhood could be said to decide anything anymore, three old men and two old women and Ines, who was neither old nor a man nor, some nights, entirely sure she was still a member of anything—what remained of it had decided that if they were going to die for their roads, they would die carrying something that mattered.

The snow thickened as she climbed. By the time she reached the shepherd's hut at Cold Saddle, the world had shrunk to the size of a blanket, and inside the hut it shrank further, to the orange circle of her shuttered lamp and the faces waiting for her in it.

There were nine of them in a hut built for four. A grandmother with hands like knotted rope. Two brothers who might have been twins except that one had a scar splitting his eyebrow. A girl of perhaps twelve holding a baby that was not hers and not crying, which frightened Ines more than crying would have. They looked up when she came in with the particular look of people who have been waiting so long they've stopped hoping and started simply enduring, and when she said, "Road's clear till dawn, maybe," the grandmother closed her eyes and mouthed something that might have been a prayer or might have been a curse on the Crown, and Ines honestly couldn't have told the difference.

"You're the last one?" the scarred brother asked. "They said there'd be others."

"There are no others." Ines set down her pack and began dividing the medicine into doses with the economy of long practice. "There's me. There's the road. Between the two of us, we'll manage."

It sounded better than it felt. It usually did.

* * *

They moved at first light, or what passed for it—a gray brightening behind the clouds like a bruise coming up. Ines led them up out of Cold Saddle along the shelf, singing her miller's song, and the families walked as quietly as frightened people can, which is to say not very, and the baby slept against the girl's chest like a stone in a sling.

Twice they heard the patrol below them, voices carrying strangely in the snow-hush, and twice Ines pressed them flat against the rock and waited with her heart going like a smith's hammer. The second time, a soldier laughed at something, a young easy laugh, and Ines found herself hating him with a clean and simple heat that surprised her. He was probably somebody's son. He was probably marching those passes because the Crown told him to, the same way she walked them because the Guild told her to, and the difference between them was only this: what each of them had been taught the roads were *for*.

His masters said the roads were for keeping people in.

Hers had said the opposite.

By midday they reached the hollow under Hanging Rock where the descent route split—one branch dropping toward the ferry at Graywater, the other winding east toward the coast and the fishing boats that no longer asked questions. The families were for Graywater. From there, a cousin of the Guild named Pell ran them downstream in a coal barge, past the customs house, into whatever life the river would give them.

"Will we see you again?" the grandmother asked, gripping Ines's wrist with rope-knuckled strength. "When it's over. When we're settled."

Ines looked at her honestly, because the woman had earned honesty. "I don't know what 'when it's over' looks like yet," she said. "But if you light a candle in a window anywhere south of the river, I'll find my way to it. That's a promise the Guild can keep."

"The Guild." The grandmother smiled with few teeth. "My mother talked about the Veilwrights. Said they could walk through walls. Said they carried her own sister over the mountains when the plague came, through a door that wasn't there, into a country that—" She stopped. Frowned, as if reaching for a word and finding the shelf empty. "Into somewhere safe," she finished, and the sentence felt smaller than the one she'd started.

"Somewhere safe is all any of us are carrying," Ines said, and helped her down onto the path.

She watched them go until the snow blurred them out, the little procession of nine descending toward the river like beads slipping off a cut string. Then she stood alone under Hanging Rock with the wind worrying at her hood, and she let herself feel it, briefly, the thing she did not let herself feel in front of clients: the weight of the arithmetic. Six families, once, had been a season's work for a single cell of the Guild. Now it was most of a year's work for all of them, and the Crown's net drew tighter every season, and the old members died or scattered or—her jaw tightened—worse.

She spat into the snow and started home.

* * *

The guildhall stood in a fold of the hills above the village of Netherby, disguised as nothing at all, which was the best disguise there was. To a passing eye it was a drover's inn long abandoned, slate roof furred with moss, sign bracket empty, windows dark. The Crown had catalogued it years ago as worthless and moved on, and there was a bitter joke in that—that the surest safety in the kingdom was to become the sort of thing the kingdom couldn't be bothered to burn.

Ines came in through the cellar, as always, ducking under the beam Master Oduya had cracked with his skull in some long-ago night of drink, a chip in the wood he'd refused ever to mend. *Character*, he'd called it. The cellar smelled of tallow and cold stone and, faintly, of the lamp oil they hoarded like dragons. She climbed the stairs into the main hall, already composing her report—road clear, families delivered, Pell expecting them at Graywater, the eastern passes will close within the fortnight—

And stopped.

The hall was wrong. She knew it before she knew why, the way you know a room has been searched. Then she saw it plainly: the long table where the chapter met, bare. The map wall, bare—every chart taken down or fallen, she couldn't tell which. The hearth swept clean. And the benches, which should have held whatever remained of them at this hour, the evening meal, the arguing, the terrible singing—

Empty. All but one.

Maren sat at the far end of the table with her hands folded around a cup of something steaming, a small straight-backed woman of seventy-odd years with white hair cropped short as a soldier's and eyes that had watched too much and forgiven too little. Of them all, only Maren had been born inside the Guild's great days. Only Maren remembered what the table had looked like full.

"They're gone, then," Ines said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "All of them."

"Hesta went to her daughter in Vell, week past. Tam and the brothers Roke took the coastal work and won't come back inland, not after the Ashcarts took Ferro." Maren said the names the way you read a casualty list you've memorized. "Osk is dead. You knew that."

"I knew." Ines hung her wet cloak on its peg—her peg, the only claim she had on anything—and crossed to the hearth to feed the ashes into something like a fire. Her hands were steady. She noticed that, and was oddly proud of it. "So it's you and me and a hall full of ghosts."

"And the bookkeeping," Maren said dryly. "Don't forget the bookkeeping. We're rich in debts owed to us by dead men."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. She crouched at the hearth and coaxed the flame up, and the light climbed the walls and found all the pale rectangles where maps had hung, ghost-outlines of the Guild's body laid out like chalk marks. She had grown up under those maps. She had learned her letters tracing the river routes with a finger Oduya guided. Every one of them gone now, and not because they'd been sold or lost—because Maren had taken them down and burned them, page by page, two winters ago, rather than let the Ashcarts find them. Ines had helped. She had fed the fire herself. She had told herself it was strategy, and it was, and it had also felt like standing at her mother's grave.

"Pell will have the families by tomorrow night," she said, because someone had to say something true and forward-facing. "Six households, delivered whole. Through the passes, in the first snow, with a patrol on the road and no one watching our backs." She looked over her shoulder at the old woman. "That's not nothing, Maren. Whatever the hall looks like. That's the craft, still breathing."

Maren regarded her for a long moment over the rim of her cup. Steam curled between them.

"No," the old woman said at last. "It isn't nothing. It's the last toll collected at a tollbooth whose road nobody travels anymore." She set the cup down with a small precise click. "I ran my first crossing at nineteen, girl. Forty souls in a single night, through the Fen Doors, and every one of them alive at the end of it, and we sang afterward until the rafters shook. Do you know what we ferried tonight? Nine people and a jar of honey." Her voice didn't break. It just went quiet, the way deep water is quiet. "I'm proud of you. Hear me. Proud as I'd be of my own blood. And I am telling you that what we did tonight was the last echo of a bell that's already cracked."

Ines turned back to the fire. The flame had caught properly now, throwing her shadow huge and wavering up the wall, across the pale rectangles where the maps had been.

"Then we'll ring it anyway," she said. "Cracked bells are louder. Everyone knows that."

Behind her, Maren made a sound that was half laugh and half something older and sadder, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the storm outside leaned its shoulder against the shutters, and the guildhall held its two remaining hearts the way an empty church holds candles—carefully, and without much hope, and faithfully all the same.

It was near midnight, Ines halfway up the stairs to the loft with a candle guttering in her fist, when the knocking came.

Three blows on the hall's front door—heavy, arrhythmic, wrong. Not the smugglers' knock, not any knock the Guild had ever taught. And beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, a sound that raised every hair on Ines's arms: a voice, high and thin, trying to speak through the door in words she did not know.

Words, she would learn soon enough, that no living tongue was supposed to know at all.
