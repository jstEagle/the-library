## 2. A Child at the Door

The snow came in from the west like a debt collector, patient and thorough.

Ines felt it in her teeth first—the old break in her jaw aching the way it always did before weather turned ugly—and then in the timbers of the guildhall, which groaned under the weight of the sky like an old man being asked to carry what he'd carried all his life and could no longer afford to set down. She stood at the long window above the common room, watching Thornfell disappear into white, and counted the things she knew for certain. The pass would close within two days, maybe three. The Ashcart patrols had stopped riding the low road a week ago, which meant they were waiting somewhere, coiled. And there were eleven people left in the world who called themselves Veilwrights, and she had just spent the better part of a month running one last job through the passes to feed them through the winter, and she was tired down to the marrow of tired.

Eleven. When she had been a girl, there had been sixty. There had been tables that groaned with food and voices, songs sung so loud the rafters sweated, masters teaching apprentices in the yard—how to read a ridge-line, how to walk a road backwards out of your own footprints, how to lie with your whole face until even you believed it. Now the great hall held four bodies on any given night, and the rest of the guildhall stood like a drowned ship: rooms sealed against the cold, dust sheets over the furniture like burial shrouds, the guild's sigil—a bridge with no river beneath it—faded on the banner above the hearth until it looked less like an emblem and more like a wound that had never quite closed.

"Ines." Corvin's voice from the stair, gravelled by age and cheap tobacco. "You should eat."

"I ate on the road."

"You ate half a heel of bread three days ago. I know because I baked it, and I know how much I baked." He came into the room carrying a bowl of barley stew, steam curling off it, and set it on the sill beside her with the particular gentleness he only ever showed when he thought she was too worn down to argue. He was getting old. That was the thing she kept forgetting and then re-seeing, every time: his hands had gone knuckled and spotted, his shoulders stooped as though he were perpetually ducking under something. Once he had been the fastest rope-man in the Guild, the one who could cross a chasm on a wet line with a child strapped to his back and make it look like dancing. Now he moved like a man crossing ice he no longer trusted.

"Corvin," she said, without turning from the window. "Where did we go wrong? Not the Crown. Not the burning. Before that. Where did *we* go wrong?"

He was quiet a while. Outside, snow filled the air so thickly that the mountains ceased to exist; the whole world had contracted to this hall, this firelight, this glass going grey with frost.

"Wrong is a big word," he said finally. "We got small. Small enough to fit inside our fear. That's not the same thing, but it rhymes."

She almost smiled. Almost. "That's very wise. Did you steal it from someone?"

"From Maren. Everything wise I own is stolen from Maren." He touched her shoulder once, briefly, the way you'd touch a doorframe for luck, and went back downstairs, leaving the stew steaming its little ghost into the cold air.

She drank it eventually. It was good. It was always good; Corvin cooked the way he did everything else, with more care than he admitted to. And when she finished she took up the lamp and made her rounds, because that was what a Veilwright did—walked the roads, walked the halls, checked the seals and the sills and the caches, kept the craft alive by keeping its habits alive even when there was nothing left to smuggle but firewood and flour.

* * *

It was on the second round, near midnight, that she heard the knock.

Three strikes, spaced wrong—too far apart, the rhythm of someone who had forgotten how knocking worked, or who was doing it with the last of their strength. Ines froze in the corridor outside the common room, hand going to the knife at her hip out of habit rather than decision. Nobody knocked at the Veilwright guildhall. Smugglers used the signs: the chalk marks on the gatepost, the triple whistle, the stone dropped down the old well-shaft where it rang off the hollow below like a bell. Knocking was for strangers, and strangers at the guildhall's door in the dead of winter meant one of two things: a lost traveller, or a trap.

She stood in the dark and listened. The wind pressed at the shutters. The fire in the common room ticked and settled.

Then, faintly, beneath the wind: a sound she could not name at first. High, thin, wavering. Singing—or trying to sing. A scrap of melody repeated over and over, breathless, breaking, picked up again each time it fell. A song with no words she recognized, in a voice so small it might have been the wind itself playing tricks.

Ines unbarred the door.

Cold hit her like a wall of water. Snow came sideways through the gap, and the lamp flame bent and guttered, and for one heartbeat she saw nothing at all—just whiteness rushing in, the night pouring through her doorway like a tide. Then the flame steadied, and she looked down.

A child stood on the threshold. Half-frozen, snow-crusted, lips gone the blue-grey of deep water. Maybe seven years old; maybe younger; hard to tell, because the child was so thin the years seemed to have blown away like the rest of them. Bare feet, or nearly bare—wraps of something rotted and stiff around them. No coat. No pack. Nothing in those small fists but a smooth grey stone, clutched so tightly the knuckles had gone bloodless.

And the child was not looking at her. The child was looking past her, into the hall, into the firelight, with an expression Ines had seen once before—on the face of a man she'd pulled out of the Sarel marshes twenty years ago, a smuggler gone down with his boat, who lived another hour and spent all of it staring at the horizon as if he could see something coming across it that no one else could see.

"Inside," Ines said. "Come inside, now, come on—"

The child didn't move. Didn't seem to hear. The singing had stopped; the mouth kept moving anyway, shaping sounds with no breath behind them. So Ines did the thing her training screamed at her not to do, the thing that got smugglers killed: she reached out into the storm, grabbed the small cold body under the arms, and hauled it across her threshold, kicking the door shut behind her with her heel.

The child weighed nothing. That frightened her more than anything else. Children were heavy; children were dense with life, dense with eating and growing and noise. This was like lifting a bundle of sticks, or a coat someone had stepped out of. The skin beneath the crusted snow was icy, and the pulse at the throat fluttered fast and shallow as a bird's.

"Maren!" Her voice cracked across the hall. "MAREN!"

Boards overhead. Then Maren came down the stairs wrapped in two blankets and forty years of composure, took one look, and became pure motion—no questions, no wasted words, just the oldest member of the Veilwright Guild moving like a woman thirty years younger. Fire built higher. Blankets. Hot water. The honey-and-wine mixture they kept for cold-struck couriers. Ines stripped the frozen wraps from the child's feet and winced at what the cold had done to the toes; Maren saw it too and said nothing, which was how Ines knew it was bad.

For an hour neither of them spoke except in instructions. Rub, don't rub, warm water not hot, don't let them sleep, small sips, more blankets. The child drifted in and out, shivering in great rolling waves, and never once opened their eyes properly, and never once made a sound—not a whimper, not a cry. Even when Ines chafed feeling back into those ruined feet and the child's whole body seized, there was no scream. Just the mouth working silently, shaping syllables, over and over, like a prayer worn smooth from use.

It was near dawn when the fever broke instead of taking hold, and near dawn when the child finally slept—truly slept, chest rising and falling in slow, even swells—and Ines sat back on her heels by the hearth and discovered her own hands shaking.

"He'll lose two toes," Maren said quietly. She had settled into the chair opposite, ancient and upright, blanket over her knees, watching the sleeping child with an expression Ines couldn't read. "Maybe three. Cold like that doesn't forgive."

"How did a child get up Thornfell in that storm? Barefoot?" Ines shook her head. "There are no villages past the high col. There's nothing past the high col but rock and the old shrines."

"I know what there is past the high col."

"Then how?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Upstairs, Corvin snored through the floorboards like a distant landslide.

"Ask me again in the morning," Maren said. "Or better—don't ask me. Ask the child."

"The child can't speak. You heard—there was nothing. Whatever language that mouth was moving through, there wasn't a sound in it."

"No," Maren said. "Not yet."

Ines looked at her sharply. "What does that mean?"

But Maren only rose, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered her blankets, and paused at the foot of the stairs. She looked old in the firelight—older than old, older than the guildhall itself, her face full of shadows that moved.

"Wake me when they wake," she said. "Whatever hour it is. Wake me first, Ines. Promise me that."

* * *

They woke near noon, in the strange amber light that comes when the sun struggles up over the storm-clouds and finds nothing worth illuminating. The child's eyes opened—dark eyes, startlingly clear after all that grey—and travelled the room slowly: the beams, the faded banner with its bridge-without-a-river, the banked fire, Ines's face.

No fear. That was the thing that raised the hair on Ines's arms. Most people waking in a strange place woke afraid. The child looked around the Veilwright guildhall the way a man looks around his own kitchen—mildly, familiarly, as though checking that everything was still where it belonged.

"You're awake," Ines said gently. "Good. You're safe. Do you understand me? Can you tell me your name?"

The child's lips parted. What came out was not silence this time.

It was sound—small, cracked, rusted with disuse, but unmistakably sound, unmistakably speech. Syllables rolled out of the child in a soft, flowing stream, vowels round as river stones, consonants falling like water over them. The words meant nothing to Ines. But they weren't babble; they were too ordered for babble, too rhythmic, with the cadence of something rehearsed ten thousand times. And threaded through them, unmistakable, was the melody from the doorway—the same scrap of tune the child had sung into the storm, now given its words.

Ines felt the words slide past her understanding like fish through fingers. "I'm sorry," she said helplessly. "Slowly. Slower. I don't—"

The child stopped. Looked at her. And then, with heartbreaking patience, began again—from the beginning, as if repetition alone might teach her, as if the fault lay in Ines's ears and not in the tongue itself.

That was when Maren came down the stairs.

Ines heard her stop. Heard the third step creak under a weight that had suddenly stopped descending. And when Ines turned, she saw the oldest member of the Veilwright Guild standing frozen on the staircase, one hand white-knuckled on the rail, her face drained of every drop of colour, staring at the child by the fire as though she were looking at a ghost—as though she were looking at a grave opening.

"Maren—" Ines started.

"*Say it again,*" Maren said, and her voice came out in pieces. "*Say that again.*"

The child looked at her. Considered her, with those calm dark eyes, the way the child considered everything, as if the world were an old house it had once lived in. Then, obligingly, it spoke again—that flowing, impossible stream, and this time Ines caught something in it, some turn of phrase repeated, some shape of sound that snagged in her memory like a burr—

Because it snagged in the *road-songs*. The old smuggling songs, the ones the Guild sang on the passes, the ones whose verses everyone had forgotten the meaning of, the nonsense-syllables passed master to apprentice for generations, *over the fell and under the stone, the river runs where there is none*—

The child was speaking the language of the road-songs.

"Maren," Ines whispered. "What is it saying?"

Maren came down the last steps slowly, like a woman walking into deep water. She knelt by the fire, joints protesting, and looked at the child for a long, long moment. And the child looked back, and said something short—two syllables, gentle, almost a question—and Maren's composure, that legendary composure, fifty years of smugglers' poker-face, broke like river ice.

Her hand went to her mouth. Tears stood in her eyes, sudden and shocking.

"It can't be," she breathed. "Fifty years. Fifty years since anyone—child, where did you learn that? Who taught you? *Who taught you that tongue?*"

The child answered at length, earnestly, gesturing at the fire, at the walls, at the storm-blind windows, at Ines herself—and Maren listened with her whole body leaned forward, drinking it in, and Ines watched understanding and horror chase each other across her face in equal measure.

"What is it?" Ines demanded. "What's it saying? Maren, *what language is that?*"

Maren didn't answer at once. When she did, her voice was very quiet, and very steady, in the way of a woman holding herself together with both hands.

"It's Old Sareli," she said. "The tongue of Sarel. The kingdom that drowned. The kingdom that has been dead for fifty years, Ines—the land, the people, the language, all of it, gone under, sealed away, ended. There is not a living soul in this world who speaks it." Her eyes went to the child, and Ines saw something in them that frightened her worse than the storm ever had: recognition. Grief. And underneath both, bright and terrible, a kind of hope. "There was no one left to speak it to."

The child, watching them both, said something more. Soft. Almost kind.

And Maren, the oldest Veilwright, the last woman alive to have walked out of a country that no longer existed, bowed her head over her folded hands and wept—while the snow buried the passes, while somewhere down in the valleys the Crown's men waited with their lanterns and their lists, and while the child sat by the fire in the ruins of the Veilwright Guild and spoke, patiently, fluently, in the voice of a drowned kingdom:

*Over the fell and under the stone, the river runs where there is none.*

And Ines understood, in the marrow-deep way she understood weather and roads and lies, that nothing—nothing—was ever going to be the same.
