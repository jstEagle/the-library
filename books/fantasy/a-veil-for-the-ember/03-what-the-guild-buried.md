## 3. What the Guild Buried

Maren had not slept in the guildhall's great room in forty years, but she woke in it now, in the chair by the cold hearth, with her hands folded in her lap like a woman attending her own funeral.

The storm had kept the child.

That was how she thought of it—not that the child had stayed, but that the storm had *kept* the child, the way a river keeps what falls into it, without malice and without intent to return anything. The little one slept upstairs in Ines's own bed, wrapped in two blankets and Corvin's old traveling cloak, breathing with the slow, rattling patience of someone who had been cold for a very long time. Every few breaths came a word, soft and slurred, in that language that had no business existing outside of a burned book.

Maren knew the language. That was the wound of it. She had known it since she was nine years old, standing on a dock that no longer existed, hearing her mother say *sleep now, we cross at moonset* in the same syllables, the same music, the same falling cadence at the end like water going over a lip.

She had told no one for fifty-three years. Not Corvin, who had taught her the ledger-books and trusted her with the routes. Not the guildmasters who came after, good men and women all, who believed they were smugglers of salt and silk and occasionally people, and who would have called her mad or worse—called her a liar of the worst kind, a keeper of Crown-forbidden things—if she had said what she truly was.

Not Ines. That was the sharpest edge. The girl had a way of looking at Maren across the counting table, patient and unblinking, waiting for the old woman to finish some half-story about the old days, and Maren had always stopped just short. Just short of the docks. Just short of the veil. Just short of her sister's face, which was the same face as her own, and which she had last seen through a closing door while her mother's hand dragged her away from it.

Now there was a child in the house speaking the dead tongue, and silence was no longer a place to live. It was a grave, and it was filling up.

* * *

The guildhall had been built to hold sixty. In its prime—the years Maren still caught herself calling its prime, though she was one of perhaps four people alive who remembered them—it had smelled of tallow and tar and roasting meat, and every window glowed, and the road below the bluff ran thick with carts whose cargo was never written down anywhere, because writing it down was how the Crown found you.

Now the hall held four: Maren, Ines, Corvin when he chose to appear, and old Tam Voss, who swept floors he was too bent to sweep properly and remembered nothing else. The great hearth took an hour to warm a room that had once warmed itself. The ledgers on their shelf were decoys—salt shipments, wool contracts, honest dull trade—because the true records were ash, and had been ash for twenty-two years, since the night the Ashcarts came up the valley with their wagons and their irons and their polite, terrible paperwork.

Maren had watched the burning. That was the part she never said aloud, even in the privacy of her own skull. She had stood in the crowd of the curious, an old woman among old women, and watched the Ashcart clerks feed the Veilwright archives into the fire page by page, cataloguing as they went, reading titles aloud in their flat administrative voices. *Manifest, autumn crossing, forty-one souls. Route-book, western passes. Roster of the Sarel ferries.* And she had stood there with a stolen page sewn into the lining of her coat, pressed against her heart like a second heartbeat, and said nothing, and gone home, and made tea.

Fifty-three years of tea. Fifty-three years of saying nothing while the guild shrank around her like a coat left out in the rain, until it was her and Ines and a sweeping man and a mentor who came and went like weather, and the whole vast machinery of mercy they had once been was reduced to moving contraband brandy through Thornfell so that the last few members could eat.

She rose from the chair. Her knees announced their objections; she ignored them, as she ignored most of her body's correspondence these days. Upstairs, the child slept on. Downstairs, in the cellar behind the false wall where the good brandy lived—or rather, where the brandy that paid for bread lived—there was a stone with a chiseled mark on it that only three people alive could read, and Maren intended to show one of them something before the day was out.

But first: the child.

She climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, listening. The room at the top was dim, snow-light coming gray through the shutters. Ember—for Ines had already named the child, of course she had, quick-hearted girl, naming things was how Ines loved them—lay curled small beneath the blankets, dark hair spilled across the pillow, and even asleep their lips moved, shaping words.

Maren sat on the edge of the bed, careful as a woman sitting on ice, and listened.

*"...sennu var, sennu var, keleth i doru..."*

The old words. The dock-words. *Wait for me, wait for me, the door is kind.* A skipping rhyme. Children on the Sarel side had sung it while they waited for the ferrymen to come through with news of the far country. Maren's sister had sung it, missing half the words, tapping the rhythm wrong, and Maren had corrected her, because Maren was older by two years and insufferable about it, and her sister had stuck out her tongue and sung it wrong again on purpose—

Maren's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees.

"Little one," she whispered, in Old Sareli. It came out rusty, cracked, the accent mangled by half a century of disuse, but it came out whole. "Little one, can you hear me?"

The child's eyes opened.

They were dark eyes, deep-set, and they fixed on Maren with a steadiness that no sleeping child should have—as if Ember had not woken at all, but had simply been waiting behind closed lids for someone to speak the right language. The small mouth worked. No sound came; Maren understood now that the muteness was not damage but discipline, or grief worn so long it had set like scar tissue. But the eyes said everything the voice would not: *You know it. You know the words.*

"Yes," Maren said, and her voice broke on the Sareli vowel like thin ice underfoot. "I know the words. I knew them before you were born, child. I knew them when they were alive."

Ember sat up. Small hands reached out—hesitated—then took Maren's wrists, grip surprisingly strong, and turned her palms up, and studied the calluses there, the old rope-scars across the right thumb that every ferry-hand of the last crossings had carried. Then the child did something that undid Maren entirely: brought one small finger up and touched, gently, the hollow of Maren's throat, where the voice lived, and looked at her with an expression of such naked, ancient longing that Maren felt fifty-three years of silence crack straight down the middle.

"You want me to speak it," Maren said. "All of it."

A nod. Slow. Solemn. Like a rite.

And Maren, who had kept her secret through fire and famine and the long gray shrinking of everything she loved, heard herself say: "Come downstairs, then. There are things this house buried that I mean to dig up."

* * *

The cellar was cold enough to see her breath. Maren lit the lamp, turned the wick low, and moved the brandy rack aside on its pivot, and then knelt—down, down, joints protesting—at the base of the false wall where a foundation stone bore the chiseled sigil of the old Guild: a door, and above it a veil, and above the veil an ember. Three symbols. Three truths the modern guild had forgotten in reverse order, until all they remembered was the door, and even that only as something to move goods *through*, in the ordinary muddy sense of mountain passes and bribe-money.

"The young ones think the name means we wove veils," Maren said, mostly to herself, running her thumb over the grooves. "Curtains. Disguises. Smugglers' tricks. And we did, child, we did do that—but it wasn't the meaning. It was never the meaning."

Behind her, at the top of the stairs, Ines stood in the doorway with a lamp of her own and an expression caught between suspicion and hunger. Maren had heard her come down. She let her stand. Some stories needed a listener standing up.

"In 1213 of the old reckoning," Maren began, "the kingdom of Sarel drowned."

"Not drowned in water." She said it flatly, because it needed saying flatly; every ballad got it wrong, singing of waves and floods. "Drowned in *law*. The Crown of that era declared Sarel heretical—its faith, its tongue, its kings—and sent armies to erase it root and branch. And Sarel, being smaller, being prouder, being doomed, did what doomed kingdoms do. It fought. And lost. And the survivors ran."

"But you cannot run from a Crown that has decided your children speak a forbidden language. So the Veilwrights were founded. Not by criminals, whatever the histories say—by ferrymen. Dock-workers. Carters. Midwives. Ordinary people of both kingdoms who looked at the roads full of refugees and said: the passes are watched, the ports are watched, the roads themselves are watched. So we will build roads that aren't anywhere."

Ines's lamp trembled slightly. "Roads that aren't anywhere," she repeated.

"Through the veil." Maren said the word the way you'd say a prayer you'd stopped believing in but couldn't stop reciting. "There are thin places in the world, girl. Always have been. The old guild knew where they lay the way a blind man knows where walls are—with his whole skin. And on the other side of the thinnest of those places lay Sarel. Not the burning Sarel. A Sarel behind the sky, whole and unburnt, where a family could walk out of one winter and into another country's summer, and no Ashcart could follow, because the Crown's writ stops at the edge of the world."

"We ferried them. Forty years, maybe more. Whole villages, sometimes. We learned the songs that opened the ways—we didn't invent them, mind, they were *found*, old as the hills, older—and we learned the tolls. Nothing crosses for free. Never coin. Coin means nothing to a door. The tolls were stranger than that. A memory freely given. A name spoken backward. One poor carter gave up his shadow for a year, and we never asked him what that cost him, because we were afraid of the answer."

She laughed—a short, awful sound. "And I was nine. My mother carried me through the third-to-last crossing. My father stayed behind with the rearguard, moving families faster than the soldiers could burn parishes. And my sister—" 

Her voice failed there, precisely where it had always failed, for fifty-three years, in every half-told story. But tonight she was done with stopping short. Tonight she walked straight off the edge she'd been circling her whole life.

"My sister was twelve, and she wanted to stay and help Father carry the little ones' bundles. Just to the next waystation, she said. Mother said yes, because we were all fools with tired hearts, and the waystation was raided in the night. By the time the ferry came back for stragglers, my sister was gone—taken south with a hundred others, and the veil sealed itself two weeks later. Sealed itself, do you understand? Because the last of the door-singers died in the raids, and a door with no singer is just a wall. The Guild spent thirty years trying to reopen it. Failed. Spent the next twenty pretending we'd only ever been smugglers. And the Crown burned our books to make sure the pretense stuck."

Silence in the cellar, except the wind worrying the eaves above.

"And you kept this," Ines said quietly. "All these years."

"I kept worse." Maren reached into her bodice, into the hidden seam she had sewn and resewn along a different line every time she changed coats, and drew out the page.

It was small, oilskin-wrapped, soft as cloth from decades of handling. Inside lay a single leaf of heavy rag-paper, browned at the edges, its ink faded to the color of dried blood. At the top, in a formal hand: *Roster of the Last Ferry, Feast of Embers, final crossing.* Below it, a list of names in Old Sareli, each followed by a tally-mark or a blank space where a tally should have been.

"This is the page I stole from the burning," Maren said. "The clerk read its title aloud and tossed it on the pile, and I pulled it out again while his back was turned, and I have hated myself every day since, because I could have saved more. I could have saved ten pages. Twenty. I could have thrown myself on the pile like a madwoman and saved a handful of names from the fire, and instead I took the one page with *my sister's name* on it, because I am a selfish old woman, and the rest is ash."

She smoothed it on her knee with fingers that shook. "Here. *Senna il-Vareth.* My sister. See—the tally-mark beside her name is blank. Blank meant *not crossed*. Every blank on this page is someone who didn't make it through. There are forty-one names, child. Thirty-three tallies. Eight blanks. I've had eight blanks in my keeping for fifty-three years."

Ember had come down the stairs without either of them noticing—the child moved like snowfall, soundless—and now stood between them, staring at the page. A small hand rose and hovered over the list, not touching, tracing the air above the faded ink, name by name, the way you'd trace the faces of people you missed.

Then Ember's finger stopped. Settled. Pointed—at a blank space near the bottom of the list, beside a name Maren had never been able to pronounce without crying, and looked up at her.

And in the lamplight, with the storm still screaming at the shuttered windows and the brandy-rack swung open behind them like a mouth, Maren saw the truth she had been circling for two days finally turn and face her.

"That's why you came here," she breathed. "Isn't it? Not lost. Not wandering. You came to *us*. To the guild that used to open doors. Child—who are you? What were you to them?"

Ember's mouth opened. Worked. No sound—but the eyes answered with a grief too large for the small face holding it, and Maren, who had spent a lifetime reading silences, read this one plainly:

*I was one of the blanks.*

Above them, boots crossed the floor of the great room—heavy, deliberate, more than one pair—and a fist struck the front door of the guildhall with the flat authority of the Crown.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

Ashcarts did not knock twice unless they already knew what they wanted.
