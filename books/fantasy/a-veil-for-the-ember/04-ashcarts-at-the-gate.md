## 4. Ashcarts at the Gate

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

I had grown up on knocks the way other children grew up on lullabies. Three quick raps meant a runner with news; two slow ones meant goods; a single knuckle-drag along the wood meant trouble walking on two legs and wearing a uniform. The Veilwrights had a whole grammar of doors, and I knew it in my bones before I knew my letters.

This was none of them. This was a fist, patient and heavy, striking four times—*one, two, three, four*—like a man counting the seconds he was willing to be polite.

I was in the kitchen with Maren, coaxing the stove to life with damp peat, when the sound rolled through the guildhall. Ember sat by the window where they always sat, wrapped in my second-best blanket, watching the snow come down in long grey ropes off the eaves. At the knock, the child went rigid—not startled, the way a cat is startled, but *still*, the way a hare goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass. As if they had been waiting for that exact sound since the night they appeared at our door half-frozen and speaking a dead language into Maren's astonished face.

Maren's hands stopped over the stove. She looked at me across the kitchen, and her eyes said what her mouth would not: *They've come.*

"Through the back," I whispered. "Now."

She took Ember's hand and drew the child toward the pantry, moving fast for a woman of seventy years, and I stood alone in the kitchen listening to my own heart count to ten before the fist struck again.

*One, two, three, four.*

I crossed the hall, pulled my coat straight, and opened the door on the coldest morning of the winter.

There were six of them. Six Ashcarts in their bone-grey cloaks, hoods back despite the cold because they wanted us to see their faces—wanted us to see the ash-grey eyes, the brand of the Crown's censer burned into each cheek like a tear that had scalded and set. Their horses steamed in the yard, shod hooves punching holes through the crusted snow. In front, holding the reins of a black gelding, stood a tall man with a voice I already knew from the knock: measured, unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it.

"Ines Oyelaran." He said my name the way a man reads an item off an inventory. "Inquisitor Second Rank Halvard Vess. By authority of the Crown's Office of Cinders, I am conducting a search of this premises."

I had rehearsed nothing. There had been no time to rehearse anything, and so what came out of me was pure guildhall: the flat, bored courtesy of a woman who has answered a hundred doors for men who thought themselves important.

"Search of what premises?" I said. "This is a tannery storehouse. We keep hides and lime and very little else worth your time, Inquisitor."

Vess smiled. It did not reach his eyes; I doubt it reached his teeth. "May we come in out of the cold, Mistress Oyelaran? Or must I note in my report that the last registered member of the Veilwright Guild refused lawful entry?"

*Last registered member.* The words went through me like a splinter finding the quick. They had records. Of course they had records—the Ashcarts had burned the Guild's books, but burning a book does not burn the memory of what was written in it, and somewhere in some ash-scented office in the capital there was a ledger with our names in it, mine and Maren's and Corvin's, listed under a heading I could not read but could well imagine.

"The door's open," I said, and stepped aside.

* * *

They came in the way frost comes into a wall—quietly, thoroughly, into every crack. Two remained in the yard with the horses. Four entered, and Vess walked the great hall of the Veilwright Guild as if he were walking through a house whose owner had died owing him money. His gloved fingers trailed along the smuggling-table where Corvin had taught me to read wax seals. He paused at the hearth where the guild's road-songs had been sung every solstice for two hundred years, and looked up the chimney with an expression of mild professional disappointment, as though hoping to find contraband roosting in the flue.

"You'll forgive the intrusion," he said, without turning around. "We're looking for a child. Small. Perhaps eight years, perhaps younger. Dark-haired. Found wandering, we believe, somewhere in the low country east of here." He turned then, and those ash-grey eyes settled on me with the weight of wet wool. "The child cannot speak—or will not. But it speaks a language. Old Sareli."

My face did what faces do. I felt the blood leave my cheeks and hated myself for it, because Vess saw it—I watched him see it—and filed it away behind those flat grey eyes like a coin into a strongbox.

"You know something of Sareli, Mistress Oyelaran?"

"I know the Guild ferried Sareli folk," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Before the kingdom drowned. That was two hundred years ago. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows the story." Vess drifted toward the window, looking out at the falling snow. "Few know the truth. The truth being that the Veilwrights did not ferry refugees *to* anywhere. They ferried them *through*. Out of the world entire. Into a country that exists nowhere on any map drawn since." He tapped one gloved finger against the glass. "The Crown takes a dim view of doors, Mistress Oyelaran. A kingdom is only a kingdom while its borders hold. A people is only a people while its records say so. When Sarel fell, the Crown did not merely lose a neighbor. It lost control of a story. And stories, uncontrolled, have a way of coming back."

"I'm a hide-tanner's clerk," I said. "You'd have better luck interrogating the lime pits."

That earned me another smile, thinner than the first. "Where is your colleague, Maren Sorrel? Registered 3rd of Frostmonth, forty-one years ago. Apprentice class. Never released from the rolls."

"She's dead," I said, and the lie came out smooth and cold and easy, and something in me recoiled from how easy it was. "Winter fever, three years back. Buried up the hill. You're welcome to dig if you've a mind to offend the dead."

Vess considered me for a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the window. Somewhere below us—beneath the floorboards, beneath the old smugglers' hollow that ran the length of the hall like a second, darker guildhall—Ember was silent. I prayed to every god whose name the Ashcarts hadn't burned that the child understood silence. Mute children understand silence. It is the rest of us who forget how.

"And you, Mistress Oyelaran," Vess said at last. "Registered seven years ago. Your master, Corvin Ashe—" he pronounced the name with a faint curl of distaste, or perhaps of pity— "was released from the rolls last spring. Cooperating subject. Which makes you, as of this morning, the last living Veilwright on Crown record." He let that sit between us like a body laid out on a table. "Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I'm owed a pension," I said, "and have yet to see a copper of it."

One of the younger Ashcarts laughed—a short, barked thing he strangled immediately—but Vess did not laugh. He walked slowly back toward the center of the hall, and I saw his gaze drop, just once, just briefly, to the floorboards. To the place—did he know? Could he know?—where a seam in the planking ran too straight for any honest floor.

"We received word," he said quietly, "from a source inside your organization. A reliable source. That a child matching the Remnant's description arrived at this guildhall during the storm of the ninth. That the child speaks Old Sareli. That the child is not a refugee, Mistress Oyelaran, but a *remnant*—a piece of the drowned kingdom that walked out of the veil and into the world, carrying something the Crown very much wants carried back." He spread his gloved hands. "Give us the child, and the Veilwright Guild passes out of history with honor. Pardons all around. Roads reopened. Your name cleared of the rolls entirely. You could grow old, Mistress Oyelaran, in a world that remembers you kindly."

The offer hung in the air, and I will not pretend it did not tempt me. That is the thing about Ashcart offers—they are built from true things. Honor. Pardon. Warmth. Age. All real coins, honestly minted. It is only the exchange rate that is murder.

"There's no child here," I said.

And then, because lying is a craft like any other, and the first rule of the craft is *commit*, I added: "If a child had come to this door in that storm, I'd have fed it and sent it down to the parish reeve, same as anyone. That's the law. I know the law. I've spent my life learning exactly which parts of the law are load-bearing and which can be walked around, Inquisitor, and a foundling is not one you walk around."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly shaped like the truth. But Vess looked at me for a long, long moment, and I understood that he was not weighing my words. He was weighing me. The way a man weighs a rope before trusting it with his weight.

"Your mentor said something similar once," he remarked. "Corvin Ashe. 'I know which parts of the law are load-bearing.' Almost word for word." He buttoned his cloak at the throat. "We'll search the premises now. Standard procedure. If you'd prefer to wait outside—"

"I'd prefer to watch," I said, "so you don't steal the silver. What little there is of it."

* * *

They searched for two hours.

I followed them through my own home like a ghost at its own funeral, watching their gloved hands open everything. They went through the dormitory where thirty apprentices had once slept and now only dust slept. They went through the chart-room, empty of charts these three years, its shelves holding nothing but mouse droppings and the ghosts of maps. They prodded the peat stores, the salt cellar, the cold hearth of the kitchen. One of them—a young one, barely older than I was, with a fresh brand still pink at his cheekbone—knelt and ran his palm along the floorboards of the great hall, feeling for seams.

I stood over him and kept my breathing slow.

Beneath us, in the hollow, lay Ember and Maren both, wedged into a space built to hide bolt-rolls of smuggled silk, breathing through a vent that opened behind the woodpile. Above them, a young man's fingers walked the boards inch by inch, tap, tap, tap, listening for the hollow note.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

I thought about the bridge at Thornfell, the one I'd dropped with a mallet-stroke when the toll-collectors came too close, and how Corvin had told me afterward: *A Veilwright's job isn't to fight, girl. It's to make the road disappear behind her.* I thought about that now, standing in my own hall, and understood for the first time that the road he'd meant was never the mountain passes. It was this. The self you show the world. The self you fold away beneath the floorboards. Every smuggler carries two of them, and lives or dies by knowing which one to open the door for.

The young Ashcart's fingers found the seam.

He paused. Tapped twice more, right along the join, and I heard it—the faintest difference in the note, a board sounding hollow where its neighbors sounded solid—and I watched him frown, and I watched him look up at Vess—

—and the kettle in the kitchen began to scream.

Maren had left it on the stove. Of course she had; she'd been taken mid-task, and the fire had eaten through the damp peat at last, and the old iron kettle sang like a soul in a fire, high and wavering and utterly impossible to ignore. The young Ashcart flinched. Vess turned toward the sound. And I moved—fast, casual, a woman annoyed at her own housekeeping—crossing the hall in five strides and calling back over my shoulder:

"That'll be the water. Forgive me, gentlemen, if you smell anything worse than lime in this place, it's because I'm a disgrace to housekeeping."

I lifted the kettle off. I did not pour it out. I set it back on the iron plate, still screaming, and let it scream, and while it screamed I talked—about the tannery accounts, about the price of lime, about the scandalous cost of peat this season—in a bright, running stream of nothing, filling the hall with the sound of a harmless woman chattering, until the young Ashcart's attention had broken and reformed elsewhere, and the seam in the floor was just a seam again, and Vess was checking his watch-chain and saying, with genuine regret, that daylight was short and there were other premises on the list.

At the door, he paused. Snow whirled past his shoulders into the hall.

"For what it's worth, Mistress Oyelaran," he said, "I believe you believe there's no child here." Those ash-grey eyes held mine. "But belief is a door too. And doors, in my experience, always open eventually. Good day."

They rode out southward, six horses punching holes in the snow, and I stood in the doorway until the last grey cloak vanished into the white, and only then did my knees inform me what they thought of the morning's work.

* * *

I got them out of the hollow myself. Maren first—seventy years old and stiff as a frozen hinge, hauled up through the trapdoor gasping curses in three languages, two of them dead. Then Ember, blinking in the sudden light, snow-pale, clutching my second-best blanket as though it were the last true thing in the world.

For a moment none of us spoke. The kettle had finally gone quiet. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves.

"They'll come back," Maren said. Her voice was raw from the dust of the hollow. "That man wasn't guessing, Ines. He *knew* things. He knew the rolls. He knew dates. Someone gave him the shape of us." She looked at me, and her old eyes were terrible with certainty. "Someone inside."

"Corvin," I said. The name came out flat, like a coin dropped on stone.

Maren didn't answer. She didn't have to. We had both been thinking it for hours, and saying it aloud only made the shape of it harder to deny.

Ember tugged my sleeve. I looked down. The child pointed one small finger at the floor—at the hollow, at the hiding place—and then pressed that finger against their own chest, and shook their head, slow and solemn, the way you tell someone a thing you have known for a long time and have finally decided they are ready to hear.

*I am what they're looking for,* the gesture said. *I brought this to your door.*

"No," I said aloud, and my voice cracked on it, and I didn't care. I crouched down to the child's height, this mute remnant of a drowned kingdom, and I heard myself say the words I had never said to anyone, the words that made the lie to Vess into something more than a lie—into a promise, into a road I was already walking whether I liked it or not.

"They can search every floorboard in Thornfell," I told Ember. "They can turn over every stone from here to the capital. They will not have you. Do you hear me? Not while I'm above ground to stand on the boards."

Behind me, Maren made a small sound—half laugh, half sob—and when I turned, she was looking at me strangely, the way you look at a stranger wearing a familiar face.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing," she said. "Only—you sounded just then like old Wenna Veilwright herself. Like the stories. Like the women who used to open roads." She shook her head slowly. "I taught you ledgers, girl. Where you learned that, I don't know."

Outside, far off in the white silence of the passes, a thin thread of smoke rose from the ridge above the north road—one puff, two, three. A smuggler's signal. Trouble on the road; move tonight.

I looked at it a long time. Then I went to pack.
