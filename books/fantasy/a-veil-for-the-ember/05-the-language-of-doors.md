## 5. The Language of Doors

The snow kept falling for three days, and on the third day the child stopped shivering and began to watch Ines work.

That was how it started—not with lessons, not with chalk and slate, but with watching. Ines mended nets in the guildhall's back room because mending was what she did when her hands needed to be busy and her heart needed to be still, and the child sat cross-legged on the hearthstone with a blanket around their shoulders like a small gray moth, tracking every pass of her fingers through the twine.

"Net-menders' hitch," Ines told them, mostly to herself, mostly to fill the silence that had lived in the hall since the Ashcarts burned the records. "Left over right, then under. The Guild used it as a signal once—two hitches meant *safe road*, three meant *turn back*."

The child's head tilted. Their lips moved, shaping something soft and syllabic, a phrase like water going over stones.

"What was that?"

They said it again, patiently, the way you'd repeat yourself to someone hard of hearing, and pointed at her hands.

Ines set down the twine. "I don't know your words."

But she knew the shape of them.

That was the thing that had been crawling up the back of her neck since the night Maren went pale at the door and named the child's language Old Sareli, the drowned tongue of a drowned kingdom. Ines didn't know Old Sareli. No one alive did, supposedly. And yet when the child spoke, certain phrases landed in her ear like coins dropped into a familiar pocket. She'd heard them before. Not spoken—but sung. Hummed under breath on long hauls through Thornfell. Chanted in rounds around guildhall fires by men who were drunk or dying or both.

Road-songs. The old road-songs of the Veilwright Guild.

She hadn't thought about them in years. You didn't, when the songs had become just rhythm, just something to keep eight sets of boots in step on a night march. But now the child opened their mouth and the sounds came out, and Ines felt them snag on something buried deep in her—the way a hook snags on a sunken log you didn't know was there.

* * *

On the fourth day she fetched the slate.

It had been Corvin's teaching slate, once, back when the Guild still took apprentices young enough to need one. The chalk was a stub worn round as a river pebble. Ines set it on the table between herself and the child and drew the first thing that came to hand: a rough map of the Thornfell passes, the switchbacks rendered as loops of thread, the waymarks as small crosses.

The child studied it with an intensity that made her want to look away, as if she'd caught them at prayer. Then they took the chalk from her hand—small fingers, chapped from frostbite that was only now healing—and drew, beside her map, a second map.

It wasn't Thornfell. The lines were wrong for Thornfell, too smooth, too deliberate, like a circuit rather than a scramble. They drew towers that looked like spindles, a river drawn as a braid of three strands, and at the center a circle with a smaller circle inside it, over which they pressed their palm flat and held it there for a long moment, eyes closed.

Then they looked up at Ines and spoke a full sentence of that liquid, ancient speech, and though she understood nothing of its meaning, she understood everything of its tone. It was a question. It was: *Where is this? Where did it go?*

"I don't know," Ines said quietly. "I'm sorry. I don't know where your country is."

The child's face did something terrible then—it didn't crumple, didn't cry. It simply closed, like a shutter drawn over a lamp, and Ines realized they had asked the question before, somewhere, of someone, and gotten the same answer, and had learned to expect the dark that followed it.

"No—wait." She pulled the slate back, wiped her own map away with her sleeve, and drew instead the sign of the Veilwrights: the arch with the thread through it, the guild-mark she'd had inked into her skin at fourteen. "See this? We were ferrymen. Smugglers. We carried people. That's what we did—that's all we did, until there was no one left to carry."

The child touched the mark. Their finger traced the arch, then the thread, and their mouth formed a word—short, rounded, heavy in the middle—and then they sang.

Two notes. Only two notes, low to high, and Ines's whole body went cold, because she knew those two notes. Every smuggler on the Thornfell roads knew those two notes. It was the opening call of the Long Haul Song, the one old Doss used to sing out before a crossing, the one whose words nobody remembered anymore so everybody just hummed the tune—

Except the words weren't lost. They were here, in this child, in this room, pouring out of a mouth that had been mute since the night it appeared half-frozen on the guildhall step.

"Say it again," Ines whispered.

The child said it again.

Ines hummed the next line of the song, the part that climbed and doubled back, and the child's eyes went wide and bright as coins, and they answered—sang the line properly, with words, real words, and then reached out and grabbed Ines's wrist with both hands, gripping hard enough to hurt, shaking her arm as if to say *yes, yes, you have it, you've had it all along.*

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and by the time the light through the shutters went amber and then gray, Ines had filled both sides of the slate and was writing on the tabletop in chalk dust with her fingertip.

It went slowly, because they had no shared ground except the songs. The child could not write in any script Ines recognized; their letters looked like hanging lamps, like hooks, like the marks on Maren's stolen page. So they built the language out of music instead. Ines would hum a fragment of a road-song—the crossing chant, the turn-back warning, the lullaby the older members sang to the younger ones on the cold marches—and the child would listen with their head cocked like a bird's, and then give back the words the melody had been carrying all along, disguised all along, hidden inside tunes the Guild had sung for generations without knowing what they said.

And that was the discovery. That was the thing that made Ines stand up from the table and walk the length of the room twice with her hand pressed over her mouth.

The road-songs weren't songs. They never had been.

Or rather—they were, but they were also instructions. Veiled ones. The crossing chant, the one with the nonsense refrain every apprentice learned to gibber along to, laid out in the child's careful singing a sequence: *low road, third waymark, wait for the moon to double.* The turn-back warning contained, folded inside its jaunty rhythm, a description of a landmark—a split stone shaped like a tooth—which Ines had walked past a hundred times in the passes above Hollowmere and never once thought about. The lullaby was a counting song, but it counted in a base of nine, and the numbers, the child showed her by tapping the slate, matched the intervals between waymark cairns.

"It's a map," Ines said aloud, to no one, to the room, to the dead. "The whole repertoire. Doss's songs, Maren's songs, the ones we burned with the rest of it—they're not memories of the roads. They're the roads. They've been in our mouths the entire time."

The Ashcarts had burned the ledgers, the route-books, the charts. They'd burned forty years of cartography in a courtyard fire and called it cleansing. But they hadn't burned the songs, because the songs were worthless—just tavern noise, just work-rhythm, just the humming of ignorant men. The Crown's scholars had stood in front of the single largest archive of Old Sareli instruction left in the world and heard nothing but noise.

Ines started laughing, and couldn't stop for a while, and the child watched her laugh with an expression of grave, slightly offended patience, as if laughter were another dialect they hadn't learned yet.

Because here was the other thing, the thing underneath the laughing, the thing with teeth: the Guild had always known more than it knew. Her masters—the ones who died poor, the ones who drank themselves under tables, the ones who muttered about better days without ever saying what had been better about them—had been carrying a dead kingdom's geography in their throats. And they hadn't known. Or worse, they had known, once, and let it fade into habit, into folklore, into nothing, the way you let anything fade when remembering hurts too much.

Maren would know. Maren, who had gone pale as whey at the sound of Old Sareli on the doorstep. Maren, who kept one stolen page hidden somewhere in her bones or her bedding. There was more under that silence than a page.

But that reckoning could wait. Tonight there was only this room, this slate, this child with frost scars fading on their knuckles, singing a smuggler's tune in a kingless tongue.

* * *

Near dusk, Ines gave them the name.

She hadn't planned it. Names were dangerous now—she'd known that since the Ashcarts came asking after "the Remnant"—and she'd been careful, calling the child *little one*, *you*, *hey* in the corridor-dark. But names were also how the Guild marked what belonged to it. Every apprentice got a road-name on their first crossing, chosen by their teacher, and Ines had taken hers from a lantern: Oyelaran, *the light that finds the path*, her grandmother's word in a language from a coast the sea had eaten long before Sarel ever sank.

The child was bent over the slate, practicing the shapes of the common alphabet with the chalk stub, tongue poking out in concentration. The firelight caught in their hair. It wasn't red exactly, and it wasn't copper either—it was the color of coals an hour after the flames die, the color heat keeps when everything else has given up.

"Ember," Ines said.

The child looked up.

"That's you. If you want it." She tapped her own chest. "Ines." Then theirs. "Ember." She made the gesture the child had taught her that first day, the one that meant *this is mine, I am this*. "Ember. A coal that stays warm. The thing you carry wrapped in wool so you can start tomorrow's fire."

The child repeated it. Their voice was small and rusty from disuse, and they said the name the way you test ice with one foot—but they said it, and then they said it again, firmer, and then they did something Ines was not prepared for. They crossed the room and pressed their forehead against her sternum, briefly, hard, the way you'd lean against a door to hold it shut, and stepped back before she could react.

Ines stood very still. Something in her chest had cracked open, and she didn't trust what might come out of the crack.

"You're Guild now," she managed, gruffly, when she trusted her voice again. "You know that. Road-name and everything. Which means you eat first and complain last, and if you ever meet a man named Corvin you're polite to him but you tell me everything he says."

At the name Corvin, something flickered across Ember's face—recognition, maybe, or just the universal wariness children learn toward adults who matter to the adults around them. Ines filed it away and said nothing.

Outside, the wind had changed. It had been blowing down from the peaks for days, the flat gray wind of ordinary weather, but now it came up from the valley instead, and it carried a different weight, a colder grain, and when Ines cracked the shutter and looked out, the snow falling past the lantern was falling slower than snow should fall. Big flakes. Unhurried. The kind of snow old men called *queen's feathers* and refused to explain.

Maren found her at the window an hour later. The old woman had been sleeping more and more, and she moved now like her joints were full of wet sand, but her eyes were clear, and they went straight past Ines to Ember, asleep by the fire with the slate clutched to their chest like a book of psalms.

"She's teaching you." It wasn't a question. Maren lowered herself onto the bench with a sound like a rope creaking. "The little one. Teaching you the songs."

"She is. Maren—" Ines turned from the window. "The songs are Old Sareli. All of them. The crossing chant, the warnings, the counting song. They're instructions. They've been instructions since before I was born. How?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Snow ticked against the shutter like fingertips.

"How do you think, girl?" she said at last, and her voice was very old and very tired and held something Ines had never heard in it before—something that sounded almost like fear, and almost like longing. "Who do you think wrote them? Who do you think taught them to the first Veilwrights, before the burning, before the fall?" She looked at the fire, and her hands worked in her lap like a woman braiding rope that wasn't there. "We didn't invent the craft, Ines. We inherited it. Ferrying folk through the veil into Sarel—that was never our cleverness. It was our *wage*. We were paid in songs by people who needed the roads kept open. And I have spent fifty years singing them wrong, singing them empty, because I—"

She stopped. Her jaw set the way it did when she was deciding whether a truth could afford daylight.

"There are verses I never taught you," Maren said. "Verses the Guild didn't sing. Verses I swore I'd take to the pyre with me." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "But that child sings in her sleep, Ines. Did you know that? Sings in a language that's been dead sixty years. And last night she sang one of the missing verses. Word for word. Note for note. A verse I haven't heard since I was nineteen years old, on the last ferry out, with my sister's hand slipping out of mine."

The wind rose against the shutters, and far off in the direction of the high passes—so faint it might have been imagination, might have been the wind worrying the rocks into whistle—there came a sound that Ines's blood knew before her ears did.

A signal-fire horn. One long, two short.

The old alarm. *Turn back. The road is watched.*

Maren heard it too. The two women looked at each other across the firelit room, over the sleeping child who clutched a slate full of hanging-lamp letters and dreamed, apparently, in the language of doors.

"Get the pack," Maren said quietly. "Get her, and get the pack. The winter's coming early this year, and I think it's coming for us."
