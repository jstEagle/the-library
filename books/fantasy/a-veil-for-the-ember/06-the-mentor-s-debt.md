## 6. The Mentor's Debt

The letter had come the way all of Corvin's worst decisions came: folded small, sealed in grey wax, and left where only he would think to look. It was tucked into the hollow heel of his old road-boot, the pair he kept by the door for appearances, and it said simply: *The Tannery. Midnight. Come alone, or don't come at all.*

He had known, of course. He had known from the moment the child appeared at the guildhall door that the quiet years were over, that the debt he'd carried like a stone sewn into his coat would finally come due. Debts always did. The Veilwrights had a saying about that—*the road collects what the traveler owes*—and Corvin had spent eleven years pretending the saying didn't apply to men who sold maps instead of walking them.

Now he walked one anyway, alone, through streets gone blue with frost, past shuttered dyeshops and the long dark bulk of the tannery yards, where the vats sat cold and the lime pits breathed their ghost of old rot into the night air. Snow was coming. He could taste it under the coal-smoke, that flat mineral hush that meant the sky was loading itself like a crossbow. The first true snow of the long winter, the almanacs were calling it, and everyone in Thornfell spoke of the coming season the way farmers once spoke of locusts.

Corvin pulled his collar up and told himself, as he had told himself for eleven years, that everything he did next was for the Guild.

The tannery's counting house still had a roof, which was more than most buildings on this street could claim. A single lamp burned in the window, turned low. He let himself in without knocking, because knocking would have been a lie too elaborate even for him, and found the handler waiting exactly as handlers always waited—seated, unhurried, hands folded on the table like a man at prayer who had long ago stopped believing anyone was listening.

"You came," the handler said. Her voice was dry paper. She wore Ashcart grey without insignia, which was its own kind of insignia—the Crown's inquisitors dressed plainly when they wanted a meeting deniable, and nothing about tonight was meant to be remembered by anyone but the two of them.

"I always come," Corvin said. "That's my whole tragedy."

"Sit down, Master Dunmore." She used his true name deliberately, the way you'd press a bruise to check it still hurt. "Wine?"

"No."

"More for me." She poured anyway, two cups, and pushed one across the scarred oak toward him. The wine was good. That was the thing people never understood about the Ashcarts—they had excellent wine, soft beds, patient ledgers. Cruelty was merely the instrument; the music was bureaucracy. "You've heard, I assume. About the child."

Corvin kept his face the way Maren had taught him to keep it forty years ago, back when he'd been a promising apprentice with quick feet and a quicker tongue: *blank as an unmarked door, boy. Let them guess what room they're walking into.* "I've heard there's sickness going through the low quarter. I've heard the price of salt's up again. Children turn up at doors every winter."

"Not children who speak Old Sareli."

The words landed in the cold room like coins dropped down a well. Corvin watched them fall and made himself not reach for them.

"Old Sareli," he repeated. "Dead language. Dead kingdom. You'd have better luck finding a man who speaks to crows."

"We found one who speaks to doors." The handler sipped her wine. Her name, he'd learned years ago, was Vess. Just Vess. He suspected she'd surrendered the rest of it somewhere along her own climb, the way ambitious people shed parts of themselves the way snakes shed skin—leaving something pale and glistening behind in the grass. "A mute child, half-frozen, delivered to the doorstep of the last Veilwright guildhall in the Thornfell passes. During a snowstorm. Speaking the tongue of a kingdom that has been under the water—or under whatever it is—for sixty years." She set down the cup. "You can imagine why that interests us."

"It interests me too," Corvin said. "As a curiosity."

"As a *Remnant*," Vess said, and now she leaned forward, and the lamplight found the fine seams of her face, the patience worn into it like a riverbed. "Come, Corvin. We've done this dance before. You sold us the Salt Road in '61. You sold us the Hollow Ways in '64. You gave us the route-book indexes so we could burn the right pages first. Do you imagine I don't know how your mind works by now? You are already calculating what the child is worth, and whether you're the man positioned to collect."

And there it was, laid out on the table between the wine cups: the truth he'd been carrying since the first grey-sealed letter, the truth that woke him some nights at the hour when even guilt sleeps. Not that he had betrayed the Guild. That was old news, worn smooth as a river stone, a thing he could pick up and put down. The worse truth was how *easy* it had been. How the routes had come out of him almost gratefully, like blood from a opened vein, like the Guild had been holding him underwater all those years and the Crown's coin was the first breath.

He had told himself it was survival. He told himself that still. Survival was respectable. Survival had arithmetic on its side. Forty members of the brotherhood, and one man's cooperation meant one man alive to keep forty names off the pyre—and hadn't that been the argument? Hadn't he rehearsed it until it shone? *They'll burn the records either way. They'll find the roads either way. At least if it's me, I can steer what burns.* And for a while it had even been true. For a while his treachery had looked, from certain angles, in certain light, almost like stewardship.

Then the Ashcarts had burned the guildhall archives anyway, all of it, the ledgers and the road-songs and the ferry-masters' journals, three days of black smoke over Thornfell while the Veilwrights stood in the street and watched their history go up in sparks. And Corvin had stood among them, mourning loudly, holding Maren's elbow so the old woman wouldn't collapse, and inside him something had quietly died and been buried without rites, and he had gone on living in the house of that unmarked grave ever since.

"The child," he said carefully, "is at the guildhall. Under Maren's eye. Under Ines's."

"Ines." Vess smiled faintly. "Your protégée. The last apprentice. Tell me about her."

"There's nothing to tell. She runs wool and untaxed brandy through the high passes and calls it keeping the faith. She's a smuggler, not a martyr."

"Mm." Vess turned her cup slowly on the table, around and around, a small orbiting moon. "And yet the Guild has always attracted martyrs. It's practically a breeding condition. Here is what interests the Crown, Corvin, and I'll speak plainly because plainness is cheaper than subtlety at this hour. Sixty years ago, Sarel fell. The official account is flood, plague, and civil collapse—a kingdom that drowned in its own troubles. But the founding charter of your little brotherhood says otherwise, doesn't it? We recovered fragments. Enough to know the Veilwrights moved *people*, not goods. Enough to know there is a veil, and a far side, and a craft of crossing it. And enough to know that the craft was never entirely lost—only scattered. Songs. Waymarks. Muscle memory in old smugglers' bones." She looked up. "And then a child walks out of a snowstorm speaking the dead tongue, and every fragment we have begins, very quietly, to make sense."

"You think the child came *from* there."

"I think the child is a key," Vess said, "and keys are Crown property when they open things the Crown has sealed. There are people above me—far above me, in ash-grey robes you will never meet—who have spent careers ensuring that Sarel stays drowned. Whatever is on the other side of that veil, Corvin, it frightens them more than plague or war ever frightened them, and frightened powerful men are generous men. Which brings us to you."

She slid a document across the table. Heavy paper, heavy seal, the Crown's ash-tree sigil pressed deep into grey wax. Corvin didn't touch it. He read it upside down, the way he'd learned to read waybills as a boy, and the words arranged themselves in his chest like stones arranging themselves at the bottom of a well.

*A full pardon. Restoration of guild standing. Cessation of all inquiries against surviving members. The roads returned.*

Every word a jewel. Every jewel baited.

"And the price," he said.

"The child. Delivered to Crown custody, unharmed, within the fortnight. Before the winter closes the passes." Vess leaned back. "You see how clean it is? No one dies. No guildhall burns—this time we'd rather it stand; empty buildings make poor martyrs. Your people walk free. The roads reopen. Trade resumes. Everything your brotherhood bled for, restored, because one mute orphan changes hands." She spread her hands. "Weigh it honestly, Corvin. What is one child against forty lives? You've done that arithmetic before. You're good at it."

*I'm good at it.* The phrase went through him like a fishhook and set.

Because that was the filth of it, wasn't it—that she was right. He *was* good at that arithmetic. He'd been doing it for eleven years, balancing lives on an abacus until the beads blurred, and every time the sum came out the same: *survive now, grieve later, call the grieving prudence.* He thought of Ines at nine years old, memorizing waymarks by candlelight, looking up at him with that fierce, unsentimental devotion, believing he was the best of the old breed. He thought of the look that would come into her eyes when she learned—not if, *when*—that the second betrayal had been his too. Because there would be a second betrayal. Vess hadn't asked him to decide tonight, but they both knew the shape of the thing. Handlers never asked twice. They simply waited, patient as lime, for the flesh to soften.

"I need time," he said.

"You have until the snow stops falling after next." Vess finished her wine and rose, smoothing her plain grey coat. At the door she paused, and for one moment something almost human crossed her face—pity, maybe, or its well-dressed cousin. "For what it's worth, Corvin, I don't think you're a bad man. I think you're a tired one. There's a difference, and the Crown spends a great deal of money exploiting it."

Then she was gone, and the lamp burned low, and Corvin sat alone in the cold counting house with the pardon lying on the table between the wine cups, gleaming like ice, like a road, like a door.

* * *

He walked home the long way, past the guildhall.

He told himself it was caution—scouting the ground, confirming the child's presence, gathering intelligence. Eleven years of self-deception had given him a fluent inner voice; it could justify anything in iambic pentameter if he let it. But his feet knew the truth of it, and his feet took him down the alley behind the hall, to the spot where, if you pressed the third board of the fence and knew the trick of it, you could see through the gap into the kitchen yard.

Lamplight in the kitchen window. Steam on the glass. And through the fogged pane, shapes: Maren at the table, bent over something—cards, probably, the old woman's eternal patience-game—and Ines moving between stove and cupboard with that economy of motion he'd taught her himself, and at the table's end, small and upright and watchful, the child. Eating. Being warmed. Being *kept*.

Corvin stood in the freezing alley with snow beginning to come down—the first flakes, fat and slow, the long winter opening its ledger—and watched the light in that window the way a drowning man watches a ship he has personally scuttled.

He tried to do the arithmetic one more time. It was his discipline, his craft, the skill that had kept him alive when better men than him were ash on the wind. *One child, forty lives. One mute orphan against the whole surviving body of the brotherhood. What would Maren want? What would the old ferry-masters have done?* And the arithmetic answered him in its familiar dry voice, and for the first time in eleven years, Corvin heard the voice clearly for what it was: not pragmatism. Not wisdom. Just fear, wearing his own accent, quoting his own excuses back to him in a reasonable tone.

Because here was the thing the arithmetic never included, the variable he'd been excluding since '61: the Guild had never been about the roads. Roads were just lines on paper; the Crown could burn paper all day. The Guild was the *keeping*. The keeping of people who needed keeping, when every lawful door was shut. That was the whole creed, the one they'd sung the children to sleep with in the ferry-camps, the one Maren had beaten into him between beatings of the road-songs: *a Veilwright carries what cannot carry itself.* And a child who walked out of a snowstorm speaking the drowned tongue was, by any honest measure, the most Veilwright cargo that had come through those doors in sixty years.

And he was being asked to sell it. Again. To the same buyers. With the same shining arithmetic.

Corvin pressed his forehead against the frozen fence board, and the cold bit into his skin, and he let it, because pain was honest in a way he no longer trusted himself to be. Through the gap he watched Ines set a bowl in front of the child, and watched the child look up at her—at this stranger, this smuggler, this last ragged heir of a murdered brotherhood—and saw something pass between them that had no price he could calculate. Trust, maybe. Or the beginning of it. The thing he had spent eleven years converting into silver and spending down to dross.

*They'll find out,* he thought. *One way or another, they'll find out what I am. Better they find out from my mouth than from an Ashcart's.*

But even as he thought it, he felt the old fluency rising, the smooth inner clerk already drafting the justifications, weighing angles, pricing loyalties. Eleven years of treachery does not wash out in one night of snow. He straightened, pulled his collar up, and turned away from the light in the window, and the snow began to thicken around him, soft and white and indifferent, erasing his footprints behind him as though the world itself were helping him pretend he had never come.

Two paths home, and he took the longer one, and all the way he told himself he hadn't yet decided.

It was a lie. It had been a lie since the grey-sealed letter. The only question—the question that would follow him up into the high passes like a wolf following blood—was which way the lie would break.

Behind him, in the kitchen of the guildhall, a child slept under a smuggler's blanket, and an old woman played out her patience game, and the first snow of the long winter fell on Thornfell, falling and falling, covering the roads, covering the roofs, covering everything except the one debt that no snow would ever reach.
