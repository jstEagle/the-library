## 7. Flight into Thornfell

The signal-fire came at dusk, and it came wrong.

Ines knew the grammar of smoke the way she knew her own heartbeat. One column, thin and steady, meant a road was open. Two columns meant a buyer waited. Three short bursts, fed with wet green pine so the smoke came fat and bitter, meant only one thing in the whole wide vocabulary of the Veilwright Guild: *run.*

She saw it from the kitchen window while she was warming milk for Ember, the smoke rising off Crag's Rest, the shepherd's hut three miles down the valley where old Toma kept his goats and his eyes on the low road. Three bursts, then a pause, then three more. Toma was alive, then. Toma was running too, or had already gone to ground. But the fire said what it said, and Ines had been raised on the saying that a smuggler who argued with a signal died arguing.

"Maren," she called, and her voice came out steadier than her hands. "Maren, we're leaving."

The old woman was in the front room, wrapped in two blankets despite the fire, the stolen page from the Guild's buried archive spread across her knees like a relic she was afraid to touch too hard. She looked up, and Ines watched her read the situation in a single glance—the milk abandoned on the stove, Ines already pulling the satchels down from the loft.

"How long do we have?"

"Until whoever lit that fire stops being the only thing between us and the valley road." Ines was moving as she talked, her body remembering the old drills, the ones Corvin had drilled into her when she was twelve and all knees and hunger: *pack light, pack fast, pack like the Ashcarts are already at the gate.* "Ember—Ember, come here, love. We're playing the packing game."

Ember came without being called, the way the child always did, silent as snowfall. They had been in the guildhall eleven days now, and in that time Ines had learned to read the child's stillness the way she read smoke. Ember stood in the doorway with their head tilted, watching her fill the satchels with the economy of long habit: dried meat, the good flint, the wax-sealed packet of medicines, Maren's page wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against her spine where no search would find it. The child's eyes tracked every item, and there was something in that watching—something old and patient and terribly awake.

"You know what's coming," Ines said quietly, kneeling to the child's level. "Don't you. You knew before the fire did."

Ember didn't nod. Ember never nodded. But the child reached out and pressed one small hand flat against Ines's chest, over her heart, and held it there a moment, and Ines felt something pass through the touch like a current under ice. A warning. A promise. She couldn't have said which.

"Right," she said, and stood, and was glad her voice didn't shake. "Maren. Can you ride?"

"I can ride." Maren was folding the page with hands that trembled only a little. "I've been riding since before your father was a mistake, girl. What I can't do is run. So we ride."

* * *

They went out the back, through the goat-shed, down the smugglers' stair that was cut into the hillside behind the guildhall and hidden under a slab of granite no Crown surveyor had ever thought to question. Ines went last, pulling the slab down over them, and the moment the stone settled she heard it—hooves, many hooves, on the valley road below, and beneath that a sound that made her blood go thin and cold: the low, sweet singing of the Ashcarts' fire-hymns, carried on the wind the way a smell is carried.

They're singing already. They haven't even arrived, and they're singing.

The smugglers' stair let out into the old goat-tunnel, and the goat-tunnel into the birch wood above the hall, where three of the Guild's remaining horses stood hobbled where Ines had stabled them two days ago, sensing trouble the way animals sense weather. She got Maren onto the bay mare first, then Ember up behind her, then swung onto the dun, and they went up. Always up. Thornfell rose to the north like a sleeping animal, its shoulders lost in cloud, and the smugglers' roads went into it the way veins go into a body.

Behind her, Ines did not look back. She made herself not look back. But she could hear the guildhall in her mind anyway—the low rooms, the smoke-stained rafters, the great table where the route-books had lain open for two hundred years, the hearth where every Veilwright had been marked with the guild's ash-sign when they took their vows. She had been marked there at fifteen. She could still feel the cool of the ash-touched iron on her brow.

*The hall will burn tonight,* she thought, with terrible certainty. *Whatever the Ashcarts find or don't find, they will burn it, because that is what they do. They are not investigators. They are erasers.*

The first flakes came an hour into the climb, drifting down out of a sky the color of dirty wool. Ines felt them land on her knuckles and knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a mountain woman, that this was no ordinary snow. This was the long winter's opening argument. The old smugglers had a name for the first snow that didn't melt—*the Verge*, they called it, the snow that meant the passes were closing and the world was shutting its eye. And here it was, arriving on the same night the Crown came for them, as if the winter itself had decided to take sides.

"Girl." Maren's voice, thin with cold but level. "The child is shivering. We need the waystation."

"There's nothing between here and Hollow Bridge."

"There's the Kettle." Maren's breath steamed. "The old boiling-spring station, up under the first shoulder. Half the roof's gone but the spring keeps the rocks warm. I haven't been up there in forty years, but I'd bet my grave the hollows are still there."

Ines hesitated. The Kettle meant leaving the main smugglers' road for a spur she'd only ever seen on maps—maps that were ash now, all of them, everywhere in the kingdom, except the one wrapped in oilcloth against Maren's spine. But the snow was thickening, and Ember's shivering had gone from a tremor to a shudder, and the child's lips had a bluish tinge that frightened Ines more than any number of Ashcarts.

"The Kettle," she agreed. "Hold on, love. Hold on to Maren."

* * *

The spur road was barely a road at all—more a suggestion of passage, marked by waymarks so old they had sunk to the hips in the hillside. Ines found them by feel and memory, and by something else, something she didn't yet have a name for: a tug, faint as a fisherman's line, whenever Ember was near a mark. The child seemed to draw the hidden things up out of the ground like breath. Twice, Ines would have sworn the waymarks *glowed*—a faint ember-light under the lichen, there and gone, like something turning over in its sleep.

She didn't say it aloud. Some things, said aloud, become true in ways you can't take back.

They reached the Kettle as full dark fell, and it was as Maren remembered: a bowl of black rock cupping a boiling spring, steam rolling off the water in great slow banners, the ruins of the station roof jutting like the ribs of a drowned ship. The hollows behind the spring were dry, and warm—warm from the rock itself—and when Ines got Ember out of the wet clothes and wrapped the child in every blanket they carried, the shivering finally slowed.

Maren sat with her back to the warm stone, and for a while none of them spoke. The spring muttered to itself. The snow came down thicker, erasing the world beyond the steam, and Ines realized that by morning there would be no trail, no tracks, no sign of their passage. The winter was hiding them. Or swallowing them. With the Verge, it had always been hard to say which.

Then she saw it. Down the valley, far off, a glow on the underside of the clouds. Orange. Flickering.

The guildhall.

Ines stood up without deciding to, and stood there in the steam with the snow landing in her hair and melting, and watched the light that was her whole life burning.

* * *

She had thought she was ready for it. She had rehearsed it, in the way you rehearse a grief you know is coming—the way you rehearse a parent's death, a master's, a friend's. She had told herself: *it's stone and timber. It's a table and a hearth and some bunk-rooms. The Guild is not the building. The Guild is the roads, the songs, the people, the craft. The Guild walks.*

But watching the glow, she found that the rehearsed grief was a thin coat over a much older, deeper one, and it came up through her like floodwater.

She thought of her first night in the hall, aged nine, orphaned and furious, sitting on the great table's edge while Corvin fed her stew and told her she'd never make a smuggler because she asked too many questions. She thought of the marking-day, the ash-sign cool on her forehead, Maren's hand on her shoulder, the whole guild singing the road-song in voices that filled the rafters—*and there were so many of them then, so many voices, and now, now there is me and a dying woman and a mute child, and the song is down to three throats, and one of them doesn't speak—*

"Ines."

Maren's hand on her arm. The old woman had come to stand beside her, wrapped in blankets, her face lit orange by the distant fire.

"I know," Ines said. Her voice came out cracked. "I know, Maren. It's just stone."

"It's not just stone." Maren's voice was rough. "It's two hundred years of us. And it's all right to grieve it. We're allowed. That's what the burning wants, you know. The Ashcarts, the Crown, all of them—they want us scattered and ashamed and too busy running to mourn. Mourning is how a thing stays alive, girl. A thing properly mourned can't be properly killed." She squeezed Ines's arm. "So mourn it. But mourn it walking."

Ines laughed—a wet, broken sound—and wiped her face with the back of her hand, and found that Ember had come to stand at her other side, small and silent, one hand raised toward the distant glow as if in blessing or farewell. The child's fingers moved, tracing shapes in the air, and though Ines didn't know Old Sareli yet—wouldn't know it for weeks yet, though she couldn't have guessed that then—she understood somehow, the way you understand a thing under language, that the child was saying goodbye too. That Ember, who remembered a whole drowned kingdom, knew exactly what it was to watch a world burn and be unable to carry more than a handful of it out.

"Come inside, both of you," Maren said. "The snow's doing our work for us. We'll rest till the small hours, then move on the night-snow. Hollow Bridge by the second dawn, if the Verge allows it."

They went back into the hollows. Ines fed the little spirit-stove and made tea from the last of the good leaves, and Ember drank from her hands like a small bird, and Maren told stories—old guild stories, the ones that had never been written down because writing them down was how the Crown found things—stories of the Ferryman, of the veil-roads, of the years when the Veilwrights had moved not just goods but *souls*, whole families, whole villages, out of the path of the Crown's fires and into a country that was no longer on any map.

Ines listened, and the grief in her chest changed shape. It was still grief. But it had a direction now. It pointed forward, up the mountain, toward whatever waited at the end of the roads Maren was describing.

* * *

They moved at the small hours, as Maren had said, on the night-snow, when the cold is so complete that the snow squeaks underfoot and sound carries for miles and no one in their right mind is abroad. Which was, of course, precisely why smugglers traveled then. Ines led, reading the waymarks by the light of a shuttered lantern, and Ember rode wrapped against Maren's back, and the world was a white silence with three small lives moving through it.

Near dawn, on a ridge with the whole of the valley spread below, Ines stopped the party and looked back one last time. The guildhall was a black ruin now, its fire out, smoke still rising in a thin gray ribbon. The snow was already softening its edges, laying a white shroud over the collapsed roof, the scorched goat-shed, the smugglers' stair she'd sealed behind them with a fall of rock. By spring it would look like nothing had ever stood there. By summer, moss. In ten years, a story.

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she did what her masters had taught her, what the Guild had always done, what the craft itself demanded: she took the memory, and she folded it small, and she put it away in the place where smugglers keep the things they cannot afford to carry openly.

"Goodbye," she said, to the ruin, to the two hundred years, to Corvin—who was not there, who had not come, who had not warned them, and whose absence had begun to feel less like luck and more like a shape with a meaning in it. "You kept us. I'll carry you."

Ember's small hand found hers in the dark.

They rode north into the passes, into the thickening snow, into the long winter's opening white silence, and behind them the valley closed like a book.

They did not see the rider who followed, three miles back, moving through the snow without a lantern, reading the waymarks as easily as Ines did—because there was only one other person alive who knew them that well.

They did not see him smile, without warmth, and murmur to the empty air: "There you are, little ember. There you are."

But the mountain saw. And the mountain, being old, and being Sarel's in its bones, remembered.
