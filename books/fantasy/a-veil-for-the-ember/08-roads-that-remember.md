## 8. Roads That Remember

The snow came down like a verdict.

Ines had walked the Thornfell passes in every season a mountain could offer—spring slush that sucked at your boots like a drowning man's grip, summer haze where the scree shimmered and lied about distance, autumn wind that sang through the pines in voices she'd learned to ignore. But this was different. This was the first snow of the long winter, the one old Maren had been muttering about since the guildhall burned behind them, and it fell with a patience that frightened her more than any Ashcart hound. It did not flurry or gust. It simply came down, flake after flake after flake, as if the sky had all the time in the world to bury them, and intended to use it.

She broke trail ahead, Ember strapped against her back beneath her cloak, Maren a step behind with a smuggler's staff for a walking stick. The old woman had refused to be carried, refused even Ines's arm, but three days of climbing had bent her low, and each breath came out of her in a thin white whistle, like steam escaping a kettle on its last boil.

"Left at the split birch," Maren rasped, when the trail forked at a wall of stone furred white with frost. "Then down through the hollow, not over."

"I know," Ines said. "I've run this road since I was fourteen."

"You've run the *road*," Maren said, with the particular sharpness she saved for correcting people she loved. "This is something else. Keep your eyes open, girl. The Thornfell roads don't all live in Thornfell."

Ines had no answer for that. She'd stopped having answers two nights ago, when Ember had touched the first waymark—a plain cairn of stacked stones, half-swallowed by moss—and the stone had lit from within like a coal breathed upon, glowing a deep amber through the dark, humming faintly under the child's small palm before fading again. Ines had told herself it was a trick of exhaustion, of grief, of the strange light off the snow. But then it had happened at the second waymark, and the third: wherever Ember's fingers found the old signs—the chiseled loops and ladder-marks the Veilwrights had cut into stone centuries ago and let everyone forget—they woke. And they woke singing, if you could call it that: a sound below sound, felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ear, like the memory of a bell.

Now, passing the split birch, Ines saw the waymark carved into the trunk itself—an old mark she had rubbed her thumb across a hundred times without ever once seeing it glow—and she watched it kindle anyway, slow and warm, as Ember's hand brushed past through the wrap on her back.

"Every time," she murmured. "Why every time?"

Maren didn't answer. The old woman was looking up at the falling snow with an expression Ines had never seen on her face—not fear exactly, and not wonder either, but the terrible tenderness of someone recognizing handwriting on a letter they'd spent forty years pretending never arrived.

* * *

They made the hollow by late afternoon, though afternoon had become a rumor; the sky was the color of pewter and gave nothing back. The hollow was a smuggler's trick older than the Guild's written records: a fold in the mountainside where the trail seemed to end at a cliff face, and only those who knew to walk *into* the stone—trusting the angle, counting eleven paces with their left shoulder brushing rock—would find the passage opening like a held breath released. Ines had brought contraband through it a dozen times: salt, silk, fugitive ledgers, once a whole family of debt-bondsmen wrapped in wool and drugged on willow tea so the children wouldn't cry.

She counted her paces. Left shoulder to the rock. One. Two. Three.

On seven, the stone under her palm was warm.

Not sun-warm—there was no sun. Warm like skin. Warm like a hand pressed flat against the inside of a door, waiting. She stopped so abruptly that Maren nearly walked into her.

"What is it?"

"It's warm." Ines pulled her glove off with her teeth and laid her bare fingers against the rock. Beneath the cold surface, faint as a pulse in a wrist, something beat. "Maren. The rock is *warm*."

"Keep walking," Maren said quietly. "Count."

"Eleven paces. I know the count."

"The count's changed, girl. Count what it asks, not what you remember."

Ines swallowed. She walked. At nine the warmth spread up her arm like water rising in a well. At ten she heard it—not felt it this time, actually *heard* it, threading up out of the stone and into the marrow of her: a melody, five notes descending, then rising again, patient as tide. A road-song. One of the ones the old masters used to hum while mending harness, the ones everyone assumed were just habit, just rhythm, just the mindless music of hands at work.

Ember stirred against her back. Small fingers found the gap between Ines's collar and neck and gripped, and the child began, very softly, to hum along—in tune, in time, in *Old Sareli*, though there were no words yet, only the shape the words would fit into.

At eleven, the cliff opened.

Not the crack-in-the-rock passage she remembered. It opened the way a curtain opens, the stone drawing aside smooth as oiled silk, and beyond it lay not the cramped smuggling crawl she knew but a gallery—a high, arched corridor of pale stone, ribbed like the inside of a shell, running deeper into the mountain than lamplight could follow. Along both walls, at intervals, stood waymarks: carved pillars waist-high, their surfaces crowded with the looped script of Old Sareli, and as Ember hummed they kindled one after another down the length of the hall, a procession of amber lights bowing awake like courtiers greeting a returning king.

Ines stood on the threshold with snow melting in her hair and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

"This isn't a smuggler's road," she said. Her voice sounded very small in all that waking light.

"No," Maren said. "It isn't."

"These are our roads. Guild roads. Corvin ran salt through here before I was born."

"And who do you think taught the Veilwrights to build roads, girl?" Maren limped forward into the gallery, leaning hard on her staff, her breath ragged. Her eyes were wet, and whether from cold or something else, Ines couldn't tell. "Where do you think the routes came from? You thought a band of river-smugglers invented the hollow-pass and the split-birch turn out of cleverness? We inherited them. All of them. Every hidden way in these mountains is a scar left by something older—and we built our little trade on top of it and called ourselves masters." She laughed, a dry, broken sound. "We weren't masters. We were squatters. We were children playing house in a cathedral, and we didn't even know whose god we'd moved in with."

Ember slid down from Ines's back and walked into the gallery without hesitation, without fear, the way a child walks into their own kitchen. The waymarks leaned their light toward the small figure like flowers toward the sun. And Ines saw, following, that the carvings on the nearest pillar were not random ornament. They were instructions. She could almost read them now—two weeks of Ember's patient teaching had given her the alphabet's bones—and what she could read made the hair rise on her arms:

*Wayfarer, be welcome. Wayfarer, be counted. Wayfarer, be remembered.*

"It knows us," she whispered. "The road *knows* us."

"It doesn't know *us*," Maren said. "Look at it, girl. Look at where the light goes."

And Ines looked, and saw. The light did not fall evenly. It gathered around Ember, pooled at the child's feet, followed the small body down the gallery like a devoted dog. Wherever Ember walked, the stones brightened. Behind the child, in their wake, they dimmed again—but slower now than before, reluctant, holding their ember-glow a few heartbeats longer each time, as if learning to stay lit.

As if remembering how.

* * *

They camped that night in an alcove off the gallery, a shallow chamber with a hearth-vent that still drew air after—who knew—centuries, and walls carved with more of the looping script. Ines got a fire going from damp wood and stubbornness, heated snow for water, and fed Maren broth from the last of their stores while the old woman dozed upright against the wall. Ember sat close to the flames, turning their hands toward the heat, and hummed the road-song over and over, softening it, varying it, the way a musician rehearses.

Ines watched the firelight move on the carvings and tried to read.

"*Sarel ieth, sarel vane*," she sounded out, clumsily, using the sounds Ember had drilled into her over soup and snowmelt. "Kingdom... under? Kingdom... beneath?" She frowned. "That can't be right. Why would a road in Thornfell say 'the kingdom beneath'?"

"Because it isn't in Thornfell," Ember signed—or rather, began to sign, and then stopped, frustrated, and did the thing the child sometimes did instead of words: pressed Ines's hand flat against the carved wall and held it there.

The stone was warm. Warmer than before. And under her palm, faint as a sleeper's heartbeat, Ines felt movement—not vibration exactly, not water, but *passage*, a long slow current flowing through the rock the way blood flows through flesh, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, with her whole animal body, that this mountain was not a mountain the way she had always believed mountains were mountains. It was a wall. A wall between rooms. And somewhere on the other side of it—

She snatched her hand back. Her heart was slamming.

"Ines." Maren's voice, thin but awake. The old woman's eyes had opened, and they were fixed on her with terrible gentleness. "You feel it too, then. Good. Better you learn it sitting down than standing up."

"Feel *what*, Maren? What am I feeling? Tell me plainly, because I swear if you say 'something older' one more time I'll—"

"The veil." Maren shifted, wincing, drawing her blanket tighter. "You've heard the word your whole life. Veilwrights. Veil-road. You thought it was a name, a flourish, guild branding." She shook her head slowly. "It's a measurement, girl. It's how *thin*. The veil between what is and what was, between here and—" her hand made a vague gesture that took in the wall, the gallery, the glowing waymarks, the whole buried architecture of a truth nobody living had bothered with, "—between the world that stayed and the world that drowned. Sarel didn't fall *away*, child. It fell *through*. And it's still there. On the other side of walls like this one. Sleeping under our winters like a bride under linen, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

Maren looked at Ember. The firelight caught the child's face, calm, luminous, utterly unafraid, and the old woman's voice dropped until it was barely more than breath.

"For somebody to knock."

Silence, except for the fire and the wind above the stone and Ember's low, endless humming. Ines sat with her arms around her knees and felt the ground under her—not the ground, the *wall*—breathing its slow warm pulse, and she thought about every road she'd ever run, every hollow and cairn and split birch, all the secret geography of her life's work, and understood that she had spent twenty years memorizing the veins of a body without once asking what the body was *for*.

"We're being watched," she said finally. It wasn't fear speaking. It was certainty, arriving the way certainty does—flat, quiet, undeniable. "The road. The marks. Whatever wakes when Ember touches it. Something's paying attention. Something's been paying attention since the first night, maybe since before the child ever reached our door."

"Yes," Maren said.

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"Everything frightens me, girl. I'm eighty-one years old; fear is my oldest friend." Maren closed her eyes again. "But I was the last soul ferried out of a drowning kingdom, and I have spent fifty years telling myself the ferryman's song was just a song. If something on the other side of that wall has been listening to me sing all this time—" her voice caught, and steadied, "—then perhaps I wasn't singing to no one after all. There are worse things than being watched, Ines Oyelaran. Being forgotten is worse. Ask any ghost."

Ember had stopped humming.

The child was standing, facing down the long gallery, toward the direction they'd been traveling—toward whatever lay at the road's end, days away yet, deeper into the mountain's heart. The waymarks along that stretch were lighting one after another, ahead of the child, unbidden, a path of amber coins laid down through the dark, leading onward. And though no wind could reach this far under the stone, Ines heard—she would swear it to the end of her days, whatever name she ended them under—she heard a sound coming up the corridor from the deep places. Not footsteps. Not voices.

A second humming.

Answering.

Same tune. Same key. Slower, older, worn round at the edges like a coin passed through ten thousand hands—but the same song, rising to meet them out of the mountain's dark, and under it, unmistakable now, that sub-heard bell-tone gathering strength, the waymarks flaring brighter and brighter down the whole length of the gallery until the pale stone walls blazed like dawn through honey.

Ember turned and looked at Ines. The child's face was shining with reflected light and something else, something that cracked Ines's heart straight down its oldest seam: hope. Raw, naked, unbearable hope, the hope of someone who has been mute for years and has just heard their own language spoken in the dark by a stranger's voice.

*Mama?* the small hands signed, trembling. *Is that—could it be—*

Ines knelt and pulled the child against her chest and held on, and over the small shoulder she met Maren's eyes across the fire, and saw her own dread mirrored there, because they both understood the same terrible arithmetic at the same moment:

Whatever waited at the end of this road knew Ember's name before Ember had one. Knew the song. Was calling the child home—or calling them *in*.

And behind them, far back down the mountain, faint but persistent, carried on the wind through the passage mouth, came another sound entirely: the belling of hounds, and the thin brass note of Ashcart horns.

The road ahead was singing. The hunters were behind. And the veil, Maren had said, was thinner than anyone believed.

Ines banked the fire, shouldered her pack, and took Ember's small hand in hers.

"Stay close," she said, to the child, to the old woman, to whatever listened in the stone. "We walk at first light."
