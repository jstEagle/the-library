## 9. The Ferryman's Grave

The waystation announced itself the way old grief does—gradually, then all at once.

First there was only the shape of the ridge against a sky gone the color of dirty pewter, and then Ines, walking point with Ember bundled against her chest, raised one mittened hand: *there*. Maren squinted through the fat flakes that had been falling since dawn, and saw it emerge from the whiteness the way a face surfaces from deep water—a low stone building crouched into the mountainside, roof sagging under years of snowpack, its chimney standing like a broken tooth. The smugglers of her girlhood had called such places *rests*, though no one resting in them had ever truly rested. They were waymarks on roads that did not officially exist, stitched through Thornfell like sutures through flesh.

Maren's knees knew this one. That was the terrible thing. Sixty years, and her knees still remembered the last twelve steps up from the trail, the way the third one wobbled loose underfoot, the way you had to lean left at the door because the frame had settled crooked sometime before the Crown was even dreamed of.

"Careful on the step," she said, and heard how old her own voice sounded, thin as paper over the wind.

Ines went in first, one hand on the knife at her belt, though Maren could have told her nothing human had sheltered here since the snows began. The girl had changed in the weeks since the child came knocking. She stood straighter and moved quieter, and there were moments—when she counted their supplies, when she read the wind off the peaks, when she hummed half a bar of an old road-song without seeming to know she was doing it—when Maren looked at her and saw not her apprentice's apprentice but the Guild itself, wearing a young woman's body like a borrowed coat.

The interior smelled of cold stone, mouse droppings, and beneath both, faint as a fingerprint, of tallow and pine tar—the old smell of a Veilwright rest. Someone had stacked firewood against the far wall decades ago and someone else had covered it with oilcloth, and the cloth had rotted but the wood beneath had kept dry enough to burn. Ines got a fire going in the great hearth while Maren lowered herself onto the bench near the eastern wall, where a family of some long-vanished smuggler had carved their initials into the seat: *T.V. loves A.M.*, and beneath it, smaller, *we go by night*.

Ember crossed the room without a word—the child never used words unless words were wrung out—and sat down close against Maren's side, the way a cat sits near a fire, and leaned their small weight into her ribs.

That was when Maren felt it happen again. The loosening. Like a knot that has been pulled too tight for too long finally beginning, against all sense, to slip.

She was seventy-nine years old. She had buried two husbands, outlived every person who had ever called her by her childhood name, and kept one secret so large and so heavy for six decades that she had come to think of it not as a secret but as an organ, something she'd been born with, something that would have to be cut out if it ever came out at all.

And now a mute child out of a drowned kingdom was leaning against her side, warm as bread, humming a lullaby in Old Sareli that Maren's sister used to sing.

*Briseis*, Maren thought, for the ten-thousandth time. *Oh, Briseis.*

* * *

The storm closed in through the afternoon. By dusk the windows were solid white and the wind had found its voice, a long low moan around the crooked chimney that sounded almost like singing, almost like the high keening notes of the Sareli mourning-songs, and Maren sat with her hands folded in her lap and watched Ember play on the floor with three river stones, arranging them and rearranging them into patterns that meant something—Maren was sure they meant something—in a grammar the world had forgotten.

Ines was out checking the snares she'd set below the treeline before full dark. Maren heard the door open and shut, heard the girl's footsteps crunch away, and understood with a strange calm that this was the moment. There would never be a better one. There might very well be no other one at all; she had been coughing blood-threads into her sleeve for two days now and telling no one, and the cold was setting up housekeeping in her chest like a tenant who intended to stay.

"Ember," she said.

The child looked up. Dark eyes, dark as the water under a dock. In certain lights—and the firelight was one of those lights—they were Briseis's eyes exactly, and Maren had stopped pretending to herself about what that did to her.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Maren said. "You won't understand any of it, and that's all right. Maybe you'll understand more than I think. Maybe—" She laughed, a dry sound like a stick snapping. "Maybe that's why you came."

Ember set the stones down in a careful row and turned to face her fully, cross-legged, hands in lap, waiting. The child had the stillness of the very young and the very old, and it unnerved people. It had unnerved Maren once. Not anymore.

"My name is Maren Voss," she said. "That's the second time in sixty years I've said my whole name aloud. The last time was at a border desk in a town that doesn't exist anymore either." She pulled her shawl tighter. "I was born in Sarel. In a city called Ilvane, on the coast, in a street of rope-makers, in a house with blue shutters my father repainted every spring whether it needed it or not. You wouldn't know it. No map knows it. That's rather the point."

The wind sang. Ember did not move.

"When I was nine years old," Maren said, "the water started coming."

* * *

Everyone thought they knew the story of Sarel's drowning. Even now, forty years after the Crown burned the archives, children in schoolrooms learned the official version: a kingdom grown decadent, a sea grown hungry, an act of God or geology, take your pick depending on which priest or scholar was doing the telling. What the schoolrooms did not teach—what the Ashcarts had spent a generation making sure no one taught—was that the drowning had taken eleven years. Eleven years of tides creeping further inland each season, of wells turning brackish, of the low streets going under first while the hill districts held church services thanking providence that it was the poor quarters flooding and not theirs. Eleven years during which anyone with money or connections got out early and clean, and everyone else stayed because staying was all they had.

And eleven years during which a brotherhood of ferrymen and road-menders—the guild that would one day be called the Veilwrights, though Maren's father had known them by their older name, the *Passage-Men*—discovered that the veil between the world was worn thin along the drowned kingdom's oldest pilgrimage roads, and could be opened, briefly, carefully, by people who knew the songs.

"They didn't carry people across the sea," Maren told Ember, whose eyes had not left her face. "There was nowhere to carry them *to*. Every border was shut to Sareli refugees by the fourth year—all the kingdoms were terrified the drowning was catching, if you can believe such stupidity, and I assure you I watched otherwise sensible men believe it. So the Passage-Men found another way. Through. Under. Sideways. Through the veil, into the country behind the country, the one that has no name because it never needed one." She shook her head slowly. "Your language has a word for it. I've heard you say it in your sleep. *Ael-Sarel.* The Sarel-behind-the-world. The reflection."

Eleven years of crossings. Whole congregations walked through singing, led by ferrymen with lanterns that burned without heat, and came out the other side into green country under a sky the color of unpolished silver. Maren's father had been a rope-maker and a stubborn man, and he had refused to leave until the water reached the second floor, and by then the passages were strained, the tolls demanded by the veil growing stranger and heavier each year, the songs degrading as the old singers died.

Maren was seventeen when she and Briseis stood in the last queue. Their parents were already through—gone ahead two winters prior to make a place—and the sisters had stayed with an aunt who insisted family duty kept her, and then the aunt died, and then there was nothing left in Ilvane worth drowning for.

"The ferryman's name was Odo," Maren said. "He had a voice like wet gravel and he'd made, by his own count, four hundred crossings. He was old. Older than the songs he sang. And at the threshold he turned around and counted heads, the way he always did, and we were one too many."

She had told herself ten thousand stories about that moment. That there hadn't been time. That Odo had chosen, and his choice wasn't hers to answer for. That the toll was already paid, the song already sung, the door already narrowing, and one more soul stepping through would have collapsed it on all of them.

All true, perhaps. All beside the point.

"Briseis pushed me forward," Maren said, and her voice did not break, because it had done all its breaking years ago and now merely spoke flatly, like a ledger being read. "She was fifteen. She put both hands on my back, between the shoulder blades, and she shoved me through the gap, and I fell through onto grass that smelled like summer, on the wrong side, alone. And I turned around and the door was closing, and she was smiling at me. She was *smiling*, do you understand, and saying something, and the door ate the sound of it, and I have spent sixty years trying to guess what she said."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, under tons of snow, the mountain ticked and settled.

"There were eleven of us who came through that night," Maren said. "The last crossing. Odo sealed the door behind us—or tried to—and the strain of it killed him within the year, and the others scattered, and none of them knew the whole craft, because Odo had been the last who did. So the Guild that survived was a Guild with its heart cut out. We kept the roads. We kept the marks and the signals and the smuggling, because smuggling pays, and we told ourselves we'd go back someday, reopen the way, bring the rest out. And the Crown found our records and burned them, and the ones of us who knew anything went quiet, and quiet became a habit, and habit became a life." She looked down at her knotted hands. "I let them turn us into thieves, girl, because thieves are allowed to forget."

Ember unfolded from the floor. Crossed the space between them. Small cold hands took Maren's face, one palm on each cheek, the way you hold something precious and breakable.

And the child leaned in until their foreheads touched, and hummed.

It was the lullaby. Of course it was the lullaby—it was the first thing the child had ever sung in that guildhall doorway—but this time Ember sang it slow, note by note, watching Maren's face as if asking a question with each phrase, and on the seventh bar the melody bent into a mode Maren hadn't heard since a blue-shuttered house on a rope-maker's street, and the words—if the soft syllables the child breathed could be called words—resolved into something she had spent six decades failing to forget:

*Sleep, little wave. The tide will carry you home.*

It was what their mother sang. It was the last thing Briseis had been humming in the queue at the threshold, Maren was suddenly, violently certain of it, certain the way you are certain of things you have known all along and refused to look at.

"You," Maren whispered, and the word came out cracked down the middle. "What *are* you?"

Ember pulled back and looked at her, and for a moment—Maren would swear this until she died, however long or short that was—for a moment the child's eyes were not dark but sea-colored, gray-green and moving, and behind them was a depth no child should own.

Then the light shifted, and it was only a mute child by a fire again, and outside the window the storm screamed like something in mourning.

*They're a message,* Maren thought, and the thought arrived not as speculation but as recognition, the way you recognize your own handwriting. *The kingdom drowned, and it wrote a letter, and it sent it through sixty years of winter to a smuggler's door. A message, or an apology. Or a summons.*

Or perhaps simply this: a small warm body pressed back against her side, a head finding the hollow of her shoulder, three river stones lined up on the floorboards like graves in a row.

* * *

Ines came back after full dark with one snow-hare and frost in her eyebrows, and she knew the moment she stepped through the door that something in the room had changed. Maren saw her read it—the wet tracks on Maren's face, Ember's hand still curled in the old woman's fingers—and saw her decide, with the particular mercy of the young, not to ask.

"Hare," Ines said instead, holding it up. "And the snares'll hold another day, if the weather lets them."

"Sit," Maren said. Her voice was raw and lighter than it had been in sixty years, the way a room is lighter after furniture is carried out. "And while you skin that, I'm going to tell you what the Guild actually was. All of it. What Odo knew, and what died with him, and what didn't."

Ines froze halfway out of her coat. "Maren—"

"I sat silent while they burned the records," Maren said. "I sat silent for forty years, Ines, and silence is a debt like any other, and I am done pretending I don't owe." She nodded at the fire, at the child, at the girl with the hare and the knife. "So sit. Both of you. Because there's a verse of the door-song Odo only ever taught to ferrymen, and I am the last living creature that carries it—and I find, tonight, that I want it to live somewhere other than in me."

Outside, the storm went on singing in its tuneless way, and under the mountain, in the dark, something that had waited a very long time seemed—Maren would have said, if she believed in saying such things aloud—to wait a little longer.
