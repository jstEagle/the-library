## 10. Hounds of the Ash

The ravine had a name once. The old route-books called it the Throat, because that was what it was—a narrow gullet of stone where the river Sorrow ran fast and black beneath shelves of ice, and where a smuggler either moved quiet or did not move at all. Ines had come through it a dozen times in her life with brandy casks on her back and Crown tariffs on her mind, and she had never once thought of it as a place where people died.

She was thinking about it now.

They had been three days in the high passes since the guildhall burned behind them, three days of Maren coughing worse each morning and Ember walking with that terrible, tireless patience children have, as though the world were simply something to be endured until it improved. Three days of looking back over her shoulder at a horizon that refused to give them anything but snow.

And now, far below them along the rim of the ravine, there was smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Woodsmoke rose and softened. This smoke climbed in straight grey columns and held its shape, like a man standing very still, waiting to be noticed.

Ines knew what burned like that. Everyone who had ever worked the Thornfell roads knew. Ashcarts fired their brazier-carts at dawn and let the wind carry the smell ahead of them—the smell of char and old paper and something sweeter underneath, like hair—so that anyone they hunted would catch it on the air and understand, before ever seeing a uniform, exactly what was coming for them.

"Maren." She crouched beside the old woman where she sat propped against a pine trunk, her breath coming in shallow rags. "How are your legs?"

"My legs," Maren said, "have been complaining since I was forty years old. They are merely louder now." She tried to smile. It cost her too much; she abandoned it halfway. "You saw the smoke."

"I saw the smoke."

"Then don't waste time asking after my legs. Ask after the road."

Ines looked down into the Throat. The smugglers' bridge crossed it half a mile south—a rope-and-plank affair called the Widow's Step, built by hands long dead and maintained by hands that no longer existed, sagging between two outcrops over water the color of a bruise. Beyond it, the trail forked: one branch climbing toward the Cold Larder, an old waystation carved into the rock, the other running east along the ridge toward country even the Crown's maps had given up on.

If they could reach the bridge before the trackers reached the ravine, they could be over and gone, and the Widow's Step would take the weight of three thin travelers without complaint. It always had.

It was the *before* that worried her.

* * *

They came down through the trees single file, Ines first, breaking trail, then Ember clutching the strap of Ines's pack, then Maren, leaning on a staff cut from green birch that bent under her grip like a living thing protesting its use. Snow fell in fat, soundless flakes, the kind of snow that swallowed noise so completely the silence felt manufactured, theatrical—as if some enormous hand were cupped over the mountain, holding all its small sounds close.

Ember kept glancing back the way they'd come. Not toward the smoke. Toward something else, lower, closer. The child's face had gone smooth and blank in the way it went when words failed—which, Ines had learned, meant the child understood something perfectly and had no language left to say it in.

"What is it?" Ines whispered.

Ember pointed at the ground. At their own footprints, filling slowly with snow behind them.

"They can't follow prints in this," Ines said. "Not even ash-hounds."

Ember shook her head slowly. Then she knelt, pressed one bare—bare, in this cold, and had been bare for three days without frostbite, which was its own quiet miracle Ines had stopped questioning—palm flat against the snow, and dragged it a handspan, leaving a smear. Then she looked up at Ines and made a low sound in her throat, a sound with a falling cadence, a sound like something being found.

Old Sareli had no word Ines recognized in it. But the meaning arrived anyway, the way meaning sometimes did with Ember, unbidden and unwelcome: *they will find us anyway.*

Maren had gone still behind them. "Child's right," the old woman said quietly. "Ash-hounds don't hunt scent, not the way dogs do. They hunt what the fire leaves. Warmth. Breath. The little heat a body gives off, like embers under ash—that's the whole name of them, girl, that's why the Crown chose it. You can't wash away your own warmth."

"Then we move faster."

"No," Maren said. "Then you stop lying to yourself about what's coming. They've been behind us since the guildhall. Maybe since before the guildhall. Corvin sold more than routes—he sold *habits*. He told them how you travel, how you think, where you'd run." Her voice cracked on his name and she covered it with a cough that bent her double. When she straightened, there was blood-bright color high on her cheeks that had nothing to do with health. "He told them you'd take the high passes, because you love them. Because they're ours."

Ines said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a curse, and curses spent breath she might need.

Below them, through a break in the pines, the Widow's Step came into view—and with it, the thing that turned Ines's blood to slush.

A man stood at the near end of the bridge.

He wore the grey-and-black of the Ashcart service, hood back despite the cold, and he was not hurrying. That was the worst of it. He stood with the patience of someone who has already won something and is simply waiting for the paperwork, and at his feet, straining gently against chains of dull iron, sat two shapes that Ines's eyes took several full seconds to accept as hounds.

They were wrong the way a drowned body is wrong—familiar outline, terrible substance. Their hides looked like packed ash, grey-black and faintly luminous, and where their eyes should have been there was only a deeper dark, and from their mouths came no sound at all. Hounds that did not bark. Hounds that hunted warmth and therefore had no need to announce themselves.

Two more figures emerged from the treeline flanking him, unhurried, spreading out along the rim.

"There's another path," Maren breathed. "Tell me there's another path."

There was not. There was the bridge, and the men, and the drop.

* * *

Ines pulled them back beneath the pines and made herself breathe the way her masters had taught her—four counts in, four held, four out—because panic was just fear moving too fast through the body, and everything in the Veilwright craft was about controlling what moved and how fast.

Think. Think like a smuggler, not a fugitive. What do you have?

She had a bridge she knew plank by plank, built by Guild hands across sixty years of winters. She had a river. She had a child who spoke a dead language and felt doors in her bones. She had an old woman who could not run and knew it and was already, Ines realized with a lurch, doing arithmetic of her own behind those faded eyes—arithmetic Ines refused to look at directly.

And she had one other thing. The thing every Veilwright carried and hoped never to use.

"Stay here," she said. "Both of you. Don't move, don't speak. If they come up this slope—" she couldn't finish it, so she didn't. "Just stay."

She went down through the trees alone, low, using the deadfall the way the route-books taught, keeping the wind in her face so her warmth blew away from the hounds rather than toward them. It was a small deception and she doubted it mattered. The hounds' heads tracked her the entire descent, twin smears of darkness turning in eyeless sockets, and she felt their attention like a hand laid flat against her chest.

Twenty yards from the bridge, the lead tracker raised a gloved hand and she stopped, because the alternative was an arrow or worse.

"Well," the man said. His voice was mild, almost kind, the voice of a schoolmaster. "The last apprentice herself. Corvin described you. He said you'd be the difficult one." He smiled. "He said it fondly."

"The pardon he was promised," Ines said. "Did it come?"

Something flickered in his face—not guilt, nothing so human, but a flicker nonetheless. "Corvin understands how the Crown repays service. Do you? Hand over the child, Oyelaran. That's all. The old woman can die warm somewhere. You can walk away. We're not monsters. We burn records, not people."

"You burned the Halloran family," Ines said. "All nine of them. In their beds. I carried the youngest son's boots out of Thornfell. He was six."

"We burned a house where sedition was stored." The kindness never wavered, and that was the most frightening thing about him. "The child is not a person, whatever shape she wears. She is a remnant of a sealed border, and sealed borders are Crown law. Give her to me."

Behind the man, past him, past the hounds, Ines looked at the Widow's Step. Sixty years of Guild rope. Guild planks. Guild knots, tied by her master's master, load-rated in her head from a hundred crossings: it would hold three travelers, ten casks, a mule if you blessed it.

It would hold five grown men and two ash-hounds too. That was the problem. That was the whole rotten problem.

"Come and take her," Ines said.

The tracker sighed, genuinely regretful, and clicked his tongue.

The hounds came up the slope like spilled ink running uphill, silent, impossibly fast, and Ines was already turning and running, and behind her she heard Maren scream her name and heard something else beneath it—the wet, grinding groan of a rope bridge taking weight it was never meant to take this fast.

She hit the span first, boots hammering planks, and felt the whole structure sway and shudder like a living thing in pain. Halfway. The ropes sang. Below her the Sorrow churned black and white-flecked between ice shelves, hungry, indifferent.

"Ember! Maren! Get on—get on now!"

They came. Gods forgive her, they came—Maren hobbling onto the planks with Ember hauling at her sleeve, and the hounds reaching the near anchorage, and the trackers following at a walk, because they did not need to hurry, did they, the bridge would carry them, the bridge would carry anything—

That was when Ines drew her knife and began cutting the anchor lines.

* * *

The Widow's Step was anchored at each end by four cables, and each cable was braided from three strands of oiled hemp, and every Veilwright apprentice learned in their first year the one unforgivable law of the craft: *the road serves the traveler. Never the hunter.* There were stories—half legend, all true enough—of bridges dropped into gorges with pursuers on them, of stairways folded into cliff-face, of a whole pass closed by avalanche by the last Guildmaster of the eastern roads. The craft of smuggling people was, at its heart, the craft of deciding who arrived. And who did not.

Her knife went through strand one. Strand two. The bridge lurched, dipped, screamed.

"Ines—" Maren's voice, ragged with horror, because the old woman understood instantly what she was doing and what it would cost. "We're not across yet—"

"I know!" The third strand parted. The whole span slewed sideways, tilting toward the river, and Ines threw herself flat and grabbed the remaining cable with both hands as planks rained away beneath her into the dark.

The hounds were on the bridge.

That was the mistake she would relive for the rest of her life, however long the Crown allowed it to be: she had counted men, not beasts, and the hounds weighed nothing, moved like thought, and came flowing across the failing span toward Ember with their eyeless heads low and their mouths finally, horribly, opening—no teeth, only a darkness that seemed to drink the falling snow.

Ember did not scream. That was the thing Ines would remember second. The child stood at the broken bridge's far end with Maren's fist locked in her collar, watching death flow toward her across thirty feet of ruined rope, and her expression was not terror. It was recognition. As if she had seen these creatures before—as if she had seen them *made*—and grief was the only reasonable response.

"No," Ines heard herself say. "No, you don't. *You don't.*"

She swung herself along the last cable, hand over hand, toward the child, toward the hounds, toward the impossible arithmetic of it—one woman against two creatures of ash above a freezing river—and cut the final strand on the far side.

The Widow's Step died the way its name promised. The cables whipped free, the whole ruin of it folded inward and down, planks and rope and hounds and forty years of Ines's crossings dropping together into the gorge. The hounds made their first and only sound as they fell—a sound like a fire dying, like a great exhale of heat—and then the Sorrow took them, and the steam where they struck rose up through the snowfall in two pale ghosts and unraveled.

But Ember had been reaching for Ines.

It happened in less than a heartbeat and lasted, in memory, a season. The child's fingers closed on Ines's wrist as the cable tore loose from the anchorage, and the recoil of it—sixty years of tension releasing at once—took them both over the edge together, out over the void, the river rushing up white-lipped, and Ines felt her shoulder wrench nearly out of its socket, felt the world narrow to a screaming point, felt with absolute clarity that she was going to watch this child fall and there was nothing in creation she could do about it except refuse, refuse, *refuse*—

Her free hand caught an ice shelf at the gorge wall.

The impact nearly took her fingers off. She slid, boot heels skating on black glass, Ember's weight swinging her like a bell, the river roaring below—and then Maren was there, impossibly, ancient Maren on her knees at the lip with the green birch staff thrust down like a lance, screaming words Ines had never heard her use, and Ines got her second hand up, and her third grip, and her fourth, and hauled them both, inch by shrieking inch, up the ice to the roots and stones of the gorge rim.

They lay there in the snow, all three of them, gasping. Steam rose off Ines's soaked shoulders. Ember was shaking—not crying, shaking, pressed against Ines's ribs with both fists knotted in her coat—and Ines put her arms around the child and held on, held on hard enough to hurt, because her hands needed something to hold that wasn't shaking too.

"It's all right," she lied, softly, into frozen hair. "I've got you. I'm not letting go."

And she understood, lying there with the river's roar fading and the trackers' voices distant and defeated on the far rim, that for the first time in her life the sentence was not smuggling talk, not craft, not a promise with conditions. She would go back down that ice. She would go down it again and again and again, into any river, after any child, and the part of her that calculated odds and loads and anchor-strands—the good smuggler's part, the careful part, the part that had kept her alive fifteen years on the roads—had gone suddenly, permanently quiet.

Some weights you didn't rate. Some cargo you simply carried, or died under.

Maren collapsed beside them, coughing, laughing, coughing again. "You dropped a bridge," she wheezed. "Sixty years. Your master built that span. He'll haunt you till your dying day."

"He'll haunt me gladly," Ines said. "He hated the Crown more than he loved carpentry."

But when she lifted her head, the laugh died in her throat.

On the far rim, the trackers were retreating into the trees—but they were retreating in good order, unhurried, unbroken, and the tall one with the schoolmaster's voice had stopped at the treeline and turned back. Even at this distance, through the falling snow, Ines could feel him considering her, the way a man considers a lock he intends to return to with better tools.

And beside him, emerging from the pines on a led horse, wrapped in a fine grey cloak with the hood thrown back, rode a figure she knew better than she had ever wanted to know anyone.

Corvin looked across the gorge at her. He did not wave. He did not call out. He only watched for a long moment, and then he drew his cloak closed and turned his horse away, and the snow closed behind him like a door.

"He knows where we're going," Maren said quietly. "Doesn't he. He knows about the Cold Larder. He knows about everything past it."

Ines got to her feet, legs shaking, and pulled Ember up with her, and looked north along the ridge to where the waystation lay hidden in the mountain's shoulder—and beyond it, though she had no name for it yet, though only a stolen page and a dying woman's memory held its location—something older than any of them, waiting under the stone.

"Then we get there first," she said. "And we pray the old roads still remember whose side they're on."
