## 11. Corvin's Offer

Corvin Ashgrave had learned long ago that the mountains did not forgive. They only forgot, slowly, the way a drunk forgets a promise—and then they buried you under the forgetting.

He climbed anyway.

The snow came down in that particular Thornfell way he had hated for thirty years: not falling so much as arriving from every direction at once, as though the sky itself had decided to become a wall between him and whatever waited above. His boots were wrong for this—city boots, Crown-issue, leather stiff with new tallow—and each step upward was a small negotiation with gravity that gravity kept winning. Behind him, two Ashcart soldiers labored in his tracks, and behind them, the ash-hounds moved like smoke given teeth, their nostrils flaring white steam, unbothered by cold because they were not entirely alive and never had been.

*Three days,* Corvin thought. *Three days since the guildhall burned.*

He could still smell it. That was the worst part—not the memory of it but the smell of it, pine pitch and old paper and something sweeter underneath, the way old wood smells when it finally gives up holding its shape. He had watched it burn from the valley road with a cup of someone else's wine in his hand, and he had told himself what he always told himself: *this is what survival costs, and survival is worth any coin.*

He was fifty-eight years old. His hands shook now when he held them out flat, an old injury from a rope gone slack at the wrong moment on the Greymire traverse, and lately they shook at other times too, times with no rope anywhere near. He told himself that was age as well.

The signal-fire had gone up two nights past—a smuggler's beacon, green-flamed, burning on the shoulder of Raven's Tooth where no honest fire would ever be lit. Corvin had read it the way he once read weather: *they run high, they run heavy, they run toward the old roads.* And the old roads, in winter, meant one place. The waystations. The hollows. The bones of the trade that had fed him and ruined him in equal measure.

So he had asked Commander Vess for three men and a flag, and Vess had looked at him with those pale, patient eyes—the eyes of a man who kept ledgers of other men's debts and found them all, eventually, collectable—and said, "Bring me the child, Master Ashgrave. Or bring me proof you cannot."

*Proof.* As if grief could be notarized.

The ravine narrowed above them, walls closing in like a throat. Corvin knew this stretch. The Smuggler's Cut, the old-timers called it, though the name had died with most of the old-timers. There was a waystation ahead—Heron's Rest, if the roof hadn't fallen in—a low stone shelter built into the hillside with a chimney that drew clean and a hearth big enough to warm six men. He had slept there as a young man, back when the roads were alive and the Guild was more than a rumor wearing a guildhall like a borrowed coat.

Back when Maren still spoke to him. Back when Ines—

He stopped himself. That was the trick of these mountains: they made a man remember whether he wanted to or not. Every bend in the trail had a ghost leaning against it.

They reached Heron's Rest at dusk, which was less a time than a condition up here, the light simply giving up. The waystation stood, half-swallowed by drift, its door hanging open on a hinge of ice. Corvin sent the soldiers around to the sides—he remembered how Ines taught him to clear a building, years ago, in another life, her voice sharp and amused: *"You check corners like a merchant checks coins, Corv. Everything gets counted."*—and stepped inside alone.

The hearth was cold. But there were signs: boot-marks half-filled with fresh snow, a scatter of straw, the ghost-smell of woodsmoke no more than a day old. They had been here. Maybe they were still here. Maybe they were watching him right now from some hollow he'd forgotten he knew.

He set his staff against the wall and took off his gloves, finger by finger, working feeling back into his hands over the dead hearthstone. Then he did the thing that thirty years of smuggling had taught him and twenty years of betrayal had perfected.

He waited.

* * *

She came an hour after full dark.

He heard her before he saw her—or rather, he heard the mountain announce her, a soft scrape of snow above the door, a pause, another scrape. She was testing the ground the way he'd taught her. His chest did something complicated and unwelcome at that.

Ines Oyelaran came through the doorway with a knife already drawn, which was, he supposed, the most affectionate greeting she had left for him.

She looked terrible. That was the first thing, and he hated himself for noticing it first, because noticing it meant admitting he'd spent three days wondering. Snow crusted her brows and the collar of her coat; her lips were split from the cold; her eyes had the flat shine of a person running on fury and trail-rations. But she stood the way she'd always stood, weight even, knife low and loose, and when she saw him sitting alone by the dead hearth with his empty hands visible, she didn't lower the blade.

"You brought hounds," she said.

"I brought a flag." He lifted it from beside his knee where he'd kept it draped—white cloth, plain, unmistakable. "Two soldiers outside. They'll stay outside while we talk. That was the arrangement."

"Arrangements with Ashcarts." She spat it like a seed. "There's a word for arrangements with Ashcarts, Corvin. It rhymes with *grave.*"

"It rhymes with *alive,* too," he said quietly. "Depending on how you say it."

Behind her, in the deeper shadow past the door, he caught movement—a small shape, quick, retreating. The child. Ember. A flash of dark eyes and then nothing, gone into the stone like water into sand. Something in Corvin's stomach turned over, and he made himself look away from the shadows, made himself look at Ines instead, because the child's fear was a weight he couldn't carry yet and do what needed doing.

"You're alone," he said.

"Maren's sick. Fever. She's sleeping." Ines's jaw tightened. "Which you'd know, if your hounds weren't the only family you've got left."

"Ines—"

"Don't." The word cracked across the room like ice giving way. "Don't say my name like we're still—we were *never*—you burned my guildhall. You gave them the routes. Twenty-two years I carried your pack through passes you sold out from under us, and you don't get to say my name like it means anything to you."

"I know," Corvin said. And he did know—that was the obscene part, that he understood her rage completely, catalogued it, agreed with almost every line of it, and was going to make his offer anyway. Survival was like that. It didn't require you to be right. It only required you to keep breathing long enough to be sorry.

"Then say what you came to say." She didn't sit. Of course she didn't sit. "The flag buys you until dawn. Then I'm gone, and you can explain to your pale-eyed master why you lost a mute child in a blizzard."

"The child isn't mute anymore." He said it carefully, watching her face, and saw the flicker there—surprise, quickly buried. So it was true. The girl—boy? he'd never been sure, the child moved like water either way—had spoken. That changed things. Vess would want to know that. Vess collected everything.

"That's none of your business."

"Everything about that child is my business now," Corvin said. "That's why I'm here. Sit down, Ines. Please. My knees are seventy winters old between them and they're staging a revolt."

Something almost human crossed her face—almost—and she lowered herself onto the far side of the hearth, knife still bare in her lap, close enough to the door, he noticed, to be through it in two strides. Old habits. Good ones. He'd taught her those too, and the thought landed in him like a splinter: everything good in her craft had come partly from him, and everything rotten in him had come partly from wanting to survive.

"I'm going to offer you something," he began, "and I need you to let me finish it before you decide I'm the devil. Agreed?"

Silence. Wind worried the loose door on its icy hinge. Somewhere below, one of the hounds whined—a thin, wrong sound, like a flute played underwater.

"The Crown knows what the child is," Corvin said. "Not guesses. *Knows.* Vess has papers out of the capital, sealed ones, older than the Ashcart office itself. The child is what's called a Remnant—the last living soul who walked out of Sarel before the veil sealed. Do you understand what that means? Not a refugee. Not a stray. A *key.* The kingdom didn't just drown, Ines—it was locked. And the locks have been waiting thirty years for someone with Sareli blood and Sareli breath to come stand in front of them."

Ines had gone very still. Stillness, in Ines Oyelaran, was never calm. He pressed on.

"The Crown wants the door sealed permanently. Burned shut, salted, ended—so that nothing out of drowned Sarel ever comes crawling into the world again. And the child is the linchpin. Hand the child over, and—" he drew a folded document from inside his coat, laid it on the hearthstone between them, did not push it closer, "—this goes into effect."

She looked at the paper without touching it. "What is it?"

"A pardon. Full. Every surviving Veilwright—Maren included, her name's on the second page—cleared of all charges, all warrants dissolved. The confiscated records restored, or compensation paid where restoration's impossible. And the roads." He heard his own voice quicken and made himself slow down, because this was the part that mattered, the part she'd feel in her bones. "The routes returned. Legally. Charter renewed under Crown license. The Guild doesn't die in a burned-out hall in Thornfell, Ines. It becomes what it always should have been—licensed ferrymen, couriers, guides. Protected. Funded. *Legal.* No more running. No more watching friends hang. You could be Guildmaster by spring."

The wind filled the silence after, moaning through the gap under the door like something grieving.

"You're asking me," Ines said at last, very softly, "to sell a child."

"I'm asking you to save a Guild."

"They're the same price?" She laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard from her, worse than crying, a dry bitter bark with nothing in it. "One child, whole Guild. That's the exchange rate now? That's what we've come to?"

"Ines." He leaned forward. His hands were shaking again; he pressed them flat against his knees. "I've done the arithmetic you refuse to do. If you don't give them the child, they take the child. Hounds, soldiers, fire—you've seen what they did to the hall, and that was them being *polite*, that was them thinking the child wasn't there. Next time there won't be a hall, there'll be a crater and a list of names, and yours will be at the top of it, and the child will end up in a cell in the capital anyway, except Maren dies of fever in between for nothing, and the Guild ends for nothing, and nothing—*nothing*—is saved. Versus: one child, taken somewhere warm, fed, questioned, kept. Alive, Ines. Vess gave me his word on that much, and whatever else Vess is, he keeps his word. He's a man of accounts, not cruelties."

"And you believe that." Her voice was almost gentle now, which was somehow worse. "You've told yourself that story so many times you sleep with it. That they'll keep the child alive. That a pardon signed by the same Crown that burned our records is worth the parchment. That you're saving us instead of delivering us."

"Yes," Corvin said. "I believe it. Because the alternative is believing I spent twenty years becoming this for nothing."

The honesty of it dropped between them like a stone into a well. He heard it fall all the way down.

Outside, a soldier's voice rose briefly—an order, muffled—and Ines's hand tightened on her knife. Corvin raised both palms.

"They're checking the perimeter. Nothing more. I gave my word."

"Your word." She stared at him across the dead hearth, and in the faint grey light from the snow-glare outside, her face seemed carved from something older than anger. "Do you know what the child said to me, Corvin? First words out of its mouth after months of silence. It said the veil is thinner than anyone believes. It said the old songs are instructions. It said—" she paused, and something flickered behind her eyes that he couldn't read, something that frightened her, "—it said the door can be opened. From this side. By someone willing to pay."

"Pay what?"

"That's not mine to tell. But here's what I know, mentor." She rose, knife vanishing into her belt, and picked up his pardon-paper from the hearthstone. For one absurd moment he thought she might accept it, and hope rose in him like floodwater, stupid and warm. She turned the paper over once, twice, studying it as she'd once studied route-maps, finding the flaws. Then she held it out to him.

"There's no seal on this," she said. "No signature. Just your promise and Vess's reputation. You're offering me a bridge made of fog and telling me it's safe to cross carrying everything I love."

"The seal comes when the child comes. That's how—"

"That's how *bait* works." She let the paper drop. It fluttered down between them onto the hearthstone, landing in the fine grey ash, and she stepped over it toward the door. At the threshold she stopped, her back to him, snow-light silvering her shoulders.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "you pulled me out of a flooded culvert on the Lowmarsh road. Held my head above the water until the others came. I was fourteen. I asked you why, and you said, *because nobody drowns on my road.*" Her voice didn't waver, but it went quiet, quiet as the snow. "You were the best man I ever knew, Corvin. That's what makes this unbearable. Not that you fell. That you fell and built a life down there and called it climbing."

"Ines—"

"Go home, old man. Tell your pale-eyed master the roads are closed."

And she stepped out into the storm, and the darkness took her the way the mountain takes everything—completely, and without malice, and forever, if you let it.

* * *

Corvin sat alone by the dead hearth for a long time.

The pardon lay in the ash where she'd dropped it. He picked it up eventually, brushed it clean with his thumb, refolded it along its worn creases. Unsigned. Unsealed. He'd known, writing it, that it was mostly theater—a prop for the offer, a thing to put weight in her hands. But holding it now, alone, with the wind screaming down the cut and the hounds pacing outside like thoughts he couldn't stop thinking, it felt less like theater and more like prophecy. This was all it had ever been. Paper and ash.

Vess would want a report. *The offer was refused. The subject remains at large. Recommend escalation.* Three lines. He could write them by lantern-light tonight and be back in the valley by tomorrow dusk with his pension intact and his soul exactly as whole as it had been before, which is to say, in pieces, arranged neatly in rows.

Or.

He looked into the cold hearth. Under the ash, scratched faintly into the hearthstone itself—old, deliberate, half-worn away—he found marks he'd forgotten: tally-strokes and a looping glyph, the old Guild sign for *safe road follows.* Someone had carved it decades ago, some ferryman warming himself at this same fire, promising the next traveler that the way ahead was kind. His own hand had probably passed over it a hundred times without seeing.

Now he saw it. Now it looked like an accusation.

*Because nobody drowns on my road.*

He rose, joints cracking, and pushed open the door. The storm swallowed him to the waist. Below, the soldiers waited with lanterns hooded, and beyond them the dark fell away toward the valley, toward the burned guildhall, toward Vess and his ledgers and his patient, collecting eyes. Above, barely visible through the driving white, the pass climbed higher, toward the old roads, toward the heart of Thornfell, toward whatever door a dying child of a drowned kingdom believed could be opened.

The hounds turned their heads to him in unison, steam rising from their jaws, waiting for direction.

Corvin stood between the two darknesses—one below, one above—and felt, with terrible clarity, the exact weight of the rest of his life resting on which way he pointed them.

"Commander Vess expects the child by week's end," he said aloud, to no one, to the storm. "And I expect..."

He didn't finish. He couldn't. The sentence had two endings, and both of them required a man he was no longer sure existed.

He pointed the hounds upward.
