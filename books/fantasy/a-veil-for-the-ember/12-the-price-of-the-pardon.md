## 12. The Price of the Pardon

The flag of truce still hung from Corvin's fist like something he'd found dead on the road—a strip of white wool gone gray at the edges, stained where his gloves had handled it too long. Ines kept her eyes on it rather than on his face, because his face was the face that had taught her to read waymarks, and she could not yet look at it without her chest tightening around something that wanted to become grief.

They stood in the lee of the waystation wall, where the wind couldn't get its teeth into them. Behind her, through the cracked door, Maren coughed in the rhythm that Ines had begun, over the last two days, to measure time by—not the rising and setting of the sun, which the clouds had swallowed anyway, but the wet, rattling cadence of an old woman's lungs filling with winter.

"You heard my offer," Corvin said. "I know you heard it. You didn't interrupt once, and you always interrupt, Ines. You've interrupted me since you were nine years old."

"I was letting you finish," Ines said. "It seemed like the decent thing to do. For a eulogy."

Corvin exhaled through his nose. Snow had gathered in the seams of his coat—the good coat, the one with the Crown-blue lining he must have thought nobody would notice, or perhaps had stopped caring whether anyone noticed. That was the detail that hurt worst, Ines thought later. Not the offer itself. The coat. He wasn't even hiding anymore.

"It's not a eulogy," he said. "It's a rescue. Look at this place." He gestured at the waystation with the weary sweep of a man showing a beggar an empty purse. The abandoned shelter clung to the mountainside below the high passes, its roof sagging under two days of snow, its hearth cold because a cold hearth left no smoke for ash-hounds to find. "Look at her." He nodded toward the door, toward the sound of coughing. "Maren is dying, and she'll die in a ruin at the top of the world because you're protecting a child who belongs to the Crown by whatever law wrote the word *Remnant* in their ledgers. Hand Ember over. Take the pardon. The Guild's roads come back to us—every pass, every hollow way, every tollbooth from here to Sarel Sound. We go back to being what we were."

"The Guild was never what we were," Ines said quietly. "That's the whole point of it. That's what you forgot."

Something moved behind Corvin's eyes—she saw it, and hated herself for seeing it, because it meant part of him still lived in there, some drowned thing turning over under the ice. "I remember everything," he said. "That's my whole curse, girl. I remember when we were three hundred strong. I remember the guildhall full of song. I remember your mother."

"Don't."

"I remember her singing the road-songs to you before you could walk, and I remember thinking: *there. That one will carry it forward.*" His voice roughened. He turned away, looked out at the white blindness of the slope. "And I remember what the Ashcarts did to those who wouldn't bend. I held Halver's hand while they burned the routes into his skin instead of paper. I made a choice that year, Ines, and I've paid for it every day since, and now I'm trying to make sure you don't pay for it too."

"By selling them the child."

"By saving everyone I can save." He said it fast, the way a man says a thing he has told himself so many times it has worn smooth as river stone. "You think I sleep well? You think there's a night I don't see the guildhall burning? But survival isn't pretty, and it isn't pure, and someday—when you're older, when you've buried enough people—you'll understand that a smuggler who keeps nothing back for tomorrow is just a corpse who hasn't lain down yet."

Ines let the silence sit between them. She let it grow. It was one of the first things Corvin himself had taught her, back when the lessons were kind: *the silence is yours, girl; most men can't bear to own it, so they'll pay anything to fill it.*

He filled it.

"Ines. The pardon. The roads. Say yes."

"No," Ines said.

It came out smaller than she'd intended. A single syllable, half-swallowed by wind. And yet it landed between them like a dropped lantern, and she watched it catch fire along every dry thing Corvin had built between himself and what he'd done.

"They'll hunt you," he said. His voice had gone flat. "Without the roads, without the book, you're three people in the snow with no way down the mountain before the true winter closes it. You'll die up here. All of you."

"Then we'll die knowing the difference between a Veilwright and whatever it is you've become."

She regretted it the moment she said it—not because it was cruel, but because it was true, and truth spoken in cruelty is a coin that spends badly. Corvin's face closed like a shutter. When he spoke again, his voice was the handler's voice, the pragmatist's voice, and that frightened her more than any threat could have.

"I'm sorry it came to this," he said. "Truly."

He reached inside his coat, and for one heartbeat Ines thought of a blade, and she shifted her weight onto her back foot the way he'd taught her—but what he drew out was worse than a blade. It was a book. Small, bound in oiled leather gone soft with decades of handling, its spine repaired twice with smuggler's thread. The route-book. Her mother's hand in the first third; Maren's cramped script in the middle; her own annotations crowding the margins of the last pages, added in the years when she'd believed—oh, how simply she had believed it—that she was writing notes for apprentices who would come after her.

There would be no apprentices now. There would be Ashcart cartographers, copying out her mother's waymarks in government ink, and surveyors driving their stakes into hollow ways that had kept the hunted safe for two hundred years, and gallows built at crossroads whose names only the Guild had ever known.

"You went through my pack," Ines said. "At the truce parley. While I was standing in front of you."

"While you were standing in front of me," Corvin agreed. "First lesson I ever gave you. Watch the hands that aren't watching you." He weighed the book in his palm, and for a moment—a long moment, long enough that the wind changed and the snow came down in a new, finer, colder grade—he only looked at it. His thumb moved across the cover the way a man touches the cheek of someone sleeping. Then he tucked it away inside the blue-lined coat, against his heart, where a man keeps the things that matter or the things that damn him, and Ines had stopped being able to tell the difference in him.

"There's a copy," she lied.

"There isn't." He said it gently. "You burned nothing, because burning the book meant burning her handwriting, and you couldn't do it. I know. I couldn't either, for eleven years. That's why I copied it out for them instead, all those winters ago. So the original could stay mine." He pulled his hood up. Snow slid from his shoulders. "Maren knows verses the book doesn't hold, but Maren's lungs are drowning. You know songs, but songs need roads, and the roads are in here now." He patted his chest once, softly, an obscene little tenderness. "Go down to the coast if you can. Surrender to a magistrate instead of an inquisitor—it goes easier. Or stay in the high passes and let the winter do what I was too much of a coward to do all along."

"And Ember?"

For the first time since the parley began, Corvin would not look at her. "The Remnant is not my ledger," he said. "The Remnant is the Crown's. What they want it for—" He stopped. Started again. "There are things in their orders, Ines, that I read twice and then made myself stop reading. Whatever that child is, the Ashcarts fear it more than they fear the Guild entire. Men who burn records are afraid of what's written in them. Men who hunt a child across two hundred miles of winter are afraid of what it opens." His jaw worked. "Don't be here when they come. That's the last kindness I have in me, and I've spent nearly all of them."

He walked out into the snow. Ines watched him go down the switchback trail with the route-book against his heart, growing smaller and grayer until the white world took him, and she stood at the ruined doorway of the waystation with her hands empty and her mother's handwriting walking away inside another man's coat, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like fever, that the Guild had not died in the fire at the guildhall. It had died here, in a truce parley, sold for the second time by the same man.

Behind her, Maren coughed, and coughed again, and then called her name in a voice worn down to thread.

The fire was small. Everything about their situation demanded smallness—small fires, small movements, small voices—but Ines built this one as much for the light as the heat, because Maren's eyes had begun to fail in the dimness, tracking shapes that weren't there, and because Ember sat close to the old woman's side with the fixed, fierce attention of someone keeping vigil.

Ember hadn't eaten in a day. The child pushed the dried plums toward Maren instead, and the barley porridge toward Maren, and when Ines forced the issue—spooning the portion back into Ember's bowl, saying nothing, just doing it with the flat authority of someone who has run out of patience for negotiation—the child ate mechanically, watching Maren breathe, the way a sailor watches the sea in bad weather.

"How long has she been hot like this?" Ines asked, though she knew. She'd been feeling Maren's brow every hour since the ravine, since the river, since the bridge she'd dropped into the water with the ash-hounds shrieking after them. Two nights of wet clothes in an unheated ruin. Maren was seventy-some years old, and she had crossed a veil once out of a dying kingdom, and bodies keep ledgers whether or not their owners show them.

"Since before the bridge," Ember said.

Ines's hands went still on the bowl. It was still a small shock, every time—the voice, the plain common-tongue words coming out of that small solemn face. Three weeks ago the child had stood in the guildhall door making sounds like water over stones, and the whole surviving remnant of the Veilwright Guild had stood around gaping, and Maren had gone gray as old bone. Now Ember spoke sparingly, precisely, like someone rationing a supply discovered to be finite.

"Since before the bridge," Ines repeated. "You should have told me."

"She was telling me stories," Ember said. "About Sarel. About the harbor, and the terraced gardens, and the bell that rang when a ferry came in. If I told you, you'd have made her stop talking to save her breath." The child looked up, and there was something in that gaze far too old for the small body holding it. "Talking is what's keeping her alive. Not the fire. Not the plums. She's been carrying a debt for fifty years and she's finally paying it, and if you take the paying away from her, she'll lie down."

Ines set the bowl down slowly. Outside, the wind had found the gap in the eastern wall and was playing it like a flute, a thin rising note that the old smugglers used to say was the mountain tuning up for the real performance. The true winter was close now. She could feel it the way sailors feel weather two days out—not the ordinary mountain season but the long closing, the white dark that sealed Thornfell until thaw, that had sealed it the year the Guild buried eleven of its own in one grave-digging thawed too late for proper rites.

"Maren," she said. "Listen to me. Corvin took the book. Every road, every hollow way, every crossing from here to the coast. Within a month there will be Ashcart posts on all of them."

Maren's eyes opened. Fever had burned her cheeks bright and sunk her eyes into shadow, but they were clear when they found Ines's face—clearer than they'd been in days, as though the news had cut through the sickness to something underneath.

"All of them?" Her voice was a ruin, but the irony in it survived intact. "Even the Cut?"

"Especially the Cut. My marks are in the margins."

"Your mother's marks are in the margins," Maren corrected. "Yours are beneath them. That book has three generations of us in its spine. And he carried it out under his heart." She began to laugh, and the laughter became coughing, and Ember held the waterskin to her lips while the fit shook her, and Ines sat with her fists clenched in her lap and hated how helpless hate was.

When Maren could speak again, she said, "Good."

Ines stared. "*Good?*"

"He took the roads, girl. He didn't take the reason for the roads." Maren fumbled under the blanket with a hand that shook like a leaf in late autumn, and Ines moved to help her, and was waved off with a flicker of the old imperiousness. "Every road the Guild ever cut existed for one purpose. To move someone the powerful wanted lost. Debt-bonded. Heretical. Fleeing a war the great lords started over a dinner table. Every road was a promise: *no matter what they do to the world, there is a way out.* Now the Crown owns the ways." Her mouth curved, and it was a terrible curve, fever-bright and utterly unbroken. "So the promise has to stop being a road."

Ines felt the words land somewhere below her ribs and sit there, heavy, like a stone swallowed. "What else would a promise be?"

But Maren only looked at Ember, and Ember only looked back, and between them passed something Ines could not read—a whole conversation conducted in silence, in the space of a breath—and she understood suddenly, with the sick lurch of arriving late to her own life, that these two had spoken of it already. That they had been speaking of it, in fragments, in story and half-story, since before the bridge. Since maybe before the ravine.

"What door-song verse comes after the ferryman's call?" Maren asked Ember, mild as a schoolmistress.

"*Veth ka saren, veth ka dor,*" Ember recited, clear and unhesitating. "*The way is not in the stone; the stone only remembers where.*"

"And the answering verse?"

"*Dor eneth su. Su eneth ka.*" Ember's voice softened on the last words, the way voices do on the names of the dead. "*A door is a person. A person is a door.*"

The wind screamed through the gap in the east wall, and the small fire bent flat and recovered, and Ines sat between the dying woman and the child who remembered a drowned kingdom, and the stone in her stomach turned over, and she knew—she knew the way she knew the weight of her own pack, the pull of her own boots, the number of hours until light—that whatever they were circling, whatever lay at the end of the verses Maren had begun teaching her on the high trail, it was going to cost more than roads. More than the guildhall. More, perhaps, than the Guild had ever been worth, because the Guild had always been worth exactly what its members could afford to lose, and the ones who mattered had always paid in themselves.

"Rest," she told Maren, and pulled the blanket up, and pressed her lips briefly to the fever-hot forehead, tasting salt and smoke. "We'll talk when the sun's up."

"The sun's not coming up in Thornfell anymore," Maren murmured, already sliding under. "Didn't you feel it? Winter took the sky two days ago. This is just what's left over." Her eyes closed. Her breathing evened into something that was almost sleep. "Teach her the walking verses, Ember. Start with the walking verses. She has her mother's ear..."

The voice trailed into breath, breath into the rattle that Ines was learning to dread, rattle into a shallow, stubborn rhythm that continued, that would continue, that Maren's body would insist upon long past any reasonable hour because the woman inside it had decided things were not yet finished.

Ines banked the fire. She checked the seals on the food-pack. She went to the broken eastern wall and wedged her oilcloth tighter into the gap, and through the last finger-width of opening she looked out at the night.

The storm had cleared in the high distance—cleared in the wrong way, the way it clears when the clouds are not passing but sinking, settling, compressing into the low white lid of the true winter. Below her, far down the mountain, where the switchbacks descended toward the valley roads, she could see lights. Small, steady, spaced with military regularity down the length of the old Cut.

Lanterns. Dozens of them, strung along a road that had belonged to smugglers for two hundred years, planted by hands that measured and recorded and claimed. Corvin's work, or Corvin's masters', already done, already lit, already burning her mother's handwriting into Crown property line by line by line.

Ines stood a long time in the cold, watching. And the grief she had been carrying since the truce parley—grief for the book, for the guildhall, for the mentor, for the whole soft dying world of her childhood—did not break her, as she had expected it to. Instead it changed state, the way deep snow changes under its own weight: silently, totally, into something harder. Something that could be carved.

*Every road was a promise,* Maren had said. *So the promise has to stop being a road.*

She turned back into the ruin. Ember was awake still, sitting watch beside Maren exactly as promised, and the child looked up at her across the dying fire with those too-old eyes, and Ines knelt down on the frozen ground, close enough that the cold of the stone came up through her knees like a message.

"Teach me the walking verses," she said.

Ember studied her for a long moment. Somewhere below, the Crown's lanterns burned on stolen roads. Somewhere above, the true winter settled over the peaks like a closing hand. And the child smiled—small, sad, and certain, the smile of someone who has known all along where the road ends and has been waiting, patiently, for the rest of them to arrive.

"I thought you'd never ask," Ember said, and began to sing.
