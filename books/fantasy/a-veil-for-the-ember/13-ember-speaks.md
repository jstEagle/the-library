## 13. Ember Speaks

The words had been building in Ember's chest for a long time, like water behind a dam of ice.

They had not chosen silence, in the beginning. Silence had been all that was left of them—the way a bell that has been rung too hard goes quiet, not from peace but from a crack running through the metal. When they had walked out of Sarel, out through the last gray shimmer of the veil with their mother's hand slipping from theirs, they had left their voice on the far side of it. Or so it had seemed. For a season and more they had lived among the Veilwrights as a ghost among the living: fed, warmed, watched, but never answered in their own tongue, and never able to answer in theirs. Ines's language sat in Ember's ears like a coat that almost fit. They understood it—had always understood it, the way one understands the lapping of water, or wind in a doorway—but the shape of it would not come out of their mouth. Every attempt was a small drowning.

Old Sareli was different. Old Sareli came out of them like breath, like birdsong, like the sound a hearth makes when it catches. It was the language of the inside of their skull. And that was the problem, Ember had come to understand. Every time they spoke it, Maren went the color of candle-wax, and Ines looked at them with a hunger that frightened Ember more than the Ashcarts did, because it was the hunger of someone standing at the edge of a well, leaning too far.

They knew what the words did. They knew what the words meant.

Tonight, in the hollowed-out waystation below the tree line, with Maren's fever-breath rattling in the corner and the snow coming down past the broken shutters like the sky emptying itself of everything it had ever held, Ember decided the dam had to break.

* * *

They had been watching Ines for hours without appearing to watch. This was a skill Ember had learned young, in a kingdom where children learned to be small and quiet the way other children learned to skip rope. Ines sat near the fire with the stolen page unrolled across her knees, the one Maren had carried folded against her heart for forty years, and her lips moved as she traced the script. She was learning it. She was learning it the way Ember had watched drowning people learn to float, desperately, with a kind of fury, as if the language were a rope thrown to her and she meant to climb it hand over hand.

Ember loved her. That was the terrible thing. They had not meant to. Love, for Ember, had become a thing like frost on a window—beautiful, and proof of the cold.

They got up. The floorboards spoke under their feet, the old smuggler's language of creaks that Ines had taught them to read without meaning to: *someone crossing, someone light, no alarm*. Ember crossed the room and sat down across the fire from her, close enough that the heat pressed against their shins, and waited until she looked up.

Ines's eyes were tired the way winter is tired—not defeated, just worn down to essentials. "You should sleep, little ember. Tomorrow's road is long, and the snow—"

"I remember the snow," Ember said.

In the common tongue.

The words came out cracked and rust-streaked, a voice that had not been used, and Ines went absolutely still. Not startled—Ember had learned that Ines did not startle, that she was one of those people whose fear went inward, into the hands, into the stillness. Her hands went still now, flat on the page, as if she were pinning it down against a wind only she could feel.

"Ember," she said carefully. "You're speaking."

"I remember the snow," Ember said again, because it was true, and because the truth had to come out in pieces or not at all. "Not this snow. The snow of the last winter of Sarel. It fell for forty days without stopping, and the priests said it was the sky mourning, and my mother said it was only weather, and she was wrong, and I have never told anyone she was wrong because she died believing it, and it seemed—" Ember stopped. The dam was cracking faster than they had planned. "It seemed cruel to take that from her. Even now. Even here."

Ines did not move. The fire popped. In the corner, Maren turned in her fever, murmuring something in Old Sareli that was half prayer and half road-song, and Ember flinched at the sound, because hearing the language in a dying woman's mouth was like hearing a dead person's footsteps.

"Say something else," Ines whispered.

"I have been saying things. Every day. Since the night I came to your door." Ember looked into the fire, because looking at Ines while saying this was not possible. "You heard me. You heard me every time I sang. You thought I was only a child singing nonsense to keep my courage up. But I was telling you. I have been telling you since the first night, and you could not hear me, and that is not your fault. It is the way of it. The door-song is not a song. It is a testimony. It is the account of the last crossing, set to meter so that it would survive in the mouth of a child, because a child was all that was left to carry it."

The firelight moved on Ines's face. Ember watched her assemble the meaning, watched it arrive in her the way winter arrives—first one cold morning, then another, then all at once the river is locked.

"You're not a message," Ines said. "You're a witness."

"I'm a key." Ember heard their own voice go flat and strange. "Those are not the same thing, though the Ashcarts are right about one part: I am the last. The last soul who walked out of Sarel while the veil still admitted passage. When I came through, the way closed behind me like water behind a swimmer. It did not close *because* of me. But it closed *with* me, the way a lock closes with its key still in it. I am the key still in the lock, Ines. I have been the key in the lock my whole life, and I did not know it until the night I stood in the snow outside your guildhall and heard the road-songs through the wall, and understood that someone, somewhere, still remembered the shape of the door."

* * *

Ember told it the way they remembered it, which was not in order. Memory, for Ember, was not a road but a house with all its doors open at once, and they had learned to let the telling wander, because forcing it into a line was like forcing a river into a pipe.

They told about the harbor at Sarel-Maren-that-was, the white terraces going down to water the color of hammered tin. They told about the Veilwrights of the old country, who had not been smugglers there but ferrymen, a priesthood of practical people who wore no robes and kept no temples and whose whole liturgy was a set of songs about back doors. They told about the last year, when the kingdom had begun to come apart—not with armies, Ember said, but with a silence, a great soft silence that crept in from the edges of the map the way rot creeps into fruit, so that villages simply stopped answering letters, and then stopped being places letters could be sent to.

"My mother was a Veilwright's daughter," Ember said. "She knew what the silence was. The veil was sealing. Not being sealed by anyone—sealing itself, the way a wound seals, because the country on this side of it had decided, in some way no one voted on, that Sarel was over. The door was being closed from the far side of everything. And the ferrymen did the only thing left. They ferried. Night after night, family by family, through the First Door and out into your mountains, into a country that had forgotten them, where they would be strangers with a dead language and no names anyone could pronounce. My mother put me through on the last night the door would open. She was meant to follow. She turned around to help the family behind us, and the veil came down between us like a curtain, and I have spent every day since standing on the wrong side of that curtain."

Ines's hands had come up over her mouth at some point. Ember had not seen her do it.

"The ferrymen knew the cost," Ember went on, quieter now, because the hardest part was coming and they had learned to carry the hardest part the way you carry something hot, in both hands, without gripping. "The door was never opened by force or by craft. Craft could find it. Craft could keep it from being found by the wrong people. But the opening—that was always a toll. The First Door asks a price, and the price is not gold and it is not blood. It is a whole self. To open the door from the outside, someone must stand at the threshold and give the door everything they are. Their name. Their history. Every road they have ever walked, every person who knows their face, every claim they hold on the world. They must hand all of it across the threshold and become no one, and the door opens for the no-one, because a no-one has nothing left to keep on this side. That is the toll. That is what the last verse means, the verse Maren never sang in front of you. *The door opens for the empty hands.*"

The fire had burned low. Ember fed it a stick and watched the flame climb the bark, and when they spoke again their voice was very small.

"I am the key, Ines. But a key does not open a lock. A key only fits one. The door needs a hand to turn it, and the hand must belong to someone willing to be emptied. And there is no one left who knows the songs well enough, and loves us enough, and—" Ember's throat closed. They had rehearsed this. They had rehearsed it in the dark for a hundred nights, and still their throat closed. "I did not come to your door to be saved. I came because you were the last Veilwright, and I thought— I thought if I put the song into your keeping, then someday, when you understood it, you would know what I was asking, and you could choose. I never wanted you to choose it. I want you to understand that. I have watched you every day hoping you would be too slow to learn it, and too stubborn to offer, and hating myself for hoping it, because if you never learn it, then Sarel stays shut forever, and my mother's people stay dead behind a curtain, and I am a key that fits a lock in a house where no one will ever live again."

* * *

Ines was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the snow changed its rhythm against the shutters, long enough that Maren's fever-breath slowed and deepened into something like true sleep.

When Ines finally spoke, her voice was rough. "The toll. If someone paid it. What happens to them? Exactly. I want it exactly. No kindness, no rounding down."

Ember made themselves answer honestly, because Ines had earned honesty, and because a lie here would be the cruelest thing Ember had ever done, and they had done enough cruel things by surviving.

"You would not die. That is the trap of it. You would be *unwritten*. Everyone who ever knew you would find the memory of you gone soft, like a name on old paper. Your guild would have no record of you. The roads you walked would simply be roads. You would stand at the threshold as the door opens, and you would see what is beyond it—Sarel as it is now, under the new sky, alive, waiting, holding its breath—and you could not cross. The doorkeeper belongs to the door. You would stay. In the cold, in the high passes, at the threshold, for as long as the door stands. Watching people come and go. Unable to follow them. Unable to remember why you chose it, after a while, because your reasons were part of what you paid. Only the door would remember you, and the door does not speak."

"Except," Ines said, and there was something in her voice Ember could not name, "except in smugglers' stories, a hundred years from now."

"Except in stories," Ember agreed, and did not understand why she smiled at that, a small and terrible smile, there and gone like a fish under ice.

Ines looked down at the stolen page on her knees. Then she did something that frightened Ember more than anything the Ashcarts had ever done: she began, very quietly, to hum. The road-song. The one Ember had taught her in fragments, the one whose last verses Maren was hoarding like a dying woman hoards warmth. Ines hummed it wrong, the way a student hums it, and Ember heard in the wrongness how far she still had to go, and how little time the winter would give her, and the two facts lay down together in Ember's chest like two stones in the same pocket.

"Ember," Ines said. She looked up, and her eyes were dry and bright and utterly resolved, and Ember recognized the look, because they had seen it on their mother's face on the last night, on the harbor terraces, in the snow. It was the look of someone who has already decided and is only waiting for the right moment to tell you. "Tell me the last verses."

"You don't understand what you're asking."

"I understand perfectly," Ines said. "That's what frightens you. Wake Maren. She has the map, and I have the voice, and the snow won't wait for either of us."

* * *

Outside, the storm thickened, and somewhere down the valley a wolf began to sing—a long, rising note that was almost, almost, the first line of the door-song. Ember stood in the dark of the broken window and listened, and felt the key-shape of themselves turning slowly in the lock of the world, and understood that there were no more nights of silence left.

Behind them, Ines was shaking Maren gently awake, saying her name over and over, as if to keep it.
