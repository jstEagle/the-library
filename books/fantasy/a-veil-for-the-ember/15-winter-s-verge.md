## 15. Winter's Verge

The cold came for Maren the way debt collectors came for debtors: politely at first, then all at once.

She had known it was coming for years, of course. She was seventy-one years old, and she had spent forty of them sleeping in drafty guildhalls and walking frozen passes, and the body keeps its own ledgers. But she had thought she would have until spring. She had thought, in the way old fools think, that she could choose her hour the way she had once chosen her roads.

The long winter had other opinions.

She felt it settle into her chest on the third day out from the burning guildhall, somewhere in the high country above the tree line, where the wind came off the peaks like a blade drawn slowly from its sheath. Not a pain, exactly. A heaviness. As though someone had laid a wet wool blanket over her lungs and was pressing down, gently, patiently, with the whole weight of the mountain behind it.

By the fifth day she was coughing blood into a scrap of linen she hid in her sleeve, and by the sixth she could no longer pretend the hiding was working, because Ines had stopped pretending not to see.

"We make camp early," Ines said that evening, in the tone she used for things that were not negotiations. She was twenty-six years old and had the stubbornness of a mule and the eyes of her mother, who had also been stubborn, and who had also died in winter. "There's an overhang past that ridge. Stone at our backs, wind off our faces."

"You'll smother me with kindness before the cold manages it," Maren said.

"Then kindness will win. Good. It's had a poor season."

The girl built the fire small and shielded, the way the Guild taught—smokeless, starved, a fire that apologized for existing—and set Maren against the warmest stone, and wrapped Ember in the second blanket because the child was small and Maren was already an old woman who had made her peace with drafts. Then Ines sat across the flames and looked at her with that level, patient gaze, and Maren understood that the pretense was over.

"How long?" Ines asked.

"Old women don't get to know that."

"Maren."

"A week. A month. A bad night." Maren smiled, and tasted copper. "The road doesn't post its tolls in advance, child. You know that better than anyone."

Ines didn't smile back. She fed the fire a stick and watched it eat, and Ember—wrapped to the eyes in wool, only a pair of dark, grave, ancient eyes showing—watched them both.

That night, for the first time in forty years, Maren dreamed of Sarel.

Not the Sarel of the histories—the white terraces, the harbor bells, the king's road running gold to the sea—but the Sarel of the last night. The lanterns in the water. The line of people on the quay, holding their children up out of the flood. Her sister's hand, slipping. The veil closing like an eye.

She woke with salt on her face that was not entirely snowmelt, and she knew, with the clarity that dying grants, that she was done keeping secrets.

* * *

They made the waystation two days later.

It was a smuggler's cache, one of the old ones, dug into the shoulder of the mountain beneath a shelf of granite—a stone-walled hollow not much bigger than a chapel, with a hearth that drew, a spring that ran sweet through a fissure in the rock, and walls that three generations of Veilwrights had carved with their names and their marks and their jokes. Maren had slept here in '61, and in '68, and in every hard year since. The Veilwrights had built their caches to outlast everything but memory, and memory, she thought now, watching Ines lay her down on the straw pallet, was the one thing the Crown's fires could not reach.

Except they had reached it. They had reached everything. The guildhall was ash. The route-book was in Corvin's traitor hands. The roads were Crown property now, every hidden way and hollowed bridge and singing stone.

Everything, Maren thought, except what she had carried in her own head for forty years. And except the page.

"Build the fire up," she told Ines. "A real one. It doesn't matter anymore. No one is looking for an old woman's smoke."

Ines looked at her for a long moment—reading her, the way the good ones read a road—and then she built the fire up. High and honest, the flames throwing their light against the carved names on the walls, and Ember sat close to it with hands outstretched, and the child's shadow danced enormous against the stone.

"Maren," Ines said, when the fire was settled. "Tell me."

And Maren, who had spent four decades saying *nothing* and *it's not your burden* and *some doors are better shut*, heard herself say:

"Get the page first. It's sewn into the lining of my pack. Left side. Under the buckle."

Ines's hands went still over the fire. Then she moved—quick, precise, a smuggler's hands—and drew the knife along the seam, and pulled out what Maren had carried through thirty winters of searches and seizures and one very memorable interrogation in which she had swallowed her fear and her pride and nothing else.

The page was small, no bigger than a hymnal leaf, and it was soft as cloth from handling. The ink had gone brown. But the map was still true: a spiral of roads descending into the mountain's root, waymarks marked in the old notation—the Guild's notation, the one that looked like nothing, like tally-marks and weather-signs—and at the center, drawn in a hand that had been shaking, a door.

Not a door like doors. A door like an eye.

"The First Door," Maren said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Beneath Thornfell's heart. The oldest road we had. The road we built all the others from."

Ines turned the page in the firelight. "I've never seen this notation. Not in the route-books, not in the songs."

"No. You wouldn't have." Maren pulled the blanket higher. The cough was coming; she let it pass, wet and long, and saw the girl's hands curl at the sound of it. "The route-books were for smugglers, child. Goods and people, passes and bribes. But before the Veilwrights were smugglers, we were something else. And this—" she nodded at the page "—this is the only record of it that survived the Ashcarts' fires. Because I stole it. The night before they burned the archive, I stole it out of the chest where the First Masters kept it, and I have been carrying my crime against the Crown for forty years like a stone in my boot, and I have never once regretted it."

"Tell me," Ines said again. Softer now. "All of it."

So Maren told her.

* * *

She told her about the Guild before the fall. Before her time, even—the old days, when the Veilwrights had been ferry-masters on the coast of Sarel itself, when the kingdom was alive and the veil between it and the world was not a wall but a door, and the Guild's whole sacred trade was the moving of souls through it.

"Not goods," Maren said. "Not contraband. *People*. That was always the true cargo. Everything else—the spice, the silk, the fugitives from one crown or another—was cover. Was the *apology* we made for ourselves. But the first Veilwrights, the ones who cut the waymarks and wrote the songs, they moved the hunted out of one world and into the other, and back again when it was safe, and the toll was never coin."

"What was the toll?" Ines asked. And Maren saw that she already knew. She saw it in the set of the girl's shoulders, in the way her eyes went to Ember, small and silent by the fire.

"You know what it was," Maren said. "Or you've guessed, and you're waiting for an old woman to say it out loud so it becomes real. The toll was the self. A name. A history. A claim on the world you were leaving. The door doesn't take coin. Coin is a thing of one world only. It takes what you *are*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the passes with a sound like a long exhale.

"And when Sarel began to drown," Maren went on, "when the winter came and the veil began to seal itself—the Veilwrights ran the last ferries. Eleven years of them. We carried out who we could. And the door-song, the one the old masters wrote into the road-songs so the Crown would hear only drinking music—" she began, very quietly, to hum.

Ines knew the tune. Every Veilwright knew the tune. It was *The Ferryman's Rest*, the song they sang at the end of a long run, the song with the nonsense verses about ale and weather and the road home. Maren had sung it a thousand times over a thousand fires.

"The third verse isn't about ale," Maren said. "Listen."

And she sang it as it was meant to be sung, in words that were half Old Sareli and half the Guild's own cant, and beside the fire Ember's head came up slowly, like a flower turning toward sun.

*The door is not in the wall, the door is in the singer,*
*the way is not on the map, the way is in the walked,*
*name the name you were, and leave it at the threshold,*
*the ferryman keeps the toll, and the toll keeps the door.*

Ines had gone very still. "The words I learned as a child—they're wrong. They're *near* the words."

"They're veiled. Everything we gave you was veiled, because veiled things survive fires." Maren's chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with the illness. "The Crown burned our records, Ines. They could not burn the songs, because songs live in mouths, and they could not burn the roads, because roads live in feet. So the old masters hid the true craft inside the false one, generation by generation, and each generation knew a little less, until my own masters believed the veil was only a story we told to make the smuggling feel holy. Until *I* believed it." She laughed, and it cost her. "I was ferried through it, child. I stood on the quay at Sarel with my sister's hand in mine. I know it's real the way you know fire is real."

Ember had moved. The child knelt now at Maren's side, close enough that she could see the firelight in those too-old eyes, and a small hand came to rest, with terrible gentleness, on the blanket over her heart.

Maren looked at the child for a long time.

"You came out," she said. "Didn't you. Before the seal. The last one through."

Ember did not speak—the child had not spoken a word of the common tongue yet, only that flowing, dead, impossibly alive language—but the dark eyes held hers, and then the small hand pressed once, and lifted, and Maren felt something in her chest that was not the illness at all.

"I left someone there," she said to the child, as though Ines were not in the room, as though forty years of silence were a door she was finally, finally walking through. "My sister. Sefa. She was fourteen. The quay was going under and there was room for one more in the boat and I—" The words caught. She had rehearsed this confession her whole life and it still caught, the way a nail catches cloth. "I got in the boat. I was sixteen and I got in the boat and I have been rowing away from her ever since, every day of my life, and I told myself the veil was sealed and nothing could be done, and then you walked out of a snowstorm speaking her language, and I knew. I knew the door was not sealed. It was *waiting*. It was waiting for someone willing to pay."

She took Ines's hand. The girl's fingers were cold and strong.

"The last verses," Maren said. "The ones I never taught you, because I swore I never would, because teaching them makes the road real and the road is a hungry thing. I'm going to teach you now, and I want you to hate me a little for it, because hating me will be easier than what comes after."

"Maren—"

"*Learn it.*" The cough took her then, doubled her, wrung her; when she surfaced, Ines was holding her upright and Ember had pressed a cup of snowmelt to her lips, and Maren laughed weakly at the absurd tableau. "Stubborn children. Both of you. Fine. Then listen, and sing it back to me until it's perfect, because the door will know a false note the way a customs man knows a false seal."

And she taught her.

She taught her through the night and into the gray of the next morning, verse by verse, the true words rising out of the veiled ones like bones out of sand. The verses of the approach, sung to the waymarks so they would wake. The verse of the asking, sung at the threshold, in which the singer names herself. And the last verse—the toll-verse—which had no melody, only a rhythm, like footsteps, like a heartbeat slowing.

Ines learned fast. She had always learned fast; it was the thing Maren had loved most in her, and the thing she now most grieved. By the time the light through the cache mouth went from gray to white, the girl was singing the approach-verse without error, her voice low and steady in the stone hollow, and the waymark carved above the hearth—an old, old mark, a spiral within a spiral that three generations had taken for a decoration—was glowing.

Faintly. Warmly. Like an ember under ash.

Ember was watching it with tears running silently down a child's face, and Ines was watching it with an expression Maren recognized from mirrors, from forty years ago, from a quay in a drowning kingdom: the expression of someone who has just understood the size of the price.

"It's real," Ines said quietly. "All of it. It was always real."

"It was always real," Maren agreed. "We just grew too frightened to walk through. And then there was no one left who remembered how." She reached for the page, and Ines gave it to her, and Maren pressed it into the girl's hands and folded the girl's fingers closed over it. "The First Door is three days down, at the mountain's root. The map is true. The waymarks will wake for you now—they'll wake for the song, and for him." She nodded at Ember. "The Crown will be behind you. Corvin will be behind them. You'll be hunted the whole way down, and the door will only open for a toll, and you know now what the toll is, and I am sorry, child. I am sorry for every year I stayed silent. I am sorry I didn't give you the choice while there was still time to refuse it."

Ines looked down at the page in her hands for a long moment. Then she looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible and calm.

"You gave me the choice," she said. "You gave it the night you taught me the third verse of a drinking song and let me think it was only a song. Everything since then has been me choosing."

Maren laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough, and the cough did not want to stop, and when it finally released her she was weaker, much weaker, and she knew the ledger had come due. She lay back against the stone and looked at the two of them—the last apprentice and the last soul of Sarel, lit by an honest fire in a smuggler's hole at the top of the world—and she thought: *this is what we were for. This, exactly this. All the rest was cover.*

"Sing it once more," she said. "The toll-verse. I want to hear it done right, once, before I go find out whether the ferryman kept my sister waiting."

And Ines—her Ines, her last student, the daughter of her craft if not her blood—began to sing.

The waymark blazed. The fire stood up straight. And far below them, Maren felt it through the stone, through the straw, through the failing drum of her own heart: a door at the root of the mountain, turning over in its sleep.

* * *

She lasted two more days.

She spent them giving orders, because dying was no excuse for sloppy tradecraft. She made Ines burn her own maps and memorize the true ones. She showed her the cache's second exit, and the third, and the trick with the counterweight stone that even Corvin had never mastered. She told her which of the scattered Guild families could be trusted and which had already sold their silence, and she made Ines repeat the names back until they were carved in her.

On the second night, she took Ines's hand and pressed something small into it: a ferry-token, lead, worn smooth, stamped with the spiral. Her own. The one she had carried out of Sarel at sixteen.

"Doorkeepers keep these," she said. "Show it at the threshold. It won't open anything. It's not that kind of token." She smiled, and her breath whistled. "It's a *receipt*. Proof of passage. So the ferryman knows you come from a house that paid its debts."

Near dawn, she sent Ines out to check the signal-line, because there were things a woman should do alone, and one of them was choosing her last view. She chose the fire, and the carved names, and the sleeping shape of the child wrapped in wool, and she found the whole sight so unbearably dear that she had to laugh at herself, quietly, one last time, for having spent forty years pretending to be a smuggler when she had been a ferryman all along.

When Ines came back in, Maren's hands were folded and her face was turned toward the waymark, which still glowed, faint and patient, like a candle left burning in a window for someone expected home.

They buried her in the snow above the cache, under a cairn of waymark stones, and Ines sang the approach-verse over her in the old words, and Ember sang it too, in a language forty years dead, in a voice like a small bell.

And it was Ember, standing at the foot of the cairn, who finally broke the silence afterward—looking not at Ines but at the pass below, where, tiny and distant and unmistakable, a line of torchlight was winding up out of the dark.

The child spoke one word of the common tongue. The first. The voice was rough from disuse and absolutely clear.

"Hunters."

Ines's hand closed around the lead token. Below them, the torches multiplied—and at their head, riding a horse far too fine for the passes, came a figure Maren had taught her to love, and taught her to mourn, and taught her, in the end, to survive.

Corvin had found the page's trail. And he was not alone.
