## 17. The Song Under the Mountain

They bound my hands with ash-rope.

I had heard of it only in the oldest of the songs, the ones the drowned kingdom sang to its children at nightfall: *rope dipped in the grey of burned names*, rope that tasted of everything it had ever destroyed. When they wound it around my wrists I felt it drinking at me, slow as frost creeping up a windowpane. It did not hurt. It was worse than hurt. It was *forgetting*—the first edges of me going soft, like a chalk drawing left out in rain.

I had been mute for so long that silence had become a country I lived in, a country with borders and weather and a single inhabitant. But silence is not emptiness. Silence is a held breath. And in the dark beneath Thornfell's heart, bound beside Ines Oyelaran while the Ashcarts stacked their firewood against a door older than their Crown, I finally understood what my whole life of quiet had been for.

It had been a breath held across an ocean of years.

The chamber was vast. I could feel its size the way you feel the size of a cathedral—not by seeing it but by hearing your own smallness echoed back to you. Drip of water somewhere far off, patient as a clock. The rasp of Ashcart boots on stone. The chemical reek of their fire-oil, sharp enough to taste at the back of the throat, a smell like the burning of libraries.

And underneath all of it, so deep that only someone who had been listening their entire life would catch it: the mountain itself, breathing.

Slow. Enormous. Asleep.

But not dead. Never dead. Sarel had taught me that much before the veil came down like a lid on the world—taught me that doors are not made things. Doors are *grown*. And what is grown can be woken.

* * *

They had beaten Ines badly. I knew this because I could hear her breathing—the particular shallow hitch of bruised ribs, the wet rattle she kept trying to swallow down whenever the Ashcarts looked our way. They had tied her facing the door, which I understood was deliberate cruelty: making her watch while they prepared to burn the last road her guild had ever kept.

"Ines," I said.

My voice came out cracked, rusted, a hinge unused for years. She turned her head as far as the ropes allowed. In the light of their lanterns her face was bloodied and swollen, one eye nearly shut, and still she managed the thing she always managed: a crooked half-smile, the smile of a woman who has been lied to by kings and cheated by weather and betrayed by the man who taught her her own craft, and who has decided, against all evidence, to keep smiling anyway.

"Well," she rasped. "There's a voice. Been wondering where you were keeping it."

"Saving it," I said.

"For what?"

I looked past her, past the ring of grey-cloaked inquisitors, past the braziers and the barrels of fire-oil, to the wall at the far end of the chamber.

To most eyes it was only rock—black stone seamed with veins of quartz, unremarkable, the kind of wall you would walk past ten thousand times without a second glance. But I had walked out of Sarel through a door like this one. I had stood on the far side and felt it seal behind me, felt the whole country fold itself away like a letter into an envelope, and I had carried the memory of that folding in my body ever since, the way a bell carries the memory of being struck.

Doors remember being opened. That is the secret no Ashcart ever learned, because it cannot be written down. It can only be sung.

"For that," I said.

Ines followed my gaze. Even beaten, even bound, I saw her do the thing the Veilwrights must have done for generations without knowing why—tilt her head slightly, the way you tilt your ear toward a song you half-remember from childhood. Her guild had sung on the road for centuries. Road-songs, marching-songs, the little tunes smugglers hummed to keep time in the dark passes. They thought the songs were theirs.

They had been singing instructions all along. Maren had known it, near the end, and had wept to know it. Ines was only now beginning to.

"The fire won't open it," Ines said quietly. "That's not how it works. Fire doesn't wake anything. Fire only ends things."

"That's what they believe." I watched the Ashcart commander—a tall woman with a voice like a ledger being balanced—direct her people as they rolled the barrels into place against the black stone. "They think the Remnant is a person. A loose thread to be cut."

"Aren't you?"

I considered this honestly, the way I considered everything, having had years of silence in which to practice honesty. "I am the last soul who walked out," I said. "Which means I am also the key that was carried out. Keys don't get to be people, Ines. Not entirely. That's why they took my voice when the veil closed—to make sure the key couldn't turn itself."

"And now?"

"Now I have spent my voice on other things. On learning your tongue. On telling you about snow that falls upward and rivers that run with light." My throat ached around the words; the ash-rope drank a little deeper each time I spoke. "Now there is almost nothing left of me except the song. Which is exactly enough."

* * *

The Ashcarts worked with terrible efficiency. I will grant them that. They had burned a guild's records, hunted its members across three winters, and reduced a brotherhood of smugglers to a handful of fugitives in the high snow—and they had done it all with the calm of clerks closing out an account. Now they soaked the black stone with fire-oil, barrel after barrel, until the whole face of the wall glistened like something newly born, slick and shining in the lantern-light.

The commander walked the line of her people, checking each torch, each fuse-cord. Her boots rang on the stone. Somewhere above us, I knew, winter was closing over Thornfell like a fist, the long winter, the true winter, the one the songs said would last until the door opened or the world forgot there had ever been another side to it.

One of the younger inquisitors hesitated, torch lowered, and asked her something I could not hear. She answered loudly enough that I could:

"Because it exists, fool. Because a thing that exists outside the Crown's maps is a wound in the Crown's skin. We do not negotiate with wounds. We cauterize them."

She believed every word. That was the frightening part. Cruelty that knows itself can be reasoned with, bargained with, bought. But cruelty that believes itself to be medicine—that burns villages and calls it healing—that kind walks forward with a clear conscience and never once looks back at the smoke.

They had left Corvin somewhere behind us. I had heard him arguing with them on the descent, his voice fraying, the voice of a man who had sold everything he loved and discovered too late that the buyer intended to take the shop as well. Whatever remained of Corvin Dunmore was pacing the tunnels above us, and I found—I surprised myself by finding—that I pitied him. He had spent his whole life calculating survival and had never once noticed that survival purchased at that price is just a slower way of dying.

The commander raised her hand.

"Ines," I whispered. "When I start, whatever happens—do not stop me. No matter what they do. Promise me."

Her good eye found mine. There was blood in her hair and terror in her face and underneath both of them, banked like coals under ash, the thing I had seen the first night she hid me beneath her floorboards from men whose job was fire: defiance, patient and absolute, the defiance of a woman whose guild had moved refugees under the noses of empires for two hundred years.

"I promise," she said. "What are you going to do?"

"What my mother did on the last morning," I said. "What every Sareli mother did, in the end. The song isn't a spell, Ines. It's a *knock*. It's a child knocking on a door from the inside, asking to be let out. All these years, everyone thought the door needed to be forced." I drew a breath that went all the way down to the bottom of me. "It only needed to be asked."

The commander's hand began to fall.

I sang.

* * *

Old Sareli does not sound like other languages. It does not sit on top of the air the way common speech does. It goes *into* things. It was made by a people who lived close to the veil, who built their houses with their backs against the thin places, and their words were shaped like keys because they had to be—they had to fit locks that were not made of metal.

The first note left my mouth and the ash-rope screamed.

Not audibly—it made no sound—but I felt it convulse around my wrists, felt it *grip*, because rope dipped in burned names knows a living name when it hears one, and the door-song is nothing if not a name: the name of the country, spoken in full, every syllable of it, the name that had been forbidden since the veil closed. *Sarel.* The word itself was contraband. The word itself was the beginning of the song.

The commander's hand froze mid-fall. Torchlight shivered across twenty faces turning toward me.

"Stop her," she said. "Gag her—"

But here is the thing about the door-song, the thing the Ashcarts' burned records had never contained, because it was never written anywhere: it cannot be interrupted. It is not a performance. It is a *process*, like water finding its level, like spring coming to a hillside. Once the first verse is given to the air, the rest follows the way dawn follows the darkest hour—not because anyone permits it, but because it must.

The second verse rose out of me, and the third, and my voice grew stronger as it went, stronger than it had any right to be, because I was no longer singing alone.

The mountain had begun to answer.

At first it was only resonance—the quartz veins in the black stone picking up my melody and returning it, faintly, the way a wineglass returns a sung note. Then it was more. The drip of water fell into rhythm. The cold air of the chamber began to move, slowly, in circles, a tide turning. And beneath our feet, so deep that it was less a sound than a change in the meaning of silence, something vast shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice calling them home.

The youngest inquisitor dropped his torch.

It did not matter. It should not have mattered—fire falling on oil-soaked stone, fire meant for destruction—and yet the moment the flame touched the black wall it did not spread the way fire spreads. It *gathered*. It ran along the quartz seams like light running through a prism, and where it went, the stone did not char. It *warmed*. The whole face of the wall began to glow from within, a deep ember-color, the color of a hearth in a house where someone is waiting for you, and the Ashcarts' own fire—the fire they had brought to end everything—was being drunk down like wine, converted, transmuted, taken in as fuel.

"No," the commander said. It was the first uncertain word I had ever heard from her. "No. Put it out. PUT IT OUT—"

They threw sand. They beat the flames with cloaks. It was like trying to smother a sunrise. Every act of destruction they offered, the door accepted and transformed. Their fire-oil became warmth. Their panic became motion. Even their fear, I think—the great grey wave of it rolling off twenty soldiers who had been taught all their lives that the old things were dead—even their fear fed it, because the door had been sealed for so long, and hunger of that duration makes no distinctions at the feast.

The wall split.

Not violently. The way ice splits on a lake in early thaw—one soft, enormous *tick*, then a seam of light opening down the center of the black stone, widening, widening, and through it came air that smelled of green growing things and salt and sun on water, air from a country that no map admitted existed, pouring into the frozen dark of Thornfell like the first day of a world.

Through the seam I could see it. Oh, I could see it.

Sarel. Not drowned. Not dead. *Reborn*—the way the songs had promised, the way the kingdom had folded itself away beyond the veil to wait out the long winter on the other side. Terraces white with blossom. A harbor full of boats with painted eyes on their prows. Light on water that was not quite the light of any sun I had ever known, gentler, golden-green, the light of a sky that had learned mercy.

And standing in that light, small and impossibly far away, figures. Turning. Looking toward the door that had begun, after all these years, to open.

* * *

"Ines," I said, and my voice was breaking now, the ash-rope tightening, taking its toll for every syllable, and I did not care. "Ines, look. Look at it. It's real. It was real the whole time."

She was staring. Bloodied, bound, beaten half to death—and staring through the widening seam of light with an expression I will carry past the end of my life: the expression of a woman watching the proof arrive that everything she had bled for was never a fairy tale. Her guild had not been mad. Her masters had not died for nothing. The roads were real. The door was real. The other side was real.

"It's—" she started, and could not finish, and did not need to.

The Ashcarts had broken. Some fled, dropping weapons as they ran, their doctrine of cauterized wounds collapsing in the face of a wound that opened instead of bleeding. Others stood frozen, torches guttering forgotten in their hands. The commander alone held her ground, sword drawn, shouting orders that fewer and fewer ears obeyed, a clerk insisting on the accuracy of a ledger while the building burned around her—no. While the building *bloomed* around her.

And above us, down the tunnel, footsteps. Running. A voice hoarse with something between rage and terror, a voice I knew from a lifetime of listening: Corvin Dunmore, coming down toward the light, carrying whatever was left of a man who had sold the map to salvation and was only now, at the very threshold, beginning to understand what he had actually been carrying all along.

The seam of light widened. The mountain sang back to me in a voice like continents remembering their youth. The ash-rope bit to the bone.

And I sang louder, because hope, I had learned in my long silence, is not the absence of captivity.

Hope is the song you sing *inside* it.
