## 21. Roads Out of Nowhere

*Collected fragments from the archive of the Free Roads, being letters, depositions, songs, and testimony gathered by the smugglers' brotherhoods in the years after the Long Winter, arranged here without comment.*

* * *

**I. From the testimony of Halda Voss, mule-driver, taken at the Wayhouse at Cold Saddle, in the ninth year of the winter**

You want to know about the door. Everyone wants to know about the door, and no one who tells you has seen it twice.

I'll tell you what I saw once. I was running salt and fever-bark over the high passes with my brother's boy driving the second mule, and the Ashcarts came up behind us on the Thornfell road with their hounds — you know the hounds, the ones that don't breathe right, that smell guilt the way other dogs smell blood. My sister had died in the Fever Year and the boy was hers, and by the law of those days that made him Crown property, a ward of the Ash Registry, and they were coming to collect him the way you'd collect a debt.

We cut off the road where no road was. I'll swear to that on any book you put in front of me. There was rockfall and there was snow to the mule's chest, and then there was a place where the mountain stood like a wall, and I sat down in the snow because I thought, well, here it ends, and at least the boy won't watch me hang.

My brother's boy started singing.

He'd heard it somewhere — off a drover, off a drunk, off the wind, I don't know. A road-song, the old kind, the kind the Guild used to sing on the long hauls, back when there was a Guild. Half of it he had wrong. And the wall of the mountain — I'm an old woman, and I've lied to priests and magistrates and my own mother, and I am not lying now — the wall *opened*. Not like a door swings. Like a curtain remembers it was ever a window.

And there was someone standing in it.

I couldn't tell you the face. I've tried, these nine years, in my head, and there's nothing there, and that's the worst part — not that I saw something terrible, but that I saw nothing at all, a smooth place where a face should be, and yet I knew — the way you know a friend's step on a stair — that I was being *looked at kindly*.

The keeper looked past me at the boy. Looked back the way we'd come, at the hounds coming up the pass with their ash-lanterns. And then the keeper stepped aside, the way a toll-keeper steps aside, and the light from inside was warm the way no light in this winter has been warm, and my brother's boy walked in, and the mules — the mules went in too, mind you, and they were never the calmest of animals — and I stood there with my empty hands and my empty ledgers and the keeper looked at me one last time, and I understood, don't ask me how, that I could go in too, that the door wasn't shut against me.

And I didn't go.

I had a husband in Cold Saddle. A bad husband, but mine. I had a debt. I had a life I knew how to carry. I turned around and walked back down into the snow and let the Ashcarts take my ledger and fine me to the bone, and I have thought about that warm light every night for nine years, and I will think about it every night until I die.

You asked what the keeper looked like. I told you. Nothing. A smooth place. But I'll tell you what the keeper *sounded* like, when the door was closing and I heard one last thing come out of it — a voice, low, a woman's voice or near enough, with an accent I knew from somewhere, the rounded vowels of the river-country, a smuggler's accent, the way the old Guild folk used to talk.

It said: *Mind the road behind you. It remembers too.*

**II. A road-song, variant collected at Thornfell, verses four through seven. The collector notes: the final verse is sung only once per fire, and never by the youngest present.**

*Oh, the road doesn't run where the mapmakers say,*
*the road runs under the road,*
*and the toll at the gate isn't silver or blood,*
*it's everything you ever called your own.*

*Sing it wrong and the mountain stays stone,*
*sing it right and the stone remembers,*
*and what the stone remembers, it opens for,*
*and what it opens for, it keeps.*

*There's a keeper at the door with no name to be called,*
*who paid the toll that we couldn't pay,*
*who gave up the roads and the fires and the graves*
*and the mother-tongue of her days.*

*So if you're hunted, come singing;*
*if you're hungry, come singing;*
*if you've nothing left but your feet and your grief,*
*come singing, come singing, come home.*

**III. From the private correspondence of Ashcart Commander Edevane Rill to the Registry at Carreth, marked *burn before filing*, and not burned, and filed, and now public, and may the Registry choke on it**

You ask me again for a full accounting of the Thornfell operation, and again I will tell you what I told the inquiry: we lost the Remnant, we lost the First Door, we lost Commander Sazan and eleven inquisitors and both ash-hound packs, and we lost them to a *song*.

I know how that reads. I have read the inquiry's face as I said it.

So let me put it in language the Registry will accept. The Veilwright Guild was not a smuggling ring that occasionally did something strange. It was the last functioning institution of a technology we do not possess and cannot burn, because its records were its people and its people were its songs, and you cannot seize a song. We burned their books in the spring of the Purge and we burned their hall in the first snow and we believed, as you believe, that we had burned the thing itself.

We had burned the *packaging*.

The door beneath Thornfell is real. It opened. I stood in the chamber and I watched the Ashcarts' own fire — the fire we carried to seal it — go into the door like water into cracked earth, and I watched the mountain drink it and *wake*. I watched a child stand in chains and sing in a language our scholars swear has been dead for two hundred years, and the chains did not so much as rattle, because the door was holding the child the way a mother holds a child, and our iron was an insult it declined to notice.

And I saw the keeper.

You will want a description for the warrants. Here it is: a woman of no age, in a smuggler's coat gone grey at the seams, standing at the threshold with her hands open at her sides. Her face — write this down exactly, because it is the only exact thing I will ever give you — her face was like a page from which the writing has been washed. Not blank. *Washed.* You could see that there had been something there, that something had been given up, the way you can see where a name was scraped off a deed.

She looked at Sazan. Sazan had her blade at the child's back. And the keeper said — and her voice was quiet and level and it carried through the whole chamber as if the mountain were passing it along — she said:

*You're on the wrong side of the door, and you always were. Go home. This road doesn't run to Carreth.*

And Sazan — Sazan, who once made a man recant his grandmother — Sazan *went*. Walked out. Walked down the mountain. Resigned by morning and gone from the Registry by the next, and I have heard since that she keeps a tavern in the lowlands and will not speak of Thornfell, and I understand her, because I will not speak of it either, except to you, in this letter, which I have decided not to burn after all.

Let the next commander read it. Let them understand what we are hunting. We are not hunting smugglers. We never were. We are hunting the last kindness in the world, and it has a door, and it knows our faces, and it is *not afraid of us*.

**IV. From the ledger of the Ferrier family, kept at the safehouse called the Lantern, entry undated but believed to be from the third year of the winter**

Brought in tonight: a girl of twelve, the Ondris girl, her father taken, her mother dead of the cough. The Ashcarts have her name on a list. A name on a list is a bell around a neck.

We can't hide her. We've hidden four this season and the Lantern leaks like the Lantern has always leaked. So we did the thing. The old thing. The thing my grandmother used to whisper about when she'd had enough plum brandy to be a Veilwright again.

We taught her the song.

It took her two days. She sang it wrong and she sang it wrong and she cried and sang it wrong, and then on the third night, up in the high pass, in the cold, with the hounds a mile behind us and closing, she sang it *right* — and I don't mean the notes, I mean she sang it like she meant the words, like the words were hers, like the road under the road had been waiting all this time for her specifically — and the mountain opened, and the keeper was there.

The keeper looked at the girl a long time. Then the keeper looked at me, and though there was no face to read, I swear on every road I've ever run that I heard, not with my ears, the question: *does she go willingly?*

And I said, out loud, in the snow, like a fool talking to a mountain: "She goes where her mother would want. That's all I know."

The keeper stepped aside.

The girl looked back at me once. She said, "Tell my father's grave I got out." And she walked into the light, and the light took her the way water takes a dropped coin, gently and completely, and the door closed, and the hounds arrived and found nothing but three mule-prints filling up with snow.

I have done this four times now. I will do it until they hang me. And I want it written down, in ink, in a ledger, in my own bad hand: the keeper never asks for anything. Not coin, not oath, not silence. The hunted come singing, and the keeper opens, and the hunters come shouting, and the keeper — the hunters find nothing. Not a wall. *Nothing.* The road simply isn't there for them, the way a word in a dead language isn't there for a man who never learned it.

My grandmother said the Veilwrights never smuggled goods. She said goods was just the cover story, the way a lullaby is just noise to a man with no children. She said they smuggled *people*, across the veil, into a country that fell off every map, and that the Guild died and the roads forgot and the songs forgot, but the door didn't forget.

The door kept the faith.

Somebody paid for that. Somebody paid a toll I can't imagine and wouldn't dare. My grandmother said the last doorkeeper gave up her name, and her roads, and every memory that ever kept her warm at night, and stood in the doorway forever so that the doorway would stand open for everyone else.

I don't pray. Nobody in my line prays; we pay tolls and mind our horses. But some nights, when I'm teaching another child the song, I say the old Guild blessing under my breath, the one my grandmother said had no author because its author gave up the right to be named:

*Roads rise to meet you. Doors know your grief. And whoever keeps the far side of the veil — may they remember us kindly, who cannot remember them at all.*

**V. Fragment of a letter, author unknown, found sewn into the lining of a smuggler's coat sold at the Cold Saddle market, in the eleventh year of the winter**

*—and so I went through. I am writing this on the far side, which no letter should be able to cross, and yet here it is, and you will believe that or you won't. The paper is Sareli paper, made from reeds that grow in a marsh that drowned two hundred years ago and here grows again under a sky the color of a healed wound, a gentle sky, a sky with no ash in it.*

*I cannot tell you what I saw. The ink runs when I try to write the truest things, as if this paper knows the difference between reporting and witness. I will say only this: there are people here. The ones who went through before the fall, and their children, and their children's children, and they have kept the language alive the way you keep a fire alive in a wet country — cupped, guarded, passed hand to hand. And they have a saying, which I will render as best I can:*

*The door remembers its keeper, and the keeper remembers nothing, and so the door remembers for two.*

*They say the keeper was a smuggler. They say the keeper's name is not lost, only held — held on the far side of the veil, in the one place the toll could not reach, because the toll was paid to open the way *out*, and a name given up does not vanish; it goes where all smuggled things go.*

*It is here. Somewhere here, her name is here.*

*They say the child — the Remnant, the key, though they would never call it that, they call them by a word that means "the ember carried through the winter" — they say the child grew up. Became a scholar. Became the first historian of the crossing, keeper of the records of everyone who ever came through the door, and that the historian sings the door-song at their own hearth every year on the night the door opened, every year, without fail, and that the singing is not mourning.*

*It is a letter. It is a letter sent the only way a letter can be sent to someone with no name and no memory and no claim to the world: sung, at a door, into the veil, year after year, in the hope that somewhere on the other side of the thin place, something that used to be a person with a smuggler's accent and a face like a washed page is listening.*

*And here is the last thing I will write, and then I will burn this letter, because some crossings are not mine to report:*

*They say that last year — for the first time in all the years — when the historian finished the song, the door did not stay silent.*

*They say something on the other side hummed the last line back.*

*In an accent from the river-country. A smuggler's accent.*
