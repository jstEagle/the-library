## 22. A Veil for the Ember

The door in Sarel was not a door the way doors were in Thornfell.

In Thornfell, doors had been things of iron and oak, hinges and locks, things that could be burned by Ashcarts or kicked down by soldiers or barred against a storm. In Sarel—this Sarel, the reborn one under its gentler sky—the First Door was a seam in the world, a fold of light between two standing stones that had been old when the drowned kingdom was young. It did not open for hands. It opened for song.

Ember had spent eleven years learning everything the crossing had erased, and eleven years learning everything it had kept.

They stood now on the near side of the veil, in the high country south of Vael Sarith where the mountains came down to meet a sea that was the color of hammered pewter. The wind here smelled different than any wind in the world they had left behind: salt and stone and something sweeter beneath it, like the memory of bread baked in an oven long cold. The grass grew silver at its tips. Even the light fell differently—slanted, honeyed, as if the sun itself had been forgiven something.

Ember had come alone. That was the tradition they were building, and traditions needed founders.

* * *

The archive at Vael Sarith called them the Historian of the Crossing, and Ember wore the title the way one wears a coat cut for someone larger—a fine thing, borrowed, still waiting to be grown into. There had never been a historian of the crossing before, because there had never been a crossing before that anyone had survived to write about. The Guild's records had burned. Maren's stolen page had gone into the fire with her body, or into the mountain with the door; Ember had never learned which, and Ines—

Ines had not been there to ask.

So Ember built the record from what remained: from the testimony of the ninety-one survivors who had walked through the light behind Ines Oyelaran's renunciation, their names inked in Ember's careful hand onto vellum made from Sareli reeds. From the fragments of Old Sareli that lived in smugglers' road-songs, half-remembered, sung wrong, sung anyway. From the waymarks that still glowed faintly in the passes of the other world, if you knew to look—and Ember knew, because Ember had been the thing that made them look, once, small and mute and frozen at a guildhall door while snow came down like ash.

Eleven years. Ember had been nine when the door took them through, small for nine, silent for longer than that. Now they were twenty, tall enough that the archivists no longer mistook them for a child who had wandered into the wrong wing, though they still moved like someone listening for footsteps behind them. Some habits outlasted kingdoms.

The work was slow and it was holy and most days it was enough.

But every year, on the day the first true snow had fallen on Thornfell—the day the guildhall burned behind them, the day Maren coughed blood into her scarf on the high road and kept walking anyway—Ember came back to the stones, and sang.

* * *

They had learned the whole song by then. Not the fragments Ines had coaxed from them in candlelit hours over a floorboard hollow, not the scraps Maren hummed with her sister's name caught sideways inside them, but the entire architecture of it: seven verses, each one a key turned in a lock older than language. Ember had found the lost verses in strange places—in the ringing of the tide-bells at Vael Sarith, in the pattern frost made on the archive windows, once, impossibly, in the dream of a dying ferryman who gripped Ember's wrist and said, *you're the one who remembers, aren't you, tell them we didn't drown, tell them we crossed.*

The fourth verse had taken three years. It was about doors, and it was also about grief, because in Old Sareli those words shared a root: *doreth*, the closing, and *doreth*, the mourning, and no Sareli speaker could say one without hearing the other. Ember had stood in the archive at midnight sounding it aloud until the candles guttered, until they understood that the people who built this song had known, all along, exactly what it would cost to use it.

Someone would have to give up everything. The song was shaped around an absence. It always had been.

Ember sang it now.

They began softly, the way Ines used to begin road-songs when Ashcart patrols were close—one voice pitched low, meant to carry only as far as it must. The syllables rose in the thin bright air, Old Sareli rolling off the tongue like water over stones, and the seam between the standing stones woke the way it always woke: a shiver of gold along its edge, a warmth like breath on winter skin. The veil thinned. Through it, dimly, Ember could see the other side—the high pass, the snow, the grey sky of a world still locked in its long winter, the waystation where Maren had died with her hand pressed flat against the stone as if she could hold the door open by stubbornness alone.

Ninety-one souls had passed through that seam. Ember had been the smallest.

And every year, for eleven years, Ember had finished the song and waited, and heard nothing but wind, and told themselves that was answer enough. The door held. The keeper held. Somewhere on the far side of the veil, nameless and bound, the person who had been Ines Oyelaran was doing what she had chosen to do, and the silence was the sound of her keeping faith.

Ember knelt in the silver grass and laid the year's offering at the threshold: a page of the new history, written in two languages, telling the stones what had become of the people she had saved. The reed-farmer Toma whose grandchildren now numbered eleven. The twins born on the crossing road, named Maren and Corvin both, because grief and redemption deserved equal memory. The guildhall rebuilt—not as a smugglers' den but as a school, in Vael Sarith, where children learned Old Sareli beside their letters and no Ashcart would ever burn it again.

*I'm still writing it down,* Ember said, in the old tongue, the way they said it every year. *All of it. Every road. Every name.*

The wind moved through the pass. The seam glowed and settled. And then—

The wind answered.

Not with wind. With voice.

It was very faint, and it came from inside the light rather than beyond it, and it spoke Old Sareli with the worst accent Ember had ever heard—vowels flattened, consonants chewed, the cadence of someone who had learned the language off road-songs in smoky rooms from masters who'd learned it the same way, four generations deep in getting it wrong. A smuggler's accent. A Thornfell accent. An accent Ember would have known anywhere, would have known blindfolded, would have known dead.

*"Doreth i salen,"* the voice said, mangled and unmistakable. *Door holds. Keep singing, little ember.*

Ember's knees hit the grass.

For one breath—one breath, no more—they were nine years old again, half-frozen on a guildhall floor, wrapped in a coat that smelled of pipe smoke and road dust, looking up into a face lined by weather and worry that was trying very hard to be stern and failing completely. They heard the floorboards creak above the smugglers' hollow. They heard the lie Ines told the Ashcart officer, smooth and steady, the first lie of her life, told for a stranger's child. They heard her voice in the dark of the ravine saying *hold on to me, hold on, I've got you, I've got you* as the river tore at them both, and they felt again the terrible arithmetic of that grip—that she had measured, in that moment, whether she could hold the child and the rope both, and had already decided which one she would keep.

One breath. Then the light stilled, and the wind was only wind, and the voice—if it had ever been anything more than longing given shape by the veil—was gone.

But the seam stayed warm. Warmer than it had ever stayed after a song. And in the grass at the threshold, where nothing had been a moment before, lay a single object, small and dark, half-buried in silver stalks:

A route-book. Tiny, no bigger than a coin, bound in leather gone soft with handling. Ember lifted it with shaking fingers and opened it, and the pages were blank—but not empty. Under certain light, the way certain roads opened only to certain songs, faint lines surfaced across the paper: the old Guild routes, every pass and hollow and hidden stair of Thornfell, drawn in a hand Ember knew better than their own. And on the last page, in the same hand, one line of common script:

*Roads remember. So do I. —I.O., keeper of the door, late of the Veilwright Guild, formerly of nowhere at all.*

She had written "formerly" like a joke. She had signed herself with initials because she had no name to sign, and had made even that sound like swagger.

Ember sat in the silver grass with the little book pressed to their chest and cried the way they had not cried since the crossing—not the controlled, historian's grief of the archive, but the ragged animal crying of a child at a door. And when the tears finally slowed, they did what historians do. They took out their kit. They inked a pen. And they recorded, in Old Sareli and in the common tongue, in a hand that shook and steadied and shook again:

*Year eleven of the reborn reckoning. At the First Door, the keeper answered. The veil does not silence. The veil only waits. Let it be written that love is a road, and roads remember, and no toll can hold what crosses willingly.*

Then Ember closed the book, stood, and faced the seam of light with the wind pulling at their coat.

"I'll come back next year," they said, in the common tongue, deliberately, so she'd have to hear the accent too. "And the year after. And every year after that, for as long as I've got breath. You gave up your name for us—" their voice cracked, and they let it crack, because she had never once let them hide a feeling behind composure, and they weren't going to start hiding them now. "So I'm giving you mine. Every year. Ember. That's yours. I wouldn't have one without you."

The seam pulsed once, soft as a heartbeat. Warmth spilled across the threshold like a hand laid briefly on a shoulder.

Ember smiled through the last of the tears, shouldered their pack, and turned down the long road toward Vael Sarith, where ninety-one lives and one burning guildhall and one faceless keeper waited inside the pages of a history still being written.

Behind them, the First Door glowed quietly in the high pass, holding fast against the winter.

It would hold forever.

But it was no longer silent—and neither, anymore, was the road between.
