## 1. Charms for Copper

The night market of Corvane woke the way a wounded animal wakes — slowly, warily, one sense at a time.

Sable Brightwater knew this because she had spent twelve years learning to wake with it. She lit her stall's three lanterns before full dark, hung the charms in their careful rows, and arranged her wares on the scarred plank table: knotted red thread against bad dreams, river-stones polished smooth and sold as *calming stones*, bundles of dried lavender that smelled like every grandmother's linen chest. Harmless things. Honest things, if you didn't count the lie underneath them all, which was that Sable Brightwater could make them actually work.

She never did. That was the whole architecture of her survival, built stone by careful stone over twelve years: she sold the shape of magic without its substance. A knot was just a knot. A stone was just a stone. If a customer came back swearing the fever had broken or the milk hadn't soured, she smiled and said they must have caught it early, and took their copper, and hated herself a little more efficiently each time.

"Evening, Brightwater." Old Maren from the fish stall two rows down paused on her way past, arms crossed against the river damp. "Cold tonight."

"Cold most nights," Sable said. "That's Corvane."

"That it is." Maren lingered a moment longer than she needed to, the way people did when they wanted to say something and decided against it. Then she moved on, her lantern swinging, and Sable exhaled slowly through her nose.

Twelve years ago, Maren would not have lingered. Twelve years ago, nobody lingered near anyone who looked like Sable — tall, sword-callused hands hidden under merchant's gloves, a stillness to her shoulders that no fishwife or charm-seller ever quite managed. She'd trained herself out of the stillness eventually. She slouched now. She laughed at the right moments, a little too loud, a little too easily. She had become so good at being small that some mornings she woke up and had to search for herself behind the performance, the way you search a crowded room for someone you know is there but can't see.

*You are a charm-seller,* she told herself, as she told herself every evening, laying out the knots and stones. *You are a widow of no particular grief, from no particular village, selling nothing that matters.*

The words fit her like the gloves fit her hands: snugly, and only after years of stretching.

The market filled around her. Lamplighters made their rounds along the canal road, and the smell of frying onions and tallow smoke braided itself into the wet-stone smell of the city. Somewhere a fiddle played badly. Somewhere a drunk sang worse. Corvane's night market did not care about prophecies or realms or oaths; it cared about copper, warmth, and the small mercies of getting through another week.

Sable loved it anyway. That was the cruelest part. She had built her little life inside the city's smallest hour, among people who would never ask where her scars came from, and she loved it the way you love anything you've chosen out of fear rather than hope — fiercely, and with a constant low hum of shame beneath the affection.

Her first customer was a boy buying a dream-knot for his mother, who had lost a child last spring and screamed in her sleep most nights. He counted out coppers one by one, tongue between his teeth, and Sable watched him with an ache she kept carefully off her face.

"It won't work," she said quietly, when he'd finished counting. She always said this. It was the closest thing to confession her life allowed. "It's just thread."

"My gran swore by yours." The boy shrugged, pocketing the knot. "Said yours held better than the temple's."

"Your gran was kind."

"She was a liar about half of everything," he said cheerfully, and left, and Sable sat with the word *kind* sitting strangely in her chest for a long moment before the next customer came.

* * *

They came in waves, the way market customers always did — three at once, then a drought, then five. A woman wanting a calming stone for her husband's temper. Two laborers splitting the cost of a luck-braid before the harvest contracts were let. A girl of maybe fifteen who hovered at the edge of the lamplight for a long time before approaching, and who asked, in a voice pitched low enough that no one else could hear:

"Do you have anything for forgetting?"

Sable's hands went still over the charm-rack.

"Forgetting what?" she asked, and heard how carefully neutral her own voice came out.

"Just — things." The girl wouldn't meet her eyes. "Things I saw. I work at the magistrate's keep, laundry. Sometimes you see things going in, coming out. There's a garden under glass, they say, and men go in and don't—" She stopped herself, visibly, the way people in Corvane had learned to stop themselves, snipping sentences short before they grew teeth. "Just something for sleeping without dreams. Please."

*There's a garden under glass.* The words slid between Sable's ribs before she could stop them, cold and precise as a blade finding the gap in armor. She thought of the Purge — of course she did, she thought of it every hour of every day, it was simply that some hours it spoke louder — and of the things she had seen going into the magistrate's keep twelve years ago, carried on carts under oilcloth.

"I don't sell forgetting," Sable said. "No honest seller does. But—" She reached under the table, past the stock she showed everyone, to the small drawer where she kept the things she gave away. She pressed a twist of dried valerian and chamomile into the girl's hand. "Steep this. It's cheap and it works because plants work, not because of anything else. And the girl — listen. Whatever you saw under glass: forget it yourself. Deliberately. It's a skill. The best one I know."

Something passed across the girl's face then — recognition, or its ghost. For one terrible, suspended moment Sable thought: *she knows*. But the girl only nodded, mouthed *thank you*, and melted back into the crowd, and Sable stood in the lamplight with her heart hammering and her hands perfectly steady, because twelve years of practice meant her body had learned to lie even when the rest of her was screaming.

*One day,* she thought, not for the first time, *someone will look at me exactly like that and mean it, and that will be the end.*

She had imagined the ending many times. Soldiers at the stall. A knock at her rented room. The specific horror of hearing her true name spoken aloud in a crowd — *Brightwater*, no, the other name, the name that belonged to swordspeakers, the name she had buried with everyone else who'd ever used it. In twelve years she had rehearsed her own arrest so thoroughly that sometimes it felt less like fear than like memory. That was the trick fear played when you fed it daily: it stopped feeling like weather and started feeling like furniture.

She was so busy hating the furniture that she almost missed him.

He came to the stall at the thin hour, when the market had emptied to stragglers and the fiddler had gone home and the canal mist was rising off the water in slow gray ribbons. He was young — twenty, perhaps — and he moved like a man being chased by his own shadow, glancing over both shoulders twice before he reached her table. His coat was archivist's gray, ink-stained at the cuffs. Under one arm he carried a leather satchel clutched the way other men carried infants.

"Charm-seller." His voice cracked on the second syllable. He cleared his throat. "I'm looking for — they said you had — a calming stone?"

"I have calming stones." Sable studied him. Sweat at the hairline despite the cold. Eyes that kept cataloguing exits. "Though between us, they're stones. The calm has to come from you."

"Right. Yes. Good. That's — fine." He was already digging in his coat for money, moving too fast, not looking at what he pulled out. "Here. Just — take it, please, I need to be quick—"

"Slow down." She put her hand over his fist before she thought better of touching a stranger, and felt him flinch like a struck horse. "Nobody's following you. Breathe."

"You don't know that," he whispered, and there was such naked, absolute terror in it that Sable's old instincts rose up unbidden — the ones from before, from the order, from the years when reading a frightened stranger had been a survival skill of a different flavor. This wasn't a man afraid of debt collectors or a jealous husband. This was a man afraid of the state.

She should have sent him away. Every instinct honed over twelve years said *send him away, small lives stay small, curiosity is a luxury bought with blood*.

Instead she said, "What did you see?"

He looked up at her then, really looked, and whatever he found in her face made him stop pretending to breathe evenly. Slowly, watching her the whole time, he opened his fist over the table.

Coins spilled out — a mix of coppers and one old silver piece that landed face-up and stopped Sable's heart in her chest.

It was worn nearly smooth. A century of thumbs had rubbed the detail soft. But the stamp survived, because stamps cut deep survive deep, and Sable had known this particular sigil since before she could write her own name: a circle broken by a single vertical blade, the blade's point resting on an open flower.

The sigil of the Order of the Verdant Oath. The sigil that had been burned off doors, chiseled off walls, scraped out of books, forbidden so thoroughly that even thinking its shape felt like shouting.

Sable did not move. Every nerve in her body was screaming at her to move — to sweep the coin into the gutter, to laugh, to say *funny old thing, where'd you get that junk?* — but she had also learned, in those same twelve years, that panic was a scent, and the state's dogs could track it.

"Old coin," she said, and her voice came out bored, and she marveled distantly at her own craftsmanship. "Where'd you get junk like this?"

"The archive." The archivist — if that's what he was — swallowed hard. "I'm Wren. Wren Halloway. Second archivist, third floor, restricted stacks." The words tumbled out of him like water from a kicked-over bucket. "I wasn't looking for it, I swear, I was cataloguing a seized estate, papers from a house in the old quarter, and the coin was sewn into the lining of a coat, and the coat belonged to—"

He stopped. Looked at the coin. Looked at her.

"—to a swordspeaker," he finished, very quietly. "Didn't it."

The market noise seemed to recede. The mist, the lamplight, the distant fiddle — all of it drew back like a tide, leaving Sable standing on the exposed seabed of herself.

"You're mistaken," she said.

"Yes," Wren Halloway agreed instantly, nodding too fast. "Definitely. Of course. Terrible mistake." He swept the coins toward himself except the silver one, which he slid deliberately, slowly, across the table until it rested under the lamplight directly in front of her. "Keep it. For the calming stone. Call it fair payment."

"That's too much."

"It's not payment for the stone." He shouldered his satchel. His eyes were bright and terrified and certain, all three at once. "It's payment for listening, later. When you're ready. Because there's a book, charm-seller, and the book has dates in it, and one of the dates is—" He glanced over his shoulder again, and this time his flinch looked real, not nervous. Footsteps somewhere behind him, boots on wet stone, more than one pair. "Tonight. Come find me at the archive. Third door past the cistern, knock twice, wait, knock once. And burn that coin if you're smarter than I think you are."

Then he was gone, swallowed by the mist, and the boots behind him faded the other direction, and Sable Brightwater stood alone at her little stall in the smallest hour of the smallest life she had been able to build, staring down at a circle broken by a blade resting on a flower.

She should burn it. That was the sensible thing, the survivable thing, the thing the woman named Brightwater the charm-seller would do without hesitation. Twelve years of discipline pointed at the brazier like a compass needle.

But her hands — her sword-callused, gloved, carefully ordinary hands — picked the coin up instead. And the metal was cold, colder than the night justified, and through her glove she felt it humming faintly, the way oath-stones used to hum when a speaker held them true.

*Tonight,* the archivist had said. *The book has dates in it.*

Sable closed her fingers around the sigil of everyone she had ever loved and every promise she had ever broken, and began, with great care, to pack up her stall early.
