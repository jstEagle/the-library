## 2. The Book That Knew Tonight

The coin sat in Sable's palm like a held breath.

She had turned it over four times already, standing beneath the awning of her stall while the night market murmured and shuffled around her — fishwives haggling over eels, a lamplighter coaxing flame into the glass globes, the smell of fried dough and wet stone and tallow. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary smells. The ordinary world she had built her life inside of, one small trade at a time.

The coin was not ordinary.

Copper, worn nearly smooth by a century of thumbs, but the stamp was still legible if you knew what you were looking at: a sword crossed with a flowering branch, ringed by nine stars. The sigil of the Order of the Verdant Blade. Her order. The order that had died screaming in the Purge twelve years ago while Sable Brightwater had run and kept running until running became a life.

No one minted these anymore. No one dared. She had seen them only twice since that night — once in a magistrate's evidence case, carried past her in the street while she'd pressed herself against a wall and prayed to be invisible; once in the hands of a drunk old soldier who wore it on a cord around his neck and wept when he told her he'd been paid with it for looking the other way.

And now here was a third, handed to her as payment for a luck-charm worth two coppers at most, by a young woman who had looked at Sable with eyes too sharp for a casual buyer and said, *Keep it. I think it's meant for you.*

Sable had sold charms in Corvane's night market for eleven years, and she had learned every species of customer the way a fisherman learns weather. The ones who wanted love. The ones who wanted revenge dressed up as justice. The ones who bought protection from bad dreams and got, instead, someone to listen to them for ten minutes while she tied knots in red thread. She could read a face in half a heartbeat.

This one had not been reading like a customer. This one had been reading like an archivist who had spent weeks confirming something and wanted to watch it land.

*Keep it. I think it's meant for you.*

Sable closed her fist around the coin and did not sell another charm all night.

* * *

The archive stood three streets back from the market square, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered playhouse, its door painted the grey-green of old verdigris so that most people walked past without ever seeing it. Wren Halloway — that was the name she'd finally given, after the third knock — opened the door before Sable finished knocking the fourth time, as though she'd been standing behind it with her hand on the latch for an hour.

Which, knowing archivists, she probably had been.

"You came," Wren said. She was younger than Sable had first taken her for — mid-twenties, perhaps, with ink stains on three fingers and hair pinned up in a style that suggested the pins had lost the argument hours ago. Spectacles perched on her nose, slightly crooked. "I thought you might throw the coin in the river."

"I considered it."

"And instead you came to a stranger's archive at midnight because a piece of copper bothered you." Wren stepped back to let her in. "That tells me more about you than any question I could ask."

"I came," Sable said, crossing the threshold, "because I want to know where you found it, and how many people you've shown it to."

"Ah." Wren's mouth twitched. "There it is."

Inside, the archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular dry sweetness of very old paper. Shelves climbed the walls into shadow, ladders on brass rails, and everywhere — everywhere — books. Not the gilded kind the magistrates kept in their public libraries, scrubbed and approved and toothless. These were the other kind. Banned kind. Books with their spines broken and their pages swollen with damp, books wrapped in oilcloth, books stacked in drifts on the floor like snowfall no one had swept.

Sable's chest tightened. She had spent twelve years being careful, and here was a woman sitting on a mountain of careful's opposite.

"You understand what I could do to you," Sable said quietly. "Report this address. One sentence to any city guard and this room burns, and you burn with it."

"I've thought about that every day for six years," Wren said. "Then I decided that a person who hides everything important she knows isn't an archivist. She's just a hoarder." She pulled off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, put them back crooked. "Sit down, swordspeaker."

The word hit her like cold water thrown at the back of the neck.

"I don't know what you mean," Sable said. It came out smooth. Twelve years of practice, smooth as river stone. "I sell charms. Thread and tin and wishful thinking."

"Yes," Wren agreed pleasantly, "and last winter you sold my neighbor's son a fever-knot that broke his fever overnight, which thread cannot do. And in autumn you sold old Marta a charm against joint-pain, and Marta danced at the harvest fair for the first time in nine years. And three years ago, when the granary fire spread toward the tannery row, someone — a woman about your height, hooded — walked through flames that should have cooked the flesh from her bones, and the flames *parted*, and everyone who saw it agreed afterward that they must have imagined it." Wren tilted her head. "You're very good. You give away almost nothing. A trickle, never a flood. Just enough kindness to keep your conscience fed and just enough smallness to keep the magistrates blind. But I read old things for a living, Mistress Brightwater, and old things teach you to notice what's missing. Your whole life is shaped like a hole where something used to be."

Silence stretched between them, taut as wire.

Sable became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and heavy, and of the coin still in her pocket, warm against her hip as though it were alive. Every sensible instinct she possessed — and she had cultivated her sensible instincts like a garden, weeded and watered for twelve years — screamed at her to stand up, thank the girl for the tea, and walk out of this room full of tinder and confession.

Instead she said, "How long have you known my name?"

"I didn't. Until three days ago." Wren crossed to a shelf near the back, where a book lay wrapped in oilcloth on a lectern of its own, apart from the others — set aside the way one sets aside a thing that frightens you even now. "It was given to me by a dying collector. He wouldn't say where he got it. He said it was waiting for someone, and that I'd know the someone when I saw her, because—" she unwrapped the cloth with the care of a woman handling a sleeping snake "—because the book would tell me."

The book beneath the cloth was bound in green leather gone dark with age, its corners guarded by tarnished brass. No title on the cover. No title on the spine. When Wren opened it to the first page, the handwriting that met Sable's eyes was elegant and faintly strange, the letters leaning as if walking into wind.

*Herein is written what will come,* the page began. *Not what may. What will.*

"A century old," Wren said softly. "We've tested the binding, the ink, the vellum. A hundred years, give or take a decade. Written by a hand that signs itself only as *the Gardener*. Most of it is... agricultural prophecy, if you can believe such a thing. Seasons. Harvests. The health of some covenant between this city and a realm called Virelai. Pages and pages of it. Dull as ditchwater, mostly — except that everything it predicted has come true. Everything. To the week, sometimes to the hour." She turned pages carefully, and Sable saw columns of dated entries marching forward through decades, through the founding of laws, through the Purge itself—

Sable's breath caught. There. Midway down a page dated twelve years past:

*The blades are silenced. The garden mourns its speakers. One remains, hidden, small as an ember under ash.*

"That's—" Sable stopped. Started again. "Books can be forged."

"Of course. But keep going."

Wren turned the pages faster now, years flickering past, the entries thinning as they approached the present, as though the writer's certainty were narrowing toward a single point. And then the final page, and Wren stopped turning, and stepped back, and looked at Sable with an expression that was almost apologetic.

The date inked there was tonight's.

Not approximately. Not in the vague way prophecies hedge themselves — *in the days when*, *when the moon turns*. Tonight's date, precise as a ledger entry, written in that same wind-leaning script a hundred years ago.

And beside the date, two lines:

*The veil thins to a thread and parts.*
*Sable Brightwater, last of the nine, stands beneath the breaking sky.*

The market noise outside seemed suddenly very far away. Sable heard her own breathing. She heard the tick of a clock somewhere in the shelves, patient as a beetle in wood.

"No," she said.

"It's your name," Wren said gently. "Your true name. Not the name on your market license — I checked that too, eventually. Sable Brightwater. Last of the nine."

"I know how many there were." Her voice came out flat and cold, and she hated how steady it was, how it sounded like the voice she used for customers. "There were sixty-three of us. Sixty-three speakers, and forty-one sworn blades, and I have spent twelve years learning to sleep at night, and you are not going to sit there with your crooked spectacles and your illegal library and tell me that a dead man's fairy tales—"

"The Gardener wasn't a man."

"—tell me that a dead person's fairy tales get to *decide my life*." She was on her feet now. She didn't remember standing. "Do you know what happens to people whose names appear in prophecy in this city? Do you know what Magistrate Dain does with—"

The words stopped in her throat, because the ink was moving.

At first she thought it was the lamplight — a flicker, a trick of the eye, the way candleflame makes shadows swim across a page. But the lamps in Wren's archive burned steady behind glass, and the letters were *moving*, the wind-leaning script shifting like grass in a breeze, rearranging itself letter by letter with a sound Sable felt rather than heard, a whisper of fiber and pigment, and where the second line had read *stands beneath the breaking sky*, the words were dissolving and re-forming, and new words surfaced in their place as though rising through water:

*stands beneath the breaking sky, and does not yet believe.*

Sable took a step backward. Her hip struck the edge of the lectern table. The coin fell from somewhere — her pocket, her nerveless fingers — and rang against the floorboards, spinning, slowing, settling with the sword-and-branch sigil facing up, nine stars ringing it like a crown.

"What is it doing?" Her voice sounded distant to her own ears. Young. Frightened. Twelve years stripped away in five seconds, and underneath the merchant's calm there was a girl of nineteen watching her world catch fire again. "Why is it — books don't—"

"I told you," Wren said, very quietly. "Everything it has ever predicted has come true."

"But it's *changing*. If it changes, then nothing is fixed, then it isn't prophecy, it's just—" Sable pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "It's just a cruel thing that writes whatever will hurt most."

The ink whispered again. Both women watched, helpless as witnesses to a crime, as a third line grew beneath the first two, letter by deliberate letter, the pen of some absent hand dragging itself across a century-dead page:

*She will want to refuse. Refusal is permitted. The veil does not wait for permission.*

Below that, the final line of the book, appearing now in strokes that trembled slightly — as if the writing hand, too, were afraid:

*Tonight. Beneath the bell tower. Bring the copper, or bring your grief. Either will open the door.*

The clock ticked. Outside, far off, Corvane's great bell began to mark the quarter hour, each toll falling through the floorboards and up through Sable's boots like footsteps descending a stair.

Nine o'clock. Three hours, if the book could be trusted. If a book that rewrote itself while she watched could be trusted to mean anything at all except that she had finally, after twelve careful years, lost her mind.

She looked down at the coin on the floor. Sigil up. Nine stars.

When she picked it up, her hand was shaking, and the metal was warm — warmer than her skin, warmer than the lamplit room, warm the way a hand is warm. Warm the way a living thing is warm.

"This is a trap," she said, and it was half a question, aimed at Wren, at the book, at the universe generally. "Dain has been hunting the last of us for twelve years. What better bait than my own name in an old book? What better hook?"

"It's possible," Wren admitted. "I've considered it. I've lain awake considering it." She straightened her spectacles, which immediately went crooked again. "But traps don't usually warn you about the trap. And traps don't write *bring your grief*." Her voice caught, just barely, on the last word. "That's not a magistrate's handwriting, Mistress Brightwater. That's something older, and sadder, and it has been waiting a hundred years for exactly this night, and I don't think it cares whether we believe in it or not."

The bell finished its counting. Silence poured back in.

Sable stood in the middle of the archive with a warm coin in her fist and a dead order's sigil biting into her palm, and she understood — with the terrible clarity that arrives like cold water, all at once, leaving no dignified interval in which to pretend otherwise — that whatever choice she made in the next three hours, the life she had built would not survive the night intact. The small life. The safe life. The shrunken, folded-away, carefully dimmed life she had told herself was survival.

*Refusal is permitted.*

She almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a single unsteady breath.

"If I walk out that door and go home," she said slowly, "and bar it, and sit with my back against it until dawn—"

"Then either nothing happens," Wren said, "and you spend the rest of your life flinching at dates. Or something happens anyway, and it happens without you, and you spend the rest of your life knowing you were named in the telling and chose to hide." She hesitated. "I know which of those I couldn't live with. But then, my name isn't in the book."

*Yet,* Sable did not say.

She moved to the window instead — a narrow slit of a window, glazed with old glass that turned the market square beyond into a smear of gold and shadow. From here she could see the bell tower rising above the rooftops, black against the deepening blue, its silhouette exactly as she remembered it from a lifetime ago: the place where the Order had trained, where sixty-three voices had once learned to speak power into the world, where she had —

Where she had run from.

The tower looked, tonight, like a held breath. Like a door standing open at the top of the city.

"Beneath the bell tower," she repeated. Her reflection in the old glass stared back at her, ghosted over the square, superimposed on the place she'd sworn never to return to. "Midnight. With a coin or with my grief."

"Those may be the same thing," Wren said softly.

Sable turned from the window. Somewhere in her chest, behind twelve years of ash, an ember she had smothered every single morning of her exile shifted in its sleep — and turned over — and began, unmistakably, to glow.

"Then I suppose," she said, picking her cloak up off the chair, "you'd better tell me everything else that's in that book. Because if I'm going to walk into a prophecy tonight, Wren Halloway, I intend to do it knowing exactly how many ways it plans to kill me."

Wren was already reaching for the oilcloth, already turning back toward the beginning, but as her fingers touched the cover, the book whispered one last time — a single line surfacing on the final page, fresh ink gleaming wet in the lamplight, addressed so plainly that both women read it aloud together without meaning to:

*Hurry.*
