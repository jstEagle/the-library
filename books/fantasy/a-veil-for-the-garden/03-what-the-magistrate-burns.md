## 3. What the Magistrate Burns

The archive of Corvane kept its secrets the way old houses kept cold: in the places nobody bothered to look. Wren Halloway had spent eleven years learning those places — the false backs of catalogues, the ledgers shelved as hymnals, the drawer beneath the map case that stuck until you lifted its left corner and spoke to it politely, as though it were a mule. The city believed the archive was a mausoleum for tax records and grain tariffs. It was, in truth, a lung. It breathed in everything Corvane wanted to forget.

Tonight it was breathing hard.

Wren felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in the air of the stacks, the particular stillness of paper that knows it is being hunted. She had been at her desk on the third gallery since sundown, the prophecy book open beneath a shaded lamp, and she had read the final page eleven times. *On the eve of the Falling Dark, the veil shall wear thin as an old coin.* Tonight's date. Sable Brightwater's true name inked beside it in a hand a century dead, as if the archivist who wrote it had been taking dictation from the future.

She should have sent word to Sable already. She had tried, twice, and both times her hand had stopped over the page, some animal instinct in her refusing to put that name down in ink where ink could be read. Names were dangerous things in Corvane. She had learned that young, watching her father's library confiscated shelf by shelf because three of his books had margins annotated in a script the censors called *suspect*. He had not been arrested. He had simply stopped being invited anywhere, stopped being spoken to, stopped, eventually, quite being himself — a man folded smaller and smaller until there was nothing left to fold.

She would not be folded. But she had also never learned to unfold anyone else.

The bell above the archive's service door rang once — the two-note chime of the night porter, except the night porter never rang twice in succession and never, ever came down the third gallery stairs this late. Wren closed the book with a sound like a held breath and slid it into the drawer beneath the map case, lifting the left corner, murmuring the small courtesy the wood liked. Then she picked up the nearest ledger, opened it to grain shipments, and became, in posture and expression, exactly what the Magistracy expected an archivist to be: dull, bent, invisible.

Boots on the stairs. Not one pair. Six.

Magistrate Corvel Dain did not enter rooms; he annexed them. Wren watched him come down the gallery between two grey-coated officers, his long coat unbuttoned despite the cold, his face carrying the particular patience of a man who has already decided how this evening ends and is merely permitting everyone else the fiction of suspense. Behind him came more officers through the stacks below, and she heard — her stomach dropped — the sound of drawers being pulled, catalogues thrown back, spines cracked.

"Magistrate." She rose, and was pleased that her voice came out level. "The archive closes at ninth bell."

"The archive closes when I close it." Dain stopped at the railing and looked out over the lower floor, where lanterns were now moving between the shelves like will-o'-wisps in a bog. "You are Halloway. Senior keeper of restricted holdings."

"I'm the only keeper of restricted holdings. There was a budget reduction in—"

"Yes." He said it the way one silences a door hinge. "Then you'll know every text in this building that concerns prophecy. I want them. All of them. Now."

Wren let a beat pass — the correct bureaucratic beat, the one that said *I am merely confused, not afraid*. "Prophecy texts were purged under the Edict of Sealed Mouths, Magistrate. Forty years ago. Whatever survived the burning was transferred to the Magisterial vault. I couldn't produce one if I wept."

"I don't require you to weep, Halloway. I require you to be useful." He turned at last and looked at her fully, and up close his eyes were not patient at all but exhausted — the exhaustion of a man who has been awake too many nights listening for something at the walls of the world. "There was a theft reported tonight. A coin dealer on Tallow Street sold certain items he could not account for. Among them, a book. Old. Bound in green leather. His testimony is that a woman bought it from him days ago, a woman with archive credentials."

*Oh, Wren,* she thought, *you clever, stupid girl.*

"Archive credentials are stolen weekly," she said. "Half the students at the Collegium forge them for access to the romance collections. If you'd like me to pull the incident rolls—"

"I'd like you to stand there while my men search your gallery." He moved past her into the stacks, trailing one gloved finger along the shelf edges as though reading them blind, and Wren understood with cold clarity that he knew this place. Not as a visitor. As someone who had stood in these aisles before, years ago, perhaps the night her order — no. Not her order. *Their* order. The swordspeakers. The night the archive had lost an entire wing to fire, officially attributed to a lamp left burning.

She followed him because not following would have been worse.

For a quarter hour the only sounds were paper sounds — the soft avalanche of pages, drawers groaning, an officer's muttered apology when something fragile broke. Wren stood with her hands clasped behind her back and performed a miracle of stillness while inside her the drawer beneath the map case sat like a coal in a snowbank. The book was warded — she had warded it herself, a simple concealment knot, nothing that would stop a determined searcher with a trained sensitivity, but Dain's men were soldiers, not sensitives. They would find nothing unless—

Dain stopped walking.

He had come around the end of the geography cases, and he stood now before the map case itself, and Wren saw him look at it not as furniture but as architecture. Measuring it. She watched his eye travel the length of it, top to bottom, and snag — she actually saw it snag — on the drawer that stuck.

"Halloway."

"Magistrate."

"Why does an archive keep a locked drawer under its maps?"

"It doesn't lock. It sticks. The humidity—" Even as she said it she heard how thin it was, tissue paper over a wound, and Dain heard it too. He turned. For a moment neither of them moved, and in that moment Wren took inventory of him the way she inventoried damaged folios: condition poor; provenance violent; value uncertain. A widower — everyone knew that much, the wife lost six years back to a fever the physickers couldn't name. A survivor of the Purge, decorated for it, promoted by it. And underneath all of it, visible now that she looked, a fear so large he had built his whole career as its load-bearing wall.

"You're going to open it anyway," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"May I ask why prophecy frightens the Magistracy? Truly. Not the official answer."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Recognition, almost. Respect for the directness, maybe, or relief that someone had finally asked instead of whispered. He glanced toward his men, out of earshot among the distant shelves, and when he spoke his voice dropped to something almost conversational, which was somehow more frightening than shouting.

"Frightens us." He tested the word. "No, Halloway, fear isn't the problem. Fear can be governed. What can't be governed is *debt*. That's what prophecy is — a bill delivered a hundred years early, itemized by people who claim to know the future. Do you know what Virelai called its covenant? Its *gift*?" His gloved hand tightened on the edge of the map case, denting nothing, wanting to. "I've seen their gardens eat a fence line in a night. I've seen what grows through when they're feeling generous, and I've seen what comes through when they're not. My grandmother remembered the tithe years — the harvests that vanished, the children marked. And when the covenant broke, when we finally burned their bridges, do you know what happened? Nothing. We lived. We *thrived*. Because it was never a gift. It was interest on a loan we never signed, and now the collectors are coming again with their little book of dates, and I am done paying."

"So you'll burn the book."

"I'll burn every book." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "And if the veil fails anyway — if their garden comes bleeding through our streets — then this city meets it with steel and fire and *no obligations*. No covenant. No oath. No debt. Let it fall. Whatever survives the fall will be ours." He straightened his coat, and the conversational man was gone, folded away like a letter. "Open the drawer."

"I can't."

"Officer Brann," Dain said mildly, without looking away from her, "the keeper's hands appear to be injured. Assist her."

The soldier's grip closed on her wrist, and Wren made her choice in the half-second between pressure and pain: she reached down, lifted the drawer's left corner, and spoke to the wood — not the polite murmur it liked, but the second phrase, the one that didn't open it. The one that sealed it. The ward-knot cinched shut with a sensation like a swallowed breath, and the drawer fused to the case, seamless, and by the time the officer wrenched at it there was nothing to find but joined oak.

Brann pulled harder. Dain watched. Wren watched Dain watch, and kept her face arranged into bafflement, and prayed to whatever kept archives that her heartbeat wasn't audible.

"She's telling the truth," Brann said finally. "Joined wood. Old work."

"Mm." Dain released the map case. He looked at Wren for a long moment, and she had the terrible sense of being read herself — cover, spine, marginalia — by a man deciding whether she was worth the fire. "You know," he said, almost gently, "your father had a fine library."

The floor tilted. "You wouldn't remember—"

"Censors' reports stay filed, Halloway. Everything stays filed." He drew off one glove, finger by finger, and from his breast pocket took a small instrument of black glass no bigger than a seal ring. Wren had heard rumors of these — censor's marks, the Collegium called them, tools for tagging documents too ambiguous to seize. She had never heard of one being used on a person. "This building holds forty thousand volumes and one sticking drawer. I don't have men enough to watch them all. So I'll watch *you* instead. You are, after all, the only keeper of restricted holdings."

He pressed the ring to the back of her right hand, just below the thumb.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it — a cool touch, a momentary tightness like frost forming, and when he withdrew it there on her skin sat a mark like a watermark, faintly luminous, a small interlocked sigil she recognized from a hundred confiscated title pages: the seal of the Magisterial Censorate. It sank beneath the surface even as she stared, settling into the dermis like sediment into riverbed, gone from sight but not, she understood instantly, gone.

"A marked keeper retains full privileges," Dain said, drawing his glove back on. "Access to any collection. Freedom of the stacks. And every document she touches, every room she enters, every name she speaks within a warded building reports itself to my office by morning. Consider yourself promoted, Halloway. You are now the most closely read text in Corvane."

"You can't—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "This is illegal. The Charter of Archives—"

"—was written by men who believed the veil would hold forever." He was already walking away, boots measured on the stone. At the head of the stairs he paused, and without turning back said: "Burn order goes up at dawn for the prophecy holdings in the vault transfer list. If any such items exist outside that list, Halloway, tonight would be the night to feel remorse about them."

Then he was gone, and his men went with him, and the great doors boomed, and the archive exhaled.

Wren stood alone in the gallery with her hand throbbing faintly, warm as a fevered brow, and did the arithmetic of her ruin. Marked keepers touched nothing unreported. The book beneath the map case might as well sit on Dain's desk already — the ward would hold against hands, but the moment she opened the drawer, the room itself would whisper. She could not carry it out. She could not send it anywhere. Every road from the archive now ran through the Magistrate's morning report.

And at dawn, the vault transfer list would put every prophecy text in Corvane into a fire, and the last book that knew Sable Brightwater's name would burn with the rest — unless it left this building before first light, carried by someone whose movements the mark would betray.

Unless it left in someone else's hands entirely.

Wren walked back to the map case and laid her palm flat against the joined oak, feeling through the wood a faint warmth that was not hers and not the book's but something older, patient, and appallingly alive — as if the pages themselves had been waiting a century for exactly this kind of urgency. She thought of Sable Brightwater's face in the lamplight of her shop, the way the woman's hands had stilled on that old coin, twelve years of silence standing in her like water stands in a well.

*I'm sorry,* Wren thought at the book, at the mark on her hand, at the whole doomed machinery of a city frightened of its own history. *I'm going to have to spend you.*

She took up her pen, pulled a clean requisition slip toward her, and began — carefully, innocently, in the dullest administrative hand she owned — to write the one request a marked keeper could make that Dain's office would wave through without reading:

*To the Guild of Couriers, night desk. One parcel, sealed, to be delivered before dawn. Addressee: the charm-seller, Tallow Street market.*

Outside, far to the east beyond the city walls, where no garden should have been able to grow, something green stirred against the dark — and Wren, glancing up at the high window, saw it only as a shadow of vines where no vines were, reaching, very slowly, toward the light of the town.
