## 4. The Garden Bleeds Through

The coin would not stay quiet.

Sable had wrapped it in three layers of linen and buried it at the bottom of her charm-box, beneath the dried rowan berries and the knotted red threads she sold to lovers and the little glass bells she sold to the frightened, but even through all of it she could feel it — a faint warmth against her hip like a hand resting there, patient, certain she would eventually reach for it. She had not reached for it. She had spent the evening doing ordinary things with exaggerated care: trimming candlewicks, counting her copper, eating cold rice she did not taste. Twelve years of practice at being unremarkable, and tonight every ordinary thing felt like a performance for an audience of one — herself, watching, waiting to see if she would break.

The archivist's face kept surfacing behind her eyes. *Wren Halloway,* she had said, with the particular steadiness of someone who had rehearsed being brave. *The book knows your name, Sable Brightwater.* As if names were invitations. As if speaking one aloud were the same as opening a door.

Sable had taken the coin and fled the archive with her heart hammering like a fist on wood, and she had told herself the whole walk home that it was nothing. A forgery. A collector's piece. A hundred years was long enough for anyone's sigil to end up stamped on anyone's metal. She had told herself that the book — that impossible book, with tonight's date inked in a dead hand beside her true name, the name she had not heard spoken since the night her order died — was a trick of coincidence, or a cruel joke, or a fever dream she would wake from with candle soot on her fingers.

She had almost believed it. She was good at almost.

Then the bells of Corvane began to strike midnight, and the air above the market square split open like a seam.

* * *

She felt it before she heard it — a pressure change, the way the sky feels before a summer storm, except this was the wrong direction. The pressure came *up*, from below and behind the world, as if something on the other side of everything had leaned its weight against the fabric of the night. The candle in her window guttered sideways though there was no wind. The coin in its linen wrappings went from warm to hot.

Sable was out the door before she had decided to be.

The streets of the Tallow Quarter were emptying in the wrong way — not the orderly drain of people going home, but a scattering, a panic without yet a name for itself. Dogs were howling from every rooftop. A man stumbled past her carrying a bolt of cloth he had clearly grabbed in place of his wits, and a woman stood in her doorway holding a child and staring upward with her mouth open, and everyone, everyone, was looking at the same point in the sky.

Sable looked too.

Above the market square — the same square where she had sold charms for twelve years, where she had haggled over copper and laughed at jokes she only half-let herself mean — the darkness had torn. Not like lightning, not like fire. Like a curtain being pulled, or like the skin of a fruit splitting to show the flesh beneath. Through the tear spilled a light that was not light: a green-and-gold luminance, soft as the underside of leaves, and it fell across the cobbles in long slanting shafts, and where it fell, the cobbles *bloomed*.

That was the only word her mind would give her, and it was a terrible word, because it was true. Moss surged up between the flagstones in a velvet tide. Vines uncoiled from the tear itself, thick as her wrist, hung with blossoms that opened as she watched — pale, luminous, impossibly detailed, each petal veined with threads of gold. A fountain that had run dry for a decade filled itself with water so clear it looked like solid glass, and then the water grew lily pads the size of dinner plates, and then the lily pads grew *flowers*, and the flowers began, very softly, to sing.

It was the most beautiful thing Sable had ever seen.

It was going to kill everyone in the square.

She knew it the way she had once known things — the old knowing, the swordspeaker's knowing, the gift she had buried so deep for twelve years that she had almost convinced herself it was dead. But it was not dead. It woke in her like a struck bell, and the sensation was so violent, so *familiar*, that she made a sound she did not recognize as her own voice. The gift poured up through her chest and out along her arms, and for one suspended heartbeat she stood in the falling green light and felt *home* — the word arriving whole and unasked, home, the way a drowning woman might think of a shore — and then the first vine reached the screaming.

It moved faster than a serpent. A flower-seller — old Marta, who bought chamomile from Sable every market day and always overpaid — stood frozen at the edge of the square with her basket still on her arm, and the vine took her around the ankle and *pulled*, and Marta went down, and the blossoms along the vine's length all turned toward her at once like faces, and Sable understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the garden was not malicious. It was hungry. It had been starving on the other side of a failing veil for longer than she had been alive, and it did not know the difference between soil and flesh.

"Marta!"

Sable was running. She had not decided to run. Her body had simply remembered what it was for.

The square was chaos now — people fleeing in every direction, a cart overturned, a watchman blowing his whistle in sharp useless bursts as though the law could arrest a vine. The tear above the square was widening, and through it Sable could see *Virelai* — really see it, the first time in twelve years: terraces of orchards climbing hills that did not exist in any mortal geometry, rivers that ran silver and slow, a sky the color of the inside of a shell. And everywhere, everywhere, the green. Growing, reaching, pouring through the wound in the world like water through a cracked dam.

The vine had Marta by the leg and was drawing her toward the fountain — toward the roots, Sable realized, toward the place where the garden was densest, where a body would be taken apart and *used* with the gentle, total indifference of a forest composting a fallen deer.

Sable's gift ignited.

There was no choosing it. Twelve years of suppression had not extinguished it; it had only pressurized it, and now the pressure blew the doors off. Light ran up her arms — not the green-gold of Virelai but something older, a white-gold like sun through a blade, the light of the oath-bound, the light she had sworn never to show another living soul. She felt it leave her hands as words she had not spoken in twelve years rose to her tongue, words in the swordspeech, the language of binding and of release, and she did not decide to speak them any more than she had decided to run.

"*Sovari en telath,*" she said, and the world obeyed.

The vine *stilled*. Not died — stilled, the way a struck animal stills, the way a river stills in winter. The blossoms turned from Marta to Sable, all of them at once, and she felt them looking at her, felt the whole spilled garden register her presence the way a room registers a struck bell. The binding held for three heartbeats, five, and in that time Sable reached Marta, hauled the old woman free — her ankle was marked with a ring of green bruises, but she was whole, she was alive, she was weeping and saying Sable's name over and over as though it were a prayer — and Sable pushed her toward the street and turned to face the square.

And the square turned to face her.

Dozens of people had stopped running. They stood in the falling green light with their faces upturned, and every face was turned toward her, and she understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with fear of the garden, what they were seeing. A woman in a plain charm-seller's coat, standing in the middle of the impossible, glowing like a lantern, speaking a language that had been outlawed before some of them were born.

"*Sovari en telath, kireth en doma,*" she said, and flung the binding wider — a ring of stillness around the fountain, around the worst of the vines, buying the square time to empty. Her voice did not shake. That was the horror of it. Twelve years of shaking, twelve years of the small tremors of a woman pretending to be small, and now that the gift was loose her voice came out level and clear and *glad*, and she could hear the gladness in it and could not stop it, the way you cannot stop your own heartbeat.

Because it felt like coming home.

That was the truth she had spent twelve years starving. The gift did not feel like a weapon, or a crime, or a curse. It felt like a coat she had outgrown being returned to her, still warm from someone's care. It felt like the first day of a fever breaking. It felt like her grandmother's kitchen, like her mentor's voice saying *again, Sable, from the beginning, and this time mean it* — it felt like every good thing she had amputated to survive, sewn back on and aching with returned blood.

A child was crying somewhere near the fountain. A vine had taken the lily-strewn water and was lifting it, lifting it in a slow glassy rope, and the child — a boy, no more than six, who must have run toward the pretty light the way children run toward everything bright — was caught at the fountain's rim, the water-rope coiling toward him with its singing flowers, and his mother was screaming from beyond the ring of stillness, hammering her hands against the air as though the binding were a wall.

Sable moved. The gift moved with her, ahead of her, and this time she did not speak the words so much as *become* them. The swordspeech rose through her whole body — *kireth, kireth, unbind, release, be free* — and the water-rope shattered into harmless rain, and the vine holding the boy's sleeve loosened and withdrew, and Sable scooped him up and ran him to his mother and put him in her arms, and the woman looked at Sable's glowing hands and did not thank her. She backed away, dragging her son, her eyes wide and white-rimmed, and Sable watched her go and felt the first cold drop of what was coming.

*They are looking at me the way they looked at the oath-bound.*

*The way they looked at us, the night the Purge came.*

* * *

She should have run. She knew it even as she stood there in the square with the light pouring off her hands like water off a ledge. Every survival instinct she had honed for twelve years was screaming it — *run, hide, be no one* — and she stood in the open, in the green-gold rain of Virelai's light, and did not run, because the tear was widening, and because there were still people in the square, and because the gift, having woken, would not be put back down. It moved through her like a second pulse. She could feel the garden's hunger as though it were her own; she could feel the veil's wound as a rawness behind her sternum; she could feel, with terrible precision, exactly which vines were reaching for which fleeing backs, and she spoke the bindings faster than she had ever spoken them, her voice layering over itself, the swordspeech pouring out of her in a river she had dammed for twelve years.

"*Kireth en doma. Sovari. Sovari en telath, kireth en doma—*"

A vine the thickness of her arm came down through the tear itself and took the watchman's whistle from his lips — took the whistle and the hand that held it, or tried to; Sable's binding caught it a finger's width from flesh and the vine strained against her word like a dog against a chain. The watchman stared at his own saved hand, then at her, and his face did something complicated — fear and awe fighting for the same ground — and then he did the bravest or the most foolish thing she had seen all night: he turned from the garden and began hauling people toward the streets, shouting, "*Away from the fountain! Away! Move, move—*"

The square was emptying. Good. Good. Sable planted her feet on the blooming cobbles and widened the binding, and the ring of stillness expanded until it held the whole fountain-court, and she stood at its center like the pin of a wheel, and everything turned around her.

And in the stillness at the heart of the chaos, she heard the garden *notice* her.

It was not a sound. It was a pressure, a turning of attention, vast and slow and vegetal — the attention of a forest, if a forest had spent a century starving behind a thinning wall and had just seen, through the wound it had made, a single figure standing in the light, glowing, speaking the old words. The blossoms all along the vines rotated toward her. The singing flowers changed their song — she could not say how she knew, but she knew — from hunger to something else. Recognition. Longing, maybe. The wordless, aching longing of a thing that has been alone.

And beneath the longing, faint as a name called across a valley, she felt the covenant. What was left of it. A thread, a frayed and dying thread, running from the tear in the sky down through the fountain, down through the roots that must be spreading under Corvane even now, down and down toward something buried deep — and the thread was *attached to her*. Had always been attached to her. Had been waiting, patient as a seed, for twelve years, for the night the book had named, for the woman the book had named.

*Sable Brightwater.* Her true name, in a dead hand, on a living page.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one, to the garden, to the girl she had been. "Oh, no."

The tear above the square pulsed wider. Through it, Virelai's terraced orchards blazed down at her, and she saw — or thought she saw, or the covenant let her see — figures at the far edge of the light. Tall, still, watching. The people of the garden, looking through the wound at the woman standing in the square with light on her hands. One of them raised an arm. Not in threat. In the old gesture, the gesture every oath-bound child learned before they learned to hold a sword: arm up, palm open — *I see you. I answer. Come.*

Sable's gift flared so brightly that the whole square lit like noon, and every remaining shadow fled the walls, and somewhere far off across the city she heard the deep bells of the magistrate's keep begin to ring the alarm — not the fire pattern, not the flood pattern, but the third pattern, the one that had not sounded in twelve years, the one that meant *the old magic. The old magic is loose.*

Her hands were shaking now. The gladness was draining out of the gift's embrace and the terror was pouring in behind it, cold water into warm, because she knew what that bell meant. She had heard it once before, from a hayloft, through a wall, with her mentor's voice still in her ears and his blood still cooling in the yard below. The bell meant the soldiers were coming. The bell meant the magistrate had remembered his lists.

She looked down at her hands. They glowed like the last two candles in a dark cathedral, and they would not stop, and she did not know if she wanted them to.

Twelve years. Twelve years of small trades and smaller lies, of rowan berries and knotted thread, of being no one so thoroughly that she had begun to disappear even to herself. And all of it — all of it — had lasted exactly as long as it took for the sky over her market to tear open and the garden she had been born to serve to come looking for her.

The last of the civilians cleared the square. The watchman was herding the final stragglers down the Tallow Street, and he paused at the corner — just a moment — and looked back at her, and gave her a single nod, soldier to soldier, before he vanished into the dark.

Sable stood alone in the garden's light.

The vines had gone still within her binding, but beyond it they were moving again, slowly, testing, and the tear pulsed overhead like a heartbeat, and the figures in the far light had not lowered the watching arm, and the alarm bells were coming closer — she could hear them now from three directions, ringing and ringing, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the drum of hooves. The magistrate's riders. Coming fast.

She should run. Every year of her hidden life said *run*.

Instead, Sable Brightwater stood in the blooming square, lifted her glowing hands, and — for the first time in twelve years — answered the garden in its own tongue.

"*En telath. I see you. I answer.*"

The light in her hands flared white.

And down the Tallow Street, around the corner, came the riders.
