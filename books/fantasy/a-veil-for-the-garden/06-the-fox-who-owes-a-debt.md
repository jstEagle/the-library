## 6. The Fox Who Owes a Debt

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and beneath both—faint as a memory of a memory—of green things growing.

Sable had noticed it the moment they threw her in. Not the rot of a root gone wrong, not the sourness of mold on damp masonry, but something sweeter: loam after rain, blossom split open, the particular honeyed breath of Virelai's summer air. It had no business existing four levels below the magistrate's keep, in the heart of the man who had burned her world down. She told herself it was her imagination. Twelve years of practicing imagination had made her very good at lying to herself, but not, apparently, good enough.

She sat with her back against the wall and counted the sounds of the keep above: boots on the stairs at irregular intervals, the groan of a gate, water moving through pipes that were older than the city's charter. Dain had questioned her once—only once—and she had given him nothing but the flat truth of a market charm-seller dragged in by mistake. He had looked at her the whole time like a man reading a letter written in a language he half remembered. He knew. Of course he knew. He had signed the order himself; he would know a swordspeaker's face even after twelve years of it hiding behind a stall of copper charms and knotted thread.

*They'll move me tomorrow,* she thought. *Or worse. Or worse first.*

She flexed her fingers and felt the ghost of her gift still humming in them, unbidden, terrifying, alive. In the square it had leapt out of her like a dog off a chain, and gods help her, it had felt *good*. That frightened her more than the cells did. She had spent twelve years building a small life, a careful life, a life shrunk down small enough to hide inside—and tonight it had burned off her like fog off a morning field, and underneath there had been not ruin but *her*, whole and blazing.

The green smell strengthened.

Sable went still. It came from the corner of the cell where the lamplight failed, the corner where shadows pooled thick as ink, and as she watched, the shadow shifted its weight. Not lightened—shifted, the way a cat shifts before deciding whether to bother standing up.

"If you say my name," she said quietly, "I'll scream before you finish the second syllable."

"Then I'll be quick about it." The voice was low, dry, amused in a way that had no real humor in it, like a joke told at a funeral. "Sable Brightwater. There. Now scream, or listen. Your choice, but I've been crouched in this corner for three hours and my knees have opinions."

The shadow stood up, and it was a man—or nearly one. Tall and lean, with rust-colored hair going gray at the temples and eyes the amber-gold of foxfire, pupils drawn vertical as slits in the dark. His coat was layered in earth-toned rags that might have been fine tailoring once, and his ears, when he tipped his head into the thin light, came to soft points.

Sable's heart slammed against her ribs. Not fear—or not only fear. Recognition, deep and unwelcome, of everything she had spent twelve years pretending did not exist.

"You're Virelai," she breathed. "Here. In Dain's own keep."

"Under Dain's own keep, technically, which I find much politer." He produced, from somewhere in those rags, a ring of keys and began testing them against her cell door with the unhurried air of a man who owned the lock. "Tam Vess. Exile, courier, occasional thief of bread and other small comforts. And you, swordspeaker, are the reason I'm risking my excellent knees."

"I'm not a swordspeaker."

"Mm." The third key turned; the lock clicked open with obscene ease. He swung the door wide and bowed, shallow and sardonic. "And I'm a traveling shoe merchant. Shall we lie to each other all the way down the stairs? It's a long flight. We could make an evening of it."

* * *

She should not have gone with him. Every instinct honed over twelve years said so—a stranger who knew what she was, waiting in the dark of the magistrate's own cells, was either a trap or a catastrophe, and neither offered better odds than staying put. But staying put meant dawn, and dawn meant whatever Corvel Dain had planned for the woman whose magic had torn a hole in his sky, and so when Tam Vess pressed a finger to his lips and led her into the dark, she went.

He moved through the underlevels like something born to it, which unsettled her more than anything else he'd done. Twice guards passed within arm's reach, and twice Tam simply stopped, tilted his head, and became—less. Not invisible. Just uninteresting, the way a stack of crates is uninteresting, and the torchlight slid past them both.

"How," she whispered, the second time.

"Practice," he whispered back. "Being overlooked is a skill, and I have been perfecting it for a hundred years."

"A hundred—" She bit the question off as he pulled her through a drain-grate and into a service tunnel where the smell of green things grew suddenly, impossibly strong. Roots had cracked through the tunnel's stone ceiling, pale and thick as her wrist, trailing leaves that had no right to exist without sun. They glowed faintly, the color of foxfire, the color of his eyes.

Tam glanced up at her expression and grinned. "Ah. You've met the keep's little secret."

"The sapling." The words came out flat. "Dain keeps a piece of Virelai caged down here."

"He waters it just enough to keep it dying slowly, yes. Sentimental of him." Tam's grin faded to something older. "Or perhaps just practical. A man who has fed on a miracle doesn't like to think where his meals come from. Come on. This tunnel runs under the market district. Four streets, two grates, and then we can argue properly somewhere with sky."

* * *

The grate opened into an alley behind a tannery, and the stench of the tannery was, oddly, a comfort—the honest filth of the mortal city, no magic in it anywhere. Above, the sky was still wrong. Where the veil had torn over the market square hours ago, the tear had been stitched shut again—not by anything human, Sable was certain, but shut nonetheless—and along its seam hung a faint auroral shimmer, green and gold, like the bruise left after a blow.

Tam stood in the alley mouth and looked up at it for a long moment, and his face did something complicated.

"It shouldn't have closed," he said, mostly to himself. "A tear like that doesn't close on its own. Something on the other side is pushing against the wound, trying to hold the door. Which means the garden is still fighting for you lot, even now, even starving." He turned, and the slit-pupiled eyes caught what little light the shimmer gave. "You felt it, didn't you. When your gift came out. Like coming home."

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Don't tell me what I felt."

"I watched you catch a falling balcony one-handed with a word you didn't mean to say." He leaned against the wall, arms folded. "Three strangers walked away from that square because of you. Half the city saw. The other half will hear about it by breakfast. So no, charm-seller, I won't tell you what you felt. But I'll tell you what everyone else felt, which was a swordspeaker doing what swordspeakers do."

"Everyone else felt fear," Sable said. "That's what my order's death bought this city. Peace. Quiet. They'll hand me back to Dain themselves by noon."

"They might," Tam agreed cheerfully. "People are cowards in crowds. It's why I work alone." He pushed off the wall. "But you're not being handed back yet, because I got you out first, and I intend to be extremely annoying about keeping it that way."

"Why?" The question finally tore loose from her. She grabbed his sleeve—rags, but underneath, cloth woven so finely it felt like water—and made him look at her. "Why break into the magistrate's keep for a stranger? Why risk the veil-tear, the guards, all of it, for me?"

"Because Maren Brightwater saved my life," Tam said, "and debts don't die with the debtor. They pass down. Blood to blood. Yours is the blood."

The name hit her like cold water thrown in the face. Sable let go of his sleeve as if it had burned her.

"My grandmother is dead," she said. "Twenty years dead."

"Yes." For the first time, all the performance dropped out of him—the banter, the sardonic bow, the cheerful cruelty of it. What was left underneath was simply tired, and old, and grieving in a way that recognized its own kind. "Yes, she is. I was there for some of it. I'm sorry. She spoke of you, near the end. The granddaughter who tied knots too tight and laughed at her own jokes. She said if the world ever came calling for you, someone ought to come calling for you first." His mouth twisted. "The world came calling early, though. Faster than I expected. So here I am, late and rude, in an alley that smells like a tannery's armpit. My grandmother would be ashamed of my manners."

Sable turned away from him. She needed, suddenly, not to be looked at.

Her grandmother. Twenty years buried under a headstone with a false name, in a mortal cemetery, because even in death Sable hadn't dared leave any trail leading back to Virelai. Maren Brightwater, who had hummed songs in a language the neighbors thought was nonsense. Maren, who had taught her knotwork and herb-craft and never once, not ever, spoken of the garden—and now a fox-eared stranger stood in the dark claiming a debt Sable had never known existed.

"What did she save you from?" Sable asked the wall.

"That's a long story."

"I've got all night. Possibly less."

Tam laughed—a short, surprised bark, as if she'd startled him into honesty. "Fair. All right. Short version: I ran a message across the border during the last good year, carrying something I shouldn't have been curious about, and curiosity got me caught in the Purge's first wave. Your grandmother found me in a cellar with three Crossed men hunting me for bounty, and she stood between us—an old mortal woman with a walking stick—and she talked them out of it. Talked, mind. Didn't raise a finger of power. Convinced them I was a fever dream and they were drunk." He shook his head, remembering. "Then she took me home and fed me stew and didn't ask my name until the third day. That's how she did things. Safety first, introductions later."

"She never mentioned you."

"She wouldn't. Mentioning me would have been a thread someone could pull." He tapped his own chest. "We understood each other, she and I. Two creatures hiding in plain sight."

* * *

They moved again before dawn, Tam leading her down through the undercity in a route that made no sense and probably made perfect sense to him—through a wine cellar, along a flooded culvert, up through the floor of a shuttered granary where pigeons muttered on the beams. Somewhere in the third hour of walking, Sable noticed he favored his right side, and in the fourth hour she stopped pretending not to notice.

"You're hurt."

"I'm fascinating," Tam said. "But go on."

She had him stop under the granary's high dusty window, where the first gray light came down like a curtain, and she made him pull his shirt aside. And then she forgot how to breathe.

Below his left collarbone, seated in the flesh itself, was a shard of something that was not quite stone. It was the size of a coin and the color of dawn on snow, and it pulsed—slowly, steadily, like a second heart—with light the same foxfire gold as his eyes. Around it, the skin had grown closed in ridged whorls, like bark healing over a nail. Like a tree growing around a thing it could not reject and could not release.

"That's—" she started.

"An oath-stone shard. First of the three. Yes." He said it lightly, the way a man says *just a flesh wound*, and the lightness fooled neither of them. "It found me eleven years ago. Or I found it—I honestly don't remember which way around anymore. I woke up one morning with it under my skin and a certainty in my gut that I was meant to carry it somewhere, and I have spent eleven years failing to figure out where. It won't come out. I've tried. Others have tried, and they screamed and apologized, so we stopped that game." He looked at her sidelong. "But tonight, when the veil tore, it sang. Loudly. Loud enough that every exile in the city heard it, and most of us ran the other way, because singing stones historically precede terrible things." His grin came back, crooked and tired. "I went toward it instead. Found you in a cell. Imagine my luck."

Sable reached toward it and stopped, her fingers hovering over the whorled skin. Even at that distance she felt it—the hum of it, familiar as her own pulse, the same frequency her gift sang at when it slipped its leash. An oath-stone. A piece of the covenant that had bound the two worlds together, that had fed Corvane's fields with Virelai's seasons, shattered and scattered since the night her order died.

"Your grandmother knew about the stones," Tam said quietly. "Before she died. She told me if I ever carried one, I should find her blood. I thought she was being poetic. Old women are allowed." He straightened his shirt, covering it, though the glow bled through the cloth anyway. "Turns out she was giving me instructions."

"And the other two stones?"

"One's rumored to be past the border, in garden ground." He shrugged elaborately, but his eyes didn't match the shrug. "The third, nobody living knows. But I know someone who knows someone, and the knowing starts with getting you out of Corvane before Dain finds you, which brings us to the unpleasant portion of the evening."

"Which is?"

"I need you to trust me. Fully. Quickly. By, ideally, sunrise." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I know. I'm a shapeshifter from a realm your city fears, I broke into your cells, and I haven't shown you a single thing worth trusting except a dead woman's name. So here's the last thing I have." He reached into his coat and drew out a small wooden token, worn smooth by decades of handling, strung on a leather cord. Carved into it, in lines so fine she had to hold it to the window-light: a knot. Her knot. The specific, obsessive over-under weave her grandmother had taught her when she was seven, the one she'd used on every charm she'd sold for twelve years, thinking it was only habit.

Sable's hands shook. She did not take the token. Taking it felt like permission to believe, and believing had cost her everything once already.

"She said you'd do that," Tam observed. "Refuse to hold it. She said, *the girl will want proof but be afraid of wanting it, so make her take it anyway.*"

"Stop talking like her."

"Gladly. It's unsettling." He pressed the cord into her palm and folded her fingers over it, and the carved wood was warm, warm as a living hand, and Sable closed her eyes and grieved for twenty minutes' worth of tears in the space of three seconds and did not shed one drop of them. Then she opened her eyes.

"The debt transfers," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Fine. Then I'm the creditor now. So I'm setting terms. You tell me the truth when I ask. No performances, no dodges. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Tam said, and for once there was no wit riding behind the word.

"Then tell me why the veil tore tonight. Tell me what's happening to it. Because the book they say started all this named tonight a century ago, and the tear stitched itself shut like something on the other side was *fighting* for us, and I need to know what I've woken up in the middle of."

Tam looked at her for a long moment, and the foxfire eyes were grave.

"Then we walk," he said, "because the answer takes the length of the undercity to tell properly, and it ends with a confession you're not going to want to hear." He turned toward the dark mouth of the stairwell leading down, and paused there, silhouetted against the gray window-light, ears sharp, shard glowing through his shirt like a coal.

"But I'll give you the shape of it now, free of charge: the covenant is dying, the garden is bleeding, and somewhere in this city there's a man who signed an order twelve years ago and has been paying for it ever since—same as you. The stones have to be rejoined, the oath has to be resworn, and there's exactly one person left alive who can speak it." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "So run or fight, swordspeaker. Either way, the silence is over."

He stepped down into the dark. After a moment, the token warm in her fist and grief sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, Sable Brightwater followed him.
