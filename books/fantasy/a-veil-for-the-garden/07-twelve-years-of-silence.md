## 7. Twelve Years of Silence

The undercity of Corvane breathed like something old and half-asleep. Water moved somewhere below them — always water, always the sound of the city's hidden pulse — and the lamplighters' tunnels gave way to older passages still, brick sweating with moss that had no business growing this far from any garden.

Sable ran until her lungs burned and then kept running, because Tam Vess ran like a creature who had never once believed in stopping. His shape flickered at the edges when he thought she wasn't looking: a lean man one moment, a red-furred shadow the next, as though his body couldn't decide what it wanted to be and had stopped apologizing for it.

"Left," he said, without turning. "The right passage floods at this hour."

She took the left. The stone underfoot was slick and cold through her boots, and above them — impossibly far above them now — Corvane's bells were still ringing the alarm Dain's men had set loose across the city. *The swordspeaker is found. The swordspeaker is found.* Twelve years of careful smallness, twelve years of copper coins and harmless charms and lies so small they barely counted as lies, and it had taken the sky opening over the night market less than an hour to undo all of it.

Her hands were still glowing. Faintly, stubbornly, gold light seeped up between her knuckles like blood from a wound that wouldn't close, no matter how tightly she clenched her fists.

"Stop doing that," Tam said, glancing back. "You're visible."

"I'm not doing anything." Her voice came out ragged. "That's rather the point."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in a kinder world. "Virelai folk have a saying. A river dammed doesn't disappear. It just gets louder about finding the sea."

"I don't want your sayings."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You want my shoulder, which happens to be carrying the only thing in either world that can fix tonight. So I'll say my sayings where I like."

* * *

They sheltered at dawn in what had once been a shrine — Sable could tell by the alcove worn smooth at knee height, by the soot-shadow on the wall where an icon had been pried out and burned. Someone had lived here since; there was a rusted brazier and a nest of blankets gone gray with dust. Tam lit nothing. He sat instead in the dark with his back against the wall, and Sable saw, in the thin light leaking through a grate overhead, the thing embedded in his shoulder.

It looked wrong there — a splinter of pale stone the size of a thumbnail, sunk into the flesh just below his collarbone, and around it his skin had gone faintly greenish, veined like a leaf. It caught whatever light existed and held it, the way frost holds the moon.

"That's the first stone," she said. Not a question. She'd seen oath-stones whole, once, in another life.

"A piece of one." Tam didn't look down at it. He looked at her instead, and his eyes were amber and too old for his face, whichever face he chose. "It went into me eleven years ago, the night the last courier from Virelai died in a ditch outside this city. She pressed it into my hands and told me to carry it to the swordspeaker." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "You can imagine my difficulty. There wasn't one. There was only a charm-seller who flinched whenever anyone said the word *oath* too loudly."

Sable turned away. The alcove wall was cool against her forehead. "I don't know who you think I am."

"I know exactly who you are, Sable Brightwater." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about him — everything arrived quiet, pre-sharpened. "Maren Brightwater's student. Last of the Swordspeakers of the Veil. And the woman whose name is written in a book a hundred years old beside tonight's date, which means that whether or not you know who you are, everyone else is about to find out."

"You don't understand what you're asking."

"Don't I?" For the first time something moved under the calm — grief, maybe, or its long acquaintance. "My people are dying, swordspeaker. Slowly, ugly, season by season. The gardens are going gray from the borders inward. Children born this year in Virelai open their eyes and see fog where the orchards should be. Your city starves a little more each winter and calls it bad luck. And the only thing standing between both worlds and the dark is—" he tapped his own collarbone, once, above the splinter, "—this, and two other pieces like it, and someone willing to speak words that haven't been spoken in twelve years."

"So find another speaker."

"There are no others. That's rather what *last* means."

Silence filled the shrine then, thick as the water-sound below. Sable slid down the wall until she was sitting, arms wrapped around her knees, and stared at her own hands. The glow had faded to almost nothing. Almost. It never quite went out. That was the joke of it — twelve years of suppression and small trades, and the light had simply waited, patient as a seed under snow.

"You don't know why I stopped," she said finally. It came out smaller than she intended.

Tam tilted his head. "No. But I've been wondering since the moment I saw you."

* * *

Twelve years ago there had been rain, and fire that would not go out in the rain, and Maren's voice saying *run* in a tone Sable had never heard from her before or after — not the gentle command of a teacher, but the flat final word of a woman spending her last coin.

Sable closed her eyes and the shrine fell away.

* * *

The sanctuary beneath the Bell Tower of Saint Aldric's had been built for exactly this night, though everyone had hoped it never come. Thirty-one of them crowded into the old training vaults while boots hammered the floors above — thirty-one swordspeakers and their students, and the stones, and the books, and Maren standing at the head of the stairs with her grey braid coming loose and her sword drawn, though everyone knew steel meant nothing against the Purge.

"They're not here for us to fight," Maren had said, and her voice was steady in the way that cost everything. "Dain's soldiers are boys with orders and fear. If we cut through them, we prove every lie the magistrate has told about us. We die as speakers, or we stop being speakers. Those are the choices."

"And if we stop being speakers?" Sable had asked. Twenty-three years old, her own sword still bright, her gift still a warm constant hum in her chest like a second heartbeat. "If we swear silence — hide the gift — survive?"

Maren had looked at her for a long moment. Rain found its way through some crack far above and traced silver down the vaulted stone.

"Then we live," Maren said, "and we learn what living costs. Go, child. Take the east passage. I will hold the words here."

"The words—" Sable had understood then, with the cold arriving like floodwater, what Maren intended. The Oath of the Veil was not a prayer. Spoken whole, by a speaker of strength, at the threshold of the realms, it could seal the door — buy years, decades, time enough for the covenant to be reforged properly someday. It could also burn the speaker out like a lamp burning oil. Every speaker knew it. No speaker in four generations had needed to do it.

"Maren, no—"

"Someone must stay and speak, or the veil tears tonight and everyone dies — us, the soldiers, the city, the garden. All of it." Maren cupped Sable's face in both hands, and her palms smelled of rain and iron. "And someone must leave, so that someday the words can be spoken again by someone young enough to finish what I begin. You swore your silence to me an hour ago. Keep it now. Even if you hear me calling. Especially then."

Above them the doors burst. Light — wrong light, torchlight and something else, something hungry that the magistrate's men had brought with them, some counter-charm bought from a hedge-witch turned informer — poured down the stairwell.

Maren turned to face it. She planted her feet the way she'd taught Sable to plant feet, and she began to speak.

Sable ran.

She ran down the east passage with the oath ringing off the stones behind her, line after line, Maren's voice rising and rising, beautiful and terrible, and Sable did not stop, did not turn, did not answer even when the voice began to fray — and it frayed quickly, faster than it should have, because speaking the whole oath alone was death and Maren had known it and spent herself anyway, syllable by syllable, buying the veil one more year with the last of her life.

The passage ended in the storm-drains. The voice behind her ended too. In between those two endings, Sable swore her silence again — alone this time, to no one, in the dark, with water in her boots and ash in her hair — a vow not of loyalty but of cowardice dressed up as obedience: *I will never speak it. I will never be what she spent herself to save. Better no speaker than a failed one.*

She surfaced outside the walls before dawn. The bell tower burned behind her. The veil held — barely, badly, one more year purchased at a price no ledger could carry.

And Sable Brightwater walked into the world and became small.

* * *

"—and you never spoke again." Tam's voice pulled her back. The shrine. The grate-light, higher now, grey-gold. Her face wet. When had her face gotten wet?

"Not once. Not a word of the oath. I barely let myself think it." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, hollow as the burned tower. "Do you know what it's like, holding a language inside you and refusing to speak it? It's like holding your breath for twelve years. Everything I sold in that market was a way of exhaling just enough to keep from dying. Little charms. Small warmth. Nothing that would matter. Nothing that would *count*."

Tam was quiet a long time. When he spoke, the mockery was gone from him entirely, and what remained was gentler than she had thought him capable of.

"My grandmother was a hedge-speaker on Virelai's border," he said. "Small magic. Weather-sense, mostly. When the covenant started failing, she told me something I didn't understand until tonight." He shifted; the splinter in his shoulder pulsed faintly green, like a slow heart. "An oath isn't a spell, swordspeaker. Spells anyone can cast. An oath is a *door*, and doors only open for people who are honest about which side of them they're standing on. The covenant of Virelai was forged by three speakers who each admitted, aloud, the worst true thing about themselves, and built the bridge on top of it. That's why it lasted a century. And that's why it can't be reforged by a woman pretending the last twelve years didn't happen."

"The stones don't need my confession. They need joining. At the root. Wren's book says—"

"Wren's book says what the book wants said." Tam leaned forward. "But I carried this splinter for eleven years, and it has taught me things a book wouldn't dare. Stone remembers. This one sings at night, did you know that? Old words. Oath-words. And every time it reaches the part where the speaker names herself—" his eyes held hers, amber and unflinching, "—it stops. Waits. As if listening for an answer that hasn't been given yet."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "What answer?"

"You know what answer." He said it softly, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse. "Why did you stop, Sable? Not *what happened*. You've just shown me what happened. Why did you spend twelve years punishing yourself for surviving? What did you decide about yourself in that drain tunnel that you've never once said out loud?"

"I decided—" The words jammed. Twelve years of silence stood behind them, a wall she had mortared brick by brick with small trades and smaller lies. "I decided that running was all I was. That Maren died for a coward, and the best thing a coward can do is stay quiet and small and harmless, and hope no one ever asks her to be otherwise."

There. It was out. It hung in the dusty air of the dead shrine, and it was the first time in twelve years she had said any part of it aloud, and it felt less like relief than like the first crack in a dam — a single thin seam of light through black stone, with the whole weight of the water leaning against it.

Tam exhaled slowly. Around him, for half a heartbeat, his shape rippled — fox, man, fox — like a candle flame steadying.

"There it is," he said quietly. "That's the first true sentence you've spoken since I met you. Feel it?"

She did. It terrified her how much she did — the way the words had torn something open, and the way light was already pressing at the tear from the other side.

"The stones will want the rest," Tam said. "All of it. At the root, in front of the covenant itself. It won't accept a perfect knight, Sable. It never wanted one. It wants a survivor who tells the truth." He rose, rolling his shoulder; the splinter flared green, then dimmed, like something taking note. "Can you walk?"

"Where?"

"East. Under the wall, through the drowned quarter, out past the leper fields." He glanced at the grate, at the widening grey light. "Into Virelai's borderlands. There's an orchard there where trees grow around the names of the dead. Your mentor's tree is in it. I've seen it." A pause, and something almost kind crossed his shifting face. "I think it's been waiting for you."

Outside, far above, the bells of Corvane had begun again — but differently now, a pattern Sable hadn't heard since childhood, since before the Purge, since the last time the city had believed the veil was failing.

Tam cocked his head, listening. "They're ringing the old warning," he said. "Midnight bells. They remember more than they think, these mortals." He looked back at her, and extended a hand. "Come on, swordspeaker. Midnight's coming. And this time, when the words stall in your throat — and they will — you'll have to decide what you're going to do about it."

Sable looked at his hand a long moment. Then she took it, and the glow in her knuckles answered, steady and gold, like a held breath finally beginning to release.
