## 10. Dain's Bargain

The cell they gave the archivist was the nicest one in Corvane's keep, and that was not an accident.

Corvel Dain had learned long ago that fear made people stupid and comfort made them honest. A prisoner beaten bloody would tell you anything — would invent conspiracies, confess to crimes she hadn't committed, swear loyalty to gods she didn't believe in, all to make the pain stop. But a prisoner given a warm blanket, a lamp with oil enough to read by, and a chair that didn't wobble? That prisoner would sit in the quiet and think about what she actually wanted. And what people wanted was always simpler than what they feared.

So Wren Halloway's cell had a desk. It had a window, barred, but a real window, high on the wall, where morning light came down in a slanted column full of dust. It had three books from the confiscated archive, chosen personally by Dain: histories, none of them heretical, or at least none obviously so. And it had been three days now, which meant the archivist had had time to count the bars, memorize the guard rotations, decide the window was too narrow, and arrive at last at the conclusion Dain wanted her to arrive at.

*She can't get out. So she'll want something else.*

He came down himself, without escort, carrying two cups of tea on a wooden tray like a man visiting a neighbor rather than an interrogation. The guards at the stair door looked at each other when he passed; he pretended not to see. Magistrates who needed soldiers to hold their conversations were magistrates who had already lost them.

Wren Halloway sat at the desk with her back straight and her hands folded, as if she'd been expecting him for hours. She was younger than he'd expected when his men first dragged her out of the archive — thirty, perhaps, with ink stains up both forearms and the particular pallor of someone who had spent her whole life indoors arguing with dead authors. Her eyes were quick, though. Quick eyes were either a blessing or a curse, depending on who owned them.

"Magistrate." She nodded at the tray. "Is that poisoned?"

"No."

"Pity. It would have been a tidy ending. Very archival." She took the cup anyway, wrapped both hands around it, and drank. Whatever she thought of the tea, she kept off her face. "Three days," she said. "You're late. I'd worked out your whole speech by yesterday afternoon."

Dain set his own cup on the desk and sat down across from her, unhurried. "Tell me my speech, then. Save us both the time."

"You open with something courteous but faintly threatening. Then you ask me about the book — the prophecy book, the one your men didn't find, because I'm cleverer than your men, no offense intended. When I don't answer, you threaten me properly. Then—" she sipped, "—you offer me a bargain, because you've read my file and you know I have no family in Corvane, no money worth stealing, and no friends powerful enough to miss me. Which means the only thing I value is access. To books. To records. To the archive you've spent three days cataloguing for confiscation." She looked up at him over the rim of her cup. "Am I close?"

Dain studied her for a long moment. Somewhere above them, water dripped through old stone, patient as a metronome.

"You missed one part," he said.

"Oh?"

"The part where I tell you why."

Her quick eyes flickered — just once, just slightly — and he knew he had her. Archivists could resist threats; they lived their whole lives being threatened by deadlines and disapproving deans. What they couldn't resist was information. A fact they didn't have yet sat in their chests like an itch, and they would scratch and scratch until there was nothing left of themselves.

He leaned back in the chair. He did not intend to enjoy this. He noted, distantly, that he enjoyed it anyway, and filed that away with all the other small dishonesties he permitted himself before breakfast.

"The book you stole," he said. "The prophecy. You've read it. You know what it says about tonight."

"I know what it *claims*—"

"I know what it says." His voice came out flatter than he meant it to, and he let it. Flatness was a tool like any other. "The veil between this city and Virelai thins to nothing. The garden bleeds through. The covenant — broken these twelve years — either reforges itself through some oath-bound hand, or tears wide and takes half of Corvane with it. You read those words in a century-old manuscript and thought: *superstition*. Old ink scaring old women." He turned his cup slowly on the desk. "I read those words and thought: *finally, someone wrote it down*."

Silence. The drip of water. Wren Halloway had gone very still, the way scholars went still when a source contradicted everything they believed — not frightened yet, but listening with their whole spine.

"You know what the covenant was?" Dain continued. "Officially, in the histories your college teaches? A blessing. Virelai fed our fields its endless summer; we gave the garden our dead to plant in names. Two worlds trading like neighbors over a fence. Lovely. I grew up on that version. My mother told it as a bedtime story." He smiled, and felt his own mouth do it wrong. "Here's the version they don't teach. Every gift has a price, Mistress Halloway, and the covenant never told anyone what the price was. It just collected. Quietly. Year after year. A harvest here. A child born sick there. A winter that came early and stayed late, in one street, in one house, while the next street over had roses in December. Nobody could prove anything. Nobody could even *ask*, because asking the garden a question required magic, and magic belonged to the swordspeakers, and the swordspeakers answered to Virelai, not to us."

"You're saying the covenant was a debt," Wren said slowly.

"I'm saying every bargain struck with something that doesn't die is a debt," Dain said, "and debts are always collected by whoever holds the ledger. We never saw the ledger. We never agreed to the terms. Our grandfathers' grandfathers shook hands with the garden, and we've been paying interest ever since, in seasons and in blood, and calling it *blessing* because we were never shown the bill." He leaned forward. "Twelve years ago, the Purge ended that. Not because I hate beauty, Mistress Halloway. Because I hate accounts I can't audit."

Something moved behind her face — not agreement, he could see that much, but recognition. The look of a woman who had spent her life among records finding, at last, a record-shaped hole in the world.

"And your family?" she asked quietly.

Ah. There it was. She'd found it faster than most — the crack in him, the seam where the magistrate ended and the man began. He considered lying. He considered silence. Both were tools too, and both were heavier than they used to be.

"My wife," he said, "was named Elira. She was a dyer — cloth, beautiful work, blues the color of deep water. You may have seen a bolt of it somewhere; her workshop sold as far as the coast. She caught a fever the autumn I turned twenty-six. Ordinary fever. Half the city had it. The healers bled her, packed her in ice, prayed to whatever saints handle fevers." He kept his voice level, the way you keep a boat level in weather you cannot control. "There was a swordspeaker then — a woman named Maren Vale, third seat of the order, healer-trained. She could have drawn the fever out through the skin like drawing a splinter. I'd seen her do it. I begged her. I offered her everything I had, which wasn't much, and then everything I *would* have, which was more. She put her hand on my shoulder and told me the covenant's allowance for mortal healing had run short that season. *Run short.* As if the garden were a granary and my wife's life a sack of grain already promised elsewhere." He looked at the barred window, at the column of light full of drifting dust. "She died on the ninth day. The roses on our street bloomed that same week. Out of season. Everyone remarked on how lovely it was."

Wren Halloway had stopped drinking her tea. Her hands were white around the cup.

"So when the order fell," Dain said, "when the Purge came and the swordspeakers burned or scattered — I signed the order, yes, I was young and ambitious and I told myself it was justice. And some nights I still believe that. Other nights I wonder if I simply wanted the ledger to burn so no one could ever again show me a page where my wife's death was written down as *expenditure*." He stood, crossed to the window, and looked up through the bars at the thin slice of sky. "Either way, the arithmetic hasn't changed. Magic is a creditor. Virelai is a creditor. And tonight, according to your stolen book, the creditor comes to collect the principal. I will not be here when it does. Neither will this city, if I have anything to say about it."

"And what *do* you have to say about it?" she asked. "You can't burn the veil, Magistrate. It isn't paper."

"No. But it can be starved." He turned back to face her. "Every thread of the covenant runs through living anchors — roots, stones, speakers. Cut the anchors, and the veil frays on its own. Frayed enough, and Virelai closes like a wound closing. The garden seals itself off, and Corvane stands alone. Whole. Unindebted. Cold, maybe. Hungry, in bad years. But ours." He let the word sit in the room. "*Ours.* No one has ever governed this city that wasn't paying rent to another world. I mean to be the first."

"That's madness," she said — but softly, the way people say things they are checking against other things they know. "If the book is right, severing the veil won't seal it. It will *shatter* it. You won't get a clean break, you'll get a flood."

"If the book is right." He returned to the desk and sat, folding his hands. "Which brings me, at last, to my speech. The part you didn't predict."

She raised her eyebrows.

"You're going to help me find Sable Brightwater."

The name landed exactly as he'd known it would. Wren Halloway's face did something complicated and fast — surprise, denial, guilt arriving in that order like carts in a train — and he watched her decide, in the space of a breath, whether to pretend she didn't know it. She decided against pretending. He respected that. It saved time.

"I don't know who you mean," she said anyway, because archivists documented even their lies.

"The charm-seller in the night market. The one who paid you a visit the night your archive burned. Twelve years she's hidden in my city, quiet as dust, and I let her lie — do you understand that? I *let* her. A swordspeaker walking free under my governance, and I chose not to see her, because seeing her meant choosing, and choosing meant the Purge all over again." His jaw tightened. "But the book has her true name in it, Mistress Halloway. Inked a century before her birth. Tonight's date beside it. Whatever she believes she is — a shopkeeper, a coward, a survivor — the covenant has decided otherwise, and the covenant does not negotiate. If she goes to Virelai, if she gathers what the book says she'll gather, she becomes the anchor. The bridge. The thing the garden pours itself through into our world, forever, and every debt comes due through her, forever, and we are right back where we started except this time the ledger has her name on every page."

"You could just kill her," Wren said. Her voice was careful. "You killed the rest of her order."

"I could," Dain agreed. "And the covenant would simply choose another. It always chooses another. No — I don't need her dead. I need her *unfound*. I need her to fail to reach the stones, fail to reach the root, fail to matter, until the night passes and the prophecy drowns in its own wrongness. And for that, I need someone she trusts." He slid a folded paper across the desk. On it, in his own hand, was a single line: terms of release, charges withdrawn, archive privileges restored, effective upon completion. "Someone she trusts, who knows the archives better than any living soul, and who owes me nothing — which means she'll never suspect you of serving me. She'll suspect everyone else first. That's worth more to me than a regiment."

Wren looked at the paper for a long time. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it.

"You want me to betray the woman whose arrest I watched you order three days ago. The woman your men dragged out of my archive in chains." She set down the cup. "Why would I ever?"

"Because of what happens at midnight if you don't." He said it gently, and hated how easily gentleness came to him when it was useful. "Because if the veil tears over the market square tonight — and every sign I have says it will — hundreds of people die in the first hour. People like my Elira. Dyers and dockhands and children who never signed any covenant and never saw any ledger. They'll pay for a bargain their ancestors made, and you'll have helped them pay it, because you were too principled to spend one woman's trust to stop it." He stood. "Or — you walk out of here a free woman, you find Sable Brightwater, and you warn her. Loudly. Honestly. Tell her everything I've told you. Let her make the choice with the whole truth in front of her instead of a century-old book whispering her name. If she still chooses the garden after that — well." He shrugged. "Then at least she chose. That's more than the covenant ever gave anyone."

It was a good argument. He knew it was a good argument because parts of it were even true.

Wren Halloway stared at the paper. He watched her weigh it — freedom against principle, one woman's trust against a square full of strangers — and he watched the exact moment the scale tipped, though her face barely moved. Scholars always broke the same way. Not at the threat. At the arithmetic.

"And if I say no?" she asked.

"Then you stay here, comfortably, until midnight. And afterward, if there is an afterward, I charge you with sedition and the story ends the way stories end in cells." He picked up the tea tray. "I'd rather not. You're the best reader I've ever interrogated."

She almost smiled at that. Almost. Then she reached out, took the paper, and read it twice, front to back, the way she probably read everything — hunting for the clause hidden in the margins, the debt folded inside the gift.

"There's no clause," she said finally, looking up. "No condition buried in the wording. You've left yourself nothing to enforce."

"I don't enforce, Mistress Halloway. I *remember*." He carried the tray toward the door and knocked once for the guards. "People who cross me tend to discover that I remember everything. It's the only magic I've ever needed, and unlike the garden's kind, it's free."

Behind him, he heard the key turn in the lock, and the soft scrape of the chair as she stood.

"One thing," she said, as the door opened. He paused without turning. "Your wife. The fever. You blame the covenant — the ledger, the allowance, the garden's arithmetic. But the woman who refused you was Maren Vale. A person. Who made a choice." Her voice was quiet and terribly steady. "Sable Brightwater was fourteen years old when your Purge came for her. Whatever she costs you, Magistrate, remember that you taught this city to see people as line items first. Don't be surprised when the ledger starts reading you back."

For a moment the corridor was very silent. The guards shifted, uncertain, glancing at their magistrate's back as though checking whether the stone had cracked.

"Midnight is in nine hours," Dain said. "Go home. Sleep if you can. Then start looking."

He walked up the stairs into the grey light of the keep's upper hall, and it was only when he reached his study — only when he had closed the door and poured himself the drink he allowed himself at odd hours and no others — that he admitted what had been sitting in his chest since the archivist said it.

*Maren Vale made a choice.*

He had spent twelve years telling the story with the choice removed. The covenant refused; the covenant killed; the covenant was a machine, and machines could be smashed. It was cleaner that way. It had to be cleaner that way, because the alternative was that a woman with kind eyes and healing hands had looked at a dying dyer's husband and weighed him against a ledger and found him light — and that such women walked the earth wearing the faces of saints, and that smashing their order had not smashed the arithmetic at all, only scattered the accountants.

Outside the window, far to the east, past the rooftops and the walls, the sky over the old forest road had begun to change color. Not sunset. Sunset was hours away. This was a greenness gathering at the horizon like a bruise coming up, soft and slow and utterly wrong, and as Dain stood with his untouched drink and watched it deepen, somewhere beneath his feet — beneath the cells, beneath the foundations, beneath the oldest stones of the keep — he felt, or imagined he felt, a faint pulse. Patient. Rhythmic.

Like a root, growing toward something.

Nine hours, he thought, and set down the glass without drinking.

Somewhere in his city, a charm-seller was lighting her stall lamps for the night market, and the horizon was turning the color of Elira's blues, and the ledger was coming due.
