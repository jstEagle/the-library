## 11. The City That Forgot

The road back to Corvane smelled wrong.

Sable had noticed it first at the border farms, where the fields should have been heavy with late-summer wheat. Instead the stalks stood pale and thin, heads empty, rattling in a wind that carried no green scent at all. A farmer watched them pass from his fence line with the hollow look of a man doing arithmetic he already knew would not come out in his favor. Tam, walking beside her with the oath-stone shard still lodged beneath his shoulder blade like a splinter of frozen light, said nothing for three miles. When he finally spoke, it was only: "It's worse than I thought."

"It's what?" Sable had asked.

"The withheld seasons." He gestured at the fields with his bandaged hand. "Virelai isn't just dying, Brightwater. It's starving. And when a realm starves, it pulls its breath back inside itself. The rain that should have fallen here in spring fell there instead — or nowhere. The warmth that should have ripened your city's gardens got spent keeping our borders from crumbling into nothing." His fox-bright eyes were unusually grim. "Your people think magic abandoned them. Magic didn't abandon anyone. It ran out."

Now, standing on the ridge where the road bent down toward Corvane's smoke-stained skyline, Sable understood what he meant, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had spent twelve years believing she was hiding from the city. She had told herself that Corvane was the enemy — the place of the Purge, the place of Dain's signature, the place where speaking one true word aloud could get her burned. But looking down at it now, at the haze hanging over districts that should have been clear in the evening air, at the long queues already forming outside the granary gates though it was hours before distribution opened, she saw something else.

Corvane wasn't her jailer. Corvane was starving too, and didn't even know why.

* * *

They came in through the river gate at dusk, when the toll collectors were drowsy and the lamplighters hadn't yet made their rounds. Sable wore her market cloak and her market face — the soft, unremarkable expression of a charm-seller named Sabine who wanted nothing and knew nothing. It fit worse than it used to. The orchard of names was still on her skin somehow, under it; Warden Ilex's vow sat in her chest like a second heartbeat, patient and enormous. She was carrying an unfinished promise to tend a grove of the dead, and every lie she told felt thinner than it had a month ago.

Corvane's streets confirmed what the farms had begun.

The night market was still there — her market, her corner by the tanner's awning — but it was diminished in a way that had nothing to do with guards or curfews. Fewer stalls. Fewer voices. The fishmonger who used to sing had gone quiet. The spice merchant's bins held more chaff than saffron. And everywhere, threaded through the crowd like a smell, was the particular silence of people conserving energy for grief.

She stopped at Old Marta's soup stall out of habit, and because habit was the last luxury she trusted.

"Two bowls," she said, and set down coin enough for four.

Marta ladled the broth without looking up. It was thinner than Sable remembered. Watered, stretched, eked. "You've been away," the old woman said. Not a question. In twelve years of buying charms and soup, Sable had learned that Marta missed nothing.

"A while."

"Mm." Marta pushed the bowls across the counter and finally raised her eyes. They were red-rimmed. "My grandson's been coughing since spring. Won't clear. The physician wants two silver a visit now — two silver, for listening to a boy breathe." Her jaw tightened. "Used to be, you'd go to the hedge-women. Used to be there was a salve that worked, a word that worked, something." She caught herself, glanced around, lowered her voice to almost nothing. "I know what they say happened to the ones who did that work. I'm not asking. I'm just saying I remember."

Sable's hands went cold around the bowl.

"I remember too," Marta went on, staring into the middle distance as if reciting a list she kept folded behind her eyes. "My mother took me to a swordspeaker once, when my lungs were bad. A woman with grey in her braid. She put her hand flat on my chest and it was like summer coming through a window." She shook herself, ashamed. "Anyway. Soup's two coppers. Don't let me keep you."

Sable paid her six and walked away before her face could betray anything.

Grey in the braid. Summer through a window.

That had been Maren. That had been her mentor's hands on a child's ribs, twenty-five years ago, in a city that still let such things happen in daylight.

* * *

Tam found her an hour later on the steps of the old counting-house, where she'd gone to sit with the first bowl untouched and the second cooling in her hands.

"You gave that woman enough coin for a week of soup," he said, dropping beside her. "And she gave you a ghost story about a woman with grey in her braid. You've been wearing that expression ever since. It's very dramatic. Very tragic-heroine. If you're waiting for me to be impressed—"

"Tam."

"—then I am, a little, but I'd never admit—"

"**Tam.**"

He stopped. Looked at her properly.

"They're not angry," Sable said. Her voice came out strange, quiet, scraped. "I always thought — after the Purge, after everything — I thought if they ever learned what I was, they'd give me to the magistrates themselves. That's why I hid. That's why hiding made sense. But they're not angry. They're *hungry*. They remember, Tam. Under all of it — the laws, the fear, the burnings — they remember being healed. Being fed. Having seasons that arrived on time." She looked out at the street, where a queue had formed outside the chandler's for tallow ends, tallow ends, the scraps of light. "The covenant broke and nobody told them what broke. They just think the world got stingier. They think *they* failed somehow."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, the banter had drained out of him entirely, leaving something older and sadder underneath.

"In Virelai we have a saying," he said. "A table set for two, with one chair pulled out, feeds no one. Your magistrates pulled out the chair, Sable. Twelve years ago and a hundred years before that. And everyone at both tables has been eating less ever since, and blaming their own hunger." He turned to look at her, and in the lamplight his eyes had the amber depth of the orchard, of the warden, of everything she'd sworn to carry now. "So. What does the last swordspeaker do about it?"

Sable looked down at her hands.

They were just hands. Chapped from the road, ink-stained from pricing charms, ordinary. But underneath the skin, coiled and patient as it had been since midnight in the market square, her gift waited — the thing that had erupted out of her without permission, that had caught a falling child and split cobblestones and lit the dark with words she hadn't chosen to speak. She had spent twelve years folding it smaller and smaller so it would fit inside fear.

But fear, she was beginning to understand, had been the wrong container all along.

"There's a boy," she said slowly. "Marta's grandson. Coughing since spring. Two silver for a physician who'll only listen." She flexed her fingers. "There are hundreds like him. Thousands. Every wound the failing seasons have opened, every sickness that a hedge-woman or a speaker could have mended in a minute if the law allowed the word." She lifted her head. "I can't reforge the covenant tonight. I need the third stone, and Dain has half the city locked down hunting Wren, and the veil is still bleeding at the seams. But I can do this. Tonight. Tomorrow. One stranger at a time."

"Do what, exactly?"

"Heal someone," Sable said. "In public. Where people can see."

Tam stared at her. Then he laughed — short, disbelieving, edged with something close to alarm. "You spent twelve years building a life out of not being seen. I broke you out of a magistrate's cell eight days ago. There are broadsheets with your description nailed to every gatehouse between here and the river. And your plan is to stand in the middle of a square and *do magic at people*?"

"Not at people," Sable said. "For them. There's a difference, Tam. Dain wants them to believe magic is a debt collector's scheme — something that takes and takes and leaves a bill. Fine. Then let them watch it pay instead. Let them watch it give." She stood, and the vow in her chest shifted, settled, steadied her like a keel. "Dain thinks fear is what keeps the city safe from Virelai. Maybe. But I think memory is stronger than fear, if you feed it right. They haven't forgotten what magic gave them. They've just been starved so long they've stopped trusting their own memories." Her mouth curved — not her market smile, but something truer. "So I'll remind them. One stranger at a time."

Tam looked up at her for a long moment. Then he sighed, deeply, theatrically, the sigh of a creature who had lived long enough to know exactly how badly things could end.

"Well," he said. "I did owe your grandmother a debt. Nobody said anything about her granddaughter having *ideas*." He rose, wincing at his shoulder, and pulled his hood up. "Where do we start?"

Sable looked toward the tenement rows east of the market, where Marta's grandson was coughing in a room above a dyer's shop.

"There," she said.

* * *

The boy's name was Fen, and he was seven, and he coughed the way old men cough — deep, wet, worn-out, a sound far too large for his narrow chest. Marta met them at the door of the tenement flat with a candle stub and an expression caught between hope and terror.

"You said—" she began.

"I said I'd look at him," Sable said. "That's all. Whatever I am, whatever this is — you saw nothing tonight unless you decide to have seen it. That part is yours to choose, and I'll not take it from you."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she stepped aside and held the door wider.

Fen lay on a pallet by the window, small and flushed, his breathing a labor. Sable knelt beside him and did what Maren had taught her a lifetime ago: laid her palm flat over the small sternum, closed her eyes, and listened past the flesh to the season underneath.

What she found made her throat tighten. The boy's lungs were full of the year itself — damp that wouldn't lift, a chill that had never been warmed out, the whole soggy weight of a spring that never came properly and a summer that arrived exhausted. The covenant's failure was *in* him, written in water and cold. This was what a broken covenant looked like in miniature: a child's body trying to make a season out of a winter that refused to end.

*Swordspeaking was never about swords,* Maren had told her once, kneeling in the training yard of the bell tower, her grey braid swinging forward. *It's about speaking to what's wounded until it remembers what whole sounds like.*

Sable breathed out. And quietly, so quietly only the boy and the room and the old woman watching from the doorway could hear, she spoke.

Not a spell. Not a command. Just a word in the old cadence, the rising-falling lilt of the oath-tongue, shaped like warmth arriving. Like *enough*. Like *spring, you may come in now.*

Heat bloomed under her palm — gentle, golden, the exact temperature of a sunlit windowsill in early June. Fen's breathing changed. The rattle loosened, thinned, and then, with a sound like a knot slipping free, released. The boy sighed — a huge, whole-lunged sigh, the sigh of someone surfacing — opened his eyes, and said, with the devastating practicality of the very young:

"I'm hungry."

Marta made a sound Sable had never heard from her before, somewhere between a sob and a laugh, and gathered the boy up against her shoulder, and held him, and held him, and held him.

When she finally looked at Sable over his head, her eyes were streaming and her voice was steel. "Twelve years," she said. "Twelve years I've watched this city get poorer and colder and quieter, and everyone saying it was bad harvests, bad luck, bad management. Bad *luck*." She wiped her face fiercely with her apron. "It wasn't luck. Was it."

"No," Sable said. "It wasn't."

"And the rest of them—" Marta's grip tightened on her grandson. "How many are lying in rooms like this one? How many wounds that won't close, fevers that won't break, babies born thin because their mothers ate thin? How many, and how long have we been told to swallow it?"

"I don't know," Sable said honestly. "But I know where I'll be tomorrow evening. Market corner, by the tanner's awning. If people come, I'll do what I can. If they tell the magistrates—" she shrugged, and found, to her own surprise, that the shrug cost her almost nothing, "—then they'll tell them. But I think most people are tired of swallowing."

Marta studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way you nod at a debt acknowledged.

"Market corner," she said. "I'll bring the soup."

* * *

Word traveled the way it always does in poor neighborhoods: faster than horses, faster than law, carried in the space between one door and the next.

The next evening, seven people were waiting at Sable's corner. The evening after, nineteen. By the fourth night, the queue outside the tanner's awning stretched past the chandler's and doubled back along the well lane, and Tam — hood up, sharp-eyed, working the crowd's edges like the exiled courtier he secretly was — counted heads and muttered numbers under his breath with the grim satisfaction of a general reviewing unlikely troops.

She healed a dyer's hands, cracked and chemical-burned, until the skin smoothed brown and unbroken. She healed a carter's herniated back while he wept openly into his cap, and sent him home walking upright for the first time in three years. She drew fever out of a baby no bigger than a loaf of bread while the mother watched with her fist pressed against her mouth. She mended an old soldier's hearing, a laundress's ruptured lung, a child's clubbed foot — small miracles, one after another, each spoken in the low golden cadence that needed no volume because it was aimed at the wound itself, at the season trapped inside the hurt, telling it what whole sounded like.

And the city began, cautiously, unbelievingly, to remember.

She heard it in fragments, in the queue's low murmur. An old man telling his neighbor that his grandmother had been delivered by a hedge-woman's hands, and he'd forgotten, he'd actually *forgotten*, until tonight. A young woman saying her mother used to sing a song to the kitchen garden, and the beans had always come up true. A boy asking his father whether the stories about the garden realm were real, and the father — after a long pause — saying, "More real than the magistrate's bread ration, son."

Nobody shouted her name. Nobody knelt. That wasn't how it worked, and Sable understood with growing clarity that this was precisely why it was working. She wasn't a prophet or a rebel or a banner. She was the charm-seller from the corner, the one who'd sold them luck-knots and traveler's wards for years, the one whose face they knew — and she was doing, openly, freely, at no charge, the thing the city had been told to fear for twelve years. Fear needed distance. She was giving them none.

On the sixth night, someone left a jar of honey at her feet. On the seventh, a bundle of candles. On the eighth, a hand-drawn sign, propped against the tanner's post: **THE SPEAKER IS IN.**

Tam read it, snorted, and said, "Subtle," but he was smiling, and the smile didn't quite hide what was moving underneath it.

It was on the ninth night that the mood changed.

Sable felt it before she saw it — a ripple running backward through the queue, heads turning, voices dropping. At the far end of the well lane, torchlight. Not lamplighters' orange but the hard white glare of magistrate's pitch, and the black-and-silver of Corvane's watch moving through the crowd in formation, unhurried, deliberate, a wall of men with a wall's intentions.

At their center walked a figure in a grey coat, and even at forty paces, even across nine nights of remembered miracles, Sable knew the set of those shoulders.

Magistrate Corvel Dain had come to see for himself.

The crowd didn't scatter. That was the thing that made Sable's heart hammer — they *didn't scatter*. They closed, subtly, the way water closes around a stone, bodies shifting to make the queue a little denser, a little harder to walk through. Marta appeared at Sable's elbow with a soup ladle gripped like a mace. Tam melted toward the alley mouth, ready to run or fight or both.

Dain stopped at the edge of the lantern light. His face was unreadable, carved and grey, but his eyes — his eyes went down the queue, taking inventory: the healed carter standing straight, the feverish baby sleeping soundly against its mother's neck, the old soldier laughing at something, *laughing*, in a city that had forgotten how.

Then his gaze found Sable's, and held it, and in it she saw something that frightened her far more than hatred would have.

Recognition. And beneath it, unwilling and unmistakable, hunger.

"Sable Brightwater," he said, and his voice carried the way stone carries sound — flat, final, filling the lane. "We need to talk about what happens when the veil comes down."

Behind him, at the lane's mouth, the torches multiplied — and among the watch uniforms, moving with a prisoner's shuffling gait and a bruise blooming along one cheekbone, walked Wren Halloway, alive, in chains, and looking directly at Sable with an expression that said, as clearly as any prophecy book ever had:

*I'm sorry. And I brought you the third stone's location anyway.*
