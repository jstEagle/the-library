## 13. What Grief Costs

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old smoke, the way all buried places in Corvane did — as if the fires of the Purge had soaked so deep into the foundations that no amount of rain could ever wash them out again. Sable had chosen a cistern chamber three levels below the tannery district, a place where the water had long since drained away and left behind a bowl of dry, echoing dark. It was the kind of hiding place she'd learned to find in her first terrified year: forgotten by the city above, unclaimed by the gangs that worked the nearer tunnels, close enough to hear the muffled heartbeat of Corvane through the stone but far enough that no one came looking.

No one came looking. That had been the whole architecture of her life for twelve years, built stone by stone: be small, be quiet, be nowhere.

And now Tam Vess was dying in her arms, and every one of those stones felt like it was pressing down on her at once.

He lay on the cloak she'd spread over the cracked basin floor, and he was still trying to joke about it, because of course he was. "You know," he said, each word costing him visibly now, his breath coming shallow and quick, "in Virelai they say a fox's debts always come due. Nobody mentions the interest."

"Stop talking." She pressed the wadded cloth against his side again, and it came away dark and heavy, more blood than cloth now. The crossbow bolt had gone in under his ribs on the left — Dain's men had shot first and shouted after, and Tam had twisted at the last instant, turning what should have been his heart into merely the worst wound Sable had ever tended. He'd carried her down four ladders and through two flooded passages with the bolt still in him before she'd finally wrestled him onto the ground and cut it free.

That had been an hour ago. Or a year. Time moved strangely when you were counting someone else's breaths.

The problem — the problem she could not stop looking at, even as she looked away from it — was that the bleeding would not stop. She'd bound it tight, packed it with the moss from her charm-basket, the good moss, the kind she sold to grandmothers for teething children because it drew fever out. It should have worked. It worked on everything. And it was working too slowly, or not at all, because the wound wasn't just flesh. She could see it now, in the low amber light of the single candle stub she'd allowed herself: the edges of the torn skin were graying, going papery, like leaves in late autumn. Whatever ran in Tam Vess's veins wasn't entirely blood, and whatever had pierced him knew it.

Iron. The bolts had iron heads. Of course they did. The magistrate's men hunted things from the other side of the veil, and they armed themselves accordingly.

"For a swordspeaker," Tam murmured, his eyes half-closed, his fox-sharp features gone slack and gray, "you're very bad at this. My grandmother's cat got better care. And she hated the cat."

"I'm not—" Sable's voice caught. She swallowed and started again, quieter. "I'm not a swordspeaker. I'm a charm-seller. I sell luck-knots. Two coppers apiece."

"Mm." His mouth twitched toward a smile that didn't have the strength to arrive. "Two coppers. What a waste."

She bent over him again, pressing, pressing, and let the silence stretch between them, because if she spoke she was going to say something true, and true things were dangerous right now. True things were the most dangerous things in the world.

* * *

The candle burned down to a knuckle's width. Tam stopped joking around the second hour. By the third, he had stopped answering her questions at all except in fragments, and then only in the old tongue of Virelai, words that flowed like water over stones and meant, she suspected, nothing kind about iron or the people who used it. His shoulder — the one where the first oath-stone shard sat embedded like a splinter of moonlight — had begun to glow faintly, pulsing in time with his failing heart, and that frightened her more than the blood. The shard was pulling at him. Or he was pulling at it. Either way, something in him was spending itself faster than any wound alone could account for.

Sable sat back on her heels and looked at her own hands.

They were steady. Twelve years of practice had taught them steadiness — hands that never shook while tying a luck-knot, never trembled while taking coin from a nervous customer, never so much as flickered when a city guard glanced twice at her stall and moved on. Steady hands were the price of survival. You paid it daily, in small installments.

But now her steady hands hovered over a dying fox-exile, and she knew — she had known since the second hour — exactly what they were capable of.

Blood-magic. The word itself was a curse in Corvane, though almost nobody remembered why. They thought it meant hedge-craft, gutter-witchery, the desperate spells of desperate women. But the oath-bound had known the truth: blood-magic was the shortcut. It was how the original covenant had corrupted itself, generations ago — speakers who grew impatient with the slow reciprocity of the veil, who took power directly from their own veins instead of asking it through the oath, who fed themselves to the magic instead of standing between the worlds as its servants. Every speaker who bled herself for power made the veil thinner and hungrier. Every drop taken without consent given freely back weakened the covenant a little more. Her mentor had told her the history once, walking her through the orchard, his voice gentle and absolute:

*The day a speaker chooses her own survival over the covenant's health is the day the covenant begins to die. It does not happen all at once, little thorn. It happens the way rot happens — quietly, sweetly, from the inside.*

She'd sworn silence rather than swear anything else. She'd spent twelve years selling two-copper charms. And now here she was, in the dark beneath the city, with a man dying beside her and her own blood singing under her skin, offering itself, *asking* to be used, the way it hadn't asked in twelve years. All she would have to do was open a vein and speak the old transfer-words, and her life would pour into him the way water pours into cracked ground, fast and greedy and warm. Blood-magic didn't heal, exactly. It *substituted*. Her vitality for his wound. Her years for his hours. A loan against herself, at interest she couldn't calculate.

It would save him. Probably. Tonight, certainly.

And it would make her, in some small and irreversible way, the same thing that had rotted the covenant from within. The same shortcut. The same choice. The first step down the road that ended with a magistrate burning an order out of existence and a veil tearing over a market square.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said suddenly.

She flinched. His eyes were open — when had his eyes opened? — fixed on her hands with the unnerving clarity of someone whose blood was mostly otherworld. "What thing?"

"The thinking-about-it thing." He tried to laugh; it became a cough that brought fresh red to his lips. "Foxes can smell intention. Yours smells like... my grandmother. When she was deciding whether to drown the runt of the litter." A pause. "Don't take offense. She usually decided against."

"Tam—"

"If you're going to bleed yourself into me," he said, and his voice had gone strange — not weak anymore but very clear, very present, the voice of someone standing at a door and choosing to say the true thing before it closed, "then do it knowing what you're trading. Not for me. I've had a good run. Stolen a lot of bread, insulted a lot of important people. But don't do it to avoid feeling this." His hand found her wrist — cold fingers, shockingly light — and gripped. "Grief is the fee, swordspeaker. You've been dodging the bill for twelve years. Don't spend your blood just to keep dodging it."

She stared at him. The candle flame stood perfectly still, the way flames did around strong magic waiting to be used.

"I hate you," she whispered.

"Noted," he said. "Debt transfers."

* * *

She did not use the blood-magic. She wanted the record of that hour, later, to show that there had been a moment — a real one, not a storybook one — when she had held the shortcut in her hand and put it down. She took off her belt knife, opened her own palm across the old scar line, and then simply... held it there, letting the pain sharpen her, letting the sight of her own blood remind her what it cost, until the singing in her veins quieted and became ordinary again, mortal again, hers. Then she bandaged the shallow cut and went back to the slow work: pressure, moss, the last of her healing charms broken open one by one and pressed against his skin, each one a tiny lie she'd sold a hundred times — *harmless, harmless, harmless* — finally being asked to tell the truth.

It wasn't enough. She could see it wasn't enough. But it was honest work, and honest work was all she had, and she did it while the candle died and relit itself strangely in air that tasted faintly of green.

That was the first sign Wren Halloway was coming. The undercity didn't smell of growing things. Ever.

The archivist came down the far ladder badly, half falling, catching herself on the rusted rungs, and stood at the edge of the cistern bowl gasping, her satchel clutched to her chest, her hair full of cobwebs and her face streaked with soot. She looked terrible. She looked like a woman who had run a very long way from something, and Sable's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up with her grief and understood what else the archivist's presence meant.

Dain's men had followed Wren. Which meant Wren had led them somewhere. Which meant—

"The bell tower," Sable said. Her voice came out flat, dead, the voice of the woman she'd been before tonight. "You led them to the bell tower."

Wren flinched as if struck. "They were already following me. From the archive — I was marked, I told you I was marked, I thought if I kept moving—"

"You *thought*." Sable rose. She hadn't decided to rise; her body simply did it, twelve years of stillness breaking all at once. "Tam took a bolt meant for the third stone. Tam is dying because you walked, and they walked behind you, and nobody — *nobody* — thought to check the street behind the archivist with the stolen prophecy book." Her voice climbed and cracked and she let it, let it break wide open, because holding it in had been her life's work and look where holding it in had gotten everyone. "Is he even worth it to you? Was any of this worth it to you, Wren, or is it just the book? Is it just the *knowing*?"

"Yes!" Wren's voice cracked too, and she stepped forward into the dead candle-light, into the faint glow of the oath-stone shard in Tam's shoulder, and Sable saw her face clearly for the first time — saw that she'd been crying for some time, that she'd been crying, perhaps, for days. "Yes, it's the book, and yes, it's the knowing, and yes, I walked too fast and looked too little and a man I barely know is bleeding out in a hole in the ground because of me. Do you think I don't know that?" Her breath hitched. "Do you think I came down four ladders and through the flooded tunnels to *watch*?"

Sable said nothing. The silence stretched, ugly and alive.

"I came to help," Wren said, quieter. "Please. Let me help. I know things — I know the archive's medical folios, I know what the old texts say about iron-taint, there's a passage in Halvard's *Materia Medica*, a moss-and-marrow poultice, it's obscure, it's probably nonsense, but please—" Her voice broke entirely. "Please don't make me stand here and watch another person die because I was careless. I've done it once already. Everyone in that archive, when Dain burned it — everyone who didn't get out — I counted them. I count them every night. Please don't add him to the count."

The words landed in Sable like stones dropped into deep water.

Another person die because I was careless. Another count. Another ledger of the dead kept by the living.

Twelve years ago, Sable had survived by running, and she had told herself a thousand stories about why — that she'd been ordered to flee, that living was the greater service, that her mentor's sacrifice demanded she honor it by staying alive. All true, maybe. None of them the whole truth. The whole truth was simpler and worse: she had run because she was afraid, and everyone who stayed had died, and she had never once added them to a count aloud. She'd just carried them, silently, the way you carry stones in a pack so long you forget which weight is yours.

And here was this trembling, bookish, guilty woman doing openly — badly, weepingly — the exact accounting Sable had refused to do for twelve years.

Mercy, her mentor's voice said in her memory, *is not the absence of anger. It is anger that has chosen its target wisely.*

Sable let out a breath that shuddered on the way out.

"Halvard's *Materia Medica*," she said. Her voice was rough. "Moss-and-marrow. Tell me the preparation."

Relief went through Wren like wind through wheat — visible, total, nearly buckling her knees. "There's a copy in my satchel. Page forty. I — I brought the book. I brought everything. I brought—"

"Page forty," Sable said. "Now."

* * *

They worked together through what remained of the night, and it was the strangest partnership of Sable's life: the hidden swordspeaker and the guilt-stricken archivist, kneeling over a shapeshifting exile in a dead cistern, grinding dried moss and beef marrow and powdered willow-bark into a paste that smelled foul enough to raise the dead. Twice Tam surfaced enough to mutter commentary — once about the smell ("like a tannery married a swamp"), once about Wren ("tell her she owes me a new coat, the coat has a hole in it") — and both times Sable pressed the poultice harder than strictly necessary and both times he smiled, faintly, because foxes liked their debts acknowledged.

But the iron-taint was stubborn. The gray at the wound's edge retreated, then crept back, retreated, crept. Toward the darkest hour before dawn, Wren looked up from the folio with ash-gray lips and said, "There's one more thing. In the marginalia. Handwritten, much older than Halvard. It says the taint yields fully only to..." She hesitated, and Sable watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and respected her for the hesitation. "...to a willing gift. Freely offered. Not blood-magic — the margin is very specific, it says the difference matters, that substitution corrupts but *gift* restores. Someone has to give him strength without taking anything in return. No exchange. Just... an open door."

"That's the oath," Sable said quietly. "That's what the oath always was. An open door, both directions."

"Can you do that? Without the—the other thing?"

Sable looked down at her hands. At the shallow cut across her palm, already closing. At the twelve-year scar underneath it, invisible, enormous.

An open door. Both directions. Everything she'd sealed shut, offered freely, without substitution, without shortcuts, without buying her way past the grief.

"No," she said honestly. "Not yet. Maybe not alone." She met Wren's eyes. "But I know someone who might teach me. If we can reach her."

"Who?"

"The warden of an orchard," Sable said, and heard, distantly, the sound of a vow she'd accepted too quickly beginning to come due. "In Virelai. Where the trees grow around names."

Above them, muffled through three levels of stone, the bells of Corvane began to ring false — a cracked, staggering note that none of them had heard before, and that all of them, in their bones, understood.

The veil was thinning faster now. Whatever Dain intended, whatever the prophecy's final page foretold, the night was moving, and it would not wait for grief to finish its arithmetic.

Sable packed the poultice, bound Tam's wound fresh, and lifted his arm across her shoulders.

"Wren," she said, "get the book. We're going to need the border."
