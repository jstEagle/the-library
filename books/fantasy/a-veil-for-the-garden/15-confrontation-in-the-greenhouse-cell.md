## 15. Confrontation in the Greenhouse Cell

The keep's underlevels smelled of wet stone and old iron, but beneath it all—faint, unmistakable, wrong in this place of bars and boots—there was green. Sable caught it the moment Wren pried open the third gate with her stolen keys: loam, and sap, and something sweeter underneath, like a fruit orchard at the far edge of summer.

"That's not possible," Wren whispered, holding the lantern high. Its light shivered along the corridor walls, across the rusted manacles bolted there, relics of a harsher century. "We're forty feet underground."

"No," Sable said quietly. "It isn't possible. And yet."

She had been down here once before, dragged in chains, and she had spent every hour since turning over what she'd seen: the cell at the end of the row that was not a cell at all, the glass panes fogged from within, the shape of leaves pressed against them like hands against a window. Dain kept his secrets close, but he kept this one closer than any. She had heard the guards speak of it in hushed, uneasy tones—the magistrate's garden, they called it, though none would say why a man who had burned every hedge-witch in Corvane tended something living behind glass.

Now she walked toward it on her own feet, with Wren's lantern trembling beside her and Tam's blood still dried under her fingernails, and the smell of growing things grew stronger with every step, until it drowned the stone entirely.

* * *

The door was oak, banded in iron, and locked with three separate bolts—and beyond it, when Sable shouldered through the last one, was a world.

The chamber had been a dungeon vault once. She could see its bones: the barrel-vaulted ceiling, the drainage grate in the floor, the iron ring where prisoners had been chained. But someone had spent years—decades—bending this place to another purpose. Mirrors had been mounted at angles to catch and multiply the light of a single enchanted lamp, coaxing daylight into a room that had never known it. Soil lay heaped two feet deep across the flagstones. And in the center, rising from that stolen earth inside a cage of black iron bars, stood a tree no taller than Sable herself.

It was dying. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat—not with her eyes but with the gift she had starved for twelve years, which woke now against her will, reaching out like a hand toward warmth. The sapling's bark was gray and splitting. Half its branches were bare. The leaves that remained clung on with the desperate yellow-green of late autumn, and even as she watched, one let go and drifted down through the bars to land on the soil without a sound.

But it was alive. And it was Virelai. She could feel the realm in it the way you feel a song through a wall—the same current that ran through the veil, through the oath-stones humming in the satchel against her ribs, through her own blood. This small caged thing was a piece of the garden realm, rooted in mortal dirt, and it was starving.

"You shouldn't be here."

Sable turned. Magistrate Corvel Dain stood in the doorway behind her, and he did not look like a man who had summoned guards. He looked like a man who had come alone because he could not help himself. He wore no coat of office, only a plain dark shirt with the sleeves rolled up, and in one hand he carried a copper watering can, dented at the spout, the kind sold in any market stall for a few coins.

He set it down very carefully, as if putting away a weapon he was ashamed of.

"Guards are coming," he said. "You have minutes. So tell me why I shouldn't call them faster."

"Because you want to know how I got past your gates," Sable said. "And because if you call them before I'm done, you'll never know what this tree is." She turned back to the sapling. "Though I suspect you already know more than you admit. You've kept it alive for years. That takes knowledge. That takes—" she searched for the word and found it bitter, "—care."

"It takes discipline." His voice was flat. "Nothing else."

"Does it?" She moved closer to the cage. Up close she could see the details that undid her: the way the soil had been turned and re-turned by hand, the careful stakes propping up the weakest branch, the strip of linen tied around a split in the bark like a bandage on a wound. Someone had bound this tree's hurt the way you'd bind a child's scraped knee. "How much water? How many hours? How long does a thing like this take to kill a man slowly?"

Dain crossed the room in four strides and put himself between her and the cage, and for a moment Sable thought he would strike her. His face had gone the color of ash, and his jaw worked as though words were being broken apart in it.

"You don't touch it," he said.

"I don't want to touch it." She held his gaze. "I want to free it."

Something moved behind his eyes—she saw it clearly, quick as a fish under ice—and then it was gone, and the magistrate's mask came down over his face like a visor dropping.

"There it is," he said softly. "The swordspeaker's pitch. I wondered how long it would take. Free the tree, restore the covenant, save us all—is that the sermon? I've heard it before. I heard it the night I gave the order. Your mentor preached it while my men cut her down, and it didn't save her, and it won't save you."

"My mentor is dead."

"Yes. I know exactly how dead she is. I was there."

* * *

They faced each other across the little tree, and the lamplight multiplied in the mirrors threw their shadows long and doubled against the walls, so that it seemed for a moment there were four people in the room instead of two, and none of them speaking.

Sable had imagined this meeting for twelve years. In the worst nights—in the nights after the market closed and the charms wouldn't sell and the silence in her chest grew loud enough to hear—she had imagined it with fire in it. She had imagined drawing steel. She had imagined the words of the oath in her mouth and his blood on the stones, and she had told herself that wanting it was not the same as being it.

Now, standing here, she found the fire had gone somewhere she couldn't reach. What was left was stranger and harder to hold.

"You were young," she said. "When you signed the order. Younger than I am now."

"I was an officer doing my duty."

"You were a boy with a commission and a fear of things you didn't understand, and someone handed you a list of names, and you signed it." She took a step toward him. He didn't retreat, but his hands curled at his sides. "I've hated you for twelve years, Magistrate. I want you to understand that first, so you don't mistake anything I say next for forgiveness. I have rehearsed your death more times than I can count. But I've been down in the undercity for three days watching people starve because the seasons stopped, and I've learned something about hatred. It doesn't feed anyone."

"Then what do you propose?" His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and he covered it with contempt. "That we apologize to the garden? Kneel in the square and beg the veil to forgive us? I have read the histories, swordspeaker. I know what the covenant cost. Every year it took. Every tithe. Every child marked at birth and carried off to serve realms we couldn't even see. My ancestors paid it because they were afraid. I ended the payments."

"And look what ending them bought." She gestured upward, toward the city above, toward the tearing sky. "The veil is failing, Dain. Not because of me. Not because of Virelai. Because a covenant needs two hands holding it, and yours let go. The garden is bleeding into the streets. People are dying. And this—" she touched the iron bar of the cage, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—this is the last living root of the thing that fed both our worlds for a thousand years. And you keep it in a dungeon and water it with a dented can."

"I keep it alive," Dain said, "because killing it would be murder."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Sable went very still. "Say that again."

His face had changed. The mask hadn't dropped so much as shattered, and what was underneath was not a magistrate at all—it was a man of perhaps fifty, exhausted in a way that sleep could not fix, standing in a cellar full of mirrors that multiplied his loneliness.

"You think I don't know what it is?" he said. His voice had gone quiet, and quieter still, as though he were confessing to the tree rather than to her. "You think I dug this vault out and hauled soil down here in secret because I enjoy gardening? I know what it is. I've known since the night I took it. There was a grove outside the city—what was left of one, after the Purge. Everything else was burning. This sapling was the only living thing my men couldn't destroy, because every axe that touched it broke, and every man who raised one wept without knowing why. So I took it. I told myself I was preserving evidence." He laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "Twelve years I've told myself that."

"Why?" Sable asked. Not *why did you take it*. She meant the other question, the one underneath, the one that mattered. "Why keep it alive at all? You believe magic is a debt collectors' scheme. You said the covenant failed everyone who trusted it. Let it die, then. Why does it live?"

Dain looked at the sapling for a long time. One of the yellowed leaves trembled, though there was no draft in the sealed room.

"Because of my wife," he said.

* * *

The name, when he finally spoke it, came out worn smooth, like a coin handled too often.

"Maren," he said. "Her name was Maren."

Sable did not move. She had learned, in twelve years of selling charms to frightened people, that grief speaks only when it is not watched too hard. So she watched the tree instead, and let him talk to the space between them.

"She was a healer's daughter. Not a witch—her family had never been sworn to anything, they were just... good with hurts. Bonesetting, fevers, childbirth. The kind of skill that runs in hands." He flexed his own hands, as if remembering. "When the fever came through the river districts—this was eight years ago, you'd have been hiding by then—I sent for every physician in the city. They bled her. They packed her in ice. They did everything medicine knows how to do, which in the end is almost nothing, and they knew it, and I knew it, and she knew it, lying there looking at me with those clear grey eyes, waiting for me to fix it, because I was the magistrate, and magistrates fix things."

The watering can sat on the floor between them, dented and ordinary, and Sable understood suddenly that it was the most honest object in the room.

"There was a woman," Dain went on. "Old, half-mad, lived under the tannery bridge. A hedge-healer, the last one in the river districts who hadn't fled or burned. Maren's father begged me to send for her. Begged me. And I—" He stopped. His throat worked. "I had built my whole life on the law I wrote. Magic is outlawed. Those who practice it are criminals. If I sent for her, I unmade myself. So I sat by my wife's bed and I held her hand and I recited the law to myself like a prayer, and she died on the fourth morning, and the woman under the bridge died that winter too, alone, and no one healed anyone."

The lamplight hummed. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of stone, a bell was ringing the hour, and the sound reached the vault thin and warped, like a memory of a bell.

"After the funeral," Dain said, "I came down here. I don't know why. I used to come down to the cells to remind myself why the law mattered—every criminal I'd put away, every danger contained. And I stood in front of this tree, this illegal, forbidden, outlawed thing, and I looked at it dying, and I thought: *you are the only creature in this city sicker than she was.*" He passed a hand over his face. "And I picked up the watering can."

"You couldn't heal her," Sable said quietly. "So you heal it."

"Don't." The word came out raw. "Don't dress it up. It's not healing. It's cowardice with a schedule. I water it twice a day because if I stop, I have to admit that everything I've built—the law, the order, the Purge, all of it—was built on a lie I told myself while my wife was dying four floors above my head. That magic wasn't the debt. Pride was the debt. Fear was the debt. And I have been paying interest on it ever since, in secret, in a cellar, to a tree."

For one moment—one breath, no longer—they were not enemies. They were two mourners standing in the same small room, on either side of the same dead night, and the tree between them held its yellow leaves up like small, tired flags of truce. Sable felt her hatred shift in her chest, not breaking, not dissolving, but settling, the way sediment settles in shaken water, and what became visible beneath it was something she had never expected to see: a man who had lost the same war she had, on the same night, from the other side of the field.

"Dain," she said, and used no title, because titles were walls and she needed a door. "Corvel. The covenant can still be reforged. I have the stones. I have the root—you've kept it alive; whatever else you've done, you kept the door from closing forever. Help me. Not for Virelai. For the next person lying in a bed with a fever, whose only crime is being born after we burned the ones who could have saved them. For Maren."

He flinched at the name as if she had struck him.

For a long moment she believed—she actually believed—that he would say yes. He looked at the sapling, and his hand rose halfway toward the iron bars, trembling, and the tree's remaining leaves stirred toward him like faces turning toward the sun.

Then his hand dropped.

"No," he said.

The word was small and final as a coffin nail.

"The covenant took tithes for a thousand years," he said, and his voice was steady now, colder than it had been all night, because he had rebuilt the wall brick by brick while she watched. "It took my wife because it was already failing—because your order's grand bargain was rotting from the inside long before I signed anything. And you ask me to trust it again. To trust *magic* again. I have seen what both worlds do when they hold power over each other, swordspeaker. Mortals burn witches. Gardens bleed seasons. Neither side has ever once chosen mercy. Why should I believe you're different?"

"Because I'm asking," Sable said. "No one in a thousand years of tithes ever asked. They demanded, and they sacrificed, and they took. I'm standing in your cellar asking you to water a dying world with me. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it's the only one that's ever mattered."

"Beautiful," Dain said. "You should sell that in the market, with the charms." He stepped back from the cage, and when he spoke again his voice carried, pitched for the corridor beyond. "I am sorry, truly, that grief made us both fools. But I did not survive one apocalypse to be talked politely into another."

He raised his voice. "GUARDS!"

Boots thundered somewhere above, descending. Wren appeared in the doorway, white-faced, the lantern shaking. "Sable—"

"I know." Sable did not move. She looked at the magistrate one last time—at the man who watered a miracle in secret and called it discipline, who had loved a woman and buried his mercy with her—and she felt the last soft thing in her chest harden into something clean and cold and usable.

Resolve.

"You'll regret this," she said. "Not because I'll make you. Because the veil won't wait for your permission any more than the fever waited for mine."

"Take them," Dain said to the guards flooding through the door. "Both of them. And then fetch axes."

Sable spun. "What?"

He was looking at the sapling, and his eyes were dry, and terrible, and certain.

"If the veil falls tonight," he said, "it falls because I end it. No more roots. No more doors. No more debts." He picked up the watering can and set it aside, gently, one last time. "Seal the greenhouse. Nothing gets in—or out."

The first guard's hand closed on Sable's shoulder, and through the iron bars, in the lamplight, she saw the sapling's last green leaf fall.
