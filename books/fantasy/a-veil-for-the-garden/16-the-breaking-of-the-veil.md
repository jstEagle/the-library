## 16. The Breaking of the Veil

The root was smaller than Dain had expected.

He had imagined something monstrous — a great pulsing heart of green, the engine of all the magic he had spent his life fighting. Instead it stood in the center of his own greenhouse cell like a starving child: a sapling no taller than a man, bark gray and peeling, leaves curled at their edges as though perpetually bracing for frost. The iron cage he had built around it twelve years ago had been absorbed into its trunk in places, bars swallowed by wood that had grown around its prison because it knew nothing else.

Dain held the axe with both hands. It was a good axe — Corvane steel, forged in his grandfather's foundry, honest metal that had never once lied to him about what it could do.

"You did this," he said to the sapling, and his voice came out wrong, cracked down the middle like old plaster. "You and your garden. Your covenant. My wife knelt at your altars when she was sick. She prayed to Virelai for a healing, and Virelai took her offering and gave her nothing. Three weeks she faded. I watched the color leave her like water leaving a cup."

The sapling did not answer. Of course it didn't. It was a tree. It was only ever a tree, and everything else — the covenant, the seasons it fed across the veil, the whole glittering architecture of debt and devotion — was a story told by people who needed the world to owe them something.

But Sable's voice was still in his ears. *Your wife died because the covenant was failing. Not because of us. Because of men like you, burning bridges while they were still being crossed.* He hated her for saying it. He hated more the small, cold part of him that had recognized the truth of it even as she spoke.

He raised the axe.

"Corvel." Wren Halloway stood in the doorway behind him — how long had she been there? — pale as paper, the prophecy book clutched to her chest. Its pages were fluttering now, though there was no wind in the cell, turning themselves forward faster and faster, as if the book were shouting. "If you strike it, you won't sever anything. You'll tear it. The archive texts are clear — a broken covenant doesn't close the door. It breaks the door off its hinges."

"Then let it break."

"The streets, Magistrate. The gardens will come *through*—"

"My city," he said, "has bled for your kind's secrets long enough."

He brought the axe down.

The sound was not the sound of wood splitting. It was deeper, wetter, older — the sound of a root tearing loose from soil it had held for a thousand years, heard from inside the soil. The greenhouse glass shivered in its frames. Every leaf on the caged sapling turned toward him at once, though there was no wind, and they were not green anymore; they were the white of an empty page.

And beneath his feet, through the stone floor of his own keep, Dain felt the city groan.

* * *

Sable felt it first as a ringing in her teeth.

She and Wren were running the keep's lower corridors, three stones wrapped in cloth against her chest, warm as sleeping birds, when the ringing started — and then every lantern in the corridor guttered green, and the stones beneath their feet moved. Not shook. *Moved*, the way a sleeper moves, rolling over with a sigh of vast discomfort.

"No," Sable breathed. She pressed her palm flat against the wall and felt it: the slow pulse of the root, arrhythmic now, stumbling like a heart missing beats. "No, no — we're too late. He's already cut it."

Wren grabbed her arm. Above them, somewhere far up through the floors of the keep, came a sound like a forest exhaling.

"We have to reach the cell," Sable said. "Now. If the root dies before I can join the stones—"

They ran. The corridors betrayed them as they ran. Ivy burst through mortar seams ahead of them, thick as a man's wrist, blossoming out of bare stone in ropes of impossible green — flowers opening as they watched, fat and fragrant and wrong, roses blooming in the dark of a dungeon where nothing had grown in a hundred years. Sable hurdled a tendril and felt it whip past her ankle, gentle as a hand trying to catch hold. Behind them, a door they had already passed groaned open on a wall of ferns.

"It's coming *through*," Wren shouted over the noise. "Everywhere. All at once—"

The floor tilted. Not metaphorically — the corridor listed sideways like a ship taking water, and Sable slammed into the wall, and through the wall she saw, impossibly, *through* the wall: moonlight, orchard rows, a sky with two moons hanging in it like a pair of watching eyes. The stone had gone translucent as vellum. Beyond it, Virelai lay spread out and beautiful and bleeding into the mortal world along a hundred thousand hairline cracks.

Then the wall was stone again, and Sable was on her knees, and the three oath-stones against her chest were so hot they burned through the cloth, singing a single note that she realized, with a lurch, was the first line of the oath. Calling to her. Begging her to hurry.

* * *

Tam had been holding the breach for eleven minutes, and he had perhaps eleven left.

He had not meant to be here. He had been lying in the undercity with a wound in his side that Wren's needlework had closed but not cured, half-dreaming of fox-runs through summer meadows, when the ground split open beneath the tanner's row and the garden poured through — and there had been people above, market folk, night workers, a woman with a child — and someone had to stand in the door.

So he stood in the door.

It was not a door one could see, exactly. It was a place where the air went soft and strange, a vertical shimmer above the ruined street where Virelai pressed through like light through frosted glass. Tam had planted himself in front of it, arms spread, and done the oldest thing his exile blood knew how to do: he had become the boundary. His skin flickered — fox-fur, then feather, then bark, then the smooth grey hide of something with no name — shape after shape shedding off him like clothes, each transformation buying the breach another minute of stability. His body was the hinge. Every shift cost him. He could feel himself fraying at the edges, thread by golden thread.

Below him, the street had gone mad. Brambles erupted through cobblestones and dragged a cart sideways; a fountain overflowed with water that smelled of rain on far hills; a cherry tree stood fully grown where a lamppost had been, shedding blossom onto the shoulders of a crowd that had stopped screaming only because the beauty of it had briefly stolen their terror. And between the beauties, the dangers: a rose-briar closing around a drunkard's leg, thorns lengthening as it tightened; a drift of luminous pollen pouring from a crack overhead, and the people who breathed it swaying, smiling, walking toward the shimmer with their eyes gone wide and empty.

Tam caught the drunkard's briar in his teeth — he was fox-shaped, then, mostly — and worried it apart, and spat thorns, and shifted back to two legs to shout at the pollen-walkers, and his voice came out in two voices at once, man and fox, and neither of them carried.

"Hold the line, Vess," he muttered to himself. "Hold the line. Debt of the Ashgrove line. You don't get to die owing a Brightwater."

His knees buckled. He locked them. Blood ran freely down his side again, and where it struck the cobblestones, small white flowers sprang up instantly, blooming and dying in seconds — his life leaking into the mortal dirt and the dirt drinking it greedily, and some distant part of Tam noted that this was how Virelai's exiles always died, feeding a world that wasn't theirs.

Then, down the shattered street, he saw her — Sable, running toward him through the chaos with Wren behind her and light streaming from the bundle at her chest, and Tam laughed, or sobbed, or both.

"Well," he said. "About time, swordspeaker."

* * *

Dain stood over the stump of the root and understood, slowly, the way a drowning man understands water, that he had made a mistake of arithmetic.

He had thought the covenant was a ledger. Debts and payments, offerings and healings, and if the garden would not pay, then burn the account books and be free of debt forever. But standing over the severed stump, watching the white-leaved wood bleed sap that was not sap — watching it pour upward, defying gravity, rising past the broken roof of his greenhouse in a column of silver mist — he understood that a covenant was not a ledger. It was a dam. And he had just knocked out the sluice-gate with an axe.

The column of mist hit the night sky and the sky *tore*.

There was no better word for it. Above Corvane, directly above his keep, the darkness ripped along a seam no eye could have seen a moment before, and beyond the rip was green — deep, layered, living green under a double-mooned sky, the true face of Virelai unhidden for the first time in a century, gorgeous and enormous and pressing down through the wound like floodwater through a cracked window. Wind roared out of the breach carrying scents that made Dain's knees weaken absurdly: cut grass, rain, his mother's kitchen, his wife's hair. Memory-scents. Lure-scents. The garden calling everything home.

Around him, his keep began to come apart into both worlds at once. Stone walls budded. The flagstones of the courtyard split and pushed up moss and mushrooms and then saplings, whole trees growing in seconds, lifting his guards' barricades into the air on roots. A staircase unraveled into ivy. Somewhere below, horses screamed. Somewhere farther, church bells began to ring wildly — every bell in the city at once, though no hands pulled the ropes, the bronze itself crying alarm.

"Magistrate!" Captain Orell found him in the courtyard, ash-pale, helmet gone. "The eastern quarter — the streets are — sir, there are *forests* in the streets, and people are walking into the breach, they're just walking in, we can't stop them—"

"I can see that, Captain."

"What are your orders?"

Dain looked at his hands. They were steady. That seemed obscene. Twelve years ago he had given an order in this same courtyard — different courtyard, same words almost — and his hands had been steady then too, and four hundred swordspeakers had died for that steadiness, and the world had not thanked him. It had only gotten hungrier.

He thought of Maren, kneeling at the garden altars. He thought of her last word, which had been his name, said like a question.

"Fall back," he heard himself say. "Get everyone clear of the keep. Let it take the keep."

Orell stared. "Sir?"

"You heard me. The building is lost. The people aren't. Go."

As the captain ran, Dain walked — slowly, absurdly calmly — back into his greenhouse cell, where the severed root lay dying in its cage, and knelt beside it in the wreckage of the floor, among the shards of his own certainty.

"I'm sorry," he said to it, and did not know until the words were out whether he meant the tree or the wife or the four hundred dead or all of them at once.

The stump's remaining leaves turned toward him. And though it had no mouth, Dain heard it — felt it, rather, in the marrow-deep way he had felt the city groan — and what it said was not forgiveness. What it said was a name:

*Sable.*

* * *

Sable reached Tam at a dead run and caught him before he fell.

He weighed nothing, which frightened her more than the wound — a body spending itself so fast it was hollowing. His skin cycled through shapes even as she held him, fox and boy and bird and a brief terrible flash of something antlered and ancient, and the breach behind him shuddered with every change.

"You're supposed to be in bed," he managed.

"So are you."

"Touché." He coughed, and the cough produced petals. Actual petals. "The breach is failing. I can't — I've been paying in installments and I'm nearly out of coin. You need to join the stones *now*, at the root, or the whole seam goes and this city learns what it feels like to live inside a garden's stomach."

"The root's in the keep. Dain's keep."

"Then run, Brightwater." He tried to grin. It was the worst grin she had ever seen. "And I'll just — hold the door. As one does."

"No." She lowered him gently against a fallen lamppost-turned-cherry-tree, and the blossoms gathered over him like a blanket, like the garden itself acknowledging his payment. "No, you'll die."

"Sable." He caught her wrist. His eyes were doing the thing they did, gold going amber going firelight, and for once there was no banter anywhere in them. "Twelve years you hid. Tonight the whole sky is open. Everything's breaking anyway. Let it break toward something." He pressed something into her hand — a fox-tooth on a cord, worn smooth. "For luck. Go make my grandmother proud. She liked you, you know. Said Brightwaters were the only mortals worth the trouble."

Sable stood. Behind her the breach screamed and shimmered; before her, the keep blazed with green fire that consumed nothing and grew everything; against her chest, the three oath-stones sang the opening line of an oath she had sworn never to speak again.

Wren appeared at her shoulder, breathless, the prophecy book open in her hands. Its final page was writing itself in letters of moving ink, and Sable read it upside down in a single glance, and her blood went cold.

*"When the veil breaks and the stones are joined, the speaker must speak the words she swore away — or all that breaks stays broken."*

"That's you," Wren whispered. "That's tonight. That's *you*."

Above them, the rip in the sky widened another yard, and through it came a sound like an entire realm inhaling.

Sable ran toward the keep, toward the dying root, toward the words.

Behind her, Tam Vess stood up, planted his feet, spread his arms, and became the door.
