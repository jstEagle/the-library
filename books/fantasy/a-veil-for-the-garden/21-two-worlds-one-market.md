## 21. Two Worlds, One Market

The night market smelled different now.

A year ago it had been tallow smoke, wet stone, the sour-sweet rot of overripe pears that no one could afford to waste. Now, beneath all of that — not replacing it but woven through it like a bright thread through grey cloth — came the green breath of Virelai. Honeysuckle and rivermint and something deeper, something loamy and alive, the scent of ground that had never been broken by anything harsher than a spade. Sable caught it every time she crossed under the old fish-arch at the mouth of Lantern Row, and every time it did the same thing to her chest: a slow unclenching she had stopped being embarrassed about somewhere around midwinter.

She walked the row slowly tonight, as she always did on the anniversary's eve, letting the stalls wash past her in their noisy abundance. A woman with flour to her elbows sold honeycakes beside a Virelayi trader whose stall was nothing but fruit — pomegranates the size of fists, their skins flushed violet where the light touched them, apples that glowed faintly from within like coals banked for morning. Nobody haggled hard anymore over the Virelayi goods. The traders set fair prices and mortals paid them, partly out of gratitude, partly because everyone remembered what hunger had tasted like two winters ago when the seasons had begun to fail, and nobody wanted to taste it again.

"Charms for copper," said a voice to her left, warm with teasing. "Charms for copper, lady? Honest charms, honestly priced."

Sable turned. A boy of perhaps fifteen — Ferren, who had been eleven and starving when she'd first healed his frostbitten fingers in the square below the torn sky — sat behind a folding table hung with knotted cords and painted river-stones and little glass vials of Virelayi dew. Her own old trade, taken up by someone else, done openly, done in daylight-darkness under the lanterns without one flicker of fear.

"You're underselling me," she said gravely. "I charged double for the dew."

"I charge what the dew costs," Ferren said. "That's the difference between us, Mistress Brightwater. You were hiding. I'm selling." He held up a vial so the lanternlight caught it, gold-green and slow-swirling. "Besides, you've got better things to do than corner your own market. Word is you're wanted at the archive before midnight."

"Word is fast these days."

"Word has legs now." He nodded toward the far end of the row, where a lean figure with fox-red hair was threading through the crowd with a leather satchel slung across his back, moving with that particular gait Tam had, half stroll and half prowl, as though the ground were a thing he had agreed to cooperate with rather than obey. "Your courier came through an hour ago asking after you."

Tam reached them and leaned against the stall's post as if he'd been holding it up all evening. Up close he smelled of both worlds too — woodsmoke and pine sap, city and forest, the particular mingled scent of a man who spent his days crossing a threshold most people still approached with held breath.

"Mistress Brightwater," he said, with the exaggerated formality he'd never quite dropped even after a year of shared bread and shared danger and one memorable night of shared grief on the anniversary of a battle neither of them had expected to survive. "I have letters."

"You always have letters."

"I have *interesting* letters." He pulled the satchel around and drew out a small stack, tapping the pages square against the table. "One from the orchard wardens — Ilex sends her regards, which is how Ilex says *I am pleased and will deny it later*. One from the ferry-folk at the north crossing about the toll dispute, which is boring, so we'll skip it. And—" his voice gentled by a degree only Sable would have caught, "—one that isn't a letter at all. It's an invitation."

He handed it over. The paper was Virelayi stock, pale as birch bark, and the writing on it shifted faintly even as she read, ink settling and resettling like silt in clear water. *To the Swordspeaker,* it began, and beneath that, an old-fashioned formal request: the council of the borderlands requested her presence at the threshold on the morrow, the anniversary, for the annual Remeeting.

"They ask every year," Sable said. "And every year I say I'll come if I can."

"And this year?"

Sable looked down the length of Lantern Row. At Ferren's charm stall, and the fruit-glow beyond it, and the mortal children daring each other to touch a Virelayi pear while its trader laughed at them. At a knot of city guardsmen off duty, buying honeycakes, one of them wearing a knotted cord charm exactly like the ones Sable used to sell in fear and now saw on half the wrists in Corvane, worn the way people wore saints' medallions — not as magic, exactly, but as memory. As proof of what had been survived.

"This year I'll walk through with you," she said. "On the hour. Properly. No slipping through side-paths at dawn like something ashamed."

Tam's grin was quick and sharp and, for just a moment, younger than his years. "The bridge herself, walking the bridge. What will they think of next."

"Don't call me that in the market, they'll start charging me tolls."

"The tolls are free for bridges. It's in the accord. Article four, I believe, right after the part where foxes get unlimited pastry." He plucked a honeycake off Ferren's table and left a coin in its place before the boy could object, and bit into it walking, and talked with his mouth full the way he always did when he was happy. "Wren's waiting for you, by the way. She says — hold on, I memorized it — *tell her the book is doing it again, and tell her to hurry, and bring nothing breakable except yourself.*"

* * *

The Corvane Archive had been a granary once, then a courthouse, then a place where inconvenient documents went to be forgotten. Now it was the busiest building in the city that wasn't a market or a garden, and Wren Halloway ruled it the way she did everything: thoroughly, anxiously, and with ink permanently staining the side of her right hand.

Sable found her in the west reading room, standing over a table on which lay a book bound in grey-green leather, open, its pages trembling very slightly — the way a bird trembles, not with cold but with the effort of staying still.

"There you are," Wren said, without looking up. Her voice had the tight brightness Sable had learned meant she was frightened and refusing to be. "Come look at this and tell me I'm not mad."

Sable came and looked.

The book was new — new-made, new-written, barely six months old. It had appeared in the archive the way the prophecy book had once appeared in Wren's hands: unbidden, unexplained, delivered by no courier anyone could name. But where the old book had written itself *backward*, recording a century of dread toward one terrible fixed date, this one wrote forward. It recorded the covenant's reforging. It recorded the veil's mending. It recorded, in careful flowing script, small ordinary things — the opening of the market, the planting of the public gardens on the ruins of Dain's keep, the return of the geese to the river in spring.

And tonight, its newest page was writing itself while they watched.

The pen-strokes appeared one at a time, unhurried, as though a hand none of them could see were taking its time over every letter. Sable read the line as it grew:

*Tonight the Swordspeaker walks the threshold openly, and both worlds watch her come, and neither turns away.*

"That's tomorrow," Sable said quietly. "It hasn't happened yet."

"It's happening right now, in the future tense, which is worse," Wren said. She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment — an old habit, the gesture of a woman who had spent too many nights believing the worst thing books could do was sit still. When she lowered them, she was smiling shakily. "Do you know what I did when I found the first book, a year ago? I ran to you with it because I thought it was a death sentence. Ink and doom. Every word a wall coming down on schedule." She laid her stained fingers gently on the new page's margin, not quite touching the fresh ink. "And now look. It writes hope. Forward. Unwritten hours, turning into words."

"Maybe it writes what it sees," Sable said. "Not what must be."

"Maybe." Wren looked up at her, and behind the archivist's anxiety there was the other thing, the thing Sable had forgiven her for almost a year ago now, in the undercity, over Tam's wound — the guilt that Wren carried and probably would carry always, the memory of leading Dain's men to the bell tower without meaning to. "Either way, I'm going to archive every page of it properly this time. Catalogued. Preserved. Read aloud to schoolchildren, eventually, if I have anything to say about it. Books should be allowed to mean good things too."

The ink finished its line, hesitated, and then added one word more, small and almost shy:

*Hurry.*

Wren laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her. "Even the book thinks you're late."

* * *

She went alone to the orchard.

It was not strictly necessary; the oath-stones were joined, the covenant whole, and no duty required her to cross early, ahead of the appointed hour, before the crowds and the ceremony and the Remeeting's formalities. But some visits wanted solitude, and this was one, and Tam knew it without being told — he'd squeezed her shoulder once at the threshold and said he'd hold the door, and that was all.

Virelai's borderlands opened around her as she passed the veil, the way they always did: not suddenly but *deepeningly*, the air thickening with green, the light going amber and patient, distances becoming suggestions rather than laws. The path to the orchard of names was worn now. That still astonished her. A year ago it had been a secret, a grief-path walked only by wardens and the dead. Now there was a footpath, trodden bare, lined with offerings — ribbons, cups of rainwater, a child's wooden horse placed carefully against a root.

The trees stood in their long rows, and inside each trunk, grown into the grain like a fossil in stone, was a name.

She found his tree the way she always did: her feet knew it now, better than her eyes did. Master Aldous Vane's oak — for the oaks took the oath-bound, the wardens said, because oaks remembered longest. His name curved across the heartwood at the height of her own chest, the letters soft-edged with a year of growth, the bark having begun its slow work of making the wound into simply wood.

"Hello, old man," Sable said.

She settled onto the ground beneath him, back against the trunk, and let the orchard's silence gather around her — not empty silence but full, the kind made of growing things. Above her a leaf unfolded, actually unfolding, slowly enough to watch.

Twelve years she had not said his name aloud. Then one impossible night she had said it — shouted it, wept it, confessed her cowardice beneath a collapsing sky — and the world had not ended. It had *mended*. She was still learning what to do with that. Some mornings she woke reaching for the old armor of silence, found it gone, and lay in the strange lightness of a body that had set down a weight it had carried so long it had mistaken the weight for bones.

"I met a girl in the market tonight," she told the tree. "Well — a boy. Ferren. Selling charms the way I used to, except honest about it. He'll never know what it cost to sell them the other way. Good. That's the point. That's the whole point, isn't it? That they get to not know."

The leaves moved overhead in a wind she couldn't feel at ground level. She had learned not to expect voices from the trees; the orchard was not a haunt, the dead here were not lingering souls but *kept names*, held safe, rooted, at rest. Whatever remained of Aldous Vane was in the grain of this wood and nowhere else, and that was enough. It was more than enough. It was the kindest theology she had ever encountered: that the dead do not wait for us, they simply remain, and our visiting is ours, and their peace is theirs.

But sometimes, sitting here, she heard the cadence of his teaching in her own thoughts — *the oath is not the words, girl, the oath is the choosing of them* — and chose not to question the mercy of that.

"I'm taking the vow seriously," she said. "The warden's vow. Tending. I know I accepted it too fast — Ilex warned me, in her way, and I didn't listen, and now I know why she smiled when I said yes. It's not a chore, is it. It's not a debt. It's just... this. Sitting with the names. Making sure they're read." She rested her palm flat against the bark, over the curve of the *V* in his name. "I tend yours first. Every time. I don't think that's favoritism. I think it's order of operations."

Far off through the trees, a bell rang — Corvane's great bell, the one in the tower where her order had once trained and bled and died, recast last spring with the old sigil worked into the crown. The sound crossed the veil the way everything crossed the veil now, softened but undiminished, arriving like news from a neighbor rather than a warning from a fortress.

The Remeeting hour was coming.

Sable rose, brushed the loam from her cloak, and stood a moment longer with her hand on the oak. "Walk with me tomorrow," she said, which was not a prayer, exactly, and not nonsense either. Names kept in living wood were not so far from anywhere. "You'd like it. There are pears."

Then she turned down the path, and the borderlands parted before her, and she felt the veil approach — her veil, hers now in a way no one else's could ever be, the place where her roots crossed. It shimmered ahead like heat above a road, and through it she could see the lights of Lantern Row, and figures gathering at the threshold on the mortal side with lanterns of their own, hundreds of them, come to watch the Swordspeaker walk home from one world into the other.

Both worlds watching her come. Neither turning away.

She lifted her hand toward the shimmer — and stopped.

Because the veil was rippling. Not the gentle breathing motion it had settled into over the year, the slow tidal swell of a boundary at peace. This was different. A tremor running through it end to end, quick and wrong, like a shiver through the flank of a sleeping animal — and in the heart of the shimmer, for half a heartbeat, Sable saw something that had no business existing in a mended world.

A thin dark line, like a seam. Like a stitch pulling loose.

Then it was gone, and the veil breathed smooth again, and somewhere behind her, deep in the orchard of names, every tree rustled at once — though there was no wind in Virelai at all.
