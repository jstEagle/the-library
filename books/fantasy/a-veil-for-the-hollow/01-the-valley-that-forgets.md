## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The thyme came up wrong that morning, and Marguerite Ishikawa noticed before she knew why.

She knelt in the garden behind the cottage, fingers buried in the dark loam, and studied the row she'd planted in April. The thyme had always been her best crop. In the Hollow Valley, where the soil was patient and the rain fell on schedule and nothing—not one thing—had gone catastrophically wrong in fifteen years, her herbs grew like they had somewhere to be. But this morning the thyme had come up gray at the tips, as though each tiny leaf had been dipped in ash and dried.

She rubbed a sprig between her fingers and smelled it. Still thyme. Still good. But the gray bothered her, the way a splinter bothers you—not enough to stop, just enough to remind you that your body is not entirely your own country.

"Overthinking," she said aloud, to the garden, to the valley, to the habit of fifteen years. Talking to plants was the kind of thing that got a woman called a witch in some places. Here it just got her called Marguerite.

The valley did not talk back. That was the whole point of the valley.

She sat back on her heels and looked up, the way she did every morning without deciding to, the way a person checks a scar. The sky over the Hollow was a soft, unremarkable blue, streaked with the kind of harmless clouds that look painted on by someone being paid by the cloud. No thunderheads massing on the eastern ridge. No pressure in her back teeth. No charge in the air raising the fine hairs on her arms.

There had never been, not once, not in fifteen years.

Lightning did not come to the Hollow Valley. That was not weather; that was law.

* * *

She had learned the law the hard way, which was the only way she learned anything. Fifteen years ago, she had walked down out of the Storm Reaches with her name in her mouth and her hands empty, and at the valley's western boundary the mist had risen up like a wall of held breath, and she had stepped through it, and everything she was had stayed behind.

Not everything. Her memories came. Her hands came. Her heartbeat came, stubborn and human and entirely unremarkable. But the other thing—the thing she had no single word for, the thing the storm-folk called *the calling* and she had learned to call *nothing, I'm nobody, I never was*—that had stayed on the far side of the mist like a coat checked at a door she was never getting back into.

She remembered the exact moment. She had been halfway through the veil when the first distant thunder rolled over the ridge behind her, and she had felt it pass through her body like a rumor passing through a crowd, touching everyone, meaning nothing. Fifteen years of storms had lived in her spine, and in one step they had become weather. Just weather. Something that happened to her instead of something that happened because of her.

She had cried for three days in a rented room in the village of Hollow's Rest, and then she had stopped, because grief is a crop and if you don't harvest it, it rots the field.

Now she had a cottage with a slate roof and a garden that fed them from March to November, and a man inside it who burned things.

* * *

Theo Vance burned things with the regularity of a liturgy.

Not on purpose. He was, in every other respect, a careful man—he mended fences before they needed mending, sharpened his own saws, kept accounts in a hand so neat the village clerk had once asked him to address the wedding invitations of a couple he'd never met. But put Theo Vance within six feet of a stove and something in the fundamental architecture of the universe loosened.

Tonight was their anniversary. Fifteen years since she'd come down out of the mountains with nothing, and twelve since a carpenter with flour on his sleeves had asked her to help him carry a table and then, halfway across the square, forgotten how to walk. Their anniversary dinner would be the same as every year: the good candles, the blue tablecloth, a stew that Theo would swear he was watching every second.

She had learned not to help. The first year, she had tried to hover, and he had shooed her out of the kitchen with a wooden spoon like a man defending a bridge. The stew was his country. She was permitted to admire it from abroad.

So she gave the gray-tipped thyme a suspicious look, promised it a conversation later, and went inside to wash the valley off her hands.

* * *

The cottage smelled like bread and smoke, which meant Theo had already begun. He stood at the stove with his back to her, sleeves rolled, stirring with the concentration of a man defusing something. He was broad through the shoulders in the way of men who have carried timber their whole lives, and his hair was going gray at the temples in a pattern she privately found unfair, because on him it looked distinguished and on her it was going to look like weather damage.

"You're hovering," he said, without turning.

"I'm admiring from abroad."

"That's a new one." He glanced over his shoulder. Flour on his jaw, somehow, though he hadn't baked. There was always flour on his jaw. She had stopped asking about it years ago, the way you stop asking a river where it gets its water. "How's the garden?"

"Traitorous."

"The thyme again?"

"The thyme again."

He turned down the flame and came to the window, drying his hands on his trousers, which was a habit she had given up correcting around year four. He looked out at the garden with her, his arm warm against hers, and for a moment they just stood there, two people looking at a row of herbs, which is one of the great uncelebrated pleasures of a long life together.

"Gray tips," he said.

"Gray tips."

"Could be the soil."

"It's not the soil."

"Could be ash from the kiln."

"The kiln's two miles east, Theo, and the wind's been out of the west for a week."

He was quiet a moment. She felt him deciding, the way she felt most of his decisions, not because she read minds—she couldn't, not anymore, not here—but because after twelve years you learn the weather of a person. "Could be nothing," he said.

"Could be nothing," she agreed, and they both let it go, because that was the arrangement. Fifteen years of nothing. It was a good country, Nothing. She was a citizen in good standing.

* * *

The afternoon went the way afternoons went. She cut lavender for the table because the blue cloth wanted company. She swept the step. She walked down to the village for salt and got waylaid at the baker's by old Marta Fenwick, who was of the firm opinion that the Henderson boy was courting the miller's daughter and that somebody ought to say something to somebody, and Marguerite agreed that somebody ought, and neither of them said anything, and it was a perfect conversation.

The valley did this. The valley made days that fit inside themselves.

She had lived other kinds of days. She had lived days that started with the taste of copper in the air and ended with cities of glass and thunder, with beings whose voices came out of cloud banks, with a mother whose word could flatten a mountain's weather for a season. She had lived days where the sky was not a ceiling but a parliament, and every storm was a vote, and her family had held seats.

Here, the sky was a sky. Here, weather was not a decision anyone had made. It simply happened, indifferent and clean, and there were mornings when she woke and felt that indifference like a mercy, and other mornings—fewer now, each year fewer—when she felt it like a missing limb.

The valley forgot for her. That was the bargain she'd struck. The Hollow's founders had built it long before her time, a pocket of the world walled off from the realms, a place where magic could not follow, could not enter, could not so much as knock. Whatever she had been, the valley had agreed to forget it, and in exchange she had agreed to be only what she could carry through the mist on her own two feet.

It was, she thought, walking home with her salt, the best trade she had ever made or ever would.

She was almost to the gate when the tremor came.

* * *

It was not an earthquake. The ground did not move. Her feet stayed planted on the packed dirt of the lane, and the fence posts stayed vertical, and the salt in her bag stayed salt.

But something moved. Somewhere beneath the world, or behind it, or in the space between one second and the next, something had shifted its weight, the way a great animal shifts in a dark room, and the floor of everything had creaked.

And she heard—*felt*—a word.

Not in her ears. Her ears had heard only the lane and the wind and, distantly, Marta Fenwick still talking to somebody about the Henderson boy. The word came from underneath hearing, the way a voice comes to you through a wall of water: muffled, bent, half-dissolved. One word, maybe two. It had the shape of a name, or a date, or a sentence she had once known and lost.

She stood in the lane with the salt bag against her chest and her heart going like a bird in a closed room, and she waited for it to come again.

It did not come again.

The lane went on being a lane. A cart rattled past; the driver nodded. The wind moved the lavender by her gate, and the smell of it reached her, calm and purple and ordinary. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Nothing," she said.

The word she had almost heard had not felt like nothing. It had felt like the first syllable of something that intended to finish itself.

But the valley did not talk back. That was the law. She had paid fifteen years of her life on that law, had built a whole self on the far side of it, and laws like that do not break for one gray row of thyme.

She went up the path, hung her shawl on its hook, and did not tell Theo, because there was nothing to tell. You cannot report a word spoken underwater. You cannot show someone the shape of a thing you only almost heard.

* * *

By the time the light went long and gold across the garden, the cottage was ready. The blue cloth lay smooth on the table. The lavender stood in its jar. Two of the good candles waited in their holders, unlit, patient as everything else in the valley. The stew—Theo's stew, his twelfth annual stew—sat covered on the stove, and the smell of it had reached the garden and was negotiating with the lavender for control of the evening air.

Marguerite stood at the window with a cup of tea going cool in her hands and watched the light come down the eastern ridge, and she did the thing she did every year on this evening: she counted what she had.

A roof that didn't leak. A garden, mostly loyal. A man in the next room humming to himself, off-key, unbothered, flour on his jaw. Fifteen years of sky that had never once cracked open. Twelve years of a table carried across a square by a man who forgot how to walk.

The valley had forgotten her, and in exchange it had given her everything a person could hold.

She raised her cup slightly, to the window, to the ridge, to the whole patient, quiet, magic-deaf valley, in a small private toast she never said out loud.

*To fifteen years of nothing,* she thought. *May there be forty more.*

Behind her, in the next room, Theo struck a match to light the candles, and the first flame stood up straight and gold and still.

The second flame bent.

It leaned, all at once, both of them, both candles, the way grass leans when something enormous passes through a field on the far side of a hill—bending toward the window, toward the west, toward the boundary and the mist and everything she had left on the other side of it—and held there, trembling, in a room where no air was moving at all.

Marguerite set down her tea.

The cup did not rattle. Her hands did not shake. Fifteen years of practice kept her voice level when she called into the next room, light and warm and entirely unconvinced:

"Theo. Your stew's burning."

But she was already at the window, looking west, at a sky that had never once cracked with lightning, waiting to see if it was about to start.
