## 2. Dinner and a Date with Destiny

The stew was dying, and Theo Vance was letting it happen.

He knew it was dying. He could smell it dying — a slow, sweet, caramelized surrender that had tipped, somewhere in the last ten minutes, from *richly browned* into *actively carbonized*. And still he stood at Marguerite's stove, wooden spoon in hand, watching the bottom of the pot turn the color of old regret, because the alternative was to admit that the most important dinner of his life was a casualty of his own attention span.

In his defense, there was a lot to think about.

The ring was in his left pocket. It had been in his left pocket for eleven days, riding against his thigh through every shift at the mill, every walk home, every evening on the porch where Marguerite read and he pretended to read and mostly watched her lips move over the words. Eleven days of carrying a small velvet weight and a larger invisible one, and tonight was the night, because tonight was the anniversary — three years to the day since she'd appeared at the valley's spring festival like a woman stepping out of a painting, asked him where a person could buy honey that wasn't terrible, and then looked at him with those gray eyes and said, "You're the least terrible thing in this valley, Theo Vance," which he had since learned was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

Three years. He'd rehearsed the speech in the mill, in the bath, in the space between waking and sleep. He'd rehearsed it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones, and now, standing in her kitchen with the stew going to charcoal behind him, he could not remember a single one.

"Theo?" Marguerite's voice came from the garden, muffled by the open window and the particular way she said his name, like she was tasting it. "If you're burning something, I can smell it from the rosemary."

"I'm not burning anything," he called back. "I'm *finishing* it."

A pause. "Finishing it into what?"

"A texture."

Her laugh drifted through the window, and the sound did what it always did — loosened something in his chest that had been wound tight since morning. He turned down the flame, which was a lie of a gesture, since the damage was geological at this point, and pulled the pot off the heat entirely. The kitchen smelled like a campfire that had ambitions.

He surveyed the room. Her cottage — *their* cottage, he was allowed to say that now, three years and most of his clothes migrated over — was small and warm and stubbornly itself. Herbs hanging in bundles from the rafters, going brittle and gold. Shelves of jars labeled in her precise hand: *feverfew, yarrow, something that bites back — don't.* Books everywhere, stacked in towers that defied several principles of engineering, and on the highest shelf, the one book that never gathered dust even though it never opened: her grandmother's, bound in leather gone dark as bark, kept behind a little curtain of embroidered cloth like a birdcage covered at night.

He'd asked about it once, early on. She'd said, "It's blank," in a voice that ended conversations, and he'd learned — slowly, the way you learn anything worth learning — that Marguerite's silences were not walls but rooms, and you waited at the door and eventually she opened it herself.

Mostly. There were rooms he suspected he'd never see. A woman didn't get exiled to a valley where the maps went vague without carrying a few locked doors with her.

He set the table. Two plates, the good ones with the blue rim that she'd bought at market and claimed were "for guests," a joke that had aged into an endearment, since he was the only guest the table had ever held. Two glasses. The candle — beeswax, from old Widow Marsh, who made them in batches and refused to sell them to anyone Theo introduced as "my girl," on the grounds that it made her feel complicit.

The ring shifted in his pocket when he bent to light the wick, and his heart did its now-familiar stutter, the one it had been doing for eleven days, the one he suspected was going to kill him before Marguerite ever got the chance to answer.

*Say it simple,* he told himself, for the hundredth time. *No speech. Speeches are how you burn stew.*

The door opened, and there she was.

Marguerite came in the way weather comes in — not dramatically, but with a change in pressure you felt before you understood. Garden dirt to the elbows, a sprig of thyme tucked behind one ear where she'd forgotten it, hair escaping its braid in the way that meant she'd been working with her hands and forgetting the rest of her head existed. She stopped in the doorway, sniffed once, and looked at him with an expression of profound and loving judgment.

"Theo."

"It's rustic."

"It's *ruined.*"

"It's *caramelized.*"

She crossed the kitchen, took the pot from the counter, and peered into it with the gravity of a physician examining a terminal patient. "There is a layer at the bottom of this pot that may qualify as a mineral."

"That's where the flavor lives."

"The flavor is dead, Theo. We're here to honor it." But she was smiling, that crooked smile she tried to hide and never quite managed, and she kissed him — quick, tasting of thyme and evening air — and went to the basin to scrub the garden off her arms, talking over her shoulder the way she did when she was happy, a low running commentary on the state of the beans and the audacity of the slugs and the fact that Widow Marsh had asked, again, when they were going to "make it official," and hadn't that been funny, Marguerite had told her that they were official the way a stone is official, and —

Theo's hand went to his pocket.

— and Marguerite kept talking, and the candle flame stood straight up in the still air, and Theo thought, *now, do it now, before the stew gets any deader, before you lose your nerve to the eleven-day weight of it,* and his mouth, which had never once in his life cooperated with his plans at an important moment, opened and said:

"Marry me."

Not the speech. Not even a sentence. Just the two words, dropped on the kitchen floor like a dropped plate, and then silence, and Marguerite turned off the water, and the silence got bigger.

She turned around. Her face did something complicated — surprise first, genuine, her eyebrows up, and then something softer moving underneath it, and Theo's whole body was a held breath. Eleven days of rehearsal, and the speech was ash, and all he had was the truth, so he gave it the truth.

"I had a whole thing," he said. "There was a speech. It had, I think, a metaphor in it, something about weather, which in hindsight was maybe not — anyway. The stew burned because I kept practicing instead of stirring, which is the most me sentence I've ever produced, and the ring has been in my pocket for eleven days and I've washed these pants twice and had to move it, and I'm doing this badly, Marguerite, I know I'm doing this badly, but I love you the way I do everything else, which is clumsily and completely, and I can't imagine a single ordinary day — the dishes, the market, the beans, the slugs — that I want to have without you in it. So. Marry me. Please. I'll even learn to cook."

The silence held for three more heartbeats.

Then Marguerite laughed.

It came out of her the way her real laughs always did, from somewhere deep, doubling her over, one dirty hand pressed to her mouth, and she laughed so long and so hard that Theo stopped being nervous and started being offended, and opened his mouth to say so, and she crossed the kitchen and kissed him mid-sentence, which was her signature move and his favorite thing in the world.

"Yes," she said against his mouth. "Yes, you ridiculous man. Obviously yes. I've been waiting three years for you to ask, and you chose the evening you committed arson against a root vegetable."

"The stew was a deliberate test of your devotion."

"You're going to make me eat that stew, aren't you. That's the real proposal. Marriage and a punishment in one."

"Scraping is a love language." He was grinning so hard his face hurt. He fumbled the ring out of his pocket — it caught on the lining, because of course it did, because he was doing everything badly tonight and it didn't matter, none of it mattered, she'd said yes — and took her hand, the left one, still damp from the basin, garden dirt in the creases of her knuckles, and slid the ring on.

It fit. He'd guessed. He'd measured her hand against his own one night while she slept and then spent a week in Bramble's back shop pretending to be interested in a fishing lure he had no intention of buying while Bramble's granddaughter, who did the metalwork, smirked at him the entire time.

Marguerite looked at the ring. Turned her hand in the candlelight. And for a moment — just a moment, so brief he'd spend the rest of his life wondering if he'd imagined it — her smile flickered. Not dimmed, exactly. *Paused.* Like a woman hearing a sound in another room.

"Marguerite?"

"Nothing." The smile came back, full and warm, and she kissed him again, and this one was longer, slower, the kind that made the stew's fate entirely irrelevant. "It's beautiful. You're beautiful. We're engaged. I'm going to feed us charcoal and we're going to toast with the plum wine Widow Marsh pretends she doesn't distill herself, and tomorrow we'll tell everyone, and it'll be terrible, everyone will cry, Bramble will say something about how he knew it in the spring festival three years ago because he knows everything—"

"—because he knows everything," Theo agreed, and went to the shelf for the wine.

That was when the candles moved.

There were two of them — the beeswax on the table and the stub of tallow by the sink she used for mending. Two flames, two feet apart, and Theo was watching them, or half-watching, reaching for the bottle, when they *leaned.*

Both of them. At the same time. The same angle — a smooth, synchronized tilt, like grass bending under a wind he couldn't feel on his own face. Toward the window. Toward the garden, and the dark beyond it.

He stood there with the bottle in his hand and waited for the draft. The window was open, sure, but the evening was dead still, the kind of summer hush where even the aspens on the ridge stood motionless, and a draft would have come *in* and bent the flames *away* from the window, not — whatever this was. Not this slow, deliberate lean, like the flames were listening. Like something outside had said a word and both fires had turned their heads.

Then it passed, and the flames stood straight again, and the kitchen was a kitchen.

"Theo?" Marguerite had gone still by the table, the wineglass halfway to her lips. She was looking at the candles. She had been looking at the candles. He realized, with a small cold shift in his stomach, that she'd stopped talking mid-sentence — he hadn't even noticed, he'd been so busy with the bottle — and that her face had gone carefully, deliberately blank, the way it went when she was standing in front of one of her locked rooms.

"The candles," he said. "Did you see—"

"Draft," she said.

"From where? There's no wind. Look outside — there's not even—"

"It's a valley, Theo. Air does strange things in a valley." She set the glass down. Her hand was steady. That was the thing he noticed, the thing that made the cold shift deepen instead of fade: her hand was *too* steady, the steadiness of a woman holding herself still on purpose. "Pour the wine. The stew is beyond saving and I refuse to mourn it sober."

He poured. He sat. He made a joke about the stew's mineral layer, and she laughed, but it was the laugh she used at market, the public one, and her eyes kept going — just flicking, quick as a tongue checking a chipped tooth — to the window, to the dark garden, to the shelf where the curtained book sat behind its embroidery like something sleeping.

They ate the burnt stew, because she'd been right, he was going to make her eat it, and it tasted like a campfire's ambitions and they toasted anyway, clinking glasses over the ruins, *to us,* and she said it back, *to us,* and she was smiling, and her left hand lay on the table with the ring catching the light, and Theo held on to all of it — the ring, the yes, the terrible stew, the whole warm ordinary evening — like a man holding on to a railing in a wind only he could feel.

Because he'd seen the candles bend. And he'd seen her face after.

And Marguerite Ishikawa, who had a room behind every silence, had looked at those two small flames leaning toward the dark like they'd been called by name — and for one unguarded instant, before she locked the door on it, she had looked *afraid.*

Outside, the summer night lay still and starless at the valley's rim, where the mountains went up like walls into a dark that had never, in all the years anyone could remember, been cracked by lightning.

Inside, Marguerite reached across the table and took his hand, the one without the ring, and held it hard enough to matter.

"Tell me about the speech," she said. "The one with the weather in it."

And Theo, who loved her clumsily and completely, began to tell her — and did not notice that her eyes had gone, once more, to the window, where the dark pressed against the glass like a listener leaning in.
