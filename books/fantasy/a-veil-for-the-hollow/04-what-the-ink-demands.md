## 4. What the Ink Demands

The stew was ruined. I want that on the record, somewhere, in some cosmic ledger: the stew was ruined, and Marguerite ate it anyway, and she laughed at my joke about the carrots having achieved a texture previously unknown to science, and for about forty minutes everything in the world was fine.

Then we went to the shelf.

I should back up. After the candles — after the flames all leaned toward the window like iron filings snapping to a magnet, all nine of them at once, silent as a held breath — Marguerite had gone very still in her chair. Not scared-still. Worse than that. *Recognizing*-still. The look of a woman hearing a voice she'd spent fifteen years pretending she couldn't hear.

"Theo," she'd said. "Go get the green book."

"The green book."

"Third shelf. Grandmother's. It's sealed with wax and it has been blank since before I could read, and if you say another word about the carrots I will lose my mind."

So I got the book. And now here's what I knew about the green book going in: nothing, except that Marguerite touched it maybe once a year, dusted it, and put it back with the particular gentleness people reserve for graves.

It was heavier than it looked. Old leather gone soft as cloth, spine cracked in three places, a blob of dark wax sealing the cover shut like a wound that had been allowed to close. The wax was unbroken. I remember noticing that. I remember thinking, *good, it's still sealed, whatever she's afraid of is still locked up.*

Marguerite set it on the table between the ruined stew and the nine bent-back candles, and she pressed her thumb to the wax, and the wax split along a seam I swear hadn't existed a second earlier, opening like a mouth.

"Theo," she said quietly, "don't—"

But I was already reading over her shoulder. That's the thing you should know about me. Curiosity isn't a flaw of mine; it's the whole engine. Theo Vance, who moved to the Hollow Valley because the maps ran out of words there. Theo Vance, who fell in love with a woman who flinched at thunder even though this valley had never once produced any. I have never in my life left a door closed that someone else wanted closed, and I am telling you this so you understand that when the ink started moving, I didn't run. I leaned closer.

The page wasn't blank.

That was the first wrong thing. Marguerite made a sound I'd never heard from her — small, punched-out, like the air leaving a tire — because the page that had been empty for sixty years was full. Dense, slanted script in brown-black ink, filling the whole right-hand leaf, and on the left, a drawing.

A drawing of me.

Not flattering. Not cruel. Just *accurate* — the crooked nose from the bike accident, the cowlick that never lies flat, the way my jaw sits slightly off-center when I'm not thinking about it. Drawn in fine confident strokes by a hand that had clearly looked at my face for a long time. My face, in a book older than my grandmother, sealed in wax, in a house I helped build.

"That's—" I started.

"I know," Marguerite said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it does when she's holding something down. "That's impossible."

The entry beside my face began mid-sentence, as though whoever wrote it had been interrupted decades ago and had just now picked the pen back up:

*...and on the eve of their ninth year, when the candles bend and the sky remembers its temper, the bridge will be found among them —*

And then, while we watched, the pen-strokes kept coming. There was no pen. There was no hand. The ink simply surfaced through the paper the way frost surfaces on glass, letter by letter, each one arriving wet and glistening and then drying to brown-black, and I stood there with my arm around my almost-fiancée watching a dead woman's book finish its sentence about me.

"—the bridge," the page said, "that must burn."

* * *

Here's what I did, in order.

First, I laughed. Short, ugly, involuntary — the laugh your body produces when it needs to buy your brain three more seconds. "Okay," I said. "Okay. So either this is the world's most committed prank, or we've got mice with calligraphy skills, or—"

"Theo."

"—or somebody broke in, which, honestly, would be a relief, because a burglar I can report to—"

"*Theo.*"

She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the page, and her face had done something I'd never seen it do in nine years: it had gone somewhere without me. Behind her eyes was a country I didn't have a visa for.

"It's real," she said. "The writing. It's — this is grandmother's hand. This is exactly her hand." She turned a page with two fingers, gingerly, like the paper might bite. Blank. Another. Blank. "Only this page. Only tonight."

"So it's a message." I heard my own voice go bright and reasonable, the customer-service register I use when I'm terrified. "Messages have senders. Senders can be found. We find the sender, we ask them very politely to stop writing horror novels about—"

"About you." She finally looked at me, and I wished she hadn't, because her eyes were wet and furious and frightened all at once, and none of those things were aimed where I wanted them. "It says *burn*, Theo. It doesn't say visit. It doesn't say change. It says the bridge that must *burn*."

"Bridges don't burn, they get burned. Big difference. Legal distinction. I'd want a lawyer present before I agreed to any combustion whatsoever—"

"Stop joking."

The words came out cracked down the middle, and they stopped me cold, because Marguerite has never once in nine years asked me to stop joking. Joking is our native language. We built the whole house out of it. And she was looking at me now like every joke was a match held near kindling.

I stopped.

* * *

We sat across from each other with the book between us like a third guest nobody invited. The candles had straightened themselves out at some point — or been straightened — and their flames stood perfectly vertical again, which somehow felt less natural than the bending had.

"Tell me what you know," I said.

"I told you. Magic doesn't work here. That's the whole point of the valley. Whatever this is, it shouldn't be able to—"

"Not that. You went somewhere just now. When you saw the handwriting. You went somewhere I've never been invited, and you came back with a face like that, and you're going to sit there and tell me the only relevant fact is that magic doesn't work here?" I kept my voice level. Mostly. "Maggie. Nine years. You owe me better than the brochure."

Something moved behind her expression — hurt, maybe, or gratitude, or the specific exhaustion of a person whose hiding place has just been named out loud. "There are things about why I'm here," she said slowly, "that I promised myself I'd take to my grave. Because taking them to my grave was supposed to keep them away from *you*. That was the entire design."

"And how's the design holding up?"

She looked at the drawing of my face. She didn't answer.

"Right," I said. "So here's where I land. Somebody — something — wrote my name into a century-old book tonight. Wrote *burn* next to it. I'm in the story whether I consent to it or not, which means the only power I have left is information. I want all of it. Tonight. Even the parts you think will break me." I reached across the table and took her hand. Her fingers were ice. "Especially those parts."

"And if knowing it breaks us?"

"Then we're broken by truth instead of by fire. Better trade."

For a long moment she just breathed. Then she pulled her hand free — gently, but she pulled it free — and stood, and took the book off the table, and carried it to the hearth.

"Maggie. What are you doing."

"What I should have done an hour ago." She knelt at the fireplace, laid the book on the grate, and struck a match. "Some things you don't negotiate with. Some things you burn first and mourn later."

"You said magic doesn't work here."

"It's not supposed to." The match caught. She held it to the corner of the cover, and the flame licked at the old leather, and the leather did not char. It didn't blacken, didn't smoke, didn't so much as warm. The flame bent around the book like water around a stone, and then, with a sound like a sigh, it went out.

Marguerite struck another match. Same result. A third. The book sat in the grate glowing faintly at its edges with a light that wasn't firelight, a pale storm-colored shimmer, and beneath the shimmer I could see fresh ink surfacing on the exposed page — new letters, arriving, beginning to spell something that started with tomorrow's date.

She struck match after match until the box was empty, kneeling there in the darkening room, and the book just lay there being immortal, politely, patiently adding to my sentence.

"Maggie." I crossed the room and crouched beside her. Up close I could see she was shaking. "Hey. Look at me. It won't burn."

"I know it won't burn." Her voice was ash itself. "I know exactly one thing that won't burn, and it's sitting in my fireplace writing about my — writing about *you* — and I renounced everything, Theo, I gave up *everything* so that nothing like this could ever reach inside these walls, and it reached anyway, and I don't—" The sentence collapsed. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I need it to burn. Don't you understand? If it burns, it's a threat. Threats end. If it won't burn, it's a *plan*, and plans have authors, and I think I know whose hand that is, and if I'm right then this isn't a prophecy, it's a *letter*, and it's addressed to me, and it's using you as the—"

She stopped herself hard, jaw clenching around the rest of the sentence like a trapdoor slamming.

"Using me as the what?"

"Nothing. I can't. Not yet."

And there it was. Our first fracture. Small, hairline, running straight through the middle of the kitchen floor — me wanting the whole map, her willing to burn half of it rather than show me the territory marked *here be monsters, and also, your mother-in-law, possibly.* I understood, even then, that she wasn't lying to me. She was rationing. She thought love meant standing between me and the blast.

I thought love meant handing me the detonator.

"Tomorrow," she said finally. "Tomorrow I'll tell you everything. Who I was before the valley. What I gave up. Whose handwriting that is. I promise. But tonight I can't — I need one night where you're still just Theo and I'm still just me, before we become whatever that book says we are."

And because I loved her, and because I was terrified, and because those two things felt identical in my chest, I said yes.

I helped her lift the book out of the grate. It was cool to the touch, cooler than the room, and the new entry had finished itself while we weren't looking. I read it as she set it on the table, face-down, like closing a dead man's eyes.

*Tonight the ink demands what ink always demands: to be believed. Tomorrow, the frost begins.*

Outside, the summer night lay warm and utterly still over the Hollow Valley. No wind. No clouds. Nothing at all on the horizon except stars.

But I swear to you — I swear on the ruined stew and the bent candles and nine years of ordinary happiness — that as we stood there in the doorway, hand in frozen hand, the air coming down off the northern ridge had an edge to it. A thinness. The first breath of something vast and patient, rolling downhill toward us at the speed of winter.

Marguerite felt it too. I saw her feel it.

"Tomorrow," she said again, but this time it didn't sound like a promise.

It sounded like a deadline.
