## 5. Ash That Won't Catch

The fire pit behind the cottage was old work, older than me. Theo's grandfather had built it from river stones the color of old teeth, and the mortar between them had gone soft as bread, so that in summer the whole ring seemed to breathe when you touched it. I had burned things there before — failed tinctures, rosemary that had bolted bitter, letters I'd written and decided against sending. The pit took everything without complaint.

I carried the book out at dusk, wrapped in a dish towel like something sick.

Theo followed me to the door and stopped at the threshold, arms crossed, watching. He hadn't said much since we closed the book on the shelf. That was his way with fear — he folded it small and put it in a pocket, and then it leaked out of him sideways as jokes, or silence, or an unusual tenderness with the dishes. Tonight it was silence.

"You're sure about this," he said. Not really a question.

"It's paper," I said. "Paper burns."

"Sure." He leaned against the doorframe. "And I'm a storm-caller's grandson-in-law."

"You're not anything yet."

"I asked you last night over burnt stew. You laughed and then stared at candles like they owed you money. So technically I'm still waiting on an answer."

I looked back at him. His hair was doing the thing where it couldn't decide which way to fall, and there was still a smudge of ash on his knuckle from raking the stove, and I loved him with the specific, unglamorous love of someone who has watched another person burn rice and apologize to it. Last night, before the book, I would have said yes so fast the word would have tripped me on its way out.

Now the word sat in my chest like a stone in a shoe.

"Burn first," I said. "Propose after."

"That's backwards."

"Everything about us is backwards."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, because we both heard it again — faint, under the wind, that pressure in the air like a word spoken underwater. It came maybe once an hour now. Each time, the candle flames in the house bent toward the window, all together, like iron filings finding north.

I walked down to the pit and knelt.

The book was warm in my hands. Not fever-warm. Hearth-warm. The warmth of something comfortable, settled, *home*. My grandmother's seal — the pressed wax stamped with the sigil of the High Storm-Caller's line, three spirals and a broken line beneath them — had cracked along one edge years ago, but the wax itself held, and I broke it now with my thumbnail and felt the small violence of it all the way up my arm.

The pages fell open to yesterday's date on their own. Of course they did. They'd been falling open to it since I pulled the book down, the way water finds the low place.

*Marguerite Ishikawa takes down her grandmother's book.* Fresh ink, my own name in a hand that was almost mine but not quite — rounder, more patient, the handwriting of someone who had all the time in the world. *She reads what has been waiting for her.*

Below it, the drawing of Theo's face, rendered in fine ink lines, more accurate than any portrait I'd ever seen made of him. And below that, the entry continued past where it had stopped this morning:

*The bridge that must burn will ask her to marry him. She will say yes too late, and no too early, and both answers will be true.*

I read it twice. Then I fed the book to the fire.

Here is what should have happened: paper catches, ink darkens, pages curl into black lace and go to nothing. Here is what actually happened: the flames touched the cover and *stopped*. Not recoiled — stopped, the way a conversation stops when someone says the wrong name. The fire lay against the leather like a dog against a door it isn't allowed through. For three full seconds the book sat in the heart of the blaze, untouched, unburnt, while the fire went on burning around it, licking at the stones, climbing the kindling, doing everything except the one thing I had brought it here to do.

Then the flames bent toward the window of the house. All of them. Every tongue of fire in the pit leaned east, toward the cottage, in perfect unison, and held.

I picked the book out of the fire with my bare hands.

It wasn't hot. That was the worst part. I'd braced for pain and got nothing — just warm leather, cool air, the smell of hearth-smoke clinging to it like a coat. Behind me, Theo made a sound I'd never heard him make, low and involuntary, and I realized he'd come down off the step without either of us noticing, drawn by whatever the fire had done.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So it doesn't burn."

"It burns," I said. "It chooses not to."

"That's worse."

"Yes."

He crouched beside me at the pit's edge, close enough that our shoulders touched, and looked at the book lying open on my knees, serene among the embers, its pages stirring gently though there was no wind.

"My grandmother built that fire pit," he said. "You know that?"

"I know."

"She'd have opinions about this."

"Your grandmother thought I was a very nice young woman who talked to her herbs. She'd have had exactly one opinion: that we're both idiots standing too close to a fire."

He laughed — short, startled, real — and the laugh broke something loose in my chest, some knot of dread that had been tightening since the candles bent. Because that was the thing about Theo. He was ordinary in the way that rivers are ordinary. You forget, living beside one, how much it holds up.

"We could leave," he said. "Tonight. There's the coast road. Nobody in Hollow Valley checks the coast road after dark."

"And go where? Theo." I turned to look at him properly. "Magic can't follow me here. Do you understand what that means? It means this valley is the one place in the world where that book should be blank. Where whatever wrote my name in it should be *blind*. And instead—" I lifted the book a few inches, let it fall open to tomorrow's page. "Instead it's writing faster than I can read."

* * *

We didn't sleep. We sat at the kitchen table with every lamp lit, because neither of us wanted to say why, and the book lay between us like a guest who wouldn't leave.

At midnight, the entry for today finished itself while we watched. Ink surfaced through the paper the way frost surfaces through a window — not written so much as *arriving*, letters blooming pale and then darkening, in that same patient hand.

*She will try to destroy the book. The book will allow her to try.*

"The book will *allow* her," Theo read aloud. "It's narrating. It's narrating us like we're already done."

"Prophecies are always written like that. Past tense. As if the future were a place you'd already visited."

"Is yours?"

I should have lied. I opened my mouth to lie and what came out instead was the truth, fifteen years of it, in a voice I barely recognized as mine.

"When I left the storm realm, I made a trade. That's what exile is — it's not banishment, it's barter. I gave up the storm-calling. All of it. The lightning, the weather-sense, the voice that speaks to thunder. In exchange, the valley took me in and made me... nothing. Wonderfully nothing. A woman with a garden." I turned my hands over, palms up, calloused from the hoe, ink-stained from the ledgers I kept for the seed trade. "There is not one drop of magic in this body, Theo. There hasn't been for fifteen years. Magic cannot follow me across the boundary. That was the whole point. That was the *price*."

"So what's writing the book?"

"That," I said, "is the question that's going to keep us awake until dawn, so we may as well be honest: I don't know. Nothing should be able to see me here. Nothing should be able to reach me. Whatever is holding the pen is either breaking rules older than the valley—"

"Or it never had to follow you," Theo said slowly. "Because it was already inside."

The lamp flames stood very straight. Neither of us spoke.

My grandmother's book. Her seal. Her hand — or a hand shaped like hers, taught by hers, descended from hers. I had carried that book across the boundary myself, fifteen years ago, tucked inside a coat lined with salt and iron, certain that the valley's dead air would smother whatever lived in its pages. A sealed book in a magic-dead valley. I had thought of it as a fossil. A stone with a shape in it.

But fossils don't fall open to the right day.

"It came with me," I whispered. "It's been on my shelf for fifteen years. Blank. Sleeping. Waiting for—"

"For a date," Theo said.

For a date. For tonight. For the night a man asked a storm-caller's daughter to marry him over burnt stew, and the candles bent toward the window, and somewhere beyond the veil something turned a page.

* * *

Near dawn, I dozed at the table, cheek on my arms, and woke to grey light and Theo's hand on my shoulder and a sound I took a moment to identify: pages turning. By themselves. Softly, steadily, like breathing.

The book stood upright on the table — not fallen open, *standing*, propped on its spine like a mouth mid-word — and it was turning its own pages, backward through decades of blankness, forward again, and settling at last on tomorrow.

New ink. Still wet. Still shining.

I read it standing up, because sitting felt like surrender.

*Tomorrow, the first snow falls out of season. It will begin at the valley's edge, where the mist stands thinnest, and it will not melt. The frost will walk inward at the pace of a slow man, a foot every hour, and the villagers will call it bad luck, and they will be wrong.*

Beside the entry, a small drawing: the boundary mist, rendered in fine lines, and within the mist a figure. Tall. Made of nothing I could name. Watching.

Theo read it over my shoulder. I felt him go still, the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish.

"Snow," he said. "In high summer."

"In high summer," I agreed. "At the edge. Walking inward."

"Marguerite." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something heavy. "This is a forecast. It's giving us a day's warning. Why would it do that? If it wanted us dead, it would just — be tomorrow already."

And that was the question underneath all the questions, and I found, examining it in the cold morning light, that I had an answer. An ugly one. One I'd been avoiding since the candles bent.

"Because it wants us prepared," I said. "Because it wants us *willing*. A trap works best when the animal walks in on its own legs." I closed the book. It let me. That frightened me more than the fire had. "Whatever wrote this knows me, Theo. Knows my grandmother's hand, knows my exile's terms, knows the exact shape of the life I built here. This isn't a stranger hunting blind in the dark. This is family."

"Your mother."

I hadn't told him. Fifteen years, and I had given him everything except the one story that mattered — the High Storm-Caller, the arranged covenant-marriage, the daughter who chose a magic-dead valley over a throne of thunder. I had buried Seraphine alive in my telling, called her dead, grieved her in advance, because a dead mother asks nothing of you.

"Theo," I said, "there are things I have to tell you. About my mother. About why I really came here. And you're going to want to burn this book even more afterward, and I need you to know—" my voice did something humiliating, wavered, steadied, "—I need you to know that if you run, I'll understand. But if you stay, we do this together, and I am done being protected from things by people who love me."

Outside the window, the summer morning lay gold and innocent over the garden. At the valley's edge, miles away, invisible from here, the mist stood waiting.

Theo pulled out his chair, sat down across from me, and laced his fingers together on the table like a man preparing to hear a long story.

"Start at the beginning," he said. "And Marguerite — don't skip the parts you think will scare me."

The book, between us, was already growing tomorrow's page.
