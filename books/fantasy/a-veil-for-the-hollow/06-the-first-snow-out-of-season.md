## 6. The First Snow Out of Season

I was fixing the hinge on the garden gate when the first flake landed on my wrist.

I want to be clear about what kind of day it was, because I've had four months to turn it over and it still doesn't add up. It was the twenty-third of High Summer. The barley was knee-high and gold at the tips. Marguerite had been out since dawn in the wide straw hat she refused to admit looked ridiculous, arguing with her tomatoes — actually arguing, low and affectionate, the way she talks to living things she suspects are listening. The air smelled like warm dust and crushed mint. It was, by any measurement available to a man who fixes gates for a living, the hottest week of the year.

And a snowflake melted on my wrist, cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket.

"Huh," I said, which is more philosophy than I usually manage before noon.

I looked up. The sky was still blue. That was the part that got me, later — not the snow itself but the wrongness of it, the way it came out of a clear sky like something leaking through a wall instead of falling from one. No clouds gathering. No wind turning. Just flakes, sparse at first, drifting down with the unhurried patience of things that have all the time in the world.

"Marguerite," I called. "It's snowing."

She didn't look up from her tomatoes. "It is not."

"It really is."

She straightened, pushed the hat back, and turned her face up to the sky with an expression I'd never seen on her before. I've watched her look at frost-blighted seedlings, at flooded fields, at the burned loaf I once proudly presented as bread. She has a face for small disasters — wry, unbothered, already three moves ahead. This wasn't that. This was the face of someone hearing their own name spoken by a stranger in the dark.

One flake landed on her cheek and didn't melt.

That's when I stopped thinking of it as weather.

* * *

By midday the whole village had turned out at the eastern edge, where the fields give way to the old stone markers and beyond them the grey shimmer of the boundary mist that most of us pretend not to see, the way you pretend not to see the crack in your neighbor's marriage or the water stain spreading across your own ceiling. Snow lay along that line in a white seam maybe ten feet deep into the valley, melting even as it fell, so the ground wore a lace hem of slush that came and went like breath.

The mood, I want to stress, was almost festive. This was Hollow Valley's relationship with the impossible: we treated it like a traveling fair. Kids were catching flakes on their tongues. Old Marta Kessler had brought a thermos of cider and was dispensing it like a woman who'd been waiting forty years for an excuse. Denny Farrow was telling anyone who'd listen that he'd read about this happening in the northern ranges, some phenomenon with a name I'm fairly sure he invented on the spot.

"Summer snow," he said sagely. "Happens when the jet stream folds. My uncle saw it twice."

"Your uncle," said his wife, "once claimed to have seen a mermaid."

"She was in the lake, Renata. Where would you have her be?"

I laughed with everyone else. But I kept glancing at Marguerite, and Marguerite wasn't laughing. She stood a little apart from the crowd with her arms crossed, watching the snow-line the way a physician watches a wound — not the wound itself, but its edges. Measuring.

"How far in is it now?" I asked her quietly.

"This morning it was at the markers." Her voice was flat. Careful. "It's past the second fence row."

"That's what — eighty feet?"

"Closer to ninety." She paused. "Since dawn."

Something cold walked down my spine that had nothing to do with the snow. "Ninety feet in half a day. That's..."

"A foot an hour." She said it like she'd done the math a hundred times already, and knowing Marguerite, she had. "Give or take."

"And does it stop?"

She didn't answer. In the four years I'd loved her, she had never once declined to answer me. She answered everything — why her tea had to steep exactly seven minutes, why she wouldn't say certain words from her old life, why there were no pictures of her family anywhere in our house. Even when the answers were painful she gave them plainly, because withholding, I think, felt to her too much like lying and lying felt too much like home.

So when she didn't answer, I understood that the answer was no.

* * *

The council called a meeting that evening in the meetinghouse, because we are a village and that is what we do with fear — we schedule it. I sat in the back with Marguerite and listened to reasonable people propose unreasonable things.

Denny Farrow wanted to organize a work crew to haul fire braziers out to the boundary. "Heat defeats cold," he said, with the confidence of a man who had never met a winter he couldn't out-stubborn. "We stand it off like we'd stand off a flood."

"With what fuel?" asked Renata. "There's a foot of snow coming down out of a clear sky and you want to burn the county?"

Old Marta said it was a judgment, though she declined to specify on whom for what, on the grounds that naming names in a meetinghouse was bad manners. Someone suggested it was volcanic ash making the weather strange. Someone else said they'd heard the northern ranges had it worse. There was a long stretch where people talked about anything except the thing itself, which was this: the snow was not stopping, the frost line was not retreating, and every hour it advanced another foot into fields full of crops that could not survive it.

I watched Bramble sit through all of it. He's the oldest man in the valley — older than the meetinghouse, people joke, older than the stones. He sat in the front row with his hands folded on his cane and he did not propose braziers or judgments or jet streams. He just listened, and once, when the frost line was mentioned, he nodded slightly, like a man checking an item off a list he'd written long ago.

That nod frightened me more than the snow.

Afterward I caught him outside, under the eaves, while Marguerite was saying goodnights she didn't mean.

"Bramble. You knew. Didn't you? You weren't surprised in there."

He looked at me a long time. His eyes are pale as river ice and about as easy to read. Then he said, in that dry rusted-hinge voice of his:

"Surprise is for things without precedent, boy. Go home. Mind your garden."

He walked off into the falling snow, unhurried, his cane clicking like a slow clock. And here's the detail I haven't been able to shake: the flakes didn't seem to land on him. They swirled around his shoulders and went on down, as if he occupied a slightly different season than the rest of us.

* * *

At home, Marguerite stood at the window for a long time.

Our house sits high enough that from the bedroom window you can see the whole valley floor laid out silver-dark under the strange sky — the roofs, the fields, the thin white seam at the eastern edge glowing faintly, like a scar catching moonlight. She stood there with her arms wrapped around herself, and I came up behind her and put my hands over hers, and her fingers were freezing.

"Theo," she said.

"Say it. Whatever it is. Say it out loud."

For a moment I thought she would do what she'd done all day — measure, withhold, protect. I braced for the careful flat voice. Instead she exhaled, and her breath fogged the glass, and when she spoke it was barely above a whisper.

"There's a prophecy. An old one. About a winter that comes out of season and doesn't stop until—" She stopped. Started again. "My grandmother's book describes it. The long winter. It seals the valley. It seals both worlds. Nothing thaws after it, Theo. Not the fields, not the boundary, not—"

"Not us."

"Not anyone."

The fog of her breath faded from the glass. Beyond it the snow kept its patient inch-by-inch progress, and I thought about the foot an hour, and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. A valley is, what, six miles across? Three hundred-odd hours. Two weeks. Give or take a blizzard's worth of grace.

Two weeks of summer left in the world.

"Why didn't you tell me this morning?" I asked.

"Because I spent fifteen years building a life where prophecies were paper and weather was weather." Her voice cracked, just at the seam, and she pressed it flat again. "Because I wanted one more ordinary evening before it stopped being ordinary. Because I was afraid that if I said it aloud, it would be true, and I have run from true things before, Theo. I am very fast."

I turned her around to face me. Up close her eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely steady.

"You're not running now," I said.

"No," she agreed softly. "Now I have something worth standing still for." She looked past me, out at the advancing white, and her jaw set in a way I'd learn to know very well in the weeks to come — the set of a woman pricing what standing still might cost, and deciding to pay it. "Tomorrow I open my grandmother's book again. All of it. Every sealed page. And whatever it demands, we find out together."

"Together," I said.

"Together," she said. And then, quieter, almost to herself, watching the snow come down on the summer fields: "It shouldn't be able to reach us here. Whatever's writing that book, whatever sent this winter — magic can't follow me into the valley. That was the bargain. That was the whole point."

She fell silent, and I followed her gaze to the white seam creeping through the dark.

"So either the bargain broke," I said slowly, "or something found a way around it."

Marguerite didn't answer. But out at the valley's edge, the frost line moved another foot closer, patient as ink, and somewhere on our shelf a closed book waited with pages that were no longer blank.
