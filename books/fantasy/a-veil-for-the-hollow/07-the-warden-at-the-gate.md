## 7. The Warden at the Gate

I didn't sleep.

The book sat on the kitchen table all night where Theo had finally left it, facedown, like something that might bite if it watched him back. Twice I got up to look at it. Twice I made myself pour a glass of water instead and stand at the window, watching the dark line of the valley boundary a half mile off, where the mist wall rose every night the way it always had — pale, slow, indifferent. The veil. The reason I was alive and magic-less and, until three days ago, perfectly content.

By the time the sky went grey, I had convinced myself of several reasonable things. The book was old; old ink can smear into shapes. Prophecy is a parlor trick performed by people who want power over the frightened — my mother taught me that one herself, which should have been my first warning about my mother. And whatever was happening at the valley's edge, whatever the frost on the morning grass meant, it would still be there after breakfast, and breakfast would still be there after the frost, and Theo would wake up warm beside me, and ordinary life is a muscle you keep by using it.

So I used it. I lit the stove with a match, the way you do when fire obeys no one. I cracked four eggs into a bowl. I was whisking them when Theo came down the stairs with his hair pointing in three directions and his collar crooked, and he kissed the back of my neck and said, "If the world's ending, I want eggs first," and I laughed, because he'd read my mind, or because we've been married in every way but the word for so long that the distinction has stopped mattering.

Except now the word existed. He'd said it over burnt stew two nights ago — *marry me* — with a ring he'd clearly bought in a panic from the trader who passes through in autumn, and I'd been laughing when the candles bent toward the window, all nine of them at once, like nine small hands reaching for something on the other side of the glass.

The eggs were almost done when I felt it.

Not heard. Not saw. *Felt* — the same way I'd felt the tremor in the garden days ago, a word spoken underwater, pressure without sound. But this time it didn't pass. It settled over the whole valley like a hand laid flat on a table.

Theo looked up from buttering bread. "Marguerite?"

"Stay here," I said.

"That's never once worked."

"I know." I set down the spatula. "Come anyway."

* * *

The frost had stopped at the gate.

That was the first wrong thing. For two days the cold had been creeping inward from the boundary, a foot an hour by my measurements, patient as arithmetic — the long winter arriving the way the prophecies always said it would, not with a roar but with a ledger. This morning the frost lay across the fields in a wide white tide that ended in a clean line six paces from our garden fence. Beyond the line, the grass was green. Behind it, everything glittered, stiff and beautiful and dead.

And standing on the line itself, where the frost met the living grass, was the Warden.

It had a shape the way a column of smoke has a shape — suggestion more than anatomy. Tall. Draped. Its edges moved continuously, mist feeding into mist, so that looking at it was like trying to remember a dream while still inside the dream. It stood perhaps forty feet from us, and it did not come closer, and I understood before anyone told me that it couldn't. That this was the farthest into the valley it would ever be able to reach, and even this much was a violation it was paying for somehow; the mist of its body kept thinning near the ground and rebuilding, thinning and rebuilding, like breath.

"Theo," I said quietly. "That's the Warden."

"The Warden of what?"

"The covenant. The thing between the storm realms and this valley." My mouth was dry. "It keeps the terms."

"There are terms?"

"There were always terms. Nobody in the valley knows most of them because nobody in the valley was ever meant to need to." I took his hand because it seemed important, suddenly, that we be touching. "They're older than the town. Older than my family. My grandmother used to say the founders signed something they only half understood, and the storm realm signed something it understood completely."

The Warden had no face. It didn't need one. When it spoke, the voice came from everywhere the mist was, which meant it came from around us, behind us, inside our own ears — a voice like wind through a keyhole, thin and cold and precise.

*"Marguerite Ishikawa."*

My name in that voice went through me like a needle finding an old scar. Beside me, Theo's grip tightened.

"It knows you," he said.

"Everyone from over there knows my name," I said, and hated how steady I made myself sound. "That's rather the point of exile."

*"The bridge must come willingly."*

Nine words. The Warden said them and then simply stood there, mist folding and unfolding, and I realized that was all it had come to say. That was the message entire. Not a threat, not an explanation — a delivery. Somewhere beyond the veil, something had written a sentence, and the sentence had been handed to the oldest servant available, and the servant had carried it through the cold to a dead valley and a woman holding her lover's hand over a line of frost.

"No," I said.

The Warden didn't move. Of course it didn't move. But the quality of its attention changed, the way a room changes when someone turns to look at you.

"You heard me," I said, louder, stepping forward so that my toes nearly touched the frost line. "No. There is no bridge. Whatever your book says, whoever wrote those pages — there is no bridge, there's a man, and his name is Theo Vance, and he's a carpenter, and he burns stew, and he is not going anywhere with you or for you or because of you."

"Marguerite—" Theo started.

"Let me finish." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "Fifteen years ago I gave up everything I was so that this valley could keep forgetting me. I honored my end. I have lived inside your terms like a fish lives in water — I never even noticed them. So you tell whatever sent you: the covenant can come and collect from someone else. It will find this door locked."

The mist rippled. If a faceless thing can express patience, it did.

*"The bridge must come willingly,"* it said again, and this time I heard what I'd missed the first time — the weight sitting under *willingly*. Not a demand. A technicality. The covenant could not take him. It needed him to choose, which meant everything that followed — the book, the frost, the winter crawling toward our garden — was not force but persuasion. Pressure applied to a decision.

Someone had built a trap whose only mechanism was choice, and left it in my house.

"Whose words are you carrying?" I asked.

Silence. Mist folded into mist.

"Wardens carry messages. They don't author them." I had learned that at my mother's knee, along with everything else I'd spent fifteen years trying to unlearn. "So whose are these? Who wrote the book?"

*"The book writes itself,"* the Warden said, and there was — I would swear this later, arguing with myself at three in the morning — something almost like reluctance in the thin cold voice. *"As it has always written. As it must write while the covenant stands broken."*

Broken.

The word landed in my chest and stayed there. I heard Theo breathe in sharply beside me, heard him understand it a half-second after I did, or a half-second before — impossible to say. Broken covenants don't send Wardens to deliver ultimatums. Broken covenants send Wardens because the alternative to a repaired covenant is nothing at all, and nothing at all, in the arithmetic of realms, looks like a winter that does not stop.

"That's not possible," I said. "It held for four hundred years. It doesn't just—"

*"It is breaking,"* the Warden corrected, gently, which was worse. *"As ice breaks. Slowly, and then at once. You have seen the frost. You have measured its pace. You know."*

I did know. I had a notebook in the kitchen with hourly measurements in my own handwriting.

"Why him?" Theo asked.

He hadn't spoken since I'd started shouting, and his voice came out level, almost curious, which frightened me more than shouting would have. He stepped up beside me, close enough that his shoulder touched mine, and faced the Warden the way he faces a warped board he intends to make straight.

"Why me? Out of everyone in the valley. Everyone in the world. Why does your book pick a carpenter?"

*"Because the bridge is not chosen for strength,"* the Warden said. *"The bridge is chosen for the crossing. You stand between what she was and what she is. You have always stood there. The book merely noticed."*

"That's nonsense," I said.

"It isn't, though." Theo said it quietly, not to the Warden but to me. "Marguerite. You know it isn't."

"Don't."

"I'm the hinge. I've always been the hinge. You came here to be no one and I—" He stopped. Started again. "You told me once that you chose me because I never asked you to be anything. Do you know what that means from where I'm standing? It means you were waiting to see if love could exist without magic answering for it. I'm the experiment. Of course their book noticed."

The frost line gleamed between us and the mist. My eyes were burning and I refused, absolutely refused, to blink first.

"This changes nothing," I said to the Warden. "Your answer is still no."

*"Understood,"* said the Warden. And then, in the same breathless keyhole voice, it added the thing I will hear for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be:

*"Know also the terms of your own confinement, Marguerite Ishikawa, so that you may choose with full knowledge, as he must. The boundary that protects you will kill you if you cross it. The magic you renounced waits on the other side, and flesh that has forgotten magic cannot survive its return. To leave this valley is to die — slowly, painfully, and entirely. You are safe here precisely as long as you stay. And the winter does not stay."*

The mist began to draw back toward the boundary wall, folding into the great pale curtain that separates the Hollow from everything else, and as it thinned it spoke one final time:

*"The bridge must come willingly. The winter comes regardless. Choose."*

Then it was gone, and the frost line remained, and the birds started up in the hedges as though nothing whatsoever had occurred.

* * *

We walked home without speaking. The green strip between the frost and our fence seemed obscene — six paces of alive world, holding.

In the kitchen the eggs had gone rubbery in the pan. Theo scraped them into the compost without comment and put the kettle on, and I sat at the table with the prophecy book lying facedown in front of me like a sleeping animal, and for a long time the only sounds were water heating and Theo moving through his own kitchen like a man learning its dimensions for the first time.

"So here's where we actually are," he said finally, sitting across from me. He'd gotten very good, over the years, at doing the math out loud when I went silent — he says it's the only way to get me to argue, and arguing is how he knows I'm all right. "One. There's a contract between storms and this valley. Two. It's failing. Three. The fix, according to a book neither of us can burn, is me. Four. You physically cannot leave to fight about it. Five. The problem is coming here anyway, at about a foot an hour." He turned his cup in his hands. "Have I missed anything?"

"My mother," I said.

His eyebrows went up. In fifteen years I had mentioned Seraphine maybe four times, each time the way you mention weather in a country you'll never visit again.

"The book writes itself, it said. Books don't write themselves. People write books and pretend they don't." I pulled the volume toward me, felt its weight, its awful warmth. "There's exactly one person alive with both the skill and the motive to fill blank pages with my future. And she raised me on the doctrine that prophecy is a weapon the powerful aim at the frightened."

"You think she's aiming it at me."

"I think—" My voice caught. I pressed on. "I think she never forgave me for leaving. And I think the Warden said *willingly* three times, and my mother has never in her life forced anyone to do anything. She makes you *want* things, and then she collects. Theo, if this is her — if any part of this is her — then the trap isn't the winter or the covenant or the book. The trap is that every road out of it runs through exactly the choice someone wants us to make."

Theo was quiet for a moment. Outside, a crow called, ordinary as bread.

"Then we don't walk her road," he said. "We find a third one. There's always a third one — you just haven't taught me enough of your world to see it yet."

"The third option in every covenant story is someone dies."

"Then we'll be the first to invent another." He reached across the table and took my hand, his thumb passing over my knuckles, warm, mortal, calloused from thirty years of wood. "But Marguerite — I need you to tell me the rest. What exile cost you. What crossing would cost you. All of the arithmetic. Because that thing offered me a choice, and I'm not choosing blind, and neither are you. Not anymore."

The kettle began to sing.

I looked at my own hand in his, and past it, out the window, at the frost line holding its six paces of green like a promise the world had already begun to renege on, and I understood the true shape of the trap at last. It wasn't a cage. Cages you can rattle. It was a circle drawn around everything I loved, with death priced at the border for me and a throne priced at the center for him, and somewhere outside it all, in a carriage of thunder I could not yet imagine, a woman who shared my face was almost certainly watching to see which of us broke first.

"All of the arithmetic," I agreed. "Starting with a number I've never said out loud."

Theo waited. The kettle screamed and he didn't move to lift it.

"Fifteen years ago," I said, "when I gave up my storms — I didn't only give up the magic."
