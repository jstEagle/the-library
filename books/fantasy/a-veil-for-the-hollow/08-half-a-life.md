## 8. Half a Life

I learned the truth the way I learn most things: too late, and from someone who wasn't Marguerite.

It was three days after the snow. Three days after summer broke its own spine at the valley's edge and frost came crawling inward like something patient with an appetite. The village council had called an emergency meeting in the meetinghouse — the one building in Hollow Valley old enough to have a name older than anyone living — and everyone went, because when the sky misbehaves, people want to be indoors with other people, even if those people are Old Man Bramble.

Marguerite didn't want me to come. She said it would be boring, said it was just farmers arguing about drainage, said she'd tell me everything after. And I believed her, mostly, because for six years I had made a comfortable religion out of believing her. Believing her was easy. Believing her felt like being loved.

But I'd seen her face that morning over the burnt stew and the bent candle flames, and I'd seen it again when the book on our shelf wrote my name in ink that hadn't existed an hour before, so when she kissed my cheek and walked toward the meetinghouse alone, I did something I'd never done in our entire life together.

I followed her. At a distance. Like a coward, or like a man finally paying attention.

* * *

The meetinghouse smelled of lamp oil and wet wool. Every soul in the valley seemed packed inside — the Hollis twins, the Ferrow family, old Marta Chen with her cane propped against a pew like she'd brought it as a weapon. Bramble stood at the front beside a lectern, and there were five others up there with him, gray-haired people I'd known my whole life as the ones who organized the harvest festival and settled property disputes. The founders' descendants. I'd never thought about what else they might be descended *from*.

Marguerite sat near the back. She'd chosen the seat deliberately, I realized — far enough away that no one would look at her twice, close enough to the door.

Bramble was talking about the covenant.

I'll give him this: he didn't dress it up. He stood there with his hands flat on the lectern like a man pinning down something alive, and he said, "The boundary is failing. Frost is through the outer marker stones, and it's moving inward at roughly a foot every hour. The Warden has appeared at the gate, which hasn't happened since my grandfather's time. Those of you who remember your grandmothers' stories know what that means. For those of you who don't, I'll say it plainly." He paused, and his eyes found Marguerite across the room, and mine found them both, and something cold slid down the back of my neck that had nothing to do with weather.

"The storm realms are dying," Bramble said. "And when they die, we die with them. The valley exists inside their protection. It always has."

A woman near the front — I think her name was Aldous-Ferrow, one of the ferry family — asked what protection meant, exactly. Protection from what?

"From everything," Bramble said simply. "Why do you think no war ever found us? Why do you think the plague of 'forty-one stopped at the ridge like a dog hitting the end of its chain? Why do you think this valley has magic-dead soil where nothing enchanted can grow, nothing cursed can enter, and nothing exiled can be touched?" He let that last word sit in the air. "The storms keep the veil drawn tight around us. In exchange, the founders gave the storm realm certain... considerations. Considerations that are now one hundred years overdue."

"What considerations?" That was Marta Chen, sharp as a tack despite her age.

Bramble's jaw worked. "That's not tonight's business."

"It's entirely tonight's business—"

"*That is not tonight's business.*" His voice cracked like ice underfoot. "Tonight's business is that the Warden came to the gate three nights ago and spoke to exactly one person in this valley, and that person has not told us what was said."

Every head in the room turned. Not toward Bramble.

Toward Marguerite.

She stood up slowly, and I watched my partner — my person, my home — face a room full of neighbors who suddenly looked at her the way you look at a locked door you've never noticed before. She didn't flinch. She never flinches. That should have been my first clue, years ago, that she'd practiced.

"The Warden said the bridge must come willingly," she said. Her voice carried clear and level to the back of the room. To me. "That's all it said. I've told you everything I know, Mr. Bramble, which is more than you told any of us, including whatever 'considerations' your family has been managing on ours for a century."

Murmurs. Bramble's knuckles whitened on the lectern. And then old Marta Chen said the thing that changed everything:

"Ask her what exile cost her. Ask her what she paid."

The room went quiet in a different way. A knowing way. Half those faces turned back to Marguerite with something almost like pity, and I stood outside in the dark pressing my ear to a gap in the door boards, feeling like a burglar at my own life.

"I don't see how that's relevant," Marguerite said, and her voice wasn't quite level anymore.

"You renounced," Marta said. "Fifteen years ago. You came down off that mountain with no name and no history and old Wren vouched for you, and none of us asked questions because the valley's whole purpose is not asking questions. But my grandmother served on the first council, girl, and she told me what the rite of renunciation does to a caller. So either you tell these people yourself, or I will."

Marguerite looked around the room. Looking for exits. Looking for mercy. Finding neither.

Then — and I will replay this moment until I die — she looked directly at the gap in the door where I was standing. She couldn't have seen me. There was no lamplight back there, no angle. But she looked anyway, straight at the place where I was, and her shoulders dropped half an inch, and I understood that she knew. She'd probably known I was behind her since the lane.

"Storm-calling isn't a skill," she said quietly. "It's not something you do. It's woven into you. Half your years, half your blood, half your — self — belongs to the storm from the day you're born. When you renounce, you cut that half away. The valley takes it as payment for your protection." She swallowed. "And the price of cutting it away is that you lose half your lifespan. Not metaphorically. Arithmetically. Whatever years you would have lived, you live half of them."

Silence. Somewhere a baby fussed and was hushed.

"And yours?" Marta asked gently. "How much is spent?"

Marguerite closed her eyes. "Storm-callers are long-lived. Mine would have been measured in centuries. Cut in half..." She exhaled. "I'm thirty-four. By the arithmetic, I have already used most of what I was allotted. What remains is—" Her hand made a small, terrible gesture. "Years. Not decades."

I didn't hear anything after that. I remember Bramble saying something procedural, remember chairs scraping, remember the crowd beginning to spill out the doors past me without seeing me, but the words went by like water over glass. I was doing math. I was standing in a churchyard doing math with numbers I didn't have, trying to divide a life I'd assumed was infinite by a number I couldn't pronounce, and the sum kept coming out the same:

The ring in my pocket had a countdown on it.

* * *

I beat her home. Barely. Long enough to stand in our kitchen with my hands braced on the counter, breathing like a man who'd run farther than the lane.

Six years. Six years of mornings. She made tea in the blue pot because Theo likes the blue pot, and she hummed when she chopped vegetables, off-key, always the same three notes, and she let me read my drafts aloud at dinner even the bad chapters, especially the bad chapters, laughing at the right places and telling me honestly when a character rang false. Six years, and I thought I knew the shape of our life: ordinary, unmagical, safe. I proposed over burnt stew four days ago and she laughed, actually laughed, and then the candles bent toward the window and everything since has been prophecy books and frost lines and a Warden made of mist, and underneath all of it, all along, underneath every single morning —

She was dying. Slowly, invisibly, arithmetically. And she'd chosen that. Chosen it over whatever waited for her up in those clouds. Chosen a short mortal life in a magic-dead valley over a long storm-touched one anywhere else, and the thing she'd chosen it *for* was—

Was it me? Was it peace? Was it escape from a mother whose handwriting was currently eating our bookshelf? I didn't know. That was the worst part. Six years and I didn't know which parts of her I'd been living beside, because she'd built the walls before I ever arrived and I'd never once thought to ask what was behind them.

Her key turned in the lock. I heard her stop in the doorway when she saw me standing there, heard her take one breath, and then she set down her bag very carefully, the way you set things down when you're deciding whether to fight or flee.

"How much did you hear?" she asked.

"All of it."

She nodded slowly. "Okay." Another breath. "Theo, before you say anything — the arithmetic isn't exact. Nobody knows the exact rate, and I'm—"

"Don't." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "*Don't.* Don't soften it. Don't manage me. Please." I pushed off the counter and crossed the kitchen to her, and I took her face in my hands, and she let me, her eyes steady and frightened and furious at being caught. "Six years, Marguerite. Six years I thought we had all the time in the world, and it turns out I was the only one who thought that. You've known since the day you walked into this valley. You've been counting down every birthday cake, every anniversary, every — every ordinary Tuesday — and you never said a word."

"There was no word to say." Her voice cracked on it. "What was I supposed to do, introduce myself with a mortality table? *Hello, I'm the exiled daughter of the High Storm-Caller, I traded half my existence for a garden and a quiet man, please pass the bread?*" She pulled back from my hands, not angrily — she pressed my palms briefly as she did it, like an apology — and turned away to grip the back of a chair. "You fell in love with a woman who grows herbs and burns stew. If I told you the rest, you'd never have been able to unknow it. You'd have spent every day watching me for symptoms. I wanted you to have one whole person who wasn't a tragedy."

"That's not your choice to make for me!"

"No," she agreed, barely above a whisper. "It isn't. I know that. I've known it for six years and I made the coward's choice anyway, and I'd make it again, and I hate that about myself, and—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Turned back around, and her eyes were wet and blazing at once. "Say what you came to say, Theo."

What I came to say. I reached into my pocket, and her breath caught audibly, because she knew what lived there — she'd known since the night the candles bent, since I'd fumbled the box open over ruined stew and she'd frozen mid-laugh. I held the little velvet square between us on my open palm like evidence at a trial.

"Four days ago I asked you to marry me thinking I was offering you a future," I said. "Now I find out the future I was offering has an expiration date stamped on it that you already knew about. And here's the thing, Marguerite. Here's the thing I need you to understand, because I think you've spent fifteen years believing the opposite." I stepped closer. "I am not retracting the offer."

Her chin trembled. "Theo—"

"I'm not done." My hands were shaking and I let them. "You don't get to protect me from this. You don't get to decide I'm better off loving you blind. I watched my father spend eight years pretending my mother wasn't sick because nobody in that house was allowed to say the word cancer, and I watched what that pretense cost every one of us, and I swore — I *swore*, on his grave, that whoever I loved, I would love with my eyes open. All of it. The countdown included." I opened the box. The ring caught the lamplight, small and stupid and precious. "So here are my terms. One: you tell me everything. The mother. The prophecy. The exile. The Warden. All of it, in order, starting tonight. Two: we face whatever's coming at that gate *together*, which means if there's a bridge and a burning in our future, you don't get to volunteer me in secret and I don't get to shield you out of stubbornness. And three—" My voice broke and I hated it and I kept going. "Three: you say yes. Not because we have forever. We clearly don't, and neither does anybody, we just all usually get to pretend otherwise and you don't anymore. Say yes because whatever years you have left, I want every single one of them to be mine, and I want every single one of mine to be yours. Even the short version. *Especially* the short version."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, somewhere north, I could almost feel the frost moving — a foot an hour, patient, arithmetic itself.

Marguerite crossed to me. She took the box from my palm, closed it, and set it on the counter between the salt and the blue teapot — domestic, deliberate, *ours* — and then she put her arms around me and buried her face in my shoulder and shook. Not crying, exactly. Something deeper than crying, some tectonic shifting of a woman setting down fifteen years of solitary weight.

"Yes," she said into my collar. "Yes, obviously, you ridiculous man, yes — but Theo, if I tell you everything, if I really tell you everything, you may wish you'd stayed protected."

"Try me."

She pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve, and I saw something settle in her expression — resolve, or surrender, or both wearing the same coat. She glanced at the shelf where her grandmother's book sat, its pages regrown overnight, patiently describing tomorrow.

"Sit down," she said. "Pour us both something strong. This starts with my mother, and I promise you, by the end of it you're going to understand why the first thing I ever did with my freedom was build a life where no one would ever think to look for me."

She went to the shelf and lifted down the book, and the fresh ink gleamed on its pages like it was still wet, like it was waiting for her.

Outside, the first flakes of impossible summer snow began to fall on the herb garden.
