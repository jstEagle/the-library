## 9. Letters to a Mother Who Isn't Dead

Marguerite had told Theo the truth the way she did everything: badly, and only when cornered.

It was three days after the frost came. Three days of snow in high summer, of neighbors hauling their goats indoors, of Old Man Bramble standing at the valley's edge with his hands behind his back like a man inspecting a fence he'd built himself. Three days of Theo not asking — which was worse than asking. He moved through the cottage with a carefulness that made her feel like a cracked cup. He refilled her tea without being asked. He stopped humming while he cooked. Marguerite could have survived a war more easily than she survived his silence.

So on the third evening, when he set down two bowls of barley soup across from her and said nothing at all, just looked at her with those patient brown eyes, she broke.

"My mother wrote that book."

The words came out flat and ugly, like something dropped on stone. Theo didn't move. Steam rose between them.

"Seraphine Ishikawa," Marguerite went on, because now that the dam had a crack in it, everything behind it wanted out at once. "High Storm-Caller. First Voice of the Thunder Court. The woman they name storms after, back home." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Back *home*. Listen to me. Fifteen years and I still call it home."

"Marguerite—"

"She's not dead." Marguerite stared into her soup. "I know I've let you think — well, I never actually said she was dead, did I. I let you assume it. That was deliberate. It was easier than the truth, and the truth is that my mother is alive, and powerful, and she hates me, and I have spent fifteen years pretending otherwise because if I pretend hard enough it's almost like it isn't true."

Theo sat down slowly across from her. He didn't touch his spoon.

"Start from the beginning," he said. "Whatever you think the beginning is."

* * *

The beginning, as Marguerite told it, was this:

Storm-callers were born, not made. You couldn't learn to pull lightning out of the sky any more than you could learn to be seven feet tall; you either carried the storm in your blood or you didn't. And Marguerite's family had carried it for eleven generations. Her grandmother's grandmother had been one of the signatories of the covenant itself — the great bargain struck between the storm realms and the mortal valleys, back when the veil between worlds was young and thin and needed tending from both sides.

Her grandmother had kept the prophecy book. Every High Storm-Caller kept one — or rather, *the* one. There was only ever a single book, passed hand to hand down the line, its pages sealed against all ink but the covenant's own. When a page filled, another grew. When a caller died, the book went cold until the next took it up.

"It was blank when my mother gave it to me," Marguerite said. "That's what I need you to understand. Blank. Sealed. A dead thing. She put it in my hands at my ascension ceremony — I was nineteen — and she said, *"It will wake when it has something to say to you,"* in that voice she used for prophecies and punishments, and everyone in the court thought it was an honor. Being given the book. The heirloom of heirlooms."

"And it wasn't," Theo said quietly.

"It was a leash." Marguerite pushed the soup away untouched. "Because here's what nobody tells you about being born a storm-caller, Theo. You're not a person. You're an *instrument*. Eleven generations of instruments, tuned and polished and handed down. My whole life was decided before I drew breath. Who I'd marry. What vows I'd speak. How many years of my life I'd spend holding the veil together while the court debated whether I was worthy of the burden." She wrapped her hands around her cooling tea. "And the person who decided most of it was her."

"The arranged marriage," Theo said. Not a question. He'd heard enough over three days of silence to piece that much together.

"His name is Kaelen Voss. Second Voice of the Thunder Court. Handsome as a lightning strike and about as warm." She said it lightly, but the lightness cost her something; he saw it land and chose not to comment. "The match was negotiated when I was twelve. Sealed by the book itself — there's a page in it, or there was, describing the joining of two lines of callers, the strength it would give the covenant. My mother called it destiny. Everyone called it destiny. Nobody asked me."

"So you ran."

"I didn't run." The old shame flared hot in her chest, fifteen years stale and still burning. "Running implies fear. What I did was worse than running. I *negotiated*. I was raised in that court, Theo. I learned diplomacy before I learned to braid my own hair. So I went to the Warden — this was before it was bound to the gate — and I offered a trade. My magic for the valley's protection. Full renunciation, sworn before the covenant itself. Storm-callers who renounce lose half their lifespan; I knew that going in. I signed anyway." She met his eyes. "I traded half my life to get away from my mother. And then I walked into a valley where magic can't follow me, and I called it freedom."

The fire popped. Outside, the summer snow hissed against the shutters.

"Fifteen years," Theo said. "You were twenty-two when you came here?"

"Twenty-one. I'll be thirty-six this winter." Half of sixty-odd, spent. The arithmetic of it hung between them; she watched him do it, watched his jaw tighten, and loved him for not saying it aloud. Not yet. "And in all that time, I sent no letters. Made no contact. I let everyone believe what they wanted — that I'd died crossing the boundary, maybe. That was the kindest version. Some of them probably believed I'd been struck down by the covenant for renouncing. Also fine. Anything was better than admitting the truth, which is that the strongest caller of her generation looked at her duty and said *no, thank you*, and left her people to hold the sky without her."

"You don't know that the covenant would be different if you'd stayed."

"Don't I?" Marguerite stood up, too fast, and began stacking bowls that didn't need stacking. "The winter started the week after I left. Slow, at first — the outer reaches frosting early, the veil thinning in places it had held for centuries. I heard the reports before I crossed the boundary. The court blamed weather-cycles. They always blame weather-cycles." She set the bowls down harder than necessary. "But I *knew*, even then. I knew what I was taking with me when I left. And I left anyway. Because I was twenty-one and in love with the idea of my own life, and I told myself the realms could spare me. I told myself the story where I'm the wounded one. The brave one. The girl who escaped."

"And now the book is writing again," Theo said, "and it's writing about me."

"Now the book is writing again," she agreed, "and it's writing about you. And I keep trying to figure out whose handwriting that is."

She turned to face him. This was the part she'd been circling for three days, the part that tasted worst.

"The book is sealed against all ink but the covenant's own," she said. "That's what my grandmother told me, and her mother before her. Only the line of callers can write in it — and even then, only when the covenant itself compels the words. Except." Her throat tightened. "Except my mother is the finest weather-witch of the last five hundred years, and she has spent her entire life finding exceptions to rules other people thought were absolute. If anyone alive could bend a sealed prophecy book to her own hand — make it write what she wanted it to write, when she wanted it written —"

"You think she's doing it now."

"I think she's known where I am for fifteen years and done nothing. I think the winter starts the moment I finally build a life worth staying for. I think tonight's date appeared in fresh ink beside your face, and tomorrow's date is already filling in, and the entry names you *the bridge that must burn*." Marguerite's voice cracked on the last words and she hated herself for it. "I think my mother found out I was happy, Theo. I think it took her fifteen years to find out, and when she did, she picked up her pen."

* * *

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Show me the book."

They sat together at the kitchen table with the prophecy book open between them, lamplight pooling on its pages. Up close, the drawing of Theo's face was worse than Marguerite remembered — not because it was cruelly done, but because it was lovingly done. Every line rendered with care. The slight asymmetry of his brows. The scar on his chin from the year he fell off Bramble's roof. Whoever had drawn it had looked at him a long time.

"That's not hatred," Theo said, tracing the air above the portrait without touching it. "That's study."

"Study can be worse than hatred." Marguerite turned the page carefully, using the edge of a butter knife, as though the paper might bite. "Look at the margins. See these small marks?" Faint glyphs clustered in the borders of every entry — tight, precise, angular. "Court shorthand. Weather-witch notation. My mother invented half these symbols. I grew up watching her write them in the air when she thought no one was looking — little notes to herself, calculations, corrections. She annotates everything. She annotated *me*. My first storm, aged six — she kept the notes for decades. Told me once she'd measured every lightning strike I ever called against the strikes of callers dead two thousand years." Marguerite's mouth twisted. "Love, in my family, looks a lot like measurement."

"And you think she measured him too." Theo nodded at his own face.

"I think she's been measuring us both since before we met." Marguerite closed the book gently, as if it might hear. "Here's what keeps me up at night, Theo. Two possibilities. One: the book woke on its own because the covenant is genuinely failing, and my mother is simply the instrument writing down what it says — in which case the prophecy is real, and you are in real danger, and I have no idea how to stop it. Two: my mother is writing it herself, bending the seal, using the covenant's own grammar to say what she wants said — in which case the prophecy is a weapon she aimed at us, and I have no idea how to stop it." She laughed, short and bitter. "Either way, I have no idea how to stop it. I just don't know whether I should be afraid of the future or of my mother. With her, historically, those are the same thing."

"Which do you believe?" Theo asked. "Right now, tonight. Gut answer."

Marguerite opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at the book, lying closed and innocent as a sleeping animal.

"Gut answer," she said slowly, "is that it doesn't matter. That's the trap. Whether she wrote it or merely transcribed it, the result is identical: you burn, the winter ends, and she gets exactly what she wants. Either way I lose you. Either way she wins." Her hands were shaking; she pressed them flat on the table. "That's the cruelest thing about her, if you want to know. She never gives you a choice you can win. She gives you two doors, and both of them lead where she's already standing."

"Then we find a third door."

"You sound like—" Marguerite stopped. Pressed her lips together.

"Like who?"

"Like me," she said quietly. "Twenty-one. Standing in front of the Warden, certain that if I just refused hard enough, reality would renegotiate." She shook her head. "Reality didn't renegotiate, Theo. Reality took half my life and let me keep the rest. That's what refusal costs in my family. We don't get to say no. We only get to choose which yes."

Outside, the snow had thickened. Through the window, Marguerite could see lanterns moving down the village lane — the founders' households, gathering somewhere, holding some meeting she hadn't been invited to. Bramble's silhouette led them, straight-backed as a fence post. She watched them go and felt the valley close around her like a hand.

"They're meeting," she murmured. "The founders' families. About the frost, about the book, about us. They've guarded the human side of the covenant for generations — Bramble told me once, years ago, drunk on plum wine, that the valley wasn't just a refuge. It was a hinge. A door with a lock, and his family holds the key." She turned back to Theo. "Which means somebody in this valley may already know what the bridge is. What the burning is. And whether there's a version of this where you survive it."

"Then tomorrow we ask them."

"'We,'" she repeated. Something in her chest loosened, just slightly, hearing the word.

"We," Theo confirmed. Then, softer: "Marguerite. The marriage. Kaelen Voss. Is he part of this? Is he why she—"

"I don't know." Honest, at least. "Kaelen and I were promised to each other before either of us could consent, and I fled rather than honor it, and if my mother has spent fifteen years engineering a prophecy to deliver me back to the altar — or to remove whatever I ran toward instead — then Kaelen is somewhere in this design too. Or he isn't. Or he's as trapped as we are. I genuinely don't know." She rubbed her eyes. "That's the worst part. I don't know if she's punishing me or saving me. With her, it was always both. Every punishment was a lesson; every lesson was a cage; every cage was built, she swore, out of love. I grew up inside a storm and called it shelter."

Theo reached across the table and took her shaking hands in his steady ones.

"For the record," he said, "I'm glad you ran."

Marguerite looked up at him. Fifteen years of shame sat in her throat like a stone.

"You're glad I doomed the realms."

"I'm glad you're here." Simple. Immovable. "Everything else is weather."

She laughed despite herself — a wet, broken sound — and gripped his hands back, and for one moment the cottage felt like the home she'd built instead of the stage someone else had designed.

* * *

She wrote the letter that night, after he'd gone to bed.

Not to send. Never to send — she had no way to send it, no road out of the valley that didn't kill her, no courier who could cross the veil. But her hands needed doing what they'd always done when the storm got loud: making marks, ordering chaos, measuring the unmeasurable. So she sat at the kitchen table by guttering candlelight with the prophecy book shoved to the far end of the table, facing away, like a guest you don't trust, and she wrote:

*Mother.*

*The book is awake. I know you know. I know the notation in the margins; I grew up under that hand. If you are writing this, I want you to know I see you. If you are only writing it down, I want you to know I see that too, and I cannot tell the difference anymore, and I think that is the point.*

*I renounced my magic so I could stop being yours. Do you understand that? Not the court's. Yours. Every vow I ever spoke, I spoke to your approval. Every storm I ever called, I called to your measurements. I traded half my life for the right to be someone you did not design — and now your ink has followed me across the world to tell me the design was never mine to escape.*

*If you want me home, say so. If you want me punished, say so plainly, and I will bear it, and I will still not come. But if you are doing this to take him — if you looked at the one unmeasured thing in my life and reached out your pen to claim it — then hear me, Mother, wherever you are: I will stand in front of whatever altar you build, and I will refuse, and I will refuse louder than the covenant itself.*

*Your daughter,
M.*

She read it twice. Then, because shame is honest at midnight in ways it refuses to be at noon, she added one more line at the bottom, small, almost invisible:

*P.S. — Did the winter start because I left? Answer honestly. For once.*

She folded the letter and did not seal it. She set it on the shelf beneath the prophecy book — tucked against the spine, hidden, absurd, a letter to a woman who might as well live on the moon — and blew out the candle.

In the dark, Marguerite lay listening to Theo breathe and to the snow whisper against the glass, and she made herself face the thought she'd been dodging for three days:

Whether her mother wrote the prophecy or merely recorded it, the next move belonged to Seraphine. Which meant the next scene of their lives was already drafted. Which meant—

The candle, snuffed, flared once. Twice. Three times, in perfect rhythm, like knocking.

On the shelf above her, in the pitch-dark kitchen, the prophecy book opened itself with a sound like a long-held breath released — and fresh ink whispered across a new page, letter by letter, in the silence.

Marguerite sat bolt upright in bed.

By the time she reached the table, heart slamming, the entry had finished writing itself, and the last line stood gleaming wet and black in the moonlight:

*A letter travels faster than a rider. Await reply.*

Beneath it, in the margin, in tight angular shorthand she hadn't seen in fifteen years — the notation of weather-witches, the hand that had measured her whole childhood — three small symbols she recognized instantly.

An address. A summons.

And below the address, in plain script, in ink that was still drying:

*Come alone, or he burns sooner.*
