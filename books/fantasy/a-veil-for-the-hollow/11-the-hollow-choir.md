## 11. The Hollow Choir

The bell at Hollow Chapel had not rung in sixty years, and on the night it rang, only three people heard it.

That was the point.

* * *

**Marta Quill**

Marta Quill heard the bell through the floorboards of her bakery and knew, the way she knew when a loaf was done by smell alone, that it was time.

She had been eleven years old the last time the bell rang. Her grandmother had lifted her onto a stool, put a cold iron key in her small hand, and said: *When it rings, you go. You don't ask why. You don't tell your husband. You go, and you sit, and you listen, and you remember.*

Marta had gone. She had sat in the back pew of a chapel that wasn't a chapel, among nine other people who suddenly looked older than they had that morning, and she had listened to old Bramble recite words that made her teeth ache. She hadn't understood a tenth of it. She had understood enough.

Now she was fifty-eight, flour to her elbows, and the bell was ringing again.

She wiped her hands. She took the iron key from its place behind the loose brick by the oven — the same brick, the same loose spot, the same key, passed hand to hand like a secret that refused to die — and she told her husband she was going to check on the starter.

He didn't look up from his paper. That was the arrangement. The valley's marriages had survived on such arrangements for a hundred years.

Outside, the snow was still falling at the valley's edge. Two days now. Summer snow, falling in a place where weather came to die. The frost line had crept from the boundary stones to the old mill, and the millpond wore a skin of ice that creaked at night like something walking on it. The village had taken to calling it *the bad luck*, the way people call a plague *the fever*, as if naming it small would keep it small.

Marta knew better. Marta had sat in the back pew at eleven years old and heard what the snow meant.

She walked through streets gone quiet too early, past the Vance house where the candles still burned in the front window — two of them, always two, she'd noticed, and wondered idly if they knew what two meant, what two had always meant — and down the lane to the chapel that wasn't a chapel.

The door was already open. The bell was still swinging.

Nine faces inside. Hers made ten. The same ten as always, minus the ones the years had taken, plus the ones the years had handed down. Old Bramble at the front, of course, ancient as the boundary stones and twice as stubborn. The Hollis brothers. Young Tam Wren, who couldn't have been more than twenty and had the look of a man who'd just been handed a live coal. And the rest — farmers, a midwife, the ferryman, the schoolteacher — the whole quiet machinery of the Hollow, assembled in a room that officially did not exist.

Marta took her seat in the back pew. The wood was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

"Two days," Bramble said, without preamble. He never used preamble. Preamble was for people with time. "The frost has moved four hundred feet since the snow started. It moved three feet a day for the sixty years before that. Do the arithmetic."

Nobody did the arithmetic out loud. Everybody did it inside.

"The woman at the boundary," Bramble went on. "The one made of mist. It spoke to Ishikawa. We have eyes on the ridge, and we have ears, and what it said was: *the bridge must come willingly.*"

Marta watched the young ones — Tam Wren especially — go pale in the candlelight. She remembered being pale in that same light. It was almost sweet, the way the body remembered fear across decades, like a song from childhood.

"The book has woken," Bramble said. "You all know what that means. You all know what it *asks*."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the chest.

"The outsider," said the ferryman, Delmar, slowly, "the Vance man. He touched the boundary and wasn't harmed."

"We know," said Bramble.

"The valley protects him. Not her. *Him.*"

"We know, Delmar."

Marta leaned forward. "Then say the rest, Bramble, because the young ones are going to hear it eventually, and better from you than from the frost."

Bramble looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the young ones — Tam Wren, and the schoolteacher's daughter, and the midwife's boy — and Marta saw him make the same calculation his grandmother had made, and hers before that, and every keeper of this room back through a hundred years of kept secrets.

"The covenant has a price," Bramble said. "It has always had a price. And the price has come due."

* * *

**Tam Wren**

Tam Wren had grown up on the same street as Theo Vance. They'd fished the millpond together — back when the millpond didn't creak — and Tam had sold him a crooked canoe and never once given him his money back, and when Theo had come down from the city with that strange, beautiful woman and bought the old Hollis place, Tam had been the first to bring them a welcome pie and the first to gossip about them at the tavern.

He had never once suspected that his whole life was a hinge.

He sat in the pew now with his hands pressed flat against his knees because if he didn't hold them somewhere they'd shake, and he listened to Old Man Bramble explain, in the tone of a man reading a grain ledger, that the valley Tam loved was a lock, and the lock had a key, and the key was a person, and the person was his friend.

"A hundred years ago," Bramble said, "the founders of this valley made a bargain. The storm realms above us — yes, boy, they're real, yes, they've always been real — were killing the lowlands with their weather. Floods. Hail that came down like God's own fury. The founders were farmers and they were desperate, and desperation makes for good negotiating. They offered a covenant: the storms would stay above, the valley would stay quiet, and in exchange —"

He stopped. Tam realized the old man's hands, folded on the lectern, were trembling.

"In exchange," Bramble said, "every hundred years, the covenant requires a mortal to walk through the veil and not come back."

The candle nearest Tam guttered, though there was no draft.

"It happened once," Bramble said. "Sixty years ago. A man named Alder Quill —" and here Marta Quill, in the back pew, made a small sound that Tam would remember for the rest of his life — "walked out to the boundary at midwinter, and the mist took him, and the valley had sixty years of quiet. That quiet is ending. The book has chosen its next payment. And the book has chosen the Vance man."

"That's murder," Tam said. His voice came out higher than he meant it to. "That's just — you're talking about walking a man into the mist to die."

"I'm talking about arithmetic," Bramble said, and his voice was terrible in its gentleness. "One life against every life in this valley. Against the lowlands beyond. You think the frost stops at the boundary stones? You think the long winter cares whose garden it kills? The covenant is the only thing standing between this valley and a death that doesn't end. I have watched the arithmetic my whole life, boy. It has never once come out any other way."

"Then the arithmetic is wrong."

"Then the arithmetic is *cruel*," said Marta Quill from the back, "and both of you stop pretending those are different words." She stood. She was a broad woman, flour-white at the knuckles, and when she spoke the whole room leaned toward her the way wheat leans toward wind. "I lost Alder to this room. My grandmother sat where you're sitting, Bramble, and nodded along, and told my family he'd gone south for the winter, and my mother died not knowing where her father went. I have kept this secret for forty-seven years and I am telling you now: keeping it has not made it lighter. Not one ounce lighter in forty-seven years."

"The secret isn't a weight, Marta. It's a roof."

"The roof is leaking," she said. "The snow's coming through it. And you want to patch it with a man."

Tam looked around the room. He counted, the way you count in a room where the vote is never spoken but always taken. The Hollis brothers, stone-faced. Delmar the ferryman, staring at his hands. The midwife, lips pressed thin. The schoolteacher's daughter, who couldn't have been older than Tam, with tears standing in her eyes and her jaw set like she'd already decided something.

And Bramble, at the lectern, with a hundred years of arithmetic behind his eyes and not one doubt in them. That was the worst part. Not the secret. The *certainty*.

"The Ishikawa woman," Delmar said carefully, "won't allow it."

"No," Bramble agreed. "She won't. Which is why this room must decide, before she does something worse than sacrifice."

"Worse," Tam repeated.

Bramble's eyes found him, and Tam felt the cold come off the old man like the cold off the millpond ice.

"The book can be refused," Bramble said. "It has been refused once before — sixty years before Alder, a bridge was chosen and the valley's keepers hid him, and the winter came early, and half the valley's children died before the next hundred-year term and its payment arrived. Refusal has a price too, boy. It's just paid in smaller coins, by more people, over more years." He let that sit. "Some of us have decided we'd rather pay one coin than a thousand. Some of us have decided the door should be locked and the key thrown into the deep water, and whatever the storm realms do with their dying covenant, it will not be our dying covenant. You're young, Tam Wren. You'll be asked to choose. I suggest you choose before the frost chooses for you."

The bell above them gave one soft, settling note, as if it had heard everything and had opinions.

Tam walked home the long way, past the Vance house, where the two candles still burned in the window. He stood in the lane for a long time, looking at that light, holding a secret that weighed more than his crooked canoe, more than his whole life on this street, and he understood at last what his grandmother's generation had understood in their bones:

The whole town was a door. And everyone in it was a hinge.

* * *

**Edith Marsh**

Edith Marsh was the schoolteacher, and she was the youngest keeper in the chapel, and she had not slept in two days, which was why she noticed what nobody else had noticed.

She noticed it at dawn, standing at her window with cold tea, watching the frost line on the far ridge. The frost had stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped.* It had crept four hundred feet in two days, relentless as a debt, and now it sat at the old mill like a held breath, and Edith — who had spent ten years teaching children to look closely, to notice the thing that doesn't fit — looked closely.

The frost wasn't stopping. It was *waiting.*

And then she saw the second thing, the thing that made her set down her tea with a hand that was not entirely steady.

Smoke from the Vance house chimney. Rising straight. And above the valley, above the dead-calm sky that had never once cracked with lightning in all her years here, the clouds had begun — very faintly, very far off, at the very top of the world — to move.

Not drift. *Turn.* Slowly, in an enormous circle, the way water turns above a drain.

Edith had grown up on her mother's stories — her mother had been a keeper before her, and her grandmother before that — and one of the stories was about the day the last bridge walked into the mist. The sky had turned like that, her grandmother said. Like the heavens were a great eye, and something below had finally caught its attention.

She dressed quickly. She took the iron key from the flour tin — everyone hid theirs in flour; it was practically a tradition — and she went first not to the chapel but to the lane, to the Vance house, because she was a schoolteacher and schoolteachers believe that people deserve to be told things before they're decided about them.

Theo Vance answered the door with flour on his sleeves — hers, she'd lent it, the whole valley was suddenly baking like a funeral feast — and behind him Marguerite Ishikawa stood at the kitchen table with a book open in front of her, and the look on her face was the look of a woman reading her own death warrant in her mother's handwriting.

Which, Edith thought, was probably exactly what it was.

"Mr. Vance," Edith said. "Miss Ishikawa. I need to tell you something, and I need to tell you quickly, because I don't think we have much time, and because there are people in this valley — good people, some of them — who would rather you never heard it at all."

Marguerite closed the book. "Then say it."

"The valley isn't what you think it is. It never was. Every family here — the Quills, the Hollises, the Wrens, mine — we're the descendants of the founders, and the founders made a bargain with the storm realms a hundred years ago, and the bargain has a price, and the price is due, and last night the keepers met in the old chapel to decide what to do about you."

The silence in the kitchen was total. Outside, a bird sang, absurdly, in the snowless morning.

"And what did they decide?" Marguerite asked. Her voice was very quiet. Theo's hand had found hers on the table, and Edith watched their fingers lace together and thought: *there it is. The two candles. The two that were always meant to be two.*

"They didn't decide," Edith said. "That's the trouble with a hundred-year-old secret — it rots the part of you that decides. Bramble wants the old way. Marta Quill is against him, and she's the only one he's ever been afraid of. And the rest of us—" She stopped. She made herself finish. "The rest of us are going to have to choose. And I wanted you to know, before the choosing, that at least one of us chooses *you*. Both of you. Whatever it costs."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Then, softly, in a voice that seemed to come from somewhere much older than her face:

"You should know that it's already chosen, Edith. The book chose him the day we met. Maybe the day we were born." She turned the prophecy book around on the table so Edith could see the page, and Edith felt the hair rise on her arms, because the ink was still moving — still *writing itself*, letter by letter, in the morning light — and what it had just finished writing was not a name.

It was a time.

*Tonight. When the bell rings a second time, the bridge will know the way, and the way will know the bridge, and the frost will wait for no one after that.*

Edith looked up. "The bell only rings for keepers."

"Then someone's ringing it for him," Theo said.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the valley, at the edge of everything — the old bell in the chapel that wasn't a chapel began, very softly, to swing.
