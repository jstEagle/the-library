## 14. The Storm Inside Her

I learned early in our life together that Marguerite has two kinds of silence. The first is comfortable — the silence of her reading across from me while I mend a net or fail to fix the pump, her bare feet tucked under my thigh like they pay rent there. The second kind I'd only heard described by villagers who knew her before I did: the silence of someone holding a door shut from the inside.

For three days after the founders' council, she lived in the second kind.

She still made breakfast. She still argued with me about whether thyme could be overwintered without cloche (it couldn't, she was right, she's always right about plants). But behind her eyes there was a door, and something on the other side of it was knocking, and every hour it knocked louder.

On the third morning I woke to find her side of the bed cold and empty and her boots gone from beside the door, and I did what any reasonable man does when his possibly-doomed lover disappears before sunrise: I put on one sock inside out, grabbed the lantern, and went looking for her.

I found her at the garden.

Not *in* it. Above it, sort of — standing at its edge the way you'd stand at the edge of a frozen pond, testing your weight against something that might not hold. The frost line had come close enough now that our fence posts wore white caps even in summer, and past them the killed ground glittered wrong, like sugar that had been salted. She stood facing all of it with her arms at her sides and her hands open, palms forward, the way people stand in front of altars.

"Margo," I said.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Don't talk me out of it."

"Out of what? You haven't told me anything."

She turned. In the lantern light her face was pale as the frost behind her, but her eyes were awake in a way I hadn't seen since the night the book started writing itself — awake and terrified and *hungry*, all three fighting for the same square inch of skin.

"I'm going to call a storm," she said.

The words sat in the air between us like a dropped knife.

"You can't," I said, because we had built fifteen years on that fact the way other couples build on a mortgage. "That's the whole — Margo, that's the entire point of this valley. Magic can't follow you here. You told me. You traded it. It's gone."

"It's not gone." Her voice was very steady, which is how I've learned to tell when she's most frightened. "It's caged. There's a difference, Theo. A dead thing doesn't rattle its bars."

"What does that mean? What's rattling?"

She looked down at her own hands. And I swear to you — I have thought about this every night since, and I swear — for a moment, in the dark, I saw light moving under her skin. Not reflecting. Moving. Like weather seen through a curtain.

"Fifteen years," she said, "I have felt it pressing. Every thunderstorm over the mountains, every summer heat-crackle, every time static lifted my hair off my neck — I felt it lean toward me and get turned away at the border. I thought that was just what exile cost. A homesickness of the blood." She flexed her fingers slowly. "But the frost keeps coming inward. Half a foot overnight now. Bramble's arithmetic says we have weeks. Maybe less. And the Warden won't cross, and I can't leave without dying, and the book wants you to burn, and I am so *tired* of being protected from everything including my own nature."

"So you're going to try to reach through the cage."

"I'm going to try to reach *around* it. Or under it. Through a keyhole, if that's all there is." She finally looked at me straight. "If it works, I can fight the winter from inside the valley. If it doesn't work—"

"If it doesn't work?"

The pause lasted exactly one breath too long.

"Margo. If it doesn't work, *what*?"

"Then I'll know the cage is load-bearing," she said, "and I'll stop."

Which was a lie, or half of one, and I knew it, and I let it pass because sometimes the price of being married-adjacent to a woman made of weather is accepting forecasts you can't verify. I didn't go back to the house. I planted my feet at the garden gate like a stubborn fence post and said, "Then I'm staying to watch," and she didn't argue, which scared me more than anything else she'd done that week.

* * *

Here is what I expected: nothing. That was the story we'd both agreed to live inside. Magic cannot follow her into the Hollow Valley. She renounced it; the valley swallowed it; she was safe and hollow and mine. Fifteen years of proof. She couldn't light a candle with a wish. She couldn't feel a front coming in. Once, at a fair, a charlatan read her palm and told her she had "storm-touched lines" and she laughed so hard she had to sit down on a hay bale, and the laugh had a crack in it I heard but didn't understand until much later.

So when she spread her feet and closed her eyes and began to breathe in the slow, deep, measured way of someone lifting something enormous, I braced for silence.

What happened instead was that the world got *quiet*.

I don't mean quiet like absence of sound. I mean quiet like held breath. The crickets stopped. The stream at the valley's edge — audible even at night, always, a constant thin silver thread of sound — went mute mid-trickle. The lantern flame beside me shrank to a blue bead and stayed there, burning without flickering, which flames do not do, which flames have never done anywhere in the history of fire.

And Marguerite stood in the middle of all that stopped noise with her eyes closed and her hands open, and nothing came.

Ten seconds. Twenty. A full minute of that pressed, underwater hush, her brow furrowing deeper and deeper like a woman listening for a knock at a door in a house where nobody lives anymore.

"Nothing," she whispered. Her voice sounded bruised. "There's nothing. It's just — quiet. It's just me."

And I watched fifteen years arrive on her face all at once — the grief she'd filed away, the funerals she'd attended for her own power, every small death of pretending she didn't miss the sky — and I stepped toward her to hold her while she mourned it properly this time—

Her head snapped back.

"No," she said. To me or to herself or to whatever had answered, I don't know. "No — wait—"

The lantern died. Not guttered. *Died*, snuffed like a thumb over a wick, and in the sudden dark I heard her say, in a voice that was hers but layered somehow, doubled, like two recordings of the same word played a hair out of sync:

"*Theo. Run.*"

I didn't run. For the record, for whoever finds this account, for the archive of my own stupidity: I did not run, because running would have meant leaving her, and I have never once in fifteen years managed to leave her, not for an evening, not for an argument, not for anything.

So I stood at the garden gate and watched my lover be struck by lightning from below.

* * *

That's the only honest way to describe it. There were no clouds. The stars were out — hard and clear, the way they've been all season, the sky above the valley holding its breath for a hundred years. But the ground itself lit up. Blue-white veins of it, crawling through the soil like roots growing at terrible speed, all converging on where Marguerite stood, and when they reached her they went *up* — a column of light and thunder-noise (the thunder arriving instantly, no lag, sound and sight fused into one impossible event) punching upward out of her body into a sky that had never once cracked with lightning since her exile.

She screamed.

I need to write that plainly because everything afterward will want to make it beautiful, and it wasn't. It wasn't awe. It was a woman being pulled apart at the seams by something that loved her too much to let her go. The column of light bent and forked and forked again, wild, untrained, a river that had forgotten its own banks, and wherever a fork touched ground, things died. Our herbs — the rosemary she'd coaxed for a decade, the sage, the feverfew — flash-blackened to skeletons in half a second. The fence posts split lengthwise with cracks like gunshots. The old pear tree at the garden's corner, older than the village, simply came apart, lifted off its roots and burst into burning fragments that fell hissing into the frost.

I was screaming her name. I remember that. I remember my voice going raw and meaning nothing against the roar, and I remember trying to reach her and being stopped — actually stopped, thrown back three feet by nothing visible, by air that had become a wall — and hitting the ground on my bad shoulder and getting up anyway because that is apparently who I am now.

"LET IT GO," I roared. "MARGO, LET IT GO!"

"I DON'T KNOW HOW!" 

And that — that admission, torn out of her between convulsions of light — is the thing that has kept me up every night since. She didn't know how. Fifteen years ago she'd known how to release a storm the way you know how to exhale. But the cage had changed her. The cage hadn't just locked the power out; it had locked the *grammar* out, the language of letting go, and now the power was back and flooding in and she had lost the muscles for anything except holding on.

The surge crested. I will not pretend I understood what I was seeing, but some animal part of my brain understood *scale*: that what was coming through her was continental, that the storm realms were pouring a decade and a half of denied mail through one keyhole at once, and that the keyhole was a human body five foot four inches tall in bare feet on scorched earth.

She turned her head and found me through the light.

Later she told me what she saw in that moment: not horror on my face, she said, but refusal. Me refusing to look away, refusing the whole cowardly tradition of protecting her by distancing myself from her. She said that was the handhold she needed. Not technique. Not memory of grammar. Just proof that somebody was watching and not leaving.

She didn't let go of the storm. There was no elegant release, no exhale. What she did was *aim* it.

She turned — staggering, screaming, wrapped in a pillar of her own inheritance — and pointed the open end of herself at the frost line. At the dead white ground creeping inward from the boundary. At the long winter's newest conquest.

The surge hit the frost like a hammer hits a window.

For one blinding instant, the killed ground ran with light, and steam screamed up off it in white sheets, and I felt — I genuinely felt, through the soles of my boots, through my teeth — the winter *recoil*. Pull back. A dozen yards of conquered land freed in a heartbeat, black and steaming and alive.

Then everything ran out at once.

The light collapsed. The thunder cut off mid-peal like a throat gripped shut. Marguerite folded — not fell, *folded*, like a coat sliding off a hook — and I was through the invisible wall before it finished falling, catching her before the ground did.

* * *

She was breathing. Say that first, because for ten seconds I did nothing but press two fingers to her throat and count, out loud, like a madman, *one-two-three-four*, until the pulse answered me.

She was breathing, but wrong — shallow and fast, the breathing of someone running a race in their sleep. Blood was coming from her nose. From her ears. When I peeled back her eyelids, the whites were shot through with fine red lightning, burst capillaries mapping little storm systems across her eyes.

"Margo. Hey. Hey, I've got you, I've got you."

"'s okay," she mumbled, which is what she says when nothing is okay, her universal lie, her oldest one.

The garden around us looked like a war had applied for permission and been granted it. The pear tree's burning remains tickled and hissed. Steam still rose off the freed strip of ground at the edge, and beyond it — I checked twice, disbelieving — the frost line had retreated. We had gained ground. Real ground. The first ground anyone had taken from the winter since it began.

And it had nearly killed her. One strike. One borrowed minute of her own nature, and it had bled her from the eyes.

I carried her inside. She weighs almost nothing when she's unconscious, which is a fact about her I resent with my whole heart. I put her in bed and cleaned the blood off her face with warm water and talked to her the whole time, nonsense, inventory of the pantry, threats to learn herb lore myself if she died and left me with the garden, and somewhere in the middle of my nonsense her eyes opened — those ruined, red-threaded, absolutely lucid eyes — and she caught my wrist.

"Theo." Her voice was gravel. "Listen to me. Before I lose the nerve to say it."

"I'm listening."

"It's not gone." She said it like a verdict, like an indictment, like both halves of a trial concluded at once. "It was never gone. It's been *pushing* against the cage for fifteen years, and tonight it found a seam, and—" She stopped. Swallowed. Fresh blood threaded from one nostril and she wiped it away with the back of her hand, furious and matter-of-fact about her own body betraying her. "Every time I've felt it press and get turned away, the cage took a little of the pressure and gave a little of itself. Do you understand? It hasn't been protecting me from the magic. It's been spending itself. Dying by degrees. And tonight I tore a hole in it, and everything behind the hole is *backed up*, Theo. Fifteen years of backed-up storm."

"Okay," I said, carefully. "Okay. So we don't do that again."

"We don't have that option anymore." She sat up, slowly, wincing, and gripped my hand hard enough to grind bone. Outside, far off toward the boundary, thunder rolled — real thunder, distant but *inside the valley*, the first thunder this valley had heard in a century, and her eyes flicked toward the window and came back to me shining and wet. "You hear that? It heard me. The storm realm knows the seam is open now. It's going to keep leaning on it. And either I find a way to open the cage on purpose — controlled, chosen, on my terms — or it opens by itself, and what happened to the garden happens to the valley."

I sat with that. I sat with it while the distant thunder spoke again, closer, patient, the knock on the door grown from tapping to pounding.

"There has to be another way," I said. "There's always — Bramble's council, the Warden, your mother, there are a dozen people who understand this better than—"

"The person who understands it best wrote a book about it," Marguerite said, "and the book wants you to burn."

We looked at each other across the lamplight, across the smell of scorched linen and copper, and neither of us said the obvious next thing, which was that if her cage was failing and the storm realm was leaning on the seam, then the countdown on all of us had just gotten shorter — and that the only person in the valley who might know how cages are built was a woman I had never met, riding a carriage of thunder somewhere above us, who had engineered all of this, or none of it, depending on which version of the truth survived contact with morning.

Marguerite closed her eyes. Under their lids, the burst vessels mapped their tiny storms, and I thought: her power isn't gone. It's caged. And the cage is killing her.

And then, through the window, over the ridge where the frost had retreated twelve hard-won yards before collapsing her —

The sky above the Hollow Valley lit up, once, briefly, silently — a single dry flash with no thunder following — like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Like something answering.
