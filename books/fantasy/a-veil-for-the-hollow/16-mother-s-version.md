## 16. Mother's Version

My mother pours the tea herself.

That is the first wrong thing. Seraphine Ishikawa has not poured tea for anyone in forty years — she summons it, or she commands a lesser hand to pour, or she lets the tea simply appear in its cup because weather obeys her and crockery should be flattered to follow suit. But here she stands in my kitchen with the pot tilted at a careful angle, steam curling toward her wrist, and when she sets the cup before me the porcelain doesn't even rattle.

"The cups are Theo's," I say, because I have to say something, and this is what comes out.

"I know." She sits across from me like a woman visiting a grave she paid for. "He bought them in pairs. You only ever use three of the six."

"You've been watching us."

"I've been *waiting* for you." She folds her hands on the table, and up close I can see what distance hid: she has aged since the last time I saw her, which was through a closing door fifteen years ago. Not much. A season's worth, maybe. But storm-callers don't age by accident. Something has been drawing against her account. "The winter is taking payment from all of us, Marguerite. Even from those who thought they'd opted out."

I don't touch the tea. "You wrote the book."

"Yes."

"Every entry."

"Every entry." No flinch. She meets my eyes the way she used to meet a rising front — head-on, unblinking, already calculating the wind behind it. "And before you say *trap*, I want you to hear me out. All of me. Not the version of your mother that fits neatly into the shape you cut for her when you were twenty-two and certain of everything."

"I wasn't certain of anything. That was the point."

"It was the *luxury*," she says softly, "of someone whose mother would absorb the consequences."

* * *

She talks, and I hate how well she talks.

This has always been her gift, worse than the storms. My father used to say that Seraphine could argue the sky out of hail, and he meant it as praise, mostly. She begins, as she always begins, with structure — the covenant, the terms, the arithmetic — because she knows I inherited her love of architecture even while I renounced her house.

"The covenant is four hundred years old," she says. "It was never a marriage of equals. It was a brace. The storm realms generate; the valley grounds. Lightning needs somewhere to land that won't shatter, and your valley — chosen precisely *because* it was hollow, magic-dead, a cup turned upside down — was the perfect landing place. In exchange, the valley received mild seasons, long growing days, protection from the worst of what lives above the cloud line." Her finger taps the table once. "A brace holds only if both sides bear load."

"And one side stopped bearing it."

"One side had its strongest caller walk out of it." She doesn't raise her voice. She never raises her voice; the thunder does that for her. "Marguerite. Do you know what happens to a lightning rod when you remove it? The charge doesn't politely wait. It finds the next-best path, and the next-best path is *worse*. I spent fifteen years doing your work. Mine and two others, running ourselves hollow to keep the load balanced, and every year the balance got harder, because the covenant isn't powered by effort. It's powered by *attunement*. By bloodline. And there is exactly one living person attuned strongly enough to anchor the valley side, and she is currently growing basil."

The word lands harder than any insult could have. Basil. My garden. The small, deliberate, mortal life I built stitch by stitch — she holds it up like evidence, and the terrible thing is that it *is* evidence. Everything I chose is real. That's what makes it damning.

"I didn't abandon anything," I say. "I renounced it. There's a difference. The valley took my oath —"

"The valley took your *magic*," she corrects. "Your oath was between you and me, and we both know it. You didn't leave the realm, daughter. You left *me*. And you dressed it up as principle so neither of us would have to call it what it was."

* * *

I make myself drink the tea. It's chamomile, grown in my own garden, dried in my own rafters, and it tastes like nothing. My hands are steady around the cup and I am grateful for that small mercy, because everything else in the room is tilting.

"The prophecy," I manage. "The date. Theo's face drawn in ink older than he is. Explain that as diagnosis."

"The book doesn't predict," she says. "It *measures*. It's an instrument, Marguerite — the oldest one we have. It reads the stress in the covenant the way a seismograph reads strain in stone. When the failure became irreversible — when the last bridge refused, ninety-nine years ago — the instrument began writing the correction. Not because I commanded it. Because no one else could read it anymore, and someone had to translate."

"Translate into a picture of my lover?"

"Into a picture of the *solution*." For the first time, something moves behind her composure — not guilt, exactly. Regret's well-dressed cousin. "I didn't choose him. Do you understand that? I would have given anything for the book to show anyone else. I checked it every year for fifteen years, hoping the ink would change, hoping the valley had found another way. It never did. He was written before you ever met him. Before you ever *left*, if we're being exact about timelines, which I know you love to be."

"That's supposed to comfort me? That you've known his face was in a death sentence since before I knew his name?"

"It's supposed to establish that I am not the author of his fate. Only its translator." She leans forward. Up close her eyes are my eyes, thirty years further along, and I have to look at the teapot instead. "The trap you're imagining — the arranged marriage, the revenge, the mother who punished her daughter by dooming the man she loved — I need you to actually consider whether that person exists. Consider what I did instead. I let you go. Fifteen years, Marguerite. I could have contested the exile. The council wanted to. I stood in that chamber and told them the valley's claim was valid and the oath was binding and my daughter was dead to the realm. Do you know what that cost me? Politically? Personally?"

"You let them believe I was dead to you."

"I let *you* believe it," she says, "because a clean wound closes faster than a dirty one. And then I came here today anyway, because the clean wound has festered into something that will kill us all, and I would rather you hate me informed than hate me comfortable."

* * *

There is a silence after that. A long one. Through the window I can see our garden — the flattened ruin of it, from the surge, the torn beds and the snapped frames — and past it, at the tree line, the frost line sitting where it shouldn't be, patient as arithmetic.

"Theo goes willingly," she says quietly, "and the winter lifts. The valley keeps its people alive. The realms keep their sky. One man, who by the book's own reading is reforged rather than destroyed — the burning is transformation, daughter, not annihilation, I have read the archivist-storms' commentary on that passage more times than you have — one man, transformed into the thing that holds two worlds together. Against that: everyone. Every farm, every child, every generation downstream of ours. You tell me where the trap is. Show me the self-interest. Because I have looked for it, and all I find is the same conclusion arriving by every road: the covenant requires a bridge, and bridges burn."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then the winter finishes what the cowardice started, and in a hundred years some other mother sits in some other kitchen explaining arithmetic to some other daughter." She rises, smooths her coat — thunder-grey, immaculate, absurdly formal for my kitchen — and looks down at me with an expression I finally recognize, because I wore it in the mirror for years after I left: the face of a person who has done the math so many times the numbers have worn grooves. "You think I'm here to take him from you. I'm here because I watched you carry the weight alone once and swore I'd never ask it again — and then the sky asked anyway, and I am not strong enough to refuse the sky. Neither are you. That's the inheritance I actually gave you."

At the door she pauses. Doesn't turn around.

"He'll decide on his own. I've made sure of that — no compulsion, no pressure from the court, the Warden enforces willingness, you know this. Whatever he chooses, he chooses freely. I want you to remember, later, that I told you that truthfully." A beat. "The stew was burnt, by the way. I saw. He proposed over burnt stew. It was the most human thing I've witnessed in a century."

Then she steps off the porch and the mist takes her, and she is gone the way weather goes — everywhere at once, then nowhere.

* * *

I sit at the table until the tea goes cold.

Here is the problem, and I set it down in my mind as carefully as she set down the cup: every word she said was true. Not righteous — true. I have lived inside her arguments my whole life; I know their grain the way a carpenter knows wood, and there is no rot in this one. The covenant *is* failing. The exile *did* remove the strongest caller. The book *is* older than her malice, if she has malice, and I cannot find the seam where the lie would live.

Which means either my mother has finally learned to build without flaws —

— or the flaw is somewhere I haven't looked yet. Somewhere structural. Somewhere in the foundation, in the part of the design nobody questions because it has always been there.

*Bridges burn,* she said. *Bridges always burn.*

Always. As in: every time. As in: no one has ever tried anything else, in four hundred years, because the ones who might try something else are the ones the arithmetic counts as fuel.

I look at the window. At the frost line. At the garden Theo and I are going to replant, because we are people who replant things.

And upstairs, faintly, I hear the scratch of a quill I know he doesn't own.

The book is writing again.
