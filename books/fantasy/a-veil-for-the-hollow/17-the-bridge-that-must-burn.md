## 17. The Bridge That Must Burn

Marguerite went to the founders' council with her spine straight and her jaw set like a woman walking into weather she'd already decided to stand in. Theo watched her go from the kitchen window, the frost-line visible past her shoulder — a gray seam across the valley's green, a mile closer than it had been three days ago.

"I'll be back by dark," she'd said. Which meant: don't follow me. Which meant: I need you to not be there when I say the thing I'm going to say.

He washed the dishes instead of arguing. He dried them badly. He stood in the middle of their kitchen holding a dish towel and listening to the house settle around him the way it always did at dusk, except now every creak sounded like a countdown.

Then he looked at the shelf.

The prophecy book had been sitting on it for two weeks like a loaded thing — grandmother's binding, sealed clasp, pages that grew back when you burned them. Marguerite kept it face-down now. She'd started doing that after the entry about tomorrow appeared, as if a book couldn't watch them if it was lying on its stomach.

Theo crossed the room and picked it up.

The clasp opened for him without resistance. It had never done that before; he'd watched Marguerite fight with it twice, her fingers slipping on bronze that should have been warm from her hands but wasn't. For him, the metal was warm on first touch. He tried not to read anything into that. He failed immediately.

*Fine,* he told the book silently. *You want to talk to me? Talk.*

He carried it to the table, set a lamp beside it, sat down the way you sit down across from someone at an interrogation. The pages fell open to the newest entry — tomorrow's date, written in ink that still gleamed wet — and above it, for the first time, the older text had begun to surface. Faint lines rising through the paper like drowned things coming up for air, words that had been blank for decades deciding, all at once, to exist.

He began to read.

---

*In the hundredth winter of the Hollow's keeping, the veil shall thin and the covenant starve, for the bridge that was promised has been withheld these ninety-nine years, and arithmetic does not forgive.*

*When the frost crosses the third milestone, a bridge shall be named. Not called. Named. There is a difference, though the ones who wait will tell you otherwise.*

*The bridge is mortal. The bridge is loved. The bridge is chosen by the book and not by the court, which is why the court's hands are clean and the book's are not.*

*And here is the part they do not say aloud, the part Seraphine wrote small, in the margin, in a hand that shakes:*

*The burning is not an ending.*

Theo stopped. Read it again. *The burning is not an ending.* His heart was doing something stupid and hopeful against his ribs, and he made himself distrust it, because hope was exactly the kind of thing a prophecy book would use as bait.

He turned the page.

*Understand what the old bridges understood, what the last one did not. Mortal flesh is the only material in either realm that can hold a covenant, because only mortal flesh knows how to change. Stone endures. Storm endures. A man who walks into the fire of the veil is not consumed — he is reforged. His stillness becomes the anchor. His warmth becomes the hearth-light the realms share. He does not die into the covenant. He becomes it. Every storm that breaks gently over the valley thereafter will break over his shoulders. Every seed that wakes in spring will wake because something in him said: enough cold, grow now.*

*This is the mercy no one believes until they stand at the altar: the bridge does not stop being. He stops being only himself.*

*The last bridge refused. He stood where you will stand, felt what you will feel, and chose his own small life over the long arithmetic of two worlds. And so the covenant has starved for ninety-nine years, and the frost walks a mile a day, and the valley he saved for one more summer has been dying ever since, slowly, politely, out of respect for his refusal.*

*Do not be him.*

*Do not be me.*

Theo sat back. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere outside, the wind moved through the herb garden — through Marguerite's garden, through the rows she'd planted the week they'd moved in, basil and thyme and something she swore was called dragon's breath and was probably just mint — and the sound it made was ordinary, was summer, was everything the book said was already dying.

He read the last page.

*The bridge must come willingly. That word — willingly — is load-bearing. A dragged bridge holds nothing. But willingness is a door with two sides, and the book can only open one. The other side opens from inside a man, and no prophecy, however patient, can reach it.*

*So this is not a command. This is a request, sent forward through a hundred years by a woman who watched the last bridge walk away and spent the rest of her life wondering if she should have said something kinder, or something crueler, or anything at all.*

*I am sorry it is you. Whoever you are. I am sorry it is anyone.*

*But if you are reading this whole — breathing, unburnt, loved by a storm-caller who gave up half her life to sit in a garden with you — then know that the fire is not what you fear. What you fear is the version of yourself that walks away. I have met him. He lives in my mirror, and he is very persuasive, and he has cost me everything I was trying to protect.*

*Burn, and become. Refuse, and remain. Both are survivable. Only one of them saves anyone else.*

*— E.V., keeper of the hollow side, ninety-ninth winter*

E.V. Theo mouthed the initials. Elowen Vance. He'd heard the name a hundred times — the founder, the gardener, the one whose portrait hung crooked in the meeting hall because nobody could agree how straight to hang a dead woman. Bramble talked about her like scripture.

Vance.

His surname. His family name, carried down through generations of valley farmers who'd never mentioned, not once, that their line had signed itself into a covenant a century ago.

"Okay," Theo said aloud, to the empty kitchen, because saying it aloud made it slightly less like drowning. "Okay."

---

He didn't decide right away. That was the strange part — he'd expected decision to be a lightning strike, a single white moment, and instead it came the way dusk comes, so gradually that you look up and it's already happened.

He made tea he didn't drink. He walked the garden path with the lamp off, feeling for the frost-line in the air the way you feel for a draft under a door. At the boundary of the property, where the cold began, he stood with his hands in his pockets and let it push against him, testing him, and thought: *it's a mile closer tonight.*

Ninety-nine years of polite dying. A man who walked away and everyone paid for it. And somewhere in that ledger, a line item with his name on it — not because he'd earned it, not because he'd sinned, just because the book had opened its eye one evening and seen him laughing over burnt stew with a woman who traded half her life for the right to love him quietly.

He thought about the proposal. The ring currently hidden in the toe of his winter boot, because Marguerite checked pockets and he'd learned that about her in month two. He thought about the wedding they might have had in the meeting hall, Bramble droning, the villagers awkwardly festive. He thought about fifty years of mornings like this one — coffee, arguments about thyme, her hair going silver while his went whatever direction it pleased.

Fifty years. Except not fifty. Half her life already spent, and if the frost reached the valley center before the covenant broke entirely, not even that.

There was no version of staying where she didn't die watching him age, or worse, died first, or worst of all, they both lived long enough to bury the world together. He ran the arithmetic Bramble would run, the cold kind, the honest kind. One man's selfhood against two realms. One man's mornings against everyone's springs.

Except the book hadn't asked for sacrifice. That was the thing nobody had told him — maybe nobody knew. Bramble spoke of it like arithmetic, a body on a scale. Seraphine spoke of it like ransom. Even the Warden's mist-voice had made it sound like a toll booth. But the oldest page, the truest page, written by a hand that shook: *he becomes it.* Stillness as anchor. Warmth as hearth-light. Every gentle storm breaking over his shoulders.

Not an ending. A translation.

He was a man who fixed fences and burned stew and remembered birthdays. He was, by every measure the storm realm cared about, nothing. And the book wanted him precisely for that — wanted the stillness, the ordinariness, the mortal stubbornness of a creature who plants things knowing he won't live to see the oak. That wasn't being used. Or — it was, but it was also being *meant*, and he'd gone thirty-four years without once being meant by anything larger than a small woman with storm-gray eyes, and God help him, it was hard to refuse the first offer.

He laughed at himself, alone in the dark garden. The laugh came out wrong.

Because there was the other arithmetic, the one that actually mattered: Marguerite. She would not see it this way. She would see a trap wearing a prophecy's clothes, a mother's revenge dressed up as destiny, and she'd be half right — he had no illusions about Seraphine, no illusions about margins written in shaking hands. Maybe the transformation was real. Maybe it was also exactly what Seraphine wanted, and both things could be true at once, and the truth wouldn't care which motive delivered him to the altar.

She would fight him. She had fought a council of founders tonight with nothing but her voice, and won, probably, because she was Marguerite. If he told her, she would find a third option or invent one or tear the veil open with her bare hands trying, and the frost would keep walking the whole time, and they would lose by inches while refusing to lose by choice.

Some doors only open from inside. He'd read that an hour ago and it was already becoming the shape of his spine.

So he did the hardest thing he'd ever done, harder than any altar: he decided to lie to her. To take the one person who had never lied to him — who had confessed exile and halved lifespans and a mother's treachery with the same flat honesty — and give her a false morning.

He went inside and packed.

Not much. The book said nothing about luggage, but it felt obscene to cross between worlds empty-handed, so: the good knife. The wool coat she hated because it made him look like a shepherd. A photograph — the two of them, first autumn, her squinting, him mid-laugh, the garden behind them looking embarrassingly alive. And the ring. He took the ring out of the boot and held it a long time under the lamp, turning it, and finally put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, where a lie keeps its passport.

Then he copied the pages.

Every word, in his own blunt handwriting, because if the book rewrote itself overnight — and it would, it was growing like mold toward tomorrow — he wanted proof of what it had said tonight, before doubt or Seraphine or his own cowardice could revise it. He copied the mercy. He copied the apology. He copied *do not be me*, and his hand shook worse than the original author's.

He hid the copy under the loose floorboard in the bedroom, beneath a layer of her winter scarves, and felt like a burglar in his own house.

* * *

She came home near midnight. He heard her boots on the step and arranged himself at the table with the lamp low and a cup of cold tea, performing calm the way he performed everything — adequately, with effort.

She came in carrying the council's verdict in her posture. Whatever she'd said to them, she'd said it and survived it, and the frost would answer for it tonight, and she knew it. Her eyes found him and softened and then sharpened all in the same breath, the way they did when she was cataloguing him for damage.

"You waited up."

"Somebody has to guard the burnt stew." He nodded at the pot on the stove. "It's achieved a new consistency. Scientists would be interested."

"Theo." She sat down across from him, close, knees touching his, her hands finding his hands the way they always did, like docking a boat. Up close he could see the exhaustion under her skin, the fine tremor in her fingers that hadn't been there a month ago. The cage was killing her. He'd read that in a book tonight too, in the space between lines.

"Council go okay?" he asked. "Other than the part where you declared war on a hundred-year-old institution?"

"They didn't declare war back. That's how I know it's bad. Old men who agree to think about it are men who've already decided." She exhaled. "Bramble wants his sacrifice. Half the council agrees with him. The other half is scared of him. And the frost doesn't care what any of us want — it crossed the second milestone an hour ago. I felt it from the road."

"Marge." He turned his hands over and took hers properly. Here it was, then. The false morning, loading itself into his mouth. "Listen to me. Tomorrow, I want to do something. Just us. Before any of it — the councils, the Warden, your mother, all of it. One ordinary day. Walk to the falls, waste some time, eat something I burn. Can we do that? Can we just have tomorrow?"

She studied him. She had a way of studying him that made him feel transparent, made him sure she could see the ring in his pocket and the copy under the floorboards and the whole architecture of the lie, and he held still under it and let her look, because flinching was the only confession available to him.

"One ordinary day," she repeated.

"That's all. Nothing prophetic. No dates written anywhere. Just a Tuesday."

A smile moved through her tiredness like sun through cloud. "You hate Tuesdays. You say they're Mondays with better marketing."

"I'm growing. People change."

"People change," she agreed softly, and leaned in and put her forehead against his, and for a moment neither of them breathed, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere north the frost walked another foot through the grass, patient as arithmetic.

"I love you," she said. Not dramatically. Like reporting the weather. Like the one forecast she trusted.

"I know," Theo said. "I love you too."

All of it true. That was the unforgivable design of it — every word true, every word a wall. He kissed her hair and held her while she drifted toward sleep in the chair, and over her shoulder he watched the prophecy book on the shelf, face-down, its clasp warm, its next entry already drying somewhere in its patient dark.

Tomorrow, then. One ordinary day. The last one, whichever way it broke.

He closed his eyes and practiced the morning: the easy shrug, the joke at the trailhead, the whole long performance of a man with nowhere to be. He rehearsed it until he almost believed it himself.

Almost.
