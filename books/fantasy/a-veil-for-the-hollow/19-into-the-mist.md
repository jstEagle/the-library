## 19. Into the Mist

**Theo**

He woke before the alarm he'd never set, because his body had known for three days that it would have to do this without being asked.

Marguerite was still asleep beside him, one arm flung across his chest like a mooring line. In the gray pre-dawn light her face looked younger and older at once — the exhaustion of the past weeks carved into it, but also something unguarded he hadn't seen since before the book opened itself on their shelf. He lay very still and memorized the weight of her arm. That was the plan. Memorize everything. The burnt-stew smell that had gotten into the kitchen walls permanently. The crooked gate. The way she said his name when she wasn't worried about anything, which he realized now had been almost never lately.

The bag was already packed. It sat by the door where he'd moved it last night after she fell asleep, and every hour he'd woken to check it was still there — not because he feared it would leave, but because he kept hoping *he* would. That some better version of himself would have thought of a third option by morning.

There was no third option. Not yet. There was only the first one, and dawn coming up over the frost-line like a slow white tide.

He slid out from under her arm an inch at a time. She didn't stir. The floorboards were cold in a way floors shouldn't be in summer, and when he breathed out, his breath showed.

*One foot per hour,* Bramble had said, back when it was still possible to pretend the arithmetic belonged to someone else. *Faster this week.*

Theo dressed in the dark. Sweater, coat, the good boots Marguerite had bought him two birthdays ago because valley winters were mild and he'd arrived with nothing. He took the copy of the prophecy page out of the book's hiding place and then, after a moment, left it folded under the honey jar where she would find it. She deserved the lie in her own handwriting terms: *Gone to talk to the Warden. Back by noon.* Eleven words, ten of them true.

At the door he stopped and looked back at the bed. She had turned toward the warm place he'd left, chasing it in her sleep, and the sight of that did more damage to his resolve than any argument the storm-court could have made.

"I'm coming back," he told her quietly. "That part I'm not lying about."

She didn't answer. Outside, the sky was the color of old pewter, and the mist at the valley's eastern boundary stood in a wall, waiting, the way it had waited for a hundred years.

He walked.

* * *

The village was empty at this hour except for Old Man Bramble, who was sitting on his porch as if he'd been installed there overnight, part of the architecture. His eyes went straight to Theo's bag.

"So it's you," Bramble said. No surprise. Maybe a flicker of something that in a younger face might have been regret.

"It's me."

"The book chose well, then. Or badly." Bramble rose slowly, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "I walked that road once, boy. Sixty years ago. My brother walked it instead of me." He said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "I've been paying interest ever since."

"I'm sorry," Theo said, and meant it, and kept walking.

"Vance." Bramble's voice caught him at the gate. "The Warden will ask you if you come willingly. Whatever else you are — whatever you've decided about what happens on the other side — don't lie to it. The mist can taste a lie. And the covenant—" the old man's hands shook, just slightly, "—the covenant has been fed enough lies."

Theo nodded and went out into the street, and behind him he heard Bramble say, so softly it might have been meant for no one: "Tell my brother's people the debt is coming home."

The frost began a half mile past the last house. Grass silvered, then whitened, then stiffened into crystal. By the time he reached the boundary, the fields on either side of the road were a single held breath, and the mist wall rose ahead of him, forty feet of slow-churning gray with no top edge he could find, just a softening into the color of the sky.

The Warden was there. Of course the Warden was there. It had been there every day since it spoke its one sentence to Marguerite — a figure roughly human-shaped, woven from moving vapor, features suggested rather than fixed, like a face seen through rain on glass. Up close it smelled, absurdly, like the air before summer rain. Like the inside of Marguerite's old stories.

"Theo Vance," it said. Its voice was wind through a keyhole. "You are early."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know what you carry." Something in the mist shifted; he couldn't tell if it was the figure or the whole wall. "I will ask once, and your answer will be tasted, weighed, remembered. Do you come willingly?"

He thought of Marguerite asleep, chasing his warmth. He thought of the altar in the storm realm, which he had never seen but which lived behind his eyes anyway, drawn from the prophecy's cold ink: *the bridge that must burn.* He thought — honestly, finally — about how much of his decision was love and how much was fear of being the man who stayed safe while everything froze, and he found he couldn't separate them cleanly, and decided that was probably what willingness actually felt like.

"Yes," he said. "Willingly. Ask me again at the other side if you want."

The Warden regarded him for a long moment. Then it extended a hand of vapor, and where its fingers touched his palm, cold wrote itself into his skin — the sigil, the mark he'd earned days ago testing the boundary, burning brighter now, a small silver brand shaped like a knot with no beginning.

"The covenant requires two," the Warden said.

"What?"

But the mist was already opening. A corridor parted in the wall, smooth-walled, lit faintly from within, leading to a grayness that was not the grayness of fog but the grayness of *elsewhere*. Wind came through it and it was wrong wind — wet with lightning, tasting of iron and ozone and something older. Every hair on his body stood up. His heart slammed against his ribs like something trying to get out before it was too late.

He took one step. Then another. Behind him the valley lay pale and frozen and beautiful, the roofs of the village just visible, the chimney of his house among them, no smoke because he'd let the fire die last night so she wouldn't wake to warmth and wonder.

*Back by noon,* the note said.

He turned around one last time, and that was when he saw her.

* * *

**Marguerite**

She had known since midnight, the way you know a storm is coming through the ache in old scars — not because anyone told her, but because the lie had a shape, and she had spent fifteen years learning the shape of leaving-from-love from the inside.

She didn't run at first. Running was for people who could still stop him. She ran anyway, down the frozen lane with her breath tearing white flags behind her, past shuttered houses, past Bramble's empty porch, past the fields gone to glass. Her lungs burned. Fifteen years of valley air, thin and quiet and dead of everything but oxygen, and now her body seemed to remember what it was preparing for and grieve it in advance.

He was a dark shape against the lighter dark of the corridor in the mist. Ten steps in. Eight.

"Theo!"

He turned. Even at this distance she could see the moment the lie died on his face — the collapse of it, all the careful architecture of *back by noon* falling down between them across the frozen ground.

"Marguerite, no—" He started back toward her, which was somehow worse than him walking away. "Go home. Please. Please go home."

"The covenant requires two," she said, and her voice came out steadier than anything in her felt. "That's what it says. That's what it's always going to say, because that's what it means. You're not crossing alone, Theo, because you were never going to be the bridge alone. Read the whole page next time. Page forty-one. The line you copied stops mid-sentence."

His face did something complicated. "You knew. How long have you—"

"Since you packed the bag. Since before that. Since the night the candles bent, maybe, if I'm honest, which apparently is the hour for it." She walked toward him, toward the corridor, and the closer she got the louder the wrong-wind became, and underneath the wind she heard it — a sound like the whole sky clearing its throat. "I spent fifteen years believing the bravest thing I ever did was leave. Leave the storms, leave my mother, leave all of it, so that nobody could use me and nobody could be used *against* me. I called it protection. I called it love." She stopped an arm's length from him, close enough to see the frost melting on his collar from the heat of him, her heat, standing too near. "It was just solitude with good manners. I'm not doing it twice."

"The altar, Marguerite—" His voice cracked. "What it wants, what the book says it wants—"

"I know what it says. I also know my grandmother's handwriting, and I know when a sentence is missing its second clause, and I know my mother has lied to me in writing my entire life. We'll read it there. Together." She reached out and took his hand, the marked hand, the sigil warm as a coin held too long. "We go together or the whole thing fails. Those aren't my rules. But they're the only ones I'll play by."

Behind them both, the Warden's voice rolled out of the mist like a verdict:

"She speaks the truth of it. The covenant requires two. It always required two. One to hold the mortal thread. One to hold the storm." A pause, and then something that was almost gentleness, in a voice made of wind: "It has been a long time since anyone asked what the bridge was tied to."

"Then we're overdue," Theo said.

She loved him for that. She would love him for less, but she loved him for that.

They turned to the corridor together.

Crossing the threshold was like stepping into a held breath. For one stride nothing happened, and she thought — wildly, hopelessly — *maybe exile doesn't end until I say it does, maybe I can walk through carrying the valley with me like a stone in my shoe.*

Then the magic hit.

It came up through the soles of her feet and down through the crown of her head at once, fifteen dammed years breaking all their levees in the same instant, and she screamed. She was dimly aware of Theo catching her, of her knees hitting something that wasn't quite ground, of light — not lightning yet, just light, raw and sourceless — pouring out of her own skin. Her lungs tore. That was the only word for it. The valley had wrapped them in cotton wool for a decade and a half and now every breath was glass and silk and thunder all at once, and somewhere very far away the Warden was saying something in a language older than either realm and the corridor was folding shut behind them like a wound closing.

She couldn't tell, in the white roar, whether she was dying or being born. It felt precisely, terribly like both.

And then — a hand in hers. Squeezing. Theo's voice, arriving from an enormous distance, grounded and ordinary and human as bread: *"Hey. Hey. Look at me. Breathe with me. One, two — come on, garden-voice, breathe like I taught you, in for the planting, out for the harvest—"*

She breathed. In for the planting. Out for the harvest. The light in her skin banked down to embers. Her lungs, torn, began the slow work of learning a new element.

When she could see again, they were lying tangled together on the far side, and the world around them was the storm realm's borderland: cloud underfoot firm as moss, air trembling with distant constant lightning, a horizon that glowed instead of ended. Above them the Warden's form stretched tall and thin against the glowing sky, no longer a figure at a gate but something vaster, a keeper taking up its full height now that it stood on its own soil.

"The bridge," the Warden said, "and the caller. Two halves of an old whole." Its face-rain seemed to shift, and though it had no eyes she felt looked through, thoroughly, and — accepted. "The court waits at the Falling Weather. They have waited a hundred years." Another shift, and the almost-gentleness again: "Some of them hoped no one would come. Some of them prayed someone would. You will find both kinds wearing the same faces."

Theo pulled her up, and she leaned into him, still shaking, magic crawling under her skin like a returning tide over rocks — painful, glorious, hers.

"Your lungs," he said quietly. "Are you—"

"I'm alive." She laughed, and the laugh came out with a crackle of static in it, and she heard him catch his breath at the sound of it. Fifteen years since her laugh had done that. "Theo. Listen. My laugh does that again."

He smiled, and it was a wrecked, terrified, beautiful smile, the smile of a man holding a lit fuse and refusing to drop it. "Yeah. I heard."

Above them, the clouds parted along a seam, and through it she saw the towers of the Court of Falling Weather rising from a thunderhead like coral grown from a dream — spires of storm-glass, bridges of braided wind, and at the highest window a light turning toward them like a slow eye.

Her mother was already watching.

Of course she was. Seraphine had written this story in ink before either of them was born into it.

"Come on," Marguerite said, and laced her fingers through his, sigil-palm to static-skin, mortal thread to storm. "Let's go ruin her ending."

They walked up into the lightning together.
