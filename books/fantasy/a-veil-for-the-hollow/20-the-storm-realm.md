## 20. The Storm Realm

The first thing the storm realm did was try to kill me.

I say that with affection. It was only being born again, and birth is violent for everyone involved.

We came through the veil at dawn — valley dawn, gray and thin — and crossed into something that had no use for dawns at all. The sky here was not a sky. It was a country. Cloud stretched in every direction, layered like sedimentary rock, lit from within by lightning that never struck, only bloomed — great silent pulses of white and violet rolling through the depths of the atmosphere the way light rolls through deep water. There was no sun. There didn't need to be. The storms were the sun, and they had been shining for longer than my family had been saying their names.

My lungs tore.

Not metaphor. Not poetry. I felt the actual tissue of my breathing separate and reknit as fifteen years of exile ended in a single step, and everything I had renounced came flooding home through the wound it left behind. Theo caught my arm — I heard him shout my name from very far away, though his mouth was inches from my ear — and then I was on my knees on ground that wasn't ground, some woven surface of compressed cloud-stuff, silver-gray and faintly warm, and I was drowning in air.

Because that's what magic is, if you've truly given it up. It's in the air. It's in everything here. Every breath was a transfusion of a blood type my body remembered and my mind had spent a decade and a half pretending to forget.

It knew me anyway.

* * *

The pain came in tides. I would surface, and the world would be there — impossibly beautiful, impossibly vast — and then a new wave would hit and pull me under into white noise and the taste of copper. Between waves, Theo held me. He couldn't do anything else. There is no first aid for a soul reoccupying its old house.

"Talk to me," he kept saying. "Marguerite. Talk to me. Tell me what it feels like."

"Like drinking the ocean," I managed, once.

"Good ocean or bad ocean?"

"Yes."

He laughed, that shaky laugh he uses when he's terrified and refuses to let it win, and I loved him so much in that moment that it hurt worse than the magic did, which is saying something, because the magic felt like being unmade and remade by patient enormous hands.

Here is what I had forgotten: magic has a flavor. Storm-calling tastes like the air ten seconds before rain — that green metallic lift at the back of the throat. For fifteen years I had breathed dead air and called it peace. Now every inhale was that moment before the storm, sustained, unending, and my body wept with relief even as it convulsed.

The power didn't come back like water returning to a dry well. It came back like a caged animal that has chewed through the bars. I could feel it all at once — all of it, the full inheritance of six generations of Ishikawa storm-callers, the deepest reservoir in the realm's southern reaches, Seraphine's daughter, the prodigy, the runaway — and it had been pressed down into me for fifteen years like snow packed into ice, and now the weight of it wanted *out*.

Lightning moved under my skin. I could see it glowing through my own forearms, blue-white threads tracing the paths of old scars I'd never had, veins of light mapping a geography my body had kept secret even from itself.

"Don't hold it," a voice said. "Let it pass through you. You're holding it like grief."

I looked up.

* * *

They had gathered while I drowned. Beings out of the cloud-country, standing in a loose ring at a respectful distance — and I say *beings* because *people* undersells it and *creatures* insults them. They were shaped the way smoke is shaped when it decides to be helpful: tall, robed in weather, faces that resolved differently depending on where you stood, so that no two observers ever saw quite the same face. Lightning ran through them the way blood runs through us — visible sometimes near the surface, a pulse along a throat, a flicker in the hollow of a collarbone.

Storm-folk. My mother's people. My people, technically, by whatever accounting matters.

The one who had spoken stepped forward. She was older than the others — you could tell by her weather; the young ones crackled and snapped but her lightning moved slow and deep, summer-storm light rather than squall. Her face settled into an arrangement of features that looked, deliberately, I think, like something out of our family's oldest portraits.

"You are Ishikawa," she said. Not a question. "Daughter of Seraphine. Daughter of Yolanda. Daughter of Meridian." A pause, and then, with something that in a human face would have been a smile: "Who was daughter to me."

My breath stopped — the good kind of stopping, this time.

"You knew my grandmother's grandmother," I said.

"I raised her, in the way we raise. She sat where you are sitting, when she first crossed. She also drowned in the air." The old storm-being tilted her head, and her face rearranged itself into something gentler. "You have her hands. She also clenched them when she was afraid and called it thinking."

I looked down. My fists were white-knuckled in my lap. I made myself open them.

"What do I call you?"

"Names are heavy here. We wear them rarely." She considered. "Your grandmother's grandmother called me Aunt Thunder, because she was four and it stuck. Four hundred years, and I am still Aunt Thunder. This is the price of loving small creatures. They name you once and forever."

Theo made a sound beside me that I recognized as a laugh trying to escape custody. When I turned to look at him, he was pale, awed, gripping my shoulder like an anchor chain, taking in the coral cities rising behind the ring of storm-folk — and they were cities, I could see them clearly now, spires and terraces grown out of thunderheads like reef out of rock, architecture that had never known a mason's hand, walls of compacted storm that glowed faintly from inside with trapped, sleeping lightning. Bridges arched between towers across canyons of empty luminous air. Waterfalls fell upward in places, feeding the clouds. Somewhere distant, thunder rolled in long conversational rumbles, and I realized with a chill that it had rhythm. Pattern. It was language. The whole realm was talking to itself, all the time, and I had just come home to a conversation that had been going on since before my species learned to make fire.

"It's real," Theo said softly. He said it the way a man says it when he has believed a thing intellectually for months and only now, finally, believes it. "It's all real."

"He's mortal," Aunt Thunder said, to me, not to him, and the temperature of her voice dropped several degrees. "You brought a mortal across the veil."

"I brought the bridge."

The whole ring of storm-folk went still. Even the ambient crackle of them quieted. Lightning paused mid-bloom in the deep sky, hanging like a held note.

"The bridge," Aunt Thunder repeated. Her slow-summer eyes moved from me to Theo and back. "Then the Court must be told. And Marguerite Ishikawa—" my name in her mouth sounded old, older than the portraits, like it had been waiting in storage, "—you should know before we go: the realm remembers why you left. Some of them remember it angrily."

* * *

They gave us time to walk. I don't know if it was mercy or protocol or both.

The path into the city was a bridge of woven cloud-glass, and beneath it the abyss of the realm opened up — layer after layer of luminous atmosphere descending toward a glow too deep and constant to call lightning anymore. It was more like looking down into the mantle of the planet, if the planet were made of weather. Things lived down there. I saw shapes moving in the lower dark, vast and unhurried, whales of storm, and one of them sang — a subsonic moan I felt in my teeth and in the roots of my surviving mortality.

Theo walked close. His hand found mine and held on.

"Your heart rate is insane," he said. "I can feel it from here."

"That would be the magic coming back. Or the terror. Coin flip."

"Which one's winning?"

"They've merged. It's very efficient." I squeezed his hand. "How are your lungs? The air's thin for mortals, thinner the higher we go—"

"My lungs are fine. Your aunt apparently thinks I'm kindling, but my lungs are great."

I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong — came out with a crackle in it, a static discharge, tiny sparks jumping between my lips. I clapped a hand over my mouth. Theo stared.

"Did you just — did your *laugh*—"

"Fifteen years of renunciation, voided in one afternoon. Apparently even my sense of humor conducts now." I took my hand away from my mouth. More sparks danced across my knuckles. I watched them with a feeling I couldn't name — horror and homesickness braided so tight I couldn't find the seam. "Theo. I need to say something before we get to the city and it gets loud."

"Okay."

"When I left here — when I exiled myself — I told everyone, including myself, that it was about escaping the marriage my mother arranged. And that was true. But it was also — God, it's hard to say this standing on a bridge made of weather." I breathed. The breath tasted like every summer storm of my childhood. "It was also because I hated it here. I hated being a vessel. Everyone in that court knew what I was going to be before I could walk, and none of them asked what I wanted, and the valley was the first place anyone ever met me without a résumé." I looked at him. "You met me without a résumé."

"You handed me a leaky watering can and told me the basil was dying and it was my fault."

"The basil was dying and it was your fault."

"And then you fixed it with your hands, no sparks, no lightning, nothing, and I thought, *huh, she's good in a crisis.*" His voice cracked halfway through and he covered it with a grin. "Best mistake I ever made with a watering can."

And there it was, the thing that made the wonder ache: I was home, and home was glorious, and home was the place I had fled, and the man who made anywhere bearable was mortal and marked and walking willingly toward a court that had already named him fuel. Grief isn't always about loss that's happened. Sometimes it's grief in advance, standing in a beautiful place, knowing the bill hasn't arrived yet but the paper is already printed.

The magic kept pouring in. By the time we reached the city gates — gates that were not barriers but thresholds of recognition, archways of fused storm-glass that flickered as I passed beneath them, tasting my bloodline and finding it on file — I was vibrating with power like a struck bell that wouldn't finish its note. My hair lifted around my face. Small objects drifted toward me and fell away. Somewhere in my chest, behind my sternum, something that had been folded small for fifteen years unfolded to its full height and stretched, and I nearly went to my knees again, not from pain this time but from sheer room.

This was what I had been. This was what I'd traded away for quiet gardens and burnt stew and a man who proposed over ruined dinner.

This was what I would give half of, if the covenant demanded it. I understood that suddenly, completely, standing under the gate. Whatever bargain we forged, part of *this* — this vastness, this weather in the blood — would be the currency. And I found, to my surprise, that I already knew I would pay it. Not because the realm deserved it. Because he was standing next to me, mortal and brave and out of his depth, and love has always been the only arithmetic I trust.

* * *

The Court of Falling Weather convened in a hall grown like the inside of a shell — a spiraling chamber whose walls were slow thunderheads, compressing and releasing in a rhythm like breathing, so that the whole space seemed alive around us. Storm-folk filled the tiers in their hundreds, faces resolving and dissolving, conversations happening in rumbles below human hearing. The sound of the assembly was like standing inside a wave.

Aunt Thunder led us to the floor. Every eye in the place — every shifting, lightning-shot eye — was on Theo.

On Theo, not me. I had prepared to be the scandal: the runaway heir, the traitor's daughter, the caller who broke covenant with her own blood. But their attention slid past me like I was scenery. It was the mortal they stared at. The impossible thing. The soft creature made of minutes, walking upright in a realm where beings lived for millennia.

"The bridge," someone said from the tiers, and the word ran around the spiral hall like fire down a fuse, in a dozen registers of thunder, until it came back to the floor as a single held resonance that made my restored magic shiver in sympathy.

Aunt Thunder raised one hand, and the hall stilled.

"The Court of Falling Weather is summoned," she said, and her voice was the voice of the deep storms now, vast and slow. "The winter eats two worlds. The veil thins. And the last of the Ishikawa line has returned across it, bringing with her—" a pause I did not like, "—a mortal man who bears the Warden's sigil."

Every face in the hall turned toward Theo's palm.

And high above, in the uppermost tier, where the eldest of the storm-court sat in shadowed majesty, a figure rose — and the figure wore my mother's face.

She was not supposed to be here yet. She was supposed to descend later, on her own terms, in her carriage of thunder, with her offer and her charm offensive fully loaded. I know my mother. She choreographs everything.

But she was here, and she was already rising, and her expression as she looked down at her daughter — soaked in returned magic, shaking, holding a mortal's hand — was not the mask she wears for courts.

It was something rawer. And it scared me more than anything the realm had shown me yet.

"Mother," I said, and my voice, full of fifteen years of storm, cracked the air of the chamber like a whip.
