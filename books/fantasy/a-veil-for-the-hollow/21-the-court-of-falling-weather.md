## 21. The Court of Falling Weather

The storm realm did not have a floor. That was the first thing Theo understood, standing on a terrace of cloud that held his weight the way a memory holds a name — loosely, and only because someone kept repeating it.

Above them, lightning moved through the vaulted dark like veins of light in a leaf. Below, if you could call it below, thunderheads rolled away into distances that had no horizon, only deeper weather. And rising out of it all, grown from the clouds themselves, were towers — spiraled and branching, white-blue and faintly luminous, like coral that had decided to become architecture.

"Try not to stare," Marguerite murmured beside him. Her hand was in his, and her hand was shaking. "They consider staring a question."

"What's the question?"

"*Why are you here.*"

He'd been asking himself that since dawn, since the mist had parted for him like a curtain drawn by an unseen hand while Marguerite had crossed behind him with a sound he would never be able to unhear — a wet, tearing gasp, as if the boundary had reached into her chest and rearranged something on its way past. She'd walked out of it gray-faced and upright, magic flooding back into her in visible shivers, and she hadn't let go of his hand since.

Now she stood straighter than he'd ever seen her, chin lifted, wearing exile like armor she'd just put back on after fifteen years. The storm-folk had come to meet them at the edge of the cloud-terrace: tall figures whose bodies seemed sketched in rain, faces that shifted like weather fronts, eyes that flickered with distant heat lightning. They moved without walking. They simply arrived where they intended to be, one gust at a time.

The largest of them drifted forward. Its face was a slow-moving front, all gray and silver, and when it spoke, its voice was the sound rain makes on a roof when you're safe inside — except nothing about this felt safe.

"The daughter of Seraphine returns." A pause, long enough to have weather in it. "And brings *this.*"

Theo felt every eye — or whatever passed for eyes — settle on him. He was suddenly, acutely aware of himself: soft-skinned, mortal, dressed in a wool coat that belonged to a world where coats made sense. Beside these beings he looked like what he was. A man who grew herbs. A man who burned stew.

A man the prophecy book called *the bridge that must burn.*

"I'm the this," he said. "Yes."

Marguerite's grip tightened. "His name is Theo Vance. He is my—"

"We know what he is," said the large one. "The book told us before you finished reading it. The bridge arrives willingly. The court receives him." It tilted its head, and its face rearranged itself into something almost like courtesy. "You will be honored, mortal. You will be fed. You will be shown wonders no valley-dweller has seen in three hundred years."

"And then?" Theo asked.

The being smiled. It had too many teeth for a smile made of weather, or perhaps exactly the right number, which was worse. "Then the covenant will be renewed, as it has always been renewed. As is right. As is *arithmetic.*"

Bramble's word. Bramble's cold little ledger, carried across the veil. Theo filed that away.

* * *

They were led up through the coral towers along paths of hardened wind, and Theo made himself look at everything, because looking was the only weapon he'd brought.

He saw cities grown inside thunderheads, their spires dripping rain upward. He saw beings weaving lightning between their hands like thread, stitching it into the sky where it hung in slow banners. He saw gardens — the word arrived in his mind before he could stop it — gardens of storm-glass and living gale, beautiful and utterly still in their violence, the same shapes frozen forever.

That was the thing he couldn't stop noticing. Everything here was magnificent and nothing here *changed.* The lightning banners had hung in those skies, he'd have bet anything, for centuries. The towers grew, but slowly, the way glaciers grow. Even the storm-folk themselves, drifting through their endless weather, had the air of people performing a play they'd memorized so long ago they'd forgotten there had ever been words.

"It's dying," Marguerite said quietly, watching him watch. "I told you. The covenant broke, and everything here is running on habit."

"Habit," Theo repeated. "How long?"

"How long since something new happened here?" She considered. "My grandmother used to say the last new thing was the last bridge burning. After that—" she gestured at a tower, at the frozen banners, at the whole gorgeous repetition of it, "—encore. Forever."

* * *

The Court of Falling Weather received them in a hall that was not a hall but a held breath — a vast chamber of compressed cloud, its ceiling alive with slow lightning, its floor a lake of rain that fell upward instead of down. Storm-folk lined the walls in ranks, dozens of them, each more ancient-seeming than the last, their faces cycling through weathers like moods.

At the center, on a dais of packed thundercloud, sat the eldest. Theo knew it was the eldest not by any sign of age but by stillness. Everything else in the room moved. This one didn't. Its face was a single fixed season — deep winter, gray and patient — and when it regarded Theo, he felt like a match being evaluated by a very old, very tired fire.

"The bridge stands before the court," the eldest said. Its voice was quiet, which somehow required the whole chamber to lean in. "Willing. As the book foretold."

"As the book *wrote,*" Marguerite said, stepping forward, and Theo heard the edge in her voice, the blade she'd carried since chapter one of this nightmare. "There's a difference."

"There is not," said a voice from the ranks, "when the ink is older than the reader."

"Mother," Theo said under his breath, "don't." But she was already bristling, storm-light gathering faintly around her fingers like static before a strike, and he understood suddenly that in this place she was not exiled Marguerite who grew thyme and argued about fence posts. She was the strongest caller the realm had produced in generations, and everyone in this room knew it, and everyone in this room was measuring her against a prophecy that wanted her lover dead.

So he stepped forward too. Past her. Into the open.

"I have a question," he said to the eldest. "Before anyone feeds me or honors me or does the arithmetic."

The fixed winter face regarded him. "Speak."

"When the last bridge burned — a hundred years ago — what changed? What actually got better?"

Silence. Not the respectful kind. The kind that comes before weather turns.

"The covenant held," said one of the ranked elders. "The realms were sealed. The winter stayed outside the valley. Balance—"

"Balance," Theo said. "Right. Here's what I know about balance. I know a woman who traded half her life for a valley's protection, and the protection failed the moment it became inconvenient. I know a frost line moving a foot an hour toward homes full of people who never signed anything. I know a book that writes tomorrow like it's already happened." He spread his hands. "From where I stand, your balance looks like a debt nobody's been paying, collecting interest for a century, and now the bill's come due and you've decided I'm the payment."

"You are the payment," the eldest said, without malice, which was the terrible part. It said it the way you'd say *the tide comes in.* "The bridge burns. The covenant renews. This is not cruelty, mortal. It is architecture."

"Architecture," Theo repeated. "Okay. Let's talk architecture. Because I've built things. Small things — a garden wall, a roof that doesn't leak, a life. And the first rule of building is: you don't load-bearing-test your structure by burning the only piece holding it up and hoping the next hundred years goes fine."

Murmurs moved through the ranks like wind through wheat. The eldest didn't move at all.

"You presume to instruct the court."

"No," Theo said. "I presume to tell you a story. Because I've been watching you since I got here, and do you know what I haven't seen? One new thing. Not one. Your lightning hangs in the same places. Your towers grow the way rust grows. You're not a civilization, you're a *recording* of one, playing over and over, and every time it loops, a little more of it wears off." He pointed up at the slow banners of frozen lightning. "When's the last time one of those moved? Actually moved? When's the last time it rained here and then *stopped* raining? When's the last time anything here ended?"

The silence now was different. Sharper. Somewhere in the ranks, a young storm-being — if such a thing existed — flickered like a candle in draft.

"The bridge burns," the eldest said again, but slower, and Theo heard the crack in it, hairline, structural. "It is the price. It has always been the price."

"A price paid once a century by someone dragged to the altar," Theo said. "And here's my story, since you asked for none of it. Where I come from, snow falls, and then it melts. Stew burns, and then you make better stew. Gardens die in autumn and come back — not the same garden, a *new* one. We have this thing we do where things end, and it's awful, and it's the whole point. Every ending is how we know the next thing is real." His voice cracked and he let it. "You took endings away a hundred years ago and called it balance. And now your whole realm is a song stuck on one note, and the note is getting quieter, and your answer is to feed me to the song."

He turned, slowly, to face the ranks. To all of them.

"I'm not refusing. Yet. I'm telling you what I have that you don't. I have *stillness.* I have a garden where nothing happens for hours and it's the best part of the day. I have a woman who hums when she thinks I'm asleep. I have snow that melts. You want a bridge between your world and the valley? Fine. But a bridge isn't fuel. A bridge is the place where two sides finally *meet* — and meeting means both sides give up something, not one side giving up everything while the other watches from a nice warm dais."

The eldest rose. It had not risen in a century; Theo didn't know how he knew that, but he knew it the way he'd known the mist would let him through. The whole chamber reacted to the motion like water reacting to a dropped stone.

"You speak boldly for kindling," the eldest said.

"Yeah," Theo said. "Kindling usually does. That's the job."

* * *

They were given quarters afterward — a chamber of warm wind and soft thunder, a window of clear air looking out on the frozen lightning banners — and left alone for the first time since crossing.

Marguerite rounded on him the moment the door sealed. "Are you *insane?*"

"Probably."

"You lectured the Court of Falling Weather. The oldest power in the storm realm. On *architecture.*"

"They started it," Theo said. "They called me arithmetic."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed her forehead against his chest. He held her. Through the window, the banners of lightning hung unmoving, and beyond them the realm went on being beautiful the way a museum is beautiful, and somewhere below them, he was nearly certain, the frost in the valley crept another foot closer to their garden.

"They're going to decide soon," Marguerite said into his coat. "I can feel it. The court's weather is turning."

"Toward what?"

"That's the problem." She lifted her head, and her eyes were doing the thing they did when she was reading weather he couldn't see. "They're not deciding whether to accept your argument, Theo. They're deciding whether you've made yourself *more* valuable as a bridge — or whether a willing, eloquent, ordinary mortal who can hold a court's attention might be worth more *unburned.*"

"And either way I'm the subject of the debate."

"Either way you're the subject of the debate."

He looked out the window again. And that was when he saw it — far off, at the edge of the realm, a disturbance in the perpetual cloud. A shape approaching, vast and slow, trailing weather behind it like a gown.

A carriage of thunder.

Marguerite saw it a heartbeat later and went rigid against him. "No," she whispered. "No, she said she'd give us time. She *said*—"

The eldest's voice filled the chamber without the eldest being present, seeping out of the walls like condensation:

"The High Storm-Caller Seraphine approaches. The court will hear her. And the bridge—" a pause, deliberate, almost gentle, "—will learn what his mother-in-law has promised on his behalf."
