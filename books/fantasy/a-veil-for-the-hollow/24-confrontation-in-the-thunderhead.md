## 24. Confrontation in the Thunderhead

The storm realm does not have a sky. It *is* one.

Marguerite had forgotten that. Fifteen years of the valley's flat gray ceiling had sanded the memory smooth, but standing on the terrace of cloud-forged basalt outside the Court of Falling Weather, she felt it come back all at once: the way the world here has no floor and no ceiling, only layers — cloud under cloud under cloud, lightning stitching between them like nerve impulses in something vast and half-asleep.

She had also forgotten how loud magic was when you were inside it. Not sound. Presence. Every bolt that forked down through the distant thunderheads registered against her skin like a finger tapping her shoulder, asking *are you awake, are you awake, are you awake.*

She was awake now. God help her, she was more awake than she had been since the day she left.

Behind her, at the edge of the terrace where the cloud hardened into something a mortal could stand on, Theo waited with the Warden's mist coiled around him like a chaperone who didn't trust him alone with sharp objects. She could feel his eyes on her back. She had learned, over fifteen years and two weeks, to read the weight of his attention the way sailors read weather.

He wanted to tell her not to do this. He knew better.

"She'll come up," the archivist-storm had said that morning, its voice a chorus of rain on different roofs. "Seraphine does not let grievances age. She lets them ripen."

So they climbed — or rather, the mountain did. The storm realm's geography was negotiable, and the court had raised a spire of thunderhead for them, because duels required witnesses, and witnesses required somewhere to stand that wasn't the blast zone. Old courtesy. Her grandmother had written about it. *We fight above the court so our anger never lands on the innocent.*

Her grandmother had written a lot of things. Some of them were even true.

The air pressure changed first. That was always how Seraphine arrived — not with thunder, but with the absence of it, a held breath a hundred miles wide. Then the carriage: a shell of braided lightning drawn by two horses made of rain that fell upward, descending out of the upper dark with the unhurried elegance of a woman who had never once been late for anything she'd chosen to attend.

It settled on the far end of the cloud-spire. The doors opened by themselves.

"Marguerite." Her mother's voice carried across the whole terrace without being raised. It always had. As a child, Marguerite had believed her mother could speak directly into your ear from any distance, and had found this comforting. She no longer found it comforting. "You look well. Exile agrees with you, apparently."

"You look exactly the same," Marguerite said, and it came out harsher than she'd planned, because it was true in a way that hurt: Seraphine looked barely older than the day Marguerite had walked out. Storm-callers aged like mountains. Marguerite was only just beginning to understand what that meant for what she herself had lost.

"You say that like it's an accusation."

"It is an accusation. It's several." Marguerite stepped forward, boots finding purchase on cloudstone that hummed faintly under her weight. Fifteen years, and her body still knew this footing. That felt like betrayal too. "Let's skip the part where we discuss my complexion. You wrote the book. You set the winter on a clock. You aimed it at Theo. Say it to my face."

Seraphine descended the carriage steps one at a time, and the light changed around her the way light changes when a storm front slides over the sun. She wore court-gray, silvered at the seams, her hair bound back in the war-braid of a High Storm-Caller at full authority. Beautiful. Terrible. Exactly as advertised.

And tired. That was the thing nobody would have seen but a daughter. There were shadows under her mother's eyes like bruises under ice.

"I came to help," Seraphine said. "I've said so."

"You said so while offering me the choice of losing Theo or watching everyone I've grown to love freeze. That's not helping. That's a menu."

"The covenant is failing, Marguerite. It has been failing since before you were born. The winter comes whether you hate me or not. All I did was make sure that when the bill came due—" her gaze slid past Marguerite's shoulder, found Theo, lingered with something cold and precise, "—it landed on the right account."

"There it is." Marguerite laughed, and the laugh came out with static in it. She felt it happen — the small crackle along her jaw, the taste of copper and ozone — and was distantly alarmed at how good it felt. "There's the love. Underneath all the diagnosis and the arithmetic. You dressed it up as prophecy, Mother, but this is just you, punishing a gardener for having the audacity to propose to your daughter over burnt stew."

"He took you from us."

"*I* took me from us." The words came out louder than she meant, and the cloud beneath their feet shivered. Somewhere below, the court stirred like a sleeping animal. "I chose the valley. I chose quiet. I chose a man who burns the rice and apologizes to the chickens. You never once asked why."

"I didn't have to ask. I knew why. Duty is heavy, and he offered you a door out—"

"He offered me a *garden*." Something in her chest tore loose, some old splinter she'd carried fifteen years. "Do you know what it's like to be loved without being *needed*, Mother? To be someone's person instead of someone's weapon? No. No, you don't, and that's the tragedy of you, and I have run out of grief to spend on it."

Seraphine went very still. Stillness, in a storm-caller, was the most dangerous state there was. Weather gathered around stillness like water around a plughole.

"Then there's nothing left to say," her mother said quietly.

"No," Marguerite agreed. "But plenty left to do."

* * *

They fought the way they had always fought, which is to say: completely, and with terrible tenderness underneath.

Not fists. Storms. Seraphine moved first — of course she did; she had never once in her life ceded initiative — and the upper clouds answered her like hounds answering a whistle. Lightning came down in sheets, not aimed to kill but to *herd*, driving Marguerite toward the spire's edge where the drop into the deep cloud would end the fight without ending a daughter. Even now. Even here. Some corner of Seraphine was always calculating how to win without paying.

Marguerite refused the herding. She dropped low, put her palm flat against the cloudstone, and pulled.

It came back to her like blood returning to a numb limb — agonizing, flooding, hers. Fifteen years of rationed silence broke open all at once and she *screamed* with it, or maybe the sky screamed with her, hard to say. A wall of pressure rolled off the spire and ate Seraphine's lightning midair, bolts dissolving into harmless purple filaments that drifted down like dying fireworks.

Below, the whole court had risen to watch. She could feel hundreds of storm-folk presences arrayed along the lower terraces, a silent amphitheater. And one mortal presence among them, standing too close to the rail, refusing the Warden's attempts to pull him back.

Theo. Watching. Unable to intervene, because intervening between two storm-callers was a way to stop existing. She felt his fear like a hand around her own throat and made herself stop feeling it. Later. Grieve later. Fight now.

Seraphine rose out of her own dissipating attack wreathed in white fire, and for the first time in the fight, she stopped being careful.

The next exchange cracked three thunderheads open like eggs. Marguerite blocked a lance of raw voltage with a shield of compressed hail and felt her palms blister from the cold-burn; she answered with a downdraft that put her mother's braid whipping across her face and drove her to one knee on the cloudstone. They traded territory in yards. They traded language, too, shouted between volleys — old arguments with new punctuation.

"You were the strongest caller in six generations!" Seraphine hurled a fistful of ball lightning; Marguerite split it on a blade of wind. "You owed the realm better than a herb garden!"

"I owed myself a LIFE!"

"You owed ME—"

"*Nothing.*" Marguerite said it very quietly, and the quiet carried further than any shout. The wind died for a moment. The lightning hung in the clouds like held breath. "That was always the part you couldn't learn. Love isn't a ledger. Nobody owes anybody. You spent twenty years trying to collect a debt I never signed, and when I finally left, you wrote me a bill instead of a letter."

Something flickered across her mother's face. For one heartbeat it wasn't the High Storm-Caller at all — just an old woman in gray, alone on a mountaintop, holding the only tool she'd ever been given and discovering it couldn't fix the one thing that mattered.

Then the heartbeat passed, and Seraphine brought down the sky.

* * *

Later, Marguerite would not be able to describe those four minutes except in fragments.

A pillar of lightning wide as a house. Her shield shattering like sugar glass. The taste of her own blood, hot and metallic. Falling, catching herself on an updraft with hands that shook so badly the draft stuttered. Rising again because rising again was the entire job description.

Seraphine came down after her, relentless, elegant, spending power like water because she had decades more practice and a deeper well and both of them knew it. This was never going to be won on strength. Marguerite had known that from the start. Her mother was the storm itself given posture and opinions.

But storms have one weakness, and Marguerite had learned it in a valley where there weren't any.

Storms cannot wait.

They must spend. They must discharge, constantly, or tear themselves apart. Every minute Seraphine threw everything she had, her reserves thinned, her precision frayed. And Marguerite — fifteen years of patience, fifteen years of making one cup of tea last a whole morning, fifteen years of *waiting for frost to pass so the seedlings wouldn't die* — Marguerite could wait forever.

So she waited. She parried, dodged, gave ground, gave ground, gave ground, letting her mother empty the sky against her. Her body was a map of small hurts. Her vision kept graying at the edges. Below, she could hear Theo shouting something — her name, probably, or *stop*, or both — and she catalogued the sound the way you press a flower, to keep.

And then, at last, the moment came.

Seraphine gathered everything. Marguerite felt it building above her like a held tide — every reserve, every last ampere of the High Storm-Caller's might, funneled into one final strike that would end the argument permanently. The clouds went white. The world went white. And in the center of all that gathering light, Marguerite saw her opening: her mother's guard dissolved, her flanks bare, her entire self committed forward.

One counterstrike. A fraction of the power Marguerite was holding, aimed true, and it was over. The court was watching. The precedent would be set. She would be the strongest caller in six generations, and alive, and free.

She looked at her mother's face inside the blaze.

She saw the shadows under her eyes. She saw the war-braid coming loose. She saw, absurdly, a woman who had once sat up all night teaching a seven-year-old to whistle wind through cupped hands, laughing when the girl produced only spit.

*Love isn't a ledger,* she thought. *So stop keeping accounts.*

Marguerite opened her hands.

Every volt she had hoarded, every stolen scrap of storm she'd banked through the whole fight — she let it go. Not at Seraphine. Upward. Outward. Into the open cloud above the spire, a fountain of wasted lightning that lit the entire realm like dawn, spectacular and useless and deliberate.

Seraphine's strike hit her full-on.

It should have killed her. It nearly did. But a blow aimed at a fighter lands differently than a blow aimed at a woman standing still with open hands, and some instinct older than combat made her mother pull the strike at the last instant — enough to throw her, to burn her, to crack the cloudstone where she landed, but not enough to stop her heart.

Marguerite lay on her back on broken cloud, smoking gently, staring up into her mother's stunned face, and started to laugh. It hurt. Everything hurt. She laughed anyway.

"That's how you win," she rasped. "You don't. You just stop playing."

"Get up," Seraphine said, and her voice was wrong. Fractured. "Fight, Marguerite. Get UP—"

"No." She tried to rise, managed an elbow, kept talking because if she stopped talking the exhaustion would take her. "You want to know what the valley taught me? Nothing ends when somebody finally strikes the big blow. Nothing has ever ended that way. Not in your prophecies, not in your wars, not in your ledgers. It only ends when somebody puts the weapon down *first*. When they'd rather lose the fight than win the feud." She coughed. Tasted ozone. "So. I'm putting it down. Right here, in front of the whole court. Strike me or join me, Mother. Those are the options now."

The silence went on for a long time. Long enough for the court's murmur to rise and fall. Long enough for Theo to break from the Warden's side and start running across the broken cloudstone toward her, because of course he did, rules were for other people.

Long enough for Marguerite to watch her mother's hands — raised, charged, trembling — and see the exact moment the charge drained out of them. Saw Seraphine look at her own fingers like they belonged to a stranger.

"I don't know how," Seraphine said at last, barely audible. "To put it down. I have never once in two hundred years put it down."

"I know," Marguerite said. "Come down to the valley sometime. We'll teach you. It's mostly naps and burnt stew."

Above them, unnoticed by either of them until it filled the whole horizon, the sky was changing. The long winter's grip on the storm realm — the dead calm at the crown of the world, the frozen eye where nothing moved — had begun, very slowly, to rotate.

And from the direction of the Court of Falling Weather came the tolling of the great bell, once, twice, a third time — the summons the court used only for one occasion.

The oldest among them wished to speak.
