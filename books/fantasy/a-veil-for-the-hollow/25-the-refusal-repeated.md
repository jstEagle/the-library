## 25. The Refusal Repeated

They dressed him in white.

That was the first thing Theo understood about the altar of the covenant: it was not a place of execution so much as a wedding. The storm-folk wove his ceremonial robe from cloud-silk that hummed against his skin like a struck tuning fork, and they braided lightning into the hem the way village women back home braided ribbon into a bride's hair. An honor, the archivist-storm had murmured, its voice a rolling pressure behind his ears. The last bridge wore white. All of them wore white.

*All of them.* As if there had been many. As if this were a tradition and not a wound.

He stood at the center of the Court of Falling Weather, on a platform of fused glass that some ancient bolt had carved out of the thunderhead itself — smooth under his bare feet, warm as a sun-baked stone in July, which was obscene given that the world around them was dying of cold. Below the platform, the clouds fell away into blue-black distance where the frozen reaches of the storm realm glowed dull and gray, like coals an hour after the fire.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the court, flanked by two wardens of wind who were not restraining her so much as *suggesting* restraint, which for beings made of weather was apparently a meaningful distinction. Her hair lifted around her face in the updraft. She had not slept. Neither had he. They had spent the night in the guest-alcove of the coral city saying everything except the one true thing, and now the morning had come to collect.

Seraphine stood near the throne of stacked thunder, radiant in storm-gray, wearing the expression of a woman watching a difficult surgery she believed necessary. She caught him looking and inclined her head — gracious, confident, certain of the outcome. That certainty was the most frightening thing in the room, and the room contained a living tornado.

The oldest member of the court drifted forward. Theo still couldn't tell if these beings had faces or whether his mind simply invented them to keep from screaming; this one presented as an old man with rain for a beard, though the rain fell upward. It spoke in a voice like distant thunder deciding whether to become close thunder.

"Mortal bridge. You have been brought to the altar as the covenant demands. One hundred years, one crossing, one burning. Speak your consent, that the realms may be sealed in witness."

The whole court leaned in. Literally — the clouds around the platform tilted inward, hundreds of weather-shaped faces, curious and hungry and tired. Tired above all. He could feel that now; Marguerite's magic had brushed off on him during the crossing, or maybe the vow they'd spoken over the garden had done something neither of them understood yet, but he could *feel* the court the way you felt a headache coming. A vast, exhausted ache. A people who had been cold for a century and had convinced themselves the shivering was dignity.

Theo looked down at his hands. On his right palm, the Warden's sigil pulsed faintly, a small silver spiral like a fingerprint left by something patient.

He thought about the archivist-storm. About what it had shown Marguerite, what she'd told him afterward in a voice scraped raw: *the last bridge refused, Theo. A hundred years ago someone stood exactly where you're standing and said no, and then ran, and both realms have been paying the bill ever since.* The court didn't speak of it. The court spoke of *the Cowardice* the way sailors speak of a nameless sea — only in the plural, only obliquely, only when the wind was right.

And he thought about Bramble, back in the valley, with his ledgers and his flat voice: *every hundred years, a mortal walks in and doesn't return. It's arithmetic, boy. Someone pays, or everyone does.*

Arithmetic. Everyone in two worlds had agreed on the arithmetic. One life weighed against millions. A clean equation. Even Marguerite, God love her, even she had come to the altar this morning with the equation written on her face — not acceptance, never that, but the terrible courage of a woman preparing to let the man she loved do the arithmetic because the alternative was letting the world freeze while they searched for a better answer that might not exist.

She thought he hadn't noticed her slipping the glass knife into her sleeve. The one that could cut a soul loose before the fire took it — a mercy blade, the archivist had called it, weeping vapor. She'd rather kill him gently than let him burn screaming. That was Marguerite. That was the woman who had exiled herself for fifteen years to keep her magic from hurting anyone. Love, in her language, always came out looking like a knife pointed inward.

Well. He'd learned some of her language.

"Bridge," the old storm said again, gentler. "Speak."

"I have a question first," Theo said.

A ripple went through the court. Questions were apparently not part of the liturgy.

"The last bridge," Theo said. "The one who refused. What was their name?"

Silence. Real silence — the kind that made the air pressure change. Somewhere below them, a frozen lightning-field cracked like lake ice.

"We do not speak the name," the old storm said.

"You remember it, though." Theo kept his voice level. His heart was going like a hammer on a nail, but he'd spent fifteen years around a woman who could call down the sky; he'd learned to stand still inside fear. "You remember it, and you've spent a hundred years making sure nobody else ever learns it, because a name makes it a person, and a person makes it a choice, and a choice means somebody in this court might look at me standing here in my nice white robe and think — wait. Why are we doing this?"

"The covenant—"

"The covenant is broken," Theo said. "It's been broken for a hundred years. You've been trying to restart a dead engine by feeding it the same fuel that stalled it. It didn't work the first time. The winter came anyway. The valley started dying anyway. And now you've got yourselves a second refusal staring you in the face, and you're all so committed to the arithmetic that nobody's asked the obvious question."

The old storm's upward-falling beard trembled. "What question."

"*Why does it have to be a burning?*"

He let it land. Let it echo off the thunder-throne and roll out across the court like a stone dropped down a well.

Below the platform, at the edge of everything, Marguerite had gone very still. Her hand had stopped moving toward the knife.

"The prophecy says the bridge must burn," Seraphine said smoothly, from beside the throne. Her voice carried the way her daughter's did when she wanted it to — effortlessly, unpleasantly. "The words are not ambiguous, mortal. *The bridge that must burn.* I wrote— " a fractional pause, sanded smooth, " — the archives record it plainly. Fire transforms. Fire releases. The bridge's mortality becomes the mortar of the new seal. There is no other reading."

"There's no other reading *you've allowed*," Theo said. "For a hundred years. Because the alternative would mean admitting the first refusal wasn't cowardice."

The court recoiled as one weather-system. Somewhere in the ranks, a small squall-shaped being made a sound like gasping wind.

"It *was* cowardice," the old storm said, and now the thunder was close. "They stood where you stand. They were given every honor. And they turned and fled, and the seal failed mid-making, and the cold came in through the gap, and my children—" the voice frayed, actual grief, raw as a stripped wire, " — my children froze *from the inside*, mortal. Do not stand at this altar and tell me their refusal was anything but the sin that killed us."

"No," Theo said quietly. "I'm telling you it was the sin of everyone who built an altar that only worked one way."

He turned, slowly, so the whole court could see his face. He was suddenly, acutely aware of how absurd he must look — a soft-handed gardener in a cloud-silk robe, barefoot on warm glass, surrounded by beings who could unmake a mountain range between breakfast and lunch. He was the least powerful creature in this entire sky.

Which was, he was beginning to understand, exactly why he was the one who could say this.

"I'm not running," he said. "I want that on the record, in whatever way you keep records. I'm not the last bridge. I'm not going to turn and flee and leave a hundred more years of winter for whoever comes after me. I came here willingly. The Warden will confirm it — the covenant requires willingness, and I walked through that mist with my eyes open."

"He did," said a voice like fog on glass, and the Warden condensed at the platform's edge, tall and gray and unreadable. "The bridge came willingly. Both bridges came willingly." Its mist-head turned, very slightly, toward Marguerite. "The covenant requires two. It has *always* required two. This court has simply forgotten, because remembering would be inconvenient."

Theo's heart slammed. He hadn't known that part — not for certain. He'd hoped. He'd lain awake half the night turning over the archivist's account of the original covenant, the one detail everyone kept glossing past, and gambled everything on the Warden's pedantic honesty.

"The first covenant," the Warden continued, in the tone of a clerk correcting an entry, "was sworn by two. A storm-caller and a mortal, together, each giving half. The burning was a *corruption*. A shortcut devised later, by a court too frightened and too proud to pay the full price twice. One death is cheaper than two half-lives. So the arithmetic said." A pause, delicate as frost forming. "Your arithmetic has been wrong for nine hundred years."

The court erupted.

Not violence — nothing so simple. The weather itself broke discipline. Winds crossed and quarreled. The temperature plunged ten degrees in a heartbeat, and out beyond the platform, in the frozen reaches, Theo saw the long winter *answer* — a wave of deeper cold rolling through the gray fields like a tide pulling back before a tsunami, frost racing up the coral spires of the outer city in white veins. The whole realm groaned, a sound he felt in his teeth.

"LIAR!" A young storm — a tempest in the shape of a woman with hailstone eyes — surged forward until the wardens barred her. "The court has burned six bridges in living memory! Six! Each one sealed the veil for a season, each one bought us time—"

"Bought you *time*," Theo said over the rising gale. "Not peace. Not spring. Time. And each time, less of it. You're not maintaining a covenant, you're bleeding it out slowly because the fast version hurts. I know that trick. We do it in the valley too. There's a fence on the north ridge my neighbor refuses to fix because fixing it means admitting he can't afford the rest of the farm."

"YOU DARE—"

"I dare because I'm the one in the white robe!" And it came out of him hot, fifteen years of being the ordinary man beside the extraordinary woman, fifteen years of holding the lantern while other people did the magic — "You want a bridge? Fine. Here's your bridge. But bridges don't burn, you magnificent, terrified idiots. Bridges *connect*. That's the whole job. You've been sacrificing the one thing the covenant actually needs, and calling the sacrifice the covenant, for nine hundred years!"

The gale faltered. Not calmed — *faltered*, like a choir losing its place in the music.

Seraphine moved then, gliding forward, and up close her beauty was worse than her reputation, all sharp angles and controlled static. "Theo Vance." His name in her mouth sounded like a verdict. "You are a gardener. A kind one, I'll grant — my daughter chose poorly in many ways but not in that. But you do not understand what you are playing with. If you refuse the fire, the winter accelerates. Tonight. Perhaps within hours. The valley will be ice inside a week. My daughter's little paradise, every soul in it, gone. Is your principle worth that?"

"No," Theo said.

Her smile began.

"But that's not the choice," he said. "That's the choice *you* need it to be. Burn or freeze. Sacrifice or surrender. You've been selling that binary since before I met your daughter — hell, you built her whole exile out of it. But the Warden just told you the truth in front of the entire court. The first covenant was two people giving halves. Not one person giving everything. Two halves make a whole. It's not even complicated arithmetic, Seraphine. It's the first sum anyone learns."

"And who?" she said softly. "Who would take such a vow? A mortal and a renegade caller whose power nearly killed her three nights ago? You would fuse yourselves into covenant-stuff and spend eternity as a seam between worlds. I have watched that magic work, mortal. It does not leave enough of a person behind to bury."

"Then it's good," Theo said, "that nobody's burying anybody."

He turned to find her.

Marguerite had already stepped past the wardens. Nobody had ordered them aside; they had simply, somehow, stopped being in her way, and Theo would spend years wondering whether that was politics or pity. She walked to the platform and climbed its single step, and the court's rage curdled into something else — attention, vast and total, a thousand storms leaning in to watch the impossible thing happen.

She took his hand. Her fingers were freezing. Her eyes were on fire.

"They're going to say no," she murmured. "The court. My mother. Half of them would rather freeze than admit nine centuries of funerals were a mistake."

"I know."

"And if they say yes, it costs us everything we are. You heard what she said. There won't be enough left to bury."

"I know that too." He squeezed her hand. "Maggie. Fifteen years ago you gave up half of yourself to protect a valley full of strangers. I've been living with the other half ever since. I'm just proposing we make it official."

Something broke open in her face — terror and laughter fighting for the same ground — and she turned to the court, and Theo watched his future wife address a nation of weather with dirt still under her nails in spirit if not in fact.

"My name is Marguerite Ishikawa," she said, and her voice carried without effort, the way it never had in the valley, the way it was born to. "Daughter of Seraphine. Granddaughter of the line of first callers. Fifteen years ago I renounced my storm-calling to escape a marriage I did not consent to — a covenant built on the same rotten arithmetic you all just heard defended. I am the strongest caller this realm has produced in three generations, and I am telling you: I will pay. Half of what I am. Here. Now. Willingly. If he pays his half beside me."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The whole storm realm seemed to hold its breath, and far below, in the frozen dark, the long winter paused — actually paused, the advancing frost hanging motionless like a held note.

Then Seraphine laughed.

It was not a kind sound. "The court will never sanction it. The rites require—"

"The rites," said the oldest storm, very quietly, "require *two*."

Every face turned to it. The old one drifted to the edge of the thunder-throne, and its upward rain had stopped falling entirely, as if even its grief needed to hear clearly.

"I was there," it said. "At the first swearing. I was younger than that squall-child there, and I have spent nine hundred years pretending I misremembered, because the truth was unbearable. The Warden speaks correctly. The burning was our invention. Our shortcut. Our sin — and the Cowardice was not one bridge fleeing an altar." Its ancient eyes found Theo's, and there was something in them that might, in a human face, have been gratitude. "The Cowardice was ours. We made the altar smaller than the promise."

The court shuddered. Out in the frozen reaches, the winter screamed — actually screamed, a sound like a continent cracking, and the frost leapt forward a mile in a single convulsive surge, as if the cold itself understood that its nine-hundred-year lease was being contested.

"The vote," the old storm said. "Now. Before the winter votes for us."

And Seraphine stepped between them and the throne, lightning gathering around her hands like a queen putting on her crown, and said:

"Over my body, it passes."

* * *

Later — much later, in the hours when everything hung balanced on a knife-edge of weather — Theo would try to remember the exact moment the court split. He never could. Memory refused to file it as a scene and kept returning it as a feeling: the sensation of standing on warm glass in a white robe while a civilization tore itself in half around him, winds against winds, thunder arguing with thunder, and somewhere beneath all of it the steady, freezing pulse of the long winter climbing toward them like water rising in a locked room.

But that was later. First there was only this: Marguerite's hand in his, the sigil burning on his palm, his own voice — hoarse, stubborn, absolutely certain — saying the words again into the teeth of the storm, because some things bear repeating:

"No one burns today. But no one freezes either. There's a third option. There always was. You just had to be crazy enough to look for it together."

The first blow of his mother-in-law's lightning split the sky above the throne.

Marguerite stepped in front of him, raised her hands, and answered.
