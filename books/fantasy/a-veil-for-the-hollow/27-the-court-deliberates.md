## 27. The Court Deliberates

They debated for three days.

I know because I counted the times the lightning changed color. In the Court of Falling Weather, light is language — I'd learned that much by then — and the great thunderhead that housed the council chamber cycled through its moods like a slow argument: white for inquiry, violet for objection, a bruised red for things too old to be said in any tongue still living. By the second day I had stopped trying to follow the words themselves. Storm-folk speech isn't sound the way we think of it. It's pressure. It's the feeling of a sentence arriving in your chest fully formed, whether you want it there or not.

Marguerite stood beside me on the gallery of cloud-stone — yes, cloud can be stood upon, if the standing is agreed to — with her hands folded and her spine straight in that way she had, the way that meant she was terrified and refusing to let terror have the good chairs.

My proposal hung in the middle of the chamber like a storm system with nowhere to go.

Two halves make one whole. That was the whole of it, really. No burning bridge. No mortal walked into the fire and spent. Instead: a fusion. Her remaining mortal years — half a life, already promised to the valley's protection, already half-spent — and my mortal permanence, the ordinary human certainty of aging one day at a time until done. Pour them together. Bind them into the covenant. Not a sacrifice. An architecture.

The court thought it was heresy.

The court thought it was beautiful.

The court could not decide which offended it more.

* * *

The oldest among them sat at the apex of the chamber, if *sat* is the word for a being who had long ago stopped pretending to occupy space and instead merely *insisted* upon it. The others called her Grandmother Gale. She remembered, the archivist-storms said, the signing of the first covenant — not as history remembers things, dimly and secondhand, but the way you remember your own wedding. She was older than the valley. Older than the word *valley*. When she finally spoke, the entire chamber's lightning went still, every bolt hanging frozen mid-fork like a held breath.

"Two," she said. "It was made by two."

Every eye in the court turned toward her. Even Seraphine — positioned across the chamber in her seat of office, High Storm-Caller, beautiful and terrible and watching me the way a hawk watches a field mouse that has done something unexpected — even Seraphine leaned forward.

Grandmother Gale drifted down from the apex, and as she descended, the chamber showed us what she wanted shown. The cloud around her thickened into images, the way frost thickens on glass: two figures at a thin place in the world, a mortal woman with her sleeves rolled up and a storm-being with no face I could hold onto, their hands clasped over a seam between worlds that was bleeding weather into a dying land.

"The first covenant was not a gift from us to you," Gale said. "Nor yours to ours. It was a marriage. A storm-caller and a farmer — yes, a *farmer*, children, before any of you sneer — who loved each other badly, inconveniently, and completely. Neither could cross whole. Neither would burn. So they made the only vow neither of their kinds had ever tried." Her voice was dry wind through a canyon. "They each gave up exactly half."

The images shifted. The two figures at the seam grew translucent together, light passing through both of them equally, neither more nor less than the other.

"And the covenant held for four hundred years," Gale finished, "until everyone forgot it began as love and started calling it arithmetic."

That last word landed on the founders' side of the chamber — Bramble's people, the human envoys who had crossed through the Warden's boundary under escort, sitting stiffly in their borrowed cloud-seats like men at a funeral they suspected was their own. Bramble himself didn't flinch. He never flinched. But I saw his jaw set, and I understood he had heard himself named.

* * *

Seraphine rose to argue against us, and I will give her this: she argued magnificently.

"Sentiment," she said, and the word came down like a dropped anvil. "You propose to reforge the founding covenant of two realms on the basis of *sentiment*. A love story. Four centuries of order, maintained by a clear and terrible price, and these two—" she gestured at Marguerite and me with a hand wreathed in deliberate static "—wish to replace the price with a poem."

Murmurs rolled through the court, literal murmurs, low thunder moving in ranks.

"The bridge sacrifice works," Seraphine continued. "It has worked eleven times. One life against two worlds. It is not cruel; it is *proportionate*. What they offer instead is untested. Unprecedented. If the fusion fails — if the vow does not take, if the realms reject it — we do not get to try again. The winter will have passed the point of turning. Every realm, every valley, every living thing under both skies dies *because a girl loved a boy who made stew.*"

"It was burnt stew," I said.

The chamber went silent in the way rooms do when someone very small says something very foolish. Violet lightning flickered — objection, I was learning — but somewhere in the back ranks, among the younger storms, something crackled that might have been laughter.

Seraphine looked at me for a long moment. And here is the thing I will remember until whatever altered span of days I'm granted runs out: she didn't look angry. She looked *tired*. Under all that terrible beauty, under the crown of thunder she wore like it weighed nothing, there was a woman who had been holding a door shut against the end of the world for longer than anyone had thanked her for it.

"You think this is a joke," she said quietly.

"I think you've done the math so long you've forgotten the math was supposed to serve something," I said. My voice shook. I let it. "Eleven bridges burned, Lady. Eleven people walked into your fire and didn't come back, and you call it proportionate because you never had to watch. Marguerite watched. From the valley, cut off, magic-dead, she felt every single one of those deaths like a bell tolling underwater. You built a world where your own daughter couldn't feel anything but the bill coming due."

"That is not—"

"Chapter and verse," I said. "Your book. Your ink. You wrote him into the prophecy before he knew your daughter existed. Don't tell me about proportion."

The chamber flared red. Seraphine's power gathered around her shoulders like a mantle catching wind — and then, and I saw it happen, I swear I saw it happen, her eyes found Marguerite's.

Her daughter hadn't spoken. Hadn't moved. Stood with her hands folded, watching her mother with an expression I had seen exactly once before: on the ridge above the Hollow, the morning after the frost first breached the boundary, when Marguerite looked down at our flattened garden — everything she'd built in fifteen years of exile, torn up in one night by her own caged power — and chose not to weep about it.

It wasn't forgiveness on Marguerite's face. It was something harder and rarer. It was *recognition*. She looked at her mother and saw the same exhausted arithmetic, the same door held shut, the same terrible habit of loving people by deciding things for them.

And Seraphine's certainty cracked.

Not broke — cracked, the way ice cracks before it goes, one bright line running through the whole sheet. The mantle of static guttered. The red lightning faltered toward violet and stayed there, caught between objection and something softer, and the High Storm-Caller of all the storm realms stood in her own court unable to finish a sentence, because her daughter was looking at her without hatred, and somehow that was worse than any duel.

"The price must be paid," Seraphine said at last, and her voice was not the voice of a hawk anymore. It was the voice of a woman reading terms she had signed long ago and never reread. "Someone must—"

"Someone already did," Marguerite said. "Eleven someones. Mother. When does the ledger close?"

* * *

The court deliberated behind closed weather after that. Literally — the chamber drew a curtain of rain between the council and the rest of us, a grey sheet of falling water that hummed with their argument. We waited in the gallery. Bramble's people were escorted out entirely; apparently mortals didn't rate the deliberation, which suited me fine, since I had begun to suspect that half the founders wanted me kindling and the other half wanted me forgotten.

So it was just the two of us, and Grandmother Gale, who had drifted over without asking and settled beside us like an aunt claiming the good pew.

"You knew," Marguerite said to her. "About the first covenant. About the two. You've known all along why the bridges kept having to burn."

Gale's form shifted — mist rearranging itself into something almost like a shrug. "Knowing and being believed are different weathers entirely, child. I told the court four hundred years ago that the sacrifice was a distortion. A shortcut taken by grief. When the last bridge refused—" she paused, and the temperature around us dropped a degree, "—the court's answer was to make the ritual stricter. To insist harder. Grief always doubles down on its own logic."

"Why didn't you stop it?" I asked.

"Because I am old," she said simply, "and old things are believed only when convenient. And because—" the mist hesitated, which I hadn't known mist could do, "—because I was not certain you two were the answer. I am still not certain. Certainty is what brought us here. I am merely certain that *burning him* is not."

Marguerite laughed — a small, cracked, real laugh. "You sound like Theo."

"Then he sounds like sense wearing a poor coat."

We stood together in the gallery while the rain-curtain muttered and flashed. Through it, in silhouette, I could see the shapes of the council shifting, rising, clashing, resettling. Once, a single bolt punched through the curtain and struck the floor a foot from where we stood — violet, then white, arguing with itself even as it died.

"She's going to vote against us," Marguerite said quietly, meaning her mother. "Even now. Even cracked. She'll vote against us because voting for it means admitting the trap was a mistake, and she'd rather lose the world than the story she tells about herself."

"No," Gale said.

We both looked at her.

"She will abstain," Gale said. "Watch. Abstention is how the proud change their minds. It leaves the door open to claim afterward that they always intended whatever happened." A dry rustle moved through her, the storm equivalent of a smile. "I have watched Seraphine Ishikawa for forty years, child. I know her weather better than she does."

* * *

The curtain fell at dusk — or what passed for dusk in a realm lit by perpetual internal lightning, which is to say the chamber simply decided to be dimmer.

The eldest speaker rose. Not Gale; another, a towering column of grey cloud called the Voice of the Third Ridge, whose pronouncements carried the weight of nine centuries of jurisprudence. The court's verdict, delivered as verdicts are delivered here, as weather:

"The precedent stands," the Voice intoned. "The first covenant was forged by two. The distortion of the bridge was just that — a distortion, adopted in mourning, perpetuated in fear. The court acknowledges..." a rumble, long and grudging, "...that the proposal of the callers' daughter and the mortal Vance is not without foundation in law."

Hope did something warm and dangerous in my chest.

"Therefore," the Voice continued, "the court will entertain the new covenant. *Conditionally.* Terms must be negotiated — every clause weighed, every cost enumerated, nothing given blindly by either realm. The negotiation begins at first light and will not pause until concluded or failed."

Grudging. Procedural. Bureaucratic to the bone.

Alive. It meant *alive*.

And then, as the court began to rise, as the chamber loosened into the rustling aftermath of decision, the Voice spoke once more — and the final words were aimed not at the assembly but at the three of us in the gallery, and they came down cold and precise as sleet:

"One condition is fixed and non-negotiable, set by the court before any term may be discussed. The fused vow requires a place where the veil is thinnest. There is exactly one such place remaining, and it lies within the Deep Thunderhead — the oldest storm in creation, the cradle of the first covenant, sealed for four hundred years."

A pause, and the temperature dropped so hard my breath came out visible.

"The seal opens for one hour at dawn. Whatever the two of you intend to become, you will become it inside that storm — or the offer dies with the winter."

Beside me, Grandmother Gale went very still.

And in the silence after the court emptied, she said, softly, in a voice that had nothing dry left in it at all:

"Child, there is something you should know about what sealed the Deep Thunderhead. Before you walk into it tomorrow — you should know what your grandmother put in there, and why it has been screaming ever since."
