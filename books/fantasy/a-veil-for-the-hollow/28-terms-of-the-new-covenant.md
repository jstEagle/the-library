## 28. Terms of the New Covenant

The negotiating table was a slab of storm-glass, and it had opinions.

I could feel them the way I'd once felt weather through my fingertips — a low, grudging hum in the material, as if the court had grown the table from a thunderhead that resented being flattened into furniture. It sat at the center of the Hall of Falling Weather, that vast chamber where the ceiling was cloud and the floor was cloud pressed thin enough to walk on, and around it the storm-folk arranged themselves like a jury deciding whether to acquit or incinerate.

Theo sat beside me. His hand found mine under the table — not for comfort, exactly. For arithmetic. We were two halves of an equation now, and he wanted me to remember we were solving it together.

Across from us, the eldest of the court unfolded herself from her seat. She was called Vess, though the title she carried was older than names: *First Witness of the Last Binding*. She had been alive when the covenant was young. Her skin moved like slow lightning under glass, and when she spoke, frost formed on the table's edge and melted again, as if even her words couldn't decide what temperature to be.

"Terms," she said. "You propose terms."

"I propose a rewrite," I said. "There's a difference."

A murmur went through the assembled storm-folk — a sound like wind finding a crack under a door. Somewhere above us, the ceiling-cloud darkened by a shade. The court's mood was weather, literally, and I was learning to read it faster than I'd learned to read my grandmother's book.

"The last bridge refused to burn," Vess said. "The realms have bled for a century because of one mortal's cowardice. And you come here asking us to believe that *two* refusals make a right?"

"One refusal made a wound," Theo said. "We're offering a suture."

He said it plainly, no joke attached, which from Theo meant he was more frightened than he was letting on. His thumb moved over my knuckles once, twice. Counting. He counted when he was scared. I'd learned that over burnt stew and ordinary evenings, back when ordinary evenings were all we had.

"Speak your clauses," Vess said at last. "We will weigh each one. And understand, Marguerite Ishikawa, daughter of Seraphine, granddaughter of the Sealed Book's first keeper — every clause will cost. Nothing given across a veil is free."

"I know," I said. "That's why they're worth giving."

* * *

The first clause took four hours.

Magic may enter the valley — gently. Not the way it had come before, all covenants and compulsion and boundaries enforced by mist and Warden. A gentler thing: magic as season, magic as guest. Rain that remembered how to fall warm. Storms that asked permission of the rooftops before arriving. Children born in the Hollow who might, if they listened carefully, hear the veil breathing.

The court hated it.

"You ask us to dilute ourselves," spat a young caller whose name translated roughly to *Hail-That-Remembers*. "To send our power into a valley that cannot hold it, to be *gentle*, like a river agreeing to be a puddle—"

"Your power is currently killing everything it touches," I said. "The long winter doesn't distinguish between enemies and children. It's sealing both worlds in death, and it will not stop because you're proud."

"And you would know about pride, exile?"

"Hail." Vess's voice dropped the temperature ten degrees. "The daughter speaks. You listen."

But the young caller's barb stayed in the room, working its way under everyone's skin, including mine. Because she wasn't wrong, entirely. I had walked away. I had chosen a garden and a man and a life with no lightning in it, and I had told myself it was survival when part of it was escape. Every clause I proposed now was built on the foundation of that leaving.

"The valley held your covenant's human side for a hundred years," Theo said quietly, when the shouting died down. "Farmers who kept old rites without knowing why. Founders' councils that guarded a door they were never allowed to open. They gave a century of blind obedience. The least the storms can do is knock politely."

Blind obedience. He didn't know how much those words would cost later, when Bramble and his council heard what we'd traded away their secrecy for. But that was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight we were spending down our own.

Vess weighed the clause in silence so long that frost crept up the table legs. Then: "Gently. Agreed — conditionally. Magic enters as guest, never as claimant. If the valley's people reject it, it withdraws."

"They'll accept," I said. "Some of them already feel the veil. They just thought it was the wind."

* * *

The second clause was mine to give, and it was the one that hurt.

Mortals may visit the storms — briefly. Not settle. Not stay. Brief crossings, sanctioned, witnessed, with the understanding that mortal flesh is not built for perpetual lightning and must return before it frays. Theo's palm still bore the Warden's sigil, faded now to a pale silver scar, and it would be the mark that let him cross and the mark that reminded him he could never belong there.

"You're asking us to open our home to people who wilt in it," said Hail-That-Remembers, and this time there was less venom in it. More bewilderment. "Why would mortals want to come here at all?"

"Because it's beautiful," Theo said.

The whole court went still.

"It's beautiful," he repeated. "I've been here three days and I've seen cities grown out of thunderheads and light that comes from inside the clouds. You think we came here only to save the valley? I came because Marguerite talks about this place in her sleep. Because half of her has been missing for fifteen years and I want to see the half I never met. And because—" he glanced at me, and I nodded, because this part was true and it was time someone said it to these people, "—because you've been dying of loneliness and calling it dignity."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Then Vess laughed — a sound like distant thunder deciding to be kind. "Oh, the bridge burns indeed," she murmured. "Just not the way anyone planned."

* * *

The third clause nearly broke us.

The winter lifts — in exchange for the fused vow. Two halves making one whole: my remaining mortal years, thinned and shared; his mortal permanence, quickened and spent. Neither of us whole again. Both of us changed. Bound to the veil itself, living anchors, so long as either of us drew breath the covenant would hold — and if one of us died, the other would feel it forever, a cold spot in the chest where half a life used to live.

The court's archivists brought out the old bindings to compare. They unrolled centuries of precedent on the glass table, and every scroll ended the same way: one soul, consumed. The math was clean. One death bought a hundred years. Our proposal bought eternity with two lives, halved, and the archivists kept returning to the same objection, phrased a dozen different ways:

*No one has ever paid in installments.*

"Because no one ever offered," I said.

"Because no one was ever foolish enough—"

"Foolish?" Theo stood up. I felt the table hum under my palms, felt the ceiling darken with the court's rising temper, but Theo stood in the middle of it with his sigil-scarred hand flat on the storm-glass and said, "You want to talk about foolishness? Your last solution was feeding a person into a fire every hundred years and calling it tradition. We're proposing marriage. Literally. We were going to do it anyway. You're just invited now."

And there it was — the thing I loved about him, laid bare in front of a court of living weather: he could find the human shape inside any horror. He could take an altar and turn it into a wedding.

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then at me.

"The vow binds until death," she said. "And past it. You understand that the grief-half does not end. When one of you goes, the other carries the absence for whatever remains. That is the price of installments. Eternity is patient, and it collects."

"We know," I said.

"Do you?" And here her voice softened into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Marguerite. You have already lost half a life to exile. You are asking to lose half of what remains. There will be so little of you left that you must decide, now, what the little is *for*."

I looked at Theo. He was looking at me the way he'd looked at me over burnt stew, candle flames bending toward the window behind him, joy and dread in the same face.

"For him," I said. "And for the valley. And for mornings. I've survived on less."

* * *

They deliberated until the ceiling turned the color of a bruise.

Theo and I waited in a side gallery carved from compressed thunder — a room that rumbled faintly, constantly, like a house with a heartbeat. We didn't talk much. What was left to negotiate between us? We'd already agreed on the only term that mattered, back in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and smoke: together, or not at all.

"My mother will argue against it," I said finally.

"I know."

"She'll be good at it. She argued the sun out of the sky once, according to family legend. Made it rain indoors for a week out of spite."

Theo smiled tiredly. "And then what happens?"

"Then she falters." I said it with more certainty than I felt. "She watched me refuse to strike her down in the thunderhead. Mercy cracks her, Theo. It always has. It's the one weapon she never learned to parry."

He took my hand — the one that still ached from calling the storm, the one that had leveled our garden and bled light at the knuckles. He kissed the ache like it was a ring I was already wearing.

"Whatever they decide," he said, "we walk out of here the same way we walked in. Together."

"That's not a negotiation strategy."

"It's the only one I've got."

The doors opened. Vess stood in the frame, ancient and luminous, and behind her the whole court was arranged in tiers of cloud and light, waiting.

"The court has weighed your clauses," she said. "Come and hear what they cost."

And as we rose to follow her, I felt it — faint, far below, threading up through the floor of cloud like a pulse through stone:

the long winter, knocking at the veil.

Asking, in its patient way, whether we had finished yet.
