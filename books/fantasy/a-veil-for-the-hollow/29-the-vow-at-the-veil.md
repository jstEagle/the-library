## 29. The Vow at the Veil

### Theo

The thin place was not what he had imagined.

He had pictured a door, a seam, a crack of light — something with edges. Instead it was a meadow on a mountainside that existed in neither realm and both, where grass grew silver and the wind came from every direction at once, and the sky above them split cleanly down the middle: storm on one side, stars on the other, and a seam of gold between them no wider than a held breath.

The Warden stood at the seam. It had stopped being a figure of mist somewhere along the climb and become something more like a person who had been rained on for a thousand years — worn smooth, patient, and very tired.

"The words are old," the Warden said. Its voice sounded like the last drop leaving a cloud. "Older than the valley. Older than the court. You will not understand all of them. Say them anyway."

"And if we get them wrong?" Theo asked.

"Then the winter takes both worlds, and no one will be alive to correct you."

Marguerite laughed — a small, cracked sound, the kind of laugh that comes out of people at funerals. Theo squeezed her hand. Her fingers were cold. His were too. They had been cold for three days, ever since the court agreed to hear the heresy, ever since the oldest of the storm-folk had risen from its seat of woven lightning and said, in a voice like a glacier calving: *The first covenant was made by two. Let them try.*

"Marguerite." He turned to face her. The gold seam lit the side of her face, and he thought — absurdly, in the last minutes of the life he had been — that she had never looked more like herself. Not the exile. Not the renounced daughter. Just Marguerite, who burned stew and talked to her herbs and laughed at his worst jokes out of pity.

"If this works," he said, "I want it on record that I proposed first. Over the stew. The burnt stew."

"It's on record," she said. "The whole valley heard you drop the ring in the pot."

"I want it on record that you said yes before I finished the sentence."

"I want it on record that you're stalling."

"I'm not stalling." He wasn't. He was memorizing. That was the thing no one told you about the end of a life: it didn't feel like an ending. It felt like a very long look. He memorized the smudge of ash still on her jaw from the garden she'd leveled with her own storm. He memorized the way her eyes had changed in the storm realm — brighter, lit from behind, the eyes of a woman whose power had come home to roost. He memorized her hand in his, the calluses from the trowel, the scar on her thumb.

Half of what she was would be gone in a few minutes. Half of what he was too. He still didn't fully understand what he was giving up — his mortal permanence, the archivist-storm had called it, which apparently meant the ordinary human right to age slowly and die completely. He would age faster now. He would hear the weather in his teeth. He would never again be a man to whom the sky was simply the sky.

In exchange, she would lose her storms. The great ones. The ones that had made her mother a legend and her exile a necessity. What remained would be whispers. Rain that fell gently on purpose. A woman who could talk to weather the way you talk to an old friend across a kitchen table.

They had argued about it, of course. In the guest-chamber of coral-grown thunderheads, in the hours before the court convened, they had argued the way only people who love each other argue — with total honesty and no exits.

"You're giving up more," she'd said. "I know what mortality is. You don't know what you're giving up."

"I know exactly what I'm giving up," he'd said. "I'm giving up the right to be protected from you. Which is the only thing I've ever wanted."

She hadn't had an answer for that. She'd kissed him instead, and the thunderheads outside the window had rumbled approvingly, and Theo had thought: *even the sky is eavesdropping on us now.*

"Theo." Marguerite's voice pulled him back to the meadow. "The Warden's waiting."

The Warden was waiting. It had been waiting, Theo suspected, for a very long time.

"Stand at the seam," the Warden said. "Face each other. The vow requires that you each see the half you are losing."

They stood. The gold light came up between them like something rising from under the ground, and Theo felt it against his skin — not warm, not cold, but *insistent*, like a hand checking whether he was real.

"Marguerite Ishikawa," the Warden said. "Daughter of Seraphine, daughter of the line of callers. What do you bring?"

She didn't hesitate. That was the thing about Marguerite that Theo had learned in the worst weeks of his life: she could be afraid and certain at the same time, and the certainty always won.

"My remaining years," she said. "Half of what's left of my life. Freely."

The gold light took the words and did something with them — Theo felt the meadow shiver, felt the seam widen by a hair — and then the Warden turned its worn, patient face to him, and the meadow went very quiet.

"Theo Vance," it said. "Son of no line that matters. Mortal of the Hollow Valley. What do you bring?"

### Marguerite

*Half of what's left of my life.*

She had said it and the words had left her like blood leaving a wound, and now it was Theo's turn, and Marguerite discovered that there was a special kind of torture in watching the person you love volunteer.

"My mortal permanence," Theo said. He stumbled slightly on *permanence* — he still wasn't sure what it meant, none of them were, the archivist-storm had explained it three times and Marguerite still only half understood — but he said it loudly, the way he did everything that scared him. "Freely."

The seam between the worlds brightened. The grass around their feet turned from silver to white-gold, and Marguerite felt the vow take hold of them both — not painfully, not yet, but with the impersonal grip of something vast checking its arithmetic.

"Freely given," the Warden said, "is freely taken. The covenant requires two. The covenant requires halves. Speak the old words."

The old words rose in Marguerite's memory the way her storms had risen in the garden — unbidden, enormous, a language she hadn't spoken in fifteen years and had never forgotten. Her grandmother had taught them to her as a lullaby, which said something about her family that Marguerite had spent half a lifetime not examining.

She spoke. The words were not in any human tongue. They were in the language weather dreams in, and as she spoke them she felt them *do things* — felt them unbolt doors inside her that she had welded shut the day she walked into the valley, felt them open every cage her exile had built, and what came out of the cages was not just power but memory. Fifteen years of it, all at once.

She was nineteen, standing in her mother's court, being told whose hand she would join and what covenant she would carry and how her whole life had been signed for before she was born. She was nineteen, walking out. She was twenty, alone in a valley where the sky never cracked, planting herbs in dead soil with dead hands and telling herself this was freedom. She was thirty-four, watching a clumsy man drop a ring into a pot of burnt stew, and the candles bending toward the window, and thinking: *it found me. It always finds me.*

The words took all of it. The words didn't care. The words were a loom and she was thread.

Then Theo spoke them.

He spoke them badly. His human mouth mangled the vowels, and the storm-folk watching from their side of the seam — the court had come to witness, a hundred shapes of cloud and lightning gathered on the ridge above — made a sound like wind through a canyon, which Marguerite chose to interpret as amusement. But the words didn't care about his accent. The words cared about the *freely*, and he had said that part perfectly, and the seam between the worlds opened like an eye.

Light came through.

Not storm-light. Not starlight. Something older than both, the light the first covenant had been made from, back when two beings — one of the cloud, one of the clay — had stood in this same meadow and decided that the worlds should touch. Marguerite had read about them in the archivist-storm's memory. She had assumed the story was symbolic.

It was not symbolic. They were standing in the story. The light was the same light, and it was looking at them, and it was *hungry* for what they had offered.

"Hold on to me," Theo said.

"You hold on to me."

They held on to each other, and the light came down.

### Theo

It did not feel like burning.

That was the lie of the prophecy, he understood now, in the moment itself, in the white-gold everything. *The bridge that must burn.* The last bridge had refused because he'd thought it meant fire, meant death, meant ending. But burning was the metaphor of people who had never been transformed. What it actually felt like was—

Being read.

The light read him. Every page. It found the day his mother died and the day he'd met Marguerite at the valley market, when she'd argued with a vendor about the price of thyme and he'd fallen for her so fast he'd tripped over his own feet leaving. It found every ordinary morning, the coffee, the garden, the way she hummed when she thought he was asleep. It weighed all of it. And then it said, in a voice that was not a voice: *half.*

And it took.

Theo screamed. He would deny this later; Marguerite would confirm it anyway. It didn't feel like losing a limb. It felt like losing a limb *and remembering what the limb had done*, phantom and present at once. His mortality — the slow human burn-down he had never once thought about, the eighty assumed years, the ordinary decay — tore out of him like a root tearing from soil, and what was left behind was flooded with something else. The weather. He could *hear* the weather. He could hear the storm realm's heartbeat on one side of the seam and the valley's slow green breathing on the other, and both of them were inside his chest now, and he was the hinge, he was the seam, he was—

Beside him, Marguerite was screaming too.

Her half was worse. He knew it was worse even as it happened, because he could feel it through the place where their hands had fused with light — he could feel her storms being taken, the great ones, the cathedral-sized ones, the power that had made her mother's name a title and her own name a warning. It left her in a long tearing exhalation, and the storm-folk on the ridge cried out as it went, because that power had been *theirs*, realm-born, and now it poured into the seam and became covenant-stuff, became the mortar of the new vow, became—

The seam sealed.

Not closed — sealed, the way a wound seals, the way a vow seals. The gold line between storm and stars thinned to a thread and then to a shimmer and then to something you would only see if you knew to look. But it was *there*. Theo could feel it the way you feel a wedding ring: not heavy, just always.

The light let go.

They fell.

### Marguerite

They landed in the silver grass, tangled together, both of them breathing like divers surfacing. Above them the sky had healed — storm on one side, stars on the other, and between them, if you knew where to look, a faint gold shimmer, thin as breath on glass.

A veil for the Hollow. That was what they had made. Not a wall. Not a door. A veil.

Marguerite lay in the grass and took inventory of herself the way you take inventory after a house fire.

The storms were gone. She reached for them out of fifteen years of habit and found — whispers. A soft pressure in her palms where the lightning used to live, like the memory of a handshake. She could feel the weather the way Theo now could, she realized; that part had been shared. But where her power had been a cathedral, there was now a garden. Small. Tended. *Hers* in a way the cathedral had never been.

And underneath it — the other half of the arithmetic. Half her remaining years, given. She felt them gone the way you feel a tooth missing: a lightness that was also a loss. She would die sooner now. Not soon. But sooner. And Theo would age faster than a man should, and they would meet in the middle, and the middle was theirs.

She started laughing. She couldn't help it. It came up out of her like the first storm she'd ever called, at nine years old, terrifying and joyous and completely beyond her control.

Theo lifted his head from the grass. His hair was streaked with silver at the temples — it hadn't been that morning. His eyes were different. Storm-colored now, or valley-colored, she couldn't tell, both, and when he looked at her she knew he was seeing the same change in her: the exile's dimness gone, the caller's blaze gentled, something new and level and *quiet* in its place.

"You're laughing," he said. "We just got half of ourselves deleted and you're laughing."

"I'm laughing because it *worked*." She sat up. The storm-folk were coming down the ridge, a hundred shapes of cloud descending with something that might have been awe. The Warden stood closest of all, and for the first time since the boundary of the valley, the Warden was smiling. Or doing whatever a thousand-year-old keeper of broken things did instead.

"Look," Theo said, and took her hand, and held it up.

Their palms, where they had gripped each other, bore the sigil — the same one the Warden had marked on him at the boundary, but doubled now, interlocked, a knot of light sunk into the skin like a brand made of dawn. It didn't hurt. It hummed.

"The covenant requires two," the Warden said. "The covenant *has* two."

Above the meadow, on the storm side of the sky, the perpetual lightning dimmed — not dying, but settling, the way a fire settles into embers. And on the valley side, far below, Marguerite felt it begin: the frost, the long winter that had been creeping a foot an hour toward everything she loved, *stopping*. Reversing. A single degree of warmth returning to the world like a first word of forgiveness.

She felt it because she was the veil now. She felt both sides. That was the price and the gift, braided so tightly she would never untangle them, and she found she didn't want to.

"Theo," she said.

"Yeah."

"I think we should get home. I think—" She stopped, because she was feeling something else now, through the new sense, through the veil-sense that was hers and his and theirs. Something at the edge of the storm realm. A carriage of thunder, parked alone on a high dark ridge. A presence inside it, vast and proud and utterly shattered, that had watched her daughter's vow from a distance and had not been invited closer.

Her mother.

"Marguerite?" Theo said. "What is it?"

She stood, pulling him up with her, and looked out across the storm realm toward the distant ridge where a woman who had written a prophecy to trap her own daughter was sitting alone with everything she had broken.

"It's my mother," Marguerite said. "And I think she's waiting for me."
