## 30. Aftermath of Becoming

The first thing Theo Vance noticed when he woke was that the rain had a voice.

Not a sound — he'd heard rain all his life, the drumming on shingles, the hiss on hot stone. A voice. It was saying something patient and enormous, the way a river says something to its banks, and it had been saying it for hours, maybe for years, and only now did he have ears for it.

He lay still on the narrow bed in the Warden's waystation, the one that sat exactly on the line between worlds, and listened.

*Slow down,* the rain was saying. *Slow down. Slow down.*

Marguerite was beside him, her hand in his, and her hand was the second thing he noticed. It was warm — it had always been warm — but the warmth had changed its character. Before, holding her hand had been like holding a wire that a current might pass through at any moment, a pleasant low hum of danger under everything. Now it was just a hand. A beautiful hand. A human hand, warm the way bread is warm, warm the way a hearth is warm, warm with no weather in it at all.

He turned his head. She was awake. He knew it before her eyes opened, because he could feel her the way he could feel the rain — a presence, a pressure, a mood in the air of the room.

"Tell me what you hear," she said, without opening her eyes.

"Rain," he said. "It's talking."

"What's it saying?"

"Slow down."

Her mouth curved, but it was a sad curve, the curve of someone watching a child do something wonderful and knowing the child will pay for it later. "Mine stopped talking," she said. "Three days ago. Or — no. I stopped hearing it. Same thing, maybe. Maybe not."

She opened her eyes then, and Theo saw what the vow had done to them, written in the only language her face had ever been fluent in: weather. Her eyes, which had held lightning the way a jar holds a storm — visible, contained, waiting — held nothing now but gray. Clear gray. Honest gray. The gray of a sky that has decided, once and for all, to be only a sky.

"Marguerite—"

"Don't," she said gently. "Not yet. Let me get there in my own time."

So he didn't. He lay beside her and listened to the rain say *slow down* and felt the valley breathing under them — because that was the third thing, the thing he hadn't told her yet, that he could feel the valley itself now, its moods rising through the bedrock like heat through a floor. The valley was relieved. That was the word for it. The valley had been holding its breath for a hundred years and had finally, somewhere in the night, let it out, and Theo could feel the long exhalation of it moving through the soil, through the roots, through the stones of the waystation wall.

He was part of it now. That was the bargain. He had given up his permanence — the plain, unremarkable, mortal permanence of a man who would age at the ordinary rate and die at the ordinary time — and in exchange he had become the valley's nerve ending. Its ear. Its skin.

He did the arithmetic without wanting to. The storm-court had been precise about it, in the way that ancient things are precise when precision costs them nothing. He would age perhaps a third faster than he should have. Sixty years of life would pass through him in forty. He was thirty-one. The numbers sat in his chest like stones in a shoe, small and unavoidable.

Marguerite had given up the other half. Her storms — the thing that had made her a weapon and a wonder and, in the end, a fugitive from her own mother — were gone. Not gone. *Gentled*, the court had said, with the delicacy of executioners. Gentled to whispers. She would feel weather the way a poet feels weather, not the way a general feels weather. She would never again call lightning out of a clear sky. She would never again be the strongest caller her realm had produced in three generations.

They had each surrendered exactly half of what they were, and the covenant held, and the winter was breaking, and this morning the rain was talking to him, and he lay there in the gray light grieving.

* * *

Marguerite got up first. She always did. She moved through the waystation's single room the way she moved through every room, like someone perpetually arriving from a great distance, and she built up the fire and set water on and did all the small domestic liturgies that Theo had once believed were the whole of her — the exile's liturgies, the mortal's liturgies — and he watched her and understood that they were no longer a disguise.

They were just her now.

She caught him watching. "Say it," she said.

"It's going to be strange."

"It's already strange. I reached for the kettle and the steam bent toward me and then — nothing. Like whistling for a dog that's dead." She said it flatly, and then her face did something he had never seen it do in fifteen years: it crumpled. Not broke — crumpled, briefly, the way paper crumples, and then she smoothed it again with visible effort, the way you'd smooth a letter before sending it. "I'm sorry. I keep doing that. It comes up out of nowhere. Grief with no manners."

"Let it have no manners," Theo said. "It earned them."

She laughed, wet and short. "When did you get wise?"

"About an hour ago. It came with the rain."

He got up and crossed to her and put his arms around her from behind, his chin on her hair, and she leaned back into him, and they stood like that while the water heated, and he felt her grief through his new senses the way he felt everything now — not as thought but as weather. Her sadness moved through the room like a front. He could feel where it was heaviest. He turned her gently and kissed the place between her eyebrows, because that was where it sat, a small gray cloud, and she made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and said, "You can feel that, can't you."

"Sorry."

"No. Don't be sorry. It's — God, Theo, it's the most anyone has ever paid attention to me." She pulled back and looked at him, and her gray eyes were fierce again, the fierceness intact even if the lightning wasn't. "That's the joke, isn't it. I spent fifteen years being the most powerful thing in this valley and nobody could touch me. And now I'm nobody, and you can feel my sadness in your teeth."

"You were never nobody."

"I know." She touched his face. "I mean I'm mortal. Finally. Properly. No asterisk." Her hand stopped on his cheek. "But you're not. That's the part I can't — Theo, they took years off you. I counted them. I stood there and watched them take them and I said yes. I said yes."

"I said yes first."

"You said yes to the vow. You didn't say yes to dying at sixty."

"Nobody dies at sixty in this valley," Theo said. "Bramble is ninety and still cheating at cards."

She didn't laugh. She pressed her forehead to his chest and stayed there, and he held her, and through his new skin he felt the valley's relief and her grief braided together, and he understood that this was what the covenant had actually made: not a bridge between realms but a bridge between them, a structure that could carry weight in both directions. She could no longer shelter him with storms. He could no longer be sheltered by not knowing. They stood in the open together, equally exposed, and it was the most terrifying and most honest thing he had ever been part of.

* * *

They walked home through the breaking winter.

The frost was retreating the way a tide retreats — not all at once but in long shivering pulls, the white edge dragging back from the fields, and where it went the ground was black and steaming and alive. Theo could feel the retreat under his feet. It felt like a fever breaking. Twice he stopped and put his palm flat against a fencepost and said, "It'll clear the mill field by tonight," and both times Marguerite looked at him with an expression he couldn't read, and the second time she said, "You're doing it already. Reading it."

"Is that all right?"

"It's going to take getting used to." She walked a few steps. "I spent fifteen years being the only strange thing in this valley. Now there are two of us and neither of us is the one I was."

"Marguerite." He stopped her, hands on her shoulders, in the middle of the road where anyone could see, because he was done with the old habit of keeping things in the house. "Tell me the worst of it. Not the grief — the grief we'll carry. The worst. What do you wake up missing most?"

She was quiet a long moment. A crow went over, and he felt the crow too, its small cold satisfaction, and marveled and hated it a little.

"The noise," she said at last. "You can't understand the noise. From the time I was four there was weather in me — all of it, everywhere, every front from here to the outer reaches, a choir that never stopped. I used to think it would drive me mad. When I renounced it, the silence was the sweetest thing I'd ever known. Fifteen years of silence, Theo. I built a whole life out of silence. And now the silence has a hole in it, shaped like everything I was, and I keep turning toward the sound that isn't there." She smiled, wrecked and steady. "What about you? What's the worst of it?"

Theo considered. The honest answer was the years, the arithmetic, the forty years where sixty should have been. But that wasn't the worst. The worst was smaller and stranger.

"I used to be closed," he said. "You know? All those years you couldn't tell me things, and I told myself it was because you were protecting me, but some of it was me. I was a shut door. It was comfortable in there." He looked down at his hands. "Now I can feel the miller's wife worrying about her roof from a hundred yards, and the river being smug, and the whole valley's mood like a smell in a room. There's no door anymore, Marguerite. I'm a window that can't close." He laughed, and heard how thin it came out. "I keep thinking — in forty years, when I'm old, I'm going to have felt everything. Every mood of every season. I don't know if a man wears out, feeling that much."

She took his hand. Her grip was just a grip now, no current under it, and he found he loved it more, not less.

"Then we'll wear out together," she said. "I've got maybe sixty mortal years left, properly counted, no asterisk. You've got forty. We'll overlap for forty years and then you'll have to haunt me for twenty, and I expect you'll be insufferable about it."

"That's the plan," he said, and his voice broke on the last word, and she pulled him in and held him in the middle of the road while the frost pulled back around them and the valley exhaled and the rain, somewhere behind the clouds, went on saying *slow down, slow down* to a world that was finally, at last, beginning to listen.

* * *

They reached the cottage at dusk.

The garden was the worst of it. The surge — her raw, bleeding, caged storm that had torn loose before they crossed — had flattened it, and the vow had not healed it, because the vow healed realms, not radishes. The bean poles leaned like drunks. The herb beds were a brown ruin. The little fence Theo had built the first summer, the one she'd teased him for overbuilding, was splintered down its whole length.

Marguerite stood at the gate and looked at it for a long time.

"I did that," she said.

"We did that. I was holding your hand when it happened."

"You were. And it burned you." She took his right hand and turned it over. The burn had healed into a pale scar across the palm — the Warden's sigil and the surge's mark, one on top of the other, a palimpsest. She traced it with one finger. "Half of me did this," she said quietly. "And half of me is never going to be able to do anything like it again. I don't know which half I'm supposed to mourn."

"Both," Theo said. "I think you're allowed both."

She nodded. Then she opened the gate, and walked into the ruined garden, and knelt down in the mud, and began — with her bare hands, in the cold, with no magic at all — to clear the wreckage. Pulling up the broken stalks. Gathering the splintered fence rails. Working in the gray last light like any mortal woman in any mortal valley, and Theo stood at the gate and watched her and felt something in his chest that wasn't grief at all.

Because here was the thing he couldn't have said before, the thing the vow had burned into him along with everything else: she was more herself now, kneeling in the mud with dirt to her elbows and no storm anywhere in her, than she had ever been in fifteen years of careful, silent, armored exile. The armor was off. All of it. And underneath, it turned out, was just Marguerite — stubborn, funny, grieving, planting things.

He knelt beside her and started on the fence rails.

They worked until full dark, and then went in, and she lit the lamps — both of them guttering toward her for a moment out of old habit before remembering themselves and standing up straight like embarrassed soldiers — and they ate bread and cheese at the table where he had proposed, and it was the quietest meal they had ever eaten, and the best.

"I keep waiting to feel robbed," Theo said.

"And?"

"And I feel — lighter. Like something got amputated that I'd forgotten was heavy." He turned his marked palm up. "Is that terrible?"

"It's the truest thing anyone's said in this house in fifteen years." She reached across and closed her fingers over his scar. "The court said we'd each lose half. Nobody said we'd each lose the half we were carrying for the other."

Outside, the frost line pulled back another mile in the dark, and he felt it go, a long cool shiver of release running through the valley's bones, and he fell asleep that night to the sound of the rain still saying *slow down* — but softer now, almost tender, the way you'd say it to someone learning.

* * *

He woke before dawn to a knock at the door.

Not a villager's knock — villagers knocked like they were asking permission. This was three measured strikes, evenly spaced, patient as tide. He knew it before he opened the door, the way he knew everything now, through the soles of his feet and the skin of the night air: the Warden.

The mist stood on the doorstep, coiled and courteous, holding something out in a tendril that was almost a hand.

"Vance," it said. Its voice was like wind deciding to be polite. "The court sends word. And there is a letter."

He took the letter. Heavy paper, storm-gray, sealed with a sigil he knew from his one terrible visit to the Court of Falling Weather: the mark of the archivists. But it was the second thing the Warden held that stopped his breath — a second letter, smaller, sealed with wax the color of a bruise, and on the seal, pressed deep, the crest of the High Storm-Caller of the outer reaches.

Seraphine.

"She crossed into exile last night," the Warden said. "As the court decreed. She asked that this be carried to the valley. To your house. Not to the council. To your house." The mist paused, and if a thing made of fog could be awkward, it was awkward now. "She asked that it be given to her daughter. And she asked me to say —" the Warden's voice did something it had never done in all its centuries of carrying messages, something that sounded almost like it had caught — "she asked me to say that she was wrong about the arithmetic."

Theo stood in the doorway with two letters in his hand and the whole valley's mood rising through his bare feet like heat, and behind him he heard Marguerite sit up in the dark and say, "Theo? Who is it?" — and he turned, and she saw the letters, and he watched her face do the thing paper does, crumple and smooth, crumple and smooth.

"Marguerite," he said. "Your mother wrote."
