## 32. The Long Winter Breaks

The crossing home was nothing like the crossing out.

Going in, the boundary had torn at me like wet cloth, my lungs splitting open as fifteen years of exile peeled away and the magic poured back in — a drowning that felt like being born. Going out, the veil parted for us like a curtain held by a courteous hand. The Warden stood aside, all mist and patience, and inclined what might have been a head.

"The valley will feel you coming," it said. Its voice was the sound of fog settling on a field. "Try to arrive gently."

Theo squeezed my hand. "No pressure," he murmured.

We stepped through together, and this time I did not fall.

* * *

The Hollow Valley in winter had always been gray and quiet and closed — a bowl of still air, a place where weather went to be polite. What we walked into was something else entirely.

Frost still clung to the upper slopes, but it was retreating as we watched. I don't mean melting. Melting is slow, democratic, a thing of sun and hours. This was retreat — the white line pulling back up the hillsides like a tide going out, mile by visible mile, the grass emerging green and shaking behind it as if it had been holding its breath all winter and only now dared exhale. The frost wasn't dying. It was leaving. It had somewhere else to be, now that the covenant said so.

"It's fast," Theo said. "That's fast, right? That's not normal-fast?"

"Nothing about this is normal anything." I watched a sheet of ice let go of Bramble's orchard wall and slide uphill instead of down, which should have been impossible, and which the ice itself seemed mildly embarrassed about. "The old covenant took a hundred years to fail. The new one doesn't have time to fail slowly. It's settling."

"Settling," Theo repeated. "Like a house."

"Like a house," I agreed. "Except the house is two worlds, and we just moved the load-bearing wall."

He looked at me sideways. He'd been doing that since the vow — checking, quietly, constantly, that I was still there, still whole, still mine. I kept doing it back. We hadn't talked yet about what we'd each lost. There hadn't been time between the altar of the covenant and the storm-court's farewell and the long walk to the boundary, and if I was honest with myself — a habit I was trying to relearn, having spent fifteen years practicing the opposite — I was grateful for the delay. Some griefs need a room with a door.

But I could already feel the shape of mine. Where there had been thunder in my chest since the crossing — vast, oceanic, the full weight of the storm realm pouring back through channels that had scarred over — there was now something smaller. A whisper where there had been a roar. When I reached inward for lightning, I found a breeze. When I asked the sky overhead for rain, I felt it answer the way an old dog answers: slowly, affectionately, without any particular urgency.

Half of what I was, given to the veil. That was the bargain. My remaining mortal years and his mortal permanence, fused and offered up, each of us halved so the covenant could hold.

I kept waiting to feel robbed. Mostly I felt lighter. That frightened me more than grief would have.

"Garden first?" Theo said. "Or town?"

I thought about our herb garden, dead or dormant under months of unseasonal frost, the rosemary I'd coaxed from seed probably gone, the sage probably gone, everything I'd built in the valley probably gone — and then I thought about Mrs. Alder standing at her window watching the frost run uphill, alone with a thing she had no words for.

"Town," I said.

* * *

We came down off the ridge road into Lower Hollow around mid-morning, and I want to record honestly what happened, because later I suspect people will tell it differently.

They were all outside.

Every one of them. Standing in their doorways and their lanes and their fields, faces tipped up toward a sky that was doing something none of them had ever seen it do. Because the clouds had begun, very gently, to rain — and the rain was warm.

Not valley-warm, which is the tepid neutrality of water that has never known weather. Warm-warm. Storm-realm warm, the temperature of rain that has fallen through lightning-lit air and carried some of the light down with it. It fell on the backs of their necks and their upturned palms and they stood there taking it like a message they couldn't quite read but knew was meant for them.

Old Man Bramble was in the middle of the square, of course, because Bramble would stand in the middle of a burning building to make sure the fire observed proper procedure. He was dry under a waxed coat, arms folded, watching us come down the road with an expression I couldn't parse until I was close enough to see his hands.

His hands were shaking.

"You're alive," he said.

"We're alive."

"The frost turned this morning. Before dawn. Went up the mountain like it was called home." His voice was the flat careful voice of a man refusing to feel relief until the arithmetic confirmed it. "I've read every founder's ledger twice. Nothing says the frost can turn. Nothing says it comes back warm."

"The ledgers wouldn't know," I said. "This isn't in them. We wrote a new page."

Bramble looked at me for a long moment. Behind him, the rain fell on the square, and steam rose faintly off the cobbles, and someone's child shrieked with laughter because the puddles were *warm*, Mama, feel, they're *warm* —

Then Bramble did something I will remember longer than the duel in the thunderhead, longer than the light braiding through us at the veil, longer maybe than anything. He unfolded his arms. Took off his hat. And let the rain hit him.

His face when it landed — eighty-some years old, a face that had presided over a hundred years of hoarded dread — went slack with something that was not joy exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man discovering the price he'd been collecting all his life had been refunded, and he didn't know who he was without it.

"Huh," he said, to no one. "It's soft."

* * *

The changes came out over the next day, the way changes do — small at first, then everywhere at once.

Mrs. Alder, who runs the bakery and has never once in her life been wrong about bread, came to the door before noon with flour on her hands and terror on her face, and said, "Marguerite, I need you to tell me I'm not sick." She could feel the oven before she opened it, she said. Not heat — she'd always felt heat. She could feel the *fire*, its mood, its attention, the way you can feel a cat watching you from across a room. "And the dough," she whispered. "The yeast. It's like they're talking. Not words. Just — wanting things."

"You're not sick," I told her, and took her hands, and felt it too: the small bright thread of the veil running through her like a vein of ore. "You're listening. You've got founders' blood. The covenant used to pass you by because the valley was sealed. Now the door's open a crack, and everything on the other side is saying hello."

"Make it stop," she said.

"Do you want it to stop?"

She thought about that for a long time, hands in mine, flour dusting between our fingers. Somewhere behind her the bread spoke its slow yeasty wanting, and her eyes flicked toward it, hungry despite herself.

"No," she admitted. "God help me. No."

By evening there were eleven of them — eleven villagers who could feel the veil, all of them founders' descendants, all of them walking around the valley looking like people who'd discovered a room in their house they'd never noticed. Young Tam Wiley, seventeen, sat up on the ridge all afternoon because he said the wind up there was telling him about the sea, and Tam had never seen the sea. The Hollis twins, six years old, were caught moving water between cups without touching either cup, giggling, and their mother came pounding on our door at dusk half furious and half on the edge of tears, saying *what did you do, what did you do to my girls* — and I said, honestly, "We opened a window. They were already at it. They'd been pressing their faces against the glass their whole lives; they just didn't know there was glass."

She left unconvinced, and I heard her later, through two walls, asking the twins to show her again.

* * *

Our garden had not survived. I knew it before we reached the gate — I could feel the plot now the way Mrs. Alder felt her oven, its whole small history of thirst and cold written in the soil. The rosemary was black. The sage had rotted under ice. The bean frames lay flat, snapped by a frost heavier than anything the valley had thrown in living memory.

Theo stood at the gate beside me and we looked at the wreck of it together, and neither of us spoke, because we both understood we weren't really looking at the garden.

"Say it," he finally said. "Whatever it is."

"I keep waiting to feel ruined." I pushed the gate open. It creaked the same creak it always had; the hinge hadn't changed; I found that stupidly comforting. "Fifteen years I built a life on the understanding that magic cost me everything, and I made peace with it, and then I paid it anyway — more than the exile ever asked — and I'm standing here in the wreckage and I feel..." I searched. "Unburdened. Like the garden died and I didn't. And I don't know what that makes me."

Theo crouched by the rosemary and broke off a blackened sprig and crumbled it between his fingers. He does this — handles things while thinking, works the question through his hands. Since the vow his hands are different. I notice it most at night. There's a hum in them now, faint, like wire carrying current far below the level of danger.

"It makes you someone whose cage is open," he said. "Cages feel safe till you're out of them. Then you spend a while grieving the bars, because the bars were doing something for you." He looked up. "I lied to you once to protect you. You left your whole life once to protect me. We're both recovering protectors. Give yourself the same mercy you gave your mother."

"That's a low bar. I nearly drowned her in cloud."

"You refused to strike the final blow against her in front of an entire court of beings who wanted you to. That's the bar, and you cleared it." He stood, brushed his palms, and surveyed the ruin with the expression of a man pricing lumber. "So. Beans again? Same corner? Or do we finally try the thing with the three sisters, like you've been talking about for four years?"

I laughed — really laughed, the first one since the storm realm, and I felt it land in my chest in the place where thunder used to live, and it fit. It fit fine.

"Three sisters," I said. "Corn, beans, squash. And rosemary along the north wall, from cuttings. Mrs. Alder's plant is thriving; she says it hums when the oven's lit."

"Rosemary that hums. Sure. Normal valley things." He slung an arm around my shoulders, and we turned our backs on the dead garden, and it was easy, it was astonishingly easy, because the soil under our boots was already warming, already drinking the warm rain, already forgetting the frost that had gripped it for a hundred years.

* * *

That night the valley threw no celebration, because valleys don't throw celebrations, they hold suppers. Someone — I never learned who — put the word out, and by dark the meeting hall was full, trestle tables dragged end to end, every pot in the Hollow contributing. Theo and I came in cold and soaked and were fed within thirty seconds of the door by four different women operating with the coordinated ruthlessness of a military campaign.

It was loud. That's what I keep returning to, trying to describe it to myself. Fifteen years in that valley and I never once heard it loud. The Hollow's silence was part of its charter — the hush of a sealed world, sound falling flat, laughter dying young. But tonight the hall rang. Tam Wiley was in the corner describing the sea to a ring of children who had also never seen it, gesturing hugely, getting it slightly wrong in ways that didn't matter. Mrs. Alder had brought bread that she swore talked back and everyone pretended to listen to their crusts solemnly before eating them. The Hollis twins made the water in the pitchers rise in little peaks whenever anyone said the word *storm*, which everyone therefore said constantly.

And underneath it all — I stopped eating for a moment just to confirm it — the valley itself was humming. Faintly. Contentedly. The sound of a mechanism running after a century seized. The covenant, binding rather than consuming, holding.

Theo leaned close under the noise. "Feel that?"

"I feel everything," I said. "It's going to take some getting used to."

"Tell me the truth. Do you miss it? The big version. The roar."

I considered lying, decided I was done with that forever, and said, "Sometimes I reach for the lightning and there's a breeze, and I think — oh. Oh, that's all. And it's like missing a limb in a dream. But then the breeze moves something. Tips a leaf. Cools a cheek." I looked down the table at the twins levitating their soup. "I spent so long believing power had to be enormous to matter. My mother believes it still. Enormous, or nothing. Burn the bridge or seal the world. She never once imagined the middle."

"She's learning," Theo said. "Slowly. In the outer reaches. Alone."

"Penance," I said. "Her word. Her terms. She knelt to the court; I didn't make her." I turned my cup. "Letters will come through the Warden eventually. Terse ones. And I'll write back. And it won't be forgiveness for years, maybe ever, but the channel stays open, because I watched what closing channels costs."

Theo put his hand over mine — the humming hand, the wire-and-current hand — and we sat like that while the hall roared, and outside the warm rain came down steady on a world remaking itself faster than anyone could narrate.

* * *

I woke near dawn to a feeling at the edge of my new senses, and lay still in the dark, listening.

The frost had finished its retreat. All night it had climbed, mile by mile, up past the high pastures, past the scree, up to the shoulders of the mountains where it belonged — and now, at the very crest of the western ridge, it had stopped. Settled. Gone still and white and permanent-looking, the way winter is supposed to look: seasonal, lawful, at home.

And beneath it, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, I felt something else on that ridge. Not frost. Not storm. Something patient and old, standing at the boundary of the boundary, where the valley's jurisdiction ends and the wild weather begins.

The Warden didn't come down. It didn't speak. It simply stood there in the pre-dawn gray, mist against snow, and I had the distinct impression — absurd, unmistakable — of a messenger who had delivered its last letter and was now waiting to see whether anyone would write another.

Down the hall, in the kitchen, our letter shelf sat empty. For now.

I got up quietly, so as not to wake Theo, and lit the stove, and put the kettle on, and stood at the window watching the sun come up over the western ridge — over the frost, over the figure of mist at the top of the world, over the first morning of the rest of the covenant.

The kettle began, softly, to sing.

And then, from the direction of the gate, came the sound of hooves — fast ones, many, riding hard through the warm rain — and Tam Wiley's voice cracking across the valley, high with excitement and fear:

"MARGUERITE! Riders on the north road! They're wearing *lightning!*"

The kettle screamed. Out on the ridge, the Warden stirred like smoke in wind.

So much, I thought, reaching for the latch, for a gentle arrival.
