## 34. A Ring Reforged

The lightning-glass had been sitting in my coat pocket for eleven days, and in all that time I had not managed to say a single word about it.

It wasn't much to look at, if I was honest. When the storm-court's oldest member — a being called Vess who had once been a hurricane and now preferred to be a quiet drizzle — pressed the small wrapped bundle into my hands, I'd expected something dazzling. A gem that threw off light. A stone with a storm trapped inside it. Instead, what I held was dull grey-green, the color of pond ice, warm as skin, and faintly, when I turned it toward the window, it caught the daylight and bent it just slightly wrong, like heat shimmer over a summer road.

"That's it?" I'd said.

Vess had made a sound like distant thunder clearing its throat. "It is cooled lightning, mortal. Do you know how rare it is for a bolt to strike and live? Most lightning dies in the air, spends itself on nothing. But sometimes — rarely — a bolt finds something worth grounding through, and survives the journey." A pause, like clouds gathering. "Your court has never given one away before. We do not make them. We only find them, after storms, in places where someone stood their ground."

I had turned the little stone over in my palm and felt, absurdly, like it was humming.

"It will hold an edge," Vess added. "And a promise."

* * *

Eleven days later, I still hadn't said anything, because saying things had gotten complicated.

Here's what nobody tells you about surviving a prophecy: afterward, everything ordinary feels like it might be a trap. I'd spent months learning that the world ran on bargains and blood prices and books that wrote themselves, and now I couldn't burn toast without wondering if it meant something. Marguerite was worse. She'd wake at night with her hand raised, half-calling a storm that wasn't there anymore — or rather, that was there, but only as a whisper, a suggestion, weather instead of war. She'd look at her own fingers like they belonged to a stranger. Some mornings she grieved out loud. Most mornings she didn't, which was worse.

But she was here. That was the thing I kept coming back to, the fact under all the facts. She was here, in our house, in our valley, planting things in the scorched patch of garden where her magic had come back too fast and flattened everything we'd grown. She knelt in the dirt every morning turning soil that smelled of ozone and rain, humming songs in a language that made the tomato seedlings lean toward her, and she was *here*, and I had a ring in my pocket shaped from a lightning bolt that had survived because it found something worth grounding through.

You don't get handed metaphors like that every day.

The problem was the first proposal. Our first proposal — the one over burnt stew, eleven days before a century-old book told me I was kindling. I'd gotten down on one knee with a ring I'd saved eight months for, and she'd laughed, actually laughed, that bright unguarded sound I'd crossed a magical boundary and nearly died twice to keep hearing — and then the candles had bent toward the window, all of them at once, like they were listening to something, and neither of us ever finished the sentence. The ring went back in its box. The box went in the drawer. And the word *yes* — hers, mine, either — hung unfinished between us for a year while frost crept across a valley and a Warden marked my palm and my almost-fiancée fought her mother in a thunderhead and refused, even bleeding, even winning, to strike the final blow.

I'd decided somewhere around day nine that I wasn't going to make a production of it. No kneeling in the town square. No arranged moment. Marguerite Ishikawa had been arranged at enough in her life; the whole point of us was that we were the opposite of arrangement. We were burnt stew and herb gardens and her falling asleep mid-sentence against my shoulder. If I was going to ask her again, it was going to happen the way our life actually happened: sideways, unplanned, probably while she had dirt on her face.

Which is why it happened on a Tuesday, in the garden, while I was holding a hoe.

* * *

The garden had come back strange. Everything grew faster now — magic moved through the valley like weather, subtle and seasonal, and the vegetables had opinions. The carrots came up curled into shapes that Bramble swore were runes (Bramble had dissolved the founders' council two weeks ago and now spent his retirement being aggressively helpful, which everyone pretended not to find alarming). The beans climbed strings we hadn't strung. And in the center, where the surge had leveled everything, Marguerite had planted a row of something she wouldn't name, and it had come up silver-stemmed, and when the wind touched it, it chimed.

She was on her knees among the silver plants when I came out, hair tied back with what I recognized as one of my shirtsleeves cut into a strip, dirt to both elbows, talking to the seedlings in that low storm-tongue that wasn't quite words. The morning was warm. Real warmth — the valley's first true spring in a hundred years, and everyone kept stepping outside just to stand in it, like dogs in a sunbeam. Down the lane, old Mrs. Okafor was hanging laundry and letting the sheets billow, and every so often she'd close her eyes and lift her face and you could tell she was feeling the veil, the way half the village could now, the way the whole valley had woken up changed and mostly decided to be delighted about it.

Marguerite looked up at me, squinting against the sun, and smiled — tired, real, hers — and I felt the pocket of my jacket like it weighed forty pounds.

"Morning," she said. "Your tomatoes are gossiping again."

"What are they saying?"

"That you overwater them out of guilt." She sat back on her heels and wiped her forehead with her wrist, leaving a smear of dirt across it. "They're right, Theo. You water them like they're going to leave you."

"They might," I said. "Everything else did."

It came out lighter than it landed. Her smile flickered — she knew exactly what I meant, the year of frost, the winter that had eaten whole seasons, the months when we'd each been preparing, separately, silently, to lose the other. She reached out and took my hand, dirt and all, and squeezed it, and I stood there in my own garden holding a hoe like an idiot and thought: *now, you coward, now.*

"Marguerite."

"Theo." She tilted her head. She always knew when I was winding up to something. Fifteen years of exile had taught her to read people like weather fronts, and I had never once managed to be unpredictable to her.

I put the hoe down. I did not kneel — my knee had been acting up since the crossing and also kneeling felt like a bit, this time, like costume. I just took the little bundle out of my pocket, the cloth Vess had wrapped it in, grey storm-silk that swallowed light, and held it between us on my open palm.

Her eyes went to it and stayed. I watched her go very still, the way she used to go still when thunder rolled in off the mountains in the years before any of this, some old animal part of her recognizing pressure change.

"That's storm-silk," she said quietly.

"Yeah."

"Where did you—" She stopped. Looked up at me. "Theo. What did you do?"

"Nothing dramatic. Which for me is basically a miracle." I unwrapped it.

The lightning-glass sat on the grey cloth in the sunlight and did its wrong little bend of the light, and Marguerite made a sound I'd never heard from her before — small, involuntary, the sound of someone seeing a ghost they loved. Her hand rose toward it and hovered, not touching.

"Cooled lightning," I said. "Vess gave it to me. Before we left. Apparently it only forms when a bolt strikes something that grounds it — something worth surviving the trip for. It doesn't happen often. They don't make them. They just find them, after the storm, wherever somebody stood their ground." My throat had gone tight. I pushed through it. "I asked them what it was for and Vess got that smug thundercloud look and said, *it will hold an edge, and a promise.*"

Marguerite wasn't breathing. I could tell because the silver plants had gone silent around her, waiting, the way the whole valley seemed to wait when she concentrated.

"So I've had it for eleven days," I said, "and I haven't said anything, because I wanted to get it right, and then I realized there is no getting it right. There's no perfect moment. The perfect moment got interrupted by a cursed book last time, and if I wait for another one, something else will interrupt it, because that's apparently how our lives work. So." I picked up the stone. "I'm doing it badly, in a garden, with dirt everywhere, and you're going to have to forgive me, because—"

"Theo." Her voice cracked on my name.

"—because the last time I asked you this, the sky interrupted me, and I've been meaning to finish the sentence for over a year, so here it is." I held the cooled lightning up between us, catching the spring sun, bending it slightly wrong. "Marry me. Not despite any of it — because of all of it. Because you burned your old life down to protect people who didn't deserve it, and you built a garden in the ashes, and when they asked you to give a man to the winter you said no in front of everyone, and when it was you or your mother you chose mercy with a split lip and shaking hands. Because you're the bravest person I've ever met and the grumpiest person I've ever met and I want to spend whatever lifespan the new covenant leaves me finding out what you'll plant next. Marguerite Ishikawa. Finish the sentence with me. Say yes."

The garden was completely silent. Even Mrs. Okafor's laundry had stopped moving, though I think that was coincidence.

Marguerite was crying, which she would deny later, dirt-striped and furious-eyed and crying, and she opened her mouth —

"Yes," she said.

"—and I know I talked a lot there, you can absolutely take a minute to—"

"*Yes.*" She grabbed the front of my shirt with both filthy hands and kissed me, hard, tasting of soil and morning, and the silver plants around us rang like a hundred tiny bells all at once, and somewhere down the lane Mrs. Okafor whooped loud enough to startle crows off the roof.

When she pulled back she was laughing — that laugh, the one from the dinner, the one the candles stole — and she held out her left hand, palm up, fingers spread. The Warden's sigil had faded from her palm months ago, but I remembered where it had been, and apparently so did she.

"Well?" she said. "Put it on me, Vance. Before the sky gets ideas."

* * *

I should explain the ring, because it took me three nights and two blistered thumbs and I want credit.

Lightning-glass holds an edge, Vess had said. It does. It also fights you. The first night I tried to shape it with the jeweler's tools I borrowed from the smithy — quietly, telling old Hana I had a project, which fooled no one, since the entire valley had been able to feel my mood like barometric pressure since the covenant changed — the stone simply refused. It wouldn't grind. It wouldn't cut. It sat in my workshop radiating patient contempt.

Second night, I tried fire. Obviously. Fire is what I know. The glass drank the heat from the forge like it was a sip of water and looked bored.

Third night I gave up and did the stupid thing. I carried it out into the yard during the evening rain — real rain now, ordinary rain, the valley's new gentle weather — and I held it up on my flat palm and I said, out loud, to the sky:

"I'm asking permission. Not commanding. Asking. He wants to be a ring. Let him be a ring."

And the rain paused. Not stopped — *paused*, the way a crowd pauses, and I swear on every burnt meal I ever made, I felt the whole grey ceiling of cloud lean down and consider me. Then one thin bolt came down, no louder than a snapped twig, struck the stone in my hand, and left.

The lightning-glass had reshaped itself into a band, seamless, no setting, with the original stone riding the top like a drop of frozen light. It fit Marguerite's finger perfectly. I hadn't measured anything. I'd just known.

"You talked to the sky," Marguerite said, staring at the band on her finger, turning it so the light bent wrong. "You *asked* it."

"It seemed polite. It's basically your family now."

"Theo Vance, did you propose to me with a ring you negotiated with the weather?"

"I proposed with a ring the weather *co-authored*, yes."

She stared at me. Then she laughed — really laughed, doubled-over, helpless, the full ridiculous sound of it, and the garden chimed along, and the tomatoes leaned in, and I thought: *this*. This was the sentence the candles had interrupted. Not the question. This. The part after.

* * *

We walked into the village together that afternoon, and I want it noted that we didn't plan the announcement. It planned itself. Marguerite couldn't stop looking at her hand. Every few steps she'd flex her fingers and the light would bend and she'd get this expression like she'd caught herself smiling in a mirror, and by the time we reached Okafor's porch the whole street already knew, because of course it did — the valley could feel joy now the way it felt storms, and half the town had presumably been experiencing a low-pressure system of happiness since noon.

Mrs. Okafor hugged us both and cried and claimed credit, which was fair, since she'd whooped first. Bramble appeared from his cottage with a bottle of plum wine older than the founders' council and announced, "About time. I had money on spring," and when I asked him who he'd bet against he said, "The book," completely deadpan, and I decided not to unpack that. Hana the smith examined the ring for a long time without touching it and finally said, "You asked the sky nicely and it helped you," and shook her head slowly, and said to no one in particular, "Fifteen years we lived next to a storm-caller and it's the *cook* who figures out how to talk to weather."

By dusk there were lanterns in the lanes, which nobody had organized, because the valley had developed a habit of collective celebration that operated entirely below the level of planning. Someone hauled out fiddles. Children — the first children who could feel the veil, born after the thaw or awakened by it — chased each other through the square making sparks jump between their palms, and their parents watched with that specific expression of terror and pride the valley was going to have to get used to.

Marguerite stood at the edge of it all with her hand laced through mine, the cooled lightning glinting green-grey on her finger, and I felt her go quiet beside me.

"What is it?" I asked.

She was looking past the lanterns, up, toward the ridge where the veil thinned. High above the far mountains, where the storm realms began, lightning played — soft now, distant, less a weapon than a conversation. Since the vow, she could feel it. I could feel it too, a little, the way you feel music through a floor.

"My mother's territory," she said. "That's the outer reaches. Where they sent her." A pause. "There was a long storm out there three nights ago. All night. I lay awake feeling it." Her thumb moved over the ring. "Last night it stopped. And today it's just... weather. Ordinary weather." She frowned. "I don't know what that means."

"Maybe it means she's done raging."

"Seraphine doesn't run out of rage, Theo. She repurposes it."

Down the lane, a lantern flared green-white — storm-colored, valley-wrong — and a figure of living mist detached itself from the shadows at the boundary road and came walking up the lane unhurried, unhindered, for the first time in the valley's history, because the veil let it now. The Warden. Villagers stepped aside with nods, almost casual; the mist-keeper had become a fixture, a neighbor, a strange tall piece of local geography.

The Warden stopped before us. Its voice was wind through a keyhole, and it was holding something: a letter, sealed with a crest I recognized — a spiral of seven storms. Seraphine's crest.

"For the bride-to-be," the Warden said. "From the outer reaches." The mist inclined its head, and I could have sworn there was something in it like sympathy. "She writes that the storms there have quieted. She writes that she has been mending what she broke." A pause, long enough that the lantern nearest us guttered. "She writes that she has read your letter, daughter-of-the-vow. Three times. And she wishes to know—" the Warden's voice did something complicated, softened at the edges, "—whether the invitation stands. Whether she may attend a wedding."

Marguerite's hand tightened in mine. I felt her heart through her palm, fast as hail.

"She asks *permission*," she said quietly. "My mother. Asks permission."

The Warden waited. The whole lane seemed to wait. Somewhere a fiddle stumbled and stopped.

Marguerite looked at the letter for a long moment. Then at the ring on her finger, cooled lightning that had survived because it found something worth grounding through. Then at me.

"Theo," she said, "help me write an answer."
