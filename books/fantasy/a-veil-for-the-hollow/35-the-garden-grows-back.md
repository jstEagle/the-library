## 35. The Garden Grows Back

The first seed went into the ground on a morning that smelled like possibility.

Marguerite knelt in the turned earth of the garden — *their* garden, the one she had leveled with her own uncontrolled storm two winters past and rebuilt by hand, row by row, the way you rebuild anything worth having. The soil was dark and loose and smelled of rain that had fallen warm for the first time in a hundred years. She pressed her thumb into it and made a small hollow, and dropped in a single moonpea seed, and covered it like tucking in a child.

Behind her, a bell chimed.

Not the temple bell. Not Theo's workshop bell, the little brass thing he rang when dinner was ready or when he'd set something on fire, which were occasionally the same occasion. This was the garden bell — an old cowbell they'd hung on the fence post the spring after the covenant was remade, because Tam Weller, aged nine, had discovered that he could hear weather coming three days off if he stood very still near iron, and had demanded a way to announce it.

"Storm's thinking about it!" Tam shouted from the gate, breathless, mud to his knees already at half past seven in the morning. "Over the ridge! It's not decided yet!"

"Not decided," Marguerite repeated, brushing dirt from her palms. "What did we say about storms?"

"They're not things." He recited it the way children recite anything — rote, impatient, but underneath it, the words had taken root. "They're *moods*. You don't fight a mood. You listen to it."

"And what is this storm feeling?"

Tam squinted at the ridge, where clouds were gathering with the slow, undecided drift of someone working up the courage to say something difficult. "Lonely," he said finally. "And a bit show-offy."

Marguerite laughed before she could stop herself. "Show-offy. Where do you get these words?"

"My mum says them about my dad."

"Well, your mum is very wise." She waved him through the gate. "Go on, then. Wash your hands. We start with the listening stones today."

He ran for the house, whooping, and Marguerite stayed kneeling in the garden a moment longer, letting the morning settle over her like a shawl.

Two years since the vow at the veil. Two years since she had given away half of what she was and received back, somehow, more than she'd lost. Her storms came to her now the way seasons came — gently, on their own schedule, asking nothing. She could feel rain gathering three valleys away as a faint pressure behind her eyes, like remembering a word she couldn't quite place. Lightning no longer answered her call; it simply visited sometimes, far off over the mountains, and she felt it pass through her like music heard through a wall.

She had grieved the old power. She would not pretend otherwise. There were nights, especially that first year, when she woke reaching for thunder the way an amputee reaches for a missing limb, and found only quiet, and wept into Theo's shoulder until dawn.

But grief, she had learned, was just love with nowhere to put itself. And there were so many places now to put it.

* * *

By nine o'clock there were six of them in the garden: Tam Weller, the twins Ilsa and Petra Halloway, small fierce Odette Bramble — great-granddaughter of Old Man Bramble, who had dissolved the founders' council with the same flat finality he'd once defended it, and who now spent his days carving toys and refusing to discuss arithmetic — plus shy little Fen Okafor, who didn't speak much but whose plants grew a full week ahead of everyone else's, and last, Juniper, the wardens' granddaughter, seven years old and certain she already knew everything, which was exhausting and also, Marguerite suspected, mostly true.

They sat in a circle on the flat stones by the herb beds. In the center of the circle, Marguerite placed a basket of river stones — smooth, gray, unremarkable, except that each one had been left overnight in the thin place at the valley boundary, where the veil between worlds lay close enough to touch.

"Now," she said. "Who can tell me what magic feels like?"

"Rain," said Fen, surprising everyone, including himself. He went red immediately and stared at his boots.

"Yes," Marguerite said softly. "Very good. Rain. Go on, Fen. What about rain?"

"It's — " He struggled. "It's patient. Rain waits. It doesn't hurry even when you want it to."

Ilsa rolled her eyes. "Magic isn't rain. Magic is *lightning*. My gran saw lightning come down the mountain during the Breaking Winter and it looked like a tree made of fire—"

"That was a battle," Marguerite said. "Battles are loud. But battles aren't most of what magic is. Most of magic is quiet." She picked up one of the stones and held it out on her open palm. "This stone sat at the boundary all night. The veil brushed against it. Can anyone hear it?"

Six children leaned in. Six faces scrunched with effort. Juniper grabbed hers and held it to her ear like a seashell.

"I hear my own blood," she announced. "That's cheating."

"It's not cheating. Your blood is part of the world too." Marguerite waited. Let the silence stretch. This was the hardest lesson, the one she'd had to relearn herself after the vow: that listening wasn't doing nothing. That stillness was a kind of attention, and attention was a kind of love, and love was — well. Love was the oldest covenant there was.

Petra heard it first. Her whole face changed, the way faces did — the way Marguerite's own face had changed, once, in a garden very like this one, when her grandmother first pressed a storm-caller's hand over her heart and said, *there, do you feel it, the sky breathing?*

"It hums," Petra whispered. "Like right before thunder. But tiny. A tiny forever-thunder."

"A tiny forever-thunder," Marguerite repeated. "I've never heard it described better, and I studied under the High Storm-Caller of the realm above, so that's saying something."

The children beamed. Even Fen smiled at his boots.

She handed out the stones, one each, with instructions: keep them under your pillow, bring them back in a month, tell me what they've learned. Because the stones learned — everything at the boundary learned; the veil was a teacher if you let it be — and because a month from now they'd have new words for what they heard, and the month after that, better ones.

This was how it worked now. Not prophecy books writing your death in fresh ink. Not covenants sealed in blood and half-lives. Just children, and stones, and patience, and a woman who had once renounced all of it teaching it back like handing out bread.

Her inheritance had been a burden. She intended to leave them a gift.

* * *

Theo found her at noon, sitting against the garden fence with her sleeves rolled up and dirt permanently embedded in the creases of her knuckles — it never washed fully out anymore, and she'd stopped trying.

"Lunch," he said, dropping down beside her and producing, from somewhere, two apples and a heel of brown bread. He split everything with the seriousness of a man performing surgery. "Your students have been telling the whole village that magic is a mood. Mrs. Weller came by asking if her son is going to grow up to be a wizard."

"He might," Marguerite said. "Tam hears weather three days out. That's real."

"He also told Mrs. Okafor that our compost heap is 'brooding,' and she believed him, and now she's worried about it."

"The compost heap *is* brooding. It needs turning. Tell her it'll feel better Thursday."

Theo laughed — that laugh, the one that had survived a proposal over burnt stew, a prophecy written in his name, a crossing into a country of living lightning, and the loss of exactly half of his mortal permanence. He was thirty-four now and aging a little faster than men should; they both knew the arithmetic; neither of them said it aloud except on the nights it needed saying. He could feel the valley's moods now, the way she could feel the sky's. Some mornings he stood in the doorway with his eyes closed and reported, gravely, that the east field was *anxious*, and she would walk out and find moles, every time.

"How were the stones?" he asked.

"One girl heard it. Petra. First one."

"First one of the valley-born."

"First one ever, maybe, to learn it this way. Quietly. As a gift." Marguerite turned her apple in her hands. "My grandmother taught me with terror, Theo. *The sky owes us, and the debt is coming due.* That was the first thing she ever said about magic. I was five. I thought the sky was going to collect me personally."

Theo was quiet for a moment. He had gotten good at knowing which of her memories needed company and which needed space. "And what will these children think the sky is?"

She watched the clouds over the ridge — the lonely, show-offy storm that had finally decided something, and was now rolling down toward the valley with the unhurried confidence of a guest who knew it was welcome.

"They'll think it's a neighbor," she said. "One who drops by when it rains."

* * *

The storm arrived at dusk, and it was perfect.

Not a battle-storm, not a wall of black fury like the ones Seraphine rode down from the high country. Just a good honest summer rain, fat and warm, hammering the garden leaves into a green shimmer, filling the rain barrels, washing the dust of the road into rivers along the fence line. And threaded through it — faintly, unmistakably, the way a voice threads through a hymn — the veil itself, moving with the weather, breathing with it.

Marguerite stood in the doorway and listened.

Once, magic in the Hollow Valley had been impossible — locked out by the terms of her exile, dead as stone, a silence so complete she'd stopped hearing its absence, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Now it moved through the valley like weather indeed: seasonal, subtle, survivable. It pooled in the low fields where the veil ran close. It made the temple bells ring a half-second before anyone pulled the rope. It gave Fen Okafor's beans their unfair head start and made the old well water taste, some days, faintly of lightning.

It asked almost nothing. It cost almost nothing. Two years in, and she still sometimes caught herself waiting for the bill.

"You're doing it again," Theo said from behind her.

"Doing what?"

"Bracing. Like the sky's going to send an invoice."

She huffed. "You can't feel that."

"I can feel *you*. Better than weather." He came to stand beside her, shoulder against hers, watching the rain. In the distance, over the mountains where the storm realm lived behind its thinned-but-whole veil, lightning played — soft, distant, unhurried. She felt it like music through a wall. He felt it too now; she could tell by the way his head tilted. They had each lost half of what they were, and between them they'd assembled something that could hear the whole song.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. It was an old question between them, worn smooth like the listening stones. They still asked it, because the answer kept being true. "Being entirely mortal. Not hearing any of it."

Theo considered. He always considered. It was one of the ten thousand reasons.

"I miss not knowing when the east field is anxious," he said. "Everything else, no." He glanced down at her. "Do you miss the old storms? The big ones? Being able to crack the sky?"

Marguerite watched the rain fall warm on the garden she'd rebuilt twice, on the bell on the fence post, on the whole green breathing valley where magic and mortality finally shared one sky.

"I don't miss being a weapon," she said. "I miss being afraid I'd have to be one." She leaned into him. "This is better. Small storms. Neighboring ones."

* * *

The letter came three days later, as letters from her mother always did: delivered by the Warden, who materialized at the boundary in its slow drift of living mist and held the envelope out at arm's length like a man offering a live coal.

The Warden never spoke anymore unless spoken to — the covenant required two, and the covenant had been satisfied, and whatever else the Warden was, it was punctilious. It accepted Marguerite's nod, deposited the letter in the boundary-stone box, and dispersed back into the haze at the valley's edge.

Seraphine's letters were terse. That was the established pattern. Two years of them: reports from the outer reaches of the storm realm, where she mended the far fences of the world alone, as her penance required and, Marguerite suspected, as she privately preferred. *The eastern squalls are holding. The archivist-storm sends regards. The coral cities are growing again beyond the third thunderhead.* Never *I miss you.* Never *I'm sorry.* Once, memorably, after the twins' birth announcement had gone across the veil via the Warden: *Twins are a storm pattern I know nothing about. Advise.*

But this letter was different. Marguerite knew it before she broke the seal, from the weight of it, from the way the paper hummed faintly against her fingers — a tiny forever-thunder, as Petra would say.

She carried it inside, set it on the table, and stood looking at it for a long moment while the kettle heated and the evening came down blue over the garden.

Then she opened it.

Three lines only, in her mother's sharp, beautiful hand:

*The outer mending is done. The far fences hold without me. The court has released me from exile a year early — they say mercy is contagious.*

*I am coming to the valley in the spring. Not to stay. To see the garden.*

*Tell me what to plant.*

Marguerite read it four times. Then she set it down, walked to the window, and looked out at the darkening rows of their garden — the moonpeas up, the herbs gone wild and glad, the listening stones waiting under six children's pillows all across the Hollow Valley.

Spring was eight months away. Eight months to decide what a mother owed a daughter, and what a daughter could bear to give.

She reached for a pen.
