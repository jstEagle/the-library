## 36. Letters Across the Veil

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a knot of mist that condensed out of the morning air above our kitchen table like a held breath finally released.

Marguerite was elbow-deep in bread dough. I was nursing a cup of coffee and pretending to read the valley's one newspaper, which that week contained a spirited dispute about whether the new magic-sensitive well water made better tea or worse. The Warden didn't knock — the Warden never knocked, being mostly fog with opinions — it simply gathered at the window, pressed itself flat against the glass like a child showing its face through a car window, and waited.

Marguerite saw it first. Her hands stilled in the flour. "Theo."

"I see it."

"It's early. The Warden doesn't come until the equinox deliveries."

"Maybe the outer reaches are closer than we thought."

She dried her hands with a care that fooled neither of us. When she opened the window, the mist poured itself politely onto the sill and sat there, condensing, thickening, until a small grey envelope formed at its center like a pearl around an irritant.

No name on it. No name needed.

Marguerite looked at it the way you'd look at a snake you weren't sure was sleeping.

"You don't have to," I said.

"I know." She picked it up anyway. Turned it over once. Set it down again without opening it, went back to her dough, and punched it harder than bread requires.

I drank my coffee. Outside, the Warden waited on the sill, patient as weather.

It took her until noon to open it. She read it standing at the counter, and her face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, which is how I've learned to recognize her worst days. She handed it to me without a word and walked out to the garden, where she proceeded to weed a bed that didn't need weeding for the better part of two hours.

The letter said:

*Daughter.*

*I have taken stock of the outer reaches. There is a storm there older than the covenant, unclaimed, that has been circling the same mountain for nine hundred years because no one ever taught it where to go. I am teaching it. It is slow work. It is good work. I am told penance should be slow, or it isn't penance.*

*I do not ask forgiveness. I write so that you will know I am alive, and so that you will know why.*

*Your mother,*

*S*

That was all. Four sentences and a signature that wasn't even a full name. Marguerite stood in the garden pulling weeds out of dry ground for hours, and when she came in she said only, "Well. She's alive." And then, after a silence: "Nine hundred years, circling. Nobody taught it where to go." Her voice cracked on the last word in a way she pretended it hadn't.

She didn't write back. Not that week, not that month. But she kept the letter in the drawer with her grandmother's prophecy book — the book whose last pages now held nothing but an open question mark and one line of her own ink beneath it — and I noticed she checked on it sometimes, the way you check on a fire you're not sure you want burning.

* * *

The second letter came with the autumn rains, and this one she read aloud, because reading it aloud made it smaller somehow, more manageable, a thing in the room instead of a thing in her chest.

*Daughter.*

*The old storm has learned to turn east. It took four months. When it first changed course it rained on a village that had never seen rain, and the people came out of their houses and stood in it with their arms out. I watched from above. I thought of you standing in the valley's first spring rain, if the archivists' records are true, and I found I could not tell whether what I felt was grief or something adjacent to it. I am still learning the difference.*

*Seraphine*

"Something adjacent to it," Marguerite repeated. She laughed — a short, startled sound. "She's doing vocabulary exercises about her own feelings. In a letter. To me."

"She's trying."

"She's *cataloguing.*" But she said it without heat, and later I found her sitting at the table with paper in front of her, a pen uncapped, writing nothing. Three nights in a row. On the fourth night she wrote three lines and burned them in the stove. On the fifth night she wrote half a page and burned it. On the sixth night —

On the sixth night she wrote a letter, sealed it, and set it on the windowsill before she could change her mind, and by morning the Warden had taken it, leaving behind nothing but a faint smell of ozone and a damp ring on the wood.

I never asked what she wrote. Some doors you let people open themselves.

* * *

Winter came gently that year, the way it does now. The veil runs soft through the valley these seasons — magic moves here like groundwater, invisible but everywhere, feeding things from below. Our garden comes up a week early. The frost ferns on our window make shapes that are almost, but not quite, letters. Old Man Bramble's successor on the dissolved council, a woman named Petra Osei who used to be the schoolteacher, says the children born since the covenant reforged hum when they're happy, low and tuneless, like jars holding bees. Nobody minds. It's the sound of a door left open.

The third letter arrived midwinter, and it was longer.

*Daughter.*

*The outer reaches are not empty as the court believes. They are full of storms that were exiled the way you were exiled — cast out for being too large, too wild, too much. I have found forty-one of them so far. They remember your grandmother's grandmother. One of them remembers you, from before. It asks after you each time I pass. I do not know what to tell it.*

*I have begun to understand that exile was never a punishment the realm invented. It was a kindness the realm invented and then used as a weapon. I gave you the weapon version. I tell myself I did it to save you. Some mornings I believe that. Most mornings I do not.*

*The court has asked me to return. My term is nearly served, they say, as though service were a sentence with walls instead of a direction. I have asked for another year. I find I am not finished teaching the old storm where to go.*

*I remain,*

*Your mother*

Marguerite read it twice. Then she sat down across from me at the table and said, "She asked for another year."

"Yes."

"She could come home. The realm would take her back. And she asked for *more* exile."

"Maybe it stopped being exile somewhere along the way."

"That's not fair, Theo." But her eyes were shining, and she pressed the heel of her hand against them, angry at them. "You can't just — decide suffering is a pilgrimage halfway through. That's cheating. That's cheating *her* way. Everything has to be architecture with her. Even remorse has to be load-bearing."

"Maybe that's just the only language she has."

"Then she should learn a new language." She stood up. Sat down. Stood up again and paced to the window, where the winter light lay thin on the sill. "Forty-one storms. Cast out for being too much." Her voice dropped. "Like me."

"Nothing like you," I said. "You came home."

She turned and looked at me then, and whatever passed between us was private, and I'll keep it that way.

But that night she wrote for a long time. And this time, when the letter went on the sill, she let me watch her do it, and she didn't burn anything first.

* * *

Spring brought the fourth letter, and with it, the first crack in the wall that neither of them would call a wall.

Marguerite read it at the table, and halfway down she made a sound I'd never heard from her — half laugh, half wound.

"What?" I said.

"She sent me a recipe."

"A recipe."

"For storm-calling. A beginner's exercise. 'For gentling the breath,' it says." Marguerite held the page at arm's length like it might bite. "My mother, High Storm-Caller, breaker of daughters, architect of covenants, has sent me a *beginner's exercise*, with annotations. In the margins. In her handwriting, she's written '*you will be tempted to force it; do not*' — Theo, she's written me *study notes.*"

I looked at my wife, who had spent fifteen years unable to call so much as a breeze, who since the vow could raise only whispers of weather, who had grieved that whisper-quietness every single day and told no one but me — and I watched her read her mother's study notes for the third time, and I watched something in her shoulders come loose.

"She thinks my storms are weak," Marguerite said slowly.

"She thinks your storms are *young,*" I said. "There's a difference."

And that — that was the crack. Because Marguerite had been carrying the vow's cost like a wound, half a self traded away, whispers where thunder used to live. And here was Seraphine, in her terrible sideways fashion, saying the quiet part in a margin note: *the gentled voice is still a voice. Teach it patience. It will grow into its own register, and it will be yours alone, no one else's.*

Marguerite wrote back that same evening. I caught a glimpse over her shoulder — just a line or two, just enough:

*The storm in the garden answered today. It is small. It is mine. Tell the one that remembers me that I remember it too, and that I am learning where to go.*

* * *

They are not reconciled. I want to be honest about that, because the valley has a habit lately of turning everything into a story with a bow on it, and I refuse.

What they are is *in correspondence.* Two women separated by a veil and thirty years of damage, trading letters through a courier made of fog, circling the same wound from opposite sides the way the old storm circles its mountain. Marguerite reads each letter three times — once fast, once slow, once aloud to me in a flat voice that fools nobody. Then she takes a day. Sometimes two. Then she writes back, and the letters she writes are getting longer, and angrier in places, and funnier in places, and once — only once — she signed one "*your daughter*" instead of just her name, and then stood at the window for an hour afterward looking like a woman who had jumped a gorge and couldn't believe the far side held.

Seraphine's letters are changing too. The margins fill up. The sentences stop performing. Last month, buried in a paragraph about wind patterns over the outer reaches, there was a single line: *I was wrong about your Theo. I am not ready to say more than that yet.* Marguerite read it, went very still, and said, "That's the most honest thing she's ever written," and then she cried into the bread dough, which ruined two loaves and was worth it.

Forgiveness, I've learned, is not a door that opens. It's a road that gets built — badly, in bad weather, by two people who argue about the route the whole time. They're building it. A mile at a time. Neither of them will admit they're building it.

But the channel stays open. Every letter goes out; every letter comes back. The Warden ferries them with the punctuality of tide, and lately, I swear, it lingers on the sill afterward, as though it likes being needed, as though mist can be fond of anything.

* * *

Last night the fifth letter of the year came, and it was different.

Shorter. Strained at the edges, the handwriting less certain, as though written in a hurry or a high wind. The news was strange: Seraphine has found a forty-second storm in the outer reaches — but this one isn't exiled. This one is *new.* Born after the covenant reforged. Born of the fused vow, she writes, or something like it — a storm with two natures braided through it, mortal weather and immortal weather in one body, the first of its kind.

And it is flying toward the valley, she writes. Toward us. It has been flying toward the valley since the day it was born, and she cannot turn it, and she is not sure she wants to, and she does not know what it wants when it arrives.

At the bottom, beneath her name, one additional line, the ink smudged as if written running:

*Daughter — I think it may be yours.*

Marguerite read it standing at the window, the last light going out of the sky, and far off past the ridge — impossibly, unmistakably — something flickered. Not lightning. Softer than lightning. More like a pulse. Like a heartbeat made of weather, coming home.

She reached for her coat.
