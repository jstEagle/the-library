## 37. The Prophecy Book Rewrites Itself

The book had been sitting on the shelf for three weeks, and I had been pretending it wasn't.

That was a skill I'd perfected over fifteen years of exile — the art of not looking at things. Not looking at the boundary mist when it thickened. Not looking at the scar on my palm where the storm had entered and left. Not looking at my own reflection in the weeks after the Vow at the Veil, when my eyes still crackled faintly at the edges, like a sky deciding whether to break.

But the book deserved better than my avoidance. It had earned, at the very least, my attention. It had spent a century writing my life down before I lived it, and then — after everything — it had gone quiet. I owed it the courtesy of finding out why.

So on a gray morning in early spring, with the smell of Theo's bread rising through the floorboards and the valley outside my window wearing its first honest green in a hundred years, I took my grandmother's prophecy book down from the shelf and carried it to the table.

It was lighter than I remembered. Or maybe I was heavier — filled up with things I hadn't been when I'd first pulled it down in a panic, the night of our anniversary, the night the world cracked open. The leather cover was still scarred where the fire had refused to take. The seal my grandmother had pressed into the spine had softened, gone waxy, like it had finally finished whatever it had been holding shut.

I set it on the table. I made tea first. I was not going to be dramatic about this. I had done dramatic. Dramatic had leveled our garden and nearly killed me and gotten my mother exiled to the outer reaches of a storm realm. I was going to open a book and drink tea like a person who had survived her own prophecy, which, as it turned out, was the rarest kind of person there was.

The pages fell open to the last entry I'd read — the one that had named Theo *the bridge that must burn*, the one I'd tried to feed to the hearth while it drank the flames like wine. The ink there had gone brown and settled, old now, finished. A record. Not a threat. I touched the words and felt nothing but paper.

I turned past it.

The next page was blank.

So was the next. And the next. A whole run of emptiness, the way the book had been for decades before it woke up and started writing my life. I flipped faster, and the pages whispered past my thumb like wind through wheat, and I was almost — almost — disappointed. The book had said its piece. It had diagnosed a dying covenant, demanded a bridge, watched us refuse the terms and invent new ones, and then closed its mouth. Books were allowed to do that. People were too.

Then I reached the final gathering of pages, and stopped.

There was ink on them. Fresh ink. I knew the difference the way a gardener knows the difference between a bud and a bruise — something in the color, something in the give. This ink was still wet at its edges, still settling into the fiber, still *arriving*.

I sat down. I drank my tea. I read.

*When the two halves are made whole, the winter breaks, and the veil does not.*

It was written across the top of the page in a hand I recognized — my grandmother's hand, the same one that had written the doom, except this line curved differently. Looser. Almost amused. Beneath it, in smaller script, the page continued:

*What the covenant was, it no longer is. What it becomes, no book can say. The veil is thin now, and thin things let light through in both directions. Somewhere in the valley there is a child who hears the rain thinking. Somewhere in the storms there is a young one who dreams of stillness. The next bridge will not be chosen. The next bridge will choose.*

And then, at the bottom of the page, where a prophecy should have sealed itself with a date or a name or a demand — there was nothing.

A question mark.

Not even a proper one. A small, curling, almost shy mark, as if whoever — whatever — had written this far had looked at the future, found it genuinely unreadable, and had the grace to admit it.

I laughed. It came out of me like a sneeze, sudden and undignified, and I pressed my hand over my mouth because Theo was upstairs and I didn't want to explain yet. But the laugh wouldn't stop. Because I had spent a year of my life inside that book's certainty. I had watched it name my lover, date my dinners, count down my winter. I had burned it and buried it and argued with it in the dark, and through all of it I had believed — the way you believe about storms, the way you believe about mothers — that it knew something I didn't. That it was *ahead* of me. That the future was a place already built, and I was only walking through its rooms.

And now here it was, the great and terrible book, shrugging at me in wet ink.

*The next bridge will choose.*

The book didn't know. It had never known. It had only ever been very, very good at reading what was already in motion — the failing covenant, the gathering cold, the shape of the trap my mother had set and the shape of the way out that my mother hadn't planned for. Prophecy wasn't fate. It was weather forecasting. It could tell you a storm was coming. It could not tell you what you'd do when it arrived.

I had done something it hadn't predicted. We both had. Two halves, made whole, and the covenant had held — not because a bridge burned, but because two people refused to let the arithmetic stand.

I turned the last page over, looking for more. There was none. The book ended in a question mark, and beyond it, the inside back cover, and beyond that, my kitchen, and the bread smell, and Theo's footsteps coming down the stairs.

"You're up early," he said, and then he saw my face and set the bread down without a word about the bread, which was how I knew he was still, after everything, the best man in either world. "The book?"

"The book," I said. "Come look at this."

* * *

He read it twice. He read the way he did everything — slowly, with his whole body, his brow furrowing at the parts that confused him and his mouth twitching at the parts that didn't. When he reached the question mark, he tapped it with one finger.

"So it's giving up?"

"It's not giving up." I took the book back. "It's being honest. There's a difference."

"There usually is with you." He pulled out the chair across from mine and sat, and the morning light came through the window and caught the faint silver at his temples — new, since the Vow. He aged a little faster than men should now. He never mentioned it. I never stopped noticing. "A door left open. That's what this is, isn't it? For whoever comes next."

"That's what it says."

"And you believe it?"

I looked at the page. The ink had dried while we talked. Somewhere in the valley there was a child who heard the rain thinking — I'd met her, actually, old Bramble's great-granddaughter, a solemn girl of seven who had told me last week that the spring rain "tastes like someone talking." I'd laughed and said children say such things. I hadn't slept well after.

"I believe," I said, "that the veil is thin now, and thin things change. And I believe that when the next storm comes — and one will, Theo, the realms don't stay balanced out of politeness — someone is going to stand where we stood. And they're going to open a book like this one, and it's going to tell them what the last people did." I turned the book around so the question mark faced him. "And I think it matters enormously what that page says."

Theo was quiet for a moment. He was good at quiet. It was one of the first things I'd loved about him, back when I was a woman made of held breath — that he could sit in silence with me like the silence was a room we both lived in, not a hole he needed to fill.

"So what do you want it to say?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question. Because I had spent my whole life being *written* — by my grandmother's covenants, by my mother's ambitions, by the prophecy's cold arithmetic, by exile's terms. I had been a character in other people's sentences for as long as I could remember. Even the Vow, the heresy, the thing we'd built together — I had thought of it as defiance. As refusal. As *no* carved into the world's *yes*.

But refusal was still a response. It still let the original author hold the pen.

"I want it to say," I heard myself answer, "that the people who came before were wrong about some things and right about others, and that the ones who come next should take what's useful and throw away the rest. I want it to say that sacrifice is a choice, not a price. I want it to say—" I stopped. My throat had gone tight, which was absurd, because nothing was happening, no storm, no magic, just tea and bread and morning light. "I want it to say that survival was never meant to be solitary. That's the whole of it. That's the thing my mother never understood and Bramble never understood and the book itself never understood, until we taught it."

Theo reached across the table and turned the book back toward me. Toward the blank space beneath the question mark.

"Then say it," he said.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the only inheritance worth leaving.

I did not write anything. Not at first. I sat with a pen in my hand — an ordinary pen, iron-gall ink, the kind you buy in the village, the kind that has never once predicted anything — and my hand shook, and I put it down, and I got up and checked on bread that didn't need checking, and I came back, and I sat down again.

Because writing in that book felt like a transgression. It had been my grandmother's. Then it had been my mother's weapon. Then it had been my jailer. Every hand that had touched it had used it to *determine* something — to bind, to trap, to forecast, to demand. The book and authority had been the same thing for a hundred years. To write in it casually felt like signing someone else's name.

But that was exactly the problem, wasn't it. That was the inheritance I'd been handed: a world where the book spoke and people obeyed. Where prophecy was a cage with beautiful bars. Where the future was a country you could only enter as a prisoner.

My mother was out at the edge of the storm realm right now, alone, mending the outer reaches, writing me letters that had grown from three cold lines to nearly a page. Last month she'd asked about the garden. Last week she'd asked about *him*. She was learning, out there in her penance, the thing I'd learned in my own exile: that solitude teaches you exactly one lesson, and it's the wrong one, and you spend the rest of your life unlearning it. I hadn't forgiven her. I might never. But I understood her now, and understanding is a door forgiveness can walk through when it's ready, and not before.

She had written the prophecy as a trap. And the trap had failed, and the failure had become a covenant, and the covenant had become a question mark, and the question mark was sitting in front of me waiting to find out what kind of world it opened into.

I picked up the pen.

Theo stayed. He didn't hover and he didn't leave — he sat across from me and buttered bread and ate it like a man watching weather, which, these days, he literally was. He could feel the valley's moods now, hear the rain thinking right along with Bramble's great-granddaughter. He said the valley felt "attentive" this morning. I believed him. The whole valley had been attentive since the Vow — the veil thin enough now that the two worlds leaned toward each other like listeners.

I wrote beneath the question mark, in my own plain hand, in ink that predicted nothing:

*To whoever reads this: the last bridge did not burn, and the world did not end. It changed. Change is what the world does instead of ending. We gave half of what we were so that no one after us would be asked to give all of what they are. This was not a prophecy. It was a decision. Make yours the same way — with the people you love, in the open, where the cost can be shared. The future is not written. It is written and rewritten and written again, by everyone, forever. That is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.*

*The door is open. We held it open for you.*

*— Marguerite Ishikawa, storm-caller, exile, half of a whole. Valley of the Hollow, in the first spring of the new covenant.*

My hand was steady the whole way through. I only noticed afterward that the ink had dried the moment each word was written — instantly, completely, as if the book had been waiting for exactly this and had accepted it the moment it arrived. Not overwritten. Not corrected. Not one letter changed.

The book, for the first time in a hundred years, was not the author. It was the page.

I closed it. The seal on the spine had dissolved entirely now, I saw — nothing left to hold shut, nothing left to hold *in*. I didn't put it back on the shelf, high up, face-out, where it had watched us like an eye. I set it on the low table by the window, where the light came in, where anyone could pick it up.

"Done?" Theo asked.

"Done."

"Good." He stood, and held out his hand, and I took it, and outside the window the valley went on being green and alive and faintly, wonderfully strange — a place where magic moved like weather now, subtle and seasonal and survivable, where children heard rain thinking and old men were learning to apologize to the future. "Because the Warden came by while you were writing."

I went still. "The Warden doesn't come by. The Warden stands at the boundary and judges."

"Not today." Theo's mouth did the thing it did when he was trying not to smile and losing. "Today it stood at the gate, made of mist, holding a letter sealed in storm-glass, and it said — and I quote, because I've been saving this — '*The court of falling weather sends its envoys for the wedding. They request the ceremony be held under open sky. They wish to bring rain.*'"

I stared at him.

"They want it to *rain*," he said, "on our wedding. On purpose. Softly. Apparently it's a great honor, and also apparently the storm-court has very strong opinions about our flower arrangements, because there's a second page."

I laughed — the real one this time, the one that came up from somewhere the winter had frozen and the spring had thawed — and I took the letter from him, and the storm-glass seal was cool under my thumb, and somewhere far away my mother was mending the sky, and somewhere closer a child was listening to the rain, and the book sat by the window with its door held open.

We were going to need a bigger garden.
