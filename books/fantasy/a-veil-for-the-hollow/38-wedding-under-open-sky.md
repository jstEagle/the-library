## 38. Wedding Under Open Sky

### I. Old Man Bramble

Bramble had attended every wedding in the Hollow Valley for sixty years, and he had never once seen the sky cry on purpose.

Rain fell over the meadow below the ridge — a soft, deliberate rain, each drop landing like it had been invited. It fell on the rows of chairs, on the flower garlands the Aldern girls had strung between two fence posts because nobody owned an arch, on the bald head of Bramble himself, and it did not fall anywhere it was not wanted. He watched a droplet curve around the hem of Tam Voss's jacket rather than through it. Watched another land squarely in the cupped palm of a storm-folk envoy, who drank it like wine.

The founders' council was dissolved. The ledger of sacrifices was burned — Bramble himself had fed it to the fire, one hundred years of arithmetic gone up in smoke that smelled, he thought, like it had been waiting a long time to leave. And here he sat in the second row, an old man in his good coat, invited like anyone else.

He had expected to feel lighter. What he felt instead was the specific weight of a man who has put down a heavy thing and discovers his hands still remember holding it.

The storm-folk had come down from the boundary at noon: seven envoys in coats the color of thunderheads, their hair full of static, walking carefully as though the grass were glass. They had never stood in the valley before. Not once in a century. Bramble watched one of them crouch and press a finger into the mud of the lane and hold it there, eyes closed, like a woman reading a letter written long ago.

"They're nervous," said Marta Aldern, settling beside him with her baby on her hip.

"So is the bridegroom."

"No." She nodded toward the boundary, where the mist stood along the ridgeline like a wall that had learned patience. "I mean them. A hundred years of stories about us, and us about them. Everyone comes to a wedding hoping the food is edible. They're coming hoping the people are."

The music started then — fiddles, three of them, and beneath the fiddles something else, a low harmonic hum that came from the throats of the envoys, a sound like the valley itself clearing its throat. The crowd rose. Bramble rose with them, knees cracking, and turned to watch Marguerite Ishikawa come down the aisle between the fence posts with rain parting around her shoulders like a rumor.

She was not dressed as a bride, exactly. She wore gray-blue, storm-colored, and her mother's ring hung on a chain at her throat, and she walked like a woman who had been to the far side of the sky and come back knowing exactly what a wedding was worth. Beside her, to everyone's astonishment including, apparently, her own, walked Seraphine's absence made flesh — no. Bramble corrected himself. Not absence. The Warden.

The Warden of the covenant had left the boundary for the first time since its making, a figure of living mist shaped roughly like a tall woman, drifting down the aisle at Marguerite's side as matron of honor, if you could call it walking when the grass bent under you without being touched. The villagers nearest the aisle leaned away, then, slowly, leaned back. Old habits died hard. But they stayed.

At the front, Theo Vance stood in a borrowed coat with a ring of cooled lightning-glass in his pocket, crying without any attempt to hide it, and Bramble thought: there it is. There is the thing we spent a hundred years trying to buy with other people's children. We just didn't know what it looked like when it was free.

### II. Tam Voss, cooper

Tam had built the barrels that hauled the wedding wine and he had also, eleven years ago, built the coffin of old Widow Marsh, so he figured he knew both halves of what a valley owed its people, and today was the better half by a wide road.

He stood at the back with his wife and he watched the storm-folk envoy next to him — the young one, the one whose name was something like a wind sound — try very seriously to eat a honey cake without offending anyone. The envoy held it in both hands. Studied it. Bit it the way you'd bite something that might bite back.

"It's good?" Tam asked.

"It is *still*," the envoy said, wonderingly. "Nothing moves in it. Nothing *wants* anything. Where I am from, every meal tastes like weather." The envoy took another bite, slower. "This tastes like someone decided."

Tam laughed, and the laugh startled a flock of sparrows out of the fence garland, and the envoy flinched at the sparrows, which was funny, and then the envoy laughed too, high and crackling, and half the back rows turned around to look, because none of them had ever heard a storm laugh before, and Tam understood in that moment that this — this exact stupid beautiful moment, honey cakes and sparrows — was the whole point of everything the winter had cost them.

The vows began. The rain gentled further, until it was less rain than a kind of bright hush.

Theo spoke first. His voice cracked twice and he did not care. "I had a whole speech," he said, "and Marguerite heard it already, last night, and told me it was too long. So: I promise to be ordinary at you for the rest of my life, as hard as I can. I promise to burn dinner. I promise to listen to the weather with you even after it stops being new." He cleared his throat. "And I promise that whatever half of me is left after all this, you get it. All of it. That's the whole estate. Sign here."

Marguerite laughed — the real laugh, the ugly snorting one she pretended she didn't have — and the sound carried over the meadow, and Tam saw more than one person wipe their eyes, and at least one of the storm envoys look around sharply as if the laugh itself were a phenomenon requiring classification.

Then Marguerite spoke, and the rain went absolutely silent. Every drop hung. Tam would swear to his dying day that every single drop of water above that meadow stopped where it was, a million small suspended suns, while she said:

"I renounced magic to be safe. I chose exile to be safe. I have learned — expensively, publicly, at considerable cost to the local architecture — that safety is not the same as survival, and neither of them is the same as *living*." Her voice shook once and steadied. "So I don't promise safety. I promise weather. Real weather. The kind that changes. I promise to stay through all of it, and I promise that whatever half of me is left — it's yours too. Half and half. Whole."

The Warden drifted forward between them. When it spoke, its voice was the sound the mist makes at dawn, and every person in that meadow felt it in the bones of their feet.

"The covenant requires two," the Warden said. "It has always required two. For a hundred years we forgot, and asked one soul to carry what two were meant to share. Let it be recorded in rain and remembered in cloud: these two stand witness for both realms. Let the veil hear them."

The rings went on. Lightning-glass for her; for him, a band of valley iron from the old foundry, melted down from — Tam knew because he'd helped carry it — the lock of the founders' council door.

When the kiss came, the held rain let go all at once, warm and brief and shining, and the crowd roared, and above the ridge, far off but not far enough off to miss, lightning answered — three slow flashes, deliberate, unmistakable, applause from the court of falling weather.

### III. The Warden

The Warden had stood at the boundary of the Hollow Valley for ninety-three years, and in all that time it had spoken perhaps four hundred sentences. It counted. There was not much else to do at a border.

Today it had spoken six sentences before noon and was experiencing something its makers had not designed it to experience, which was the question of whether it was enjoying itself.

It stood now at the edge of the dancing — the mortals danced in circles, hands joined, and three of the storm-folk had been pulled in despite themselves, moving stiffly, then less stiffly, sparks coming off their heels with every turn so that the grass glowed faintly behind them in the dusk. The Warden observed. The Warden was good at observing. It had ninety-three years of practice and nothing to show for it except a very complete record.

"You're smiling," said Marta Aldern, appearing beside it with a cup of cider it could not drink. "Or doing whatever your version is. Your edges are softer."

The Warden considered this. "The boundary I keep was built from refusal," it said. "One realm saying *no* to the other across a line of mist. Today both realms are inside the line. I am mostly decoration."

"Good decoration," Marta said, and clinked her cup against the Warden's mist, which was either nonsense or the most meaningful toast in the history of the valley, and moved off toward the music.

Later — much later, when the fiddlers had given out and the stars were thick overhead and Theo Vance had fallen asleep in a chair with his new wife's head on his shoulder and a piece of honey cake balanced on his chest like an offering — the Warden drifted up the ridge alone, back toward the boundary it kept.

Halfway up, it paused. Below, the valley lay quiet, lamplit, washed. Beyond, the storm realm rolled its slow silver weather across the mountains, and the distance between the two had thinned — measurably, permanently, by the terms of a vow spoken that afternoon. The veil was thinner than it had ever been. Thin enough, the Warden calculated, that things could pass through it gently now, in both directions, without tearing.

Thin enough, it calculated further, that a message could pass.

Something did. It arrived as most messages from the deep storm arrive — not as sound but as pressure, a change in the weight of the air, a sentence pressed into the mist the way a seal is pressed into wax. The Warden received it, decoded it, and stood very still on the dark ridge for a long moment.

Then it turned back down the hill, mist gathering itself into the shape of purpose, and began walking toward the sleeping house where the bride and groom lay tangled together under one blanket, dreaming separate halves of the same life.

There was a letter coming through the veil. The first of its kind. And it was addressed, in a hand the Warden recognized from a hundred years of sealed records, to *my daughter*.

* * *

The knock, when it came near midnight, was softer than rain.

Marguerite woke with her storm-sense singing before her eyes opened — a presence at the gate of their small house, vast and diffuse and familiar. Theo stirred against her shoulder. "Whazzit," he managed.

"Warden," she said, and was already standing.

It waited at the door, edges luminous in the dark, and it did not come in — some boundaries, she understood now, the Warden kept by choice. It extended what might loosely be called a hand, and in it lay a folded page, damp with cloudwater, ink that shifted like distant lightning when she tilted it toward the lantern.

"From beyond the veil," the Warden said. "The channel is open now. It will not close again. Whether you answer—" the mist paused, and Marguerite would have sworn the ancient thing was choosing its words with care— "is weather no one can forecast."

Marguerite unfolded the page. Her own name in the greeting. Her mother's handwriting underneath — the same hand that had written doom in a blank book, that had built a trap out of prophecy and called it protection. But the words themselves were short. Uncertain. Almost human:

*The outer reaches are cold, and I am learning to be alone in them. I am told the rain at your wedding fell softly. I would like, someday, to hear how it sounded.*

Marguerite read it three times. Behind her, Theo's hand settled warm between her shoulder blades — half of him, all of it, hers.

"She wants to talk," he said quietly.

"No," Marguerite said. Folding the letter carefully. Feeling, under her ribs, where the gentle storms lived now, something turn toward the mountains like a plant toward light. "She wants to start. Talking comes later."

She looked up at the Warden, standing patient in the dark with the whole thin veil humming behind it.

"Tell her," she said, "that the rain sounded like forgiveness. And that she can have some — the rain, or the forgiveness — whenever she's ready to stand in it."
