## 40. Chapter 40

**Marguerite**

The first thing I do, on the morning of the first day of the rest of our lives, is burn the toast.

This is not a metaphor. It is an actual slice of bread, blackened to a crisp in Theo's grandmother's skillet, and I stare at it with the particular satisfaction of a woman who has spent a year facing down gods and has finally found an opponent she can defeat.

"You're doing it wrong," Theo says from the doorway, hair still sleep-flattened, one sock on, one sock nowhere to be found. He has never once in our life together gotten dressed in the correct order. "You have to take it off the heat before it looks like that."

"I know how toast works, Theo."

"Do you? Because I have documentation." He crosses the kitchen, kisses the top of my head, and slides the skillet out from under me with the ease of long practice. "I have a whole year of documentation."

He's right, of course. I spent fifteen years in this valley learning to be ordinary, and I never quite mastered it. I can call a storm now — a whisper of one, a rumor, rain that falls soft on purpose — but I cannot make toast. Some things are simply beyond me.

The kettle begins to sing. Outside the window, the garden is coming up green in rows that are not quite straight, because Bramble's grandson helped me plant them and the boy has his grandfather's hands and none of his grandfather's arithmetic. The frost line is a memory now, a story the villagers tell each other in the tavern with the embellishments that stories acquire. *A foot an hour*, they say now. *The snow came down sideways and sang.* The snow did not sing. But I have learned that a valley that spent a century forgetting needs its stories exaggerated, the way a starving person needs their bread.

The kettle sings, and under it — beneath it, through it — I hear the veil.

That is the thing no one warned me about, the thing I could not have been warned about: that after you give half of yourself away, what remains is not less. It is more attentive. I lost my storms, the great rolling thunderheads of my girlhood, the lightning I used to hold in my teeth. What I kept was the listening. And the listening says the veil is there, always there, a breath away, like a neighbor humming through a shared wall.

I used to find that thought unbearable. The whole point of exile was silence. I came here to be a woman, only a woman, nothing but a woman — and I failed at it so thoroughly that I ended up something new instead.

"Marguerite." Theo sets a plate in front of me. The toast on it is golden. He has performed his small domestic miracle again, the one he performs every morning, and I have stopped taking it for granted exactly never. "You went somewhere."

"I'm here."

"You're here and you went somewhere. Both things. I've learned to live with it." He sits across from me with his own plate and his terrible tea — he brews it too long, always, and drinks it anyway, and I have stopped correcting him because I have come to understand that some flaws are load-bearing. "The Warden came by while you were sleeping."

I look up. "The Warden doesn't come by. The Warden stands at the gate and judges us all silently. That's the whole job."

"The Warden came by," Theo repeats, with the maddening calm he deploys when he is enjoying my surprise, "and stood at the end of the garden, and it said — and I quote, because I've been practicing the voice — *'The letter came.'*"

The letter.

I put down my fork. My mother has been writing to me for four months now, letters carried across the veil by a creature of living mist, letters that began as dispatches — *the outer reaches are mended, the eastern squalls are quiet, the court sends its regards* — and have been softening, month by month, the way frost softens into rain. The last one ended with a sentence that I read eleven times and then put in the drawer with my grandmother's ring: *Your grandmother grew roses, before the covenant. I never told you that. I don't know why I'm telling you now.*

She doesn't know why she's telling me. That is the most honest thing Seraphine Ishikawa has ever written.

"What did it say?" I ask.

Theo slides it across the table. He doesn't open my letters anymore — we agreed on that, after the packed bag and the copied page, after the lie between us taught us both what secrets cost. He just carries them. "I didn't read it," he says. "Obviously. But the Warden waited. It doesn't wait, Marguerite. It stood there for twenty minutes looking at the tomatoes."

"Looking at the tomatoes."

"Judging the tomatoes. It's hard to tell with the Warden."

I break the seal. The paper smells faintly of ozone, the way everything from her side does, and the handwriting is the fierce backward-leaning script I have known since childhood, since copybooks and scoldings and the long years of silence.

*Marguerite.*

*I have finished the outer mending. The court asks what I will do next, and for the first time in my life I do not have an answer prepared, and I find I do not hate the feeling.*

*There is a place on the far edge of the storm realm where the veil is thin and the weather is gentle and nothing of consequence happens. The Warden tells me the valley has a boundary. The boundary has a gate. I am told that visitors may now cross, briefly, if the valley permits it.*

*I am not asking to come home. I don't know that I have a home, or that I deserve one, or what I would do in it. I am asking whether the gate opens.*

*I will stand at the edge of the mist on the first day of harvest, at dawn. If you are there, I will cross. If you are not, I will go back to my nothing-of-consequence and write you another letter, and another, for as long as you let me.*

*Your grandmother's roses were white. I thought you should know that too.*

*— S.*

I read it twice. Theo watches me the way he watches weather now, which is to say with his whole body, his hand drifting across the table until it covers mine.

"Well?" he says.

"She wants to come. For one dawn. She wants to see the valley." I set the letter down. My hands are steady, which surprises me. "Theo, the last time she came to this valley, she came in a carriage of thunder to take everything I'd built."

"The last time she came to this valley, she thought she was saving you." He says it without bitterness. He has had four months of watching me read her letters to arrive where I have arrived, which is not forgiveness — I want to be clear about that, I am not a woman who forgives on schedule — but something adjacent. Something that admits the shape of her reasoning without endorsing it. "People do terrible things with love in their hands. You told me that yourself. Chapter eighteen, as it were."

"Don't quote our own history at me."

"It's a very good history. I cried."

I laugh — a real laugh, the kind that startles me out of myself — and then I am quiet for a while, looking out at the garden, at the rows of green, at the far ridge where the mist still stands at the boundary, thinner than it was, gentler, but there. The veil between realms. Thinned but not broken. That was the whole design, ours, the heresy that worked: not a wall, not a door thrown open, but a veil. Something you can see through. Something you can speak through.

Something a mother might stand at the edge of, at dawn, and wait.

**Theo**

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving the end of the world: the world doesn't end. The dishes still need doing.

I think about this a lot. I thought about it during the winter, when the frost was eating the valley a foot at a time and the founders' council was doing its terrible arithmetic and my girlfriend's mother was descending from the sky in a carriage made of weather. I thought: *if we live through this, everything will be different.* And it is different, it is unrecognizably different — I can feel the valley's moods the way you feel a mood in a room, I can hear rain coming three days out, I age a little faster than I should and I have made my peace with it, mostly, in the mornings, before the tea — and yet the dishes still need doing. The toast still burns. The roof over the kitchen developed a leak in the spring thaw and I spent an entire Saturday on a ladder swearing at it, while the veil hummed at the edge of my hearing like a patient audience.

I used to be the ordinary one. That was my whole identity, going in: the mortal, the stillness, the man with the garden and the burnt stew and the one thing the storm-folk lacked. And I have learned — through the marked palm, the sigil that still faintly glows when lightning falls on the far mountains, the whole impossible year — that ordinary is not a fixed quantity. Ordinary is a door. You can walk through it in either direction.

Marguerite comes out of the house with her shawl over her shoulders and the letter in her hand, and she stands beside me at the garden fence, and for a while we just look at the rows together.

"She asked if the gate opens," she says.

"It opens."

"You don't know that."

"I know that we rewrote the covenant from scratch," I say, "and I know that when we wrote the terms, you insisted on the clause about visitors. *Mortals may visit the storms briefly; storm-folk may enter the valley gently.* You fought the archivists for three days over the word *gently*."

"Because words matter in a covenant. *Gently* is a promise. *Briefly* is a promise."

"And *the gate opens* is a promise too. You made it. You just made it before you knew who'd come asking." I turn to look at her. The morning light is doing what it does to her now — she is mortal-bound, her storms gentled to whispers, and it has made her more beautiful rather than less, the way a river is more beautiful than a flood. "So the question isn't whether the gate opens. The question is whether you'll be standing there when it does."

She is quiet for a long time. A bee works its way down the bean rows. Somewhere past the ridge, so far away it's more memory than sound, thunder rolls — and I feel it the way she once felt it, and I watch her feel me feeling it, and this is what we are now, two halves of one listening, and I would not trade it for the unbroken selves we used to be. I want to be honest about that. We grieved our old selves together, properly, the way you grieve anything real. And then the grief finished, and what was left was this: a life with a seam in it, and the seam is where the light comes through.

"I'll be there," she says finally. "Dawn, first day of harvest. I'll be there." A pause. "Will you?"

"I was going to be there before you asked."

"I know." She almost smiles. "I asked anyway. It's different, asking."

It is different, asking. That is the whole of what we learned, both of us, at the cost of everything we used to be: that survival was never meant to be solitary, and neither is anything else. Not gates. Not grief. Not breakfast.

**Marguerite**

The first day of harvest comes up gold.

The whole valley turns out, though I didn't ask them to and wouldn't have. That is the other thing the year taught us: the valley was always a hinge, and the people in it were always part of the covenant, and when the covenant changed, they changed with it. Some of them can feel the veil now — the children first, then the young, then, one by one, the stubborn old ones who swore they never would. Old Man Bramble's grandson can hear rain coming the way I can. Bramble himself, before he dissolved the council, sat me down on his porch and told me the arithmetic of his own cruelty, out loud, in daylight, which is more penance than most traditions ever pay, and then he asked me to teach the boy. Not to call storms. To listen.

I teach them on Thursdays. There are six of them, valley-born, and they sit in a circle in my garden and practice hearing the veil the way you practice hearing a stream under snow. Last week the smallest one, a girl named Wren with her grandmother's stubbornness, looked up suddenly and said, "It sounds like someone humming." And I said, "Yes. It always has. We just weren't taught to hear it."

At dawn, Theo and I walk up to the boundary.

The mist is thinner than it was a year ago — that was the design, the vow, the price: the veil thinned but not broken, magic and mortality sharing one sky. Through it, the far side shimmers like the surface of a lake looked at from below. The Warden stands at the gate as the Warden always stands, a figure of living mist, and as we approach it inclines what might be a head.

"The bridge," it says to Theo, and there is something in the old word that is no longer a sentence. It says *bridge* the way the valley says *neighbor* now.

"The Warden," Theo says back, gravely, and I know he is enjoying himself.

And then, at the edge of the mist, on the far side — a figure. Tall. Straight-backed even now, even after the outer mending, even after the kneeling. She is not in a carriage of thunder. She is not in storm regalia. Seraphine Ishikawa, High Storm-Caller, architect of prophecies and of my exile and of the trap that became a door, is wearing a plain grey traveling coat, and her hands are empty, and she is waiting.

She said she would wait for dawn. She is there before dawn. Of course she is. She has always been early for everything except the things that mattered.

My heart does something complicated. I let it.

I look at Theo. He looks at me. And I understand, standing there with the mist between me and my mother, that this is the last page of one story and the first page of another, and that the difference between them is not fate, and never was. The prophecy book sits on my shelf at home, its final pages filled with a new prophecy — not doom, a question mark, a door left open — and beneath it, in my own ink, in my own hand, the line I added: *What comes next is authored, not fated. Write carefully. Write together.*

The gate is not a gate, really. It is just the place where the mist is thinnest. It has been open since the vow. The only question, the only question there ever was, is who is willing to stand at it.

"Marguerite," my mother says through the veil. Her voice carries the way voices carry across water. "I don't — I don't know what to say that the letters haven't said."

"Then don't say anything," I tell her. "Just cross. Gently."

And she steps through.

The mist takes her and gives her back, and she comes out the other side into the valley's ordinary morning — into the smell of bread and cut hay and the green smell of my garden, into a world where magic moves like weather, subtle, seasonal, survivable — and she stops, and she breathes, and for one unguarded moment the High Storm-Caller looks like a woman standing in her daughter's garden for the first time, not knowing what she's allowed to touch.

Theo squeezes my hand. Down in the valley, the first bell of harvest rings. Above the far mountains, lightning plays over the peaks, and I feel it like music, and I know he feels it too, and I know she feels us feeling it, and the seam in the world lets the light through.

"Come on," I say. "The toast's probably burnt. It's tradition."

And we walk down into the valley, all three of us, out of the winter, into the morning — two halves and one whole, and the sky above us holding everything at once.
