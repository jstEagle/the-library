## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, I learned early, is that they insist on being accounted for.

Most people assume a guild that brokers bottled storms and borrowed years runs on mystery — incense, chanting, the rustle of unseen wings. In truth it runs on double-entry bookkeeping. Every wonder has a price, every price has a payer, and every payer, sooner or later, tries to weasel out of the second half of the installment plan. My name is Theo Winterbourne, and I keep the ledgers of the Gilded Veil. It is the safest job in the city, provided you don't look too closely at what the numbers mean. I have gotten very good at not looking.

That morning's first entry was for Maren Ostler, widow, of Copperlane. One hour of her drowned husband's voice, drawn up from wherever drowned voices go, so she could hear him say the thing he'd never said before his boat went down in the Squall Line. She paid in memories — the guild's preferred currency from clients with more love than money. Three hundred and forty memories, itemized: their wedding, her mother's funeral bread, the way he hummed while mending nets, all of it weighed and discounted and entered against the balance due.

I wrote *336* in my neat hand, because four of her memories were duplicates — grief does that, circles the same bright moment until it wears thin — and duplicates are worth less. Then I sat with my pen hovering over the page for longer than a professional ought, wondering what it says about me that I could put a number on a woman's whole marriage before breakfast.

It says I'm good at my job. That is the answer I give myself. It doesn't always take.

* * *

The Gilded Veil occupies a building that can't decide what century it belongs to. From the street it's a narrow countinghouse between a chandler and a shop that sells nothing but locks, with brass fittings gone green and a door that opens only if you knock in threes — or so I'm told; I use the key. Inside, the hall goes back further than the street allows, which is the sort of architectural lie nobody in this trade finds remarkable. Shelves line the walls floor to ceiling, and the shelves hold jars, boxes, reliquaries, birdcages with the doors soldered shut. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. A name that was never spoken, kept under triple wax because names like that itch when you ignore them.

Vespera Quill, the guild's mistress, calls our inventory "the accounts receivable of heaven." Vespera says things like that with a straight face and expects you to be impressed. I am many things, but impressed is not currently one of them, and she knows it, and I suspect that's why she keeps me. Everyone else who works here thinks the trade is holy. I think it's arithmetic. Arithmetic, at least, tells you the truth about itself.

At midmorning the mercenary came in — Corvane, they called him, though I had it on good authority from Bramble that he'd bought that name off a dead man in Vell and it didn't fit him any better than his coat did. He wanted to trade his shadow.

"You understand what you're offering," Vespera said, from her chair by the window where she conducted negotiations like a queen holding court over dust motes. "A shadow is not a trifle. It's everything you refuse to look at about yourself. Walk around without it and you'll find out fast."

"I've been walking without it for years," Corvane said. "This way somebody else carries it and I get paid."

I will grant the man this: he understood the transaction better than most. The guild took shadows, regrets, first loves, the memory of your own face as seen in water — anything integral, because integral things hold value the way ore holds metal. He signed the contract in my ledger with an X, because mercenaries who sell their shadows rarely keep their letters either, and I watched the shade peel off the floor behind him like wet paper and fold itself into the jar Bramble held out. It moved even folded. They always do. I noted *one shadow, adult male, sullen disposition,* and Corvane counted his coin twice, and left lighter in every sense.

"What does the guild *do* with them?" I asked Vespera after the door shut. I ask this question roughly once a month. It is a ritual. She gives the same answer every time.

"Balanced books stay balanced, dear. Don't worry which side of the scale you're on until you have to."

I had been worrying about which side of the scale I was on since I was nineteen years old, but there seemed no profit in saying so.

* * *

Here is the part of my life I do not enter into any ledger, including the private ones.

Under the third floorboard of my room, beneath the loose board that also hides my savings and a letter I have never sent, there is a grimoire bound in grey leather with a crack down the spine. It belonged to Aldous Merrin, my former master, hedge-witch, scholar, and the single most patient cruel man I have ever known. I stole it the night I ran. Not for the spells — though God knows the spells are worth a hanging — but because I thought it was mine by right. He made me copy it out three times, cover to cover, as punishment for asking questions. He said copying builds character. What copying actually built was a memory so thorough I could recite the thing in my sleep, and eventually the realization that I remembered it better than he did, having read it more carefully than he ever bothered to.

Some nights I take it out and read it anyway, by candle stub, with the shutters latched. And some nights — more often lately — I notice things in it that frighten me.

Not the spells themselves. Spells are recipes; recipes bore me. But the marginalia, the little annotations Aldous never explained, the columns of figures running alongside incantations like a merchant's tally beside a prayer — those had begun to look less like decoration and more like *bookkeeping*. Debits. Withdrawals. As if every working in the grimoire drew down against some vast account, and someone, somewhere, was keeping track of the balance.

I told myself it was coincidence. I am a professional arithmetician; I know how rarely coincidence survives contact with a column of figures. I told myself anyway, because the alternative was to start asking questions, and questions are how you end up dead or worse — noticed.

* * *

Sister Ondine found me in the counting room at dusk, doing what I always do at dusk: reconciling the day's entries. She moves quietly for a woman built like a belfry, Ondine, and she has the ex-priestess's habit of appearing exactly when you're thinking something blasphemous.

"The widow Ostler came back," she said. "After her hour was spent. She wants another."

"Her account won't cover it. I priced it fairly."

"She's offering her remaining years. All of them."

My pen stopped. "She has maybe twenty years left. The exchange rate on human years alone would buy her a week of his voice. With sentiment factored—" I stopped. Sentiment factored heavily in this guild; Vespera liked desperate clients, and desperation was its own currency. "Twenty years for a week. Nobody sane makes that trade."

"No," Ondine agreed. She sat down across from me, folding her hands — hands that had once administered last rites in a proper temple, before whatever happened happened, a story she told no one and I never asked. "But then, sanity was never a requirement for walking through that door. Only need." She watched me write something that wasn't anything, just ink shaped like work. "You'll deny her?"

"The books deny her. I just hold the pen."

"Do you believe that?" Ondine asked gently, the way you'd ask a child if they believed the stove isn't hot. "That the pen is innocent?"

I looked at her. She is the only person in this building who asks me real questions, which is why I trust her least, or tell myself I do. Trust, in my experience, is just a debt you haven't been billed for yet.

"I believe," I said, "that if the pen is guilty, then so is everyone who ever balanced a butcher's tally or a tithe roll, and the world runs on those. Somebody has to count. Counting is honest."

"Counting is honest," Ondine said. "People less so. Goodnight, Theo."

She left me with my candles and my columns and the small cold feeling that she hadn't been talking about the widow at all.

* * *

Later — much later, past the hour when even Kesh comes staggering home smelling of other cities' stables, past the hour when Bramble stops forging bills of sale and starts forging excuses for why he's still awake — I climbed to my room and lifted the floorboard.

I hadn't planned to open the grimoire. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger that keeps honest books: I had not planned it. But the candle was lit and the house was quiet and the numbers from the day were crawling under my skin like ants, and there it was, grey leather, cracked spine, patient as Aldous himself.

I turned to the pages near the end, the ones I'd been circling for a month the way Maren Ostler circles her wedding memory. Columns of figures beside a rite for divining lost things. The same figures, nearly, beside a rite for binding an oath. The same figures again — *diminished*, each time, the balance dropping entry by entry like a well going dry — in the margins of a prayer so old the language predated the Empire.

Someone had been drawing against something enormous, for centuries. And the handwriting in the oldest margins was not Aldous's. It was older, spidery, faded brown. It looped its descenders in a way I recognized, because I loop mine the same way, because my grandmother looped hers that way, because—

I closed the book. I put it back under the floor. I sat in the dark and listened to the Gilded Veil settle around me: Kesh snoring, the jars whispering their jar-whisper, a shadow in the inventory turning over in its sleep.

This place is not home. I want to be clear about that, mostly to myself. This place is shelter, and shelter is a transaction like any other — I keep the books, the books keep me. When the bill comes due I'll pay it and walk out clean. That has been the arrangement from the first night, when Vespera looked at my forged references, saw through them in four seconds flat, and hired me anyway because, she said, "anyone who lies that competently understands incentives."

But lying in the dark with the ghost of my grandmother's handwriting behind my eyelids, I caught myself doing arithmetic I had forbidden myself. Calculating how long it would take to move Ondine and Bramble and Kesh somewhere safer than here. Catching the word *family* before it finished forming and shoving it back down where it couldn't cost me anything.

Wary belonging. Shelter with an exit mapped. That's all this is. I checked the exits twice before bed, as always, and slept with the grimoire's weight under the floorboard beneath me like a stone at the bottom of a well.

In the morning, a client came to the shrine on Copperlane Way — just some poor devil lighting a candle at the cracked old altar everyone walks past — and got down on his knees and prayed.

And for the first time in three hundred years, something answered.
