## 4. The Guild of Borrowed Years

*Bramble*

I have forged the signatures of kings, the seals of three archdukes, and once — may the ink forgive me — a papal indulgence for a man who wanted to sin in advance. So believe me when I say I know a forgery when I see one, and Theo Winterbourne's grief that morning was the realest thing in the Gilded Veil.

She came down to the counting room before dawn with her stolen grimoire under one arm like a sick cat and a look on her face I recognized from mirrors. The look of someone who has read something she was happier not knowing.

"Your margins are wrong," she told my current commission, which was a bill of sale for a haunted orchard. "The wax seal's too clean. Nobody in Vellham markets seals wax that carefully unless they're lying."

"Everyone who buys a haunted orchard is lying," I said. "It's practically the point of orchards."

She didn't smile. That was how I knew it was bad.

I'd been watching her for months, the way you watch weather. She'd arrived half-starved with a book full of somebody else's sins and a genius for numbers that made the rest of us look like we were counting on our fingers. Vespera had taken one look at her arithmetic and hired her before the ink dried. The rest of us took longer. Trust, in our trade, is a currency that inflates badly.

But that morning she stood in my workroom worrying a hangnail and asking, far too casually, whether I'd ever forged anything *old*. Documents from before the god-war. And when I said no — nothing survives from then except stone and regret — she went pale in that particular way of someone doing sums they don't want finished.

I should have pressed her. Instead I poured her tea, because Bramble of the Gilded Veil has exactly two talents: forgery and cowardice, and I like to keep both sharp.

That was the morning the door came down.

Not knocked. Not opened. Came *down*, hinges and all, and three men in ash-grey cloaks walked through it wearing the kind of confidence that only comes from being paid extremely well or believing extremely hard. Their leader held up a warrant sealed in black wax.

"In the name of the Rekindled Dawn," he announced, "we claim all texts of the old covenant."

I didn't know what the old covenant was. But I knew what a raid looked like, and more usefully, I knew where my escape hatch was, because a forger who can't flee his own forgeries doesn't live to forge again.

I also knew that Theo had gone white as unbleached vellum, and that her hand had gone to the grimoire, and that whatever those grey men wanted, they wanted *her* book most of all.

Well. Cowardice is a talent. Every talent has its uses.

* * *

*Sister Ondine*

I was praying when the shouting started — proper praying, the kind I'd sworn off, kneeling in the guild chapel that nobody else used because gods were bad for business. Three hundred years of silence from heaven and still I knelt, which Sister Anselm at the convent would have called faith and which I call a habit with teeth.

Three days ago, a client had prayed at a wayside shrine and been *answered*. Ash had fallen upward. I had heard Vespera order the man silenced, and I had said nothing, and the nothing had been sitting in my chest ever since like a swallowed stone.

So when Bramble burst into the chapel hauling Theo by the wrist, both of them trailing grey-cloaked men like wasps, my first thought was shamelessly practical: the crypt tunnel exits behind the reliquary, and the reliquary lock hasn't worked since the schism.

"Ondine," Bramble gasped, "you're a priestess, absolve us quickly—"

"I'm an *ex*-priestess."

"Then ex-absolve us!"

Theo wasn't running properly. That was the thing I noticed, kneeling there with dust sifting from the rafters. Bramble pulled; her feet obeyed; but her eyes kept going back toward the counting room, toward the ledgers, as though the books were calling her name. I have seen that expression once before, on novices hearing their first true vocation — the terrible pull of a thing that wants you specifically.

"The ledgers," she said. "They can't have the ledgers."

"They can have everything," Bramble said. "That's rather the philosophy of fleeing."

I put my hand on the reliquary. It swung open on its broken hinge, obedient as an old dog, and cold air breathed up from the crypt, smelling of stone and older stone.

"Sister," Theo said, catching my sleeve as I ushered them down. "You've read scripture. The binding of the gods — the Veil of Sorrows, the Oath of Ash — is any of it *true?*"

I looked at her, this hedge-witch's runaway with ink on her fingers and terror in her eyes, and I answered honestly, which I rarely do before noon.

"It was true enough that they wrote it down very carefully. Why?"

She didn't answer. Behind us, the grey men reached the chapel, and their leader began to pray aloud — loudly, joyfully, in a voice pitched for recruitment — and every word of that prayer was one I'd never heard, in a cadence older than my order, and it made the crypt stones hum under my palms like a struck bell.

I stopped asking questions and started running.

* * *

*Kesh*

Here is what nobody tells you about dragon blood: it doesn't make you strong. It makes you *warm*. While everyone else shivered in the crypt tunnel, I was comfortable, which is my entire inheritance from a grandmother who could melt castle gates, and I have made peace with that. Mostly.

I heard them coming long before the others did. Not the grey men — the *others*. Underneath the pursuit, underneath Ondine's prayers and Bramble's complaints, there was a sound like a second heartbeat in the walls, slow and vast and patient, and the scales along my forearms rose without asking me.

"Left at the fork," I said, taking point. "And don't touch the third lantern bracket. It's a tripwire for the wine cellar."

"Why does the crypt connect to the wine cellar?" Bramble demanded.

"Because Vespera believes in contingency planning and cheap burgundy. Keep up."

We came up through the trapdoor into the cellar, and the cellar into the alley, and the alley into rain, which felt like freedom until the grey men boiled out of the crypt exit behind us and I saw there were more of them now. Eight. Twelve. Moving in step, chanting under their breath, and the chant was making the rain fall *wrong* — drops hanging in the air, turning over, showing their undersides.

"That's not natural," Bramble said.

"That's not anything," I said. My blood knew better than my education. Dragon-blood remembers things the mind never learned, and mine was screaming a single syllable: *before*. This magic was from *before*. From when the gods walked.

Theo grabbed my arm. Her fingers were ice even against my cool skin.

"Kesh. How fast can you run carrying three people's worth of excuse?"

"For you lot? Fast enough to be legendary." I looked at them — the forger, the priestess, the ledger-keeper, huddled in the rain with everything they owned left behind in a burning-or-possibly-fine guildhouse — and something in my chest went sideways. I'd been courier for the Gilded Veil four years. Packages, letters, contraband. You don't carry people. People are heavy in ways packages aren't.

"West," Theo said suddenly. "We go west."

"West is the Wastes," Ondine said. "West is the shrine-mountain and everything dead around it."

"West," Theo repeated, in the voice of a woman balancing a column that must balance, "is where we'll find a dragon."

* * *

*Theo*

We ran.

I could give you the route in ledger form — fourteen streets, two roofs, one drainage culvert of genuinely criminal smell — but what I remember is not geography. What I remember is this:

Bramble, who has never in his life run anywhere voluntarily, stopping mid-flight to snatch his forgery kit from a hiding place in a wall, because "if we're fleeing into legend, someone will need documents," and pressing a fresh set of papers into each of our hands before we'd gone another block.

Ondine, doubling back under a bridge to leave a coin at a beggar's feet and murmur a blessing — a real one, old ones, the kind she claims not to believe in — while twelve fanatics hunted her name.

Kesh, taking the roof line alone to draw off three pursuers, and coming back down an alley thirty seconds later grinning with soot on her jaw and someone else's blood on her knuckles, saying only, "They'll live. They'll be embarrassed longer."

And somewhere between the culvert and the west gate, in the dark and the rain, with the grey men's impossible prayer still humming in my teeth, I stopped running *from* and started running *with*. I don't know the moment it happened. I know that Bramble fell, and I caught him, and he said "my kit, save the kit," and I said "the kit is fine, you enormous fraud, *move*," and he laughed — actually laughed — and moved.

At the west gate, past the last of the lamps, Kesh called a halt in the shadow of a milestone. We stood there breathing like bellows, four people with nothing in common except a guild, an employer, and a set of enemies who prayed in pre-human tongues.

"So," Bramble panted, "when we inevitably survive this, remind me to invoice someone."

"You can't invoice a raid," Kesh said.

"I can invoice *someone*. I always do."

Ondine was looking at me. She had been looking at me since the crypt, patient as her old God, and now she asked, quietly, "You know what they want. Don't you, Theo?"

The honest answer sat in my chest beside the grimoire, beside the marginalia I hadn't shown anyone, beside the arithmetic that wouldn't stop resolving itself in the back of my skull into a single unbearable sum. My master's voice, from years ago, floated up out of memory: *a ledger is just a prison with better handwriting, girl.*

"Not yet," I said.

Which was a lie, and I think all three of them knew it, and none of them called it — and that mercy undid me more thoroughly than the raid had. Colleagues would have demanded. Family lets you keep your secret until you're ready, and stands close enough to catch you when the ready breaks.

"West," I said again, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "There's a dragon in the Wastes, if the stories hold, and I think — I think she's the only fire left that can fix what's breaking."

"What's breaking?" Ondine asked.

Far behind us, back toward the city, the sky over the Gilded Veil lit briefly gold — vault-fire, ward-fire, everything Vespera had built going up like a struck match — and beneath our feet, faint but unmistakable, the ground answered it with a pulse. One heartbeat. Then another. Slow. Vast. Patient. Counting.

"Everything," I said.
