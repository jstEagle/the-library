## 5. Dragon-Fire, Guttering

I have carried a great many things for the Gilded Veil. Bottled storms, which bite. Borrowed years, which itch. A duke's regret, once, sealed in amber, which wept at night until I threatened to sell it to a tax collector.

But I had never carried hope before, and I will tell you honestly: it was the heaviest cargo of my career.

We climbed for nine days into the Ashen Teeth, the broken spine of mountains where the maps go shy and the compasses go drunk. Bramble complained from the first switchback. Sister Ondine prayed from the second. Theo said nothing at all, which worried me more than both of them combined, because Kesh Ember-son has hauled enough secrets in his saddlebags to know the weight of one by the way it makes a person walk.

She walked like a woman carrying a cathedral in a satchel.

"Tell me again why we're trusting a dragon," Bramble said on the fifth day, when the wind started tasting of cinders. "Specifically, why we're trusting the last dragon. That's not trust, that's putting all our eggs in one extremely flammable basket."

"Because her fire is the only thing that can reforge the oath," Ondine said. She said it the way she said everything now — quietly, like a woman who had spent her faith and found something sturdier underneath it. "And because the alternative is Aldous selling the keys to heaven to men who think divinity is a throne."

"I liked it better when the gods were dead," Bramble muttered. "Dead gods don't answer prayers. Dead gods don't make widows weep at shrines. Dead gods let a forger sleep at night."

"You don't sleep at night."

"That's *my* insomnia, and it's mine by right."

I let them argue. It kept their legs moving and their fear at a manageable simmer. And I watched Theo, who had gone quiet as a held breath ever since the night she'd sat up with the oldest ledger and come away looking like someone had read her own name back to her in a voice she didn't recognize.

Nine days. Then the mountain opened.

Not dramatically — dragons, I would learn, do not do dramatic. The pass simply narrowed, narrowed, narrowed, until the walls were close enough to touch, and then they weren't walls anymore but ribs. Ribs the size of ship masts, arched over a valley floor of black glass, fused smooth by heat older than any kingdom whose coins I'd ever couriered.

Bramble stopped complaining. That's how you know a place is serious.

"She's here," Ondine whispered. Not a guess. An ex-priestess knows the temperature of a holy place, and this whole valley was one long held note of it.

The cave mouth at the valley's end breathed out warm air that smelled of woodsmoke and, strangely, of bread. Of home. Of every hearth anyone had ever fallen asleep beside. I felt my own fire stir under my skin — the dragon-blood I've never once discussed with these people, though I suspect Theo balances more than ledgers — and it leaned toward that warmth like a child toward a lit window.

We went in.

* * *

She was dying. You didn't need to be dragon-blooded to see it; you needed only eyes.

Ember-that-Was filled the cavern the way winter fills a valley. Vast beyond sense, scaled in colors that had burned down to embers — dull red going grey at the edges, like coals left too long unbanked. Her hide was cracked in places, and through the cracks glowed not fire but a faint, failing light, the way a hearth looks an hour after the flames give up.

One eye opened. It was the size of a cart wheel and clouded white as frost, and it rolled toward us with terrible patience.

"*Small ones,*" she said. Her voice did not boom. It settled, slow as snowfall, into your bones. "*Three small ones and one who is not.*"

My knees went strange. I have faced mercenaries, monsters, and one memorable tax audit, and never once lost my footing. But there is a difference between being seen and being *known*, and this old creature knew me across forty feet of dark.

"Kesh," Theo murmured, without turning her head. "You want to explain?"

"Not especially."

"*Dragon-blooded,*" Ember rumbled, and the word moved through the stone like a stone itself. "*A spark off an old anvil. Come here, little cousin. Let me see what the years made of my sister's line.*"

I went. I'm not ashamed of it. When a mountain speaks, you walk toward it.

Her breath washed over me, warm as a forge-mouth, and her blind eye turned and turned as if reading something written in the air around my skin. Whatever she read, it made a sound come out of her that I had never heard from anything that large — a low, cracked hum, almost like purring, almost like grief.

"*They scattered well,*" she murmured. "*After the war. Little embers in little lamps, walking about thinking themselves merely quick-healing and fond of high places.*"

"Fond of high places," Bramble repeated faintly. "That tracks, actually."

Theo stepped forward then, and everything changed.

I saw it happen. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger: I *saw* it. The old dragon's head swung toward Theo with a groan of stone, and her nostrils flared once, twice — and then the great clouded eye went wide, wide as the sky, and Ember-that-Was began to weep.

Smoke, not tears. It poured from her eyes in slow grey ribbons and rose to the cavern roof, and the sound she made was the sound I imagine mountains make when they crack — deep, ancient, and full of a sorrow so old it had forgotten its own name.

"*Oh,*" she breathed. "*Oh, little keeper. Little daughter of the writing hand. You smell of ink and iron and the first oath, before it broke. Who bore you, child? Who carried the seal in their veins and let it thin to water?*" Her voice fractured. "*I watched them write it. I stood witness. My fire was meant to keep it whole.*"

"Theo," Ondine said carefully, "what is she talking about?"

"I don't know." And Theo lied beautifully, smoothly, with the flat calm of a woman who has balanced false books her whole life — but I had watched her face for months, and I saw the truth land behind her eyes like a debt coming due. "I'm just a ledger-keeper."

"*Ledger-keeper,*" Ember echoed, and something in the vast old voice was almost tender. "*Yes. They always were. The writers of the binding kept accounts, little one. Debits against the sleeping. Credits of silence. Your blood did not guard the prison, child. Your blood IS the prison. The lock wears a family like a coat.*"

The silence afterward had weight. It pressed down on us all.

Bramble looked at Theo. Ondine looked at Theo. I looked at the dragon, because looking at Theo just then would have been a cruelty, and I am many things but rarely cruel.

"Theo," Ondine said at last, very gently, "is there something you'd like to tell us?"

"There's something I'd like to *not* tell you," Theo said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, hanging at her sides, were not. "Can that be enough? For now?"

Ondine studied her for a long moment — the ex-priestess weighing a soul on scales only she could see. Then she nodded, once, and I understood that forgiveness can arrive ahead of confession, like payment rendered before the invoice.

"For now," Ondine said.

Bramble said nothing, which from Bramble was a sermon.

* * *

We stayed three days in the dragon's warmth while she gathered herself to speak plainly, and what she told us by that failing hearth-light I set down here exactly, because some things should survive even if I don't:

The oath was forged in dragon-fire and written in mortal blood, one seal per generation, thinned by intermarriage and time until it stretched across a whole lineage like gold leaf across old wood — everywhere and invisible. The ledgers of the Gilded Veil were not records. They were maintenance. Every balanced entry fed the lock. Every impossible trade brokered in secret kept the gods dreaming, because the Veil's true business was never storms or shadows or borrowed years.

It was silence. Bought and sold and balanced, century after century.

"And now the books are failing," Theo said quietly, on the third night. She sat against Ember's foreleg like a woman leaning on a wall she'd trusted all her life. "The miracles. The answered prayers. The seal is cracking, and Aldous is out there selling pieces of the breakage."

"*Then my fire must reforge what was broken,*" Ember said. "*As it was always meant to. But hear me, small ones, and hear me truly.*" The great head lowered until her blind eye was level with us, and up close I could see the light in it guttering like a candle in a doorway. "*Fire spends. Mine has been spending for three hundred years, waiting for a reason worth the last of it. When I burn to reforge the oath, I will not kindle again. This is not a warning. It is arithmetic.*"

Nobody spoke. Bramble took off her hat, which for Bramble is a funeral rite.

"So we find another way," Theo said. "There's always another entry. Another—"

"*There is not.*" Gentle. Final. "*There is the fire, and there is the hand that writes, and between them the world goes on sleeping safely. Or there is neither, and the gods wake hungry.*"

That night I couldn't rest, so I climbed to the cavern mouth and watched the stars through the rib-arches of the mountain, and I thought about cargo. About how a courier learns early that some deliveries change the recipient forever, and the wise ones refuse those jobs. And how I had already accepted this one, months ago, in a guildhall full of strangers who had since become the only address my heart recognized.

Behind me, soft as ash falling, I heard Theo speaking to the dragon in a language I didn't know — old words, accounting words, the grammar of the binding — and Ember answering in that snowfall voice, patient as the dying.

I didn't turn around. Some conversations are doors, and you can hear through a door whether the people inside are saying goodbye.

In the morning, Ember-that-Was rose for the first time in a century. The whole mountain groaned awake around her. Scales the color of banked coals scraped against stone, and dust fell from the ceiling like grey snow, and Bramble grabbed my arm and Ondine grabbed Bramble and none of us breathed.

The old dragon turned her clouded eye toward the east — toward the shattered shrine-mountain where a man who had once been Theo's master was picking the lock on heaven — and when she spoke, her voice no longer sounded like snowfall.

It sounded like the moment before lightning.

"*Little ones. Fetch my fire its pen. We go to settle the account.*"
