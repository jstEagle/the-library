## 6. The Name in the Margins

There are three things I know about the oldest ledger in the Gilded Veil's vault, and until last night I would have told you only two of them.

One: it is called the First Book, and it is chained to a lectern of black iron as though it might wander off, which — given what the guild trades in — is not an unreasonable precaution.

Two: nobody reads it. Vespera dusts it. Sister Ondine blesses it on feast days out of habit she claims to have abandoned. Bramble once offered to forge a copy and Vespera looked at him the way a hawk looks at a mouse that has made a joke. The First Book is not for reading. It is for *keeping*, the way you keep a grave.

Three: it is written in my grandmother's hand.

I did not learn that third thing so much as fall into it, which is how most true things arrive. I had gone down to the vault at midnight because I could not sleep, because Kesh's scales had started shedding in patches like autumn leaves and Ember-that-Was was dying two valleys away and every entry I balanced lately felt like bailing a boat with a thimble. Also because Aldous was out there somewhere selling god-secrets to people with more ambition than sense, and I had decided — in the way one decides things at midnight — that if the end of the world was coming, I wanted to at least understand the arithmetic of it.

The First Book does not open easily. The chain allows perhaps a hand's width of page, which means whoever designed the lectern wanted a reader who could look but not linger. I picked the lock anyway. Old habits; my master taught me locks before he taught me letters, though he'd be furious to hear me say he taught me anything useful.

The first pages were prayers. Actual prayers, the kind with invocations and genuflections, written in a script so old it predated the script. Then, somewhere around the twentieth leaf, the prayers stopped being prayers and started being *entries*.

*Received: the name of the storm-god, unspoken. Debited against: one binding, perpetual.*

*Received: the sleep of the sea-mother. Debited against: one oath, sworn in blood-line, irrevocable.*

I recognized the format because I invented nothing and improved nothing; I had simply been doing, for three years, exactly what this book did. Debits and credits. The guild's whole grammar of impossible trade was here, in embryo, in a hand that slanted left and crossed its sevens twice.

My hand does that. Crosses its sevens twice. My mother's did. Her mother's, I am given to understand, did everything twice, including marrying and burying husbands.

I sat down on the vault floor, which is not built for sitting, and read until the candle drowned in its own wax.

* * *

Here is what the marginalia says, when you finally stop flinching from the main text long enough to read the small cramped annotations crowding the edges like ivy on a wall:

The binding of the gods was not a spell. Spells are cast and finished. This was a *ledger* — a running account, maintained generation after generation, each keeper entering debits against the divine prison to keep its weight balanced. A god cannot wake while the books balance. Every miracle must be answered by a debit. Every prayer must be paid for. The old keepers understood something the guild has forgotten in its pursuit of profit: that the trades were never the point. The trades were *maintenance*. Bottled storms, borrowed years, names never spoken — all of it, every impossible thing the Gilded Veil has ever brokered, was a payment against a debt so old no one remembered there was a debtor.

And the marginalia names the keepers. Not their titles — their names, which is worse, because names are how the book knows you. There is a list running down the inside back cover like a family tree drawn by someone in a hurry, and the ink changes color down through the centuries, iron-gall fading into sepia into something modern and cheap, and the last four entries are:

*Maren Winterbourne, keeper.*
*Sable Winterbourne, keeper.*
*Corvane Winterbourne, keeper.*
*Theo Winterbourne, keeper.*

In my own handwriting, dated the day Vespera hired me, though I swear to you — and I know how this sounds — I do not remember writing it. I remember signing the employment contract. I remember the scratch of the quill. But I have gone back through my memory of that afternoon a dozen times since, and there is a gap where the signature should be, a small white absence, like a tooth knocked out.

Every entry I have balanced for three years has fed the seal. Every widow's hour of her drowned husband's voice, every mercenary's shadow, every borrowed year — debited, debited, debited, and the prison held, and I thought I was keeping books for a guild. I was keeping books for a cage. And the cage knew my name before I ever wrote it down.

* * *

I did the cowardly, sensible thing. I locked the lectern, straightened the chain so the sag wouldn't show, and went upstairs and made tea.

Bramble was awake, because Bramble is always awake at hours when honest forgers should be asleep; he works best when the city's eyes are closed. He had spread a sheet of near-perfect Vespera handwriting across the kitchen table and was frowning at it like a suitor reconsidering. He looked up when I came in, and his face did the thing it does — the quick warm rearrangement, the *oh, good, you* — and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs and I hated it, a little, because I had just come from a basement where I learned that loving these people was the most dangerous thing I could do.

"Tea's stale," he said. "Ondine bought it off a man who swears it came from the spice islands, which means it came from a warehouse behind the tanneries."

"It tastes like tanneries."

"There you go." He slid the forgery aside. "You were down in the vault."

Not a question. Bramble notices things; it's half his trade. "Couldn't sleep," I said. "Thought I'd reconcile the quarter's accounts."

"At midnight."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Midnight is when the numbers are honest."

He laughed, and poured me tea I didn't want, and told me about Kesh — that her shedding had slowed, that Ondine thought the dragon-blood in her was pulling toward Ember like water finding a crack, that we might need to make the journey to the shrine-mountain sooner than anyone wanted. He talked with his hands. He had flour on his knuckles from bread he'd started and abandoned. And the whole time he talked, I sat there holding my cup in both hands like a woman warming herself at a fire that was, unbeknownst to him, being fed by her own family's bones.

I could have told him. That was the moment, if there was a moment. He was right there, warm and rumpled and trusting, and the truth was sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, and all I had to do was open my mouth.

Instead I said, "Kesh shouldn't travel until the shedding stops," and drank my tannery tea, and let him believe the night had been about arithmetic.

* * *

Sister Ondine found me at dawn on the roof, which is where I go when the walls get too full of other people's breathing. She doesn't ask permission to sit next to anyone; she was a priestess for twenty years and it shows in her spine. She settled onto the shingles beside me and watched the smoke rise off the bakeries and said nothing for a long time, which is her particular cruelty and her particular gift.

"You look," she said finally, "like a woman who has read something she wishes she hadn't."

"Do I."

"I was a confessor, Theo. I have seen that face on kings." She folded her hands. "You don't have to tell me. But I'll say this, and then I'll shut up, which will be a first. A secret kept out of love is still a secret. It still stands between you and the people who'd stand between you and anything."

"Theo Winterbourne, keeper," I said, before I could stop myself — just the phrase, no context, testing its weight in the open air.

She didn't laugh. She didn't even blink. She looked at me for a long moment with those gray priestess-eyes, and whatever she saw made her reach over and take my cold hand in her warm one, and she said, "Then we'd best find out what it keeps," and I almost broke, right there, dawn and all.

Almost. But almost isn't telling, and telling felt like handing them a lit fuse. If the books knew my name, they might learn theirs. If Aldous learned what I was — and Aldous was out there, patient as interest accruing — then everyone under this roof became leverage. I had spent my whole life learning that love is a liability listed on someone else's balance sheet. I could not seem to unlearn it now, when it mattered most.

So I squeezed her hand, and said, "It keeps accounts," which was true, and insufficient, and she let me have the lie by omission because she is kinder than I deserve, and we watched the sun come up over a city that didn't know it was sleeping on top of a prison I was apparently feeding with every stroke of my pen.

* * *

That afternoon I balanced the week's ledgers. It took two hours. Widow Anselm's hour of voice, closed out. The mercenary's shadow, interest accrued, payment deferred — again; the man treats his own shade like a pawnshop ticket. A client in the river districts wanted a decade of her life back that she'd sold at nineteen, and Vespera approved the trade, and I entered it without my hand shaking, which I counted as a professional triumph.

Then, alone, I unlocked the lectern again and turned to the inside back cover, and beneath my own name in my own unknowing hand, I added a line in pencil — pencil, because ink is commitment and I wasn't ready:

*Entry pending.*

Because here is the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing, the sum that followed me up out of the vault and sat on my chest all night: if the seal runs on Winterbourne blood, and Aldous is hunting Winterbourne secrets, then the warlord cult that bought his knowledge isn't buying gods. They're buying *me*. And the marginalia had one more line, tucked at the very bottom of the keeper's list, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday, in a hand I knew better than my own:

*Aldous Fen, claimant.*

He hadn't stolen his way to the truth. He'd signed himself in.

I blew out the candle, and in the dark of the vault, beneath the city, beneath the sleeping gods, I heard — or imagined, and I honestly could not tell you which was worse — the faint, dry sound of a page turning.
