## 7. Aldous Ascendant

I have been called many things in my long and distinguished career: fraud, heretic, the Rot of Ravelin, "that man" (delivered with a particular curl of the lip by a bishop whose name I later sold to a debt-collector). But *thief* — thief is what stings, because it is imprecise. A thief takes. What I do is closer to *redistribution*. The gods hoarded their secrets behind three hundred years of silence; I merely moved them into hands that would use them.

The warlord's compound sat in the caldera of a dead volcano, which I considered a trifle on-the-nose but the cultists seemed to find meaningful. They had carved their new god's name into the rock in letters you could read from the valley floor, which struck me as poor operational security for people who had spent three centuries pretending their deity didn't exist.

"Brother Aldous." The warlord herself met me at the gate — Sera Vane, they called her, though she'd been born something forgettable in a fishing village and had renamed herself the way one buys a coat. She was perhaps thirty-five, hard as ship's biscuit, with ash worked so deep into her knuckles it looked like tarnish. "You bring word?"

"I bring more than word." I let my satchel swing from my fingers, just enough weight in it to make her eyes drop. Never hand over the goods first. Anticipation is the true currency of every trade, and I had spent forty years minting it. "I bring the *sequence*."

Her guards flanked me as we crossed the courtyard. Cultists knelt along the walls, foreheads to stone, murmuring prayers that had not been answered since before their grandparents were born — until six weeks ago, when a wayside shrine outside Corrow's Ford answered a widow's prayer with fire and the world tilted. Word of it reached me within four days. I was in Corrow's Ford within nine. That is the difference between me and every other scholar who ever chased divinity: I keep good ledgers of gossip.

Vane led me to a chamber that had once been a temple — you could tell by the alcoves, scraped clean of icons but still shaped like longing. A brazier burned at its center, and above it, suspended in nothing at all, hung the thing she had bought from me piece by piece: a shard of answered prayer, still warm, still humming like a struck bell that refused to finish its note.

I confess, even now, even knowing everything, my mouth went dry looking at it.

"The sequence," Vane said again. Not a question this time. A demand wearing a question's clothes. Warlords are all the same underneath — they think volume is authority. I find that charming, in the way one finds a mastiff charming: right up until it decides your arm is a chew toy.

"*Patience*," I said, and took my time unpacking the satchel. Three folios. My own fair hand, which has been complimented by men who then died owing me money. "Your shard is a miracle, yes. But a single miracle is a crack, and cracks close. The old binding — the Veil, the ledgers, whatever pious name you prefer — is a system of accounts. Every prayer answered draws against a principal that someone, somewhere, has been quietly balancing for three centuries. Your shard worked because the balance has finally slipped. Someone stopped paying attention. Or—" I smiled at her, "—someone started helping."

"And you know how to widen the gap."

"I know where the books are kept." I laid the folios on the table between us, one, two, three, each with the deliberate ceremony of a conjuror. "I know the order in which the seals must be loosened. And I know the name of the bloodline that wrote the original contract — which, you will appreciate, means I also know the name of the bloodline that can unwrite it."

Vane went very still. Warlords are never still when they're thinking about money or murder, but they go still when they hear a number they can't immediately count.

"You're selling us the key," she said slowly, "and keeping the door."

"I'm selling you the *door*," I corrected. "Keys are trivially copied. Doors are geography. Doors are leverage. When your god rises, Brother-Serene Vane, he will need a steward who understands the terms of his tenancy. I am offering myself for the position." I spread my hands. "And, naturally, whoever stands beside me."

Flattery, in my experience, works best when it is structurally accurate. Vane wanted to be indispensable; I made her sound like architecture rather than ornament. She almost smiled. It was the smile of a woman doing arithmetic on my lifespan, and I respected her enormously for it.

"We will want proof," she said.

"You always do." I tapped the middle folio. "Second seal, the shrine at Hollowmere, three days from now, moonrise. Pray there — loudly, specifically, greedily. Ask for something impossible but small. A dead bird singing. A cup filling forever. If the accounts have slipped as far as I believe, you'll get it. If I'm wrong—" I shrugged, magnanimous, "—execute me. I've had an excellent run."

* * *

Here is the confession I make to no living soul, set down only here, in the privacy of my own skull, where even I rarely visit: I did not begin this intending to wake anything.

Forty years ago I was a hedge-witch's apprentice with ink-stained cuffs and a master who believed knowledge was a candle to be cupped in one palm, shielded, hoarded, shared with no one. Aldous, he would say, a secret is a seed. Plant it carelessly and you choke the field. And I believed him. Gods, I *believed* him, the way you believe the ground under your feet, without ever once asking it to justify itself.

Then I watched him let a girl die of a fever he could have cured, because the cure required a name from his grimoire, and the name was *his*, and giving it away would diminish him. He stood at her bedside with the answer in his pocket and told her mother the gods had decided. The gods hadn't decided anything. The gods were asleep. He simply preferred being a small god over being a large man.

So yes. I stole from him. I stole his grimoire and his method and his cowardice, and I spent four decades doing precisely what he did — except I gave the secrets away, sold them cheap, scattered them like bread, because a secret hoarded serves one master and a secret sold serves a market, and markets, whatever else you say of them, are honest about appetite.

I told myself I was a librarian. An emancipator. The man who opened the windows in a stuffy room.

It was only recently — recently meaning the night I decoded the marginalia in a ledger page I'd bought off a corpse, the night I learned that the entire divine prison was, in essence, an act of bookkeeping, sustained by balances maintained by a single hidden bloodline — it was only then that I understood what I had actually been doing all these years. I had not been scattering seeds. I had been *unbalancing the books*. Every spell sold, every secret leaked, every hedge-witch and warlord and desperate widow I'd armed with a fragment of the old truth had been, unknowingly, drawing down the account. I had been making withdrawals against a prison for forty years, and the tellers had no idea the vault was emptying.

Any decent man would have felt horror. I felt — and I record this with clinical honesty, because self-deception is the one vice I have never permitted myself — I felt *vindication*. All those years of petty trafficking, and it had been building toward this. I had been the leak in the dam the whole time. I hadn't known I was engineering a flood, but the flood knew, and it had been waiting for me.

There is a version of this story in which that realization should have stopped me. In which I should have ridden to the Gilded Veil, thrown myself on Theo Winterbourne's mercy, and confessed everything. I want to be clear that I understood that version. I could diagram it. I could teach it.

But consider: if the binding falls regardless — and it falls, now, whether by my hand or Vane's or simple entropy, because you cannot re-tighten a seal once ten thousand people know where the screws are — then the only moral question remaining is *who holds power when the gods wake*. And I have seen what happens when power goes to the pious. The pious build shrines and starve the curious. Power should go to someone who has already done his worst, someone whose appetites are documented, itemized, and *priced*. Power should go to a man with a ledger of his own sins, because such a man at least knows the exchange rate.

That is the argument. It is elegant. I built it myself, stone by stone, over forty years, and it holds weight, and I live inside it comfortably, and if you press your ear to any wall of it you can hear my master's fevered girl breathing.

* * *

Theo Winterbourne.

Let me speak of her plainly, since I am being plain with no one.

She was the worst apprentice I ever had and the best student I ever taught, and both facts infuriate me. Worst, because she had no reverence. I would unveil some principle of the craft — the true grammar of names, the arithmetic beneath prayer — and she would look at it the way an auditor looks at a suspicious column. Not wonder. *Scrutiny*. Where other students saw mystery, Theo saw entries. Where they whispered the old words with bowed heads, she checked them against the receipts.

Best, because scrutiny is the rarest gift. Anyone can feel awe. Almost no one can *reconcile* it.

I knew, of course. From the beginning. You do not spend your life among hedge-witches without learning to smell a bloodline — it comes off them like woodsmoke, faint, wrong, old. The final seal walking around my kitchen stealing my jam. I said nothing. I told myself it was strategy: better to keep the weapon where you can watch it. That was partly true. The rest of the truth is that she was the only company I had enjoyed in twenty years, and I am not so disciplined as I advertise.

When she ran, taking the grimoire, I let her get a full day's lead. I have never told anyone that either. A day's lead is nothing to a tracker of my quality and everything to a conscience trying to pretend it made a choice.

Now she keeps ledgers for a guild of sentimental criminals, and those ledgers — oh, the poetry of it — those ledgers *are the lock*. She has spent years balancing the very accounts that keep the gods asleep, doing with perfect diligence the work her blood was made to do, and she does not know. She thinks she is a clerk. She is a keystone wearing an apron.

And I am about to offer her the world.

* * *

I sent the letter through four intermediaries and a dead drop in a hollow oak, because if you're going to be theatrical, be *correctly* theatrical. No signature. A single line in a cipher only she and I ever used, back when she was sixteen and we communicated in margins because her master — ha — disapproved of teaching girls to write in code.

*You balanced the books beautifully, little auditor. Come see who's been making withdrawals.*

She would come. Not because she trusted me — she trusted me the way one trusts a knife to stay sharp, i.e., with appropriate fear. She would come because Theo Winterbourne could not leave a discrepancy unexamined. It was her flaw and her glory, and I intended to spend it the way I spent everything: wisely, and without apology.

We met at the ruined priory of Saint Verren, midway between her city and my volcano, neutral ground with good sightlines and worse acoustics. She arrived early — she was always early, another thing I never praised aloud because praise makes apprentices comfortable — and stood in the nave with her arms crossed, thinner than I remembered, harder, with a scar along her jaw that I found myself cataloguing and discarding, cataloguing and discarding.

"Aldous." My name in her mouth had lost all its warmth and kept all its history, which is more than most names manage. "You have some nerve."

"Nerve is my second-best asset." I gestured to the broken pew across from mine. "Sit. Please. I promise the lecture is short and the offer is not."

She didn't sit. Of course she didn't sit. "Whatever you're selling, the guild isn't buying."

"Oh, I'm not selling to the guild." I leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let the mask slip just far enough — not to show her a monster, monsters are easy to refuse, but to show her the tired old teacher underneath, the man who once split his last apple with her in a kitchen that smelled of tallow and rain. "I'm selling to *you*, Theo. Absolution and partnership. In that order, if you like."

"There's nothing to absolve."

"There's everything to absolve. That's why it's worth offering." I pulled out the ledger page — the corpse's page, the one with the marginalia — and laid it on the pew between us like a card turned face-up. "You've been keeping the seal alive for years with your careful little entries. Do you want to know what happens to clerks who discover they've been feeding a prison? They either break, or they take ownership. I'm offering ownership. Rule beside me as the gods' stewards — not their jailers, not their slaves, their *stewards*. We hold the keys because someone must, and wouldn't you rather it were two people who understand double-entry than a warlord who thinks miracles are ammunition?"

Something moved behind her eyes. I watched for horror and instead caught — brief, quickly murdered, but unmistakable — *interest*.

There it is, I thought. There is my masterpiece. Not the spells, not the sales, not the unbalanced books. This: making corruption wear stewardship's coat so well that even the honest try it on.

"You're lying," she said at last, but her voice had gone quiet in the way voices do when they're negotiating with themselves.

"About nearly everything," I agreed pleasantly, rising, gathering my folios. "But not about this: the prison is opening, Theo. Within the month, whichever of us moves first decides who's standing when the door swings wide. Think about which of us you'd rather owe."

I left her there in the nave with the truth laid out between us like a body on a slab.

Three days later, my scouts reported movement out of the Gilded Veil — five figures, traveling light, headed east toward the last mountains. Toward the dragon.

Toward the fire that could reforge what I had spent my life unwriting.

Well. I had always known the girl would keep me honest eventually. I simply hadn't planned on her having help.
