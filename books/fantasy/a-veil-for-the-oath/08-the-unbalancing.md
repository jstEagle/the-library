## 8. The Unbalancing

I have always believed that disasters announce themselves. That they arrive with trumpets, with the smell of ozone, with some grand and terrible punctuation that lets a person say, afterward, *I knew. I knew the moment the bells rang wrong.*

The truth is uglier. The truth is that the night the cult breached our vaults, I was thinking about tea.

Specifically, I was thinking about how we were out of it, and whether Bramble had drunk the last of the blackleaf again without telling anyone, because she did that, she drank the last of everything and left the empty tin on the shelf like an apology she couldn't be bothered to write out. I was standing in the counting room with the great ledger open before me, running my thumb down a column of borrowed years — a client had returned eleven of them, early, in suspiciously good condition — and I remember thinking the numbers looked *restless*. That's the only word for it. The ink hadn't dried properly on any page in three days, as if the ledger itself were sweating.

"You're doing the face," Kesh said from the doorway. She was upside down, or her braid was; she'd taken to draping herself over the stair rail like laundry when the guild was quiet, dragon-blood running warm and bored in her veins.

"The face."

"The one where you look like the ledger insulted your mother."

"The ledger has been insulting my mother all week," I said. "Look at this. Eleven years come back early and every one of them is misdated by a day. That doesn't happen. Years don't *drift*."

Kesh came and looked over my shoulder, which required her to drape herself over mine, which required her elbow in my ear. "Maybe time's just tired."

"Time isn't tired. Time is *accounted for*." I tapped the margin, where the old script lived — the marginalia I'd spent too many sleepless nights deciphering, the words that had rearranged my entire understanding of my own existence and which I had told no one about. Every entry balanced here fed the seal. Every seal was fed by a bloodline. My bloodline. I was not a ledger-keeper who had stumbled into the guild's business. I was the business, the last living column of it, and the gods slept because I balanced books.

"Whatever you're not telling me," Kesh said, "it's making you smell like burnt sugar."

"I always smell like burnt sugar."

"Yes, but usually it's *less*."

That was when the lights went out.

Not dimmed. Not guttered. *Out*, all of them at once, the way a held breath ends, and in the dark I heard Vespera Quill's voice rise from the floor below — not alarmed, nothing so useful as alarm, but flat and cold as a bill of sale: "*No.*"

Then the windows came in.

* * *

There were nine of them at first, then more — cultists in ash-grey robes, faces smeared with something that glowed faintly, the way things glow when they've been written on with prayers nobody should know. Aldous had sold them the knowing. I recognized his hand in their wards, his tidy, self-satisfied penmanship written in light across their shields. He had always signed his work. Even betrayal, with Aldous, was a form of vanity.

The Gilded Veil did not fight like soldiers. We fought like people defending a home we'd never admitted was one.

Bramble met the first breach with forged documents — actual documents, flung like knives, each one a contract that bound its reader to terms no mortal could survive. Two cultists read them. I will not describe what the reading cost them. Sister Ondine stood in the center of the hall with her ex-priestess's hands raised, and the dead god she no longer served apparently still remembered her, because the prayer-fire meant for us bent around her like water around a stone. Kesh burned. That's the simplest way to put it. She went up the stairs like a struck match and came down wreathed in it, laughing, and the laughing was the frightening part.

And Vespera Quill stood at the vault door with the ledgers stacked in her arms like a newborn she intended to carry out of a fire, and she said, in that voice that had priced impossible things for longer than I'd been alive:

"They are not for sale. They are not for *taking*. Get out of my house."

The cultists' leader — a tall figure whose glowing sigils I now saw covered not just his face but his throat, his wrists, everywhere a pulse lived — smiled at her. I heard him say, gently, almost kindly: "We don't want the ledgers, mistress. We want what the ledgers *keep*."

He threw something. It wasn't a knife or a spell. It was a name — an unspoken one, one of ours, stolen from inventory, and when it landed at Vespera's feet it spoke itself aloud in a voice like tearing silk.

Vespera dropped the ledgers to cover her ears. That was the opening. That was the whole war, decided in the half second it took a woman who loved her books more than her balance to choose between protecting them and protecting herself.

She chose the books. Of course she chose the books.

The blast took her off her feet and into the vault door, and I heard the sound her body made against old gold-laminated oak, and I made a decision in the space between one heartbeat and the next that I still cannot call brave because it happened faster than thought: I threw myself over the ledgers where she'd dropped them, and I screamed at Kesh — "*Get her OUT*" — and then, because the leader was reaching past me for the oldest volume, the one with the marginalia, the one with my family's handwriting in it —

I opened it.

Right there, on the burning floor, with the vault breached and Vespera bleeding and Bramble screaming my name, I opened the First Ledger to the last sealed page, and I let my blood do what it had been trained since before my birth to do.

The page drank from my palm. The seal answered. And the whole building *shook* with a power that did not come from me but through me, and every cultist in the hall went to their knees as if gravity had doubled out of respect.

The leader looked at me — really looked, for the first time — and his smile changed. It stopped being patient. It became *hungry*.

"There you are," he said softly. "The final seal. Aldous didn't tell us you were a *she*."

* * *

They retreated. That's the thing I keep turning over — they *retreated*, orderly as clerks at closing time, once the leader had seen what I was. They took nothing from the vaults. They didn't need to. They'd come for confirmation, and they'd gotten it, written in my own blood across a page three hundred years old.

Afterward there was silence, and smoke, and Vespera alive — barely, ribs broken, one lung filling — carried up to the sickroom while Ondine worked over her with the last prayers she had sworn never to use again. There was Bramble, methodically salvaging scorched contracts with shaking hands. And there was me, sitting on the floor of the ruined hall with a cut palm and a ledger open in my lap, waiting for the question I knew was coming.

It came from Bramble, quietly, terribly: "Theo. What did you just *do*?"

I could have lied. I'm good at lying; I was raised by a man who treated truth as a currency to be debased. But Vespera was upstairs drowning in her own blood because she stood in front of books I now understood were, in a sense, *me*, and lying felt like spending her.

"My blood wrote the binding," I said. "My family. Generations back. Every entry in those ledgers feeds the seal that keeps the old gods asleep. I've known since before the raid on the safehouse. Since the grimoire. I've known for *weeks*."

The silence that followed had weight. Bramble set down the contract she was holding very carefully, the way you set down something you might later throw.

"Weeks," she repeated.

"I hid it because—" Because duty. Because protection. Because every lie I told myself had sounded so reasonable at the time. "Because if Aldous learned, he'd sell it. Because if the cult learned, they'd come. Because I thought if I carried it alone, it would hurt fewer people."

"You thought," Bramble said, and her voice cracked down the middle, "that we were *fewer people*."

"Bramble—"

"No. You watched me forge a dead bishop's signature. You watched Ondine give up her god. You watched Kesh nearly die getting us out of the safehouse, and the whole time you were sitting on the one secret that explains why any of it is happening, and you didn't— you didn't *trust* us."

"I trusted you not to *die*," I said, and heard how small it sounded. How true, and how useless.

Ondine came down the stairs then, grey-faced, smelling of prayer and iron. She had heard, evidently, enough. She looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for judgment from a woman who had actually given something up, who knew exactly what sacrifice weighed, who had every right to despise a coward playing at martyrdom.

Instead she sat down on the floor beside me. Her skirts pooled in the ash. She took my wounded hand, turned it over, and examined the cut the ledger had drunk from.

"In my order," she said, "we had a saying. *A burden hoarded is a burden worshipped.*" She pressed her thumb, gently, over the cut. "You weren't protecting us, Theo. You were adoring your own sacrifice. It's a very common heresy."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Fairness ended tonight when Vespera's ribs broke. But." She squeezed my hand once, hard, and let go. "You also threw yourself over the ledgers. You bled for them. Whatever else you are, you're ours, and being ours means being angry with you *in the house*, not outside it."

Bramble made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob or both. "You're both unbearable."

"Yes," said Ondine.

"Yes," I said, and discovered I was crying, which was inconvenient, and that Bramble had sat down on my other side, which was worse, and that none of us moved for a long time, three women on a smoking floor holding onto each other like the building might try to take even that away.

Upstairs, Vespera breathed shallowly but breathing. Outside, the city pretended at normalcy. And somewhere far off, in the direction of the shrine-mountain, I felt the seal I had just flexed begin to *ache* — a deep, tectonic soreness, like a muscle torn.

Kesh appeared in the doorway, singed and soot-black and wearing the expression of someone about to deliver news no one wants. "So. Bad news and worse news."

"Of course there is," I said.

"Bad news: Ember-that-Was is dying faster than we thought. If we want her fire to reforge anything, we leave within days." Kesh crouched, and her strange amber eyes fixed on mine. "Worse news: there's a message nailed to our door. Wax seal I've never seen. Inside, it says — and I quote — '*Come to the mountain, little sister. Bring the pen. I'll bring absolution.*'"

Aldous.

He knew. He had always known, perhaps, or known enough, and now he was inviting me to the place where gods slept and dragons died, as if this were all a family reunion he'd been planning for years.

Ondine squeezed my hand again. Bramble stood, brushed ash off her skirts, and said, in a voice that brokered no argument:

"Then we go together. All of us. And this time, Theo, you tell us *everything*."

I looked down at the First Ledger in my lap, at my family's handwriting in the margins, at the empty space on the last page where a new name could be written.

"All of it," I promised. "Starting with the part where I think I'm supposed to end."

* * *

We left at dawn, five of us and a dragon-courier's worth of supplies, and behind us the Gilded Veil stood gutted but standing, like its mistress. I did not look back. Or rather — I looked back exactly once, and what I felt was not grief but accounting: a debt incurred, a debt recorded, a debt I fully intended to collect.

The mountain waited. So, apparently, did my brother-in-all-but-blood with his tidy offers of absolution.

Let him bring absolution. I'd bring the pen.
