## 9. The Last Entry

*Theo*

The shrine-mountain did not look shattered so much as *unwritten*. That was the only way I could describe it, and I am a woman who has described bottled hurricanes in three columns and a footnote. The peak of Mount Caelum had been a place of worship once — terraces of white stone, pilgrim roads worn smooth by centuries of knees. Now it was a place of subtraction. Whole staircases simply weren't there anymore. Not crumbled. *Absent*, the way an entry is absent after you strike it from a ledger.

Aldous had struck a great deal of the mountain.

He stood at its heart, at what remained of the old high altar, and even from this distance — two terraces down, my boots skidding on gravel that remembered being marble — I could see him clearly, because he wanted to be seen. My old master had always believed that presentation was nine-tenths of power. He wore white now, which I found obscene. Aldous in white, glowing faintly with borrowed divinity like a man who'd stolen his neighbor's sunlight and hadn't yet learned to shade his windows.

"Theo!" His voice carried without effort, which meant effort was being made somewhere, by something, and that something was being billed for it. "You brought friends! How touching. How *expensive*."

"He's monologuing," Bramble muttered beside me. She had a forger's fingers and a crossbow she'd never once cleaned, and both were trembling. "Why do they always monologue?"

"Because he rehearsed," Sister Ondine said quietly. Her ex-priestess hands were folded, her knuckles white as votive candles. "He has been rehearsing this since before Theo was born."

Kesh said nothing. Kesh was looking up — not at Aldous but past him, past the altar, to where the air itself had begun to peel. Through the gap where the prison-seal was fraying, I could see color that had no business existing: a red so old it predated fire, moving like something turning over in a sleep three hundred years too long.

The first god was stirring.

And here is the thing I will confess only here, in the privacy of my own skull, where I keep all my best accounts: some traitorous corner of me *sang*. Three hundred years of dead prayers, and I was standing close enough to hear one breathe. The wonder I had been drowning since the wayside shrine — since the widow's hour of her drowned husband's voice, since every impossible entry I'd ever balanced — rose up in me like floodwater.

I drowned it. There would be time for wonder later, if there was a later. Right now there was arithmetic.

Because that was the secret, wasn't it? Aldous thought the grimoire was a spellbook. The cult thought it was scripture. But spells are just debits, and scripture is just terms of service, and underneath all of it, the oldest ledger in the world ran on the same principle as mine in the guild vault: *every entry must be balanced.*

The gods slept because someone paid their debt. Someone wrote themselves into the margin and said: *charge it to me instead.* And my bloodline had written the original binding, and every column I had ever balanced at the Gilded Veil had fed the seal, and now the seal was broken, and there was exactly one currency left that could close the account.

Me.

"Stay behind me," I told the others, which was ridiculous, because I had never been behind anyone in my life; I was a ledger-keeper, I stood at desks. But they heard what I actually meant, because they always did — the infuriating, miraculous thing about family is that they audit you whether you want them to or not.

Ondine's hand caught my sleeve. "Theo Winterbourne. Whatever you are about to write—"

"You don't know what I'm about to write."

"I have read your ledgers for two years." Her voice was gentle and terrible, like absolution. "You write your sacrifices in the smallest possible hand."

* * *

*Kesh*

My grandmother used to say dragon-blood runs thin in a family the way wine runs thin at the bottom of the barrel — still red, still honest, but mostly memory. She was wrong. It doesn't run thin. It runs *deep*, down where the heat lives, and it has been screaming at me since we started climbing this broken mountain.

Ember-that-Was flew ahead of us, or what passes for flying when you are older than most nations and your wings have more holes than a forger's alibi. The last dragon. The only fire left in the world hot enough to reforge a broken oath. And she was dying — I could feel it through my blood, a banked-fire feeling, coals going gray at the edges. Every wingbeat cost her something she could not earn back.

We crested the terrace below the altar and I saw him properly for the first time. Aldous. Theo's old master. The warlord-cult's new godling. He stood in the wound in the world with his arms open like a man greeting a returning fleet, and behind him the first god turned in its sleep, and the turning made my teeth ache and my scales — the ones I pretend I don't have, under my collar — itch like sunburn.

"You're the courier," Aldous called down, and his eyes found me with horrible accuracy. "Dragon-blooded. Oh, that's *delicious*. Did you know your line was bred as a fuse? Little walking matches, so the old stewards could light the prison's lamps when they guttered. You're not a person, girl. You're an *expense*."

Here is what nobody tells you about rage: it balances beautifully. Every insult is a debit. Every cruelty accrues interest. And I had spent twenty years as a courier learning exactly how far a body can travel between one heartbeat and the next.

But Ember landed before I could spend anything.

She came down onto the shattered altar terrace like a mountain deciding to be an animal again, and the impact rang through the whole unwritten peak, and Aldous — glowing, floating, insufferable — actually took a step back. Because whatever else he had bought and sold and stolen, he had never stood next to a dragon, and there are things no ledger can prepare you for.

"*Little steward's whelp,*" Ember said, and her voice was a landslide with opinions. Her blind milky eyes found Theo anyway. Dragons see heat, and Theo was burning — I could see it myself, the seal-light rising in her veins like dawn coming up through skin.

"No," Theo whispered. She had the grimoire out, and the oldest ledger, and a pen. Ondine's knife-sharpener pen, the one that writes in anything. "*No,*" Theo said again, louder, to the dragon, to the god, to the whole collapsing account of the world. "She's not spending herself. Find another way."

"There is no other way, child," Ember said, almost kindly. Smoke wept from her nostrils, and I understood suddenly that dragons weep smoke when the grief is too large for fire. "There is only the last page, and who signs it."

* * *

*Theo*

Aldous tried to stop us, naturally. He threw everything he had — borrowed miracles, purchased secrets, the cult chanting somewhere below in voices that hurt the geometry. A pillar of white fire took the terrace where Bramble stood, and Bramble rolled clear, swore with a forger's fluency, and put a bolt through Aldous's shoulder that shouldn't have worked against a man glowing with divinity.

It worked because Aldous was still, at the root of himself, an accountant of power. And an accountant knows: nothing borrowed is free. Every miracle he spent opened a hole somewhere else. My bolt-sister had found the hole.

"You cannot reforge the oath alone!" Aldous screamed, bleeding light from his shoulder. "One vessel! One life! That is the *price*, girl, that is the ancient term — the seal demands a single soul to hold it, that is why your ancestors wrote it so! One bloodline, one burden, one grave!"

"Yes," I said, stepping forward, opening the ledger to the last clean page. "That's what it says."

I looked back at them once — Bramble with her ruined crossbow, Ondine with her folded priestess hands finally unfolded and open, Kesh with fire in her eyes and tears she'd deny later, Ember with her dying furnace-heart already leaning toward the torn sky. My family. The word still fit strangely in my mouth, like a shoe I kept expecting to pinch.

Then I wrote.

*I, Theo Winterbourne, ledger-keeper of the Gilded Veil, last of the binding line, offer this account: my name, my years, my breath, held in trust against the sleeping debt. Let the gods charge their waking to me. Let the veil be my skin. Balance entered. Seal set. Signed—*

Ember breathed.

Her final fire went through the page like sunrise goes through glass, and I felt the oath take hold of the words, felt it begin to wind around me, felt the enormous lonely weight of three hundred years settle onto my shoulders the way it had settled on every ancestor whose marginalia I'd ever deciphered —

— and then the pen was pulled from my fingers.

Not taken. *Seized.* Bramble's ink-stained hand closed over mine. Then Ondine's, cool and steady. Then Kesh's, hot as a stove-door. Ember lowered her great scarred head until her brow touched our knotted fists, and her last flame poured not into the page but into *us*, five together, splitting like light through a prism.

"Amendment," Bramble said, grinning through tears, writing in her terrible scrawl beneath my signature. "*Joint account.*"

"Five signatories," Ondine murmured, adding her name in a priestess's careful script. "Let duty be shared among the keepers, and shared duty corrupt no one."

"Add the dragon," Kesh said fiercely. "She's earned a signature. Write her *in*."

And I — I who had planned to vanish into a margin like a corrected error — I watched my sacrifice become a covenant, my solitary grave become a partnership, and the weight that should have crushed one spine settled across five and became, impossibly, bearable. The oath rewove itself around us. The tear in the sky began to close, the first god sighing back into sleep like a storm settling beyond the horizon.

Below us, Aldous screamed — not in defeat, I realized, but in *recalculation*. Five vessels. A distributed seal. Terms the original binding had never imagined.

"That's not how it works," he shrieked, clawing at his borrowed light as the prison sealed and his supply lines of stolen divinity began, entry by entry, to come due. "That is *not how the books work*—"

"Aldous," I called down, and I smiled, and it felt wonderful, "you really should have audited your assumptions."

The last thing I saw before the closing light swallowed the altar was his face — and it was not despair.

It was a man reading fine print he'd missed, and finding, in the margin, one final clause.

His lips moved. I couldn't hear the words.

But I knew, with a ledger-keeper's certainty, that whatever he'd just realized — the account between us was not yet closed.
