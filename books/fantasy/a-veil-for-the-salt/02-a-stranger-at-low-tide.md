## 2. A Stranger at Low Tide

The seventh day came in gray and smelling of iron.

Noor went down to the harbor steps anyway, because she had promised herself she would, and because promises made to oneself were the only debts a clerk could settle without paperwork. The fog had not lifted overnight; it had only thinned enough to let the sun through as a pale coin behind gauze, and the water below the Saltgate Guildhall lapped against the stone with the sound of something patient counting.

She brought her ledger with her. Habit. She had carried a ledger since she was nine years old, since the year that lived in no record anywhere — the missing year, the one the guild's own books skipped like a scratched groove in a phonograph cylinder. She had learned young that if you wrote things down, they could not be quietly taken from you. Or so she had believed, once, before she understood that ink could be edited too.

The harbor steps descended forty-two stones into the tide, worn hollow in the middle by three centuries of boots. At low water they exposed a shelf of green-slicked rock where the fishwives gutted their catch and the smugglers — though the guild pretended otherwise — moved their small cargo of impossible things: bottled tides, borrowed years, love letters written in languages that did not exist until they were read aloud.

Noor sat on the thirty-first step, opened her ledger across her knees, and began reconciling yesterday's entries against the guild's master copy, which she kept in duplicate because the master copy lied.

It always lied a little. A tide sold twice. A year of a fisherman's life debited without his signature. Small discrepancies, each one explicable, each one deniable, and together — if you laid them end to end over decades, as Noor had done in the privacy of her own hand — together they formed a pattern like teeth marks.

She was halfway through the column for bottled fog when she heard the sound.

Not a sound, exactly. An absence. The gulls stopped. The water's patient counting faltered. And then, from the fog out past the last step, came a wet dragging noise, rhythmic and terrible, like something learning again how to crawl.

Noor stood. Her hand found the banister stone, cold and furred with salt-bloom.

At first she thought it was driftwood. Then the fog peeled back a hand's width, and she saw it was a man.

He was wedged against the lowest step, half in the water, and he had been in the water long enough for it to have opinions about him. His skin was burned — not sunburned, not scalded, but *salt*-burned, the flesh gone white and cracked in patterns like dried riverbeds, like the mudflats at the edge of the world where nothing grew because nothing could hold its shape. His clothes had been fine once. Courier's grey, guild-cut, though no guild Noor knew of used that particular shade of grey, which seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it.

One arm dragged ahead of him. The other was pressed flat against his chest, holding something there.

"Help," he said, or tried to. What came out was mostly water. He coughed, and what he coughed up glittered on the stone between them — not seawater. Brine so dense it caught the pale coin of the sun and threw it back in tiny cruel sparks.

Noor should have called for the harbormaster. She should have rung the bell at the top of the steps, the one wired to the guildhall's interior, the one that would bring enforcers in their oiled coats within four minutes. That was procedure. Procedure was the spine of everything she was.

Instead she went down the steps to him.

Her shoes were ruined immediately; she registered this distantly, the way one registers weather. She knelt on the thirty-ninth step, above the reach of the water, and turned him carefully onto his side so he could breathe.

Up close, the salt-burn was worse. It wasn't only his skin. His eyes were bloodshot to the edges, and when he focused on her face, she saw that his lashes were crusted white, and that beneath the crust his eyelids were cracked too, weeping a thin brine that steamed faintly in the cold air. Whatever sea he had crossed, it had not been water all the way down. It had been *salt* — salt as a force, salt as an appetite.

"You're guild," he rasped. His voice sounded like paper being torn slowly.

"I keep the ledgers," Noor said. "Second Reconciliation Office. Who—"

"The ledgers." Something moved through his ruined face that might have been relief or might have been despair; at that distance they looked the same. "Good. Good. Then you can read it."

His hand came off his chest. In his grip, white-knuckled even now, even dying, was a roll of heavy paper sealed in wax — black wax, shot through with threads of gold that caught the light and held it wrong, the way certain shells hold the light wrong, showing you colors from depths where no color lives.

He pushed it into her hands.

Noor took it before she decided to. That was the thing she would turn over and over later, in the sleepless dark: that her hands had accepted it the way her hands accepted anything — automatically, clerkishly, as if all the world were correspondence and she its unwilling addressee. The wax was warm. Warmer than a living man's skin. Warmer than it had any right to be, pulled from cold seawater.

"Read it," he said again. "Before they come. Before—" A cough racked him, and more of the glittering brine came up, and this time there was a thread of red in it, thin as a signature. "The crown is hungry again."

The words landed in Noor like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash and heard none.

"What crown?" she said. "There's no crown. There's the gate, and the gate is sealed, and the seal is maintained, I maintain it myself, the wards are checked every—"

"Every ninth day." He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that smile, cracked across the salt-burned mouth. "I know your handwriting, keeper. I've been reading your ward-signatures for eleven years. You count better than any of them. That's why it has to be you."

"I'm not a keeper. I'm a clerk. Second Reconciliation. There are no keepers anymore, the office was dissolved in my grandmother's time—"

"It was never dissolved." His hand closed around her wrist. His grip should have been nothing — he weighed less than the ledger on her knees, surely, less than the drowned man he resembled — but it closed like a shackle, and the cold of it went straight through her sleeve into the bone. "It was renamed. Renamed and renamed until the name wore thin and they hung a new word on the hook. Clerk. Warden. Auditor. But under every name, the same work. Someone keeps the gate. Someone pays."

"*Pays*," Noor repeated, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"Every thirteenth turn of the moon." His eyes were losing focus now, sliding off her face toward the fog, toward something in the fog she could not see. "It wakes. It reaches. And someone must stand at the door and give it what it asks, or it takes what it wants. Three hundred years of some poor soul standing at that door with an open ledger, saying *take from me instead*. Do you know what it takes?"

"No," Noor said. "I don't know anything about—"

"Memory." The word came out almost gently, the way one delivers a diagnosis. "It eats memory. The guild calls it tribute. The guild calls it many things." His grip tightened. "Ask yourself, clerk. Ask yourself why there is a year missing from your books. Your books. The ones you keep so carefully. The ones nobody is allowed to touch."

The well inside her filled with cold.

Because she had asked herself that. She had asked it a hundred times, a thousand, lying awake with her duplicates spread around her like a fortress of paper: *where did the year go?* Age nine to ten, gone, not blank but absent, a hole shaped exactly like a year, and everyone — her mother, her teachers, the guild archivists she'd begged at fifteen — everyone had looked at her with kind bafflement and said *what year?*

"You knew," she whispered. "You've never met me. How do you know about my—"

"I don't know you." His head sagged; she caught it, her palm against the awful cracked skin of his cheek. "But I know the pattern. I've carried this map through six ports and two deaths' worth of ocean to find the one person whose books would show the wound. The crown marked you long ago, keeper. It's been feeding on you in installments since you were a child. Small payments. They told you they were nothing. They tell every keeper they're nothing."

Footsteps above. On the thirty-first step, where she had been sitting.

Noor's whole body went still in a way that had nothing to do with calm. The footsteps were unhurried. Professional. And they had arrived — she counted backward through the morning, through the fog, through the empty harbor where the fishwives were conspicuously absent today, where the smugglers' boats were conspicuously elsewhere — far too fast. She had not rung any bell. She had not called anyone. The courier had been crawling out of the water for perhaps four minutes.

Enforcers took four minutes to arrive *from the bell.*

Two of them came down the steps side by side, oiled coats dark as spilled ink, faces the guild hired for their forgettability. Noor knew the taller one — Vessic, who signed the requisitions she audited, whose handwriting leaned left like a man perpetually dodging blame.

"Clerk Oyelaran." Vessic's voice was mild. Mildness, in an enforcer, was worse than shouting. "Step away from the body."

"He's not dead yet."

"That's not what I said." Vessic's gaze touched the map in her hands, the black wax, the gold-threaded seal, and something flickered behind his forgettable face — hunger, or fear, or the place where those two things share a border. "That item is guild property, recovered from an unauthorized vessel. Hand it over and return to your office. There will be an audit notice regarding your presence here."

"There will be an audit notice," Noor repeated slowly, "regarding my presence at my own harbor."

"Regarding irregularities." Vessic extended one gloved hand. "The map, please."

The courier made a sound. It took Noor a moment to understand it was laughter — a dry, cracking sound, like ice on a pond giving way.

"They always come fast," he rasped, to her, to the fog, to no one. "Always. Fast as guilt. Ask them, clerk. Ask them how they knew where to find me. Ask them who told the sea where to spit me ashore." His fingers found hers where they gripped the map, and squeezed, and his eyes came back into focus one final time with a ferocity that burned through the salt-crust like a lamp behind frosted glass. "They didn't lose me. They *sent* me. And when I ran, they let the water do their work. But the map chose you. Wax doesn't lie. Wax knows hands."

"Last chance, clerk." Vessic's glove was still open. Behind him, the second enforcer had drifted, casually, terribly, to block the top of the steps.

And Noor Oyelaran, who had spent her whole life being reasonable, being precise, being the woman who filed things in their proper places — Noor looked at the open glove, and at the dying man, and at the fog beyond the harbor where something patient was counting, and she did the first unreasonable thing of her adult life.

She stood, and she stepped back, down, onto the thirty-ninth step, putting the water between herself and the enforcers, and she said, "Requisition number 4-4-1-9, filed by Enforcer Vessic, third of last month: *one recovered document, provenance withheld pending review.* Withheld, Vessic. By whom? Because I audit the withholding log, and there is no entry above your signature. Which means someone gave you this order verbally. Which means it isn't procedure. Which means if I hand you this map, I am handing it to a theft."

The silence lasted three heartbeats.

Vessic's face did something complicated and small. "Clerk. You are making a reportable error."

"I make reports for a living," Noor said. Her heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt. The map's wax was warm against her palm, warmer now, almost pulsing, like a second heartbeat answering hers. "Come take it yourself, then. Come down onto the tide-stones. Unless the guild's orders specify the map must be surrendered *dry*."

Another silence. Longer. The second enforcer shifted his weight, and the tide-stones shifted theirs beneath him, green and treacherous, and Noor saw him do the arithmetic every harbor-child learned before letters: wet stone, low rail, deep water, and a clerk who had grown up on these steps and had not.

"Note her insubordination," Vessic said finally, to the other one, without taking his eyes off her. "Audit notice will follow. Full reconciliation of her office. Everything." And there — just for a moment, just before he turned — his mildness slipped, and Noor saw underneath it, and underneath it was not anger. Underneath it was *pity*, which frightened her more than anger ever could.

They climbed back up into the fog. Their footsteps faded. Somewhere above, a bell rang the quarter-hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

Noor knelt back down.

The courier's breathing had gone shallow and rapid, a bird's rhythm, a rhythm with very little time left in it. His lips were moving. She bent close.

"...name," he was whispering. "Have to... the title. It matters. The door won't..." A breath. Another. "Listen. Keeper Unbound."

"I told you, I'm not—"

"Not yet." His hand rose — trembling, weightless now, the shackle-strength gone — and his fingertips brushed the back of the hand that held the map, and where they touched, the salt-burned skin left a mark: faint, white, cool, a print like frost that sank into her skin and vanished. "The wax chose. The wax doesn't lie. When the door asks its question — and it will ask, oh, it will ask — you answer with the title. Not the guild's names. Not clerk, not warden, not auditor. Those are the names they built to *pay* the door. Yours is the name that opens it. Keeper Unbound. Say it."

"No."

"Say it, so the wax hears you."

And because he was dying, and because refusing a dying man his last request was a debt Noor did not know how to carry, she bowed her head over the black-waxed roll in her hands and said, quietly, into the fog and the cold and the patient counting of the water:

"Keeper Unbound."

The wax flared.

Not fire — light, gold-threaded light running through the black like veins, one pulse, two, and then it settled, dimmed, and went on beating its slow warm pulse against her palm as if nothing had happened. As if it had merely noted her signature. Filed it. Opened an account.

The courier exhaled. It was a long exhale, the kind that empties.

"Good," he said. The word was barely air. "Now run, keeper. They'll come back with more than gloves. And when the nightmares start — and they will start — listen anyway. He's not what they say he is. He's never been what they say he—"

The sentence never finished. The fog finished it for him, closing over the harbor steps, over the green stones, over the small still figure with the salt-white face, and the water resumed its counting, patient, patient, one wave and another wave, as if tallying.

Noor stayed kneeling for a long moment. Long enough for the tide to lick the toe of her ruined shoe. Long enough to be sure.

Then she stood, and folded the map into her coat, against her breast, where its warmth beat like a stolen heart, and she climbed the forty-two steps with her legs shaking and her clerk's mind already, helplessly, horribly, doing what it always did:

Counting.

Four minutes for enforcers from the bell. No bell rung. Fishwives absent. Smugglers gone. A dying man who knew her ward-signatures by sight and her missing year by name. A title that had never appeared in any guild record she had ever audited — and she had audited all of them — and yet the moment she spoke it, the wax had known her the way a lock knows its key.

Which meant the records were wrong. All of them. Deliberately. Which meant the discrepancy was not a discrepancy at all but a design, three centuries wide, and she had spent her life balancing the books of a lie.

At the top of the steps, the fog was lifting at last, and the Guildhall of Saltgate stood above the harbor in its grey permanence, its windows catching the pale sun, its doors open for the day's business, its towers flying the honest scales of trade.

Noor looked at it for a long time.

Then she turned her collar up, put her head down, and walked — not toward the hall, not yet, not with the map warm against her heart and the words *the crown is hungry again* ringing in her ears — toward the archive annex, where the old records slept, where the dissolved office of the Keepers would be filed if it existed anywhere at all.

Behind her, at the bottom of the steps, the harbormaster's men were just arriving to collect the body, and finding, with professional puzzlement, that the tide had already taken it.

And in her coat, against her skin, the black wax pulsed on — patient, warm, and hungry in a way she could not yet name — keeping its own count, in its own ledger, under a title she had spoken only once, and could not unsay.
