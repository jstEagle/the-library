## 4. Thieves Who Owe the Dead

The letter came wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a knot Ishra had taught a boy to tie twenty years ago, on a dock in Corvess, when his hands were shaking too badly to hold a line.

She knew it before she opened it. She knew it the way you know a storm by the smell of the air — not the shape of it, not the hour of its coming, only that something had turned and would not turn back.

She sat in the stern cabin of the *Vagrant Tide* with the letter unopened in her lap for the length of one bell, then two. Around her the ship creaked through her ordinary business: Denni arguing with Sefa about the price of pitch in Tarrow's Reach, the groan of cargo shifting, old Brannic's cough from the fo'c'sle like a metronome counting down nothing in particular. An ordinary morning. The last one, though she didn't know that yet.

When she finally broke the seal, the paper inside was salt-stained to the color of old bone, and the handwriting was her friend's — cramped, left-leaning, the hand of a man who wrote while running.

*Ish. If you're reading this I'm dead or close enough. Don't waste tears on the close enough.*

*They found me at Saltgate. The guild found me. I got the map out but I couldn't carry it far. There's a clerk there — guild ledger-keeper, name of Noor Oyelaran. She keeps the wards on the gate and doesn't know what she keeps them FOR. I put the map in her hands because she was the only one down there who looked like she still asked questions. Trust her or don't. But get to her before the guild does, because the guild will kill her for holding it and the crown will do worse if they don't.*

*The moon's turning, Ish. Thirteenth turn. You remember what I told you about the thirteenth turn, that night off the Teeth when we were both too drunk to lie. It's real. It's all real. I spent my whole life carrying other people's secrets and this is the one that killed me, so it's yours now, and I'm sorry, and thank you, and burn this after.*

*You still owe me a drink. Pay it to her.*

*— Vell*

Ishra read it three times. Then she did what captains do, which is stand up, wash her face, and go give orders as if her hands were steady.

* * *

Vellam Orr had been dead to her twice before. Once when he'd taken the courier's oath and gone gray-cloaked and lawful, which for Vell had been less a career than a disguise he wore so well even he stopped noticing it was a disguise. And once, six years ago, when he'd walked off her ship at Port Amity without goodbye and hadn't written in four years — long enough that she'd started rehearsing the speech she'd give him when he finally showed up again, the speech about how friends don't just vanish, the speech that was really just hurt wearing armor.

He'd never gotten the speech. Now he never would.

She found herself composing it anyway, that evening, standing at the rail watching the water go dark. *You absolute bastard. You couldn't have written sooner? You couldn't have—* And then the rest of it, which had no words, only the memory of a boy on a dock in Corvess with shaking hands, and a man on her quarterdeck laughing so hard at his own terrible jokes that Denni fell out of the rigging, and the night off the Teeth when they'd both been too drunk to lie and he'd told her about a crown, a king, a gate, a debt older than any nation either of them had ever smuggled for — and she'd laughed and said, *Vell, you tell prettier lies asleep than most people do awake,* and he'd gone very quiet and said, *I wish to every salt god that were true.*

She'd thought about that quiet more than once since. She'd never once thought it meant *believe me.*

"Captain." Denni's voice behind her, careful. He always came careful when she stood at the rail too long. "Sefa says weather's fair for the Reach run, tide turns at second bell, and Brannic wants to know if we're loading the Tarrow wool or letting it rot on the pier another day."

"We're not loading the wool."

A pause. "We're... not?"

"Tarrow's south. We're going north." She turned. Denni stood in the lamplight with his forearms crossed and his face doing the thing it did when he was working out whether an argument was worth the bruises. He was twenty-three and had been forged entirely out of stubbornness and ink stains; he'd been forging documents since he was fourteen and forging excuses since he was nine, and he could read her moods the way he read seals — quickly, and looking for tampering.

"North," he said. "North is Saltgate."

"North is Saltgate."

"Saltgate is the Guildhall city. Saltgate is where law-abiding ships go to be bored and law-breaking ships go to hang." His eyes narrowed. "Captain, what's happened?"

She weighed it. She'd kept the crew's secrets the way she kept her charts — accurate, current, and shown to no one who didn't need them. But Vell had been theirs too. Vell had pulled Denni out of a burning customs house in Corvess with his eyebrows half-singed off and told him afterward, with perfect gravity, that arson was a respectable trade if you diversified. Vell had taught Sefa to read. Vell had carried Brannic two miles on his back when the fever took him, and sat up three nights doing it.

"He's dead," she said.

Denni didn't ask who. She watched him understand anyway — watched it land on him in stages, like ballast going into a hold, each piece heavier than the last. "Vell," he said finally. "Vellam Orr is dead."

"Died at Saltgate. Salt-burned. Washed up at the harbor steps." She heard her own voice go flat and clerical and hated it. Flat was how she kept from breaking. "He got a message to me first. It's in my cabin if anyone wants to read it, and everyone will want to read it, so come below and we'll do this properly instead of you all pretending you weren't listening at the door."

"They were listening at the door," Denni confirmed. "All of them. Since the letter came. Sefa's been standing there so long her feet are asleep."

"Then wake her up and bring her in."

* * *

They read the letter around her chart table by lamplight, passing it hand to hand, and Ishra watched her crew grieve the way families grieve — badly, loudly, in all the wrong order. Sefa cried without noise, which was worse somehow, tears just falling off her chin onto the deck while she stared at nothing. Brannic read it twice, folded it with enormous care, and said, "He owed me a rematch at cards," in a voice like gravel underwater, and then went up on deck alone. Denni read it last and longest, and when he finished he said, "Keeper Unbound. That's what he named her? The clerk?" and Ishra nodded, and Denni said, "That's not a title. That's a prophecy with a job description," and then he also went quiet, which for Denni was practically a funeral rite.

"The map," Sefa said at last, scrubbing her face with her sleeve. "The guild has the clerk, the clerk has the map, and the guild kills people who know things. So the girl's already dead, isn't she? Or will be by the time we get there."

"Not yet." Ishra spread the chart north. "Vell chose her for a reason. He wasn't sentimental — gods know he tried to be and failed — but he was *shrewd*. If he put that map in her hands, it's because he judged she'd hold it and live. Maybe he judged right. Either way, we'll know when we get there."

"And if the guild's already taken her?"

"Then we take her back."

Brannic had come silently back down the companionway and stood filling the doorway like weather. "Captain," he rumbled, "that's the Guildhall. That's not a customs house with bad locks. That's the spine of the whole rotten thing."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because I'm hearing *we sail into the one port on this coast where we cannot run, cannot bribe, and cannot bluff our way out*, and I'm hearing it from a woman who taught me that the first rule of this life is *never steal anything you can't carry.*"

Ishra looked at him. Old Brannic, sixty years of salt in his beard, who had buried more crews than he could count and loved this one with the specific ferocity of a man who refused to bury another.

"She can't carry it either," Ishra said. "Whatever she's holding. Whatever Vell made her. She's one clerk with a satchel, Brannic, and the entire weight of the guild coming down on her, and she doesn't even know why yet. Vell spent his whole life paying other people's debts quietly and dying for it, and the last thing he ever did was hand his worst debt to a stranger because he thought she might survive it." She tapped the chart, on Saltgate, on the harbor steps, on the X of it. "We owe him. All of us. Every one of us in this room is alive because of that man, and most of us never even said thank you, because he made it impossible to catch him at it. Well. The debt's come due, and he can't collect. So we collect for him."

Silence. The kind of silence a crew makes when a decision has already been made and everyone is just waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to say it aloud.

Denni said it. "Blood-debt," he said. "Proper one. Witnessed."

"Witnessed," Ishra agreed. "Vell bled on my deck twice and I bled on his once, and that's a ledger no honest person wants to hold, but we hold it. We sail on the morning tide."

Sefa was already smiling through the tear-tracks, that fierce crooked smile she saved for the worst ideas. Brannic sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to the inevitable, which in Brannic's case meant he'd been planning the approach to Saltgate Harbor in his head since the word *north*. And Denni — Denni had gone still over the letter, his finger resting on one line, reading it again.

"*Trust her or don't,*" he murmured. "He never wrote that about anybody. Not once in eleven years of letters. Vell trusted his legs and his routes and nobody else." He looked up. "Captain, whoever this clerk is, Vell thought she mattered more than the map. Read it again. He says *get to her before the guild does.* Not *get the map.* Get to *her.*"

Ishra had noticed. She'd been trying not to notice, because noticing meant thinking about it, and thinking about it meant admitting that her dead friend had, in his final act, handed her not a package but a person — a stranger he'd decided, in his last hours of salt and blood, was worth a smuggling crew's lives.

"I read it," she said. "We get to her first. After that, we decide what she's worth."

"And if she's worth nothing?"

"Then we'll have lost nothing but time, and we'll have paid a drink we owed." She rolled the chart closed. "But I don't think we'll have lost nothing. Vell didn't gamble. He counted. And when a man like that spends his death on a stranger—" she shook her head. "Strangers like that aren't nothing. Strangers like that are trouble. Good trouble or bad, we'll find out."

* * *

They made Saltgate roads in four days, running easy under a sky the color of pewter, and Ishra spent those four days learning everything the crew's scattered contacts could tell her about one guild clerk.

It wasn't much. That was the first strange thing. Saltgate ran on gossip the way mills ran on water, and the guild clerks were famous in it — their vices, their promotions, their scandals. But Noor Oyelaran appeared in the talk only as a function. *Ledger-keeper, third rank, gate wardens' division.* Twelve years in post. Never promoted past third rank, which the contacts found either suspicious or pitiable depending on their politics. No debts recorded. No lovers recorded. No vices recorded at all, which in a guild city was the loudest vice of all.

"She's a ghost with a desk," Sefa reported, back from the taverns with salt-beer on her breath. "Nobody knows her. Everybody knows *of* her. The wardens say she's never late, never wrong, and never friendly. One of them called her 'the woman who counts the tide.' Said she goes down to the sealed gate every week with her books and comes back up with the same face."

"What face is that?"

"Like she's listening to something down there and doesn't like what it says."

Ishra lay in her bunk that night with the letter open on her chest and the ship breathing around her, and she tried to assemble the stranger out of what little she had: a woman who kept impossible accounts and wards on a door that must never open, who had survived twelve years inside the guild's cold hierarchy without becoming it, whom a dying man had trusted above his own crew, above his own guild, above Ishra herself.

*Trust her or don't.*

"Don't make me regret this, Vell," she said to the deckhead. The deckhead, being a deckhead, said nothing.

* * *

Saltgate rose out of the sea haze on the fifth morning like a jaw of white stone, all spires and salt-works and the great blind hulk of the Guildhall squatting over the harbor steps like a toad on a stone. Ishra brought the *Vagrant Tide* in flying honest colors under a cargo manifest in Denni's best forgery — dried fish, tanning bark, and a bill of lading so boring it was practically armor — and took a berth at the far end of the commercial quay, where the harbor watch was lazy and the mooring fees were negotiable.

From the quarterdeck she could see the harbor steps. Gray stone, wet with spray, crowded with fishwives and porters and one lone figure in guild gray descending them against the crowd's grain, a satchel at her hip, moving with the particular unhurried urgency of a person who had somewhere to be and intended to arrive exactly on time.

Ishra raised Brannic's spyglass. Found her. Small, dark-haired, plain-dressed, walking like a woman carrying two sets of books — Ishra knew that walk; she'd invented it. And there was something else, something in the set of her shoulders, in the way her eyes flicked once, twice, toward the water and away, that Ishra recognized from a hundred harbors in a hundred ports:

That was a woman being hunted who hadn't admitted it to herself yet.

"There," Ishra said. "Gray coat. Coming down the steps. That's her."

Denni took the glass, whistled low. "She looks like a tax form."

"She looks like a woman with a map in her satchel and enforcers somewhere behind her. Look at the quay. Third pillar. Two men in guild colors who haven't moved in ten minutes and aren't watching the ships."

Denni looked. Swore softly. "They're waiting for her to come back up."

"Or following her down." Ishra lowered the glass and felt the grief in her chest do what grief did best aboard the *Vagrant Tide*: compress, harden, and turn into ballast. Vell was dead. Vell's last trust was walking down those steps with hunters on her shadow, and the moon was turning somewhere overhead, thinning toward its thirteenth night, and the only question left worth asking was the old one, the smuggler's one:

What are you willing to take, and what are you willing to break, to keep a promise to a dead man?

Everything, she thought. Same as always.

"Sefa, get the launch down. Denni, with me — bring your kit, we may need papers that don't exist. Brannic, you have the ship, and if we're not back by nightfall, you're captain, and you're to sail without us, and I mean that, and you'll ignore me, and I love you for it." She was already moving, knife at her belt, coin purse heavy with bribes, Vell's letter burned to ash in the brazier behind her per his instructions and its words copied into the place she kept everything that mattered, which was not paper.

She paused at the rail one last time, looking down at the small gray figure reaching the bottom of the harbor steps, and said, quietly, to a man who couldn't hear her anymore:

"Drink's getting paid, old friend. One way or another."

Below, at the foot of the steps, the clerk named Noor Oyelaran stopped — stopped dead, mid-stride, staring at the water where the *Vagrant Tide* rode at anchor, staring as if the ship itself had spoken her name.

And Ishra, who had planned a careful abduction, a patient one, a polite one, felt the plan begin quietly slipping its moorings.
