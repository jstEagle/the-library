## 6. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The Vagrant Tide smelled of tar, salt pork, and a freedom Noor had never once been able to enter into the ledgers.

Three days out from Saltgate, she had claimed the corner of the chart table nearest the stern lantern and refused to surrender it. Ishra had not objected. Neither had anyone else, though Denni watched her with the wary fascination of a boy watching a cat decide whether to sleep on a stove. The crew had learned quickly that the guild clerk did not ask for things; she assessed them, entered them into some private column of her mind, and then took them as though they had always been hers.

It was the only way she knew how to hold onto anything.

The map lay open beneath her hands, pinned at four corners with coins borrowed from the ship's gambling tin — a debt she intended to repay with interest, if interest could be said to exist on a vessel that answered to no ledger but its own. By lantern-light the parchment looked ordinary enough: coastlines, soundings, a route drawn in faded ochre from the harbor steps of Saltgate down through the straits toward the drowned coast where the gate itself stood sealed.

But Noor had spent three nights watching it redraw itself.

It happened in the dark hours, when even the helmsman's attention wandered. The ochre line would shift — a hair's breadth, then a league — bending around reefs that were not yet marked, finding channels before the lead-line found them. This morning the route passed through a passage that, according to every chart aboard, did not exist until the spring tides of the waning moon. The map was not showing them the way.

The map was counting down.

She had copied it, of course. Three times, in three different inks, on paper taken from Denni's own forgery stock. The copies held still — obedient, inert, honest as any document should be. Only the original moved, which told her something important: whatever animated it lived in the wax seal, or in what the seal had been pressed against, and it did not tolerate duplication. A thing that could be copied could be audited. A thing that refused to be copied wanted to remain the sole authority on its own contents.

Noor had spent nine years catching exactly this kind of fraud in the guild's books. She had simply never expected to admire one.

* * *

The margin was where she finally caught it lying.

Every map has margins, and margins are where cartographers confess. Scale bars. Compass roses. Little prayers against leviathans, in the old style. The courier's map carried all of these, rendered in that same faded ochre, so unremarkable that Noor had catalogued them on the first night and never looked twice.

But tonight, by the third lamp, with the ship rolling easy on a long swell and the crew asleep below, she looked twice.

The compass rose had thirteen points.

Not eight. Not sixteen, as a fine instrument might show. Thirteen, unevenly spaced, and the thirteenth point — the one that should have fallen between south-southeast and southeast, in the empty quarter of the rose — was drawn slightly heavier than the others. Darker ink. As if the pen had paused there, and pressed.

She fetched her abacus beads without quite deciding to, and worked the spacing the way she would work an account: summing the arcs between points, looking for the error, because errors are how documents tell the truth. Twelve of the thirteen points divided the circle evenly. The thirteenth sat exactly where the moon would rise tonight.

Tonight. Which meant tomorrow night it would sit somewhere else. And the night after.

She checked the previous night's position against her memory of the chart — she remembered everything, that was her curse and her craft — and found the point had advanced along the ecliptic by precisely one-thirteenth of its circuit. Each night. One step of thirteen.

The moon turns twelve times in a year. Everyone knows this; children learn it with their numbers. But the old guild calendars — the ones locked in the archive cellar behind the door with no handle, the ones Noor had dusted for years without reading — kept a thirteenth reckoning. A turn of turns. The dark moon within the year, when the count came back around to itself and the books were supposed to balance absolutely.

She had assumed those calendars were ceremonial. Bookkeepers love ceremonies; they are the rituals by which we pretend arithmetic is sacred.

Now, with the thirteenth point crawling across the compass rose toward fullness, Noor understood that the ceremony had been a warning label.

She turned to the scale bar. Beneath its divisions, so faint she had to wet the parchment with a breath to raise them, ran letters. Not words — figures. Numbers in the old guild shorthand, the double-entry cipher used for accounts too sensitive to write plainly: payments, remissions, balances owed.

She translated for two hours. Her fingers cramped. The lantern burned low twice and she fed it without looking up.

And when she finished, she sat back very slowly, because the margin of the map was a ledger, and ledgers do not lie, and this one read:

*Turn 1: the crown wakes. Turn 2: the crown feeds. Turns 3 through 12: the crown feeds. Turn 13: the crown chooses.*

Beneath it, in a different hand — newer, hurried, the hand she now recognized as the dead courier's:

*Thirteenth turn begun. It will take a keeper. Find Aldric before the moon is full or it takes the one who carries this.*

Noor looked up through the stern windows at the sky.

The moon hung above the water, a coin bitten at its edge, and she counted — the way she counted everything, involuntarily, helplessly — the nights remaining until it stood whole.

Eleven.

* * *

"You've gone grey," Ishra said, setting a mug beside Noor's elbow without asking whether she wanted it.

They were alone on deck in the grey hour before dawn, the sea flat as hammered pewter, the sails slack. Ishra slept, Noor had learned, perhaps three hours a night, and prowled the rest, touching rigging and rail and tiller the way a mother touches a sleeping child's forehead — not checking for fever, just confirming presence.

"I'm always this colour," Noor said. "It's the ink."

"It's not the ink." Ishra leaned on the rail beside her, close enough that Noor could smell rope and woodsmoke and, faintly, the lilies the woman chewed when she thought no one saw — an affectation she clearly believed was hidden, and which Noor had entered into the private ledger of her heart under *things about these people I refuse to find charming*. "You found something in the map."

"Margins," Noor said. "Maps keep their confessions in the margins."

"And?"

Noor considered lying. It was a genuine consideration; she weighed it the way she weighed everything. But she had traded the first decoded line of this map for her passage, and a merchant who shorts her partners finds her ledgers seized. Besides — and this was the part that unsettled her more than the moon did — some traitorous part of her wanted to say it aloud to someone who would understand what it meant for a number to be a countdown.

"The crown that wears the king," Noor said. "The founding bargain. It doesn't just feed. On the thirteenth turn it chooses. A keeper. Someone bound to carry it forward." She turned the map so Ishra could see the compass rose, the heavy thirteenth point. "The courier knew. That's why he was running. That's why he was dying to get this to someone who'd follow it."

Ishra studied the rose for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and flat, the voice Noor had heard her use only once before, over the courier's body they had buried off the stern with ballast chains and lily petals.

"How many nights?"

"Eleven. Give or take the weather."

"And you're the one carrying it."

"I'm the one holding the map. There may be a difference. I intend to find out before—"

"No." Ishra said it simply, the way she gave orders, the way she paid debts. "There isn't. You're the one. I can see it sitting on you like weather coming." She pushed off the rail. "Wake me when you know more. Wake Denni if you need light or paper. Wake Brannic only if something's on fire, he's useless otherwise."

She was three steps away when Noor said, "Why do you care?"

Ishra stopped but did not turn. The grey light made her look carved rather than living.

"He pulled me out of the water once," she said. "Off Corsa Point, after my old crew went down with our cargo and our names. He didn't know me. He owed me nothing. He dove into winter water because a girl was drowning, and then he fed me for a month on his own rations while I learned to want to live again." Now she turned, and her eyes were steady. "That's the whole ledger. One entry. It doesn't balance. It never will. So I spend it where he'd have spent it."

She walked away. Noor sat with the map and the cold mug and the strange ache of having been entered, without her consent, into somebody else's book of debts — and of not minding.

* * *

The dreams began the second night.

Not the ordinary dreams of travel — the falling, the missed appointments, the ledgers whose columns multiplied faster than she could total them. Those she knew like old acquaintances. These were different.

In the dream she was small. Seven years old, maybe eight, standing in the doorway of the archive cellar where her father had worked, back when her father still existed as anything other than a name in a guild registry. The lamps were the old whale-oil ones, guttering gold. And a man's voice — low, worn smooth as a stone in a current, a voice that sounded like it had been reciting for three hundred years — was speaking from the dark between the shelves.

*"Your seventh birthday," the voice said, gentle as a clerk confirming a figure. "Your mother baked honey-bread and burned the bottom, and you ate the burnt piece anyway so she wouldn't feel bad. She cried in the pantry afterward and you pretended not to hear."*

Noor, in the dream, said: "Who are you?"

*"The year your father stopped coming home," the voice continued, inexorable, kind. "You waited on the harbor steps eleven evenings. On the twelfth you brought a blanket. On the thirteenth you brought nothing, because you had done the arithmetic, and you understood that hope is an account that must eventually be written off."*

"Wake up," Noor told herself. "Wake up."

*"You kept the blanket," the voice said. "Folded, in the bottom of your sea-chest, for six years. You told yourself it was for the cold."*

She woke with her heart slamming and the cabin dark around her, and for a moment she could smell whale-oil smoke and honey-bread, and her chest was full of a grief so specific and so ancient that she had to press both hands over her mouth to keep it inside.

Then it drained away, like tide going out, and she was left with the fact of it: something had just read her childhood back to her. Accurately. In order. With tenderness.

Things that eat memories, she thought, staring at the beams overhead, do not bother to memorize what they intend to swallow whole. Only a connoisseur savors. Only a collector catalogs.

Something was learning her. Entry by entry.

* * *

By the fourth night she had stopped pretending she would sleep, and instead rigged the chart lamp over the map and worked. The crew noticed. Crews notice everything; it is their single professional virtue. Brannic left bread outside her door each morning without comment. Denni left ink. On the fifth evening, Ishra appeared at the chart table with a second mug and sat down across from her without invitation, and Noor, who had spent nine years defending the perimeter of her desk against auditors and superiors alike, found she did not mind the occupation of territory.

"It talks to me," Noor said. "Or something does. Through the map, or through the moon, or I've finally cracked like everyone at the guild always predicted." She laid out her notes: the cipher translations, the nightly positions of the thirteenth point plotted as a curve, and — written last, in smaller handwriting, the way one writes the shameful entries — the contents of the dreams.

Ishra read everything. Twice. Then she looked up, and Noor braced for pity, which she had procedures for repelling.

What she got instead was fury. It moved across Ishra's face like weather — not directed at Noor, but at the situation entire, at the crown and the guild and the moon itself, as though all three were debtors who had defaulted together.

"It's tasting you," Ishra said. "Softening you up. Like a butcher hangs meat."

"That's roughly my assessment."

"And the guild let this go on for three hundred years."

"The guild *ran* it," Noor said, and heard her own voice come out colder than she'd intended, colder than she'd known it could be. "Someone kept those books, Captain. Every feeding. Every thirteenth turn. Somewhere there's a ledger with three hundred years of harvests entered in a fair hand, probably with a summary page and quarterly totals." She pressed her palm flat on the map. "When I find that ledger, I'm going to audit it."

Ishra smiled then — a hard, bright, dangerous smile, entirely unlike the warmth she showed the crew, and Noor realized with a jolt that it was the first genuinely honest expression she had seen the woman wear.

"There she is," Ishra said. "The Keeper Unbound."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? He died saying it. Names given by the dead are binding."

"They're *expensive*, is what they are." Noor gathered her notes, then paused, because there was one more thing — the thing she had saved for last, the entry she had been circling for two days the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. "Captain. If the crown is choosing a keeper on the thirteenth turn, I need to know what happens to the previous keeper."

Ishra's smile faded. "You think there's a previous one."

"I think the guild's Master of Keys sent enforcers after a *clerk*. I think the audit notice was in my own handwriting. And I think—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "I think whoever wore that title before me is either dead, or empty, or wearing the crown's attention like a yoke. And in eleven nights, I find out which."

The lantern flame leaned in a draft. Out beyond the stern windows, the moon rose over the water — fuller than the night before, silvering the swells, climbing along the path the map had drawn for it.

One step of thirteen.

Ten remaining.

And far below the hull, deeper than any sounding-line, Noor thought she felt it — faint as a pulse through a floorboard — the turning of something vast beginning to wake.

* * *

She did not sleep again that night. Instead, near dawn, she took a fresh sheet of Denni's good paper and began, in her fairest hand, a new ledger.

*Voyage of the Vagrant Tide. Opened by Noor Oyelaran, sometime clerk of the Saltgate Guild, sometime keeper of the sealed gate, soon-to-be-determined keeper of what else.*

*Debts outstanding:* she wrote, and listed them — the coins, the ink, the bread, the mugs, the lilies, the lives already owed to a dead man's kindness.

Then, after a moment's hesitation, she added a final line at the top of the first page, where accountants write the governing condition of the whole account:

*Balance due in ten nights.*

Under the hull, in the drowned dark, something turned another page.
