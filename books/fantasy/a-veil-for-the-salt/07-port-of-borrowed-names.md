## 7. Port of Borrowed Names

Denni Quill had three names stitched into the lining of his coat, and by nightfall he would need a fourth.

He'd sewn them there himself, of course — that was the whole point of him. Denni was the Vagrant Tide's forger, its maker of papers, its quiet magician of ink and wax. He was nineteen years old and had been more people than most men met in a lifetime. There was a version of him that was a spice merchant's clerk out of Vell; a version that was a widowed rope-maker with a limp he could produce on command; a version that was nobody at all, a boy with no papers, which in certain ports was its own kind of passport. He kept them all folded against his ribs like letters from people he used to be.

"Port of Lanterns," Ishra said at the rail, as the harbor rose out of the morning haze. "Everyone stays sharp. Everyone stays boring."

The port's real name was Correnhal, but nobody called it that, because names were currency here and the true ones spent fastest. Correnhal sat on a spit of land no kingdom claimed, governed by a council of harbormasters who had long ago realized that the most profitable trade in the world wasn't silk or salt or stolen relics. It was *who you were allowed to be*. In Correnhal, a man could buy a new name over breakfast, wear it until sundown, sell it back at a loss, and hang for a crime committed by someone else entirely. The lanterns along the seawall burned green — harbor-light, tide-light, the color of money changing hands — and beneath them moved a crowd in which every third face was borrowed.

Denni loved it the way other boys loved fairs.

"We dock at the third pier," he told Ishra, unrolling his sleeve of sketches. "I've got us as a salvage crew out of the Sable Reach. Wreck-divers. Nobody asks wreck-divers questions; they're assumed to be half-drowned and simple." He tapped the page. "Noor is our charter-clerk. She'll hate it."

"She'll do it," Ishra said.

Noor did hate it. Denni watched her read her false identity — *Maren Solle, clerk of record, salvage vessel Kestrel's Due* — with the expression of a woman handed a counterfeit coin and asked to spend it. Her fingers kept wanting to check the ledger-work. Twice she caught an error in his forgery before he did: a date that didn't align with the tide-tables, a harbor fee rounded wrong.

"You wrote a ship paying moorage in silver," she said, not looking up. "Correnhal takes copper at the piers and nothing else. Any clerk worth her salt would know that."

Denni stared at her. "How do you know what Correnhal takes at the piers?"

"I don't," Noor said. "I know what ledgers want. They want to agree with each other. Yours disagree."

From across the deck, Brannic the bosun snorted. "She's been aboard four days and she's already better at being us than we are."

That was the thing about Noor Oyelaran that Denni was still learning to see properly. On the guildhall steps she had looked like what she was supposed to look like: a clerk, small and precise, a woman made of neat columns. But out here, stripped of her guild seal and her sanctioned desk, she had turned out to be something stranger — a person whose entire mind was built like a harbor, every fact moored to every other fact, everything checked against everything, nothing adrift. She couldn't stop doing it. She corrected the ship's ration accounts in her head while eating breakfast. She'd reorganized Ishra's chart-locker by tide-season without asking, and when Ishra found out she'd gone very quiet for a moment and then said, "Do it again next month," which from Ishra was roughly a knighthood.

* * *

They came ashore under the green lanterns just after the morning bells, six salt-stained strangers wearing one shared lie.

Denni walked point, because that was the choreography they'd rehearsed and because Correnhal rewarded confidence the way deep water rewarded weight. Behind him came Brannic, playing a wreck-diver's slow sullen bulk to perfection. Then Sefa and the twins, Rho and Cassa, who between them carried enough hidden blades to arm a militia and enough practiced boredom to make the blades invisible. Then Ishra, captain-shaped even in a borrowed coat, and last Noor, clutching her forged charter-roll like a woman holding a snake she'd been assured was tame.

The port swallowed them.

Denni had walked these lanes a dozen times in a dozen skins, and he still marveled at how the city worked — not despite its lies but *through* them. At the Weighhouse of Names, brokers sold identities off iron hooks like dried fish: HERE IS A MAN WHO NEVER EXISTED, GENTLY USED. A customs officer glanced at their charter-roll for exactly as long as it took Denni to slide two coppers under it, and then looked through them instead of at them, which was the highest compliment Correnhal paid anyone. A beggar near the fish-market held out a bowl and whispered, "Names for the nameless," and Denni dropped a coin and felt, absurdly, homesick.

But it was watching the others that undid him a little.

Because here was the thing Denni knew and never said aloud: he had joined this crew as cargo, essentially. Fourteen years old, caught forging harbor passes in a port where forging was punishable by the loss of the offending hand, hauled before a young captain named Ishra Vane who was supposed to turn him over to the magistrates and instead had looked at his work — really looked, the way no one ever had — and said, "Your kerning's better than the harbor office's. Come be useful or come be arrested, your choice." He had never once doubted the choice. But he had also never stopped waiting, somewhere underneath, for the day the usefulness ran out and the family went with it.

And now here was Noor — a guild-woman, a stranger, four days old to them — moving through the port inside their lie as if she'd been sewn into it. When a toll-keeper squinted at her face and asked a question too specific, it was Sefa who drifted to her elbow without being summoned, laughing at nothing, turning the man's attention aside. When the twins split to scout the chandlery, Rho left Cassa covering Noor's blind side without a word passing between them. When a pickpocket's hand found the charter-roll in Noor's coat, it was Brannic — enormous, unsubtle Brannic — who simply appeared beside the thief and stood there radiating such patient menace that the roll returned itself.

Nobody ordered any of it. That was what struck Denni hardest, standing in the fish-market noise watching it happen. On the Tide, protection wasn't assigned. It was *reflex*. The crew moved around each other the way water moves around a hull — not because anyone instructed it, but because that was what water was for.

Noor saw it too. He could tell, because she kept stopping. Three times in the first hour she halted mid-stride, visibly startled, watching some small unspoken coordination resolve around her — and each time her expression did something complicated that Denni recognized because he'd worn it himself for years. It was the face of a person discovering that safety might not be a wage you earned but a thing people simply *did* for each other, and not knowing what to do with hands that had only ever been paid.

"The guild," she said quietly, once, when they paused under an awning. "At Saltgate, if I stumbled, the audit found me. If I excelled, the audit found me. Everything was watched. Nothing was *held*." She looked at the crowded lane, at Sefa casually shadowing a stranger away from them, and something in her face was almost angry. "I spent nine years thinking that was order."

"It's inventory," Denni said, surprising himself with the bitterness in his own voice. "Ishra says it. The guild doesn't keep people. It keeps *records of* people." He shrugged, nineteen years old and suddenly feeling older. "Difference matters."

* * *

The warning came at midday, in the back room of a tea-house called the Drowned Bell, and it came from a woman named Odji, who was the closest thing Correnhal had to a friend of the Vagrant Tide — which is to say she had sold them information twice and betrayed them never, which in this port constituted a marriage vow.

Odji was a broker of rumor, a small gray woman who dealt in truths the way other dealers dealt in opium: measured, diluted, always cut with something to make it go further. She took one look at their faces — at Noor's face especially, the guild-trained stillness of it — and pulled the curtain shut behind them.

"You're carrying something hot," she said. "Don't tell me what. I don't want to know it, and knowing it wouldn't change what I have to say." She poured tea nobody drank. "Saltgate has posted a retrieval writ. Not a bounty — a *writ*. Bounties are public. This one's sealed, couriered, and paid in advance, which means whoever holds it isn't working for coin." Her eyes went flat. "They've hired the Hollow Choir."

The room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the tea.

Even Noor stiffened, and she was the one person aboard who hadn't grown up in the free ports, where children learned the Choir's reputation before they learned to swim. Denni had heard the stories since he was small enough to hide under wharf-boards. The Hollow Choir weren't assassins in any ordinary sense. They didn't cut throats. They *sang*. Somewhere in their founding — nobody agreed where; the Ashen Coast said a drowned monastery, the Reach said a debt-collector's guild that had gone strange — they had learned a craft that made Denni's own seem like child's play. They could take a name and sing it apart. Not kill the body: unravel the *name*, the identity, the self, thread by thread, until the victim remained alive and breathing and no longer entirely anyone. Fishermen spoke of men pulled from the Choir's wake who could not remember their mothers' faces, who answered to nothing, whose own names slid off them like wet rope. The Choir wore masks of white lacquer and moved in harmony, and it was said that when they sang together, you heard your own name inside the melody, calling you kindly, patiently, home.

"They're already in the port," Odji said. "Three singers came in on the morning tide under pilgrim papers — good ones, incidentally, better than yours, no offense intended, Denni." She smiled thinly at his flinch. "They'll walk the lanes tonight listening. You can't outrun them and you can't bribe them; they're not paid in coin. Your only advantages are speed, noise, and the fact that Correnhal hates them — the harbormasters won't give them aid, though they won't bar them either. And—" she hesitated, and for the first time looked almost sorry, "—they're singing already. Low. Testing. Two names went missing off the rookeries last night. Just gone out of people's mouths. That's how you know they're working a harbor. The names stop working."

Ishra's voice came out level as a keel. "How long?"

"A day. Maybe two, if the harbormasters slow-walk their permits, which they will, because the Choir frightens their wives." Odji folded her hands. "There's a ship leaving on the evening tide for the Ashen Coast — the *Mercy of Coins*, honest smugglers, decent rates. I can get you berths under fresh papers within the hour. Or you can stay and gamble on the Choir's patience. I know which I'd choose, but I'm old and cowardly and my name is the only thing I own."

Denni looked around the room and understood, with a lurch, that everyone was looking at Noor.

It made sense. The writ was for her. The map — whatever it truly was, whatever it wanted, the thing that redrew itself nightly in their hold like a living thing straining toward Saltgate — was hers. Every mile of danger out here traced back to the choice she'd made on the guildhall steps, and every soul in this room had signed onto it anyway, and now the bill was coming due in the shape of white lacquer masks and songs that ate people.

He expected her to say she'd take the Mercy of Coins alone. Cut the risk. It was the arithmetic answer; it was what the guild would have taught her; it was what Denni himself, in her place, would have calculated.

Instead Noor set down her untouched tea, squared her shoulders, and said, in the flat, precise voice of a woman reading terms aloud:

"Then we leave tonight, all of us, and we leave *loudly* — loudly enough that the Choir follows the noise instead of the names. Odji, I'll need your help buying something strange." A pause, and then the ghost of a smile, the first Denni had seen on her. "I want to purchase six identities that are *deliberately wrong*. Papers that contradict each other. A salvage crew whose ages don't match their faces, whose ship doesn't exist, whose fees were paid in a currency this port doesn't use." Her eyes glinted. "If the Choir hunts by name, let them chase a name that falls apart the moment anyone looks at it closely. Let them sing to a forgery."

Odji stared at her for a long moment, and then began, very slowly, to laugh — the laugh of a professional recognizing an amateur who has just invented a technique.

"Oh," she said, wiping her eye. "Oh, the harbormasters are going to *hate* you. Yes. Yes, I can find you six beautiful lies that eat themselves." She stood, gathering her shawl. "Evening tide. Don't be late, don't be followed, and whatever you're carrying—" she paused at the curtain, gray eyes unreadable, "—keep it away from the Choir. Whatever it is. Things like that are why the Choir exists. Someone taught them that names are debts. And you, little clerk, are carrying what looks very much like a debt with legs."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in a rented loft above a sailmaker's, and it was, Denni thought later, the strangest and best day of his life.

Because Noor sat down at his worktable — his table, his inks, his knives, his sacred territory that no one aboard touched — and became, overnight, his apprentice and his rival and his colleague all at once. She couldn't forge a hand to save her life; her script was too disciplined, too honest, trained into her by nine years of countersigning documents whose falseness meant the gallows. But she could *audit* a forgery the way no forger alive could audit one. She read his papers and found the seams he'd stopped seeing. She designed contradictions the way other people designed alibis — systematically, ruthlessly, each false detail chosen to collapse into another false detail, a paper trail that unraveled into nonsense under any sustained gaze.

"Where did you learn to think like this?" he asked her at one point, genuinely bewildered, watching her cross out his carefully crafted biography of Captain Maren Solle and replace it with something deliberately, artfully broken.

"Ledgers," she said. "A ledger that agrees with itself perfectly is either true or very well made, and auditors assume the second. So you build the disagreement in on purpose. A flaw you chose is a door you control." She looked up. "Didn't the guild teach you anything useful?"

"The guild taught me to fear paper," Denni said. "You're teaching me to argue with it."

By dusk they had six identities that were masterpieces of deliberate ruin, and Odji's runner delivered the berth-papers for the *Mercy of Coins*, and the plan was set: walk to the pier separately, in pairs, loud and ordinary, and let the broken papers do their strange defensive work.

And then, crossing the lamplit lane toward the harbor with Sefa at his shoulder, Denni heard it.

Faint. From somewhere up-slope, past the rookeries, threading down through the evening air like smoke finding a draft. A sound almost like singing and almost like wind and almost like a name being called by someone who loved you very much and wanted you to come home.

Sefa's hand closed hard on his arm. "Walk," she said. "Don't listen. Whatever it sounds like, it isn't for you yet."

But it was, a little. Denni could hear his own name inside it — not *Denni*, not any of his three stitched names, but something older, something from before, a name from a house in the Ashen Coast that had burned when he was eleven, a name only one living person still used. His mother's word for him. The Choir had dug that deep already, or the port's rumor-brokers had sold it to them, or the songs simply found what was truest and pulled.

His feet wanted to turn uphill. Sefa's grip kept him walking toward the water, toward the green lanterns, toward the ship and the family and the evening tide, and he fixed his eyes on the pier-lights and counted his steps and did not listen, mostly.

Behind them, up in the dark, the song grew a fraction clearer. Patient. Kind. Certain.

Two days, Odji had said. Denni gave them one.

He touched the three names stitched against his ribs and began, silently, drafting a fourth.
