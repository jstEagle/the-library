## 8. Songs That Eat Names

Ishra had heard the Choir once before, twenty years ago and half a world away, from the far side of a harbor in Vesh. She had been young enough then to think the sound beautiful — a dozen voices braided into one rising line, like a hymn sung at a wedding — before she saw what it did to the man on the quay. He had stopped walking. He had turned toward the singing the way a man turns toward his own name spoken across a crowded room, and he had stood very still while the song took him apart syllable by syllable, until there was nothing left standing there at all but a coat sliding off empty air.

She had never forgotten how kind it sounded. That was the worst of it. Cruelty that snarls can be fought. The Hollow Choir came at you sounding like someone who loved you, calling you home.

So when the green lanterns of the Port of Borrowed Names guttered all at once along the customs row — every flame bending landward, as if inhaling — Ishra knew before her ears confirmed it.

"Off the street," she said. Not loud. Loud was for later. "Now."

They were fifty paces from the port gates, forty from the *Vagrant Tide*, and the night had chosen this exact stretch of cobbled road between the chandler's warehouses and the customs wall to stop being a road and start being a trap. Sefa heard it first among them, head cocked, ears better than any of them; Denni's hand went to his ribs where he kept his stitched names; and Noor — Noor Oyelaran, guild clerk, ledger-keeper, woman who had been aboard six days and still walked the deck like a guest at someone else's dinner — looked up at the bending flames with an expression Ishra had last seen on a mathematician watching an equation come out wrong.

"That's not one voice," Noor said. "That's twelve. In unison. Why would you sing in unison if—"

The song crested the warehouse roof.

It came down the customs row like fog with intent, and it was, gods help her, beautiful. It sounded like rain on a cabin roof. It sounded like Ishra's mother's voice saying her name over a table heaped with food, twenty years drowned and here it was again, note-perfect, warm as bread: *Ishra. Ishra of the Tide. Ishra who owes. Ishra who—*

"Noor." Her own name was fraying at the edges already; she could feel it loosening in her mouth like a tooth. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it now."

Noor had gone still in the particular way she went still when reading — Ishra had watched it a dozen times on deck, the clerk's whole body becoming an eye — and then she said something so absurd that Ishra would laugh about it later, much later, when there was air enough for laughing:

"It's a recitation."

*What?*

"The song. Listen." Noor grabbed her wrist, hard, and dragged her behind a bollard while the others pressed into the shadow of the chandler's awning. "It has structure. Verses. A refrain — it repeats your patronymic every fourth line, it's *metered*, it's — oh, it's *audited*." Her eyes were doing something terrible and bright. "Ishra, it's not magic wearing the shape of a song. It's a contract being *performed*. Names are just — they're just entries. And every entry—"

"—has terms," Ishra finished, because she'd spent thirty years sailing under smugglers' codes and articles and blood-debts, and she knew a clause when it was eating her name.

Above the rooftops, the voices gathered themselves for another verse, and this time Ishra heard it the way Noor evidently heard it: not a hymn but a *reading aloud*, the founding horror of it landing in her gut. Somewhere behind that wall of sound were people. Singers. Bound, probably, bound as tightly as their victims, each verse a term they were compelled to execute.

*Ishra of the Tide, called captain, called debtor, called—*

Her hands wanted to cover her ears. She made them do something else.

"Sefa. Paper. Any of you — ink, chalk, charcoal, I don't care—"

Denni was faster than all of them, because Denni was a forger and a forger's satchel was welded to his spine. He had a stub of graphite and a folded sheet of good rag paper out of his coat before Sefa finished patting his pockets, and he pressed both into Noor's hands without being asked, which Ishra noted, filed away, and intended to tease him about when they were old.

"What do you need?" Noor asked. The song was closer. It had found Odji's name now, and Brannic's, weaving them in, patient, kind. Odji had stopped walking toward the awning. He was facing the customs wall with tears running openly down his face, listening to whatever the song knew about him.

"Terms," Noor said, mostly to herself, kneeling on the wet cobblestones with the paper flat against the bollard. Graphite moving. "Every contract has terms. Every performance is an execution of terms. If I write the terms down — if I formalize what they're already doing — then the song isn't free anymore. It's *bound to its text*." She looked up at Ishra, and her voice cracked into something fierce and disbelieving, a clerk discovering the whole world was a filing error waiting to happen. "You can't improvise a signed agreement. If I draft it fast enough — if I bind the recitation to my draft — they'll have to sing exactly what's written."

"And what are you writing?"

"Loopholes." Noor smiled, and for one second Ishra saw why a dying courier had trusted this woman with the only map in the world. "Gods, I love loopholes."

* * *

Later, everyone aboard would tell the story differently. That was the way of stories aboard the *Vagrant Tide*; ownership was collective and accuracy was optional. But Ishra had been standing closest, so hers was the version that mattered, and hers went like this:

Noor wrote for ninety seconds. Ninety seconds, on wet paper, by lantern-light that kept trying to bend away from her, while the Hollow Choir sang Brannic apart one loving syllable at a time and Brannic swayed on his feet like a man deep in his cups, smiling at the wall.

She wrote fast because, she explained afterward, the song itself dictated its own contract — it couldn't help announcing its terms aloud, that was the mechanism, that was the whole obscene engine of it. *We call, therefore we claim. We name, therefore we unmake.* Twelve verses per name. Refrain every fourth line. Termination upon completion of the final verse. It had told them all of this, over and over, in the guise of melody, because the Choir's makers had never imagined a listener who took notes.

At the bottom of the page Noor drew two lines, side by side, and shouted — actually shouted, the quiet clerk, over the top of a killing song:

"I need signatures! Theirs or ours, doesn't matter whose first — a contract needs *parties!*"

"How in the salt-dark do we get *them* to sign?" Sefa bellowed back, hauling Odji bodily backward by the collar as the tenor verse closed around him.

"We don't!" Noor's graphite was already moving again, adding lines below the signature rules, tiny precise characters, a lawyer's ambush laid inside a merchant's document. "A party to a contract is anyone bound by its terms! They're bound — the singers are *bound*, that's how the Choir works, they're compelled to perform — so they're parties whether they consent or not! I just have to make the binding *mutual*—" and here she did something Ishra didn't fully understand then or now: she read three lines of her own draft aloud, deliberately, into the teeth of the song, matching its meter, dropping her words into the spaces between refrains like coins into a slot—

—and the song *stumbled*.

Twelve perfect voices broke unison for the first time in three hundred years. Just for a heartbeat. Just a skipped beat, a hiccup in the hymn, the sound of a machine encountering an input its makers never specified. Ishra felt her name settle back into her mouth, whole, hers, and gasped like a surfacing diver.

"It heard me," Noor whispered. She was pale as bone-paste and grinning like a woman possessed. "It *read* me. Oh, this is the most fun I have ever— *DOWN!*"

The counterattack, when it came, was not subtle. The song abandoned its gentle approach entirely and came down the customs row like a wave, all twelve voices converging on the bollard, on the kneeling clerk, on the scrap of paper that had insulted them — and Ishra understood, in the half-second she had to understand anything, that the Choir meant simply to drown the contract in volume, to sing the paper and its author into unraveling together.

Noor slammed her palm flat on the signature lines.

"EXECUTED," she screamed, in the flat carrying accent of the guild counting-houses, a word with the weight of a gavel in it, "BY MUTUAL PERFORMANCE. READ YOUR OWN TERMS."

And the song — which could not lie, which was nothing but terms made audible, which had announced its own conditions to every victim for centuries — the song read them.

Ishra heard it happen. Everyone did. The twelve voices came down on them at full flood and then, mid-wave, the flood hit its own bedrock and went over it instead, and what poured across the customs row was Noor's draft, sung back in perfect unison: *the claiming shall proceed verse by verse without interruption or abbreviation; should any verse be interrupted, abbreviated, or answered in kind, the claiming shall be deemed contested and suspended pending resolution between the parties; resolution shall be conducted in writing; no party bound by these terms may resume performance until resolution is complete.*

Resolution conducted in writing. Pending. Between the parties.

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Forty paces away the port gates hung open and empty and the green lanterns burned straight up again, released from their inhale, and somewhere above the chandler's roofline Ishra could hear twelve people breathing hard.

Then, faintly, from beyond the wall: a single voice, hoarse, furious, and utterly human.

"*Who,*" it demanded of the night, "*wrote that?*"

"RUN," said Ishra.

* * *

They ran. Of course they ran. Nobody in Ishra's line of work lingered to exchange pleasantries with an assassin whose weapon had just been notarized against her will. They took the alley behind the chandler's — Sefa knew it, Sefa knew every gap and gutter in every borrowed port from here to the Spur — and came out sideways onto the pier road with the *Vagrant Tide*'s riding lamp bobbing ahead of them like a promise.

Brannic had to be carried. He came back to himself slowly, in fits, like a man hauled up from deep water, and by the time they got him over the rail he was weeping and swearing in alternating verses and gripping Ishra's arm hard enough to bruise. "It knew my mother," he kept saying. "It knew her voice, Captain. How does it know—"

"Later." She shoved him at the galley and the brandy bottle. "Drink. All of it."

Odji wouldn't go below. He stood at the rail staring back at the dark shore with his collar clutched in one fist, and when Ishra joined him he said quietly, "It had my daughter's name. I don't have a daughter, Captain. I never — but it *had* one, ready, like a knife it keeps sharpened for everybody it might someday meet." He swallowed. "What kind of people make a thing like that?"

"People with ledgers," said Noor, from the companionway.

Ishra turned. The clerk was leaning against the frame, gray-faced, graphite-stained to the second knuckle, holding her drafting hand inside the other like an injured bird. The paper — the contract, the weapon, the miracle — was folded into quarters and tucked inside her coat with the unconscious care of a woman shelving a rare volume.

"You cut yourself on the stones," Ishra said. "Your hand."

"It's nothing." Noor flexed the fingers, winced, kept flexing. "I've written through worse. Guild audits run long."

"Denni." Ishra raised her voice, scanning the deck. "Denni, get the kit, our clerk needs—"

The pause before he answered was wrong.

"Aye," Denni said, from the shadow by the mainmast. "Kit. Yes."

He moved like himself, mostly. He found the kit where it always lived. His hands worked the buckle fine. But when Noor thanked him and reached for the needle-scarred leather, he looked up at her with a polite, faintly strained smile — the smile of a man being introduced at a party to someone he ought to know — and said:

"Sorry. Have we met?"

* * *

The cost surfaced slowly, the way bodies surface.

Sefa noticed first, because Sefa noticed everything about Denni: he'd taken his watch at the bow without argument, which Denni never did, and he'd stood it humming, which Denni also never did, except that the tune was wrong — not a song Denni knew badly, but no song at all, just intervals, a scale climbed and descended, climbed and descended, patient as water dripping.

By the middle watch he had stopped answering to Denni.

Not sulking. Not concussion — Brannic checked his eyes and swore in three languages. He simply no longer owned the name. When Ishra said it, his head turned politely, the way it turns at any clear syllable, and no more. When she asked him directly, *what's your name?*, he considered the question with genuine scholarly interest and answered, "I... haven't decided yet," and the horrible thing was that he wasn't frightened. He seemed almost relieved. Lighter. A man setting down luggage he'd forgotten he was carrying.

But his hands remembered. That was the part that made Ishra's chest hurt. Give him a blank document and he forged — beautifully, instantly, three seals and a chancellor's hand flowing off his pen while his face stayed pleasant and vacant, a master craftsman dreaming at his bench. Everything Denni had ever made himself into was stored somewhere below the name, intact. Only the handle was gone.

"It's the refrain," Noor said. She hadn't slept. She sat in the stern lamplight with the contract unfolded beside her and her forefinger resting on a line of graphite, and her voice was scraped raw with something Ishra recognized, eventually, as guilt. "The third verse. When the song stumbled — when my draft interrupted the claiming — the suspension applied to the *contested* claims. Mine. Yours. Brannic's. Odji's." Her finger didn't move. "His wasn't contested yet. It was *in progress*. And 'in progress' clauses — I wrote 'pending resolution' to protect us, and pending means *held*, held means suspended, suspended means—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I froze his account mid-transaction. His name is in escrow."

"Can you get it back?"

"Yes." Flat. Immediate. Certain in the way she was certain about numbers. "The contract requires resolution between parties in writing. I am a party. They are parties. I can demand resolution — written resolution, certified, witnessed — and settlement of all accounts held in suspension." She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and blazing. "Tomorrow. At the port gates. In front of witnesses, because a witnessed resolution binds harder, and I want those singers *bound*, Ishra, I want their whole obscene instrument audited line by line before I give back one syllable of his."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The water slapped the hull. Forward, the man who was not Denni hummed his nameless scale at the stars.

"He gave you his paper," Ishra said finally. "Before you even asked. He saw you kneel down and he handed over the best sheet in his satchel without a word."

"I know." Noor's jaw tightened. "Which is why I'll be giving the Choir my terms tomorrow morning instead of theirs. Full settlement. His name, restored, plus damages." A ghost of the earlier grin, fiercer now, banked coals rather than firelight. "They keep records too, you know. The Choir. I heard them — the whole contract is memorized, verse-perfect, generation after generation. Which means somewhere in that song there is a complete accounting of every name they've ever eaten."

"So?"

"So, Captain." Noor folded the contract with the reverence other people reserved for relics, and her voice dropped low, the way it did in the guildhall archives, the way it did when she talked about the sealed gate below the Saltgate, and Ishra felt the next words arrive like a change in the wind. "Assassins who keep perfect books of stolen names — that's not a weapon. That's a *ledger*. And every ledger ever kept has hidden columns. Payments someone didn't want entered. Debts someone hoped would be forgotten." She tapped the folded paper once, softly, against her knee. "The guild taught me to find those columns. Tomorrow I'm going to find the Choir's."

Ishra looked at her — this clerk, this stowaway of fate, this woman the sea had washed against their hull with a dead man's map in her coat — and felt the old blood-debt shift its weight in her chest, and thought: *Vessik, you scheming drowned bastard. You didn't leave her the map. You left us her.*

"All right," she said. "Tomorrow, the gates. Tonight—" she raised her voice forward, gentle, "—our friend at the bow comes below where it's warm. Nameless or not, nobody stands my watches alone."

The man who was not Denni came aft agreeably enough. But at the companionway he paused, tilted his head at Noor with that same polite, searching smile, and asked the question that kept Ishra awake long after the lamps were doused:

"You look at me like you're reading something," he said. "What is it my name used to say?"

And Noor opened her mouth to answer — and went white as sailcloth, because beneath her feet, rising up through the timbers, through the hull, through the black water of the harbor, came a voice none of them had heard except in dreams. Deep. Rusty. Three hundred years unused and desperate as a drowning man.

*"Keeper,"* it said. *"Keeper Unbound. The moon has begun to turn. You are running out of nights — and I am running out of me."*
