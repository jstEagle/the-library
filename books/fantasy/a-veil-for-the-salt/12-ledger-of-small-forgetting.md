## 12. Ledger of Small Forgetting

Noor did not sleep. She sat in the stern of the *Vagrant Tide* with a lantern hooded to a coin's width of light and the guild's ledgers spread across her knees — not the physical books, which were three hundred miles behind her in the Saltgate Guildhall, but the memory of them, which she carried always, the way other people carried scars.

She had begun keeping copies in her head at fourteen, her first year as an apprentice clerk, when Master Ellery had caught her writing figures twice and told her that a good bookkeeper trusted nothing she could not hold between her ears. He had meant it as discipline. It had become survival. Every column she had ever entered, every balance she had ever struck, lived in her mind in her own small precise hand, and tonight she was reading them backward.

The Master of Keys had shown her the hidden columns two nights ago at the shrine — payments of forgetting, he'd called them, small mercies, the guild's kindness to its keepers. *We feed it crumbs so it never takes the loaf.* He had said it gently, the way one offers a warm coat to someone standing in cold rain, and Noor had felt the temptation of it settle over her like wool: return the map, resume her post, be protected. Be safe. Be *kept*.

But she was a ledger-keeper before she was anything else, and ledgers do not lie unless someone makes them lie, and she had spent three days now doing what no guild auditor had done in three hundred years.

She was auditing the crown.

* * *

It began with dates. That was the trouble with hidden columns — they recorded amounts but rarely reasons, and an amount without a reason is only half an entry. So Noor went back to the dates, cross-referencing each payment of forgetting against the keeper who made it, against the year, against the season, against what else appeared in the official record during those same months.

Keeper Maren, 211 years after the Binding. Official record: promoted to First Clerk. Hidden column: three payments that spring, totaling — by Noor's reckoning of the old units — something substantial. And in the margin of the official ledger, in Maren's own hand, a note Noor had catalogued years ago without thinking: *Request for leave denied. Family correspondence to cease.*

Keeper Ossian, 174 years after the Binding. Payments clustered in autumn. Official record: transfer from the harbor office to the inner hall. Margin note, Ossian's hand: *Wife remarried. Accounts closed.*

Keeper Sefa, 96 years after. Payments in winter. Margin note: *Mother deceased. Grief noted; duties unaffected.*

Noor sat very still with the lantern trembling on her knee.

Duty last. Love first. She turned the pages faster now, in her mind, faster than any physical hand could move, because the pattern was no longer hiding. Every keeper's payments clustered around the same seasons of their lives — marriages, births, deaths, homecomings, the whole bright clutter of being loved and loving. The crown did not take at random. The guild had told its keepers — had told *her*, would have told her still, if she'd taken the Master of Keys' open hand — that the payments were arbitrary, scattered, painless little losses. A name here. An afternoon there. The taste of a particular soup.

They were not scattered. They were harvested. Always love first, duty last, until what remained of each keeper was exactly what the guild required: a person with nothing left to go home to, nothing left to grieve, nothing left to want more than the work. Obedience, manufactured one small mercy at a time, and billed as protection.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way she noticed errors in other people's arithmetic — with a clerk's cold attention, as though the shaking hands belonged to someone whose account she was keeping.

* * *

"You haven't slept."

Ishra lowered herself onto the crate beside her, moving with the ship's roll the way she moved through everything, as if the world were a deck she'd been born on. Her hair was tied back for sleep and her eyes were not sleepy at all.

"I'm working," Noor said.

"At this hour the work is either very bad or very important."

"Both." Noor closed her eyes, and the ledgers opened behind them. "Ishra. When you signed on with Tam — when he saved you, whatever the debt was — do you remember the date?"

Ishra blinked. "The ninth of the Drowned Month. Why?"

"Because I asked you yesterday and you said the eleventh, and this morning Denni said the tenth, and none of you are wrong." She opened her eyes. "Memory drifts. That's normal. Memory is supposed to drift — it's how living works. But the guild's records don't drift, and I've been comparing the two, and Ishra—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There's a year missing from my childhood. Everyone has gaps. But mine isn't a gap. I've checked it the way I check everything. The edges are too clean. Something was cut out and the cut was cauterized."

Ishra said nothing. Above them the rigging creaked its long slow litany, and somewhere forward, Denni laughed in his sleep.

"So I went looking for the payment," Noor continued. "One year of a child's memories would be a large withdrawal. Large withdrawals leave large entries. And I found it — or I found where it should be. There's a payment logged against my mother's name, the winter I was nine. Except my mother was never a keeper. She never set foot in the Guildhall. She was a fishwife from the Stilt Quarter who couldn't read." Her voice came out flat and strange. "The entry is dated eleven years before the guild hired me."

The silence stretched. Ishra's jaw worked once.

"Say the rest," she said. "You've got a rest. I can hear it."

"The payment wasn't mine." Noor's hands had stopped shaking; the fury underneath had burned the tremor away, leaving something harder and colder and far more useful. "My mother sold a year of my childhood to the guild before I was ever theirs. Or — no. Listen to me, I'm getting the order wrong, and the order is everything." She pressed her palms flat against her knees. "The crown doesn't wait for keepers. It reaches. It reached into my house when I was nine years old and took a year of me, and the guild recorded it as a *payment*, a voluntary offering, a small mercy — and then when I showed aptitude with numbers, when I was fourteen and useful, they hired the girl they'd already hollowed, because a keeper with a missing year is a keeper who already knows how to live around an absence. Who won't ask too many questions about the holes. Who will be *grateful* for the next small mercy, because she remembers — some part of her remembers — what it feels like to lose something and survive it."

"Gods below," Ishra breathed.

"And the worst of it—" Noor laughed, and hated the sound. "The worst of it is that I remember the year ended. I remember my mother crying at midwinter and I thought it was about money. I remember a boy named Teo who lived on our stair and then didn't, and I assumed his family moved. I built explanations for all of it. Children do. We're excellent bookkeepers of our own lives — we make every column balance, even the ones someone else emptied." She looked up at Ishra, and the lantern light must have shown her something, because the captain's face had gone grim as weather. "It was never a gap. It was a harvest. My first harvest. They took the crop early to prove the field was fertile."

* * *

She worked until the sky went grey at the edges, and when the crew gathered for the morning meal — hard bread, salt fish, Denni nursing a mug of chicory he pretended was coffee — she stood, and she did something she had never done in the guildhall in eleven years of service.

She read the accounts aloud.

"This is Keeper Maren," she said. "Two hundred and eleven years after the Binding. In one spring, the crown took from her: the memory of her sister's face. The sound of her own wedding vows. The name of the village she grew up in. The guild recorded these as four separate payments totaling less than a standard annual levy. Four payments. Four pieces of a heart, invoiced as crumbs." She turned the page in her mind; the crew watched her turn nothing at all. "This is Keeper Ossian. They took his wife's laughter first, then her name, then the fact that he had ever been married at all — in that order, over six years, so that each loss could be explained to him as ordinary grief. When they finished, he requested a transfer to the inner hall. He said he wanted to be closer to the work. He said it was the only thing that mattered to him anymore."

"That's not protection," Denni said quietly. "That's farming."

"That's farming," Noor agreed. "And here is the entry that matters most." She looked down at her own hands. "Here is me. Age nine. One payment, logged under my mother's name, amount unrecorded — because the guild does not record what it takes from children. It records only that the child was later suitable."

The wind moved through the rigging. Nobody ate.

"I spent three days looking for another explanation," Noor said. "I'm telling you honestly — I wanted one. If the payments were random, if the crown was merely hungry the way a storm is hungry, then the Master of Keys' offer might even have been true. Take the crumbs, keep the loaf, serve, survive. But it isn't random. It has never been random. Three hundred years of entries and every single one follows the same rule: love first, duty last. They are not feeding the crown to keep it quiet. They are *training* it. Teaching it to strip a person down to obedience and calling the stripping mercy." She closed the ledger that existed nowhere but inside her. "And I am the next keeper on the schedule. The moon is turning. And they offered me a contract written in the same hand that wrote all of this."

Ishra rose. She crossed the deck slowly, and Noor braced herself for pity — she had seen pity coming for people her whole life and had learned to step aside from it — but what Ishra did was take Noor's hands in both of hers, salt-cracked and strong, and hold them like something being counted.

"Say what you're going to do," Ishra said. "Not what you're afraid of. What you're going to do."

Noor looked at her. At Denni with his chicory, at Brannic by the tiller pretending not to listen and listening with his whole body, at the grey sea and the thinning moon still hanging pale above the morning water like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

"I'm going to finish the audit," she said. "All of it. Every keeper, every payment, every forged mercy back to the founders' own hands. Because a bargain that was made by fraud is not a bargain — it's a theft with paperwork. And thefts can be reversed." Her voice steadied as it went, the way it always did when the numbers came right. "Aldric told us the vault holds the original bargain. Fine. But bargains have terms, and terms have flaws, and the people who wrote this one were so busy protecting themselves from the crown that they never once audited it. They never asked what the crown was learning from three hundred years of practice." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and she was glad of it. "I have. And I know exactly which clause they never read closely."

"Which one?" Denni asked.

"The consideration clause," Noor said. "What's given, and why. Every valid bargain requires it. And a crown that has spent three centuries taking love by force has never once received anything *given freely* — which means it has no idea what happens to a debt when the debtor pays it in love instead of fear." She looked east, where the sun was coming up the color of new ink. "Debts paid in fear compound. Debts paid in love—"

She stopped. Not because she didn't know the answer.

Because Aldric's voice had come to her, faint and urgent across the water in her mind, clearer than it had ever been, and it was not reciting her childhood this time.

It was saying her name. And beneath it, rising like a tide through the words, she heard the other voice — the crown's — and for the first time she understood what she was hearing, because she had just spent the night reading three hundred years of its appetite.

It wasn't hungry.

It was *lonely*. And loneliness, Noor knew better than anyone alive, was the one debt that could not be audited — only answered.

"We need to go faster," she said aloud. "It's starting to speak to him again. And I think—" She swallowed. "I think when it finishes speaking, it means to take him entirely. Before I ever reach the vault. Before I get my chance."

Ishra was already turning toward the helm. "Brannic. All canvas. Now."

The *Vagrant Tide* leaned into the wind, and Noor stood at the stern with her ghost-ledgers closed and her fury banked like a coal, watching the moon fade into the daylight sky — still there, still turning, still counting down — and began, silently, in her own small precise hand, to draft the counter-bargain she intended to make the crown sign.
