## 14. Overland to the Gate

The Vagrant Tide had died burning on the shoals off Corren's Teeth, and what remained of her crew walked now across a country that hated everything she'd ever loved.

Ishra had crossed the Salt Flats twice in her life — once as cargo smuggled in a water-barrel, once as a woman running from a marriage — and both times she'd sworn never again. The flats were not land. They were the sea's abandoned argument with the sky: a crust of white stretching to every horizon, cracked into hexagonal plates like the shell of something vast that had died mid-dream. By day the sun came down through it and up again until there was no shadow deep enough to hide in. By night the cold rose out of the ground itself, and the salt sang — a thin mineral whine underfoot, as if the whole plain were one struck bell that had forgotten how to stop.

They walked in single file because the crust would not bear them abreast. Denni led, reading the map by daylight and memory by dark. Behind him came Brannic hauling the water, then the twins, then Noor, then Ishra at the rear where she could watch everyone's backs and no one could watch hers.

Three days out from the wreck, they had seen the first riders. Not guild cavalry — those would come later, slower, heavier — but outriders, scouts, men who rode the flats' edge the way gulls ride a wake, waiting for something to die.

And behind them all, fainter than the wind but never quite gone, came the singing.

The Hollow Choir did not hurry. That was the horror of them. They sang as they walked, a low three-note phrase that Ishra could almost name and could not stop hearing, and each day the phrase gained a syllable of *her*.

*Is-hra.* Just the shape of it. A hook without bait yet.

She spat into the salt, which was a waste of water and she knew it.

"Captain." Noor had fallen back beside her, which was against file discipline and typical of her. "Your lips are bleeding."

"Salt does that."

"You've been chewing them since noon." The clerk dug in the satchel she wore crosswise — the satchel with the map in it, which she slept with strapped to her ribs like a second heart — and produced a twist of waxed paper. Tallow salve, ship-made, greasy and wonderful. Ishra took it without thanks because thanks cost breath and breath was rationing.

They walked. The bell-sang. Somewhere ahead, Denni called a course correction toward a line of standing stones that might have been waymarkers or might have been teeth.

It was near dusk, when the flats turned from white to rose to a bruised violet, that Aldric spoke through Noor again.

Ishra heard it before she saw it happen — the change in the clerk's stride, the hitch, the way her head tilted as if listening to a voice coming up through the floor of the world. Then Noor stopped walking and said, in a voice that was hers and layered over with something older, drier, worn thin as sailcloth:

*"The moon is failing. Do you feel it? Three hundred years I have counted its turnings and this is the thinnest moon of my captivity. When it dies, the crown feeds. It will not wait for the vault. It will reach for the nearest keeper and take what it needs where she stands."*

"Aldric," Ishra said flatly. She was tired of kings using her crewmate's mouth. "Tell him we're moving as fast as legs allow."

*"Legs,"* said the voice, and there was something terrible in it, something that was almost laughter and entirely grief. *"I have watched empires cross these flats in a season. Tell her—"* and here the voice dropped, and the king was gone from it, and only the man remained, ragged and urgent — *"tell her the crown has begun to sing back."*

Noor shuddered and came back to herself, swaying. Ishra caught her elbow. Up the line, Brannic had stopped too; he always stopped when the possession happened, planted like a mast, watching the horizon for whatever might use the moment to come.

"What did he say?" Noor asked. Her own voice again, frayed at the edges.

"He says the crown's begun to sing back." Ishra let go of her elbow. "Meaning what?"

Noor looked at her hands. In the violet light they seemed stained to the wrist, though Ishra knew it was only the salt-glare playing tricks. "Meaning it knows we're close. Meaning—" She stopped. Started again, in the careful tone she used when she was about to present an account she knew wouldn't balance. "Ishra. We need to talk about the end of this road."

"No."

"You don't know what I'm going to say."

"I know exactly what you're going to say. You've been drafting it for two days. You do that thing with your jaw when you're composing a ledger entry." Ishra started walking again, forcing the clerk to fall in or be left. "No."

Noor caught up. Of course she caught up; the woman had walked archives for twelve years and had calves like cable. "The vault holds the original bargain. Aldric told me. To unbind him, someone has to sign a counter-bargain in what they value most. For me, that's—"

"Don't."

"—it's the voyage. All of you. Every memory since the courier died on those steps. And I want you to hear me out, because I've been doing the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is *honest*, Ishra—"

Ishra wheeled on her. The file stopped behind them; the twins muttered; nobody moved away, because nobody on this crew had ever once had the good sense to move away from their captain mid-rage.

"Honest," Ishra repeated. "You want to talk to me about honest prices. On my salt. After my ship burned so you could keep walking."

"The ship burned because you chose to burn her," Noor said quietly. "To save us. That was an honest price too, wasn't it? You didn't ask the Tide's permission."

That landed somewhere under Ishra's ribs and stayed there, sharp. She turned and walked. Noor walked with her. The Choir's three notes drifted after them across the white, patient as tide.

"I buried a daughter," Ishra said at last, because the flats made people say things, that was their nature, they were a confessional four hundred miles wide. "Before any of you. Before the Tide. Fever, in a port whose name I paid a forger to make me forget how to spell." She kept her eyes forward. "I know what it costs to lose someone whole. What you're proposing isn't loss. It's worse. It's losing someone while she stands there smiling at strangers."

"And what Aldric loses if I don't is three hundred more years of being eaten alive from inside," Noor said. "By a crown your guild — *my* guild — bolted onto a dying man so they'd have a wellspring of impossible things to sell. Bottled tides. Borrowed years. Every miracle in those ledgers was wrung out of him drop by drop, and every thirteenth moon they topped him up with a keeper's memories, and they told the keepers it was small payments, fair rates, honest trade." Her voice cracked on the last word and she pushed through it. "I reconciled those accounts for twelve years, Ishra. I balanced books built on a man screaming. Some prices are honest. This one is. I can pay it. So I will."

"No."

"You can't refuse on my behalf. There's no clause for that."

"I'm the captain."

"This isn't your ship."

"It's my *crew*," Ishra snarled, "and you're on the manifest."

For a moment neither of them walked. Behind them the line waited. Ahead the standing stones rose out of the dusk like the fingers of something buried, reaching.

Then Noor did the thing Ishra hated most, which was go calm. Clerk-calm. Ledger-calm. "When the Choir took Denni's name," she said, "you held him all night and read him the crew list until he remembered which one he was. You told me afterward — you said, *a name isn't yours anyway, it's a thing everyone else agrees to call you.*" She shrugged, small and terrible. "So agree harder. That's all I'm asking. Agree to call me by what I chose. I chose this. Write it down. Witness it. That's all a bargain is — a thing witnessed. You taught me that too, on the night you told me why you owed Sef the blood-debt. You said debts are just love with a due date."

"That's not what I said."

"It's exactly what you said. I wrote it down." A ghost of a smile. "I write everything down. You'll still have me, even when I don't have you. Everything I gave will exist in your keeping. Isn't that worth—"

"No," Ishra said, and her voice came out low and final as a keel touching bottom. "Here's my counter-offer, clerk, and you can enter it in whatever book you like. Nobody spends you. Not the crown, not the guild, not the bargain, and not yourself. If the price of freeing that king is the whole of you, then we find another price. We renegotiate. You taught me that word. Contracts have seams. You found one in the Choir's own song, in a canyon, with knives at our throats. Don't tell me the founders wrote a perfect bargain. Nobody writes perfect bargains. They were scared old liars hiding a crime, and crimes leak."

Noor stared at her. The violet light was dying; the first stars were coming up through the salt like bubbles rising through ice.

"You'd gamble the thirteenth moon on a seam," she said.

"I'd gamble anything except you," Ishra said. "That's the whole of my ledger. Now walk. We're losing the stones."

* * *

They reached the standing stones at full dark and made camp in their lee, and it was only then, huddled against the cold rising out of the ground, that Ishra understood what the stones were.

Waymarkers. Guild work — she recognized the cut of them, the same masons' marks she'd seen on the Saltgate quays, worn nearly smooth. But between the stones, the salt had melted. Had *receded*, in long dead channels, as though something beneath had once been warm enough to keep the ground clear. A road, maybe. Or a vein.

Or a throat.

Denni crouched over the map by lamplight, and Ishra watched his face do something complicated. "Captain," he said. "It changed again. But it's not shifting away from us anymore." He turned the map so the light fell on it. The ink routes, which had wandered nightly for weeks like spilled mercury, had gone straight. All of them. Converging on the gate's mark like threads pulled taut.

"It's not drawing us a path," Noor said softly, kneeling beside him. "It's showing us where we already are. We're inside the route now. We've been inside it since the ship burned."

Brannic laughed, short and grim, from the darkness. "Comforting. The haunted map thinks we're on schedule."

"On whose schedule?" one of the twins asked.

Nobody answered, because at that moment Noor went rigid, and the voice came up through her, and this time it did not bother borrowing her cadence at all. It filled her like wind fills a sail, and it carried across the camp, and it was *loud*, and it was *afraid*:

*"It sings my mother's name. Do you understand? Three hundred years and tonight it has begun to sing my mother's name, and I cannot remember her face, and the crown is eating what is left of me faster than the bargain allows, because it knows, it KNOWS the keeper is coming to unmake it — it will not wait for the full moon, it will not wait for the vault, it will come UP—"*

The lamplight guttered. Under their feet, faint but unmistakable, the salt sang back — the same three notes the Hollow Choir used, answered by a fourth note that no human throat could hold, rising from somewhere very far below.

Noor collapsed forward into Denni's arms, gasping, human again, tears freezing on her cheeks. "He's still in there," she whispered. "He's still *in there*, Ishra. But not for long."

Ishra stood, and looked south along the dead channels toward the gate she could not yet see, and felt the thinning moon above her like a lid being lifted off a pot.

Behind them, far off across the white, the Choir's song added a new syllable to her name.

Ahead, the ground began, very gently, to shake.

"Break camp," Ishra said. "We run."
