## 15. The Choir's Last Verse

The canyon found them the way a trap finds a foot.

It hadn't been there on Ishra's map — or rather, it had, but drawn as a shallow gully, a half-day's inconvenience between the salt flats and the high pass. What they walked into at dusk was a wound in the earth a hundred feet deep, its walls banded red and white like muscle and bone, so narrow in places that Noor could touch both sides with her outstretched hands. The salt flats behind them had gone the color of an old bruise, and ahead the canyon bent twice before opening onto the pass, and somewhere beyond that, three days' hard walking, the gate.

Three days. The moon overhead was a coin worn nearly smooth, one night from fullness of its thirteenth turn. Noor could feel it now even without sleeping — a slow tugging behind her eyes, as if something were reading her by candlelight, deciding which pages to turn first.

"We camp at the second bend," Ishra said, voice low. "Walls like this funnel sound. If anything sings, I want to hear it coming."

Noor had learned to read the crew's silences over the weeks of the crossing, and the silence that fell at Ishra's words was its own kind of map. Denni's hand went to the pouch at his belt where his name lived now — he'd carried it written on paper since the port, taking it out at night to make sure it was still his. Sefa, the old quartermaster who spoke perhaps forty words a day, spat carefully into the dust. Even Brannoc, who feared nothing that bled, shifted his weight toward the canyon mouth.

They all knew what hunted them. They'd heard it at a distance two nights ago, a sound like a hymn played underwater, and Noor had watched Denni press his palms over his ears until his face went white.

* * *

The Choir came at moonrise, and it did not come quietly, because it had never needed to.

The first note arrived before the singers did — a single held tone threading down the canyon like water finding a crack. Noor felt it pass through her teeth. Then the harmony gathered, six or seven voices braided together, and beneath the beauty of it she heard the structure: not a song but a *filing*, each note a finger running down a ledger column, searching for a particular entry.

"Run," Ishra said. "Don't listen. Run."

They ran. The canyon floor was treacherous with salt-crusted stone, and the walls threw their footsteps back at them multiplied, so that they sounded like twenty people fleeing instead of six. The singing followed, unhurried, patient as tide. That was the horror of it, Noor thought as she ran, lungs burning — the Choir never hurried, because names could not outrun their own unraveling.

Then the bend, and the canyon opened into a bowl walled on every side, and the singing stopped, and the Choir stepped out of the shadows along the rim like figures stepping out of a painting.

Seven of them. They wore gray robes gone stiff with salt, hoods thrown back, and their faces were ordinary — tired, travel-worn, human — which made it worse. At their center stood a broad woman with a conductor's stillness, her throat moving gently, always gently, even in silence. Noor understood with a lurch that the woman wasn't resting her voice. She was holding the others' song inside her mouth, coiled, ready.

"Hollow Choir," Ishra said. Her hand was on her knife, and Noor saw clearly that the knife meant nothing here. "There's blood-money in you, then. Guild's gold."

"The guild pays," the conductor said. Her voice was roughened, like someone who had sung past the limits of singing. "We keep our own accounts." Her eyes moved across the crew, itemizing, and settled last on Noor. "The clerk first. The guild wants the map and the girl. The rest of you are a convenience."

"And us?" Denni's voice cracked on the second word.

"You," the conductor said, with terrible gentleness, "are already entered."

She opened her mouth, and the Choir sang.

Noor had braced for pain, and there was none — that was the unbearable part. The harmony wrapped around Ishra like warm water, and Ishra staggered, caught herself on the rock, and Noor watched the color drain from her captain's face as if something were being poured out of her. The song was saying a name. It was saying *Ishra Vael*, syllable by syllable, and each repetition took a little of the word apart — the way if you repeat any word enough times it becomes only sounds, only breath, only nothing.

"Ishra!" Noor lunged for her, and Ishra looked up at her, and for one heartbeat there was nothing in her eyes. Not fear. Not recognition. Just the flat polite blank of a stranger looking at a stranger.

Then she blinked, and herself again, furious and terrified. "It pulls loose in layers," she rasped. "I'm still — mostly — Noor, whatever you're going to do—"

The song deepened. On the canyon walls, Noor saw the sound made visible: faint lines of light tracing themselves in the salt crust, writing and rewriting the same word, and each rewrite was fainter than the last. A document erasing itself.

*Ishra is going to die,* she thought, with absurd clarity, *and I am going to watch, and there is nothing in my satchel but ink.*

And then the thought turned over, because that was wrong. That had never been right. There had never been anything in her satchel but ink, and ink was the whole world.

"Noor—" Denni grabbed her arm. "We have to run, we have to get her out of—"

"No." She pulled free, dropped to one knee, and dragged her satchel open against the stone. Knife out. Left palm. She didn't let herself think about the cut; thinking was for afterward. Blood welled dark in the moonlight, and she pressed her palm to a page torn from the back of her ledger book — good paper, guild stock, paper that held a contract the way stone held heat.

The guild trained its clerks in one doctrine above all others, drilled into them through ten years of apprenticeship until it sat in the spine like marrow: *a contract binds the parties exactly as written, and no further.* Every ward she'd ever maintained on the gate was a contract. Every impossible trade that moved through Saltgate's ledgers — bottled tides, borrowed years — was a contract. And the Hollow Choir, whatever else they were, whatever songs they carried in their ruined throats, were hired. Hired meant contracted. Contracted meant *written*.

The guild had drafted those contracts, centuries of them, in the same house style as everything else. And Noor had spent ten years reconciling the guild's books, which meant she had read things no one read — the boilerplate, the clauses, the fine print at the bottom of the page where clerks' corrections accreted like sediment. She had found the Choir's standing contract once, filed under External Instruments, and she had almost laughed at it, alone in the archives at midnight, because it contained a mistake. A beautiful, ancient, load-bearing mistake.

Clause Nine. *Services rendered may be discharged by satisfaction of equivalent obligation.*

In plain speech: the Choir could be paid in something other than gold. In plain speech: the contract could be *bought out*. The guild's drafters had left the clause in because it seemed harmless — what equivalent obligation could anyone ever offer an order of assassins? But equivalence was not a matter of value. Equivalence was a matter of *kind*. And the Choir's service was the unmaking of bound things.

So was hers. She had spent her whole life binding: wards, ledgers, seals, gates. Binding was just unmaking done slowly, done carefully, done in reverse. She was, by kind, an equivalent. More than that —

More than that, she knew what the Choir wanted. Not gold. You did not give your voice to the guild's machinery for gold. She had seen their faces just now: tired, human, *ordinary*. People who had traded everything for the contract and never read the clause that might have freed them.

"Listen to me!" she shouted, and her voice cracked against the canyon walls, and the singing faltered — barely, a quarter-note's hesitation, but she heard it. She stood, bleeding hand raised, the page pressed against her forearm to keep the wind from taking it. "Holders of the instrument executed between the House of Salt and the company styled the Hollow Choir! I invoke Clause Nine!"

The song broke apart like a dropped rope.

The silence rang. Seven faces turned toward her, and in five of them she saw nothing but the flat attention of instruments. But in two — the young man at the end, and the conductor herself — she saw something crack open.

"That clause is dead letter," the conductor said. Her voice shook on *dead*, and Noor knew she was lying, and knew that she knew.

"It's live letter. It's been live for four hundred years. Your contract binds you to render services until discharged, and discharge requires only *equivalent obligation* — obligation of the same kind, freely offered." Noor's blood was dripping off her fingertips onto the salt crust, and she let it. "Your services are the unbinding of named things. Here is my obligation of the same kind." She set the page down on a flat stone and began to write, fast, the blood thinning in the cold, spitting on it when she needed to, cutting a fresh line across the pad of her thumb. "I, Noor Oyelaran, clerk of the Saltgate Guild, being myself a bound thing — bound to a gate, bound to a bargain older than your contract, bound to be consumed at the turn of this moon — do hereby offer my own unbinding as satisfaction of all obligations outstanding under your instrument."

Denni made a small wounded sound. "Noor, no — that's *you*, that's the crown, you can't pay them with—"

"Equivalent in kind, Denni. Not in degree." She didn't look up. Her handwriting stayed steady, because her hands had been trained by people who believed that a trembling hand wrote void contracts, and she would not write a void contract tonight. "My unbinding is scheduled. It will happen whether they take payment or not. Which means it costs me nothing they can value more — and it means, hear me, that the guild has been paying you in gold for a debt it could have discharged four centuries ago with a single sentence."

That landed. She watched it land, in the two cracked-open faces, spreading outward. The conductor's lips had stopped their constant gentle motion.

"And there's more," Noor said softly. "Because whoever accepts satisfaction on behalf of the company discharges it for *all* parties. That's not my drafting. That's yours — Common Instrument, Third Recension, the law your order predates. One signature ends it. For everyone. Forever." She finally lifted her head and met the conductor's eyes. "You could walk away. Names intact. Voices your own. All you have to do is stop pretending you never wanted to read the fine print."

The canyon held very still. Above the rim the worn coin of the moon hung at its turning point, and Noor felt it tug at her, patient, certain, and understood suddenly that she was racing more than the Choir. Somewhere behind her own ribs, the crown was already paging through her, and it would start — she knew the pattern now, she'd audited the guild's hidden columns — with love.

"Finish the instrument," the conductor said.

Noor wrote the final lines kneeling in her own blood: the offer, the acceptance line, the witness line. When she reached the signature block for the Choir she held the page up, and for a long moment nothing moved.

Then the young singer at the end of the line pulled back his hood entirely, and Noor saw that his throat was scarred — old scars, surgical-straight, the marks of a price paid young. He crossed the bowl of the canyon, took the pen from her bloody fingers as if it were something holy, and signed.

The conductor closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet.

"I held this order together for thirty years," she said, to no one, to the moon. "Thirty years telling myself the gold was honor." She signed beneath him, her letters tall and broken like a child's.

One by one the others came forward. Seven signatures, and as the last pen stroke dried, the change moved through them like a tide going out — Noor felt it in her teeth the way she'd felt the song, a great tension releasing, some vast instrument finally allowed to rest. The gray of their robes seemed literally to lighten. The young man laughed, a rusty, astonished sound, and said something to the singer beside him, and she answered by *name* — his name, given back, tested like a healed limb.

The conductor stood before Noor last. Up close her eyes were the pale gray of weathered wood.

"You knew we'd want it," she said. "Even if we hated you for the manner of it."

"You'd have killed us otherwise. I decided to gamble on the wanting." Noor folded the contract with her uninjured hand and held it out. "Custody is yours now. It's your discharge papers. Keep it somewhere the guild will never audit."

The conductor took it, and then did something no enforcer, no assassin, no instrument of the guild had done in all of Noor's life.

She bowed. Low, and long, and to the waist.

"The guild will send others," she said, straightening. "But not us, and not quickly — they'll need to find singers willing to bind anew, and after tonight, in our world, word travels." A ghost of a smile. "Singers talk."

* * *

Ishra was sitting where the song had left her, knees up, shaking, with Denni's jacket around her shoulders and Sefa silently pressing a flask into her hands. When Noor knelt beside her, Ishra caught her wrist — the cut one — and turned it over, and looked at the ragged bandage Noor had tied, and looked up.

"You paid them with yourself."

"I paid them with something that was already spent. There's a difference." Noor kept her voice level, and knew from Ishra's face that the levelness fooled neither of them. "The crown takes my memories in three nights regardless. The Choir's claim attaches to the same debt. First claim satisfied; the crown can fight over what's left with the founders' ghosts." It was even mostly true. It was true enough to say aloud.

Ishra's grip tightened. "You keep doing arithmetic with your own life, clerk. One day the sums are going to come due all at once, and I won't be able to—"

"Then don't lend me anything," Noor said, and heard her own voice go soft in the middle of it. "Just — stay owed to me a little longer. All of you. It helps. Being owed to people. It's the only collateral I've got that the crown can't put a price on."

Above them, the freed singers were climbing the canyon rim in the moonlight, unhurried now, seven ordinary tired human figures against the stars. One of them — the young man — paused at the top and called down, his voice ragged and real and entirely his own:

"Clerk! When you get to your gate — check who countersigned the founding bargain. We sang for the guild thirty years and never once asked. Check the *names*."

Then he was gone over the rim, and the canyon was quiet except for the wind.

Noor sat back on her heels, and the words settled into her like a hook into a fish. *Check the names.* She had read the founding bargain a hundred times in the archives — the terms, the clauses, the schedule of payments. But signatures were filing, not content. Clerks skimmed signatures. Clerks were trained to.

She looked up at the moon, one night from full, hanging over the pass and the drowned vault and the king wearing his crown like a shackle.

"Aldric," she murmured, to the tugging behind her eyes, to the listening thing in her blood. "Who signed for you?"

Far away, beneath three centuries of hunger, something stirred — and began, slowly, to answer.
