## 18. Unbinding

I have been a glove for three hundred years. You forget, after the first century, what it is to be a hand.

The vault is flooded to my knees, black water lit by the founders' ghost-lanterns, and the crown is singing through my skull the way it always sings when it is about to feed — that sweet, grinding harmony, like a millstone turning through honey. It is stronger here than it has ever been, because here is where it was made, and because the moon above the salt flats has come around to its thirteenth turn, and because the woman standing on the vault's threshold is exactly the kind of keeper it has always preferred: the kind who loves.

I can taste her from across the water. That is the crown's gift to me, its single mercy, though it does not feel like mercy — I taste what it intends to eat. It tastes her like I once tasted wine. Salt and ink and stubbornness, and beneath all of it, banked like a coal wrapped in ash, an enormous and unguarded affection for a ship's crew she has known barely a season. It is already sorting them. It has already begun choosing which name it will take first.

I have watched it do this to nine keepers before her. I have watched from inside my own head, screaming into a throat that no longer belonged to me, as it picked a keeper's mother out of her memory the way a gull picks a soft thing out of a tidepool, and swallowed, and left the woman standing in the guildhall wondering why she felt nothing when the letters came.

No, I want to say to her. Not that way. There is no *that way* — the founders made sure of that — but not *all* of it, girl, not the whole ledger, you do not have to give it everything—

But my mouth is not mine. It has not been mine since the night I signed the first bargain, when I was a king with a wasting sickness and an army at my back and a kingdom that would not survive my dying, and three founders of a merchant guild came to me with a crown of salt-iron and a contract written in a hand so beautiful it looked like mercy. I remember the moment of my last free breath the way a drowning man remembers the surface: one flash of lamplight, the smell of tallow, the founder called Vessia saying *it will only hurt until it doesn't.*

It never stopped hurting. It just stopped being mine to feel.

* * *

The water between us shivers. Noor Oyelaran has waded in to her thighs without hesitating, and the founders' ghosts have turned from their eternal desks to watch her, their ledger-pens stilled for the first time in three centuries. She holds the map's final page above the waterline, and the page is burning without being consumed, and the light of it turns the flooded vault into something out of the oldest stories — gold on black, a sunken chapel, a coronation held at the bottom of the sea.

She is reading the founding bargain aloud. I know every word; I have heard them recited at every feeding, every thirteenth moon, for three hundred years, in the voices of nine keepers grown progressively more obedient and less themselves. But I have never heard them read like this. She does not read them as scripture. She reads them as an auditor reads a fraudulent account — slowly, precisely, with a rising contempt that makes the ghosts flinch on their benches.

"—and the crown shall take as payment that which the keeper most values, in the measure the crown elects, in perpetuity, the guild holding no liability for the condition of the keeper's soul—"

"*No liability,*" she repeats, and her voice cracks against the vault stone. "You wrote yourselves out of the cost. Three hundred years of payments and not one of you ever owed anything." She looks up at the ghosts — Vessia, Ondrel, the third one whose name even the crown has worn away from me — and something in her face makes even me afraid. "I'm reopening the account."

The crown roars through me. Not sound — pressure, a fist closing inside my skull, the millstone lurching toward its feed. It has been patient with her. It has let her walk the salt flats, survive the Choir, burn a page of its own map, because it believed she was walking herself to the table. Keepers always come to the table. That is the elegance of the founders' design: the gate calls them, the moon drives them, and by the time they stand in this water they are so worn down that surrender feels like their own idea.

But Noor Oyelaran has not come to the table. She has come to the books.

She wades the last distance to the plinth at the vault's heart, where the original contract lies under glass older than glass, and she sets the burning page down beside it, and she takes out — I feel the crown's attention sharpen to a needle's point — a pen. An ordinary pen. A clerk's pen, nib worn at the angle of a woman who has written ten thousand entries in ten thousand ledgers.

And she begins to write.

* * *

I know the form. Gods help me, I know the form better than any living soul, because I dictated its ancestor on the worst night of my life. A counter-bargain. The founders built the mechanism into the vault because they were merchants before they were anything else, and a merchant's law must run both directions or it runs nowhere. Any bargain signed in the vault can be unmade by a bargain signed in the vault — but the counter-signature must be paid in what the signer values most, freely given, fully witnessed, and the founders were confident no one would ever dare. They were confident because the only people who ever reached the vault were keepers, and keepers arrive already halved.

Noor is not halved. She is whole, and furious, and she is writing in a hand so steady it frightens me more than the crown does.

The crown understands, in the last moments, exactly what is happening. It throws everything it has at her. The water goes black and boiling around her thighs; the ghosts rise from their desks with their pens drawn like knives; the vault fills with the voices of every keeper it ever ate, my own voice among them, crying out in the tones it has learned will stop a loving heart — *help me, don't, I'm afraid, you're killing me* — and I feel it find the shape of her crew and press down, hard, showing her Ishra's throat bared in a canyon, Denni's unraveling name, the Vagrant Tide going down with all hands—

She writes through it.

I will remember this as long as I am anything at all: the image of a clerk standing waist-deep in a haunted sea, ink and salt water running down her wrist, writing the unbinding with the crown screaming every grief it has ever harvested directly into her ears, and her hand never once faltering. Not because she does not hear. Because she has spent her life reconciling impossible accounts, and this — this is simply the last entry, and she will not leave it unbalanced.

The clause is simple. The founders' law demands the counter-signature name its price, and she names it, and the crown goes silent — truly silent, for the first time in three centuries — as it reads.

*In payment for the unbinding of Aldric, last of his name, I surrender the whole of my memory of the crew of the Vagrant Tide: every hour, every kindness, every name. Freely given. Fully witnessed.*

The ghosts stop. Even the water stops.

She signs it. Noor Oyelaran, in the hand the guild taught her, the hand they will never again own.

* * *

What happens next happens to me.

Three hundred years of the crown come off like a scab the size of the sky. There is no gentleness in it. The founders' contract tears along the line she has written, and the crown — salt-iron, hungry, older than its own makers now — lifts from my head the way a hand lifts from a drowning man's chest, and I come up into my own body gasping, and my first breath in three hundred years tastes of brine and blood and freedom, and I weep like a king at a funeral, because that is what it is.

I am old. That is the first thing. The bargain held my body at the edge of death for three centuries and now the debt comes due all at once: my joints are ash, my hair falls white into the water, my hands are a dying man's hands. I do not care. They are *my* hands. I flex them in the black water and feel the cold and feel the ache and feel, underneath everything, the enormous ordinary animal relief of a body that belongs to itself.

The crown falls. It hits the flooded stone and does not shatter — things like that do not shatter — it *unmakes*, unraveling outward in a shriek of released hunger, and everywhere its power has rooted, the roots die. I feel it go. I feel the guild's founding bargain collapse like a tide going out: the wards on the gate above us thinning to gauze, the enforcers' oaths dissolving mid-word, the ledgers in a thousand guildhalls across the world opening to blank pages. Three hundred years of compounding interest, called due in a single breath. Vessia's ghost makes a sound I will hear in my dying — which, now, will be soon — the sound of a merchant watching her fortune burn. The three founders close their books. I do not know if they choose to or are made to. Their pens go down, their lanterns gutter, and the vault is darker and quieter than it has been since the night they built it.

Noor stands at the plinth with the pen still in her hand.

* * *

I wade to her. It takes a long time; I am an old man crossing a flooded room, and every step costs me. She does not turn. The light has gone out of the burning page — the map has finished its work, and the last of it drifts on the water in white flakes, a route with no traveler now.

"Noor," I say. My voice is a ruin. I have not used it for anything but screams and the crown's recitations in three hundred years. "Keeper Unbound."

She turns.

And I see it happen. I watch the payment collect.

It is not dramatic. That is the obscenity of it. It is not fire or light or a cry. It is a woman standing in black water, and her eyes going — not empty, exactly. *Unfurnished.* The way a room looks when the movers have come and gone and the shape of the furniture is still printed on the floorboards. She looks at me with perfect courtesy, the courtesy of a clerk for a stranger, and she says, in a voice with no fear in it because there is nothing left in it to be afraid *with*:

"I'm sorry — do I know you? I was meant to be meeting someone here. I think." Her brow creases, a small honest confusion. "I can't think who."

The crown is dead. The bargain is dead. And the law the founders wrote holds anyway, holds like gravity, holds like the sea: what is freely given cannot be taken back. Not even by the king she freed. I search myself for the power to undo it and find only an old man's frailty and a debt I did not choose but benefited from, because her love for her crew bought my freedom, and there is no version of the ledger in which I did not spend it.

"No," I say, and my ruined voice breaks on it. "No, I don't suppose you do."

She looks past me, up, toward the long stair and the sealed door and the gray light of a moon completing its turn, and something in her — not memory, not love, something older and dumber than either, the animal that chooses before the mind is built — tilts toward that light, as if she has heard her name called from very far away.

"Someone's waiting for me," she says, uncertain. "I feel like someone's waiting."

* * *

They come down the stair while the vault is still draining — the smugglers, the found family, the debtors of a dead courier. The captain first, Ishra, sword out, taking the steps like the water is an enemy; the forger boy behind her; the others crowding the stairwell with lamps. They find their king standing in the shallows and their keeper standing beside him, and the keeper looks at all of them with a polite, blank attention, and steps back — a half-step, barely anything, the distance you keep from strangers.

I watch it land on Ishra like a blade. I have been worn by the crown for three hundred years; I know what a wound that doesn't bleed looks like.

"Noor," she says.

"Hello," Noor says kindly. "Were you waiting for me? I thought someone was."

I tell them what happened. All of it, quickly, because I am dying in the ordinary way now and I will not waste breath: the counter-bargain, the price, the law that cannot be reversed. I tell them what she paid. I watch them carry it, each in their own way — the boy's face going white, the captain's jaw setting like mortar — and when I finish, Ishra looks at me with an expression I remember from my own war councils, from men deciding whether a cost was acceptable.

"Can you fix it?" she says. "You were a king. You're a *thing out of stories* now. Can you give it back?"

I am quiet for a moment. The water laps. Above us, through the opened gate, I can hear the guild's empire quietly dying, and somewhere far off, the first honest silence this coast has known in three hundred years.

"No," I say. "What is freely given, the law protects. Even from me. Even from love." I look at the woman who bought my life with her heart and stands among her own people like a guest. "But the law says nothing about what was written. And she is a keeper of ledgers, and keepers of ledgers—" I stop, because I am suddenly, absurdly certain. "Keepers of ledgers always keep duplicates."

Ishra turns to the boy, Denni, and something passes between them that I am three hundred years too old to name and too newly free to miss.

Above us, the moon finishes its turn, and begins — ordinary, patient, mercifully only itself — to wane. And Noor Oyelaran, Keeper Unbound, empty-eyed among strangers, asks the captain of the Vagrant Tide if she might come aboard, because she has the strangest feeling she has somewhere to be.
