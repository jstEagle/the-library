## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The last river went in at dusk.

Isandro Voss dipped his pen a final time, touched it to the parchment's lower edge, and drew the water's course with a single unbroken line — the way it had appeared to him three nights ago, silver-bright and impossibly wide, cutting through a forest of trees that grew their leaves in spirals instead of fans. He had argued with that river for two days. Rivers on maps wanted to wander; this one refused. It ran straight as a blade through country that had no business holding it, and when he tried to bend it even slightly, the whole composition went wrong in some way he couldn't name, the way a face goes wrong when you draw the eyes a hair too far apart.

So he let the river have its way. He was learning, after twenty-nine years of this, that the maps knew better than he did.

He sat back and looked at what he'd made.

It was good work. It was always good work, if he was honest with himself, which he tried not to be too often, because honesty of that kind led nowhere useful. The coastline curled like a sleeping animal. The mountain range marched across the northern third with snowcaps rendered in strokes so fine they seemed less drawn than whispered. A city sat at the delta — he'd named it Cael Serrath without knowing why, the name arriving whole one morning while he waited for his bread to bake — its towers inked in a gray-violet wash that no pigment sold in any shop in Aldenmere could produce. He'd mixed it himself, following an instinct so strong it felt less like choice than obedience.

The trouble, as always, was that none of it existed.

Or rather: Isandro had never seen it, and no traveler had ever described it to him, and no book in the valley's archive contained so much as a rumor of its shape. The realm had no name that anyone here could give him. He called it the Spiral Country privately, for the trees, and had finished eleven maps of it over the past six years, each more detailed than the last, each arriving in his mind the way weather arrives — gradually, then all at once, usually in dreams.

His neighbors thought him a fantasist of unusual discipline. His mother, before she died, had thought him touched by something she preferred not to name aloud, because naming things in the valley gave them weight, and weight was the one thing the valley would not abide.

Isandro blew gently across the parchment to dry the river, and the valley hummed around him.

That was the only word for it, though there was no sound. The silence of Aldenmere Valley wasn't empty the way other silences were empty. It pressed. It had texture and temperature and, if you were foolish enough to listen for long, something almost like intention. Visitors from beyond the passes — there were fewer every year, the roads being what they were — often complained of headaches their first week, of a feeling like a word stuck on the tip of the tongue that never came loose. They said the valley felt *watched*, though nothing in it moved unless wind or beast or person moved it.

The people born here felt none of that. To them the hum was simply home: the particular quality of quiet in which bread rose well and grief sat quietly and nothing, ever, happened that hadn't happened before. The valley forgot things. That was its nature and its gift. Old quarrels dissolved within a season. Gravestones wore smooth within a generation. Even sorrow, given time, lost its edges here, worn round as river stone, until you could hold the memory of your dead without bleeding.

Isandro loved it. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in case the record ever mattered: he loved the valley the way you love a parent who has never once asked what you want.

He rolled the map carefully, tied it with cord, and set it atop the stack by his door — nine finished, two abandoned, one, this one, still warm with the strange satisfaction of completion. Tomorrow he would carry it down to Merrin at the waystation, who traded valley wool and dried apples for his maps and never once asked where they came from, because Merrin was the kind of man who understood that some questions are doors best left shut.

Tonight was the Centenary.

* * *

By the time Isandro walked down into the village proper, the lanterns were lit and the whole of Aldenmere had turned out into the square, which was not properly a square but a widening of the road where four lanes met and the old foundation-stone stood under its wooden shelter. A hundred years ago tonight — so the story went — the founders had set that stone and spoken the words of founding, and the valley had closed itself like a hand around them all.

There were no records of those words. There were no records of much of anything from before the founding, which the archivists attributed to fire and flood and the general carelessness of history. Isandro had spent enough evenings in the archive to know better. The gaps were too clean. It wasn't that the records had been lost; it was that they seemed to have been *erased*, deliberately, thoroughly, by someone who understood exactly what they were doing. He'd mentioned this once, years ago, to old Archivist Tull, and Tull had looked at him for a long moment with an expression Isandro had never been able to classify — fear, maybe, or pity — and said only: *"Some foundations are laid on things that shouldn't be dug up, boy. Mind your maps."*

So he minded his maps. Mostly.

The square smelled of woodsmoke and spiced cider and the particular scorched-sugar sweetness of the fritters that appeared only on festival nights. Children ran shrieking between the stalls with paper lanterns. The fiddlers had claimed the steps of the meetinghouse and were working through the Founders' Reel, which everyone danced and nobody enjoyed, because the Founders' Reel had forty-eight figures and took half an hour and left you dizzy in a way that had nothing to do with spinning.

"There he is." A hand landed on his shoulder — Tam Broadhurst, flour to the elbows, grinning. "The hermit descends. We'd begun taking wagers on whether you'd come down at all or just send another map as your proxy."

"Send Merrin," Isandro said. "Merrin would dance."

"Merrin would *charge* admission." Tam steered him toward the cider barrel with the shameless efficiency of a man who had been steering Isandro toward social obligations since they were boys. "One cup. You'll drink one cup, you'll be seen, and then you can go back up your hill and commune with your invisible countries."

"They're not invisible," Isandro said, accepting the cup. "They're just far away."

"That's what invisible means, Isandro."

He laughed, because it was true, and drank, because it was expected, and let himself be pulled into the current of the festival. This was the part he was good at, the part he'd practiced: standing in the middle of warmth and appearing to belong to it. He knew everyone here. He had delivered Tam's daughter's birth-gift map — a small thing, the valley's eastern meadows rendered in golds, hung above the child's bed. He had drawn the memorial cartouche for the flood year. He had repaired the mural in the meetinghouse twice. He was woven into this place as thoroughly as thread into cloth.

And yet.

It was a feeling he could not explain to Tam, or to anyone, without sounding ungrateful, and he was not ungrateful, he was certain of it. But standing in the square tonight, with the lanterns swinging and the reel scraping on and the whole valley gathered in its hundredth year, Isandro felt the way he imagined a guest must feel at a family celebration — welcomed, embraced, included in every photograph, and known by no one all the way down. They loved him. He believed that. But they loved a version of him that fit inside the valley, and the version that finished maps of countries no one had ever heard of — the version that woke some mornings with salt on his lips from a sea he'd never sailed — that version stayed up the hill, politely, the way you keep a trunk of letters you don't show guests.

"You're doing it again," said a voice near his elbow.

Sera Quill, the baker's widow, seventy if she was a day, sharp-eyed as a kestrel. She held her own cider and watched him with the frank amusement of someone too old to pretend.

"Doing what?"

"Being here and elsewhere at the same time." She sipped. "I've watched you do it since you were six years old, standing at your mother's knee looking at the pass road like it owed you money. Your mother used to say—" She stopped. Her face did something complicated.

"Say what?" Isandro asked, though he wasn't sure he wanted the answer.

But Sera Quill only shook her head, and when she spoke again it was about the fritters, and whatever his mother had said went wherever the valley's forgotten things go.

* * *

Later — the reel finally ended, the fiddlers resting their chins, the crowd gone soft and slow with cider — the ceremony began.

They did it the same way every year, because the valley did everything the same way, forever, gladly. The eldest resident — this year it was Sera Quill herself, to her visible disgust — approached the foundation-stone and laid her palm upon it. Then the youngest child born in the valley, a red-faced infant named Odile who slept through her own historic moment, was carried forward, and her tiny hand was pressed beside the elder's. Old hand, new hand. That was the whole rite. No words were spoken, because the words had been forgotten, or erased, or perhaps — Isandro had sometimes thought, watching the stone — because they had never needed saying. The gesture was the covenant. Continuity was the covenant. *We were here. We are here. We will be here.*

The crowd bowed its heads. The lanterns burned steady in the windless dark. And Isandro, standing at the edge of the light with his untouched second cup, felt the hum of the valley rise — just barely, just for a moment — into something that was almost, almost a voice.

It happened every year at this moment. He'd asked others about it once, cautiously, and found that nobody else felt anything at all. So he kept it to himself, the way he kept the maps, the way he kept the dreams, the way he kept everything that didn't fit inside the valley's careful forgetting.

This year, though, the feeling was different.

This year it didn't fade when the ceremony ended. It followed him as he drifted away from the square, up the lane, past the darkened archive with its shuttered windows — and he stopped, because the hum sharpened there, tugging at him like a fisherman testing a line. The archive was closed; Tull had gone home hours ago; the building sat in its familiar silence like a dog pretending to sleep.

Except it wasn't silent. Not to him. Not tonight.

Something in there was awake.

Isandro stood in the lane a long moment, cider going cold in his hand, the festival's music thin and distant behind him. Every sensible instinct he owned told him to go home, to bar his door, to finish the Spiral Country map and start the next one and let the valley keep its hundred-year secret the way it had kept everything else.

But he was a cartographer. His entire life was built on the conviction that blank spaces are not the same as empty ones, and that the pull you feel toward the unmapped is not madness but invitation.

He set the cup on Tull's windowsill, tried the door — unlocked, which it never was — and stepped inside.
