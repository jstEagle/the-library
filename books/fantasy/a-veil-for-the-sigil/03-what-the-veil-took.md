## 3. What the Veil Took

He should have burned the book.

Isandro knew this the way he knew contour lines and river bends — absolutely, without argument. The librarian had told him to burn it. His own hands had trembled when he'd carried it home through the festival crowds, wrapped in his coat like something still breathing. Every rational instinct he owned had spent the whole evening screaming at him in a small, sensible voice: *burn it, burn it, burn it.*

Instead he had set it on his worktable, weighted its corners with brass map-weights, and poured himself a glass of the plum wine his neighbor Marta pressed every autumn. The festival was still going outside. He could hear the fiddles down in the square, the pop of lantern-oil, children shrieking happily as the centenary fireworks climbed over the eastern ridge and burst against a sky that never once, not in thirty-one years, had shown him anything but ordinary stars.

The valley did not do magic. That was the whole point of the valley. Everyone said so, the way you say the sky is blue or bread needs salt — casually, without thinking about what it meant. No charms worked here. No blessings took root. Witches passing through found their spells dying on their tongues like words in a dream. Isandro had grown up inside that silence the way a fish grows up inside water: completely, and without ever once wondering what water was.

But he had wondered what was *outside* the water. That was the problem with him. That had always been the problem.

He turned another page of the prophecy book by lamplight. The paper was a century old and soft as cloth, and yet the ink that named him sat on top of it like a fresh wound — black, glossy, wet-looking. *Isandro Voss,* it said, in a hand that matched no other entry in the book. *On the night of the valley's hundredth year, the veil will take him back.*

*Back.* Not *away*. Back. As if he had been somewhere before, and everyone had simply agreed to forget it.

"Back where?" he asked the book, because talking to impossible objects was better than admitting his hands were shaking.

The ink did not answer. Ink rarely did. But as he watched, the last line of the entry — the one that had been blank an hour ago, the one he'd checked three times — began to darken, letter by letter, like frost forming on a window. He leaned close enough that his breath fogged the page.

*When the last lantern of the festival gutters, the door opens.*

Outside, distantly, the fireworks were winding down. One final volley, the fiddlers would play until dawn, and the town's last lanterns would be snuffed one by one as people staggered home full of wine and centenary pride.

Isandro looked at the clock on his mantel. Eleven forty-one.

He stood up so fast his chair went over backward, and for one absurd moment his first coherent thought was that he'd need to redraw the coastline of the southern continent, because he'd knocked his elbow into the wet ink of the bay and smeared it half an inch northward. Thirty years of discipline. Even now. Even with the world apparently coming apart at the seams, some part of him was worried about a smudged bay.

He grabbed the book. He got as far as the hearth. He knelt there with the thing held over the coals, feeling their heat climb his knuckles, and he could not do it.

Because here was the truth he'd never said out loud, not even to himself, not even at three in the morning when it pressed on his chest like a hand: every map he had ever drawn of realms he'd never seen had felt less like invention and more like *remembering*. The drowned cathedral with its bells beneath the waterline. The mountain pass shaped like a broken tooth. The city built into the ribs of something vast and dead. He'd drawn them all in dreams and woken with ink on his fingers and the certainty, fading like morning fog, that he had just come home from somewhere.

If he burned the book, he burned the only proof that he wasn't mad.

And if he wasn't mad — if any scrap of this was true — then burning it wouldn't matter anyway. Prophecies, in every story he'd ever drawn margins around, did not care whether you approved of them.

The clock struck eleven forty-five. Down in the valley, the last great firework went up — gold, enormous, hanging over the ridge like a second moon — and began, slowly, to fall.

Isandro set the book on the table. He put on his boots. He filled a satchel, mostly on instinct: pens, ink, the good knife, a coil of string, a week of hardtack. A cartographer's kit. If he was going to be dragged into an unmapped country, he was damned well going to survey it.

At eleven fifty-eight, the lamps in his house began to die.

Not all at once. One by one, in order, from the front door inward, like footsteps approaching through the dark. The lamp by the door. The hall lamp. The kitchen. Each flame didn't gutter — it simply *stopped*, mid-burn, leaving threads of smoke standing perfectly straight in air that had gone utterly still. No draft. No sound from the festival below. The fiddles had cut off between one note and the next, and the silence that replaced them was so complete he could hear his own pulse in his ears, and under it, faintly, something else.

A hum. Deep, subterranean, coming up through the floorboards and the stone beneath them and the bones of the valley itself. The sound of a held note finally being released after a hundred years.

The book lay open on the table. Its pages were turning themselves, gently, like something reading over his shoulder, and stopped at the entry with his name. The fresh ink gleamed.

*The door opens,* the page said again, though no new letters appeared. Or perhaps they had, and he simply couldn't read them anymore, because the air above the book had begun to bend.

That was the only word for it. Bend. The lamplight — the one surviving lamp, the one above his bed — bent toward the table the way light bends through water, and then the dark above the book bent too, and then the table itself seemed to lean toward a point that wasn't there, and Isandro's stomach dropped the way it drops when a stair isn't where your foot expects it.

The veil tore.

There was no other honest word. The air split along a seam he couldn't see, and through the split came a light that was wrong in a way his eyes refused to file properly — silver and green and a color he had no name for, a color he suspected existed nowhere in his world, spilling across his worktable and setting his unfinished maps glowing like windows at dusk. Cold poured through. Not winter-cold. *Old* cold. The cold of a place that had been waiting.

The wind of it hit him and he grabbed the table edge, and the table tore free of the floor like a leaf off a branch, and so did the chair, and the weights, and the book — the book spun past his face, open to his name, and then everything went into the seam at once, and Isandro Voss went with it.

* * *

Falling through the veil was not like falling.

It was like being *read*. That was the closest he could come to it, later, when language reassembled itself enough to try. Something paged through him — quickly, impatiently, the way he flipped through a sheaf of rough drafts looking for the true map underneath. It looked at his name. It looked at his blood. It lingered there, on the blood, with an attention that felt like fingers pressing a bruise, and then it flung him onward, deeper, the way you toss aside a page you've finished with.

He fell through colors. Through a darkness that had texture, like velvet worn thin. Through fragments of places he had drawn and places he hadn't and places he suddenly understood he had always been drawing — the drowned cathedral rushing past him whole and lit from within, bells swaying silently underwater; the ribbed dead city; a road of white stone running toward a horizon cracked like old glaze. His own maps, alive, enormous, indifferent to him. The mapmaker inside the map, falling between its lines.

He screamed. He was almost certain he screamed. But the veil ate sound the way his valley ate magic, and his voice left him in pieces that drifted away like sparks.

Then the seam spat him out, and there was ground, and he hit it hard enough to knock the century out of him.

For a long time there was nothing but pain and gravel and the animal business of breathing. He lay on his side on a slope of broken stone, cheek against grit, one arm folded wrong beneath him, satchel strap cutting into his chest. Air went in. Air came out. In between, the world arranged itself.

He opened his eyes.

The first thing he saw was the sky, and the sky made him forget how to breathe all over again.

It was cracked. Not cloudy, not stormy — *cracked*, the way a glazed pot cracks, fine dark fissures running from horizon to horizon, and through the fissures leaked light. Not sunlight. Something rawer, older, moving behind the sky like water behind ice, glowing silver-green and that nameless fourth color, pulsing faintly, rhythmically, like light through a wound in a living thing. Like the sky itself had been hurt, a long time ago, and had never healed, and was still bleeding.

Magic. He understood it without being told, the way he understood a map's legend on sight. This was what magic looked like when it leaked. This was a realm bleeding its own substance into the air, and the air glowing with it, and somewhere far above those cracks, he would have bet his best pen, was the seam he'd just fallen through — the hole in the roof of this wounded world, punched through from the other side.

From his valley. From the silence.

"Oh," he said aloud, and his voice worked here, cracked and small. "Oh, no."

He pushed himself up. His arm protested; he made it cooperate. He was on a hillside of shattered slate overlooking a country that stopped his heart twice before it finished beating once.

Veylmoor. He knew the name the way he knew his own — it had been in the book, in entries centuries old, in the margins of maps he'd drawn half-asleep. And it was *here*, spread below him under the leaking sky, and it was dying. He could see it the way he could see error in a false coastline: instantly, totally. Rivers ran thin and strange, their banks crusted white as if the water itself were evaporating into light. Forests stood gray and leafless in midsummer rows, not dead exactly but *drained*, trees like candles burned down to stubs. Far off, a city's towers caught the leaking glow, and half of them were simply gone — not ruined, *gone*, their foundations ending in smooth rounded edges as if reality had given up on them partway through and moved on.

And everywhere, drifting over the land like dust motes in a sunbeam, motes of light rose off the ground and slid upward into the cracks in the sky. The realm was leaking out of itself. Slowly. Patiently. The way a life bleeds out of a man who has decided not to call for help.

Isandro got his feet under him and stood, swaying, and did the only thing he knew how to do with terror: he surveyed.

Hillside: fractured slate, sloping south-southwest, maybe forty degrees. Landmark: the dead city, bearing roughly west. Second landmark: a line of mountains to the north, jagged, one peak split like a broken tooth — *his* broken tooth, the pass he'd drawn at nineteen and never believed in. Water: none visible, which in any healthy country would mean bad mapping and here meant something worse. Sky: cracked, luminous, bleeding. Time of day: unknowable; the light came from everywhere and nowhere and cast no shadows at all.

No shadows. He noticed that third, and it frightened him more than the sky. Nothing in this landscape cast a shadow. Not the rocks, not the burned-down trees, not him. Light without shadow. A world with no dark side to anything, which sounded beautiful until you understood it meant the light wasn't coming from anywhere. It was coming from *everywhere*, from the wound in the sky, from the ground itself, thin and sourceless, the glow of a thing dying.

He knelt, because his legs wanted him to, and pressed his palm flat against the slate. Under the stone, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, he felt it: the hum. The same hum that had risen through his floorboards at midnight. Here it wasn't a held note being released. Here it was a note fraying apart, unraveling strand by strand, and every strand that snapped sent up one of those motes of light.

The covenant. Whatever the book had meant, whatever the prophecy had been circling for a hundred years — it lived in that hum, and the hum was failing, and he was kneeling on the skin of a world that was coming apart because of it.

His blood ached. That was the strangest part, the part he kept returning to because the rest was too large to hold. Not injured — *aching*, deep in the forearm, in the wrist, in the pads of his fingers, the exact places a cartographer's blood lives. As if the vein-maps of him had been drawn for this country and were only now, after thirty-one years of silence, being allowed to beat in time with it.

The book. He looked around wildly and found it six feet downhill, lying open on the slate, unharmed, patient. He scrambled to it. The entry with his name had changed again. New ink, fresh as blood:

*He is through. Now the hunt begins. Run east, cartographer, and do not touch the hollow ones.*

"Hollow ones," Isandro read aloud. "What are hollow—"

He heard them before he saw them. Or rather, he heard the absence of them — a patch of the hillside's faint hum going silent, the way a chord goes silent when a hand is laid on the strings. He looked up.

Three figures stood at the crest of the slope, silhouetted against the bleeding sky.

They had been people. He could see that much, could see the shapes of coats and boots and the posture of shoulders, and one of them wore what had once been a soldier's uniform, fine and gray, buttoned with care by someone who no longer remembered buttons. But where faces should have been there was smoothness, faintly luminous, like the surface of water at dusk, and where thoughts should have been there was nothing at all — and the nothing reached toward him. He felt it reach. Felt it like a draft under a door, like attention without a mind behind it, hungry and empty and *patient*, three hollow shapes tilting forward as one, beginning to come down the slope toward him with the unhurried gait of things that had all the time in the world and no reason at all to hurry.

*Do not touch the hollow ones.* He was already backing away, heels scraping slate, the book crushed against his chest. East. Which way was east? There were no shadows. There was no sun. There was nothing in this whole luminous dying country to steer by except—

A whistle. Sharp, human, two notes, from the rocks to his left.

"Down!" a voice shouted — a woman's voice, ragged and real and the most beautiful sound Isandro had ever heard. "*Get down!*"

He got down. Light seared over his head and struck the lead hollowed one full in the smoothness of its face, and the figure stopped, swayed, and stood still as a fencepost. The other two turned — slowly, terribly — toward the rocks.

And from behind the rocks stepped a woman with a scarred sigil blazing on her bare forearm like a brand fighting to stay lit, holding a rod of pale fire in one hand and an expression of furious, exhausted recognition in her eyes as she stared — not at the hollowed ones, not at the cracked sky —

—at him.

"You," she said. "You're late."

The hollowed ones began to run.
