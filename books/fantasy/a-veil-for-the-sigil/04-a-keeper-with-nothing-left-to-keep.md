## 4. A Keeper With Nothing Left to Keep

The hollowed ones came at dusk, the way they always did.

Wren watched them from the ridge above the old mill road, her breath shallow, her scarred hand pressed flat against the dead stone in her palm. Three of them tonight. Maybe four — the fourth was lagging behind the fence line, moving with that terrible patience they all had, like puppets whose puppeteer had wandered off years ago and left the strings to the wind.

They had been people once. That was the part nobody in Veylmoor liked to remember. The regent's proclamations called them *the unmoored*, as if they'd simply lost their boats. Wren knew better. The covenant's decay didn't kill you outright. It drained you slow, siphoning whatever made you *you* through the widening cracks in the sky, until one morning you woke up and there was nothing behind your eyes but weather.

She'd seen it happen to her teachers. Her sisters. Every keeper she'd ever sworn beside.

So when she saw a man lying crumpled in the ditch below the mill road — flat on his back, arms flung wide, dressed in soft valley cloth that hadn't been seen on this side of the veil in a hundred years — her first thought was not rescue.

Her first thought was: *the prophecy said he'd come, and prophecies are liars, and I am not doing this again.*

* * *

She climbed down anyway. That was the problem with her, and always had been. Bramble said it kindly once: *You've got a door in your chest, Wren, and you keep forgetting to lock it.* Ossian said it less kindly: *Your mercy is going to get us all killed.* Pip just held her hand tighter when they walked past the hollowed ones, because Pip understood without being told that Wren would always, always go down the ditch.

The stranger was breathing. That surprised her. Most things the veil spat out arrived empty, or broken, or both. This one looked intact — whole, even, in a way that seemed almost rude given the state of the world around him. Dark hair, ink-stained fingers, a leather map case still clutched against his chest like a child holding a blanket. He'd kept hold of it falling through a tear between worlds. Something about that snagged in her, an old hook catching on old cloth.

"Hey." She crouched beside him, keeping her weight ready, her boots planted for a fast retreat. "Wake up. You've got maybe four minutes before the things on the road find you, and I'm only saving one of us."

His eyes opened. Brown, unfocused, swimming with the particular terror of a man who has just woken up inside his own nightmare. He saw her face and something in him steadied — the animal relief of finding another human shape — and then he saw her hand.

Wren watched him look at it. The blackened fingers, the sigil burned into her palm in lines that no longer glowed but rather *seeped*, faint threads of grey light crawling beneath her skin like smoke trapped under ice. Keepers' sigils were supposed to shine. Hers had been dying for eleven years, ever since the night at the second anchor, ever since—

"Your hand," he rasped. "What happened to—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended. "Don't ask me questions. Can you stand?"

He tried. He got halfway up and swayed, and she caught his arm without meaning to, and that was the mistake. Skin to skin, her bare fingers on his wrist.

The sigil woke.

Not fully — nothing of hers did anything fully anymore — but enough. A pulse of light stuttered through the scarred lines of her palm, grey flickering toward gold for one impossible heartbeat, and the stranger gasped like a man pulled out of deep water, and Wren felt it too: a pull, a recognition, a *click*, as if some vast mechanism far beneath the world had dropped one tooth into place.

She let go of him so fast she nearly fell over backward.

"No," she said. "No, no, no."

"What—" He was staring at her hand. At the fading gold ghost of light. "What was that? What did you do?"

"I didn't do anything." Her heart was slamming. "That was you. Whatever you are, whatever's in your blood — don't touch me again."

Behind them, on the road, one of the hollowed ones stopped walking. Turned its head. Not toward Wren.

Toward *him*.

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about the prophecy, and she wished she knew more:

There were five anchor-sigils that bound Veylmoor to the other realm — the green one, the quiet one, the one across the veil where magic supposedly couldn't follow. The covenant had been forged a thousand years ago by blood and oath, and for nine centuries it had held. Then, a hundred years ago, on a night the histories refused to describe plainly, the fifth anchor went dark, and everything since had been a long, slow unraveling. Four anchors failing. The sky cracked like river ice. The hollowed ones multiplying every season.

And a book, hidden in the archive of a fallen order, that said: *When the last keeper's light gutters, a bearer will come from beyond the veil, carrying the founder's blood, on the night of the hundredth year.*

Wren had read those words eleven years ago, sitting in the ashes of her order's sanctum with her hand burned and her sisters dead, and she had decided — carefully, deliberately, with the full force of her considerable stubbornness — to stop believing in them.

Hope was expensive. She'd paid for it once already, in lives she could not count twice. She wasn't going to buy it again off some stranger in a ditch, no matter how his blood lit her dying sigil.

Except.

Except that the sigil hadn't reacted to anyone in eleven years. Except that he'd come through the veil on the exact night, wearing the exact face of a valley-dweller, clutching a map case like the founder's own heir. Except that the hollowed ones had turned toward him the moment her sigil flared — drawn to the light of a living anchor-blood the way moths are drawn to a lamp, or wolves to a wounded thing.

"You're him," she said. It wasn't a question, and she hated how much she wanted it to be wrong.

"Him who?" He'd gotten to his feet now, wobbling, looking from her to the figures on the road. They were closing. Slowly. They always moved slowly; it was the worst part. "Those people — are they sick? What's wrong with them?"

"Nothing a healer can fix." She was already moving, pulling him by the sleeve toward the treeline, because if she was doing this — and her feet were deciding before her head agreed, which was how all her disasters started — then she was doing it *now*. "Keep walking. Don't run. Running makes them chase."

"What *are* they?"

"People," she said. "Or the shape people leave behind."

He made a sound she recognized. She'd made it herself, eleven years ago, standing in the sanctum doorway.

They reached the trees as the nearest hollowed one reached the ditch where he'd lain. It bent over the spot, head tilting, and Wren felt the stranger tense beside her, felt the question building in him — *why did it stop, why isn't it following* — and she answered it before he asked.

"It lost you. They see warmth, mostly. Body heat, breath, the little fires living things make." She glanced back. "But they see sigils better. And yours just rang like a bell."

"My what?" He stopped. She had to haul him forward by the arm. "I don't have a sigil. I don't have anything. I'm a cartographer. I draw maps. I was at a festival, and there was a book, and the ink—"

"The ink named you. I know." He stared at her. She sighed. "The prophecy's been circulating among what's left of my order since before either of us was born. *A bearer from beyond the veil, carrying the founder's blood.* I memorized it hoping I'd never need it. Congratulations. You're the least hopeful thing I've ever believed in."

Ahead, through the thinning pines, she could see the lights of the safehouse — Bramble's wards glowing faint amber between the trunks, the old signal lantern swinging in its tree-crook. Home, or the closest thing to it. Her chest did something complicated at the sight, and she realized, with distant irritation, that she was already thinking of the stranger as *coming home with her*, which meant she'd lost the argument with herself somewhere back on the mill road.

"One rule," she said, stopping at the ward-line and turning to face him. In the lantern light, his face was pale and smudged and utterly lost, and he looked, she noted with annoyance, exactly like a person. Exactly like someone worth saving. "One rule and we get along fine. Whatever happens next — whatever my family tells you, whatever the anchors show you, however bright your blood burns — you don't promise me anything. No oaths. No vows. People who swear things to me end up dead, and I've buried my quota."

"That's," he began, and swallowed. "That's a very specific rule."

"I've had a very specific life."

The lantern above the ward-line swung once, hard, though there was no wind. Bramble had seen them coming. Good. Bad. Both.

The stranger looked past her at the lights in the trees, at the crooked little house barely visible among them, and Wren watched him take it in — the wards, the lantern, the evidence that someone, somewhere, had built a small warm thing in the middle of a breaking world — and she watched something shift in his expression that she recognized because she'd worn it herself for years before she let herself feel it again.

Homesickness. For a place he hadn't been yet.

"They're going to want to help you," she said quietly. "Bramble will feed you. Ossian will drill you. Pip will adopt you inside an hour, so don't fight it, nobody wins that fight." She flexed her scarred hand, and the grey light beneath her skin pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat remembering itself. "I'm telling you now so it doesn't ambush you later: I nearly left you in that ditch. I stood there for a full minute doing the arithmetic. One stranger against three hollowed ones and a company of four people who trust me to bring back supplies and not complications."

"And the arithmetic?" His voice was careful. Kind, even. She hated that.

"The arithmetic lost." She pushed through the ward-line, and the amber light washed over them both, and beneath her bandages her dying sigil gave one more flicker — gold-grey-gold — like a coal refusing to admit it was out. "It usually does. That's the whole tragedy of me."

Inside the clearing, doors were opening. A small figure was already running toward them across the frost-bright grass, shrieking *"WREN'S BACK AND SHE FOUND SOMETHING"* with the flawless radar children have for trouble, and Wren closed her eyes for one second and allowed herself the dangerous, expensive, eleven-years-forbidden feeling.

*Please,* she thought, not quite praying, because she'd stopped believing anyone was listening. *Let this one be real. Let the blood be true. Let me not bury anyone else.*

Then Pip hit them both at full speed, and the stranger staggered, and laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, astonished sound — and Wren opened her eyes and decided, provisionally, warily, against all her better judgment and all her history:

Maybe hope was worth one more purchase.

From the treeline behind them, silent as snowfall, the hollowed ones had stopped at the edge of Bramble's wards. All four of them. Standing in a row.

Watching the house.

Waiting.
