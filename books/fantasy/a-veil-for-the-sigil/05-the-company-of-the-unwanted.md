## 5. The Company of the Unwanted

Wren watched the stranger sleep for exactly as long as it took to decide he wasn't going to die, and then she stopped watching, because hope was a currency she'd learned not to spend.

He slept like a man who'd never been hunted. Flat on his back in the hollow beneath the leaning stone, one arm flung out, breathing slow and even, his bloodied palm curled open against the moss. The sigil she'd pressed into that palm had gone quiet again — but quiet wasn't dark. It sat under his skin like an ember banked for winter, patient, waiting for air.

*The blood remembers,* her dead master used to say. *Keepers are made. Blood is chosen.*

She'd thought it a pretty lie when she was young enough to believe pretty things.

Bramble found her at the mouth of the hollow, arms crossed, gray braid swinging. "You didn't leave him."

"No."

"You said you would."

"I know what I said."

Bramble studied her with those milky hedge-witch eyes that saw too much and judged too little. "His blood woke your sigil."

"I'm aware."

"Then he's either the end of everything or the beginning of it." Bramble shrugged. "Either way, we should feed him before Ossian decides which."

Wren almost smiled. It hurt her face. "Where is Ossian?"

"Perimeter. He's been walking the perimeter since dawn like the world might apologize if he catches it sneaking up on us." Bramble turned back toward the camp. "Pip's making him breakfast. God help us all."

* * *

Isandro Voss woke to the smell of something burning and a child standing over him with a stick.

Not just any child — a girl of perhaps eight, freckled, gap-toothed, hair chopped short in the way of someone who'd cut it herself with a knife and no mirror. She poked him once more in the shoulder, experimentally.

"You're real," she announced. "Wren said you were real but Wren says lots of things."

"I'm real," Isandro croaked. His throat felt full of ash and other worlds.

"Pip," she said, tapping her own chest with the stick. Then she pointed the stick at the smoking pan balanced on stones nearby. "That's breakfast. It's mostly edible. Bramble says hunger makes everything taste better, which is a witch way of saying don't complain."

He sat up slowly. Every joint reported in. The sky above the hollow was wrong — cracked along its whole eastern seam, a fault line of pale gold light bleeding through like yolk from a broken shell — and yet the girl stood under it chewing on a strand of grass as though cracked skies were weather, nothing more.

"How long have you been here?" he asked. "In this realm. Veylmoor."

Pip considered the question with the gravity of a scholar. "I stopped counting after the second winter. Counting makes it worse. Bramble taught me that." She crouched, elbows on knees, studying him the way he studied maps. "You came through the veil last night. Through the *tear*. Nobody comes through the tear. People go through and come back hollowed, or they don't come back at all. You're the first one who fell *out* of it."

"Lucky me."

"That's what I said!" She beamed. "Wren didn't say anything. Wren does that thing where she goes quiet and sharpens things. She sharpened three knives while you slept. That means she's scared."

Isandro looked toward the mouth of the hollow, where Wren stood silhouetted against the wounded sky, talking low with the gray-braided woman. The sigil-keeper who had saved him from the hollowed ones without knowing why. Who had looked at his opened palm like it was a door she'd been knocking on for years.

"She's not the only one," he said quietly.

Pip nodded solemnly, then thrust the stick at the pan again. "Eat. Falling between worlds burns a lot of calories. Probably. I made that up but it sounds true."

* * *

Breakfast was a gray porridge that tasted faintly of pine and less faintly of regret, and it was the best meal Isandro could remember eating, because he ate it sitting beside living people under a broken sky and neither fact had killed him yet.

The gray-braided woman was Bramble. She introduced herself by handing him a tin cup of bitter tea and saying, "Drink. It tastes awful on purpose. If it tasted good you'd trust it, and trusting things in Veylmoor is how you end up hollowed." She had a voice like gravel wrapped in wool, and hands that moved with the economy of someone who had healed wounds and set bones for a very long time.

The knight arrived mid-meal.

Isandro heard him before he saw him — boots on scree, deliberate, unhurried — and then a big man ducked into the hollow, mail shirt patched with mismatched rings, a sword worn at his hip with the easy familiarity of thirty years' company. Gray at the temples. A face built for frowning. He stopped when he saw Isandro awake, and his hand did not go to his sword, which somehow felt more significant than if it had.

"So," the knight said. "The prophecy's stray dog finally wakes."

"Ossian," Bramble warned.

"What? I'm welcoming him." Ossian lowered himself onto a rock with a groan that suggested every joint had opinions. He looked Isandro over — the fine stitching of his coat, the ink stains on his fingers, the bandaged palm — and grunted. "A cartographer. They sent us a *cartographer*."

"The prophecy sent him," Wren said, entering the hollow behind him. "Nobody sent anyone. That's rather the point."

"A book sent him. Books are written by people, and people want things." Ossian tore a piece of flatbread and chewed. "No offense, mapmaker."

"Some taken," Isandro said.

Something flickered across the knight's face — surprise, maybe, or approval. "Good. Take offense. It means you've got spine. The last three people Wren dragged home half-dead spent their first morning apologizing for existing."

"The last three people," Wren said evenly, "were dying."

"And now there's a fourth, and he's got a lit sigil in his hand and no idea what it costs." Ossian leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the sarcasm dropped away and something older and wearier looked out of his eyes. "Listen to me, cartographer. Whatever that book told you, whatever she told you—" a jerk of his chin at Wren "—there's no version of this that ends clean. Sigils burn. Anchors break. I've buried better people than either of us for smaller causes. So before you go falling in love with destiny, ask yourself what you're willing to lose."

The hollow went quiet. Even Pip had stopped swinging her legs.

Isandro looked down at his bandaged palm. He thought of the valley humming its safe, silent hum. Of the archive, the fresh ink, the librarian's voice: *burn it.* He thought of the veil tearing open like wet paper and the long fall between worlds.

"I already lost my home the moment that book wrote my name," he said. "Everything after this is just deciding what I do about it."

Ossian held his gaze for a long moment. Then he snorted, tore another piece of bread, and held it out. "Hedge-witch food. Eat it anyway."

It was, Isandro would think later, the closest thing to a blessing the knight ever gave.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, because Wren trusted no place longer than a night, and Isandro got his first real look at Veylmoor as they walked.

It was beautiful the way ruins are beautiful. Rolling country stitched with wildflowers that grew in colors he had no names for — colors that seemed to leak slightly, their edges soft, as though the flowers themselves couldn't quite hold their shape. Rivers ran uphill in slow silver threads. In the distance, mountains floated a hand's width above the horizon, unmoored, drifting with geological patience.

And everywhere, everywhere, the cracks. Pale seams in the sky. Fissures in the earth glowing faint gold. A farmhouse they passed whose walls had split neatly in two, both halves still furnished, still standing, occupied — Bramble said — by a family who refused to choose which half to live in.

"It's coming apart," Isandro said quietly. "The whole realm. Like a map left out in the rain."

"Now you understand why we need you," Wren said.

"Do I? Because so far I understand that I can make a sigil glow. That seems like a thin résumé for saving a world."

"It's more than anyone else has managed in a hundred years." She walked beside him, eyes always moving — ridgelines, treeline, the sky. "The covenant held five anchors. Four have gone dark. When an anchor dies, the land around it starts to... forget itself. Magic leaks instead of flows. People near a dead anchor too long—" she paused "—hollow out. Empty. Still walking, still breathing, but nobody home."

"The ones that attacked me."

"The ones that attacked you." Her jaw tightened. "They weren't always monsters, Isandro. They were farmers. Teachers. A whole village near the third anchor, gone in a single night when it failed early. That's what we're walking toward. Not glory. Arithmetic."

Pip appeared at Isandro's elbow, materializing with the silent efficiency of children everywhere. "Are you really from the valley?"

"I am."

"Was there a festival?" Her eyes had gone wide and fierce. "With lanterns? On the water?"

He blinked. "There was. Last night, actually. The centenary. Lanterns on the millpond, and—"

"I remember the lanterns." Her voice had gone small. "I remember the water going strange, like glass, and then it wasn't glass anymore, and then I was here. I was six." She kicked a stone hard. "Nobody believed me when I said where I was from. There's no such place, they said. Valleys don't hide from magic."

Isandro's chest did something complicated and painful. "There is such a place," he said. "I've lived there my whole life."

Pip looked up at him with an expression he'd spend a long time trying to describe afterward — hope and grief sharing one small face. "Then maybe," she said, "you can draw it for me sometime. So I don't forget it right."

Before he could answer, she ran ahead to walk with Bramble, chattering about something else entirely, the wound closed over as quickly as it had opened. Children, Isandro thought, were the bravest people alive.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ribs of some enormous fallen structure — pillars thick as houses, toppled in patterns too regular for accident. Wren called it a waystation of the old covenant roads; Ossian called it a graveyard with good acoustics; Bramble called it shelter and started a fire.

And Isandro, who had spent his life drawing homes he'd never seen, sat among strangers in a broken world and felt, for the first time in longer than he could measure, warm.

It wasn't comfort, exactly. The tea was terrible. Ossian was teaching Pip to whittle and losing badly. Bramble was grinding herbs and singing under her breath in a language that made the fire lean toward her. Wren sat apart, as always, sharpening a knife that was already sharp.

But there was a rhythm to them. An ease bought with years of shared danger and shared meals. When Ossian cursed his whittling knife, Bramble handed him a bandage before he asked. When Pip laughed, all four of them glanced over — all four, even Wren — and something in each face softened before discipline took it back.

They were exiles, all of them. A witch cast out of her hedge-coven. A knight who'd broken his oaths. A keeper with nothing left to keep. A child the veil stole. And now a cartographer who'd never walked a single mile of any map he'd drawn.

*The unwanted,* Isandro thought. *Collected like strays by a woman who claims she doesn't collect anything.*

"You're staring," Wren said, appearing beside him without sound.

"I'm mapping," he said. "It's different."

"Map away, then." She followed his gaze around the fire. "Regrets?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

"Hm." She was quiet a moment. "For what it's worth — none of them should be here either. Ossian's order branded him a traitor for refusing an order that would have killed children. Bramble's coven exiled her for healing the wrong person. Pip—" Wren's voice caught, barely, a hitch so small he'd have missed it if he hadn't been listening for it. "Pip was taken. Like others were taken, over the years. Children the covenant pulled through as it decayed. Looking for vessels. Looking for bearers."

"Bearers," Isandro repeated. "Like me."

"Like you." She met his eyes, and hers were steady and terribly sad. "None of them worked. Their blood wasn't right. But the covenant kept trying, and the children kept coming through, and I kept finding them, and I kept failing to send them home." She looked at the fire. "So now you know. You're not the first the prophecy promised. You're just the first one whose blood answered."

Across the flames, Pip shrieked with laughter as Ossian's attempt at a wooden bird lost a wing. Bramble scolded them both on principle. The fire leaned and swayed.

Isandro looked at these people — this ragged, stubborn, impossible family assembled from the wreckage of other lives — and understood suddenly why Wren kept them. Not because they were useful. Because they were hers.

"Well," he said. "Then I suppose I'd better not waste it."

"Waste what?"

"The answer." He flexed his bandaged hand, feeling the ember under the skin pulse once, faintly, like a second heartbeat. "Whatever the blood wants. Whatever the covenant needs. I'd better be worth it."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then she did something Isandro suspected she did rarely: she let a smile actually arrive.

"Don't make promises to the dark, cartographer," she said. "It takes them literally."

She rose and moved to check the perimeter, and Isandro stayed by the fire, turning her words over — *children the covenant pulled through, looking for bearers* — when Pip plopped down beside him, wooden bird in hand, missing one wing.

"Can I see your map?" she asked.

"My what?"

"The one in your bag. Wren said you had maps. Real ones. From the other side." Her eyes were enormous in the firelight. "I've never seen one. From the other side."

He hesitated only a moment before reaching for his satchel. The leather was scuffed, the buckle cold. Inside, rolled tight, were the charts of a lifetime — realms he'd drawn from dreams alone, coastlines he'd never touched.

And lying on top of them, where no map had been when he packed it, was a sheet of paper he didn't recognize.

Drawn in crayon. In a child's careful, wobbly hand: a valley, a millpond, tiny lanterns on the water. And at the bottom, in letters pressed so hard the wax had crumbled:

*I KNOW WHERE HOME IS.*

Pip had gone very still beside him.

"That's mine," she whispered. "I drew that. Before. I thought I lost it when I fell through."

Isandro stared at the crayon map, at the lanterns, at the words, and felt the ember in his palm wake and begin, very softly, to sing.
