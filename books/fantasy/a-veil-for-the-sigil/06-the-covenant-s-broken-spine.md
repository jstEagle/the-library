## 6. The Covenant's Broken Spine

The fire was small and mean, the kind of fire you build when smoke means death. It threw more shadow than light across the hollow where the company had made camp, and Isandro sat with his back against a slab of fallen stone that might once have been a wall, or a temple, or nothing at all. Above him, through a rent in the world's ceiling, the cracked sky bled its slow auroral light — green and violet threads spilling from a wound in the dark like ink from a punctured page.

He had been in Veylmoor for two days. His hands still shook when he wasn't watching them.

Bramble had given him something bitter to drink — "for the crossing," she'd said, as if everyone crossed between worlds often enough to need fortifying — and Pip had fallen asleep against his side within an hour of meeting him, the way children do with strangers who don't frighten them. Ossian stood watch at the mouth of the hollow, a broad silhouette with a sword he held like a man holding a grudge.

And Wren sat across the fire from him, turning her left hand over and over in the firelight, studying it.

That was when he noticed what he should have noticed sooner. The sigil on her palm — he'd seen it flash when she'd caught his arm on that first terrible night, seen it flare against the hollowed ones — was wrong. Not faded exactly. *Scarred.* The lines of it ran crooked, like a river map drawn by someone who had never seen the river, and at its center was a knot of pale tissue, smooth and dead, where ink should have lived.

"You keep looking at your hand," Isandro said.

Wren closed her fist. "You keep looking at everything," she said. "Like you're memorizing it."

"I'm a cartographer."

"Of places you've never been." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Pip told me. You draw worlds from dreams. That's how the prophecy found you, I expect. The veil needed someone whose mind already leaked through it."

Isandro looked down at his own hands — ink-stained, steady now only because he willed them so. "Then explain it to me," he said. "All of it. Whatever it is you're deciding whether to tell me."

Wren was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When she spoke, her voice had changed — flattened into something recited, worn smooth by repetition, the voice of a woman who had given this lecture before and hated every word of it.

"Before there were borders, there was one world," she said. "Or one *skin*, anyway. The realm you came from and this one were pressed together like wet pages. Everything bled through. Magic moved like weather. Things wandered across without ever noticing they'd traveled." She turned her scarred palm up again, reluctantly, letting the firelight find the dead center of the sigil. "The keepers called it the Undivided. It didn't last. It never does."

"What happened?"

"People happened. Ambition happened. A war on one side bleeds through to the other; a plague crosses like a rumor. So the founders of both realms did what founders do." Her mouth twisted. "They built a lock. Five anchor-sigils, set at the five thinnest points between the worlds, each one bound to a bloodline sworn to hold it. Together they made the Veil — not a wall, exactly. A seam. Strong enough to keep the realms apart, gentle enough to let small things through. Dreams. Songs. The occasional stray child." Her eyes flicked toward Pip, asleep in the crook of Isandro's arm, and something in her face went briefly, unbearably soft before it hardened again. "The covenant was supposed to be maintained. Renewed every generation by the bloodlines. Each anchor held willingly, or it doesn't hold at all."

"And yours—"

"Four of the five anchors are dark." Wren said it the way you'd report casualties. "Dead for decades, some of them. Centuries, maybe. No one renewed them because no one remembered they existed, or no one wanted the burden, or the bloodlines just... ended. And a covenant is a chain, cartographer. Break one link and the whole thing bears the strain. Four links gone, and the fifth—" she gestured upward, at the cracked sky, at the leaking light, at the hollowed ones that walked the dark country beyond the firelight, "—the fifth has been holding the entire weight alone. And it's failing."

Isandro felt cold settle into his chest, slow as floodwater. "The hollowed ones. The people the decay—"

"Are what's left when the Veil stops being a seam and starts being a tear." Wren's voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. She said horrors in the tone of a woman reading a supply list. "When magic leaks through unfiltered, it doesn't enliven people. It empties them. Takes the self out and leaves the shape standing. We call them hollowed. They were farmers. Soldiers. Mothers. Now they walk toward the nearest source of leak-light and stand there, drinking, until they fall apart."

* * *

The fire burned lower. Somewhere out in the dark, something moaned — long, low, almost human, and Ossian shifted his grip on his sword without turning around.

"Tell me about the fifth anchor," Isandro said.

Wren looked at him for a long moment. He watched her decide — watched the war behind her eyes between mercy and necessity, and watched necessity win, the way it always seemed to in this place.

"The fifth anchor isn't in Veylmoor," she said. "It's in your valley."

The words landed and kept landing, echo after echo. Isandro heard himself laugh — one short, disbelieving note. "That's not possible. Nothing magical has ever happened in my valley. Not once. Not in living memory. That's the whole *point* of the place. We joke about it. The Hollow Vale, we call it, because prayers don't stick and charms don't work and the fortune-tellers who visit always leave early with headaches."

"Yes," Wren said. "Exactly that."

"No." He was shaking his head. "You're saying my home is an anchor? An anchor is a point where the worlds press thin. Mine is the opposite. It's the one place the Veil can't reach. Magic can't follow anyone in. It's—"

"A hole," Wren said. "In the Veil. In the covenant. In the world." She leaned forward, and the firelight carved her face into planes and shadows. "Think about what you're describing, Isandro. A place where the covenant cannot follow. Where the binding goes silent. Do you know what that means to the four other anchors, straining under all that weight? Every year, the fifth link doesn't just fail to help. It *pulls*. Like a man chained to four others who simply walks away and lets the rope go slack. Your valley isn't sheltered from the covenant. It's a wound in it. The reason the sky above us is cracked. The reason the hollowed ones exist. The reason the whole seam is unraveling faster every year." She paused. "Your valley is the last anchor, and it has been dark for a hundred years."

"A hundred years." The number arrived before the understanding did, and then the understanding arrived all at once, like ice water. "The festival. Tonight was the centenary. A hundred years since the valley was founded, they say. Since Aldous Voss led the first families in and sealed the passes behind them."

"Aldous Voss," Wren repeated quietly.

"My ancestor." His own voice sounded far away to him. "The founder. There are statues. There's a statue in the square with his name on it, and every centenary they lay wreaths and the mayor gives a speech about how he *saved* us. How he found a refuge where the old world's sorrows couldn't follow." Isandro's hands had become fists without his permission. "You're telling me that refuge is—"

"An anchor-sigil, severed from the covenant by the man sworn to hold it." Wren's words fell like stones into still water. "Aldous Voss was the fifth keeper. The last of the five bloodlines. And instead of renewing his oath, he cut his anchor loose, gathered whoever would follow him, and hid inside the silence he made. He built himself a pocket where the covenant's demands could never reach him, and he called it salvation, and he sealed the door behind him, and for a hundred years your people have laid wreaths at the feet of the man whose cowardice is killing two worlds."

The fire cracked. Out in the dark, the moaning came again, closer, and this time it answered itself, a second voice joining the first, and Ossian finally turned from his watch, sword drawn, face grim.

"We should move at dawn," he said. "Maybe earlier. They've caught the scent of him." He nodded at Isandro. "Keeper's blood. To the hollowed ones, it smells like the light they're starving for."

"Wonderful," Isandro managed.

But he wasn't listening to Ossian, not really. He was seeing the valley behind his eyes — the terraced fields, the millrace, the slate roofs going blue at dusk, the statue in the square with its stern bronze face and its wreaths. He had spent thirty years loving that place. He had spent thirty years feeling like a guest in it, a man drawing maps of everywhere else because his own home refused to be drawn — because no matter how carefully he surveyed it, the valley never quite fit on the page, its edges smudged, its distances unreliable, as though it declined to be known.

Because it wasn't a place. It was a *severance*. A wound dressed up as a village. Every road into it a suture. Every generation born inside it another layer of bandage over something his family had broken.

"All those stories," he said hoarsely. "The founder's providence. The blessed silence. My grandmother used to say we were chosen. Kept apart for our own protection." He laughed again, and this time it hurt coming out. "We weren't chosen. We were *hidden*. By a man running from an oath."

"Now you understand why the prophecy named you," Wren said. Not gently. She didn't seem to have gentle in her anymore; she had honesty, and she spent it like a woman spending her last coins. "The covenant doesn't want vengeance, Isandro. It wants renewal. The fifth anchor can't be reforged from Veylmoor's side. It needs keeper's blood on the other side of the Veil — needs someone to walk back into that valley and take up what Aldous Voss put down. The prophecy names you because you're the heir. The debt collector has come for the family estate."

"And if I refuse?"

Wren held up her scarred hand, turned it slowly in the failing light. "Then the Veil finishes tearing, the hollowed ones finish spreading, and eventually the leak reaches your valley too. Magic-dead ground won't stay dead forever, not when there's nothing left holding the worlds apart. Your blessed silence will fill up with everything you were hiding from." She lowered the hand. "There's no refusing. There's only choosing how."

Isandro stared into the fire. He thought about the archive, the book with fresh ink, the librarian's terrified face — *burn it, boy, burn it and never speak of it* — and understood, suddenly, that the old man had known. Some knowledge of the covenant must have survived in the valley, fossilized into superstition. *Burn it* hadn't been fear of prophecy. It had been fear of inheritance.

"Pip," he said quietly. "She came through the Veil. From my side?"

"Years ago. One of the leaks — the covenant frays worst near the fifth anchor, and things slip through. Children especially. Children are thin places themselves, keepers' theory says. Most of them don't survive the crossing." Wren glanced at the sleeping girl, and her voice roughened. "Pip did. Barely. She doesn't remember much. Just a garden. A woman singing."

Isandro's chest tightened. He thought of the missing-children notices that appeared in the valley archives every few years, old ones, yellowed, always quietly unresolved. He had catalogued them. He had filed them under *unexplained*.

He had filed children under *unexplained*.

"We'll get her home," he said. "After. When the covenant's reforged and the Veil is a seam again instead of a wound. We'll get everyone home."

Wren studied him across the embers, and for the first time since he'd fallen into her dying world, something other than wariness moved through her expression — something that might, in better light, in a kinder life, have been hope.

"That's the speech," she said. "Every keeper gives it. *We'll set it right.* My order said it for three hundred years." She rose, brushing ash from her knees, and held up her scarred palm one last time — a map of failure written in flesh. "I gave that speech too, once. It cost me everything. So understand me, Isandro Voss: I'm not following you because I believe in you. I'm following you because you're the last key I haven't tried."

She walked to the edge of the firelight to take her watch, leaving him alone with the crack in the sky and the weight of a name he'd carried all his life without knowing what it owed.

*Aldous Voss.* He said the name silently, testing it, and it tasted different now — not like marble and wreaths but like a locked door, and behind the door, a sound like water rising.

Sleep didn't come. Instead he took out his journal — the one constant from the other side of the Veil, the one thing the crossing hadn't taken — and by the light of the mean little fire he began to draw. Not Veylmoor. Not yet. He drew the valley: the terraces, the millrace, the square, the statue. And for the first time in thirty years of trying, the lines came true. The edges held. The distances obeyed him.

Because now he knew what the valley was, and a mapmaker, at last, was drawing a wound.

He worked until the sky's bleeding light paled toward a dawn that Ossian said would come too late, and when Bramble woke and saw what he'd drawn, she went very still, and then she pointed one gnarled finger at the square of his map, at the statue he'd rendered without thinking, and said:

"That stone. That's where the anchor sleeps. And child — the way your hand shook drawing it?" She met his eyes. "Something over there already knows you're coming."
