## 7. Road Through the Hollow Country

Ossian counted his sins in the rhythm of his boots on the road. It was an old habit, one he'd learned from a man who now walked toward them out of the dark with no eyes worth the name.

Six days out from Wren's camp. Six days of gray country where the grass grew pale as old bone and the trees stood too straight, like soldiers at attention who had forgotten they were ever allowed to rest. The road itself was the worst part — it had been a king's road once, wide and paved and lined with waystones, and now the stones were cracked and the mile-markers read numbers that didn't match the miles anymore, because distance itself had started to fray along with everything else.

"Third marker this morning that's wrong," Bramble said, falling into step beside him. She walked with her staff slung across her back and her hands buried in her sleeves, which meant she was cold, which meant she was worried. Bramble only got cold when she was worried. "The last one said eleven leagues to Corrow Ford. We've walked four. This one says nineteen."

"Maybe the road's grown," Pip offered from Isandro's shoulders, where she rode more often than she walked these days. The child had taken to the cartographer immediately, the way she took to anything new and unbroken. "Wren says space gets stretchy near the anchors."

"Space isn't stretchy," Ossian said.

"It is when the thing holding it together is dying," Wren said from the front, not turning around. She hadn't turned around much in six days. She walked like a woman following a sound only she could hear, her scarred hand bare to the air, the ruined sigil on her palm catching what little light the cracked sky let through.

Ossian adjusted the sword on his hip and said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. He'd been knighted for it once — for standing silent at a border post through a winter siege while better men panicked. Ser Aldric Vane had put a hand on his shoulder afterward and told him, *A wall doesn't need to speak, Ossian. It needs to hold.*

Ser Aldric Vane was walking toward them out of the dark right now.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a coaching inn, its roof fallen inward, its hearth still black with a fire someone had lit and abandoned years ago. Isandro drew maps by candlelight — he always drew maps, even here, especially here, sketching the country they'd crossed as though putting it on paper might make it behave. Pip sat cross-legged beside him, coloring in his coastlines with a stub of red crayon she guarded like a relic.

Wren stood watch on the inn's broken porch. Ossian took the other end of it without being asked. That was their arrangement: she never asked, and he never explained, and between them the watches got covered.

"You felt it too," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The hollowed ones." He nodded. "Three of them, maybe four. North and west. They've been paralleling us since noon."

"They're not hunting."

"No."

"They're *following*." Wren's jaw tightened. "Like pilgrims."

Ossian looked out at the dark. The moon above Veylmoor came up in pieces these days — the crack in the sky ran straight through its face, so it rose looking shattered, light leaking through the fracture in thin silver threads. By that broken light he could see them, far off across the pale fields: figures standing motionless in the grass, spaced apart like fence posts. Not approaching. Not leaving. Just *there*, the way grief is there.

"How many have you seen before?" he asked.

"Dozens. Never a group like this." Wren flexed her scarred hand. "Hollowed ones wander. They drift toward places the covenant used to touch — old shrines, anchor sites. They don't organize. They don't *follow* anyone." She glanced back through the doorway at Isandro, bent over his map, Pip's head nodding against his shoulder. "Until we picked him up."

"He's the heir of Voss. If they remember anything, maybe they remember that."

"Or maybe whatever's left of them can feel the fifth anchor walking around in his blood, and they want it back." Wren said it flatly, but Ossian heard the fear under it. He'd learned to hear fear under things during the war. It was most of what he'd learned during the war.

"I'll take first watch," he said.

"You always take first watch."

"One of us should be consistent."

Almost — almost — she smiled. Then she went inside to sleep, and Ossian sat alone on the broken porch with his sword across his knees, watching the figures stand in the fields, and counted his sins until the counting became remembering, which it always did.

* * *

He had been twenty-two when he swore his oaths to the Order of the Last Light, kneeling on cold stone while Ser Aldric Vane held the blade against his shoulder. Aldric had been commander of the eastern garrison then, a broad, weathered man with a laugh like a rockslide and a habit of naming every horse he rode after his late wife, each one more sentimentally than the last. He'd taught Ossian three things: how to hold a shield line, how to write a letter to a dead soldier's mother without lying, and how to stand silent when silence was the hardest thing to do.

Then the covenant had begun to fail — slowly at first, then all at once, the way everything in Veylmoor failed — and the Order had been given orders Ossian still could not say aloud without tasting iron.

*Hold the villages,* the writs had said. *Prevent panic. Prevent exodus.*

What the writs had meant, everyone understood: keep the people where they were, because the regency needed the tax rolls, and because a moving population near the failing anchors made the decay worse, and because the truth — that the realm was coming apart and no one knew how to stop it — was a weapon too dangerous to hand to common folk.

So they had held the villages. They had held them while the hollowing spread through them like frost through a windowpane. Ossian had watched a miller's daughter stop mid-sentence one morning and simply not finish it, standing with flour on her hands, looking at nothing, forever. He had watched whole families go quiet in the same week, houses full of people breathing and blinking and gone. And he had stood at his post, silent, holding, because a wall doesn't need to speak.

The night he broke his oaths was the night Aldric ordered the garrison to seal the gates of Corrow Ford against refugees fleeing the second anchor's collapse. Two hundred people outside the walls. Winter coming. Aldric had given the order in a voice like gravel and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, and Ossian had understood, even then, that the commander hated the order more than any man alive — and gave it anyway, because that was what oaths were for. That was what they were *for*. They were machines for making you do the unforgivable thing neatly.

Ossian had opened the side gate himself. He had walked two hundred people out into the dark and turned in his sword and his cloak and his name, and he had never learned what happened to them, because he had not stayed to find out, because staying was the one thing he could no longer do.

He'd told himself for nine years that Aldric had deserved his contempt. That the commander was the oath and Ossian was the man, and the two had finally come apart, cleanly, the way a blade comes apart from a rotten hilt.

He had not let himself wonder, in nine years, whether Aldric had ever opened a gate of his own.

* * *

They came in the hour before dawn, when the sky's crack bled its faintest light and the world was all gray edges.

Ossian saw them move for the first time. All six days they had stood or drifted, and now, as the company slept in the ruined inn, the figures in the fields began to walk. Not fast. Not slow. With the terrible patience of water finding a level, they converged on the inn from three directions at once.

He woke Wren with a hand on her shoulder and a finger to his lips, and she came awake the way she did everything — completely, instantly, without noise. Her eyes went to the window, to the fields, and he watched her understand in the space of a breath.

"Not running," she whispered.

"No."

"Get the others up. Slowly."

Bramble woke like a cat, already reaching for her staff. Isandro woke confused, then terrified, then — Ossian noted it with something like respect — quiet, gathering Pip against his chest and covering the child's mouth gently before she could cry out, murmuring something into her hair that made her go still. The cartographer had spine. Wren had seen it first; Ossian was starting to believe her.

They gathered in the inn's common room, by the dead hearth, and waited.

The footsteps came up the porch. Six sets of them, uneven, unhurried. The door — hanging by one hinge — swung inward with a groan.

They filed in one by one. Ossian had seen hollowed ones before, from a distance, from behind walls, always from somewhere safer than this. Up close they were worse than the stories. They looked like people who had been *edited* — all the parts that made a self cut away, leaving something that moved like a person and wore a face like a person and had nothing at all behind the eyes but a faint gray shimmer, like light through dirty ice. Their clothes were the clothes they'd worn when it happened, years or decades ago, worn to rags, patched by hands that must have still remembered how to sew if not why.

The first one stopped an arm's length away and looked at Isandro. Its head tilted. The gray shimmer in its eyes flickered — actually flickered, like a lamp in wind — and its mouth worked, shaping sounds that had no breath behind them.

Isandro went white. "It's trying to—"

"Don't answer it," Wren breathed. "Don't give it your name. Names are hooks."

But the second figure had already pushed past the first, and Ossian's hand was on his sword hilt and then off it again, because he knew the walk. He would have known it blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. A limp on the left side, from a wound taken at the Battle of the Salt Marshes, thirty years ago, that the man had joked about for the rest of his life because the alternative was complaining and Aldric Vane did not complain.

The hollowed one stopped in front of him.

Up close, Ossian could see what six days of distance had spared him. The broad frame gone gaunt. The gray stubble on a jaw he'd seen shaved by campfire light a thousand mornings. And the eyes — the eyes were the worst, because they were empty, and emptiness is a shape, and Ossian knew this shape. He had spent nine years imagining Aldric Vane's face whenever he imagined the man he'd failed to save or the man he'd failed to forgive, and now here it was, wearing neither expression, wearing none at all.

"Commander," Ossian said. His voice came out low and wrecked.

The hollowed one's head tilted, the same small tilt as the first one's. The gray light in its eyes stirred. And its mouth began to move — working, working, shaping syllables with no air, no voice, just the ghost of speech, over and over —

*Os-si-an. Os-si-an.*

"It knows you," Isandro whispered. "How does it know you?"

"They keep something," Bramble said softly. She had come up beside him, and her old eyes were wet. "I've seen it. Whatever the hollowing takes, it takes the middle of a person first — the wanting, the choosing. The edges go last. Faces. Names." She touched Ossian's arm. "It kept yours."

*Os-si-an.* The mouth kept moving. One gaunt hand rose, trembling, and reached toward him — not grasping, not threatening. Reaching the way you reach in a dream when you're trying to hold something you already know is gone.

Every trained instinct Ossian owned told him to draw steel. These things were dangerous; he knew it; Wren had buried friends because of it. But his body would not move. His body was twenty-two years old again, kneeling on cold stone, feeling a blade touch his shoulder, hearing a rockslide laugh call him *the quiet one, my best wall.*

"Aldric." He said it aloud, and the word cost him something he'd been carrying since Corrow Ford. "I opened the gate. I never told you I—" His throat closed. Nine years of silence, and the one time he needed to speak, it betrayed him. "I'm sorry. For the gate. And for thinking you were the wall and I was the man. You were both. You were both, and I made you carry it alone."

The hollowed one's hand hovered an inch from his cheek. The gray light in its eyes flickered, flickered — and for one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat, Ossian saw something look *out* of that emptiness. Not recognition exactly. Something older and simpler. The look of a man at a winter siege seeing the relief fires on the horizon: too late to matter, impossible not to see.

Then it was gone, and the emptiness closed over, and the hand fell.

And all six of them — Aldric and the five others — turned as one, smooth and terrible, and walked back out into the pre-dawn gray, resuming their stations in the fields, patient as fence posts, waiting for whatever they waited for.

No one spoke for a long time.

Pip broke the silence, small-voiced, from behind Isandro's leg. "Was that your friend?"

"Yes," Ossian said. "That was my friend."

"He's sick," Pip said, with the absolute authority of a child stating a fact. "Somebody has to fix him."

"Somebody does," Wren said quietly. She was looking at Isandro, and Isandro was looking at his own hands, and Ossian understood suddenly that the child was right in a way none of them wanted to say aloud. The sickness had a source. The source had a name, and the name was carved into a prophecy book a century old, and the cure was walking toward the drowned cathedral with a cartographer's ink still on his fingers.

"We move at first light," Wren said. "Faster now. No more easy stages."

Ossian cleaned his sword by the dead hearth, though it did not need cleaning. It was what his hands knew how to do while the rest of him reorganized itself around a new fact: that the breaking of the covenant was not an abstraction, not a chapter in Wren's grim lectures, not a problem of sigils and anchors. It was Aldric Vane standing in a field saying his name with no voice.

He had run from one broken oath. He would not run from this.

When the gray light strengthened, they broke camp and took the road east, toward Corrow Ford and the drowned cathedral beyond it. And Ossian noticed — walking at the rear, as always — that the hollowed ones followed. All six of them. Patient, silent, keeping their distance.

Keeping, he thought, the way a garrison keeps a wall.

Waiting to see if the quiet one, the best wall, would hold.
