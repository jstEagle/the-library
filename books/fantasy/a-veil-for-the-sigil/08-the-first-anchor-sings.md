## 8. The First Anchor Sings

The drowned cathedral announced itself three days before we saw it, by sound.

At first I thought it was the wind. Veylmoor's wind had a vocabulary I was still learning — the dry hiss of it through the hollow grass, the low moan it made in the cracked sky's throat at dusk. But this was different. This was *toned*. A single sustained note, so far beneath hearing that I felt it more than heard it, a pressure behind my sternum like a thumb pressed gently against a struck bell.

"It knows you're coming," Wren said, when I finally asked.

We were walking the spine of a ridge that overlooked what had once been a river valley. The river was still there, mostly — a wide silver sheet of water lying flat and patient across the drowned land, broken here and there by the tips of stone spires rising like drowned men's fingers. The largest of them sat perhaps two miles out, a great dark shape just breaking the surface, its bell tower leaning at an angle that no honest architecture would permit.

"The anchor," Ossian said from behind me. He'd said little all morning. Since the hollow country, since he'd seen what he'd seen in that emptied face of his old commander, he spoke only when words were necessary, as if he were rationing them against some future famine. "The first of five."

"And it's singing because of me."

"It sings for anyone with the blood," Bramble said. She was walking beside me, her satchel of jars and dried things clinking softly with every step, her eyes on the water below. "But most folk with the blood are generations thin. It hums for them. Like recognizing a face across a market square." She glanced up at me, and there was something in her expression I hadn't seen before — not pity, exactly, but its quieter cousin. "It'll do more than hum for you, I'd wager."

Pip skipped ahead along the ridge, arms out for balance, singing her own song back at the cathedral — something tuneless and defiant, a child's counter-melody. She had appointed herself our herald three days ago and no one had had the heart to relieve her of the post.

"How deep is it?" I asked.

"Deep enough that the founders put it there on purpose," Wren said. "Water muffles. Water hides. The fifth anchor got your valley. The first got a flood. They were hiding their treasures, even then." She paused at the crest of the ridge and looked out over the silver sheet and the leaning tower, and I watched her scarred hand flex once at her side — the sigil burned into her palm catching the grey light. "Or maybe they just knew that anything worth binding should be hard to reach."

* * *

We reached the shore at midafternoon, and the note in my chest deepened until my teeth ached with it.

Up close, the cathedral was worse and better than it had looked from the ridge. Worse, because the scale of it became real — a whole city of faith, it seemed, sunk to its eaves in still water, its buttresses fanning out beneath the surface like the ribs of some vast patient whale. Better, because the stone was *alive*. Every inch of it above the waterline was carved with sigils, thousands of them, tens of thousands, flowing one into the next in lines of script that predated any alphabet I had ever mapped. And they were faintly warm-looking somehow, a shimmer just beneath perception, like heat off summer stone.

I stood on the shore with my sketchbook already in my hand before I remembered I wasn't supposed to be working.

"You're doing the thing," Pip said.

"What thing?"

"The map thing. Your eyes go all—" she squinted, mimicking me with brutal accuracy, "—faraway and hungry."

"I'm sorry. It's a sickness."

"Don't apologize. Draw me the fishies when we're on the boat."

"There are no fishies. There's no life in that water at all."

"That's why you have to *draw* them," she said, with the immense patience of the very young explaining the world to the very tired, "so they can exist."

Ossian had found the boat. Of course he had; Ossian found everything practical within a mile radius without appearing to look for it. It was a flat-bottomed ferry, half-sunk in the reeds, and he was bailing it with a dented helm that I suspected had once belonged to someone who had used it for more dignified purposes.

"It'll hold," he said. "Probably."

"Wonderful. I'll write that on my tombstone."

Bramble laughed. Wren didn't. She was staring at the cathedral with an expression I had learned to read over the past week: the look of a woman returning to the scene of something she had failed to prevent.

"You've been here before," I said quietly, while Ossian pushed us off the shore.

"Once. Eleven years ago." She took the tiller without being asked, and the boat swung toward the leaning tower with uncanny obedience, as if the water itself were cooperating. "It was brighter then. Not bright. But the sigils had a pulse to them, like embers under ash. Now—" she gestured at the pale carvings, and I saw what I hadn't let myself see from shore: they weren't shimmering with warmth. They were flickering. Unevenly. Like a candle in a draft. "Now it's a candle down to its last inch. When it goes out, the first anchor goes with it, and then the realm starts coming apart at the seams in earnest. Not decades. Months."

"So we light the candle."

"We don't know how to light the candle," Wren said. "That's the point of today. We know your blood does *something*. We don't know if it's enough, or if it lasts, or what it costs." Her eyes came to mine, grey and steady and terribly tired. "I need you to understand that before we get out on that stone. If anything happens—"

"Nothing will happen."

"If anything happens," she repeated, with the flat patience of someone who had buried enough people to earn the right, "you tell me immediately. No heroics. Mapmaker, I've watched a proud man try to hold a sigil alone and I'm not watching it again. Promise me."

The boat rocked. The note in my chest deepened.

"I promise," I said.

* * *

Climbing the drowned cathedral was like climbing a mountain that had been dreaming.

The stone was slick but not treacherous — another small mercy that I was beginning to suspect wasn't mercy at all but design. The founders had wanted pilgrims to come. There were stairs carved into the western face, ascending through the flooded levels in graceful switchbacks, each landing marked with a sigil that glowed faintly as we passed, like lamps being lit ahead of us and going out behind.

At the third landing, the water inside the walls became visible through great shattered windows, and I made the mistake of looking down.

Below me, impossibly clear, the flooded nave stretched away in blue-green silence. And standing in it, upright and orderly in rows upon rows, were figures.

"Pip," I said carefully. "About those fishies."

She looked. So did the others.

They weren't fish. They weren't quite people either, anymore. Hundreds of them, standing in the drowned congregation space, pale shapes in the green murk, arranged as if they had simply knelt down one day during a service and never gotten up. Their faces were tilted toward the high altar where the anchor must have been, and though they did not move, I had the absolute unshakeable conviction that they knew we were there.

"Hollowed ones?" My voice sounded very far away.

"No." Wren's jaw was tight. "Worse. Congregants. They came here to pray, centuries ago, and the anchor drank them slow instead of fast. They're not empty. They're..." She trailed off, searching for words that wouldn't come easily.

"Asleep," Pip said suddenly. Everyone looked at her. She was gripping the windowsill, her face pressed close to the glass, and her voice had gone very quiet and very certain. "They're not dead. They're waiting."

Nobody argued with Pip about the veil. It was one of her rules.

We climbed.

The higher we went, the louder the note became, until it wasn't behind my sternum anymore but *in* it, humming in my blood like a tuning fork held against bone. The sigils on the walls around us grew denser, brighter, more frantic — spiraling inward toward the bell tower's peak, converging on the chamber at its heart.

And in the middle of that convergence, when we finally hauled ourselves through a collapsed window into the tower's crown chamber, I stopped being able to think of myself as a cartographer at all.

The chamber was round, open to the cracked sky above through a roof long since fallen. Rain had worn the floor smooth. In its center, set flush into the stone like a coin pressed into bread, lay the sigil.

It was enormous — fifteen feet across, a wheel of interlocking script whose outer ring alone contained more distinct marks than I could count. And it was dying. The light in its channels guttered as we watched, flaring weakly, dimming, flaring again, each flare thinner than the last. Around its rim, whole sections had gone dark entirely, dead letters in a living sentence.

The four of us stood at the rim of it, and the note in my chest rose until I could hardly breathe, and I understood — with the simple clarity of a man reading a map of his own hometown — that the sound was a call. That it had been calling for a hundred years. That it was calling *me*.

"Well," Bramble said into the silence, "go on then. Before it loses its nerve."

I stepped onto the sigil.

Nothing happened.

Then everything happened.

The moment my boot crossed the outer ring, the light beneath me surged — not the guttering candle-flare of before but a true blaze, racing outward from my footfall through every channel of the great wheel, chasing the dark sections back letter by letter, and the note that had lived in my chest for three days suddenly *left*, pouring out of me and into the stone, and the sound it made when it arrived was—

I have no adequate word. I am a professional with words and I have none.

It was a chord. It was a chord struck on an instrument the size of a world, and the drowned cathedral rang with it, and below us through the flooded nave I saw the sleeping congregants *turn their heads*, hundreds of pale faces lifting in unison toward the sound like flowers finding sun, and the water itself shivered into rings, and above us the cracked sky—

The cracked sky *knit*.

Just for a heartbeat. Just above the tower. The jagged seam that has scarred Veylmoor's heavens for a century pulled closed, smooth and whole and blue, and light poured through it that wasn't leaking magic but simply *daylight*, honest daylight, and for one single breath the whole ruined realm remembered what it was supposed to look like.

Then it tore back open, and the chord faded, and the sigil settled into a glow that was stronger than before but still uneven, still starving, and I was on my knees on the wet stone with blood running freely from both nostrils and no memory of falling.

"Isandro!" Wren was beside me, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Talk to me. What happened, what did you feel—"

"Did you see it?" My voice came out in pieces. "The sky. Tell me you saw the sky."

"We saw it." Ossian's voice, from the rim. Careful. Controlled. I looked up; he was staring upward at the torn seam with an expression I couldn't parse, and his hand was white-knuckled on his sword hilt — not pointed at anything, just holding on. "It healed. For a second, it healed."

"One second." Bramble crouched next to me, pressing a cloth to my nose with brisk unsentimental hands. "One second of repair for a nosebleed and whatever that color is that your face has gone. That's not reforging, love. That's proof of concept. You're the right key for the lock, but one key doesn't make a door."

"I know." I wiped my face. My hand was shaking. I watched it shake with a strange detachment, because underneath the shaking, underneath the exhaustion, something else was blooming in my chest, warm and terrible, and it took me a moment to name it.

Hope. Real hope. The kind that makes everything worse.

"It answered me," I said. "Not my blood in the abstract. Me. It's been calling for a hundred years and it called *me*, Wren. Which means—"

"Which means anyone else listening knows it too." She finished the thought for me, and her face had gone grim as winter. She rose and turned a slow circle, scanning the horizon in every direction, and I followed her gaze and understood.

The chord had rung across a world. A world with a regent in a spire, and armies, and hollowed ones scattered through the countryside like ash on the wind. We had just struck a bell that could only be heard by everyone hunting the same thing we were.

And unlike us, they knew exactly where the sound had come from.

"We need to move," Ossian said quietly. "Now."

He was pointing north.

At first I saw nothing. Then the light shifted, and I saw them — along the distant shoreline, spilling out of the tree line like ink poured into water, a column of soldiers. Miles off, moving fast, banners snapping in the grey wind. And ahead of them, ranging far in front of the column like hounds thrown ahead of the hunt, came shapes that moved wrong. Too smooth. Too steady. Dozens of them, crossing the flooded fields without slowing, walking straight out onto the surface of the water as if it were road.

"Hollowed ones," Wren breathed. "Sereth's hounds and her leashes too. She heard it. Everyone heard it." She spun to face us, and all the weariness in her had burned away into something harder and faster. "Everyone heard it, and now they all know exactly what you are."

I looked down at the sigil beneath my feet, glowing its stubborn half-light, and I felt the truth of it settle over me like cold water.

For one second, I had made the sky whole. I had woken the sleeping and rung a chord that a world had been waiting a century to hear. And in exchange, I had traded away the last of my anonymity forever.

I was useful now. Useful things get hunted.

"Off the sigil," Wren snapped. "Down the west stairs, into the boat, and if any of you waits for any of the rest of you, so help me—"

The chord, faint as an echo, shivered through the stone one last time.

And from far below, up through the flooded nave, through the water and the ranks of sleeping congregants, hundreds of pale faces turned toward the sound —

and began, slowly, to rise.
