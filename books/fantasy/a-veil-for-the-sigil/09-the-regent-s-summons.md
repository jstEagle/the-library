## 9. The Regent's Summons

The summons came at dusk, riding on wings that should not have existed.

Wren heard them first — a wrongness in the air above the drowned cathedral's broken tower, where the company had made camp in the shadow of the first anchor. The sigil beneath the floodwater still glowed faintly, a bruise-colored pulse under black water, and every bird within a mile had gone silent since Isandro's blood had touched it three days past. Silence, then wings. Six of them. Pale as bone, jointed like fingers, beating against a sky that was already cracked.

"Riders," she said, before she could think better of it. Then, correcting: "Not riders."

Ossian was up with his sword half-drawn before she finished speaking. Bramble stirred the fire down to embers with one bare foot, casual as a woman banking a hearth, though Wren saw her other hand close around the iron nail strung at her throat — hedge-witch habit, old protections for old fears. Pip didn't wake. The child slept through storms, through hollowed ones shuffling past in the dark, through everything. It was either a blessing or the saddest thing Wren had ever seen.

Only Isandro kept sketching. He sat with his back against a fallen pillar, the cathedral's stone worn smooth by a century of water that no longer fell, drawing by the light of the anchor itself. He drew the way he breathed. Wren had watched him map the road behind them, the country ahead, the shape of the crack in the sky overhead — all of it rendered in careful lines while the rest of them watched the dark.

"They're coming here," he said, not looking up. "I can feel it. Like the anchor feels me." He tapped his own chest. "Since the cathedral, I feel... pulled. Toward things. Stones, roads, people. Is that what magic does? It pulls?"

"It takes," Wren said flatly. "Whatever it gives, it takes more. That's the covenant's whole history in one sentence."

The winged things descended into the torchlight without landing properly — they *settled*, the way a spider settles, folding their finger-jointed wings and standing upright on two legs that bent backward. They had faces like smoothed clay, featureless except where a mouth should be, and where a mouth should be each carried a scroll sealed with white wax.

Wren knew the seal. Every exile in Veylmoor knew the seal. The white stag of House Sereth, antlers spread like a crown of thorns.

"The regent knows we're here," Ossian said quietly. "Of course she does. The anchor lit up like a second dawn. Half the realm felt it."

"Then half the realm will come for him." Wren nodded at Isandro. "Sereth is simply the first, because Sereth is always first. She's been waiting a hundred years for an heir of Voss to crawl out from under his rock."

"You know her?" Isandro asked. He'd finally looked up. There was ink on his fingers and fear underneath the curiosity, and Wren hated how much she was beginning to care which one won.

"I know *of* her. Everyone does. Lady Sereth Vale-Regent, Protector of the Sundered Realm, First Servant of the Covenant." She said the titles the way you'd spit out gristle. "She's held Veylmoor together with silk thread and terror since before I was born. Kept the cities fed. Kept the hollowed ones out of the walls. Kept order." A pause. "And kept every sigil-keeper who might have fixed any of it either dead, disgraced, or running. Myself included."

"You don't know that's true—"

"I know that when my order tried to reach the anchors twenty years ago, we were denied passage, permits, escort. Denied everything but advice. And when we went anyway, we were branded traitors before our bodies cooled." Wren stood, brushing ash from her knees. "Lady Sereth has spent two decades ensuring no one reforges anything. Now she sends invitations. Ask yourself why."

One of the clay-faced creatures extended its scroll toward Isandro. It did so with a strange delicacy, like a servant presenting wine.

He looked at Wren.

"Don't," she said.

He took it anyway. Of course he took it. He was a man who'd found his name in a century-old book and hadn't burned it; taking dangerous things was apparently the only thing he knew how to do.

He broke the seal and read aloud:

*To Isandro Voss, son of no house and heir to all houses: The Regent of Veylmoor extends her protection and her purpose. You stand at a dying anchor in a dying country, hunted by things that were once your neighbors. Come to Caer Serevan. Bring your companions. An army waits to serve the covenant's restoration, and a table waits set for six.*

*You have nothing, heir of Voss, except enemies and exhaustion. We offer rest, road, and the means of your great work. Refuse, and refuse alone — the road to the mountain anchor is long, and the dark between here and there does not negotiate.*

*Come and be guarded. Stay and be taken. The choice, as ever with the blood of Voss, is yours alone.*

He lowered the scroll. "That's..." He exhaled. "That's the most reasonable thing anyone's said to me since I got here."

"That's what makes it a trap," Wren snapped.

*

They argued until the moons crossed.

Bramble wanted to go — "an army's an army, love, and I'm tired of sleeping on graves" — but wanted to go armed and suspicious, which Wren privately admitted was the correct way to do everything. Ossian said nothing for a long time, which meant he was thinking about oaths, which meant he was thinking about betrayal, which meant he would eventually land wherever his gut landed and defend it like a wall. Pip woke near midnight, listened to ten minutes of argument, ate half a loaf, and went back to sleep, which Wren thought was the sanest response anyone offered.

It was Isandro who broke the deadlock, in the end, by being exactly what he was.

"Look at it geographically," he said, unrolling one of his maps across the stones, weighting the corners with pebbles. "Caer Serevan sits here, on the river road. The second anchor is here, in the mountains east of it. Any route to the mountain anchor passes within a day's march of the regent's seat. We cannot avoid her territory. So the question isn't whether we deal with Sereth. It's whether we deal with her on her terms or ours."

"And if her terms are a gilded cage?" Wren asked.

"Then we'll be caged *inside* the answer." He tapped the map, over the mountain anchor. "Four anchors are dark. One just barely lit because of me. If there's an army willing to carry us to the next one, refusing it out of pride gets us killed slower, not safer."

"He's right," Ossian said heavily, and Wren could have screamed. "It smells wrong. But wrong and fatal aren't the same thing. And I've marched to worse places for worse reasons."

"Of course you have," Wren muttered. "That's why you're an oathbreaker."

But she was outvoted four to one, counting the sleeping child, and she knew the truth underneath the vote: they were tired, and hunted, and the offer was *kind*. Kindness was the most dangerous weapon in Veylmoor. Nobody sharpened a blade that carefully unless they intended to use it.

"Fine," she said. "We go. But we go awake. Nobody eats anything she serves us. Nobody drinks anything she pours. Nobody signs anything, swears anything, or stands anywhere I can't see them. And if I say run, you run."

Isandro smiled — that tired, crooked smile that was starting to feel like something Wren might build a life around, which terrified her more than the hollowed ones ever had. "You know, for someone with nothing left to keep, you guard an awful lot."

"Someone has to," she said. "Everyone else keeps dying."

*

Caer Serevan rose out of the morning mist like a lie told beautifully.

It had been a fortress once, all black stone and hard angles, but a century of Sereth money had softened it — towers capped with pale roofs, bridges hung with lanterns, gardens spilling over terraces in colors Wren hadn't seen since childhood. Roses, actual roses, growing in a realm where the sky bled. It was obscene. It was also, she noted grimly, immaculately defended: sight-lines raked every approach, ballistae sat behind decorative hedges, and the guards wore armor polished bright enough to blind.

"A city pretending it isn't dying," Bramble murmured beside her. "Like a face painted on a skull."

The clay-winged messengers led them through gates that opened before they reached them, through courtyards where servants bowed low enough to hide their eyes, up stairs carpeted in deep red. Everything smelled of beeswax and lavender. Underneath both, faintly, Wren caught the other smell — the cold, empty smell of the hollowed ones — and made herself keep walking.

They were received in a hall of white marble, beneath a dome painted with the night sky of a century ago, when all five anchors burned whole. In the painting, the constellations were arranged in a great ring, five stars brighter than the rest. Four of the painted stars had been repainted recently. Darker.

Even her ceiling is lying, Wren thought. Or mourning. With Sereth it was impossible to tell.

And then Lady Sereth came through the far doors, and Wren understood why a hundred years of exiles spoke her name softly.

She was not beautiful, precisely. She was *composed*, the way a drawn bow is composed. Silver hair bound in a coronet of black iron, a gown the gray of old steel, and eyes the pale green of deep water — eyes that went first, inevitably, to Isandro, and stayed there with an attention so total it felt physical. When she moved, the whole hall adjusted around her without her seeming to ask it to.

"Isandro Voss," she said. Her voice was warm. Warmth, Wren thought, poured from a jug with a cracked lip. "A century the covenant has waited for you. A lifetime I have waited. Welcome home."

"I'm not from here," Isandro said carefully.

"No." She smiled, and it transformed her face into something almost gentle. "You're from the wound. From the valley your ancestor carved out of the world when he couldn't stomach what stewardship cost. And yet look at you — you came anyway. Blood remembers. Blood returns." She gestured, and chairs appeared, food, wine dark as garnets. "Sit. All of you. You have walked through my country bleeding and afraid, and you will sit at my table and be safe tonight. This I promise."

Pip tugged Isandro's sleeve and whispered, too loudly, "Her ceiling's got wrong stars."

"Children see truly," Sereth said, without missing a beat, her gaze flicking to Pip with something that was not quite warmth. "The ceiling is a memorial. As am I."

They sat. Wren sat last, at the angle where she could watch the doors, Sereth, and Isandro all at once, and refused the wine with a small shake of her head that the serving-woman pretended not to see.

For an hour, Sereth asked nothing and gave everything. She spoke of the road conditions east. Of the hollowed ones' movements, mapped and dated — she had maps, Wren noted, good ones, maps that rivaled Isandro's, and that fact bothered her more than the ballistae. Of the mountain anchor, its history, its guardians. She answered Bramble's questions about herbs and supplies with genuine knowledge. She even drew a dry laugh from Ossian with some wry observation about knights who outlive their oaths.

She never once looked away from Isandro longer than necessary. And when the meal was nearly done, she set down her glass and folded her hands, and the warmth in the room shifted — not vanished, but *reorganized*, the way a room reorganizes around a drawn blade.

"Now," she said. "The work."

She laid a case of black wood on the table and opened it. Inside, on velvet, lay a shard of pale stone shot through with veins of silver light. Even from across the table Wren felt it — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper and snow. Anchor-stone. True anchor-stone, torn from somewhere, and alive.

Every exile instinct Wren owned stood up and screamed.

"This is a fragment of the second anchor," Sereth said. "Taken by my grandmother's grandmother when the anchor began to fail, to keep it from shattering entirely. For three generations my house has guarded it. For three generations my house has been called tyrant for it." Her pale eyes lifted to Isandro. "I offer it to you freely. Along with my army, my roads, my granaries, my engineers. March to the mountain anchor under my banner. Reforge the covenant with the full strength of Veylmoor behind you."

"And the price?" Wren asked, before she could stop herself.

Sereth turned those deep-water eyes on her, and for a moment the kindness drained away like water finding a crack — not into cruelty, exactly, but into *clarity*. Into seeing. What she saw, apparently, was something small and already dead.

"The disgraced keeper," Sereth said mildly. "Wren of the Ashen Order. Your order died twenty years ago trying to do alone what this man may do with help. I wondered if any of you survived." She returned to Isandro without pause, as if Wren had been weather. "There is no price, heir of Voss. There is only *order*. The covenant shattered because it was left to wanderers, zealots, and cowards. It will be reforged under authority — one authority. Mine. The anchors will be gathered, carried, and set by soldiers who answer to me, and the binding will be sealed by your blood under my seal. You will be honored. Protected. Provided for the rest of your life. You will simply never be *alone* with the work again."

She smiled again, warm as a hearth.

"You've wandered lost your whole existence, Isandro Voss. Let someone else hold the map."

The hall was silent. Somewhere above them, in the painted sky, the four darkened stars seemed to press down.

Wren watched Isandro's face — watched the temptation move through it like weather. Rest. Safety. An army instead of a ragged handful. Someone else to decide, someone else to blame, someone else's shoulders under the weight he'd carried since a book in a valley archive had spoken his name. She watched him want it, honestly and visibly, and loved him for wanting it, and feared him for it.

And then she watched something else come up underneath the wanting — the stubbornness she'd first seen in a flooded cathedral, when he'd put his hand in black water because a dying sigil asked him to.

"My blood," Isandro said slowly. "Sealed under your authority."

"The covenant was strongest when it served the realm's single voice," Sereth said. "It failed when everyone claimed a piece."

"Maybe," Isandro said. "Or maybe it failed because one person decided safety mattered more than everyone else." He glanced at Wren — one glance, quick, checking — and she realized he was talking about Aldous Voss, and about her, and maybe a little about himself. "I need to think about it."

"Of course." Sereth rose, and the hall rose with her. "Guests do think. Rooms have been prepared. Guards at your doors — for your protection, naturally. Rest well, heir of Voss. Tomorrow we discuss roads." Her gaze swept the table once, resting on Wren a heartbeat longer than on the others, and Wren read the message in it plainly: *I know what you are. I know what you'll try. I am patient.*

"Sleep behind locked doors," Sereth added pleasantly, "and trust no corridors after dark. This castle is old, and old castles have moods."

She swept out, trailing lavender and cold stone, and the hall exhaled.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Pip said, into the silence, "She's got a hole in her middle. Like the hollowed ones. But hers is full of something. Not empty. Full of something sharp."

Wren looked at the child. "What kind of something?"

Pip shrugged, crayon-calloused fingers already digging in her pocket for her map. "Dunno. Sharp and hungry. Like a needle wants a thread."

Later — much later, in the guest rooms that were too fine and too quiet, with guards posted outside whose shadows didn't shift quite naturally — Wren found Isandro awake, mapping by candlelight. Mapping the castle. Every corridor, every stair, every guard-post, drawn from a single evening's walk with that uncanny memory of his.

"Planning an escape already?" she whispered.

"Planning *routes*," he said. "Escape, arrival — same lines, different arrows." He looked up at her, and in the candlelight his face was grave and young and stubborn. "Wren. The army's real. The anchor-stone's real. I think she even believes what she's saying. That's what frightens me — she's not lying. She just thinks the world should belong to whoever holds it steadyest."

"Aye," Wren said. "That's what makes her worse than a liar. Liars you can catch." She sat on the edge of the bed, elbows on knees, and stared at the candle. "Tomorrow we smile, we eat her breakfast, and we watch. Because she's going to show us what she really wants soon — they always do. Kindness runs out. It always runs out."

"And when it does?"

Wren blew out the candle.

"Then we run," she said. "And pray to whatever's left that we're faster than an army."

Outside the shuttered window, far off toward the eastern mountains, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky — or something that sounded like thunder, deep and rhythmic, like a heartbeat in the stone of the world.

The second anchor, calling.

Or answering.
