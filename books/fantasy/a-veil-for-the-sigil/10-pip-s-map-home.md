## 10. Pip's Map Home

Pip kept her most important things in a tin that used to hold peppermints. She knew it used to hold peppermints because it still smelled like them, a little, if you got your nose right down inside, and she checked most mornings just to be sure the smell hadn't gone away. The tin lived under a loose floorboard in the wagon, under the place where she slept, and it held: one blue button, one moth wing, a curl of copper wire Ossian had given her for no reason at all, and the map.

The map was the most important thing. The map was the only important thing, some days.

She had made it three times. The first version had been lost when they crossed the Ashfen and her satchel went into the black water, and Pip had cried about the button and the wire but not about the crayons, because Bramble had fished the crayons out of the mud and dried them on a flat stone in the sun like little stranded fish. The second version had been burned by accident when Ossian set the old wagon's bedding alight to kill the lice, and Pip had not cried at all that time, which Bramble said was worse, and Pip supposed it was, because she had felt the burning of it somewhere behind her ribs for a whole week.

The third version was the good one. The third version she had worked on every night for a season, asking Wren questions and then not asking Wren questions because Wren's face went strange, and asking Ossian questions and getting honest answers, and asking Bramble, who always said, *I've never been, love, you're the only one of us who's seen it,* which Pip knew wasn't quite true but which she let stand because it was kind.

The map was drawn on the back of a flour sack, because flour sacks were the biggest paper they had, and it showed a valley.

* * *

She waited until the evening the company stopped at the standing stones — the ones with the worn carvings that Wren said were older than the covenant, which Pip thought was a very long time, older than anything — and she waited until Isandro had finished his supper and was sitting on the flat stone with his knees up, doing his map-things. He did map-things every night. Ink and a pen with a nib like a tiny beak, and he'd hunch over and make the tip of his tongue poke out the corner of his mouth, and lines would come out of the pen like he was pulling them up out of the stone from somewhere deep.

Pip liked watching him do map-things more than she liked watching anyone do anything. Because he was a mapmaker from the valley, and Pip had a map of the valley, and she had decided — she had decided it a long time ago, back when he first fell out of the sky with his coat smoking and his eyes all wide — that one day she would show him.

Today was the day. She had decided that at breakfast.

She got the tin from under the floorboard and carried it against her chest with both arms, the way you carry something that could break, and she came and stood beside his stone until he noticed her, which took four breaths, because he was deep in the lines.

"Hallo," she said.

"Hallo, Pip." He looked up and did his smile, the one that was a bit crooked, like a door that didn't hang right but still opened. "Want to see something?"

"I want to show *you* something." She set the tin down on the stone and opened it, and the smell of old peppermint came out, and Isandro breathed it in and went very still, and Pip knew why. Wren had told her. The valley smelled like mint on the north slopes. Wren had never said how she knew that, and Pip had never asked, because some things you don't ask.

"That's — " Isandro started.

"It's not for you yet." Pip took out the flour-sack map and held it folded, folded, folded, and then, before she could be scared, she opened it all the way across his knees.

* * *

The valley, as Pip had drawn it, was a big green smile of a place. She had done the hills in the green crayon and pressed hard, because the hills had been *so* green, greener than anything in Veylmoor, and the sky over it was blue — not the bruised, cracked, leaking blue of Veylmoor's sky with its white wound of light down the middle, but a plain, whole, ordinary blue, the bluest crayon in the box, used down to a stub. There was a river, silver, wiggling through the middle like a piece of hair. There were houses with smoke coming out of the chimneys, drawn as little grey spirals, one spiral for each house she could remember, and she could remember eleven spirals' worth, and she had labeled some of them in her careful letters: *bakery. the mill. gran's house.* There was a tree with a swing on it, drawn much bigger than the houses because it had mattered more. There was a festival in the square — she had drawn the lanterns as yellow dots, dozens of them, and people as dots too, and she had drawn herself as a dot with a red dress, near the lanterns, near the edge, near the place where the map just stopped.

Because that was the thing about Pip's map. It stopped.

The valley's edge was a line of brown, and beyond the brown was nothing, just the flour-sack, just the empty cloth, and she had tried once to draw what was past the edge and her hand had hurt, actually hurt, a deep tired ache up the wrist, and she'd stopped, and she'd never tried again.

"That's where I was standing," she said, and put her finger on the red dot. "The festival. For the hundred years. There were lanterns, and a man with a fiddle, and Gran said I could stay up late, and then — " She moved her finger to the brown line. "There was a sound. Like the sky clearing its throat. And the lanterns all bent sideways, the flames, all the same way, like wind, but there wasn't any wind. And then it was dark, and then it wasn't dark, and it was here."

Isandro hadn't said anything. He was looking at the map the way he looked at things he was memorizing, and Pip knew that look because she'd seen him do it at Wren's sigils, at the drowned cathedral, at the stars through the wagon's torn roof. But there was something else in it too, something wet around the edges, and Pip felt suddenly shy, the way she hadn't let herself feel shy in years.

"It's not very good," she said. "I was little. And the crayons — the blue was nearly gone, so the river's more grey than — "

"It's the best map I've ever seen," Isandro said, and his voice came out rough, like a door that stuck. "Pip. This is the best map I have *ever* seen."

"It's a child's map."

"I'm a professional," he said, "and I'm telling you: cartographers spend their whole lives trying to draw a place the way the person who loves it sees it. Most of us never manage. You did it in crayon on a flour sack." He touched the edge of the paper, very lightly, at the brown line. "What's here? Past the edge?"

"Nothing. I mean — I don't know. My hand hurts when I try." She showed him, held up her wrist. "It's not like a hurt-hurt. It's like when you carry water too far and your arm remembers it after."

Isandro looked at her wrist for a long time. Then he looked at the brown line. Then he did something strange: he took his pen and, without touching the map itself, held the nib a finger's width above the empty cloth beyond the edge, and Pip watched his hand begin to shake.

Not much. Just a tremble, fine as a spider's leg. But she could see the pen fighting him, could see the nib drifting, wanting — she would have said *wanting* — to swing away, back toward the valley, like iron near a lodestone, like a moth near a lantern. Like something being pushed.

"Does it hurt you too?" she whispered.

"It's not pain," he said slowly. "It's — refusal. Like the air itself won't let me draw there." He set the pen down and flexed his fingers. "Pip, when you were taken. The lanterns bending. The dark. Do you remember anything else? Anything at all? A voice, a light, a — "

"There was a lady," Pip said.

She hadn't said that part out loud in a long time. She hadn't said it because the first time she'd told Wren, Wren had gone white as bone and left the fire and been gone half the night, and Pip had learned that some memories are heavy enough to hurt other people, and she was careful with heavy things.

But Isandro was looking at her with his crooked door of a smile all gone, and his eyes were the eyes of someone standing at the edge of a map, and Pip thought: *he's the one the book was about. He's the one it's all about. Maybe he can carry it.*

"There was a lady made of light," she said. "Or wearing it. She knelt down in the dark where the festival had been, and she looked at me, and she said — " Pip closed her eyes to get it right, because she had kept the words in the tin of her head all these years, wrapped in the same cloth as the button and the moth wing. "She said, *You're one of the chosen. Come and be a door.* And she held out her hand. And I took it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, Ossian was walking the perimeter, and his boots made their slow patient sound.

"And then?" Isandro asked, very quietly.

"And then it hurt," Pip said. "Crossing. It hurt like — like being pulled through a keyhole. And when I got here, the light-lady was gone, and I was alone in a field, and the sky was broken." She looked up at it now, the bruised blue with its white seam of leaking light, the wound that never healed, that everyone in Veylmoor had stopped seeing the way you stop seeing a scar on your own hand. "I waited for her to come back. I waited three days. I ate berries that made my mouth numb. And then Bramble found me, and she fed me soup, and she never once asked me to stop crying, and I stayed."

* * *

Isandro was quiet for a long time. Pip let him be quiet. She was good at letting people be quiet; she'd had practice, with Ossian after his bad nights, with Wren after the word *disgraced* came up, with Bramble whenever anyone mentioned the hedge she'd been driven from.

"You said she called you chosen," Isandro said at last. "A door."

"That's what I said."

"Pip." He turned on the stone to face her fully, and he took her hand, the one that had held the light-lady's hand, and turned it palm-up, and looked at it like it was a page. "The covenant — the thing that holds the two worlds together — it's held by five anchors. Five sigils. Wren told me they were made by bloodlines. Families chosen to keep the doors between worlds. Four of the anchors have gone dark. The fifth is my family's. My valley." His thumb moved over her palm, and Pip felt it, faint as a far-off fiddle: a hum, a warmth, like standing near a hearth through a wall. "But the anchors were weakening for a hundred years before they started failing. And the old keepers — they must have known. They must have looked for new blood. New bearers." He looked up at her. "The children, Pip. The children who slipped through the veil. You weren't stolen. You were *called*. You were all meant to be what I am."

"A door," Pip said.

"A door." His voice cracked on it. "And it hurt you, and the light-lady vanished, and no one came. Because the veil was already too torn to carry you safely. They sent children through a breaking door to save a breaking world, and the door — " He stopped. He looked at the brown line on her map, at all that careful empty nothing, and Pip watched him understand something that made him look sick. "The door ate the attempt. Every attempt. And the children just — landed. Alone. In a broken world."

"Am I the only one?" Pip asked. It was the question she never asked, the one that lived at the bottom of the tin with the moth wing. "The only one who made it?"

"No," Isandro said. "There's a village two days east of the drowned cathedral. Wren mentioned it. Hollowed ones come out of the hills there, and the villagers leave offerings, and some of the hollowed ones — " He stopped again, and this time he didn't start again, and Pip understood the shape of what he wasn't saying. Some of the hollowed ones were small. Some of the hollowed ones had been little once, and called, and pulled through a keyhole, and had not landed whole.

Pip looked at her map. At the green smile of the valley, the eleven spirals of smoke, the swing tree, the yellow lantern-dots, the red dot that was her. She thought about the other red dots she had never drawn, the ones that would be scattered all over Veylmoor if she'd had enough flour sacks — children from her festival, children from other festivals, other hundred-year nights, all of them called through, all of them landed, all of them alone in the dark with a broken sky.

And she thought about what Isandro had just said, the thing underneath the thing: *you were meant to be what I am.* Meant to hold a door. Meant to carry a world. And she hadn't. She'd eaten numb berries and cried and been carried off in a hedge-witch's cart, and the world had gone on breaking, and the light-lady had never come back, and Pip had spent six years telling herself it was because the lady had forgotten her.

She hadn't forgotten her. Pip knew that now, with a certainty that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. The lady hadn't come back because the lady couldn't. Because the door was broken, and the lady was on the other side of it, or gone, or — and this was the thought that made Pip's eyes sting — because the lady had sent a hundred children through a tearing veil hoping one of them would live, and none of them had been enough, and there was no lady anymore, maybe. Maybe the sending had used her all up.

"If I'd been stronger," Pip said. "If I'd held on. If I'd been a door instead of just — a girl who fell through."

"Hey." Isandro's hand came to her shoulder, warm and steady. "Listen to me. You were six. You were *six*, Pip, and something tore you out of your grandmother's festival and dropped you into a dying world, and you survived. You built a map of home from memory so you'd never lose it. That's not failing. That's the strongest thing I've heard of in either world." He tapped the flour sack, gently, at the swing tree. "And for what it's worth — this isn't a child's map. This is the only accurate map of the valley's heart that exists outside my own head. The festival square. The mill on the river bend. Pip, this is *cartography*."

Pip sniffed and wiped her nose on her sleeve, which Bramble said was a habit and Pip said was a system.

"Will you keep it?" she asked. "While we travel. In case I lose it. You keep things safe. You keep them *exactly*."

"I'll keep it like it's the founding charter of both worlds," Isandro said, and he folded it along her careful folds, and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where the important papers went, and Pip felt something in her settle that had been unsettled for six years.

* * *

They sat together while the fire burned down, and Pip told him about the fiddle man, and the mint on the north slopes, and Gran's hands, which had flour on them always, even on festival night, and Isandro listened the way Wren listened to the anchors — like every word was a reading, like the words might tell him whether the world could be saved.

It was Ossian's boots that changed. Pip heard it first, because she always heard Ossian first: the slow patient perimeter-walk stopping. Then his whistle, two notes, low — the *someone's coming* whistle, the one that meant not-hollowed, because hollowed didn't get whistled at, hollowed got swords.

Bramble was up with her staff. Wren came out of the dark with her scarred sigil already faintly glowing, which it only did when she was afraid, though she'd deny it.

"Riders," Ossian said, from the edge of the firelight. "Six. Banners folded, but I'd know that grey anywhere." His jaw did the thing it did. "That's Sereth's vanguard. And they're not riding like a patrol, Wren. They're riding like an escort."

"Escort for what?" Bramble asked.

Wren's eyes found Isandro, and then found Pip, and Pip saw the keeper do the arithmetic she herself had just finished: a boy who could relight the anchors, and a girl who'd been called through the veil and survived it, sitting side by side at a fire, both of them exactly what the regent of Veylmoor wanted most in all the world.

"Pip," Wren said quietly, "get your tin."

Pip got her tin. And she put her hand flat against Isandro's coat, over the folded map, over her home, and she watched the grey riders come out of the dark with their torches burning steady, and she thought: *the light-lady held out her hand, and I took it, and it was the worst and best thing I ever did.*

These riders were holding out their hands too.

This time, she was going to watch very carefully before she took anything.
