## 11. Betrayal at the Second Anchor

Ossian had sworn oaths twice in his life, and both times the words had been the easy part.

The first oath he'd spoken at nineteen, barefoot on cold marble, a sword flat against his chest while old Commander Haleth pressed it down and said, *The realm above your name, knight, always the realm above your name.* The second he'd spoken eleven years later, alone, in the dark, when he broke the first one — because the realm had turned out to be a woman in a high tower who wanted his sword pointed at farmers, and Ossian had discovered that some oaths are cages dressed as honor.

He thought about both oaths now, climbing the switchback trail toward the second anchor, because the air had that taste again. The one he remembered from the day everything ended. Cold iron and old pennies. The taste of something about to go wrong.

"Stop sniffing," Pip said from her perch on his shoulders. "You do it every time you get nervous."

"I'm not nervous."

"You're doing the thing with your jaw."

"The thing with my jaw is called thinking."

"It's called being scared but with your face hard," she said, and dug her heels into his ribs like he was a pony. Which, after four days on the road, he more or less accepted he was.

Below them, the others picked their way up the mountain path: Wren in front with her scarred hand wrapped to the wrist, Bramble hauling her pack of bottles and dried herbs and at least three things she refused to explain, and Isandro behind them all, walking like a man reading an invisible page. He'd been doing that since the drowned cathedral. Since his blood had lit a dead sigil brighter than sunrise.

*Useful,* Ossian thought, watching him. *And therefore hunted. And therefore doomed, if we're careless.*

The mountain anchor sat near the summit of Karreth's Tooth, in a hollow of black stone that the locals — the ones who still existed, the ones Sereth hadn't taxed into flight — called the Choir Seat. You could hear it before you saw it: a low harmonic hum, almost below sound, vibrating in the teeth and the breastbone. Wren said it was the covenant breathing. Ossian said it was the world clearing its throat before saying something terrible.

Either way, they reached it at midday, under a sky so cracked it looked like dropped porcelain, and Ossian's stomach did the thing it always did before a fight.

The anchor itself was smaller than he expected. A stone the size of a man's heart, floating waist-high in the center of the hollow, turning slowly. Sigils crawled across its surface like living things — half of them dark, dead, their light gone out — and around it, carved into the rock floor in a ring wider than a house, the binding script ran in letters older than any kingdom Ossian had ever betrayed or served.

Wren knelt at the ring's edge and didn't touch it. She never touched them first. "It's holding," she said quietly. "Barely. Same as the cathedral was."

"But this one still has light left," Isandro said. He crouched beside her, and Ossian saw it again — that hunger in him, the mapmaker meeting new terrain. "Three of the sigils are still burning."

"Four," Wren corrected. "Look again. There." Her scarred hand rose, pointing without touching. "That one's guttering. It'll be dark within a month. Maybe less."

"And then?"

"And then the mountain starts forgetting it's a mountain." Wren's voice stayed flat, which was how Ossian knew how much it cost her. "Stone slides where stone shouldn't. Rivers run uphill. People wake up hollowed and don't know their own children. We've seen what happened around the third anchor's territory. This will be worse. More people here."

Isandro stared at the floating stone for a long moment. Then he rolled up his sleeve.

"No," Ossian said.

"We don't know how much time—"

"You lit one sigil by accident and half the realm felt it. You think Sereth didn't?" Ossian came down off the rocks, setting Pip on her feet, and crossed the hollow to stand over both of them. "The regent's men found us within two days of the cathedral. Two. That wasn't luck. That wasn't tracking. She knows exactly where the anchors are, and she knows exactly what you are. If you bleed on this stone, you might as well send her a map with our camp drawn on it."

"He's right," Bramble said from behind her pack, not looking up. She was grinding something that smelled like burnt rosemary. "And before anyone asks — yes, I'm listening, no, I'm not helping, and yes, I already made the warding sachets. Six of them. Don't ask what's in them."

"What's in them?" Pip asked immediately.

"Bramble," Ossian warned.

"Mushrooms and spite."

Wren hadn't moved. She was still looking at the fourth guttering sigil, and Ossian watched something happen in her face that he'd only seen once before — the night she'd told them about her order, all of them dead, every keeper burned out or broken, and how she'd walked away from the ashes believing the world could fix itself without her. It never had. Now here was a man whose blood could fix it, kneeling next to her, asking permission to try.

"Wren," Ossian said softly.

She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Not today. Today we study it. Map it, sketch the script, take rubbings. Tomorrow, if the sky holds, we decide." She looked up at Isandro, and her voice gentled in a way it almost never did. "You gave us proof at the cathedral. You don't have to keep bleeding to convince us you're real."

Isandro exhaled. Nodded. Pulled his journal out instead, and for the next hour the hollow filled with the small domestic sounds of work — charcoal scratching, Bramble muttering at her mushrooms, Pip narrating everything she saw to no one. Ossian took the high ground, as he always did, and looked out over the broken country below.

That's when he saw the dust.

* * *

It came up the western road in good order — too good order. Not raiders, not refugees. Soldiers moved in columns or they didn't move at all, and these moved like they'd drilled it. Banners snapped at the column heads: silver thread on white, the sigil of Veylmoor's regency, and beneath it the personal mark of Lady Sereth — a closed hand holding a star.

"They found us," Ossian called down. "Fast."

Wren was on her feet before he finished. "How fast?"

"Half a day out. Maybe less. They've got horses on the lower switchbacks." He counted banners, counted again. "Sixty? Seventy? Heavy on crossbows."

"That's not a hunting party," Bramble said, coming to stand beside him. All the flippancy had gone out of her voice. "That's a garrison."

"They can't know we're here," Isandro said, climbing up to join them. "We covered our tracks. Wren laid wards—"

"Boy." Ossian kept his eyes on the column. "When a lord sends seventy soldiers up a mountain nobody climbs, they weren't following you. They were already coming. The question is whether they were coming for the anchor—" he paused, and the old cold settled into his gut, the same cold from the marble hall, from the farm fields, from every time the words *for the realm* had preceded a knife — "or whether they were coming for *you*, and we simply arrived at the appointment they'd already scheduled."

Nobody answered that. Nobody needed to.

"We go up," Wren said. "There's a goat track on the north face. Bleak, but it drops into the valley beyond, and from there—"

"And leave the anchor?" Pip asked.

Wren hesitated. It was half a heartbeat, but Ossian caught it, and he could see Isandro catch it too.

"We come back," Wren said. "With better preparation. With—"

"With her army," Isandro said quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

He was staring down at the column, at the neat white banners, and Ossian watched the mapmaker's mind working — the same mind that had read a century-old prophecy and found its own name in fresh ink. "Think about it. She offered us sanctuary. She offered soldiers to guard me while I reforge the covenant. What does a regent want with guarding a cartographer?" He turned, and his face had gone pale in a way Ossian recognized from mirrors. "Unless the cartographer isn't the point. Unless the anchors are the point, and I'm just the key that fits them."

"You don't know that," Bramble started.

"She knew about the cathedral," Isandro said. "Her soldiers arrived before we'd even finished burying the dead hollowed ones. She knew where the second anchor was and sent a garrison before we got here. She's mapping the anchors faster than I am." His voice cracked slightly. "I map realms for a living. I know what it looks like when someone's building toward a claim."

The wind came up the hollow then, through the cracks in the sky, carrying that iron-and-penny taste stronger than ever, and beneath their feet the anchor hummed — low, steady, like something bracing itself.

Ossian looked at Wren. Wren looked at the anchor. And Pip, small and sharp-eyed at the edge of the group, said the thing none of the adults wanted to say:

"If we run, she takes the stone. If we stay, she takes Isandro. Either way she wins unless somebody does something clever." She kicked a pebble into the hollow. "I vote Ossian. He's the cleverest when he's being miserable."

"Thank you, Pip."

"You're welcome."

* * *

They did not run. Later, Ossian would turn that decision over in his mind the way he turned over old oaths, looking for the flaw in the wording, and he would find the same answer every time: there wasn't one. They stayed because running meant surrendering the last lit anchor-stone in the eastern range, and because Wren believed — still believed, after everything — that titles and uniforms were not the same thing as intent.

Seventy soldiers climbed the mountain. By late afternoon they were filing into the upper switchbacks, and by dusk the vanguard reached the plateau below the hollow.

Ossian stood alone at the ring's edge to meet them, because he was the one wearing a sword, and because some conversations only work when they start with steel visible.

The officer leading the vanguard pulled off her helmet at thirty paces. Young. Cropped red hair. The kind of earnest face that oaths love to devour.

"In the name of Lady Sereth, Regent of Veylmoor," she called, "you are asked to stand aside. No harm is intended to the covenant-bearer or his companions. The regent sends her regards and her protection."

"Her protection arrived before we did," Ossian called back. "Interesting kind of protection. Knows the roads better than we do."

"The regent keeps watch over all the anchors. It has been her charge for nine years."

"It has been her *harvest* for nine years, more likely. Where are the rest of them, soldier? The watchers at the other anchors. The garrisons assigned before us. Because I've walked this whole cursed country, and I haven't seen one lit sigil outside the ones this man relit with his own blood."

The officer's jaw tightened. Earnest faces lie worst; they haven't practiced. "Stand aside, ser. Please. I have orders regarding the stone, and I would rather not carry them out past you."

"There it is." Ossian didn't move. Behind him, at the hollow's edge, he heard the company gather — Wren's uneven footsteps, Bramble's bottles clicking, Pip's small breath, Isandro saying nothing at all. "*Orders regarding the stone.* Not the man. Say it plainly, and I'll believe you mean no harm."

For a long moment the officer said nothing. Then, quietly, honestly, terribly: "I'm told the covenant-bearer requires... assistance. Extended assistance. Under the regent's direct supervision. For as long as the reforging takes."

"How long does the reforging take?"

"I wasn't told."

"No," Ossian said. "You weren't. Because the answer is *forever*. Because a covenant bound to one person's supervision isn't a covenant — it's a throne with extra steps. I've carried a sword for lords who talked like that. They all used the word *protection* right up until the moment protection became possession."

"Ser—" The officer's voice dropped further, until only he could hear it. "I served under Haleth. Before. I remember what you did at Aldenford, when you put your sword down and walked. Half the company thought you a traitor." She met his eyes. "The other half thought you the only honest man among us. I cannot disobey. But I can be slow."

She turned and gestured, and the vanguard advanced anyway — because of course it did, because orders move like water and find every crack — and Ossian drew his sword and set himself at the ring's edge, and understood, with the bitter clarity of a man hearing a familiar song in an unfamiliar key, exactly how this night would go.

They fought, briefly and badly, because the officer was slow as promised, and because Ossian had spent eleven years learning exactly how much force dishonor required. Three soldiers bled. Nobody died. And in the middle of it — while blades rang and Bramble's sachets went up in stinking smoke and Wren shouted for Isandro to stay back — Ossian saw the second party slip past the fighting along the northern rocks. Twelve men. Quiet ones. No banners.

They went straight for the stone.

He killed the distance screaming, but screaming is slow and twelve quiet men are fast, and by the time he reached the hollow's center they had done the impossible thing — the thing Wren had sworn could not be done, the thing that proved they had not come ignorant. They had brought chains of dark iron etched with counter-sigils, and they had thrown them over the second anchor, and the great harmonic hum that had lived in the mountain since before kingdoms stuttered —

— and stopped.

The silence was worse than any battle. Ossian felt it go out of the rock under his boots, out of his own bones, like a hearth dying in a house he'd begun to think of as warm. One soldier lifted the floating stone — now merely heavy, merely rock — and packed it into a lead-lined case as casually as a man pocketing a wallet.

From across the hollow, Isandro cried out — and Ossian saw him stagger, saw him clutch his chest, saw blood seep between the fingers pressed over his heart as if somewhere a thread tied to the stone had just been cut.

"No," Wren whispered. She had gone gray. Her scarred hand was shaking. "No, no — they can't hold it sealed, the counter-script is crude, it will leak, it will *sour* — do they understand what they've done? A living anchor caged in dead iron? Within a week it won't be an anchor at all. It'll be a wound with a lid on it."

One of the twelve — the leader, graying at the temples, unhurried amid the chaos — turned and looked directly at Ossian. Not at the sword. At him.

"Lady Sereth's compliments, ser knight," the man said, bowing slightly, infuriatingly polite. "She bids you keep the covenant-bearer safe. All the way to the Spire. She says—" and here the man smiled, thin as a paper cut, "—she says he cannot forge what she holds, and he cannot run from what he is. She will collect him when the roads are clear. See that he arrives intact. She was very specific about *intact*."

Then the twelve were gone, down the northern rocks, into the gathering dark, carrying the heart of the mountain in a box.

Ossian stood in the sudden silence with his sword hanging loose at his side, and the bitter recognition he'd been outrunning all day finally caught him and held him still. He had stood exactly here before — metaphorically, at least. On a marble floor at nineteen, swearing the realm above his name, while a commander smiled and meant something else entirely. He had learned the lesson once, paid for it in farms and friends and eleven years of exile: *an oath given to power is a leash, no matter how gold the collar.*

Sereth had never wanted help reforging the covenant.

She wanted the covenant. Full stop. Bound to her hand, held in her boxes, spent at her pleasure — and Isandro, the only key that fit, delivered to her side intact.

"Ossian." Wren's voice, very close. He hadn't heard her cross the hollow. Her scarred hand gripped his sleeve, and her eyes were fixed on the empty air where the anchor had hummed for a thousand years. "The stone. If she seals it long enough—"

"I know."

"The fourth anchor is all that's left in the east. And after that—"

"*I know.*"

Behind her, Isandro sat slumped on the carved rock, pressing his bloody shirt against his chest, pale as milk — and Pip was crouched beside him, and Bramble was already digging through her pack, and the whole fragile, unwanted family of them was looking to Ossian the way they sometimes did, as if exile had taught him things worth knowing.

Maybe it had.

He sheathed his sword. Picked up his pack. And said the only thing left to say:

"The Spire, then. If she wants him delivered intact—" he glanced at Isandro, and let the old wolfish smile surface, the one Haleth's marble hall had never managed to beat out of him, "—then we'd best make sure we arrive *first*."
