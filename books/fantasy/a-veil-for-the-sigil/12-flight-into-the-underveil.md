## 12. Flight Into the Underveil

The mountain anchor's stone was still warm in Isandro's memory when the horns began.

They came from the east ridge, three notes rising in a pattern he'd learned to dread in only two days among these people: not a hunting call, not a warning, but a *summons* — Sereth's soldiers telling each other where the prey had broken cover. Ossian heard it first, of course. He always heard things first, the way old soldiers hear weather coming in their knees.

"Down," he said, and they went down.

There was no time for a proper plan. There had been no time for a proper plan since the second anchor, since the moment Bramble had yanked Pip behind her and Wren had spat a word in a language that made the air taste like copper, and the six of them had run from the people who'd arrived wearing helpful faces and carrying chains. Isandro ran now with his lungs full of frost and his mind full of the sigil-stone's dying light, and behind him the hollow country unfolded like a map he'd drawn from dreams and hated being right about.

"They'll cut us off at the Greyford pass," Ossian said, running beside him, sword still sheathed because a drawn blade made noise and noise made graves. "We can't outrun them. We can't outfight them."

"So we do what we always do," Bramble panted from the rear, one arm slung around Pip, who had gone unnaturally quiet — the silence of a child who has learned that quiet is survival. "We go where they won't."

Wren stopped so suddenly Isandro nearly collided with her. She stood at the lip of a ravine where the ground simply ended, black air falling away into nothing, and on her face was an expression Isandro had come to know: the look of someone consulting a memory she wished she didn't have.

"There's a door down there," she said.

"A door." Ossian's voice was flat. "In a hole."

"In the *Underveil*. The covenant's architects built beneath the world before they built upon it. Every keeper knew the entrances. We were forbidden to use them." She looked at her own scarred hands, the sigil-marks that had burned her order down to one. "I am very good at doing forbidden things. It's what got everyone killed."

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then Pip tugged Bramble's sleeve and pointed down into the dark, and said, in her small cracked voice, "It's warm down there. I can feel it. Like under blankets."

Isandro felt it too — or thought he did. A faint updraft from the ravine, carrying a smell of dust and deep stone and something else, something older: ink. Old ink, vellum, candle smoke. The smell of an archive. The cartographer in him sat up like a hound scenting game.

"How far down?" he asked.

"Far enough that Sereth's maps don't include it," Wren said. "Which is the only kind of far enough there is."

She went over the edge first.

* * *

Descent was a word for it. What it actually was, was falling with extra steps — handholds worn smooth by centuries, ledges that crumbled under Ossian's weight, Bramble muttering charms against the dark that glowed faintly green and died within arm's reach. Isandro counted the turns the way he counted everything, because counting was how he kept terror in its cage. Forty-one ledges. Sixteen switchbacks. One long stretch where the wall fell away entirely and they crossed a natural bridge of fused glass, and below it something vast shifted in the darkness with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and nobody spoke for a hundred steps after.

Pip rode on Isandro's back for the last part, her arms around his neck, her breath hot against his ear. She weighed almost nothing. Children who slip through veils, he was learning, weigh less than they should, as if the crossing took some part of them and never gave it back.

"It smells like my crayons," Pip whispered. "Before. Do you remember before?"

"I remember paper," Isandro said. "It's the thing I remember best."

"Me too," she said, and held on tighter.

They reached bottom in a place that should not have existed: a cavern the size of the valley itself, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, and rising from its floor — stairs. Wide ones, cut from pale stone, ascending toward a doorway carved with five interlocking rings. The rings were unlit. But as Wren approached, her scarred sigils flickered once, weakly, like a lamp running out of oil — and the outermost ring answered with a pulse of white so brief Isandro might have dreamed it.

"The doors know keepers," Wren said quietly. "Even ruined ones."

"And me?" Isandro asked.

Wren looked back at him. In the green witchlight her face was all angles and exhaustion, but underneath it, unmistakably, was hope — the dangerous kind, the kind she kept trying to kill and failing.

"You're the blood," she said. "Doors are just locks, and locks are just questions. Touch it and see what answer you get."

He touched it.

The five rings lit — not all at once but in sequence, like a sentence being read aloud, and the last ring to light was the fifth, and it lit *gold*, the color of the valley, the color of home, and the door swung inward on air that smelled, unmistakably, of his grandfather's study.

* * *

Inside was a library.

Not a room of books — Isandro had known rooms of books his whole life, had loved them, had built his career out of them. This was something else. Shelves rose in terraces carved directly from the living rock, climbing into darkness beyond any lantern's reach, and between them hung lamps of glass that burned without flame, cold and steady, the same color as the veil-light he'd seen bleeding through the sky's cracks. And everywhere — *everywhere* — documents. Scrolls in sealed tubes. Folios chained open on reading stands. Great sheets of vellum pinned to the walls like maps of nowhere, covered in diagrams of the five anchors, the five realms-threads, the covenant itself rendered as architecture.

Bramble let out a low whistle. "Hedge-witch heaven."

"Don't touch anything chained," Wren warned, already moving down the central aisle with the stiff urgency of someone returning to a place that had hurt her. "The chains aren't punishment. They're *addresses*. Each folio is fixed to the question it answers. You pull a chain, you're asking."

Ossian stationed himself at the entrance, back to the room, watching the stair. "Then ask quickly. They'll find another way down eventually. Soldiers always do."

Isandro barely heard him. He was standing before the largest of the wall-hung vellums, and his heart was doing something complicated in his chest, because he recognized the hand.

He'd spent his life drawing maps of places he'd never seen, guided by dreams he'd never trusted. The hand on this document drew the same coastlines. The same impossible bays. The same little annotations in the margins — *verify by dream, third iteration* — that Isandro had been writing in his own workbooks since he was nineteen years old.

"This is Aldous Voss's hand," he said. His voice came out strange. "This is — I know this handwriting. It's mine."

"It's your inheritance," Wren said, coming to stand beside him. "For better or worse. Mostly worse, if the confession chapter thirteen of your family history is anything like I fear."

But Isandro wasn't listening anymore. He had pulled a chain.

The folio that swung free was bound in grey leather, unmarked, and when he opened it the first page held a single line in that familiar-unfamiliar hand:

*Let it be recorded why the fifth anchor was severed, so that those who come after may judge us.*

Below it, the text began — dense, careful, the writing of a man building a legal case against himself. Isandro read the opening lines with his pulse loud in his ears:

*"We did not build the covenant to imprison magic between worlds. We built it to teach it manners."*

He read that line three times. Around him, his companions had drifted close without his noticing — Wren at his shoulder, Bramble with Pip half-asleep against her, even Ossian abandoning his watch for ten steps to look.

"We were taught," Wren said slowly, "that the covenant was a wall. That the founders sealed the realms apart to protect Veylmoor from the wildness beyond. Every keeper learns it as law. It's the first thing—"

"That's what Aldous wanted taught," Isandro finished. He turned pages with increasing speed. Diagrams bloomed across the vellum: not walls, but *valves*. Not seals, but *gates with hinges*. "Look at this. The anchors aren't plugs. They're regulators. The covenant was designed to let magic flow between the worlds — gently, in measure, like water through a millrace. Enough to feed both realms. Not enough to flood either."

"Then why does everything here starve for it," Ossian said, "and everything there choke on it?"

"Because someone turned the valves wrong." Isandro's finger stopped on a passage near the end of the opening folio, and the words seemed to rise off the page. "*Aldous feared the flow. His brother Meric did not. When the great reforging came due — the centenary renewal the covenant demands — Aldous refused. Not out of wisdom. Out of fear. He severed the fifth anchor rather than stand at the table where his brother meant to widen the channels.*"

The cavern library seemed to tilt. Isandro gripped the reading stand.

"He didn't flee the collapse," he whispered. "There was no collapse. Not then. There was a *choice*, and he chose a wall instead of a gate, and the wall has been failing ever since, and everything since — the hollowed ones, the darkened anchors, Pip torn from her family, Wren's dead order, all of it — it's not the covenant working badly. It's the covenant *strangled*, trying to do its job with four-fifths of its lungs tied off."

Silence held the library. Somewhere above, faintly, the horns sounded again — muffled now, distant, absurd.

Pip stirred against Bramble and murmured, half-asleep: "That's why it doesn't work right. My valley. It's holding its breath."

"Yes," Isandro said softly. "Exactly that."

Wren had gone very still. When Isandro looked at her, her scarred hands were trembling.

"All my order's teachings," she said. "Everything we were punished for questioning. The regent's laws. Sereth's 'restoration.'" Her jaw tightened. "Sereth knows this. She must know this. And she wants to reforge the covenant with herself holding every anchor — which means she doesn't want to *open* the gates. She wants to own the only keyhole left."

"Then we get there first," Ossian said, returning to his post at the stair, "with the truth instead of the army."

Isandro turned back to the folio — and stopped.

At the bottom of the final page, beneath the founder's careful self-accusation, a newer hand had written a single line. Fresher ink. Recent. The letters small and precise:

*You are not the first to read this, cartographer. Come deeper. The archives keep more than confessions — and the regent's men are already inside.*

Isandro looked up. The lamplight ran fifty terraces deep into the dark, and somewhere in that dark, a shelf creaked.

Wren followed his gaze. Her hand found her knife.

"Well," she said. "Ask quickly indeed."
