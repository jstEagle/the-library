## 13. The Founder's Confession

The Underveil did not echo.

Isandro had noticed it within the first hour of their descent, when Ossian's boot scuffed stone and the sound simply died, swallowed by walls that drank noise the way the valley above drank magic. It made the silence feel less like absence and more like attention. As if the dark beneath the world were listening for something worth hearing.

Now, three hours deeper, he stood in a chamber that had clearly been built to be listened to.

The archives were not what he'd expected. He'd imagined vaults, chains, dust, the defensive clutter of people hiding dangerous truths. Instead the room opened wide as a cathedral's nave, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, and every surface — floor, pillars, the curving walls themselves — was inscribed with script. Not painted. *Grown*, somehow, into the stone like veins in marble, faintly luminous, shifting color when he moved past them: silver to gold to a bruised violet that hurt to look at directly.

"It responds to bloodline," Wren said quietly behind him. "Or it used to."

She stood near the entrance, arms crossed, her scarred sigil-hand tucked against her ribs where she always kept it when she thought no one was looking. Pip was asleep against Bramble's shoulder by the door, crayon map still clutched in one fist even in sleep. Ossian had taken watch at the stair, though Isandro suspected there was nothing left down here to keep watch against except themselves.

"Which way do I look?" Isandro asked.

"That's the problem." Wren stepped forward, her voice dropping into the cadence she used when she recited keeper-lore — flat, precise, afraid of being wrong. "The archives were keyed to the five anchors. Four sections for four anchors, and the fifth—" She gestured at the chamber's center, where a raised dais rose from the floor, bare and blank, its surface smooth as closed eyes. "The fifth section should be here. The valley anchor. The Voss anchor."

"Should be."

"The whole room is lit up like a festival," she said. "Except that stone. It's been dead a hundred years, same as everything your family touched."

The words landed the way they always landed now — a small cold coin dropped down his spine. *Your family.* Two days ago his family had been a cottage, a printing press, an archive job in a town where everyone knew his name and none of them knew why. Now his family was a wound in the world and a name that made sigils light up or go out.

He climbed the dais anyway.

The blank stone was warm. That surprised him more than anything else could have — after the drowned cathedral, after feeling the first anchor sing under his palms, he had expected cold. But the dais was warm the way a hearthstone is warm long after the fire dies: heat without life. Residual. Inherited.

"I don't know how to open it," he said.

"You might not need to." Wren came to the edge of the dais but didn't step onto it. Keepers, he was learning, had rules about standing on things that belonged to other people's power. "Blood opened the first anchor. But this one isn't waiting for blood. Look at the inscription ring."

He looked. Around the base of the dais, cut deep and old, ran a single line of text in a hand he recognized — because he'd spent his whole childhood copying it. The founding charter of the valley hung in the archive back home, behind glass, and every schoolchild traced its letters:

*Here we lay down wonder so our children may keep their lives.*

Aldous Voss's handwriting. His ancestor's. The founder's.

But the ring around the dais wasn't the charter. It was longer, denser, written small and hurried in the same unmistakable script, and as Isandro crouched to read it, the letters began to glow — softly at first, then brighter, feeding on his proximity the way the first anchor had fed on his blood.

*To whoever of my line comes seeking,* the ring read. *I will not ask forgiveness. I will ask only that you understand before you judge.*

"Oh no," Wren breathed.

The dais opened.

Not dramatically — no grinding of stone, no burst of light. The surface simply softened, the way ice softens, and folded itself aside, revealing a recessed well lined in the same living script. And inside, resting where a heart would rest if the chamber had one, lay a book.

It was bound in gray leather gone stiff with age, and Isandro knew before he touched it that this was the thing the whole Underveil had been carved to protect. The architects hadn't built these depths for records. They'd built them for *this* — a confession sealed beneath the world, too heavy to carry up and too shameful to destroy.

His hands shook as he lifted it out.

"Isandro." Wren's voice had changed. Careful now. The tone of someone approaching a wounded animal. "Whatever's in there—"

"It has my name on it." It did. Embossed on the cover, worn shallow by a century of darkness: A.V.

He carried it to the nearest pillar, where the light of the walls was steadiest, and sat down on the cold floor with the book across his knees. Wren hesitated, then sat beside him — close enough that their shoulders almost touched, far enough that she could pretend they hadn't. From the doorway, Bramble watched with Pip still asleep against her, and said nothing, which was its own kind of kindness.

Isandro opened the cover.

The first pages were ordinary enough — inventories, correspondence, the meticulous records of a sigil-keeper's apprentice risen high. Aldous Voss had been Third Keeper of the Fifth Anchor, it appeared. Trusted. Honored. Entrusted. Page after page of careful notation, sigil-diagrams drawn with a precision Isandro recognized from his own maps, because he'd inherited the hand along with the name.

Then, a third of the way in, the handwriting changed.

It got smaller. Tighter. The ink went from brown to black, as if the man had switched pens and never switched back, and Isandro understood with a lurch that this was where the confession proper began — that Aldous had known exactly which pages would damn him and had written them anyway, deliberately, in a different ink so that any reader would feel the shift like a change in temperature.

*They told me the covenant was failing,* the new hand wrote. *I want that recorded plainly, because everything I did afterward, I did believing I was the only one who saw it truly.*

*The four elder anchors were dying. Not weakening — dying, the way a body dies, slowly and then all at once. The council spoke of reforging. Sacrifice. The willing gift of blood and years, keeper after keeper, generation after generation, forever. An endless tax paid in the only currency the covenant accepts.*

*I had children.*

*Forgive me the smallness of that sentence. I have written it and struck it through eleven times and I write it again because it is the whole of the truth: I had children, and the council asked me to spend them.*

Isandro stopped reading.

His throat had gone tight. Beside him, Wren had gone very still, in the way he was beginning to learn meant she already knew how a story ended and was bracing herself to watch him find out.

He turned the page.

*So I studied instead of sacrificing. I found what the others were too pious to look for: that the covenant does not require five anchors. It requires five willingly given ones — but it can survive on four, briefly, painfully, if the fifth is removed cleanly rather than allowed to fail. Cut away the rotting limb and the body lives. The realms would suffer. Magic would fray at every seam. Thousands would hollow. But my valley — my hidden, warded, magic-dead valley — would stand outside the suffering entirely, a pocket sewn shut against the storm.*

*I told myself I was saving a remnant. Noah in his ark. I told myself the covenant was already broken and I was merely choosing who would break with it.*

*These were lies. I record them as lies so that no descendant of mine may ever repeat them comfortably.*

*On the night of the centenary festival — fitting, is it not? I chose the celebration, because no one watches a keeper closely on a night of fireworks — I went alone to the fifth anchor and I severed it. Not broke it. Severe is the wrong word; there is no clean word. I unwove it. I took the binding-thread of the covenant into my own hands and I pulled, and I felt every anchor in the world flinch like struck bells, and I sealed the thread inside my own blood so that no keeper could ever reforge what I had unmade.*

*My children woke the next morning in a valley that magic could not touch. Safe. Whole. Cursed.*

*The hollowed ones you will meet beyond our borders — those are mine. Every emptied face, every wandering shell. When I pulled the thread, the decay that should have taken a century arrived in a day. I did not flee the collapse. There was no collapse until I caused one.*

*And the worst of it, the thing I set down last because my hand refuses it: the council had been wrong. The sacrifice they demanded was terrible, yes — but the covenant was mendable. My fellow keepers died over the following decades holding together what I tore, buying time with their lives, and every one of them died knowing whose fault it was. I lived to ninety-four. I buried my children in soft earth. I am the reason the word "hollowed" exists.*

*I seal this confession beneath the world because I could not bear to leave it where my heirs would find it easily, and I could not bear to destroy it, because a coward who erases his crime commits it twice. If you are reading this, you bear my blood and therefore my debt. The thread is in your blood now, as it was in mine. You can reforge what I unwove.*

*It will cost you what it should have cost me.*

*I am sorry. I know that word is worthless. I use it anyway, because it is the only thing I have left to give, and I refuse to die owing everything.*

*Aldous Voss, Third Keeper of the Fifth Anchor, Founder of the Valley, Coward*

The last page fell still under Isandro's fingers.

For a long moment nobody moved. Even the living script on the walls seemed dimmed, as if the chamber itself were holding its breath around the two of them and the dead man's handwriting.

"So," Isandro said finally. His voice came out strange, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall. "He didn't flee it."

"No," Wren said softly.

"He *built* it. The collapse. The hollowing. All of it—" The words snagged. He tried again. "Every hollowed one we've passed on the road. The towns going gray. The sky cracked open over Veylmoor. That's not decay. That's not some ancient failure wearing down. That's a hundred years ago, one night, one man, one *decision*—"

"Yes."

"And my family slept safe. For a hundred years. In the hole he cut in the world." Something hot was climbing his throat, and he let it, because swallowing it felt like agreeing with Aldous. "While keepers died holding the seams shut. While your order burned itself out patching his damage. While *Pip*—" He couldn't finish it. He looked toward the doorway, at the small sleeping shape against Bramble's shoulder, a child slipped through a torn veil into a realm coming apart at the seams, and the fury in his chest turned over into something worse. Shame. Cold, total, ancestral shame, the kind that doesn't belong to you and takes up residence anyway.

Wren reached over. Not for his hand — for the book. She closed it gently, both her hands flat on the gray leather, her scarred sigil pressed white against the cover.

"Stop," she said.

"He confessed, Wren. He wrote it all down, the whole—"

"*I know what he wrote. I've read it.*"

That stopped him. He stared at her. "You've read it?"

"My order knew the fifth anchor didn't fail naturally. We never knew why, or who. We spent sixty years and forty-one lives trying to hold a covenant that one man sabotaged in an evening." Her voice stayed level, but her scarred hand had begun to tremble against the book's cover. "So yes, Isandro. I have imagined this confession a thousand times. And in every version, I hated him perfectly. Clean hate. The kind that makes you feel righteous." She looked up at him, and her eyes in the wall-light were fierce and wet at once. "But sitting here, watching you read it — I can't do it. Because I know what he was. I *was* him."

"Don't—"

"Seven years ago I tried to reforge the fourth anchor alone. Alone, Isandro. Because I was certain I saw truly, just like him. Certain the sacrifice could be minimized, managed, that I could be clever where the old keepers had only been dutiful." She tapped the scarred sigil against the leather, once, twice. "Forty-one keepers. I killed my entire order with certainty. So I don't get to hand you clean hate for Aldous Voss. Neither do you."

"Why not?" It came out ragged. "He's not yours. The shame isn't yours to—"

"Neither is the fury," she said. "You're allowed the fury. You're allowed all of it. But hear me on this one thing, cartographer, because I've walked further down this road than you have: the moment the shame gets big enough, it starts whispering. It says *you owe a debt so large no one could ask you to pay it. It says you're the heir to cowardice, so maybe cowardice is in the blood. It says the only clean ending is for you to die paying for him.*" Her grip tightened on the book. "Sereth will offer you that ending eventually. They always do. The noble sacrifice, the tidy grave. Don't you dare confuse it with justice."

Isandro looked down at the closed book. At the embossed initials. A.V.

"He mapped it all out," he said quietly. "Didn't he? Right down to me. He knew someone of his line would come looking. He left the thread in the blood and the instructions in the stone and a confession so detailed it's practically a *manual*." A breath that was almost a laugh, and had nothing of laughter in it. "Even his repentance is control. Even his apology is architecture."

"Yes," Wren said. "And?"

"And—" Isandro pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. Behind the darkness, the valley rose unbidden: the millpond at dusk, the smell of the print shop, the festival lanterns he'd helped hang only — God, was it really only four nights ago? — four nights ago, while the whole town celebrated a founding that was actually a severance. Every lantern a lie. Every song about the brave founder a hymn to the wound.

"They sang about him," he said. "We sang about him. A hundred years of festivals. There's a *statue*, Wren. I used to eat my lunch under it."

"I know."

"He stole a century from two worlds and my family got to be the *heroes* of it."

"I know." Her hand settled on his shoulder — the scarred one, he noticed, deliberately, the ruined sigil resting warm through his shirt. "So here's your choice, same as his, same as mine, same as everyone who inherits anything: what do you do with a legacy that's rotten at the root?"

Isandro lowered his hands.

The fury was still there. He could feel it banked in his chest like a coal that wouldn't cool, and he suspected it would be there for a very long time — through everything that came next, whatever that was. But underneath it, steadier, something else had kindled. Not comfort. Nothing so gentle. More like a bearing. The fixed point a cartographer needs when every landmark burns: *this is where the lie sits, and therefore this is where the correction goes.*

"We reforge it," he said. "All five anchors. Willingly. No shortcuts, no cleverness, no cutting away the limb — the full sacrifice the council asked for and he refused." He looked at Wren. "Starting with telling the truth everywhere we go. The valley hears the real founding story or I don't lift a finger to save it."

"Agreed."

"And when it's done—" His jaw worked. "When it's done, the valley opens. The veil comes down. Whatever magic wants to flow between the worlds flows, and my town learns to live in the world instead of beside it. That's the price. That's *his* price, paid a century late, with interest."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — small and tired and real.

"Aldous wrote that the thread was in your blood," she said. "He thought he was damning you with it. He never considered that a man who draws maps for a living might look at a broken thing and see not the ruin, but the *route*."

From the doorway, Bramble cleared her throat softly. Pip was stirring against her shoulder, blinking, one hand still curled around her crayon map.

"Sorry," Bramble murmured. "Hate to break up the ancestral reckoning. But the walls have started doing something."

Isandro looked up. The living script that covered the chamber — dimmed since he'd finished the confession — was brightening again, all of it at once, every vein of silver and gold pulsing in slow rhythm. Like a heartbeat. Like something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, with great reluctance, to wake.

Only the blank dais stayed dark.

No — not quite. As he watched, a final line surfaced on the dais's rim, glowing fresh and urgent, in handwriting that was *not* Aldous Voss's:

*HE KNOWS YOU HAVE IT. COME NOW. THE STAIR IS NO LONGER SAFE.*

Ossian's voice drifted down from the watch-post above, tight and low: "Company's coming. Lots of it. And they're not walking like the hollowed ones walk."

Isandro closed the book and held it against his chest, gray leather over his heart, his ancestor's debt and his own resolve pressed together in one armful.

"Then we go out another way," he said, and the walls pulsed once, brightly, as if in answer.
