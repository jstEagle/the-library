## 14. Wren's Broken Sigil

The Underveil gave them a night's mercy, and Wren distrusted it the way she distrusted all gifts.

The founders' archive was a cathedral turned inside out — vaulted stone ribs overhead like the skeleton of some buried god, shelves of glassy black tablets that drank their lantern light instead of reflecting it. Somewhere below them, water dripped a slow count through chambers no one had walked in three hundred years. Isandro had fallen asleep against a shelf of records with Pip curled against his side and a founder's tablet still loose in his fingers, and Bramble had thrown a blanket over the both of them without comment, and Ossian had taken first watch at the stair with his sword across his knees.

Which left Wren awake, because Wren was always awake, sitting on the edge of the camp with her scarred hand open in her lap and the sigil in her palm guttering like a candle in a draft.

It did that more now. She'd noticed it since the drowned cathedral, since Isandro's blood had lit a dead anchor for the first time in living memory. Her sigil — the last working sigil of an order of forty — flickered when he was near, as if it recognized something in him. As if it knew its debt had come due.

She should sleep. Everyone kept telling her to sleep. But there was a thing she'd been carrying since the mountain, since Ossian had said *the reforging needs a bearer's life* and everyone had looked at Isandro like he was already a grave with a pulse, and she'd said nothing, and her silence had felt like the worst kind of lie.

He stirred when she moved. He'd gotten good at waking fast — a week in Veylmoor would do that to anyone. "Wren?"

"Go back to sleep."

"You're doing the brooding-in-the-dark thing." He sat up carefully, disentangling from Pip, who made a small protesting sound and burrowed into his abandoned warmth. "I've seen it before. Chapter four of our acquaintance."

"I don't brood."

"You absolutely brood. You have a whole posture." He came over anyway, quiet-footed on the old stone, and lowered himself beside her at the edge of the firelight. Up close his eyes were tired in a way maps didn't fix. "You've been watching me for two days like you're counting down to something. So either you're planning to kill me — which I doubt, you've had ample opportunity — or there's something you're not saying."

Wren looked at her hand. The sigil pulsed once, sickly, and went still.

"There's something I'm not saying," she admitted.

* * *

She told him about the order first, because the order came first, always. It was how keepers thought — covenant before self, anchors before names.

"There were forty of us," she said. "The Order of the Lattice. We kept the five sigils — not the anchors themselves, but the sigils that let a bearer touch an anchor without burning out. Every bearer in history worked with a keeper. That's how the reforging is supposed to go. Bearer's blood, keeper's craft. Two halves of one act."

"I know. You've said."

"You don't understand what it means that I'm the last." Her voice came out flatter than she wanted. "You think I survived something. An attack, a sickness, bad luck. You think the order *died*, the way things die — all at once, unfairly. It didn't. It died because of me. Slowly. One keeper at a time. And I walked away from every single one of those deaths with this—" she raised her scarred hand, "—still beating in my palm."

Isandro said nothing. He was good at that, she'd learned. He listened like a man reading a map, marking every contour.

"Nine years ago the fourth anchor started failing early. The readings were wrong — or I read them wrong, I've never been sure which is worse. I convinced myself there wasn't time to gather all five bearers. There weren't any bearers left by then anyway, not true ones; the bloodlines had thinned, scattered, gone mundane. Just us. Forty keepers and five dying lights." She laughed, and hated the sound. "So I decided I could do it alone. Me and my sigil. I'd hold the anchor-side myself, channel through the lattice, force the reweave. No bearer needed. The founders' texts said it couldn't be done. I decided the founders were cowards too."

"And it wasn't just you at the anchor."

"It was never just me. That's the whole point of a lattice." Her hand closed over the sigil. "Forty keepers linked, feeding power up the chain to me at the top, and me pushing it into the fourth anchor. If it worked, we'd have proven the covenant could be maintained by will alone. No bloodlines. No heirs. No waiting a century for some child to be born right."

"What happened?"

"The anchor rejected me." Simple words for a simple truth; she made herself say them plainly. "A reforging isn't power, Isandro. It's permission. The covenant has to *agree* to be remade, and it agrees through a bearer — through someone the bloodline acknowledges. I wasn't asking. I was breaking in. And the covenant defended itself the way anything defends itself." She watched the fire. "The backlash ran back down the lattice. All forty links. Thirty-nine people died holding the chain so I wouldn't take the full weight. And I took almost none of it. Just this."

She turned her palm up. In the firelight the sigil looked like a wound that had learned to glow — the clean lines of keeper-craft crossed by a jagged scar of dead silver where the backlash had bitten through and healed wrong.

"Maren died third from the top," she said. "Maren, who taught me my first binding. She held long enough to shout at me to let go, and I didn't let go, because letting go meant admitting I'd killed everyone for nothing, so thirty-eight more people died while I hung on like a fool trying to finish what was already finished. The anchor sealed itself half-mended. That's why it lasted nine years instead of failing outright. My failure bought it time. Every day that anchor holds is a day bought with my order's lives, and it's running out, and when it goes—"

"When it goes," Isandro said quietly, "it takes everything."

"Yes."

* * *

The silence between them filled with dripping water and the far-off creak of settling stone. Wren realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against her knees.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever you're thinking. Don't do the kind thing."

"All right." He was quiet a moment, choosing. "You tried to save the world alone, it went catastrophically wrong, and thirty-nine people paid for your arrogance. That's the shape of it?"

"That's the shape of it."

"It's ugly. It's genuinely ugly, Wren. I'm not going to tell you it wasn't." He pulled his knees up, rested his arms on them. "But here's what I keep snagging on. Nine years ago, the fourth anchor was failing. Not in a century — then. If you'd waited, gathered bearers who didn't exist, done it properly — the anchor dies, the covenant unravels from the fourth link, and everything we've seen out there happens nine years sooner, with nobody ready. Your way killed thirty-nine people. The careful way might have killed everyone."

"That's not—"

"It's arithmetic. Horrible arithmetic, but arithmetic. You chose between disasters and picked the smaller one, and you've spent nine years telling yourself you picked the evil one because guilt doesn't do math." He shrugged. "Cartographer's habit. When two routes both cross the flood, you stop asking which road was virtuous and start asking which one got more people across."

"You weren't there."

"No. And I can't absolve you, and I'm not going to pretend I can. Maren's death belongs to you whether I like it or not." He said it gently, and it landed harder than cruelty would have. "But here's the part you haven't said yet. The actual thing keeping you up."

Wren stared at the fire.

Because there it was. The floor of the pit she'd been circling for nine years, and he'd found it in an evening, this soft-spoken mapmaker who drew realms from dreams.

"Say it for me, if you're so clever."

"A reforging needs a bearer's life," Isandro said. His voice didn't waver, which she was grateful for and resented in equal measure. "That's what the records say. Blood freely given, at the fifth anchor, or the weave won't take. And the only reason there's a bearer at all — the only reason the bloodline survived, the only reason any of this is possible — is that your failed reforging half-mended the fourth anchor and bought the century it took for Aldous Voss's line to produce me." He exhaled slowly. "So in your ledger, my death isn't a tragedy the prophecy demands. It's an invoice. I die to pay for your mistake."

"Yes," Wren said. It came out as barely air. "Yes. That's exactly it. I built the trap you were born into. Every step of this road — the prophecy, the veil taking you, the anchors, all of it — it exists because I couldn't stand to fail quietly. I should have died on that lattice with my order and the world would have found another way, a cleaner way, a way that didn't require dragging a man out of the one safe place in creation to bleed out at an altar. Instead I lived, and my living is a debt, and the currency the debt gets collected in is *you*."

She heard her own voice crack on the last word and despised it.

"So yes," she said. "I watch you sleep. I count days. Because every kindness you show Pip, every joke you trade with Ossian, every map you sketch in the dirt — I'm watching a man walk toward a death I authored, and he doesn't even hate me for it, and that's the cruelest part. You should hate me. It would make this simpler. It would make me simpler."

* * *

For a long moment Isandro didn't answer. Down the chamber, Ossian shifted at his post and settled again. Pip murmured something in her sleep about blue crayons.

Then Isandro reached over and took Wren's scarred hand.

She almost pulled away. Nobody touched the sigil — it was doctrine older than her order, keeper's craft was not to be handled, not to be leaned on, not to be held like something human. But his fingers closed around hers, around the dead-silver scar, and the sigil didn't gutter. It steadied. Like a held breath finally let go.

"Here's my problem with your invoice," he said. "You're charging yourself for a debt in a currency you don't control. Yes — the century exists because your failure half-mended the anchor. Also, the century exists because Aldous Voss severed the fifth anchor and hid his family in a hole rather than face his own work. Also, the century exists because Sereth spent it building an army instead of a solution. Also, it exists because a hedge-witch kept a stolen child fed, and a knight broke his oaths to guard them, and a librarian pressed a forbidden book into a stranger's hands instead of burning it." He ticked them off on his free hand, cartographer's precision. "The prophecy didn't come from you, Wren. It was written before either of us drew breath. You didn't author my death. You've been standing in front of it for nine years trying to take the blow yourself, and tonight's the first time you've even mentioned it."

"Confessing isn't the same as being forgiven."

"No," he agreed. "But it's the door forgiveness walks through, and you just opened it." He squeezed her hand — the scarred one, the ruined one — like it was nothing, like it was just a hand. "So here's my stubborn forgiveness, since you asked. I forgive you for the lattice. I forgive you for surviving. I forgive you for every day you didn't tell me, because I know exactly how heavy a true thing can get when saying it costs someone else their peace." His mouth twisted. "And I forgive you in advance for the next stupid thing you do to protect us, because you absolutely will, and I'd rather be braced for it."

"That's a terrible way to run an order."

"I'm not running an order. I'm running a family. Different rules." He nodded toward the sleeping shapes under the blanket. "Bramble lied to the hedge-witch covens to smuggle Pip across three borders. Ossian burned his oath-scrolls. Pip steals bread and lies about it with total conviction. None of us are clean, Wren. We're just honest about the dirt. That's the whole trick. That's what makes it a family instead of an order — an order needs you pure. A family just needs you present."

Wren found, to her horror, that her eyes were burning. She hadn't cried since Maren. She'd promised herself she never would again — tears were a luxury the last keeper couldn't afford, grief was fuel, feel it later, feel it never.

"If I lose you," she managed, "after everything — after them —"

"You won't lose me."

"Don't promise that. You can't promise that."

"I know." He smiled, tired and crooked. "I'm a mapmaker. I know better than anyone that you can draw the route perfectly and still fall in the river. I'm not promising the outcome. I'm promising I'll be there for all of it — the falling included. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

The sigil in her palm pulsed once, warm, and went quiet — not guttering anymore, but steady, like a coal banking itself for the long night ahead. She looked down at it and felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing grief easing by a single degree. Not healed. Nothing about her would ever heal right. But carried differently. Carried together.

"Thank you," she said, because it was all there was.

"Don't thank me yet." He tipped his head back toward the black shelves above. "Tomorrow we dig through the founders' records and find out if 'freely given' means what the old texts claim — a whole life, or just a whole heart. There's a difference. Founders loved technicalities, and technicalities are how people like us survive."

"And if it means the whole life?"

"Then we keep reading until we find a footnote." He stood, offered her his hand, and hauled her to her feet. "Get some sleep, Keeper. Even lattices rest."

She was actually smiling — an event rare enough that Ossian raised an eyebrow from his post — when the light changed.

Not the lanterns. The far wall of the archive, the one facing the long stair up into the Underveil's throat: it flushed pale gold, then white, then a hard surgical blue, and along its base the ancient seal-glyphs began to shriek — a thin metal sound, stone remembering it was a door.

Ossian was on his feet with his sword drawn before the second breath. "Someone's opening the Underveil from above."

Bramble sat bolt upright, already reaching for her pouches. Pip woke crying without knowing why.

Wren's scarred sigil flared — full alarm, the old order's warning-shape, one she hadn't felt in nine years — and she read its meaning before her mind caught up:

*Not soldiers coming down.*

*Something being pushed ahead of them.*
