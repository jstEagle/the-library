## 15. Sereth's Bargain

They took his hands first.

Not violently — that was the worst of it. Two soldiers in Sereth's livery, silver-grey and sea-glass blue, wrapped Isandro's wrists in padded manacles lined with wool, the way you'd swaddle an infant or handle something breakable and valuable. A cartographer's hands. A covenant-bearer's blood. They had orders about him, clearly, and the orders were careful.

He didn't fight them. There had been a moment, back at the ruined waystation on the road from the second anchor, when fighting had still seemed like a thing he might do. Then there had been six soldiers instead of two, and Ossian face-down in the dirt with a boot between his shoulder blades, and Bramble's hedge-magic sputtering out against a suppression net that smelled like burnt copper, and Pip—

Pip they hadn't found. Wren had gotten her out. Wren had vanished into the treeline with the girl under one arm and her scarred sigil-hand trailing light like a wound that wouldn't close, and Isandro had held onto that image all through the bound march down the mountain road: *Wren got away. Wren got away. Whatever else happens, she got away.*

Now he stood in a chamber at the top of a spire so tall the air tasted thin, and the windows on three sides showed Veylmoor spread out beneath him like a map he'd once drawn in his sleep.

That was the cruelty of it. He recognized the country. He had drawn it, months before he'd ever fallen through the veil — the crooked silver spine of the Arrenwater, the drowned lowlands, the mountain wall with its three broken teeth. He'd thought then that he was inventing it. Now he knew the dreams had been leaking through the failing anchor in his valley, and every line he'd ever put to paper had been a confession he didn't know how to read.

The fourth wall held no window. It held a map.

It covered the stone floor to ceiling — not painted but *inlaid*, threads of luminous sigil-work set into the rock itself, and Isandro's breath caught because he'd seen its like before. In the Underveil. On the founders' great table. But this copy had been altered. Four points blazed along the thread-lines like fixed stars: the drowned cathedral, dim but burning; the mountain anchor, brighter now, freshly taken; a third point far to the south he didn't recognize, dark as a closed eye. And at the center of them all, rendered in cold unlit stone, a small blank valley shaped like a cupped hand.

His home. Rendered as an absence.

"You're staring at the part that matters," said Lady Sereth.

* * *

She was smaller than her reputation. That was the second surprise; the first had been the chamber itself, warm-lit and civilized after days of hollow country and hard road, smelling faintly of ink and lamp oil instead of ash. She wore no armor. Her greying hair was simply braided, her hands unadorned, and if you passed her in Isandro's hometown market she'd have been just another woman of late middle age with tired eyes and a scholar's posture.

But her eyes did the thing that regent's eyes did. They weighed him, continuously, without blinking enough.

"Cartographers understand territory," she said, gesturing at the inlaid map. "I'm told you're the finest in your valley. Show me you understand what you're looking at."

Isandro said nothing. His wrists ached inside their soft shackles.

"Four anchors." She walked the map's edge, her shadow crossing the blazing points. "Two in my possession now. The cathedral sigil-stone recovered from the drowned church — your blood woke it nicely, by all accounts, which is why I've been so eager to meet you. The third will come south within the week. The fourth—" a pause, delicate as a surgeon's pause, "—is currently in the keeping of the last sigil-keeper, who is running, and a burned-out husk of a keeper cannot hold anything forever."

"And the fifth," Isandro said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that.

"Your valley." Sereth stopped beside the blank space in the stone and looked at it the way other people looked at graves. "A century of silence. A century of stolen children pulled through a dying veil because the covenant, starving, grasps for anyone with bearer's blood. A century of this realm coming apart at its seams while that little pocket of the world sat smug and magic-proof and pretended the rot wasn't real." She turned to face him fully. "I have watched towns go hollow, Master Voss. Whole towns. I signed the death-rolls. I wrote to the families. So forgive me if I don't weep for your valley's seclusion."

"It wasn't seclusion," Isandro said. "It was theft. My ancestor built it by breaking the covenant—"

"Yes." Something flickered across her face — not shame. Recognition. "I read Aldous Voss's confession too, or what survives of it. A coward with remarkable craftsmanship. The difference between us, cartographer, is that I intend to fix what he broke. Not preserve it. Fix it."

"And you need me for that."

"I need bearer's blood." She said it plainly, and he gave her credit for the plainness. Every lie so far in his short violent education in Veylmoor had come dressed as comfort. This woman wasn't dressing anything. "Blood alone won't finish it — the reforging must be *willed*, the old texts are very firm on that, which is why I can't simply bleed you into a basin and be done. You must choose it. Freely." Her mouth curved, dry. "You've been traveling with Wren Ashwick. You know how badly this goes when someone tries to force it."

"The third anchor," Isandro said quietly. "Bramble told me what your soldiers' partial reforging did. A town went hollow."

"A mistake made under duress, by frightened people, with half the necessary components." For the first time her voice gained an edge, and beneath the edge, something hot and old. "I don't do half-measures anymore, Master Voss. That's precisely what I'm offering you."

* * *

She fed him first.

That unsettled him more than the manacles. A table laid in a room off the map-chamber — real bread, real salt, roasted river-fish that tasted like the Arrenwater he'd only ever drawn. He ate because his body betrayed him and ate hungrily, and Sereth sat across from him and drank water and watched, patient as erosion.

"Let me show you what victory looks like," she said, when the plates were cleared. "Then let me show you the alternative. And then you choose. That's all I ask. All I've ever asked."

She led him to the window on the eastern side, where the evening had gone violet over the lowlands, and pressed two fingers to the sill. Light ran out from her touch into the stone, and the view changed — not a view anymore but a rendering, sigil-light sketching itself over the real horizon, showing Veylmoor *as it could be.*

"The covenant reforged, whole and strong, with me holding four anchors and your valley holding the fifth — sealed, warded, permanent." The cracked sky in the east knitted itself shut in the light-image, seams of gold closing over wounds. "Magic stops leaking. The veil stabilizes around a sealed heart instead of tearing around a missing one. The hollowed ones — many of them, most of them — wake up. I've seen it in the old records. When the covenant was whole, the hollowing reversed. People came back." Her voice stayed even, but her fingers had whitened on the sill. "My brother has been hollow for eleven years. He holds a broom in the courtyard of my own keep and doesn't know my name."

Isandro looked at the mended sky rendered in light and felt the pull of it in his chest, physical, gravitational. A clean solution. God, but it was beautiful. One act, one reforging, one sealed valley — and everything else healed.

"And my valley," he said. "Sealed forever."

"Protected forever," Sereth corrected. "Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out. No more children pulled through the veil in the night. No more prophecy-books writing your name. Your mother, your neighbors, everyone you grew up with — safe. Genuinely safe, for the first time in a hundred years, because the covenant will draw its strength from a sealed anchor instead of bleeding out through an open wound." She turned to him. "Yes, they'll never leave. Yes, they'll never know magic again beyond what leaks in as dreams. They'll live ordinary lives in a green valley until they die of old age. Compare that"—her gesture took in the window, the real sky, the visible crack in the eastern dark that no amount of light-rendering quite hid—"to what happens if we fail. If the last anchors die. Both realms come down, Master Voss. Yours simply comes down last, and slower, and thinking itself spared."

* * *

He should have refused instantly. He knew that. Some part of him had rehearsed the refusal on the whole march down the mountain — *I would never trade my home, never* — and he waited now for that part to speak.

It didn't speak. Because the picture was good. Because it was, infuriatingly, almost certainly true. He could see the arithmetic as clearly as he saw coastlines: one valley's freedom against a realm's survival, weighted, measured, and found wanting. His family alive and ignorant behind a veil rather than dead in a collapsing world. It was the kind of solution a cartographer loved — clean lines, complete borders, everything in its place.

And that was exactly what terrified him. Because he'd read Aldous Voss's confession in the archives under the world, and Aldous had loved clean lines too. Aldous had done the same arithmetic with the same steady hand and concluded that one family's safety justified severing a realm, and the wound had been bleeding for a hundred years.

"You're doing sums," Sereth said, watching him. It wasn't mockery. If anything, she sounded approving. "Good. Wren Ashwick would have you decide with your heart, and hearts make terrible surveyors. I watched her order try it. Eleven keepers, all heart, no map. Do you know what's left of them? Ask her, sometime, why her sigil is scarred. Ask her whose names are carved on the failed anchor at Harrowmere." She stepped closer. "Decide like your ancestor couldn't, Master Voss. With clear eyes. What is one valley against a world?"

"One valley," Isandro said slowly, "where a girl named Pip drew a crayon map of home from a memory she was stolen out of. Where my mother waters plants that haven't bloomed since the founding and calls it coincidence. Where—" His voice caught. He hadn't expected it to. "Where a hundred years of quiet was paid for by children pulled through a tear in the night, and everyone pretending not to hear."

"Sentiment."

"Facts." He met her eyes. "Your clean solution has the same flaw my ancestor's did. It works perfectly — for the people inside the lines. Everyone outside the borders pays. You're not fixing the covenant, Lady Sereth. You're redrawing it with yourself holding the pen. Wren told me what the reforging requires. Five anchors, held willingly, by five who consent. Your version needs my consent and then locks everyone I love behind glass forever. That's not a covenant. That's a cage with excellent masonry."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the lamplight moved on her tired face, and he understood suddenly that she had heard him. Fully. And remained unmoved — because she had run this argument against better speakers than him, for years, alone, at night, over her brother's empty-eyed courtyards, and she had decided.

"Here is what your conscience can take to bed," she said at last, quietly. "If you refuse me, I will reforge it anyway. Partially. With what I hold and whatever of your blood I can persuade out of you. It may work. It may hollow another town, or ten. But I will not stand in a crumbling tower doing nothing while the sky comes down out of respect for a valley that spent a century pretending the rest of us didn't exist." She straightened. "You have until the third anchor arrives. Choose the world, Isandro Voss. Or choose a pretty map of it. Either way, the reforging happens here, in this spire, under my hand."

She left him at the window with the false mended sky still glowing on the horizon, gold seams over black cracks, beautiful and temporary and wrong.

* * *

They put him in a comfortable room. That was the final insult — a soft bed, a washbasin, a shelf of books, a locked door with wool-wrapped hinges so it closed silently. They were being kind to the instrument. Instruments got maintained.

He sat on the bed and did the thing he always did when the world grew too large: he mapped it. In his head, in the dark, he drew the spire — its stairways glimpsed during the march up, the guard rotations he'd counted out of pure habit, the map-chamber with its four blazing points, the blank valley at the center. He drew the windows and remembered, with a lurch, something a soldier had grumbled on the stairs: the eastern delivery chute ran from the roof to the harbor stores, and it hadn't been swept since the festival of the Ascension.

Festival. Which meant crowds. Which meant carts, and noise, and—

Which meant, possibly, that he was not the only person in this city who had been counting things.

He lay back and stared at the ceiling and let himself, for exactly sixty breaths, want the clean answer. Sealed valley. Mended sky. Mother safe. Pip's crayon map framed on a wall in a world that no longer needed rescuing, and no one asked to burn for it, and the terrible arithmetic finally finished.

Sixty breaths. Then he sat up, because his ancestor had wanted that too, and wanting it was the trap, and the trap was wearing a kind face and offering him bread.

Somewhere below, deep in the spire's stone bones, something began to hum. Low at first — a frequency he felt in his teeth, in the scars on his palms where the cathedral anchor had marked him. The four points on the inlaid map, he'd have sworn it, were burning brighter.

And under the hum, faint and far down through the wool-hinged silence, he heard guards shouting — and one word repeated, running up the stairwells like fire:

*"Breached. The southern gate's breached — she's here—"*

Isandro was already moving.
