## 16. The Third Anchor Burns

Bramble had been a hedge-witch long enough to know the smell of magic about to go wrong.

It was not dramatic, at first. That was the thing nobody warned you about. Bad magic didn't announce itself with thunder. It smelled like copper pennies left in rainwater, like a hearth fire that had eaten something it shouldn't have, like the moment before milk turned. She'd smelled it over her own cauldron a dozen times in her exile years, and every time she'd dumped the batch and started over, because that was what hedge-witches did: they respected the warning.

There was no dumping this batch.

She stood on the ridge above Cinderhollow — a town named for an old forge-fire, which felt, at present, like a bad joke the world was telling itself — and watched Lady Sereth's soldiers ring the third anchor like a noose closing. The anchor sat in the town square, a stone the size of a hay wagon, its sigil carved deep and dark as a dead eye. Around it they'd built scaffolding of pale wood and silver chain, and around *that* they'd cleared the square of everyone who lived there, herding the townsfolk to the edges with pikes and polite voices, as if politeness were a kind of mercy.

Isandro was down there. So was Wren. Both of them in chains that weren't quite chains — sigil-work, Bramble could see it from here, the faint blue shimmer of binding-craft woven into iron. Sereth had taken them two days ago at the river crossing, quick and clean, before Ossian could so much as draw steel. He still hadn't forgiven himself. He stood beside Bramble now on the ridge, armor dulled with ash, saying nothing, which from Ossian was the loudest sound there was.

"They'll do it tonight," he said finally. "Moonrise."

"I know."

"We should go down there."

"And do what?" Bramble kept her eyes on the square. "Fight forty soldiers? Get ourselves hollowed alongside everyone else?" She said it flatly, because she'd learned long ago that anger spoken softly cut deeper than anger shouted. "We wait. We watch. We find the crack in it. There's always a crack."

Ossian's jaw worked. Below them, Pip slept in the hollow of a fallen tree where Bramble had warded her twice over, the child's crayon map clutched against her chest even in sleep. Bramble had checked the wards three times since dusk. She would check them three more.

The moon came up fat and white over the broken sky, and the cracks in it caught the light and bled it sideways, the way they always did, light leaking from the wound in heaven like marrow from a split bone. Bramble had lived under that sky for eleven years. She had stopped seeing it most days. Tonight she saw it clearly, and hated it, and understood suddenly how much of her life here had been a slow surrender to ugliness — learning not to look up.

Down in the square, torches flared. Sereth mounted the scaffold.

* * *

Sereth wore white for the occasion. Of course she did. Bramble had met her kind before — in courts, in covens, in the comfortable houses of people who paid hedge-witches to make their problems disappear without asking how. The ones who believed that if you dressed the ritual cleanly enough, the mess wouldn't count.

Her voice carried up the ridge unnaturally well. Sigil-amplification, probably. Or maybe the whole dying realm just wanted to hear what was going to be done to it.

"People of Cinderhollow," Sereth called. "You have lived under a failing sky. You have watched your fields sour and your children dream of teeth. Tonight, that ends." She turned to the anchor-stone, and even at this distance Bramble could see the hunger in the set of her shoulders. "Tonight we begin the reforging."

"She doesn't have all five," Ossian breathed. "She can't—"

"She thinks she can." Bramble's hands had gone cold. She flexed them, working feeling back into her fingers, into the old burn scars across her knuckles. "That's the trouble with people like her. They think the covenant is a machine. Turn the crank hard enough, get the result you want. It isn't a machine." Her voice dropped. "It's a living thing. And you don't heal a living thing by breaking its leg to make it stand straighter."

Below, Isandro was dragged forward. Even from the ridge Bramble could read his posture — the set of his shoulders, the way he planted his feet. He wasn't struggling. He was *watching*, cataloguing, the mapmaker's habit worn into his bones. He'd been doing it since the day they pulled him out of the veil-torn field, half-dead and full of questions. Even now, chained at the foot of an anchor about to be forced, he was learning everything he could.

Good, Bramble thought fiercely. Learn it. Remember it. Live long enough to use it.

Wren was hauled up beside him, and Bramble's chest tightened. Wren moved wrong these days — slower, careful, favoring her left side where the scarred sigil sat beneath her skin like a brand that had never healed. Since the Underveil, since the confession, since Isandro had taken her ruined hands in his and told her, in that stubborn cartographer's voice, that her mistake did not get to decide what she was worth — since then, Wren had been quieter than ever. But there was something in the quiet now that wasn't despair. It was aim.

Sereth raised her arms. The soldiers knelt. The chains on Isandro and Wren flared blue-white.

"No," Bramble whispered. "No, no, you absolute fool, you don't have the anchors aligned, you don't have willing hands, you're forcing a door that has to be *opened*—"

Sereth began the reforging words.

They were real words. That was the worst part. Bramble recognized fragments — old keeper-cant, the liturgy of the five anchors, the same phrases Wren murmured in her sleep. Sereth had stolen the liturgy the way she stole everything, and she spoke it beautifully, and it was exactly as wrong as a eulogy sung to a wedding march. Every syllable true and every sentence a lie.

The anchor-stone woke.

Not gently. Not the way the drowned cathedral's sigil had sung when Isandro's blood touched it — that low, wondering hum, like a bell remembering itself. This was a scream. Light poured out of the carved sigil, but it was the wrong color, too white, edged in violet, and the scaffolding around it blackened and curled like paper in a flame. The chains holding Isandro and Wren blazed; both of them went rigid, backs arching, and Bramble heard Ossian roar beside her and start down the slope without any plan at all.

She grabbed his arm. "Wait — *wait* — look at the stone!"

He looked. And Bramble watched him understand what she was already understanding, horror arriving in her gut like swallowed ice.

The anchor wasn't taking the power. It was *refusing* it. The sigil flared and guttered and flared again, a heart being shocked, and each time the light surged, it rebounded — off the stone, off the misaligned ritual, off Sereth's beautiful stolen words — and spilled outward into the town.

Into the people.

* * *

Bramble had seen hollowed ones before. Everyone in Veylmoor had. They were the covenant's decay made flesh — people emptied out, their eyes gone matte, their faces smooth as unfired clay, wandering the roads in slow flocks. She'd warded her camps against them for years. She'd once burned a hollowed one off Ossian's back with a fire-spell she still dreamed about.

She had never seen anyone *become* one. Not all at once. Not hundreds at once.

The first was a woman near the well, close to the square's edge. The rebound light washed over her like water over sand, and Bramble watched it soak in — watched the woman's face simply *stop*. Not pain. Worse than pain. The expression drained out of her the way color drains from cloth in bleach-water, and her hands opened, and the basket she was carrying fell, and bread rolled into the street, and the woman stood there among the rolling loaves with eyes like frosted glass.

Then the light pulsed again, wider.

A man. Two children holding hands. An old soldier. A girl selling candles. Ten of them, twenty, the rebound spreading through Cinderhollow street by street like frost through a windowpane, and the screaming started, the *living* screaming, mothers grabbing children and running while the light chased them, and the soldiers broke ranks because soldiers are brave in battle but this was not battle, this was the sky itself reaching down and scooping people empty—

"Go," Bramble snarled, shoving Ossian toward the slope. "Get Isandro. I'll take the town."

"You can't—"

"*I said go!*"

And then she was running, staff in hand, wards spilling off her fingers like sparks off a grindstone, and the hedge-witch who had spent eleven years hiding in the hollow country forgot how to hide.

* * *

Here is what a hedge-witch knows that court mages forget: small magic saves more lives than great magic. A shield that covers one person perfectly beats a barrier that covers a hundred badly. You cannot stop a flood, but you can pull people out of the water one at a time, and if you are fast enough and angry enough, most of them live.

Bramble was fast. Bramble was furious.

She hit the edge of the square at a dead run and threw her strongest warding — not at the anchor, useless, the anchor was beyond anyone now — but in a ring around the fleeing crowd, a dome of woven light that caught the next pulse of rebound and shed it like rain off oiled canvas. The ward cracked down the middle on impact. She rebuilt it while running.

"THIS WAY!" she screamed at the crowd. "TO ME! HANDS ON EACH OTHER, DON'T LET GO!"

They came. Of course they came — terror is the oldest obedience. A miller with his wife over his shoulder. Three sisters in matching shawls. A boy carrying a dog too big for him, both of them crying. Bramble counted heads the way she counted herbs, automatic, ruthless, and shoved them behind her warding one cluster at a time, and every time the anchor screamed and the light rolled out, she paid for the shelter with pieces of herself — her wards burning through her reserves like a fever burning through a body.

She found the woman from the well still standing among her scattered bread. Hollowed. Gone. Bramble put two fingers to the woman's throat anyway, because hedge-witches check, hedge-witches always check, and there was a pulse, slow and vast and terribly calm, like the tide inside a shell.

"Not dead," she breathed. "Oh, thank every god that ever got prayed to wrongly — not dead. *Held.*" Something in the hollowed ones' emptiness had always bothered her, some itch under her craft, and now, with the cause blazing fifty feet away, she finally saw it: they weren't emptied. They were *borrowed from*. Their selves had been siphoned somewhere, drawn toward the failing anchors like water toward a crack in a dam.

Which meant they might be given back.

If the covenant were truly reforged. Willingly. All five anchors.

"Hold on," Bramble told the hollowed woman, gripping her clay-smooth face, looking into those frosted eyes as if the person behind them could hear. "Hold on. We're going to fix this properly, you hear me? None of this half-rotted royal nonsense. Properly."

A soldier stumbled past her, helmet gone, sobbing. Behind him, the anchor's light was changing — guttering now, the violet edges swallowing the white, the ritual collapsing inward. On the scaffold, Sereth stood rigid with her arms still raised, and even from here Bramble could see the dawning horror on that composed face. The reforging was failing. Half-measures, forced hands, stolen words — the covenant was rejecting her the way a body rejects a bad transplant, and the rejection was burning everything nearby.

Ossian reached the scaffold. Bramble saw it in flashes between pulses of light: Ossian cutting through the two guards at the base, climbing, his sword sheared through the binding-chains around Isandro's wrists. Isandro collapsed, caught himself, dragged Wren upright — she was barely conscious, her scarred sigil visible through her sleeve, glowing a sickly gray. Then the three of them were moving, Ossian covering the retreat, and Sereth was shouting orders in a voice cracking at the edges, and the soldiers were too busy surviving their own lady's ritual to pursue.

Bramble gathered her flock — thirty-one souls, she'd counted, thirty-one breathing un-hollowed people — and drove them toward the north road, away from the square, away from the light.

The last pulse came as they reached the town's edge.

It was the biggest yet, the anchor's final convulsion as the ritual died, and it washed over Cinderhollow like a wave over a sandcastle. Bramble's warding shattered. She threw herself backward, wrapped what remained of her craft around the nearest family, and felt the light pass through her own body like cold water through a net — taking something, she'd swear later, taking *something*, a handful of warmth she never got back.

When she could see again, the anchor-stone was dark. Darker than dark — the sigil hadn't merely failed, it had *scarred*, the carvings filled with a blackness that drank the torchlight. And the town —

Bramble walked back into Cinderhollow alone.

The living had fled or hidden. The streets were full of the others. Hundreds of them, standing in doorways and gardens and the middle of the road, still as furniture, faces smooth, breath rising faintly in the cold air. A baker with flour still on his hands. A woman at a spinning wheel, her fingers resting motionless on thread. An entire family at a dinner table visible through a window, four hollowed faces turned toward a meal no one would eat.

An entire town, borrowed from.

Bramble stood in the middle of the silent street with ash settling on her shoulders, and let herself feel all of it — the horror, yes, but underneath the horror the rage, clean and enormous, rage with a direction. At Sereth, who had dressed her ambition in liturgy. At Aldous Voss, dead a century, whose cowardice had rotted the anchors to the point where a fool could convince herself force would work. At every powerful person in two realms who had looked at a breaking covenant and decided their own comfort was worth the interest accruing on the debt.

Half-measures have a body count. Well. Here it was. Standing very still in the moonlight, breathing tide-slow, waiting to be given back.

"Bramble."

Ossian at the town's edge, supporting Wren between himself and Isandro. Wren's face was gray, her lips moving — counting, Bramble realized. Counting the hollowed. Keeper's arithmetic. Isandro stood beside her with his freed wrists raw and bleeding, and he was looking at the town the way he looked at maps, except his face had gone the color of tallow, because he understood what Wren was counting and what it meant.

"The fourth anchor," Wren rasped, when they reached her. "Cinderhollow's people will be drawn to it. All of them. What Sereth borrowed, the failing anchors will hold — unless—" She swayed. "Unless someone holds the fourth anchor *first*. Willingly. Before she can try again."

"Then we get there first," Isandro said.

Bramble looked at him — this soft-handed mapmaker who'd fallen into their lives out of a torn sky, who had every reason to run home to his quiet valley and nail the door shut. His wrists were bleeding. His jaw was set.

"Time's done playing games," she said, and slung her staff across her back, and took the hollowed baker's arm to guide him onto the road, because somebody had to move first and it might as well be her. "Fourth anchor. All of us. And Isandro?"

He looked at her.

"When we get there — we do it *right*, or we don't do it at all. No more half-measures. Swear it."

Behind them, Cinderhollow stood silent under the wounded sky, three hundred borrowed souls waiting in the dark for someone brave enough to give them back.
