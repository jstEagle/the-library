## 17. What We Choose to Carry

They ran through the burning outskirts of Hollowmere while the sky came apart above them.

Isandro had thought the cracks in Veylmoor's sky were permanent — old wounds, healed over into scars the way a tree grows around a nail. He had been wrong. The partial reforging at the third anchor had torn them wider, and now light poured through them in slow, wrong-colored sheets, turning the smoke above the town the green of pond ice. Behind them, Sereth's soldiers were still pulling bodies from the rubble of a neighborhood that had simply *stopped* — four hundred people standing in their kitchens and doorways with nothing behind their eyes but weather.

Bramble carried Pip on her back and did not speak. Ossian brought up the rear with his sword drawn, though nothing followed; even the hollowed ones had fled the backlash, as if whatever emptied them wanted no competition for the vacancy.

Wren's hand was in Isandro's. She had come for him herself — through a wall that should have killed her, her scarred sigil flaring white enough to blind two guards — and she had not let go since. Her palm was hot. Fever-hot. Sigil-hot.

"Left," she rasped, and pulled him down an alley between two leaning houses, and Isandro went, because he had learned in three weeks in this world that Wren's instincts were a better map than any he could draw.

* * *

The safehouse was a miller's cellar two miles out, reached by a culvert that stank of rot and old water. Bramble lit no candles. She worked by the green bleed of the wounded sky through the grate overhead, moving around the little room with the practiced economy of a woman who had set up field hospitals before, laying out bandages, willow bark, a clay pot of something that smelled like pine tar and grief.

Pip fell asleep the moment she sat down, mid-sentence, one hand still fisted in Isandro's sleeve. He pried it loose gently and covered her with his coat. She had drawn a map on the back of his hand earlier that day, in crayon: a wobbly square for the valley, a scribble of mountains, a heart where the schoolhouse stood. He kept looking at it.

Ossian barred the door, then stood with his back against it like a man who intended to be part of the lock.

"Talk," he said.

So Isandro talked.

He told them what Sereth had offered him in her tower room, laid out over wine he hadn't touched: reforge the covenant under her seal, all five anchors bound to her bloodline's authority. Veylmoor saved. The decay stopped. The hollowed ones stabilized — not healed, not returned, but frozen at the edge of the dark instead of falling in.

And the valley sealed forever. Every soul inside it locked behind the magic-proof shell his ancestor had built, cut off from both realms until the sun burned out. His mother. His neighbors. The librarian who had told him to burn the book. A hundred years of festivals, preserved like flies in amber, and none of them ever knowing there had been a choice.

"It's a clean solution," Isandro said. His voice sounded far away to him, flat and careful, the way it got when he inked coastlines. "That's what frightens me about it. Clean things in this world cost somebody everything, and Sereth had already decided who'd pay."

"She always was efficient," Ossian said. There was ash in his voice. He had spent the run past the houses where the hollowed town used to live.

Wren sat on a flour barrel with her hands pressed flat against her sternum, right over the sigil scar Isandro had seen once and never asked about — a branching white mark like frost on glass, running from her collarbone up the side of her neck. In the green light it seemed faintly luminous.

"The reforging can't work the way she wants," she said. "Not cleanly, not dirty. I told your regent that six years ago and she had me stripped and exiled for it." She laughed, short and without humor. "The covenant isn't a chain you forge and hang. It's a *handshake*. Five anchors, five keepers, each holding willingly, each able to let go. That's why it lasted a thousand years. Not because it was strong. Because everyone holding it *chose* to, every day, generation after generation."

"Aldous Voss didn't choose," Isandro said quietly. "He took his hand off the table."

"He took the whole fifth anchor with him," Wren said. "Severed it, buried it, built your valley over the grave so nothing could ever reach it again. And the other four started dying the day he did." She looked up at him, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "Sereth's plan is Aldous's plan with better manners. One hand on everything. It will hold for her lifetime, maybe her children's. Then it fails worse, because a handshake held by force is just a grip, and grips tire."

Silence. The green light shifted through the grate. Somewhere above, a roofail collapsed in soft dust.

"So what does it need?" Bramble asked. She had not looked up from winding bandages, but her hands had gone still. "To reforge it true. Say it plain, Wren. I'm too old for riddles and too tired for hope I can't use."

Wren drew a breath that seemed to cost her.

"Five anchors. Five holders. Willing — truly willing, marked by choice, not just blood. Blood opens the door; choice walks through it. Four we know: drowned cathedral, mountain, the burned one at Hollowmere, and the spire where Sereth keeps her stolen stones." She paused. "And the fifth. The valley. Which means someone has to stand inside the place magic cannot go, carrying a covenant that is made of magic, and hold it open from the side that has spent a century trying to stay shut."

"And that someone," Ossian said slowly, "has to be a Voss."

"That someone has to be *willing*," Wren corrected. "But yes. It has to be Isandro. And here is the rest of the arithmetic, since you asked for it plain." She met each of their eyes in turn. "A keeper channels the covenant through themselves. I have done it once, badly, and it burned forty people I loved and left me like this. A full reforging, with the veil already tearing, will pour through all five of us at once. I don't know that any of us survives it. I know that *I* probably don't — my sigil is scar tissue, and scar doesn't flex." She said it the way she reported distances. "I'm telling you now so nobody learns it in the moment and does something stupid."

Nobody did anything stupid. Isandro noted, distantly, that his own hands were perfectly steady, and wondered when terror had become something that happened to other parts of him.

* * *

He stepped outside near dawn — or what passed for dawn; the cracked sky bled grey rather than brightened — and found Ossian sitting on the culvert bank sharpening a sword that did not need sharpening.

Isandro sat beside him. For a while neither spoke. This was, he had learned, one of the privileges Ossian extended to very few people: silence without obligation.

"Six years ago," Ossian said finally, without looking up from the whetstone, "I broke my oaths because I believed a clean solution too. My commander ordered a village emptied to starve a rebellion. Efficient. Tidy. I put him down instead of putting him to death, and the order I broke did the breaking." He turned the blade. "I've regretted it every day and never once wished I'd obeyed. Both things are true. Nobody told me that about being an adult."

"I keep thinking about my mother," Isandro said. It came out before he could stop it. "She's in the valley. She doesn't know any of this exists. She thinks I'm — I don't even know what she thinks. Dead? Run off? And if I do this, if I stand at the fifth anchor and hold it open, either the valley opens and she wakes up in a world with monsters in its sky — or it goes wrong, and I'm the second Voss to break it, and everyone I grew up with pays for my arithmetic."

"Yes," Ossian said. "All of that." He sheathed the sword at last. "You're waiting for me to say the part where it's fine. There isn't one. There's only the part where you decide which failure you can carry, and then you carry it." He glanced sideways, and for once the knight's granite face was almost gentle. "For what it's worth, cartographer — you've been choosing since the night you didn't burn that book."

* * *

Bramble found him at midday, if the green-grey shifts deserved the name, sitting with Pip's coat-wrapped form asleep against his shoulder. The hedge-witch lowered herself onto the packing crate across from him with a grunt and pressed a bowl of something into his hands. Barley and roots. It was the best thing he had ever tasted, which said more about the last three days than the cooking.

"Eat," she said. Then, when he had: "Wren told you she'll die."

"She told all of us."

"Mm. She left out that I've been brewing against that outcome since the drowned cathedral. Willow, marrowroot, and three things I won't name because you'd worry." Bramble's eyes, deep-set and shrewd, held his. "It might keep her heart beating through the channeling. Might. I've never tested it on a full reforging, because in a thousand years of keepers, apparently nobody was fool enough to try one." She sniffed. "Until you lot."

"You think we're fools."

"I think," Bramble said, "that I was exiled from the Hedge Courts for insisting that healing is a duty owed to strangers. I have been alone a long time, boy, and then I wasn't, and now there's a child asleep on your shoulder who calls me Gran when she thinks I'm not listening." Her voice roughened and she mastered it. "Fools die alone. We are going to die, possibly, together, for something. That's the whole difference, and it's the only one that matters."

Below them, in her sleep, Pip murmured and turned, and her small hand crept out from under the coat and found Isandro's wrist and gripped it.

Later, when she woke, she climbed into his lap without asking, the way children do when they have decided someone belongs to them, and studied his face with grave attention.

"You're scared," she announced.

"Very."

"Me too." She considered. "When I went through the veil I was scared the whole way and nobody held my hand. Did anyone hold your hand?"

"Wren did."

"Good." Pip nodded decisively, as though a debt had been entered in a ledger. "Then it's fair. We hold each other's hands this time. All five." She counted them on her fingers, lost track, started over. "Even Ossian. Especially Ossian, he pretends he doesn't want holding but he does."

Isandro laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, surprised sound — and hugged her, and over the top of her head he saw Wren standing in the cellar doorway watching them, her expression doing something complicated that resolved, finally, into the smallest real smile he had ever seen on her.

* * *

They gathered at dusk around Bramble's single candle, five shadows on a curved stone wall, and Isandro spread the map he had spent the day drawing — not with dreams guiding his hand this time, but with memory and Wren's corrections and Ossian's soldier's knowledge of roads. Veylmoor in charcoal. The four anchor-sites ringed. And beyond the eastern mountains, sketched in lighter strokes, a small square valley that no survey had ever mapped, because it had spent a century refusing to be found.

"Sereth holds three stones," he said. "After tonight she'll know I refused her, so she'll move to force the reforging at the spire — it's the only anchor site she fully controls, and the stolen stones have to be physically joined. We can't let her. Half a reforging hollowed a town. A false one, sealed to her bloodline, would end both worlds slowly instead of quickly, and call it mercy."

"So we take the spire," Ossian said.

"We take the spire," Isandro agreed, and heard how impossible it sounded, and kept going. "Four stones freed. Four of us at four anchors — Wren, Ossian, Bramble, and me. And Pip—"

"I'm coming," Pip said immediately.

"Pip." He crouched to her level. "I need you somewhere safe, holding something too. Can you do that? Hold something for me?"

Her chin jutted. But she thought about it, the way she thought about everything, seriously and completely. "What?"

"Hold the map," he said. "Hold the idea of it. Somebody has to remember what we're making while the rest of us are holding on. That's not the smallest job. It might be the biggest."

Pip looked at him for a long moment, five years old and ancient, and then nodded once, gravely, like a general accepting a commission.

"And the fifth anchor," Wren said quietly. "Say it out loud, Isandro. All of us should hear you choose it."

The candle guttered. Five faces turned toward him, and Isandro understood suddenly that this was the true reforging — not the blood and the light and the tearing sky, but this: five unwanted people in a root cellar deciding, together, that they would rather break than be broken carefully.

"The valley," he said. "My home. I'm going back through the veil. I'm going to stand in the town square during the festival my grandmother helped found, in front of everyone I've ever known, and I'm going to tell them the truth about what our name bought us. And then I'm going to ask them to hold it open with me — not because they owe it, not because a prophecy says so, but because it's the only way anyone keeps a home worth having." His voice didn't shake. He was proud of that, and then ashamed of the pride, and then done with both. "And if it kills me, it kills me knowing I chose it. Which is more than Aldous Voss ever gave anyone."

No one applauded. That wasn't this family's way. Instead Ossian checked his sword-belt. Bramble began counting her pouches aloud, lips moving. Wren crossed the room and stood in front of him, close enough that he could see the frost-white sigil pulsing faintly under her jaw, and took both his hands in her fevered ones.

"When we met," she said, "your blood answered my dying sigil. I thought it meant the prophecy had chosen you." Her thumb moved across his knuckles. "I was wrong. Prophecies don't choose. They just describe the shape of a choice someone hasn't made yet." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and alive. "You made it. Whatever happens at the spire — whatever happens after — I needed you to know that I saw it happen."

Outside, far to the west, a horn sounded from the direction of Sereth's tower — long, low, and answered by another, and another, the sound of an army waking with purpose. The hunt was called. The window was closing.

Ossian rose and unbarred the door, and cold air and green light spilled in together.

"Spire's three days' march," he said. "Two, if we don't sleep."

"Then we don't sleep," said Bramble.

Pip picked up the map and folded it with tremendous care and put it inside her shirt, over her heart, exactly where Isandro had told her to keep it.

They went out together into the wounded dark.
