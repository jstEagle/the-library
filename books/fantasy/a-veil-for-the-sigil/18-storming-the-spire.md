## 18. Storming the Spire

The spire rose out of the ash-plain like a needle driven through the world's hand.

Ossian had seen fortresses before. He'd garrisoned three of them, held one for a winter against the Regent's own banners, and burned another on orders he still couldn't say aloud without tasting copper. He knew how they were built, how they were defended, and how they fell. But he had never seen anything like this. Sereth hadn't raised the spire — she had *grown* it, or rather the anchor-stones had grown it around her ambition, black glass spiraling out of the broken earth in a slow corkscrew, its peak lost in the cracked sky where light bled down like rain through a torn roof.

"It's humming," Pip said. She stood at Ossian's shoulder in the lee of a fallen obelisk, her crayon map folded into her pocket like a talisman. "You can feel it in your teeth. Like when you put a spoon on a bell."

"That's the stones," Wren said. Her voice was flat, which Ossian had learned meant she was afraid. "Three anchors in one place, all of them screaming. They were never meant to touch each other."

"Then we'll introduce them politely," Bramble said, and spat into the ash. "Politely, with fire."

Ossian let them talk. He was watching the spire's base, where the defenses showed themselves the way defenses always did — not in walls, but in patterns. Sereth's soldiers patrolled in a slow orbit, disciplined, well-fed, well-armored. Real soldiers. But between the patrols, at intervals too regular to be chance, something else moved. Figures that didn't walk so much as *drift*, their steps identical, their spacing identical, their heads all tilted at the same slight angle, as if listening to a song only the spire could play.

Hollowed ones. Dozens of them. And Ossian knew, with the certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that one of them would be wearing a face he knew.

"Ossian." Wren touched his arm. "Say the plan back to me."

He said it. "Bramble takes the eastern vents and gives them something to burn. Pip stays with the cart — no argument, Pip, don't look at me like that — and keeps the horses calm. Wren and Isandro go up the pilgrim stair on the north face while the fires pull the garrison east. I take the west approach alone and open the vault door."

"Alone," Isandro said. He'd been quiet all morning, in the way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like the answer to. "The hollowed ones will be at the vault. You said so yourself."

"They'll be wherever the stones are strongest. That's the vault." Ossian rolled his shoulders, felt the old scar tissue pull across his back like a second skin. "And there's one of them I need to meet alone. If it comes to that."

Isandro looked at him for a long moment. The cartographer had changed since the drowned cathedral — since his blood had lit a dead sigil and told every enemy in Veylmoor exactly where he was and exactly what he was worth. He stood differently now. Not taller. *Heavier*, somehow, as if he'd finally accepted the weight of the thing he was carrying.

"You knew her," Isandro said. Not a question.

"Commander Halvard Vane." The name came out of Ossian the way a splinter comes out — slow, and leaving a hole behind it. "She taught me the sword. She taught me what an oath was worth. She taught me that when you swear a thing, you *are* the thing, or you're nothing." He laughed, short and ugly. "Then I broke my oaths, and she didn't. And when the covenant started to rot and the hollowing came for the garrison at Kelder's Watch, she stood her post until there was nothing left inside her to stand. They found her walking the walls. Empty. Still walking her post. She's been walking it for six years, I'd wager, and now Sereth has pointed her at a new one."

"Oaths," Wren said quietly, "are how they catch you. Hollowing doesn't take your strength. It takes everything *else*, and leaves the duty. That's why Sereth loves them. A hollowed soldier never questions, never deserts, never sleeps."

"Then she'll be easy to find," Ossian said. "She'll be the one still standing when everyone else has run."

* * *

Bramble's fires went up on the eastern side like a second dawn, and the garrison's discipline held for almost forty seconds before it broke. That was the thing about real soldiers, Ossian thought as he came down the western slope at a dead run, hood up, blade low: they were trained to respond to the loudest threat. Hollowed ones didn't respond to anything. They stayed at their posts. Which meant the vault approach was guarded by the empty, and the empty didn't burn.

The first one came at him out of the ash-haze without a sound. Ossian took its legs out and kept running; he didn't fight the hollowed if he could help it. There was nothing behind the eyes to defeat. It was like dueling a coat on a hook. But they were fast, and tireless, and there were more of them than he'd counted, and by the time he reached the vault door — a slab of black glass veined with dead silver, the anchor-stones' light pulsing behind it like a heartbeat heard through a wall — his breath was sawing and his left arm was bleeding where something had raked him.

The door was sealed with a sigil. Of course it was. Wren had taught him the three keeper's signs that would unmake it, and his hands were steady enough, and he was reaching for the first one when he felt it.

The stillness behind him.

Not the stillness of the hollowed, which was the stillness of a held breath. This was the stillness of a soldier at rest, weight balanced, blade sheathed, absolutely certain of her ground. He'd stood behind that stillness through ten years of drills and two years of war. His body knew it before his mind caught up, and his body *wept*, some animal part of him relaxing the way it never did anymore, because for one impossible second it believed it was safe.

"Hello, Commander," he said, and turned.

Halvard Vane stood ten paces away in the ruin of her uniform, ash-grey and dust-caked, her sword still sheathed at her hip. Her face was her face. That was the worst of it. The hollowing didn't rot you; it *simplified* you, pared you down to the load-bearing wall of whatever you'd been. And what Halvard Vane had been, at her core, was a soldier standing a post. Her eyes were open and fixed on him and there was nothing in them at all, and Ossian had imagined this meeting a hundred times on the road — imagined her dead, mostly, because dead would have been a mercy — and none of his imaginings had prepared him for the way his chest cracked open at the sight of her.

"You're supposed to be at Kelder's Watch," he said. His voice broke on the second word.

She drew her sword. Smooth. Perfect. The same draw she'd drilled into him ten thousand times, wrist turning at the top like turning a key.

"Right," Ossian whispered. "Of course."

She came at him the way she'd taught him to come at everything: total commitment, no feint, the first blow meant to end it. He caught it on his edge and the shock of it ran down both arms. She was as strong as she'd ever been. Stronger. The hollowed didn't tire, didn't feel pain, didn't hold back out of respect or love or the memory of a boy she'd once called *the best of my oath-sworn*. She pressed him back against the vault door and the blades screamed and her face never changed, and Ossian found himself doing the one thing he'd sworn he would never do in a fight.

He started talking.

"You told me once—" he caught her second cut, turned it, gave ground, "—that an oath wasn't the words. You said the words were just the door. You said the *oath* was what you did every morning after. Get up. Stand the post. Keep the people behind you breathing." Her third cut opened his sleeve and the skin beneath. He didn't feel it. "I believed you. That's why I broke mine, Halvard. Because I finally understood what you'd actually taught me — that the oath was the *people*, and the order was just the words, and when the two came apart I had to choose. You'd have chosen the same. The woman you were would have."

Nothing. No flicker. She drove him sideways, off the door, into the open, and he realized with cold clarity that he was losing. Not because she was better — she might be, hollowed, tireless — but because he couldn't commit. Every cut he threw pulled its punch at the last inch, some part of him still her student, still unable to strike at that face.

"You'd have hated this," he said. He was crying now, and fighting, and both without shame. "You'd have hated being this. A coat on a hook. You told me the hollowing was the only death a soldier should fear, because it takes the post and leaves the corpse standing it. *Your words*, Commander. I've carried them six years like a stone."

She backed him against a shard of black glass taller than he was. He felt it bite his shoulder blades. Her sword came up for the finishing cut — the same one, always the same one, the seventh form, the one she'd said he had a gift for — and something in Ossian went very quiet and very clear.

The seventh form had a flaw. She'd shown it to him herself, years ago, in a training yard in the rain, laughing: *and if your opponent knows the seventh, boy, pray they love you, because there's no recovering from it.* She had never imagined teaching it to someone who would one day stand on the other side of it. She had never imagined the hollowing. She had never imagined any of this, and that was the whole tragedy of Halvard Vane — she'd imagined every enemy except the ones inside the walls.

The cut came down.

Ossian stepped *inside* it. Into the flaw, into the gap she'd shown him with rain running down both their faces, and drove his sword up under her guard and through her chest.

She didn't cry out. Her sword rang on the glass behind him. And then — as the light of the anchor-stones pulsed through the vault door, as if the stones themselves had been waiting for this — her eyes changed.

Not cleared. Nothing so kind. But something moved in them, the way a draft moves a curtain in an empty house, and her free hand came up and gripped his shoulder with a strength that was all hers, and her mouth worked once, twice, and what came out was not words. It was a sound. Almost a sound. The ghost of an order she'd given a hundred times on the walls of Kelder's Watch, and Ossian heard it anyway, heard it in her cadence, her voice, the voice that had made him:

*Stand the post, boy. Keep them breathing.*

Then the hand slipped from his shoulder, and Halvard Vane was gone, and what he held in his arms was only what the hollowing had left: a body at the end of a long, long post.

Ossian knelt there and held her and did not make a sound, because she had taught him that too. A soldier weeps after the post is stood, not during.

When he finally laid her down, he unbuckled her sword and took it. Not as a trophy. As a debt receipt. He laid his own blade beside her instead — the one he'd carried since the day he broke his oaths, the one that had never once felt clean.

"Post's stood, Commander," he said. "I've got it from here."

Then he stood, and turned to the vault door, and made the keeper's signs with steady hands, and the black glass sighed open on a chamber where three anchor-stones hung in a cage of silver light, humming the note Pip had heard in her teeth, the note the whole broken world was singing.

* * *

He was halfway to the stones when the light above him went wrong.

The cage of silver flared white, then guttered, and Ossian felt the hum change pitch — rising, urgent, a bell struck too hard — and from somewhere high on the spire came a sound he felt in his sternum before he heard it: a crack, deep and vast, like the sky itself clearing its throat.

Then Isandro's voice, thin with distance and terror, ringing down the spire's hollow heart:

"Ossian! *Ossian!* The stones — they're not holding Sereth back, she's using them — the fourth anchor's going, Wren's holding it alone, and I need these stones *now* —"

Ossian put Halvard's grief in the place where fear would go, and reached for the cage.
