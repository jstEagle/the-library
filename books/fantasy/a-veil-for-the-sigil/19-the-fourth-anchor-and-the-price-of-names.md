## 19. The Fourth Anchor and the Price of Names

The fourth anchor was a tree.

Not a monument, not a spire, not one of the drowned or buried things the other anchors had become — a living oak so vast its roots had cracked the valley floor into a maze of stone, its crown lost somewhere in the low clouds that never seemed to lift from this part of Veylmoor. The sigil carved into its trunk had been the size of a door once. Now it was a scar of dead wood, black and split, weeping sap that smelled like burnt sugar and old grief.

Wren stood at its base with her scarred palm pressed flat against it and felt nothing at all.

That was the joke of it, she thought. A hundred years of keepers dying to hold these anchors, and hers couldn't even feel the thing it was meant to save.

"You don't have to do this."

Isandro's voice came from behind her, careful in the way he'd learned to be careful with her — not soft, exactly, because softness would have insulted them both, but measured. Like a man walking a riverbank he knew could give way.

She didn't turn around. "Yes, I do."

"Wren—"

"Isandro." She said his name the way you'd set down something heavy. "I've been running the arithmetic since the Underveil. It doesn't change. Four anchors held by blood, one held by choice. Yours is the fifth. Mine is the fourth. There's no version of tomorrow where someone else stands here."

"There's a version where nobody stands here," he said. "There's a version where we run for the coast and let Sereth choke on her own ambition and—"

"And the veil comes down on both worlds like a roof falling in." She turned then, finally, and found him standing at the edge of the root-maze with Pip's hand in his, Bramble behind him with her staff planted like a spear, Ossian watching the treeline with the hollowed commander's sword still slung across his back. Her family. Gods help her, when had that happened? "You've seen what half-measures cost. You stood in that town after the third anchor burned. So have I. I'm done paying interest on my mistakes. I'd rather pay the principal."

Pip tugged Isandro's hand. "What's principal?"

"It's the part you owe first," Wren said, crouching to the girl's level despite everything, because some habits were load-bearing. "And I owe first. That's all this is. Settling up."

Pip studied her with those too-old eyes children get when they've already survived more than they should have. "It'll hurt?"

"Yes."

"Will you come back?"

Wren smiled, and made herself mean it. "Ask me again tomorrow."

* * *

They prepared the way keepers always had — which was to say, badly, quickly, and with too much singing.

Bramble chalked warding circles around the roots while muttering hedge-cant that hadn't been written down in three generations. Ossian drove the commander's sword into the earth at the four compass points and hung it with charms that were half blessing, half apology. Pip sat cross-legged against a root and drew, with the fierce concentration of the very young, a picture of all five of them holding hands inside a circle, because Pip believed drawings could protect people and Wren had decided, sometime in the last few weeks, to believe it too.

Isandro worked beside her in silence, unrolling the map he'd drawn of Veylmoor itself — the real one now, inked from memory and dream and the company's testimony, every anchor marked in gold leaf that caught the grey light like embers. He pinned the fourth anchor's mark with a sliver of bone and looked at her over the parchment.

"When it starts," he said, "you'll want to stop. Everyone wants to stop. The light asks you questions, and every answer it likes best is surrender."

"I know how the light works, cartographer."

"You know how it worked before your sigil was scarred." He didn't flinch when she glared. He never flinched anymore; she'd broken him of it somewhere on the road through the hollow country, and wasn't sure whether to be proud or sorry. "I read the founders' records too. The channel isn't a door you open. It's a river you stand in. And rivers don't care how strong you are. They care how long you last."

"Then pray I'm stubborn."

"I don't pray." He folded the map with the economy of a man who handled precious things daily. "I make contingency plans. So: if the light takes more than you can spare, I pull you out. I don't care what the arithmetic says. I've spent my whole life being told where I belong by books and prophecies and dead ancestors. I'm telling you now — you belong wherever I can reach you. That's the plan."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere above them, the cracked sky groaned like a ship's hull.

"That's a terrible plan," she said.

"I know."

"It ignores every variable."

"I know."

"It's going to get us both killed."

"Probably." He almost smiled. "But it's mine."

* * *

The hollowed ones came at dusk, as they always did, drawn to the anchor like moths to a candle they could no longer remember wanting.

Ossian met them at the treeline and did not draw the commander's sword. He simply stood in their path, arms wide, and spoke names into the fog — the names of soldiers he'd served with, of friends, of the man whose body still walked toward them wearing an empty uniform. Some of the hollowed slowed. One or two stopped entirely, swaying like trees in wind, as if the sound of being remembered was a rope thrown to drowning men. It bought them minutes. Minutes were all anyone had left.

Bramble lit the wards. Pip drew faster.

And Wren put her scarred hand back on the dead sigil and opened herself to the river.

It hit her like the memory of every keeper who had ever died here, all at once, in the first second — and she understood, with terrible clarity, why the order had sung. You sang so you wouldn't scream. The light poured through the scar tissue of her gift and found every place she'd ever been broken and widened it, patiently, thoroughly, the way water finds cracks in stone. She felt her training burn away first: the forms, the disciplines, the beautiful machinery of control she'd spent thirty years building. Then the memories went, selectively, cruelly — no, not cruelly, she corrected herself through gritted teeth, the light wasn't cruel, it was only honest, taking exactly what it needed and nothing she'd have chosen to keep—

The scarred sigil on her palm ignited.

Not white, not gold — a deep bruised violet, the color of a wound healing wrong, and yet it held. Because it was scarred. Because scar tissue is stronger than skin. She laughed, or tried to, and the sound came out as light.

Through the blaze she saw Isandro drop the map and cross the root-maze in four strides, and she wanted to tell him to stay back, the channel would take him too, but her mouth was full of dawn.

He took her anyway.

His arms closed around her from behind, his chest to her back, his hands over her ruined one where it burned against the oak, and the light went through them both — through her scar and his blood, the heir's blood, the covenant's own current finding two channels instead of one and splitting its burden like a river meeting a fork. It should not have worked. Every text she'd ever memorized said it should not have worked. But the texts had been written by people who thought the covenant was about bloodlines, and Isandro had learned in the Underveil what the founders actually knew: it was about sharing weight.

The pain didn't stop. But it stopped being hers alone.

She heard him speaking against her hair — words she couldn't parse at first, then could, then would never forget.

"I've got you. I've got you. Whatever it takes, whatever it burns, I've got you — you're not doing this alone, do you hear me? Not one second of it. You told me once you stopped hoping. Fine. Then hope through me. Borrow mine. I've got enough for both of us, I've been hoarding it since the archive, since the book with my name in it, since the night I realized the map I loved most was the one leading to you—"

"Isandro—" His name tore out of her, half warning, half prayer.

"I know." His arms tightened. "I know. Say it anyway."

The light crested. The dead sigil drank from her like a starving thing drinking from a spring, and she gave it everything — the order, the disgrace, the years of exile, the scar, the shame, the stubborn worthless hope she'd carried like a stone in her pocket for a decade — gave it all freely, because that was the price of names, the old keepers' saying, the price of names is that you spend them —

and the oak answered.

Sap ran gold along the black scar of the carving. Roots shuddered beneath them. Far away, faint as a bell across water, the third anchor — burning, corrupted, Sereth's half-work — stuttered, and dimmed, and steadied into something cleaner, because anchors were not separate things, they had never been separate things, they were fingers of one hand, and one finger straightening taught the others to uncurl.

The violet light guttered.

Went out.

Wren fell.

* * *

She woke to birdsong, which was impossible, because there were no birds left in Veylmoor.

Then she understood: the song was coming from the oak. From the gold-veined sigil, humming like a struck chord, and from the sky overhead — the crack in it, visible even here, narrower than it had been that morning. Narrower. Healing.

Isandro sat against the roots beside her, her burned hand cradled in both of his. Her palm was smooth. Unmarked. The scar was gone — spent, spent utterly, and with it, she knew without looking, the last of her keeper's gift. She reached inward and found silence where the sigil-sense had lived her whole life. A quiet room. An empty house.

She was, at last and completely, nothing but a woman.

She started to laugh, and it came out wrong, and then she was crying instead, and Isandro gathered her in without a word, and she let him, because there was no one left to see the last keeper weep and no reason left to hide it.

"It's gone," she managed. "All of it. I'm just — me."

"Yeah." He pressed his lips to her hair. "Just you. Thank the gods."

"You're supposed to say I'm still useful."

"You were never useful to me. You were necessary." He pulled back enough to look at her, and his eyes were wet too, and she thought absurdly that he had the nerve to look surprised about it. "Wren. Before anything else happens — before the spire, before Sereth, before whatever's waiting — I need you to know it plainly, because plainly is the only way I know how to say anything: I love you. Not the keeper. Not the prophecy's instrument. You. The woman who nearly left me for the hollowed ones and then fed me soup instead. I have loved you since the drowned cathedral, and I intend to go on loving you past the end of this, whichever end it turns out to be."

She looked up at him through the wreckage of her tears and found she had no cleverness left, no armor, no scar to hide behind. Just the truth.

"I love you too," she said. "Freely. Stupidly. At the worst possible time."

"The best possible time," he corrected gently. "We're alive to say it."

Above them the oak hummed its impossible birdsong. Below them the ground trembled — once, twice — a rhythm too regular for any natural thing. Marching. Many boots, many miles away, closing fast.

From the direction of Sereth's spire, a horn sounded. Long. Low. Unmistakable.

Ossian appeared at the edge of the root-maze, sword already in hand, face grim as winter. "Company's coming," he said. "All of it. She knows what you did here. She knows the fourth anchor lives — and she knows the fifth is the only one left."

Wren rose on legs that shook, and found they held.

"Then we'd better get home," she said, "before she does."
