## 20. Through the Veil, Home

The veil did not open so much as admit us.

Wren had warned me it would feel like drowning in reverse — that the crossing would take something from each of us as toll. She was wrong about the taking. What it took was nothing. What it gave, for one endless half-second between worlds, was everything: every map I had ever drawn, every dream of Veylmoor's cracked sky, Pip's crayon valley, my mother's kitchen, Aldous Voss's confession bleeding off the page beneath the world — all of it layered like vellum on vellum until I could not tell which life was the tracing and which the original.

Then grass. Real grass, wet with festival-eve dew, under my palms.

I was kneeling in Miller's Field behind the Harrow farm, a quarter mile from the square where carpenters were still stringing lanterns for tomorrow's centenary. The sky above me was whole. No crack. No wound-light. Just the fat gray clouds of an October evening and the smell of woodsmoke from a hundred chimneys.

Home.

I had crossed back into the valley, and the valley did not know me.

That was the first thing I understood, lying there in the wet grass with Wren groaning beside me and Ossian already on his feet with his sword drawn at nothing. The silence hit me like a wall of glass. In Veylmoor, magic leaked everywhere — it hummed in the stones, sang in the drowned cathedral, whispered through Wren's scarred sigil even now that her gift was spent. Here there was nothing. Not quiet. *Nothing.* A held breath a century long, pressing down on everything that lived here, and every soul in the valley so used to the weight they called it peace.

I had grown up inside this silence and called it comfort. Now it felt like a hand over my mouth.

"Isandro." Wren caught my sleeve. Her face was gray, her lips nearly blue. The fourth anchor had burned through whatever remained of her keeper's gift, and she had paid more than she'd admitted — I could see it in the way her fingers wouldn't quite close around my arm, in the fine tremor running through her like a plucked string that wouldn't still. "How far?"

"Quarter mile to the square. The anchor's under the well-house. My father showed me when I was small — said it was the town's first cistern, older than the founding." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He said it was older than the founding. Nobody ever asked him what he meant."

"Your family knew," Ossian said. He wasn't looking at me. He was scanning the tree line the way he always did, left to right, counting exits. "Aldous Voss's heirs kept the key and told no one."

"My family kept a garden shed over a broken covenant and called it heritage." I got up. My legs shook. Crossing the veil hadn't hurt, but it had *cost* — I felt thinner somehow, like a page copied too many times. "It doesn't matter. What matters is we're here before Sereth."

Bramble came through last, hauling Pip against her side, both of them streaked with ash from the spire's collapse. The child looked around at the ordinary field, the ordinary clouds, the lantern-glow rising over the rooftops, and burst into tears.

"It looks the same," Pip sobbed. "It looks *the same* and nobody knows where I went."

I crouched and gathered her in. Over her head, I looked at Wren, and Wren looked back at me, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: that the night was young, that Sereth had three stolen anchor-stones in her possession and an army at her back and a head start we'd only barely beaten, and that the collapsing veil would not wait politely while a child cried herself out in a hayfield.

"We move," I said gently, untangling Pip's fists from my coat. "And when this is done — however it ends — everyone who was taken gets to come home. That's the whole point, Pip. That's why we ran all this way."

She sniffed hard, wiped her nose on Bramble's apron without apology, and nodded like a soldier twice her age. It broke my heart and steadied it at once.

* * *

We almost made the square unseen.

Almost.

The valley at festival eve should have been loud — fiddlers tuning, bakers working late, children forbidden to sleep. Instead the streets were wrong-quiet, doors shut, shutters closed, and when we slipped down Cooper's Lane toward the well-house I saw why. People weren't hiding from strangers.

They were hiding from the *sky*.

Above the valley rim, where the veil met the world, the air had begun to tear. It wasn't visible exactly — it was visible the way heat shimmer is visible, a wrongness in the light, a suggestion that the dusk beyond the hills was a curtain and someone had hooked a finger in it. And through that wrongness, faintly, came light that wasn't light: Veylmoor's wound-glow, bleeding through the seams.

"They can see it," Wren breathed. "The valley folk. They've never seen magic in their lives and now it's leaking in over the hills like weather."

"The covenant's failing everywhere," I said. "Four anchors dark, the fifth starved. There's nothing holding the shape of either world anymore." I thought of the prophecy book in the archive, the fresh ink that had named me, and the librarian's cracked voice — *burn it, boy* — and understood at last what she'd known that I hadn't. She hadn't been afraid of the book. She'd been afraid of the date arriving.

We reached the well-house. My hands knew the latch in the dark, the way they knew the latch of every door in this town — cartographer's memory, a lifetime of mapping streets I'd walked ten thousand times. Inside, the smell was cold stone and standing water and, beneath it, faintly, something else. Something I had never smelled in this room before but knew instantly from the drowned cathedral and the mountain vault and the Underveil archives.

Old magic, dying. It smelled like a fire two days out.

Ossian pried up the floor stones. Beneath, exactly where a cistern should be, the fifth anchor waited in its cradle of granite: a sigil-stone the size of a loaf, its carved lines gone gray as ash, dead except for one thin thread of light that pulsed like a failing pulse. Failing because it had been starved. Failing because a frightened man a hundred years ago had cut it off from everything it loved and built a valley of forgetting on top of the grave.

My ancestor's cowardice, sitting under my hometown like a buried heart.

"Isandro." Wren's voice was very soft. "You don't have to touch it yet. We wait for the others — the plan needs all five anchors held willingly, and Sereth holds three of the stones. If we light this one alone, she'll feel it, and—"

"She'll come," I finished. "Yes. She was always going to come. The moment we crossed the veil with the knowledge of how to reforge the covenant, she was coming. The only question was whether we'd be ready."

"And are we?"

I looked at the four of them — the disgraced keeper, the oath-breaker knight, the hedge-witch with singed sleeves, the child from the other side of the world — and I wanted to lie. I opened my mouth to say something brave and found instead the truth, which was that I had no idea, that the plan was almost certainly fatal, that I had led these people across a dying world on the strength of a bloodline I'd only learned I had.

"No," I said. "But we're here. And we're together. I've stopped believing there's a better position to fight from."

Bramble snorted. "Cartographers," she said, and started unpacking her herbs onto the well-house floor.

* * *

They came an hour after midnight, and the valley's silence ended all at once, like a struck bell.

Not soldiers first. Light. Three points of it cresting the western ridge in formation — the stolen anchor-stones, carried in iron litters by Sereth's honor guard, burning white and merciless. Under that light the streets of my town appeared stark as engravings, and in the streets moved ranks of Veylmoor steel, hundreds deep, silent as snowfall. Behind them, at the ridge line, a palanquin of black glass, and in it a figure I knew even at this distance, because some people you simply recognize the way you recognize a blade.

Lady Sereth had come in person. Of course she had. The fifth anchor was the last piece of her crown.

"They're forming up in the square," Ossian reported from the doorway, low. "Pikes front, crossbows second rank. They'll take the well-house in ten minutes if we do nothing." He paused. "There are hollowed ones with them. Dozens. Driven ahead like... like cattle. Or shields."

My stomach turned. Hollowed ones — people emptied by the covenant's decay, faces smooth as unmarked paper. Ossian's commander was somewhere in that gray procession, and I watched him swallow his grief and close his visor and become, for perhaps the hundredth time since I'd known him, exactly what an oath-breaker was actually for.

"Bramble?" Wren said.

"Hedge-witch special." Bramble was already pouring something acrid in a ring around the well-house door, muttering words that made the candle flames lean toward her. "Nobody crosses that ring without me knowing what they're made of. Won't stop an army. Will stop an army being *quiet* about it."

"Pip." Wren crouched to the girl's level. "You know the cellars under the bakery? You told me you fell through near there, years ago. I need you to take the townsfolk who come to the door tonight down into those cellars, away from the windows. Can you do that? Can you knock and tell them Wren says run?"

Pip's chin came up. "You said my name right."

"I say everyone's name right. It's a keeper thing." Wren's mouth twitched — the ghost of a smile, rare as rain in the hollow country. "Go with Bramble's apprentice charm in your pocket. It will keep the hollowed ones from seeing you. Run fast, knock loud, come back to me."

Pip threw her arms around Wren's neck, then mine, then — surprisingly — Ossian's armored knee, and was gone into the dark with a hedge-charm glowing faintly in her fist like a firefly.

And then it was the three of us, and the dead stone in the floor, and an army assembling itself in the square outside with the patience of people who had never once been refused.

"Isandro." Wren took both my hands in hers. Her fingers were ice; her eyes were the same steady green-gray they had been the first day, when she'd found a stranger bleeding in the hollow country and nearly left him, and didn't. "When you touch the stone, your blood will answer. It answered at the drowned cathedral with one drop and half a heartbeat of song. This time it will have to hold. It will have to *sing*. And everything in Veylmoor will hear it — Sereth, the hollowed ones, the veil itself. There's no taking it back. Once the fifth anchor wakes, the reforging begins, and the reforging finishes or it kills us."

"I know."

"You crossed a world to get here. You could still stop. Take the townsfolk into the cellars. Let the veil tear, let both worlds end their long slow sickness naturally, and live out your life in the last quiet place anywhere. It's what your ancestor chose. He wasn't a monster. He was just tired, and scared, and he loved his home." Her voice caught. "I would understand. Whatever you choose, I would understand."

I looked at her — at this woman who had burned out her last gift holding the fourth anchor, who believed herself the reason I might die, who stood in a doomed cellar offering me a coward's exit with more tenderness than most people offer a gift — and I finally understood the thing the whole terrible journey had been trying to teach me.

Belonging was not a place you inherited. The valley had raised me, fed me, taught me my letters and my linework, and I loved it the way you love a childhood — completely, and in the past tense. But belonging was *this*: four impossible people in a cellar, betting their lives on a mapmaker because he asked them to. Belonging was chosen, and chosen daily, and it could be carried anywhere — through veils, across hollow countries, home.

"Wren," I said. "When this is done, the valley opens its roads. Both ways. Magic comes back gentle, the way it was meant to flow, and the people here learn to live with it, and the people there learn to live without fearing it. That's the covenant I'm reforging. Not my ancestor's walls. A bridge."

"That's not what Aldous Voss built."

"No. It's better." I knelt at the cradle. The stone's last thread of light pulsed against my shadow, slow as a sleeper's breath. "Hold the door. All of you. Whatever comes through it — hold the door until the singing starts."

Ossian drew his sword. Bramble spat in her palm and pressed it to the doorframe. Wren knelt opposite me across the stone, her scarred sigil bare on her wrist, dark as a brand long healed.

I opened the knife-scar on my palm — the same cut, reopened a dozen times since the drowned cathedral — and pressed my blood to the fifth anchor.

Nothing.

One heartbeat. Two. The silence of the valley bore down on me like deep water, and I thought, wildly, *it's forgotten how to wake, a century of forgetting, I've brought them all this way to a stone that's forgotten how to wake—*

Then my blood remembered.

Light climbed out of the granite like dawn climbing a mountainside — slow, then all at once, gray thread becoming silver vein becoming a blaze that filled the well-house and poured up through the floor stones and out through the walls and into the streets of my hometown, and the fifth anchor *sang*.

It did not sound like the cathedral's drowning bell or the mountain's grinding hymn. It sounded like a valley waking up. Like a hundred chimneys exhaling smoke at once. Like every clock in town striking a hour that had been waiting a century to be struck. The sound went through me and out of me and up through the torn sky, and far away in Veylmoor I felt the other anchors answer — four notes rising to meet a fifth, the broken spine of the covenant straightening vertebra by vertebra.

And everything heard it.

The army in the square froze mid-step. On the western ridge, the black palanquin flared with fury — I felt Sereth's attention land on the well-house like a physical weight, cold and precise and *hungry*. The hollowed ones turned as one, smooth faces tilting toward the light, and began to move. And above the valley rim, the tearing in the air widened with an audible rip, as though the veil, hearing its own name sung after a hundred years, leaned in to listen — and started to come apart.

"Here they come!" Ossian roared from the doorway, sword high, as the hedge-ring ignited blue and the first wave broke against it. "Isandro — whatever happens next — *do not stop singing!*"

I didn't intend to. But as the light poured through me, I saw what the awakened anchor was showing me, what it needed me to see: the reforging required more than my blood. It required willing hands on both sides of the veil. Four anchors held by choice, a fifth by blood — and the joining point, the seam where the new covenant would be stitched, was *here*, in this cellar, in the space between my palm and the stone.

It required the company. It required them to cross.

Through the widening tear in the western sky, I could already see shapes gathering — Veylmoor's exiles, the freed, the hollowed ones wavering between emptiness and the pull of a song that promised them themselves again — and among the distant lights, unmistakable, three anchor-stones burning in Sereth's iron litters, racing down the ridge toward my town.

Everything I loved was converging on one fragile square, and the battle for both worlds was about to begin in the place where I'd learned to draw maps of places I'd never been.

I pressed my bleeding hand harder against the singing stone, felt the covenant gather itself like a lung filling, and shouted into the light:

"*Come through!*"

The veil tore wide.
