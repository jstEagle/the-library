## 2. The Night Without Stars

The moonrise came wrong.

Zephyrine felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her teeth, the same ache she got when a blade in her care wanted something. She was at her desk on the third landing of the Meridian Guild, cataloguing the evening's acquisitions by lamplight: a thunderstorm in cobalt glass, its lightnings still sulking against their confinement; a widow's grief, decanted and stoppered; a name so new it hadn't yet decided whom to belong to. Ordinary work. Safe work. Ten years of safe work, each entry in her small precise hand, each night ending with her alone in the ledger room speaking softly to the sword beneath her cloak, telling it about her day because no one else could ever hear.

She had just uncapped her ink when the ache sharpened into a ringing.

The lamp guttered. Not from wind—the ledger room's air never moved—but as if the flame itself had flinched. Zephyrine set down her pen with the deliberate slowness of someone who had learned that panic was a debt collected with interest. She rose, crossed to the narrow window that overlooked the Meridian's inner courtyard, and looked up out of habit, the way she looked up every night, confirming the stars were where stars belonged.

They were not.

The sky over Vaelharrow was black—not clouded, not misted, but *empty*, a vaulted ceiling of absolute nothing where six hundred recorded constellations should have hung. She counted reflexively, the way she counted everything: the Spear, gone. The Drowned Queen, gone. The slow amber eye of Ilion that sailors steered by, gone, gone, gone. Every star extinguished in the same instant, as if some hand had passed down the length of heaven and pinched them all out between thumb and forefinger.

"No," she said aloud, and then, quieter, in the old tongue that was not meant for human ears: *"No."*

The sword beneath her cloak answered. It shivered against her ribs like a living thing waking badly, and through the bond she did not use, had sworn ten years ago never to use for anything but comfort in empty rooms, she felt its wordless terror pour into her: the wards are falling, the wards are falling, the wards—

At moonrise, exactly at moonrise, the border wards shattered.

She heard it the way one hears a great tree fall miles distant—not sound but a displacement, a hush that swallowed the city whole. The lamplight bent. The rain of dust from the ceiling came down fine as flour. And somewhere far to the north, past Hollowmere, past the salt marches, along the entire seam of the world, something that had held for three hundred years let go all at once, like a knot cut rather than untied.

Zephyrine stood at the window while the Guild erupted below her—shouting, bells, the clatter of apprentices dropping ledgers—and understood, with the strange clarity that arrives only when everything else has been subtracted, that the sky had not merely failed. It had been *opened*. A veil torn is ragged; this was a door flung wide, and through it came no wind, no cold, no sound at all. Just absence, pressing down like deep water.

Her hands were steady. That frightened her most of all. Her hands had been trained by people who believed steadiness was survival, and they did not know yet that the person who trained them had lied.

*Corvane.*

The name arrived without invitation and refused to leave.

* * *

She took the back stairs, the ones reserved for debtors slipping out under cover of dark, and moved through the Guild's chaos unnoticed, which was her talent, her trade, her ten-year project. Invisibility was safety. She had written that sentence into herself so many times it had become a kind of scripture, and she recited it now like a prayer while the halls filled with frightened clerics and the guildmaster's runners shouted orders that made less sense the louder they got.

The sealed vault was two levels down, behind the iron door she had audited four days ago.

Four days ago it had been a routine assignment. *A legacy acquisition,* Corvane had said, laying his ring against the lock, his smile warm as banked coals. *Old paperwork, mostly. You have the best eyes for provenance in the Guild, Zephyrine. Catalogue what you find and tell no one.* And she had gone down with her lamp and her ink and found rows of shelving holding contracts older than the Guild itself—parchment that hummed faintly, vellum that wept when unrolled, one scroll bound in seven chains that had whispered her childhood name as she passed. She had catalogued forty-one items and noted, with mild professional unease, that item thirty-three was missing from its plinth: a contract of unusual size, its cradle shaped for something vast, dust undisturbed except by one recent handprint.

She had told no one. She had told Corvane, briefly, in his office, and he had thanked her and paid her double and looked at her with an expression she had filed away as gratitude and was only now, with the sky bleeding overhead, re-filing as *pity*.

Now the iron door stood open, and the vault breathed darkness at her.

She went in anyway. That was the thing about careful lives, she would think later: they teach you to walk toward the sound of the collapse, because if you don't inventory the damage, someone worse will.

Her lamp found the plinth first. Item thirty-three had been returned—or what remained of it had. The contract lay unrolled across the shelf like a shed skin, and it was dead. Whatever had lived in those clauses was gone, spent, *executed*, the sigils burned brown as autumn leaves, and across the seal-line, in ink still wet enough to shine, ran a signature she knew better than her own.

*C. Corvane, Guildmaster of the Meridian, in good faith and full payment.*

Full payment. The words swam. Zephyrine read them eleven times, because eleven was how many years she'd been running and the number seemed suddenly relevant. Below the signature, in a script that predated the Guild, predated the borders, predated perhaps the concept of *below*, the counterparty had signed with a single glyph that hurt to look at—a spiral drawn inward forever, the mark she recognized from every ward-stone she'd ever touched, the mark of the covenant between realms.

This was the covenant. Or a copy of it; or the original, stolen from whatever reliquary had kept it; or something worse, a *replacement*, and she understood none of it and understood everything: the stars had not fallen. They had been *sold*. The border wards had not failed. They had been surrendered, legally, deliberately, by the man who signed her wages, and whatever he had received in exchange was worth more to him than the sky.

The dead god's parchment stirred under her lamp, though there was no draft. Along its lower margin, half-erased by time, she found a clause fragment in the old swordspeech—the language her blades dreamed in, the language of her extinct order:

*...the last speaker being the final installment...*

Her mouth went dry. *Final installment.* Of what? Payment for what?

Below her feet, the Guild shook. Dust sifted from the vault's ceiling in grey ropes. Somewhere above, Corvane's voice rose over the din, smooth and reassuring, organizing the panic into profitable channels even now—he would be selling evacuation passage before midnight; he had probably already sold it twice.

Zephyrine did the bravest and most foolish thing of her carefully invisible decade. She drew her knife—the little ledger-knife, silver, for trimming quills—and cut a single page free of the dead contract. The margin with the clause. The fragment with her fate written in a dead woman's grammar. She folded it small, tucked it inside her bodice against her heartbeat, and felt the sword beneath her cloak go very still, the way a creature goes still when it scents its own history.

Then the floor gave way.

Not literally—worse. The lamplight swung as the vault door slammed somewhere above, and in its swinging glow she saw, scratched into the plinth's underside where only an auditor kneeling in the dark would ever find it, a message in fresh-cut letters, hurried, human:

*IF YOU ARE READING THIS I AM SORRY. HE BOUGHT US ALL. FIND THE OTHERS. — M.*

M. Merrow. The warden-mason who had audited this vault before her, who had resigned by letter eight months ago and vanished, whose disappearance Corvane had explained with a shrug and the word *coast*. Whose neat hand had annotated half the reference volumes Zephyrine had learned from.

The other others. Find the others.

The vault door groaned open above, and boots began to descend.

* * *

There was a service shaft behind the seventh shelf, because there was always a service shaft; the Meridian Guild had been built by smugglers and it had never quite stopped being one. Zephyrine went up it hand over hand in the dark, lamp doused, breath shallow, while behind her men with lanterns turned her careful audit upside down and Corvane's voice called down the stairwell, silk over steel: "Zephyrine? Are you down there, child? The wards have fallen—we must account for everything."

*Account for everything.* As if the sky were inventory. As if the stars were line items he could write off.

She climbed until her arms burned, emerged through a grate into the coal cellar, and walked out through the delivery yard with a courier's satchel over her shoulder and her face arranged into the bored blankness of a woman going home after a long shift. The gate guards waved her through without looking up. They were staring at the sky.

So was everyone. The streets of Vaelharrow had emptied onto the cobbles, hundreds of faces tilted upward into starless dark, and the sound rising from them was not screaming—it was quieter and more terrible than that, a collective murmur, the noise of an entire city realizing at once that the stories about what lay beyond the veil might be true, and that nothing now stood between them and it but distance.

Zephyrine walked through them unseen, which had been her greatest skill, and for the first time in ten years it felt like cowardice instead of safety.

In the satchel against her hip: one page of a dead god's contract. Against her heart: a clause naming her as the final payment. Beneath her ribs: a sword singing wordless warnings in a language only she could hear, telling her what she already knew.

The floor of her life had not cracked. It had opened entirely, and far below it there was no ground at all—only the old oath, waiting at the bottom of the fall, patient as debt.

She reached her rooms, barred the door, and by candlelight unfolded the stolen page. The swordspeech clause gleamed up at her, and this time, alone, in private, with no one left to hide from, she let the words resolve fully—and saw, beneath the fading ink, the name of the one who had countersigned the covenant three hundred years ago.

It was not a stranger's name.

It was the name her mentor had whispered on her deathbed, the name carved into the pommel of the blade Zephyrine had carried, hidden, silent, for a decade.

She sat down slowly on the floor among her tidy ledgers, in her careful invisible life, and began, very quietly, to laugh—or perhaps to weep; from the outside there was no difference.

By dawn she would need allies, and she knew exactly one place in Vaelharrow where debts could be bought, sold, and forgiven.

By dawn she would need to be at the Debtor's Market.
