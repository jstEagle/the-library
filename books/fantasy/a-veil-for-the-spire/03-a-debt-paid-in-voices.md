## 3. A Debt Paid in Voices

The page she had stolen from the vault was written in ink that remembered being alive.

Zephyrine discovered this in the third watch of the night, hunched over the single candle in her rented room above a chandler's shop that no longer needed candles. When the light shifted, the letters moved—not shimmering, not glowing, but *leaning*, as if straining toward something on the other side of the parchment. She had seen a great many impossible things catalogued in the Meridian Guild's ledgers, but she had never seen writing behave like grief.

She turned the page toward the window, where no stars hung where stars should be.

*COVENANT OF THE VEIL,* read the header, in a hand far older than any living script. Beneath it, columns of terms in a language she did not know—except that some part of her, some place behind her sternum where the oath lived, understood every word.

*Sung, not sworn,* said that voiceless knowledge. *Wards are not built. They are sung, and they must be sung forever, or until someone agrees to sing them instead.*

Her hands began to shake. She set the page down on the table, pressed her palms flat against the wood, and breathed the way her mentor had taught her—in through the ruin, out through the fear—until the shaking stopped and only the cold remained.

On the desk beside the ledger page lay three letters, tied in a ribbon gone soft with age. They had been delivered to her eleven years ago, on the day Master Ondreille died, and she had never opened them. She had told herself it was because grief needed privacy. The truth was simpler and worse: she had known, the way you know a wound is deeper than it looks, that whatever Ondreille had left her would change the shape of her hiding. And hiding had been the only thing she had left.

The stars were gone. Hiding was no longer the point.

She untied the ribbon with fingers that would not quite obey her.

* * *

*My Zephyrine,*

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, and you are angry about it, and you have not spoken to a blade in months, and you believe these three facts are unrelated. Let me disabuse you of the first two, at least: I am dead in the way a candle is finished when it has given all its wax to the flame, which is to say completely, but not unhappily. As for your anger—I earned it. Read on anyway.*

*You were seven when I found you in the wreck of the Kestrel Marches, standing over a broken sword and speaking to it as though it could answer. It could. You knew things at seven that took me thirty years of study to confirm: that blades remember the hands that held them; that a swordspeaker's voice does not command metal but converses with it; and that every conversation has a price.*

*I let you believe the price was small. Words spent on steel, and nothing lost but breath. Forgive me. A teacher's first duty is to make the student love the gift before telling her what the gift will cost, and I was a coward about the telling. You had already lost so much. I wanted you to have one thing that was simply yours.*

*Here is what I should have said:*

*There were once ten thousand swordspeakers. They sang the border into being—not a wall, child, never a wall, for walls invite siege, but a veil, a shared agreement between two realms that each would keep to its own sky. The song is long. Longer than a life. No single voice can hold it, and so the covenant takes payment from each singer: not her life, but a piece of what makes her herself. One gives her memory of home. Another, her face in mirrors. Another, the ability to weep. The old texts call these tithings. The singers called them, more honestly, amputations.*

*Each generation pays. Each generation believes it will be the last, because hope is the only currency that costs nothing going in.*

*You are the last installment. I am sorry. I am so sorry. Keep reading.*

*—O.*

* * *

Zephyrine read the letter four times. On the fourth reading, she noticed that tears had fallen on the parchment, and that the living ink leaned away from them, as if even the covenant's words could not bear to touch an honest grief.

The last installment.

Ten years in the ledgers had taught her to read contracts the way other people read faces—for the clause hidden under the flourish, for the debt disguised as a gift. And now the largest contract in creation resolved itself in her mind with terrible clarity. The wards had not failed because Corvane stole something. The wards had failed because the covenant had come due, and the only collateral remaining—the last swordsinger in either realm—had spent a decade refusing to pay, mistaking silence for safety, mistaking her hiding for a private matter.

Every star in the sky, extinguished, because one woman would not open her letters.

She laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it, and the chandler's stock downstairs seemed to shiver in its bins.

* * *

*My Zephyrine,*

*Second letter. You have thrown the first one across the room by now—or you have burned it, which is more your style, controlled and thorough, grief conducted like a military operation. Burn this one too, if you must. Paper is patient. So am I, wherever I am.*

*Some history, because you were always better with ledgers than with legends:*

*The first singers were volunteers. Do you understand what I am telling you? The veil was not imposed. It was chosen—a treaty sung into permanence by people who had looked into the other realm and decided, freely, that separation was kinder than contact. The price was known. They paid it with open eyes and open mouths, singing as the covenant took from them, note by note, self by self.*

*But choice does not survive its own institutions. Within four generations, the singing was assigned. Within nine, it was inherited. By my grandmother's time, swordspeech was a blood-oath, bound into families before birth, and the tithing was no longer described to those who would pay it. My mother sang her half-century at the western anchor and gave up, in sequence: her dreams, her mother tongue, and finally her name. I found her at the end calling herself only "the west voice," serene and hollowed, and I ran.*

*I ran, Zephyrine. I took the oath's mark and I hid, exactly as you hide now, and I told myself that if no installment sang, the covenant would simply lapse gently, like a lease expiring, like an old law no one enforces.*

*It does not lapse. It accrues. Every year unpaid, the veil thins somewhere else, and something crosses, or nearly crosses, or presses against the membrane between realms like a face against wet cloth. The things in the dark beyond the stars are not the covenant's children. They are what the covenant was made to keep out, and the covenant is the only reason our ancestors' nightmares remained stories.*

*I broke my piece of the song, and I have watched the interest compound for fifty years. You inherited my debt compounded by my cowardice. There is no forgiveness large enough. There is only this: you can do what I could not. You still have your whole voice.*

*Almost all of it. More on that in the last letter. I dread writing it. You will hate me, briefly, and then—I hope—you will understand.*

*—O.*

* * *

The candle had burned to a stub. Dawn refused to arrive; without stars and without moon, the night outside simply deepened into a gray nothing, the city of Meridian lit now entirely by lamplight and panic. She could hear the guild bells ringing wrong hours in the distance, and beneath them, fainter, the sound of people in the streets arguing about whether the darkness was an omen, an eclipse, a war.

Zephyrine sat with both letters and did not burn anything.

She thought about the blade in her trunk—the one she had spoken to on quiet nights, alone, never for coin, never where anyone could hear. She had believed that small defiance was harmless. A conversation here, a question there. But speech was the coin of the realm she belonged to whether she willed it or not, and she had been spending it, drop by drop, on trivialities, while the account itself sat unpaid and the whole sky went into arrears.

And yet—and here was the thing that kept circling back, sharp-beaked, refusing to leave—

Ondreille had said the first singers chose. Had said the covenant began as a treaty, not a chain. Somewhere between the volunteering and the inheritance, between the open-mouthed song and the blood-oath, something had been taken from the arrangement that was never returned: the right to say yes with full knowledge, or no with full consequence. Generations of fear-fueled amendments, each one adding a lock, a penalty, a tithe described in euphemism. The covenant she owed was not the covenant that was founded. It was a creditor wearing its ancestor's face.

She was not certain why that distinction mattered. Only that it did—that somewhere in the gap between *chosen* and *assigned* lay the difference between a debt and a theft, and she had spent her whole life among ledgers, and she knew theft when she read it.

She opened the third letter.

* * *

*My Zephyrine,*

*Last one. My hand is unsteady; forgive the script.*

*When you were nineteen, you asked me why your sword-speech came with headaches, why your ears rang after long conversations with steel, why sometimes words you spoke came out wrong—shifted, syllables rearranged, as though your mouth belonged to a slightly different woman than it had that morning. I told you it was fatigue. It was not.*

*It was the tithe, beginning. The covenant does not wait politely for its due. It nibbles. Your speech is already partly pledged, child—it has been since your blood first carried the mark. Each time you speak to a blade, the covenant takes its percentage: a sliver of your ordinary voice, the one that speaks to people. That is the price of swordspeech, the true one. Not breath. Not time. The human register, spent coin by coin, until a singer reaches the Spire with almost nothing left for anyone but the song.*

*That is how they kept us. That is the amendment no founder ever signed: they made the gift itself the debt collector, so that every exercise of our own nature deepened our obligation. My mother could not have refused to sing even had she run as I did. Her voice would have arrived at the Spire owing everything.*

*Yours will too, in time, unless the veil stays broken—and there is the cruelty, Zeph, there is the trap inside the trap: you cannot simply refuse to pay AND refuse to sing, because the dark beyond the veil does not care about accounting. If the veil falls wholly, both realms lie open, and what waits there devours the willing and unwilling alike.*

*So you have two doors, and only two. Sing the old covenant, and be consumed gently, respectfully, by installments. Or do what no singer has dared in a thousand years: stand at the Spire and renegotiate. Find the original terms. Strip away the amendments. Make the veil again what it was—a choice, renewed, priced fairly, paid knowingly.*

*I do not know if it can be done. I know that you are the last person who could try, because you have spent ten years reading contracts, and the covenant is, in the end, only the oldest contract of all.*

*You will need help. Trust debtors; they understand terms. Trust defectors; they understand fear. Trust no one who offers you an easy escape—I met one such man recently, young, ambitious, frightened in exactly the way I was frightened. His name is Corvane. He asked me, very reasonably, whether the cycle could simply be ended rather than honored. Be careful of him. He is not wrong, which is what makes him dangerous.*

*I am out of wax now, and out of time, and out of apologies. You were the best thing I ever did and the worst thing I ever lied to. Sing loud, if you sing. Sing free.*

*—Ondreille, who owes you everything*

* * *

She folded the letters along their old creases, carefully, as if they were inventory. Outside, the false dawn grayed and failed and grayed again. Somewhere below, the chandler opened his shop out of stubborn habit and stood staring up at the lightless sky.

Zephyrine rose, went to the trunk, and drew out the blade she had not spoken to since moonrise—the night the stars went out. It was plain work, guild-stamped, worth perhaps eight silver marks. She laid it across her palms.

"I'm listening," she whispered.

And the blade answered—not in words, but in the way a plucked string answers a tuning fork, a low resonance that climbed her arms and settled behind her sternum where the oath lived, and she felt, unmistakably, a tithe being taken: something small slipping out of her ordinary voice, a syllable's worth of self, spent.

She also felt, for the first time, the direction the resonance pointed. South and east, through the debtor's markets and the black ledgers, along the thin places where the veil had frayed first. The song needed singers, and the covenant had left its remaining collateral scattered across the city in the form of people who owed debts they could not name.

Corvane would be hunting them already. He had a decade's head start and a signature on a dead god's contract.

Zephyrine wrapped the blade, tucked the letters against her heart, and pulled on her coat. Wary contentment was finished; vertigo had done its work; grief was cooling now into something with edges. She blew out the candle out of old habit, though the room had been dark for hours.

"Then we renegotiate," she told the darkness, in the voice she had left.

The debtor's market opened at dawn. Wherever dawn was. She meant to be there when it wasn't.
