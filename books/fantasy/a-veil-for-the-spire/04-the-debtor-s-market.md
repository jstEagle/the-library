## 4. The Debtor's Market

The Debtor's Market opened at the hour when honest people stopped pretending to be asleep and dishonest ones stopped pretending to be awake.

Zephyrine came down through the Guild's under-stairs, past the counting rooms where bottled storms ticked in their glass like restless hearts, and out into the cistern quarter where the Meridian's shadow pooled thickest. She had dressed for it: no guild colors, no ledger-sash, just a gray traveling cloak and her hair bound back so tightly it ached. A woman hunting allies could not look like a woman with anything worth taking.

The market filled the flooded foundations of an old customs house, torchlight standing on black water in long trembling columns. Stalls floated on rafts; bargains were struck on bridges of rope and plank; and everywhere, quietly, debts changed hands.

She had grown up—professionally speaking—in this economy. The Meridian Guild traded in impossible things, but every impossible thing was collateral for something else. Bottled storms were owed against rain that hadn't fallen yet. Borrowed years were repaid in borrowed deaths. And names not yet earned sat in their little lacquered boxes, humming faintly, waiting for the lives that would justify them.

Zephyrine walked the walkways with one page folded against her ribs, inside her shirt, over her heart, where the ink seemed to warm like a second pulse. The covenant page. Corvane's signature at its foot. The words she still couldn't fully read without her eyes sliding off them like rain off oiled leather.

*Find the others,* her dead mentor's letter had said. *A covenant is not kept by one voice. It never was.*

So she had come to buy voices.

Or rather—to borrow them. She had almost nothing left to pay with. Three years of unspent wages, a signet ring she'd won in a card game she'd cheated at, and one secret she intended to keep until the very last possible moment: that the last swordspeaker in either realm was walking through this market with a blade at her hip that she had not spoken to in six days, and missed it the way you miss a limb.

* * *

Sable found her before she found Sable. That was, Zephyrine would learn later, simply how Sable did everything.

"You're the audit-woman." The thief dropped from a rafter beam onto the plank walkway beside her, light as ash falling. Small, quick-eyed, wrapped in a coat with too many pockets, each one shaped around something stolen. "Corvane's quiet one. The one who counts things nobody wants counted."

"I'm nobody's now."

"Everyone's somebody's down here. The only question is whose." Sable fell into step beside her, matching her pace without appearing to try. "You want the sleep-debtors' row. Third bridge. But I'll save you the walk—you're looking for someone who owes ten years of sleep and can't collect it, because the person holding the debt is dead, and dead men's paper doesn't circulate. That's me. Sable Vess. Ten years of dreams, sitting in escrow, gathering interest I can't touch." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I haven't slept since the moon went out. None of us have. But mine's worse. Mine's *owed*."

"Why would you help me?"

"Because you flinched." Sable said it simply, watching her. "When the stars went dark, everyone in this market did one of three things. They prayed, they priced, or they ran. You—" she tapped the air near Zephyrine's chest, where the folded page hid, "—you put your hand over your heart like something in there had been shot. That's not a merchant's grief. That's a believer's."

"I don't believe in anything."

"Good," said Sable. "Believers negotiate badly. What's the job?"

Zephyrine hesitated. Six days ago she would have lied. But the floor of her careful life had already given way once, and she was learning what it cost to stand on air. "The wards weren't broken by accident. Someone sold the covenant between realms. I intend to find out who, and I intend to make them un-sell it."

"And if it can't be un-sold?"

"Then I'll write a new one."

Sable laughed—a short, sharp sound that scattered a raft of sleeping gulls somewhere above. "With what army? With what authority? With what—"

"With whatever I can gather tonight," Zephyrine said, "starting, if you're finished asking questions, with you."

The thief looked at her for a long moment, torchlight moving on the water below them both. Then she held out her hand, palm up, the old market gesture: *show me your stake.*

Zephyrine put her three years of wages into it—all of it, in guild scrip and one small coin of star-iron that had been her mother's—and watched Sable weigh it, bite nothing, and pocket all of it without ceremony.

"Expensive," Sable said. "But fair. Where next?"

"The warden-mason."

And here Sable's grin finally arrived, arriving crooked and delighted. "Oh, you *are* ambitious. Ilex Ashgrave. The man who built half the border wards and then got caught apologizing to them."

* * *

They found him where the disgraced always end up: repairing things no one would pay for, beautifully, out of spite.

Ilex Ashgrave worked by lamplight on a floating platform at the market's drowned edge, his sleeves rolled over forearms mapped with old ward-scars—the fine white lattice-work that masonry for the veil left burned into the hands that laid it. He was re-pointing the stones of a sunken chapel's archway, fitting mortar into joints with a trowel smaller than a spoon, and even ruined and drunk and exiled, he handled the stone the way other men handled children: gently, and with an expression that suggested he expected to be disappointed.

"Go away," he said, without turning around. "I can hear two sets of feet pretending to be one set."

"Master Ashgrave." Zephyrine stepped onto the platform, and felt it sway, and steadied herself against the archway—and felt, unmistakably, the stone lean *into* her hand, faint as a cat. Old wards remembered kindness. "I need to know how the wards died."

"They didn't die." Now he turned. He was younger than his scars, older than his eyes, and there was mortar dust in his beard. "That's the obscene part. Wards fail. Wards crack, fray, starve. These were *released*. Unwoven from the inside, by someone who knew the grammar better than I ever did. I've spent six days telling anyone who'd listen, and six days being told I'm a drunk traitor making excuses." He looked at her hands, then—sharply, suddenly—at the sword at her hip. His face went through several expressions and settled on something like awe curdled with fear. "You're carrying a spoken blade."

"I'm carrying a sword."

"No one carries a swordspeaker's blade unspoken unless they're afraid of it," Ilex said. "Which means either you're afraid of it, or you're hiding what you are, and either way—" he set down his trowel with great care, "—either way I want to know who sold the covenant too, because whoever unwove my wards used a *voice* to do it, and there is exactly one kind of voice that grammar answers to."

The water lapped. Somewhere behind them, Sable made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"I'll tell you on the road," Zephyrine said.

"There is no road. The border's gone. There's just a hole in the world with refugees falling out of it."

"Then we start at the hole."

Ilex looked at her for a long time. Then he began, methodically, to pack up his tools—each one wiped, wrapped, bedded in straw—and Zephyrine understood that she had just been answered.

"One condition," he said, not looking up. "When we get wherever we're going, and you do whatever it is you're going to do with that sword—Ilex Ashgrave gets to watch. I spent twenty years building walls. I'd like to see, once, before I die, what they were actually *for*."

* * *

They left the market by the eel-catchers' stair, three shadows in single file, and that was when the hunters closed in.

Zephyrine heard them first—not footsteps but the absence of them, a patch of the market's noise gone strangely smooth, like ice over deep water. She caught Sable's arm. Sable, who needed no catching, was already flat against the wall, one hand raised: *three of them. Bridge above. Moving quiet.*

Guild hunters. She knew the make: gray hoods, silver pins shaped like scales, and the particular patience of people paid by the recovery rather than the hour. Corvane had sent them. Of course he had. The moment the vault was found empty and the ledger page missing, she had become an entry in someone else's book—an asset to be recovered, or a liability to be written off.

"Stair's blocked below," Sable breathed. "Two more on the water, poling a barge. They're not chasing us. They're *herding*."

"Then we stop being herdable." Zephyrine drew her sword.

Not to fight. To speak.

Six days of silence broke out of her like a bird out of a closed room. She gave the blade one word only—one thread of the old language, pitched low, meant for the stairway's iron rail and the chapel's patient stones and the barge-pole leaning against the wall:

*Sleep.*

It wasn't a command. It was a suggestion, the way dawn is a suggestion to the sky. The iron rail sighed. The stones settled deeper into their mortar. On the water, the two hunters mid-stroke found their arms heavy as mooring chains, and sat down on their own barge, blinking, already halfway into a nap neither of them would be able to explain.

Above, on the bridge, the third hunter froze—then turned, slowly, and walked away with the dreamy obedience of a man remembering an urgent appointment elsewhere.

Silence again. Real silence, this time.

Zephyrine stood with her sword bare in the lamplight, shaking slightly, hating that she was shaking. She hadn't spoken to steel in front of another living soul in ten years. The secret she had built her whole hidden life around lay open on the platform between them, glowing faintly along the blade like banked coals.

Sable stared. Ilex stared harder, and then—he knelt. Not to her. To the sword, or to the fading echo in the stones, pressing his scarred palm flat against the chapel arch as though taking a pulse.

"That's the first covenant-grammar I've heard since the wards fell," he whispered. "That's *real*. That's the root-tongue. Do you have any idea what you're carrying around doing errands with?"

"Yes," Zephyrine said quietly, sheathing it. "That's why I don't use it."

"Well." Sable swung down from the wall, landed light, and grinned at them both with all the warmth of a drawn knife. "Our leader speaks to weapons, our mason kneels to rocks, and I owe ten years of sleep to a dead man. We are going to die spectacularly, and honestly? After six nights without dreaming, I can't think of a better way to earn it."

They went up the eel-catchers' stair together, three shadows becoming one company, toward the hole in the world.

Behind them, far across the black water, a fourth hunter—who had never slept at all, because sleep was the first thing Corvane had traded away about himself—watched them go, and turned to carry the news home.
