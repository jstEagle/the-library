## 6. The Crossing at Hollowmere

The border at Hollowmere did not look broken. That was the first cruelty of it.

Zephyrine had imagined a wound in the world—a seam of black fire, a scar across the hills, something that announced itself. Instead the old wardline ran through a shallow valley of grey grass and standing stones, and it simply *ended*, the way a sentence ends when the speaker dies mid-word. On one side of the invisible line, the stars were still there, thin and cold but present. On the other side, nothing. Not clouds. Not darkness exactly. An absence so complete that her eyes kept trying to fill it, inventing shapes, then apologizing for them.

"It's like looking up from underwater," Sable said quietly. She stood close enough that Zephyrine could feel the warmth of her shoulder through two layers of wool. "Like the sky is water above us and someone pulled the plug."

"More like looking into a room where the lamp went out," Ilex said. He crouched at the boundary stone, his broad mason's hands flat against the earth as if he could take its pulse. "The wards weren't shattered. I told you. They were *unclenched*. Someone opened their fist." He looked up at Zephyrine, and in the starlight his face was older than it had been three days ago, or perhaps she was only finally seeing it. "Whoever did this knew the old forms. Knew them better than I do."

Zephyrine said nothing. She had the ledger page folded against her heart, Corvane's signature riding there like a splinter she couldn't reach. Three days of walking north with a thief who owed a decade of sleep and a warden-mason who had been stripped of his guild-ring for asking questions, and every mile had made the page heavier.

"We cross tonight," she said. "While the hunters are still arguing about whether we went east."

"And if what's on the other side argues back?" Sable asked. There was no fear in her voice—Sable spent fear like a miser spent coin—but there was care, which was rarer and cost more.

"Then we argue better," Zephyrine said, and drew her sword.

She didn't speak to it. Not yet. But she felt it wake against her palm, the way it always did, a long low attention running up her arm like a struck bell's last vibration. Ten years of silence in the Guild's counting rooms, ten years of speaking to blades only in locked rooms with the shutters drawn—and now here was the border, and here was the oath, humming in her blood like a debt collector at the door.

*One step,* she told herself. *Just one.*

She stepped through.

* * *

Crossing felt like walking through a curtain of cold water that never touched her skin. One heartbeat she stood in the valley under a wounded sky; the next, the stars were gone and the dark pressed against her eyes like wet velvet. Her breath came out silver anyway—some light still lived here, some faint ambient grey that came from everywhere and nowhere, enough to see by, barely.

The grass on this side was wrong. Not dead—wrong. It grew in slow spirals, each blade tipped with a bead of pale luminescence, and when the wind moved through it the whole plain sighed in unison, one vast inhale-exhale, like something sleeping.

"Don't touch the spirals," Ilex murmured. "Resonance fields. The whole landscape is a ward-matrix—old ones, pre-covenant. This realm was warded long before ours learned to sing." His voice had changed since they crossed. Steadier. Hungrier. A mason returned to his cathedral.

They walked for hours by the grey light, following the line of the fallen ward because it was the only road they knew. Zephyrine kept her sword drawn. Twice she almost spoke to it—the words rose in her throat like birds startled from cover—and twice she swallowed them. Not here. Not where anything might be listening.

It was Sable who heard them first.

"Down," she breathed, and pulled Zephyrine into the spiral-grass, and Ilex dropped beside them an instant before the light came.

Not firelight. Not lantern-light. A procession of soft white glows moving along the wardline a half-mile distant, dozens of them, bobbing gently, and beneath the glows—shapes. Tall, hunched, many-limbed or few; her eyes couldn't resolve them, refused to, the way eyes refuse a stair in the dark. Her hand tightened on her sword. Every story she had ever heard about the far side of the veil stood up inside her at once: the devourers, the nameless, the things that ate covenants and drank oaths and left the bones of worlds picked clean.

"They're coming toward the crossing," Ilex whispered. "Toward *us*."

"No," Sable said. Her night-eyes were the best of theirs; she'd told them once, laughing, that a decade of stolen sleep had left her able to see in the dark out of sheer spite. "Look at how they walk. Look at the little ones."

Zephyrine looked. And once she let herself truly see, she couldn't unsee it: the procession moved the way refugees move. Not hunting—*fleeing*. The tall shapes carried the small ones. Some of them limped. One of the glowing lights dragged behind, flickering, held aloft by two figures who kept stopping to adjust their grip on whatever burned inside it. And threaded through the grey air, faint as memory, came sound: not roars, not howls. Weeping. A low, layered weeping, dozens of voices, in no language she knew and every language she had ever grieved in.

Her sword arm lowered without her permission.

"That's a trick," she said, but her voice shook. "That has to be a trick. Lures. The old texts say—"

"The old texts were written by people who never crossed," Ilex said. He had gone very still. "I helped maintain the wards for twenty years, Dravenmoor. Do you know what I fed them? What the covenant asked us to pour into the border-stones every season?" His jaw worked. "Fear. Rendered fear, distilled at the Spire, poured into the matrix so the veil would hold *and so no one would ever want to see what was on the other side*. I thought I was maintaining a wall. I was maintaining a blindfold."

The procession was closer now. Close enough that Zephyrine could see the nearest figure clearly for the first time: tall as two men, its body a weave of shadow and that same pale glow, and where a face should have been there was something like a lantern of folded light—and around the lantern, wrapped like a scarf against cold, a child. A small one of its kind, clinging, its own tiny glow guttering.

The tall one saw them.

It stopped. The whole procession stopped behind it, a tide going suddenly still, and Zephyrine felt the fear come up in her like floodwater—felt her oath answer it, the sword leaping bright in her grip, the old words pressing at her teeth, *speak and be answered, speak and be armed*—

And the tall creature raised its many-jointed hands, slowly, palms open. Empty. The universal grammar of surrender, older than any covenant.

It was afraid of *her*.

"Oh," Zephyrine said. The word fell out of her small and stunned. "Oh, they think *we're* the monsters."

* * *

Later—much later, in the lee of a wardstone that Ilex swore still held a thread of function—she would try to reconstruct how the first contact happened, and fail. Memory would give her fragments: Sable stepping out of the grass with her hands visible and her thief's grin replaced by something gentler; Ilex drawing a ward-shape in the dirt, the oldest courtesy-sign of his guild, *safe ground, safe hands*; the tall lantern-faced one kneeling, folding itself down and down until its glow was level with Zephyrine's eyes, and holding out one long finger on which light pooled like water in a cupped leaf.

In the light's surface, images moved. It was showing her things.

A sky full of stars—not absent ones, *full* ones, constellations she had no names for burning over towers of woven dark. Then the sky cracking. Not cracking—*opening*, torn from within by something that poured through with a hunger she felt in her teeth even secondhand, a hunger for light, for names, for the warm fact of being. Then flight. Thousands of the lantern-folk pouring toward the veil because it was the only door, because beyond it was a realm whose sun still rose, whose people still sang, whose covenant—broken now, broken by Corvane's signature, broken by a frightened man's bargain—had at least once promised shelter.

*We are not the storm,* the light said, in pictures, in pressure, in grief. *We are what the storm is chasing.*

Zephyrine sat down in the spiral grass and put her face in her hands, and the sword lay across her knees, silent, and for the first time in her life she understood that everything she had sworn to hold back might have been holding *out*.

"I can't—" she started, and stopped, because her voice broke on the word *can't*, and Sable's hand found her shoulder, warm and certain.

"You can breathe," Sable said. "Start there. The rest is just ledgers. You're good at ledgers."

Zephyrine laughed—or made a sound adjacent to laughter, wet and cracked—and lifted her head. The lantern-folk had settled around them at a respectful distance, a constellation of patient lights, and among them moved the small ones, curious, and one of those—braver than the rest, children being braver than the rest in every realm—came toddling out of the crowd toward Sable, trailing its glow like a hem.

It reached up toward her with both hands, the way children do, the way that means *carry me* or *look at me* or simply *you*.

Sable bent toward it.

The attack, when it came, came from the dark beyond the camp—from the direction none of them had been watching, because all their watching had turned toward the refugees, toward the people, toward the new thing growing in Zephyrine's chest that felt unbearably like hope. No shape. No warning. Just a reaching cold, a hunger given velocity, the storm itself arriving ahead of everything it chased—and it went past the child, past Ilex, past the kneeling lantern-folk, straight for Zephyrine, for the swordspeaker, for the last voice that could sing a door shut in its face.

She saw it in the last instant. She was too slow. Ten years of ledgers had slowed her, and the oath rose in her throat too late, syllables still forming—

Sable moved.

Not away. *Into.* She stepped between Zephyrine and the cold with the particular economy of a woman who had spent her life reading where value lay, and the cold took her across the ribs and side, and the sound she made was small, and they both went down into the luminous grass together.

What happened after, Zephyrine would remember as light and screaming and Ilex's hands blazing with borrowed wardcraft, and the lantern-folk closing ranks around them like a wall of candles, and the cold recoiling, hissing, unhurried—it had time; it had all the time of the starving. She remembered pressure under her palms, Sable's blood shockingly red, the only true color in that grey country. She remembered Sable's eyes finding hers, unfocused, and the ghost of the grin.

"Told you," Sable whispered. "Debt of sleep. Ten years. Can't... collect on me yet. Bad arithmetic."

"Hold the ledger open," Zephyrine said, and her voice came out in the swordspeech without her choosing it, without her consent, ringing off the wardstones and the standing dark, and every blade of luminous grass for a mile stood up straight and listened. "Hold it open, do you hear me? Nothing collects on anyone tonight."

Far behind them, back across the fallen border, in a tower above a city of ledgers, something felt that voice ring out and turned toward Hollowmere like a compass needle finding north.

Corvane knew where she was now.

And he was coming to make her an offer.
