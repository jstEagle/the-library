## 7. Corvane's Bargain

The night the stars went out, Corvane Ashgrave slept better than he had in thirty years.

He woke before dawn out of habit rather than need, in the narrow room above the Guild's counting floor where he had lived since the fire that took the old guildhall and, with it, most of his patience for ceremony. The ceiling was low. The bed was plain. He had chosen both deliberately, the way a man who has once owned too much chooses to own almost nothing afterward.

He lay still and listened to the Meridian Guild breathe beneath him—the settling of shelves, the tick of bottled weather cooling in their racks, the faint chime of a debt coming due somewhere in the lower vaults. For thirty years this had been the sound of his life: commerce, careful and quiet, in things no other house dared touch.

For thirty years it had also been the sound of a man waiting for someone to come and kill him.

He rose, dressed in the grey wool of a minor functionary, and went down through the counting floor, past the clerks bent over their columns like monks at prayer. None of them looked up. That was the discipline he had built here—attention flowing always downward into the ledgers, never upward toward the man who kept them. Invisibility was not merely Zephyrine's art. It had been his first.

In his private office, the dead god's contract lay on the desk where he had left it, weighted at the corners with river stones because paper that old did not like to lie flat. The vellum had gone the color of old teeth. The signature at the bottom was his own, thirty years younger and written in ink mixed with something that had cost him more than money—he could still remember the taste of it, copper and lavender, the flavor of a thing surrendered.

He did not regret it. He made himself look at the signature every morning so that he would never have the luxury of forgetting what he had done, and so that he would never, in some soft hour, mistake it for something he regretted.

*The covenant between realms is hereby renewed,* the contract said, in the formal hand of an age when even gods wrote like lawyers. *One voice shall be given, whole and willing, from the line of the swordspeakers.*

Whole and willing. He almost laughed. There had been nothing willing about it in four hundred years, and nothing whole since the second amendment.

A knock at the door. Not the clerks' knock—two soft raps, apologetic—but three hard ones, spaced like a heartbeat under strain.

"Enter."

Vess slid in sideways, the way she did everything, a small woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had learned early never to hold anyone's gaze longer than politeness required. She was his best reader of people, which meant she was the only person in the building who ever looked frightened in his presence.

"She's gone," Vess said. "Dravenmoor. Took a page from the vault ledger—the covenant page—and went out through the sump gate. And she's not running blind. She went to the Debtor's Market within the hour. Sable's stall. You know who Sable is?"

"I know everyone," Corvane said mildly. "That's the entire business."

"The thief who sold her sleep. And she met with Ilex. Ilex, sir. The warden-mason they struck off the rolls after Hollowmere." Vess swallowed. "She's gathering them. Like a—a crew."

Corvane turned the contract over on its stones, gently, the way one turns a sleeping child. "Of course she is. What else would she do? She's her mentor's student to the bone." He looked up. "Send the hunters. Quietly. No blades unless she speaks first."

Vess blinked. "Sir—if she reaches the Spire—"

"If she reaches the Spire, we will have a conversation. That is all I have ever wanted with Zephyrine Dravenmoor. A conversation." He smiled, and knew from the way Vess flinched that it was not a comforting smile. "Go."

When she was gone he sat for a long time with his hands flat on the desk, feeling the contract's age come up through the wood like cold from a stone floor.

They would call him a traitor now. They already did, those few who knew—the wardens who had felt the border wards release like a held breath finally let go, the remnant priests of the star-cults who had looked up into a sky wiped clean and understood that someone had signed away the oldest agreement in the world. Traitor. Seller of skies. He had heard worse words in his own head, over thirty years, and none of them had teeth.

Let them say it. Let Zephyrine say it, when the time came, with all the righteous grief of the last swordspeaker confronting the man who unmade her purpose. He would stand and take it. He had earned the taking of it.

But she would listen first. That was the thing they all misunderstood about her—that they had always misunderstood about swordspeakers, mistaking the gift for a weapon when it was closer to a wound. Zephyrine listened. It was why he had hired her ten years ago, pulling her out of a debtor's queue with a forged reference and a straight face, because he had known her name the moment he saw it on the intake roll and had needed, desperately, to know what she was becoming before the world found out.

*Dravenmoor.* The last of the line. The final installment on a debt four centuries old.

He had watched her for a decade, cataloguing storms and borrowed years, speaking to her blades only when she thought herself alone—and he had seen what she could not: that she was happy. Wary, guarded, half-starved for company, but happy, in the way of a woman who believes she has slipped the noose. Every year she did not speak the covenant was a year the oath stayed dormant, patient as interest accruing. Every year was stolen. He had wanted her to steal decades.

And then the wards had begun to fail on their own—not broken, *released*, from the far side, by hands that had decided the bargain was rotten—and the choice he had put off for thirty years came due all at once, the way choices do.

So he had paid. One signature. One renewal cancelled before it could be collected.

* * *

His sister's name had been Maren.

No one living used it. The wardens' histories recorded a swordspeaker called Maren Ashgrave, Voice of the Eastern Reach, who sang the autumn wards in the year of the Long Frost and came down from the Spire silent, smiling, and empty, and lived another eleven years in a chair by a window, being fed, being dressed, being loved by a brother who was fourteen years old when it started and twenty-five when it ended.

Corvane had been the one who fed her. Their parents were dead by then—ward-work took families the way plagues took villages, quietly and without pattern—and so it fell to a boy to carry porridge up the stairs to a woman who had once sung mountains into obedience and now could not remember how spoons worked.

He had talked to her anyway. Every day. About the weather, about the neighbors, about the books he was reading, because the healers said voices might reach her even if hers never came back. And sometimes—this was the part he had never told anyone, the part that had decided the shape of his entire life—sometimes her eyes would fix on him with something behind them, deep down, like a light at the bottom of a well, and her mouth would work, and no sound would come, and he would understand that she was trying to tell him something. A warning, maybe. Or just her name. Or *run.*

She died in winter, mid-sentence, or what would have been a sentence. Her last expression was frustration. He had closed her eyes and thought: *I will end this.* He was twenty-five and had no money and no power and no plan, but grief is a fine architect. It builds whatever you ask it to.

It took him fifteen years to claw his way into the Meridian Guild, another ten to take it, and five more to find what he needed: the original covenant, the dead god's copy, the one written before the amendments. He read it in a sealed vault by candlelight, alone, and discovered what the wardens' histories had carefully omitted.

The first covenant had asked for a voice. One voice, freely given, to sing the veil into being—and then it was done. Finished. A treaty signed once, honored forever. It was the *amendments* that had turned it into a harvest. Four hundred years of frightened men, each generation adding clauses: renewal upon renewal, payment upon payment, each swordspeaker giving more than the last because the last war or the last omen or the last frightened council had demanded stronger walls. Fear, amended into law. Greed, notarized. By Maren's time the covenant didn't want a voice—it wanted the whole singer, mind and memory and self, poured out like wine until the cup stood empty.

And the realm beyond the veil had agreed to all of it, because the realm beyond the veil had never been asked what *it* thought the bargain was.

Corvane had sat in that vault until the candles drowned, and understood two things with perfect clarity. First: that the covenant was a machine for eating people, and that it would eat Zephyrine Dravenmoor the moment the wards next failed, and that no one would stop it, because stopping it would require courage rather than fear and the world had run short of that centuries ago.

Second: that he could buy his way out. The Guild traded in impossible things. Somewhere in its ledgers was a price for everything, including the survival of the last swordspeaker—including his own. All he had to do was decide whose survival mattered.

He had spent thirty years deciding. He had decided, in the end, on everyone's. Just not honestly. Not openly. Because openness required trust, and trust required faith in institutions, and he had watched institutions feed his sister to a god one amendment at a time.

So: the signature. The released wards. The dark sky. A crime committed in the name of mercy, which was, he suspected, the only kind of crime worth committing.

* * *

They brought word of her at dusk: the Debtor's Market, the thief, the disgraced mason, and now hunters reporting movement toward Hollowmere, toward the crossing, toward the Spire itself. Toward the place where the covenant waited, patient as a hook, for the last mouth that could speak it.

Corvane stood at his window and watched the starless sky come down over the city like a lid. People in the streets below had begun leaving lamps burning all night. He approved. Light you choose yourself is worth more than light you're given.

"You'll hate me," he said to the darkness, to the girl he'd hired with a forged letter, to the sister whose face she had never worn but whose silence she carried in her throat like a coin she hadn't yet had to spend. "You should. But hear me first. That's all I ask. Hear me, and then choose—and if you choose the covenant, if you climb the Spire and open your mouth and give them everything—" His hand pressed flat against the cold glass. "Then I will spend everything I have left buying back what I can of you. Even if what's left isn't enough to be called you anymore."

The hunters were closing. The Spire was waiting. And somewhere on the road to Hollowmere, Zephyrine Dravenmoor was walking toward the same door he had locked thirty years ago, believing it opened onto duty, never suspecting it opened onto a table where the price had always been negotiable—and that the man who'd sold the sky would give anything, anything at all, to teach her that before she paid.

He took the dead god's contract off its stones, folded it into his coat, over his heart, and went down to meet her himself.
