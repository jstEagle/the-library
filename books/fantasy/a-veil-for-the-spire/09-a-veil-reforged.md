## 9. A Veil Reforged

The Spire had no door. It had never needed one, because nothing was meant to come back.

Zephyrine stood at its base with the wind of two realms pulling at her coat—one breath warm and starless from beyond the border, one cold and thin from the sky above, which still held nothing but a black so complete it felt like standing inside a closed eye. Behind her, the road up the mountain was littered with the signs of their passage: Ilex's mason-chalk marks glowing faintly on the stones, Sable's footprints in the frost, and further back, the bodies of hunters who had chosen, in the last hour, to stop being hunters.

Three of them had defected at Hollowmere. Two more at the river. The last had laid down her crossbow at the mountain's foot and said, in a voice cracked with ten years of orders, *Tell me what to do instead.* Zephyrine had no rank to give her. She had only a page torn from a ledger, a covenant half-deciphered, and a voice she had been hoarding like a miser for a decade, spending it only on steel in empty rooms.

It would have to be enough.

"The threshold is here," Ilex said. He knelt at the Spire's root, where the stone met the mountain, and pressed his palm flat against it. His fingers were black to the second knuckle with chalk dust; his eyes were wet, though he would have denied it under oath. "The old ward-anchors run down beneath us like roots. Your mentor's letters were right—the covenant doesn't live in the sky. It lives *here*. They built the Spire on top of it so no one would ever think to look down."

"Corvane knew," Zephyrine said.

"Corvane has always known. That's the worst of him." Ilex looked up at her, and in the starless dark his face was all shadow and chalk. "The original wording, Zephyrine. The one before the amendments. Do you have it?"

She touched the page in her breast pocket. Her mentor's handwriting, the last gift of a woman who had paid for the old covenant with her voice and then her mind, and had still found the clarity, in some lucid hour before the end, to copy out what the first swordspeakers had actually sung. Not a wall. Zephyrine had read it forty times in the last three days and it still undid her a little each time. Not a wall. An *agreement*.

"I have it," she said.

"And you understand what it will cost."

She understood. That was the cruelty of it—the old covenant had demanded a voice entire, but the original asked only for a *choice made aloud, irrevocable, in the hearing of both realms*. The amendments had grown fat and frightened over the centuries, each generation of wardens adding clauses, each clause demanding more of the singer, until the price was everything and the singers were spent women in towers. Strip the fear away, and what remained was almost gentle. Almost.

Almost.

"Zephy." Sable's voice, low, at her shoulder. Zephyrine had not heard her approach, which was usual; Sable moved like a debt coming due. She was still favoring her left side, the wound from Hollowmere bound in Ilex's chalk-salve and Zephyrine's shirt, torn into strips. She should have been lying down. She had refused, in the particular flat tone that meant the refusal was not open to appeal. "Say it again. What you're going to do. I want to hear it in your voice, not in your silences."

Zephyrine turned to face her. Around them, the defectors were setting the last of Ilex's anchors—seven stones in a ring, each carved with a word in the old ward-script: *witness, threshold, guest, host, return, mercy, dawn.* Seven words. Seven singers, if you counted the mountain itself, and Ilex had insisted they did.

"I'm going to sing the covenant as it was first written," Zephyrine said. "A treaty, not a prison. The veil stays—but as a door with a keeper, not a wall with a corpse behind it. Both realms get to see each other. Both get to say no. And no one—*" her voice caught, and she let it, because Sable had asked for her voice and she would not hand over a polished lie instead* "—no one pays with their whole self ever again."

"And you?"

"I pay with my swordspeech. All of it. Every blade I've ever spoken to goes quiet, and I never sing to steel again." She had rehearsed this part and it still came out ragged. "It's the only thing I have that the covenant recognizes as mine. My name belongs to my father's line. My debts belong to the Guild. But the songs—" she touched her throat, "—the songs are mine. So that's the coin."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Then she did the thing she only did when she was terrified and had decided to be terrified anyway: she smiled, crooked and sharp, and took Zephyrine's hand.

"Then I'm standing inside the circle," she said. "I owe a decade of sleep and I've decided this is how I'm spending one night of it."

* * *

Corvane came at the moment the anchors lit.

Of course he did. Zephyrine felt him before she saw him—the particular pressure of a man who had spent thirty years making himself necessary, arriving at the exact hour his necessity was about to expire. He came up the mountain road alone, no hunters, no guards, his coat unbuttoned and his hands empty, and for the first time since she had known him he looked *old*. Not frail. Old the way mountains are old, the way the Spire was old, worn down to the load-bearing truth of himself.

"Zephyrine." Her name in his mouth had always sounded like an entry in a ledger. Tonight it sounded almost like an apology. Almost.

"Guildmaster."

"Former," he said. "The Guild voted at dawn. It seems selling the sky is finally the sort of scandal that outranks profit." He stopped at the edge of the anchor-ring, and his eyes moved over it—the stones, the defectors, Sable's hand still in Zephyrine's—and something in his face shifted, some weight set down after a long carry. "You found the original wording."

"My mentor kept it."

"I know. I looked for it for twenty years." He laughed, short and hollow. "She hid it better than I hid my sister's name."

The wind from the starless realm gusted through the ring, and in it Zephyrine heard—not heard, *felt*—the refugees of Hollowmere, the ones who had followed them up the mountain at a respectful distance, waiting to learn whether the door would open or be sealed against them forever. They were not monsters. They had never been monsters. They were a people whose own sky had been eaten by something worse, who had pressed against the veil for generations like faces at a window, and the wardens of Zephyrine's world had answered with amendments and fear and, at the last, with Corvane's signature on a dead god's contract.

"You could stop this," Corvane said. "One word from you and I'll help you seal it forever. A wall. No door, no treaty, no more installments. Your voice stays yours. You could go back to your ledgers."

"I could," Zephyrine said. "And in a hundred years some frightened child with a gift like mine gets chained to that wall, and pays, and pays, and pays. My mentor paid. Your sister paid." She stepped closer to him, inside arm's reach, and saw his jaw tighten. "You didn't sell the covenant to save yourself, Corvane. I've read your history now, all of it. You sold it to stop the paying. You just couldn't bear to do it *with* people instead of *to* them."

For a moment he looked at her the way a man looks at a mirror he has been avoiding for decades.

"The threshold has to be held while the covenant sets," Ilex said quietly, from the ring's edge. "The old texts are clear. Someone has to stand at the door and *mean* it—mean both sides, mean the opening and the guarding—until the last anchor binds. Hours, maybe. And whatever is chasing the refugees will feel the door open, and it will push."

"Then it needs someone with nothing left to lose," Corvane said, "and a great deal to answer for." He rolled up his sleeves, an old man's deliberate motion, and Zephyrine understood that he had been walking up this mountain to this exact sentence since the night he signed the contract. "I held the door shut for thirty years, warden-mason. Let me hold it open once."

* * *

She sang at moonrise, because the old covenant specified moonrise, and because the moon was the only thing left in the sky and it seemed right that the sky should attend.

Seven anchors. Seven voices. Ilex spoke the words of *witness* in his mason's cadence, and the stone drank them. The defector with the cracked voice took *mercy*, and wept as she sang it, and the weeping made it stronger. Sable, who had no gift for anything but taking, sang *return* in a voice Zephyrine had never heard before—rough, untrained, and utterly unashamed—and Zephyrine felt the anchor catch like a hook set in the world's own flesh.

And Zephyrine sang *threshold*. The original words. The agreement. *Let there be a veil, and let the veil be a door, and let the door have keepers who choose it, and let the choosing be renewed each generation, freely, or let the veil fall and both realms face together what comes.* Her swordspeech rose in her like a tide, every blade she had ever woken singing back to her one last time from the armories and the graves and the museum-cases of the world, and she gave it all—not as payment extracted, but as a coin spent *on purpose*, which was, she understood at last, the entire difference between an oath and a chain.

The Spire lit from the roots up. The sky split like an eye opening.

And the threshold pushed back.

It came up the mountain road as a cold that was not cold, a hunger that had eaten a whole realm's stars, and it found Corvane standing in the doorway with his arms spread and his thirty years of guilt worn like armor. Zephyrine saw him take its weight. She saw him *mean* it—mean the opening and the guarding, both sides at once, the penance and the welcome—and she sang louder, and the anchors blazed, and Corvane held.

He held for an hour. He held while the veil set like glass cooling, while the refugees of Hollowmere passed through weeping and were met by defectors with open hands, while Ilex carved the final word—*dawn*—into the last stone with fingers that bled. He held until the covenant bound, and then he held a little longer, because that was who he had always been underneath everything.

When the last anchor sealed, the sky began to return. Not all at once. One star, low in the east, like a coin placed carefully on black velvet. Then another. Then a scatter of them, then a river of them, the Milky Way pouring back into the world like a debt repaid across centuries.

Corvane did not see it. He was kneeling at the threshold, and the threshold had closed gently behind him, the way a door closes when the last guest has come in from the cold.

Zephyrine stood in the returned starlight, voiceless of steel, and found she was not grieving. She opened her mouth to say something to Sable—something the covenant had not taken, something plain and human—and discovered that plain and human was the one language she had never practiced.

So instead, she took out the ledger page, turned it over, and began to write.

* * *

They came down from the mountain at first light, seven singers and a growing company, and the stars were still there—every one of them, as if the sky were afraid to lose the habit. At the foot of the mountain, on the Guild road, Zephyrine saw the thing that would shape everything after: a line of figures waiting at the border of her world's first true morning. Refugees from beyond the veil, and wardens without a wall to keep, and a merchant with a cart full of bottled storms, all of them looking up at her with the same question in their faces.

*What now?*

Zephyrine Dravenmoor, last of the swordspeakers, first of whatever came next, took out her ledger, opened it to a clean page, and wrote the Guild's new motto at the top in a steady hand:

*All debts negotiable. All doors kept. All voices heard.*
