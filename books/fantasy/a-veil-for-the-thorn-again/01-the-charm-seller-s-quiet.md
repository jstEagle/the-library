## 1. The Charm-Seller's Quiet

The bells of Corvale rang seven times before Hazel Dunmore opened his shutters, and he waited for the last echo to die before he touched a single charm.

This was not superstition. It was arithmetic.

Seven bells meant the night-watch had gone off duty and the day-watch had not yet finished their bread in the guardhouse on Tanner's Row. There was a gap, three or four minutes wide, when no one official stood beneath his window with nothing better to do than count what a charm-seller carried downstairs. Hazel had learned the width of that gap the way other men learned their own hands: by failure, by repetition, by five years of never once letting it close while he was watching.

He took the tray from its shelf and counted as he always counted. Twelve iron nails, filed smooth and knotted with red thread — *for mending*, the placard said, and that was even true, in the way that an ordinary knot can hold a fence rail if you believe in the fence more than the rope. Six pouches of dried rowan berries. A jar of goose-fat salve scented with enough lavender to hide the fact that it was only goose fat. And at the back, wrapped in plain linen where casual eyes would slide past it, four charms that were not harmless at all.

Those were not for sale. Those were for remembering who he was.

Hazel carried the tray out into the grey morning light and set it on the worn sill of his shop, which occupied the ground floor of a leaning house on Quill Lane, wedged between a cooper's and a dyer's, so nondescript that even the pigeons seemed to overlook it. The sign above the door read *DUNMORE — CHARMS & CURES* in letters he had painted himself, deliberately badly, so that people would take him for a man whose ambitions extended exactly as far as his brushwork.

"Morning, Dunmore." Old Cass the cooper passed with a yoke of staves across her shoulders. "My hip's singing again."

"Wet coming," Hazel said. "Rub the salve in clockwise. Mind, it's the rubbing does the work; the fat's just an excuse."

She laughed, because that was the joke they had been performing for three years, and went on her way. That was the whole architecture of his life. Small jokes. Small cures. A small man in a small doorway whom the great machinery of Corvale had no reason to notice.

Because Corvale had outlawed magic forty years ago, and it had done so with fire.

Not the polite kind of ban, the kind that gathers dust in a law book. King Aldric's grandfather had burned the Register of Mages in Coronation Square and then, over one long winter, burned eleven of the mages whose names had been written in it. Their apprentices had scattered. Their charms had been confiscated by the barrelful. And the witch-hunters — the Order of the Ashen Seal, gray-cloaked and soft-voiced — had taken up permanent residence in the city like a fever that never quite breaks. They paid well for names. They paid better for proof. They paid best of all for the ones who fought.

So Hazel sold knots and goose fat, and every night he went down a ladder behind a false wall in his cellar and checked a door that must never, ever open.

He thought about the door all day, the way a man with a bad tooth thinks about his tongue. He was thinking about it when the widow Marren haggled him down two coppers on rowan berries. He was thinking about it when the dyer's boy ran past chasing a hoop, and the hoop rolled against Hazel's boot, and he felt — faintly, faintly, like warmth through a keyhole — the residue of a luck-charm some grandmother had tied into the boy's coat hem. Sloppy work. Sweet work. It would get someone hanged someday.

He bent, returned the hoop, and did not touch the hem.

"Careful," he said mildly, and the boy grinned and was gone, trailing a thread of borrowed fortune down Quill Lane, and Hazel stood in his doorway feeling the old hunger rise in him like water into a ditch.

That was the thing nobody understood about giving up magic. It was not like giving up theft or drink, sins that pull at you from outside. Magic pulled from inside. It sat in his blood the way song sits in a struck bell, and every day of his quiet life was a matter of not ringing.

* * *

At dusk he closed the shutters, turned the sign, and ate bread and hard cheese standing up, the way he always did, because sitting down to a meal made a home and homes could be described to hunters. Then he moved the grain sack in the cellar corner, lifted the trapdoor under it, and took down the lamp with the shuttered flame.

The ladder went down farther than the house had depth, which was the first wrongness about it, and Hazel had stopped noticing the wrongness years ago the way you stop hearing a river. At the bottom, the passage ran back into the earth beneath Quill Lane — older than Corvale's walls, older than Corvale's name — and the air changed there. It smelled of cold iron and crushed green things, of sap and frost. It smelled, though he had never found words to tell anyone this, *awake*.

Twenty paces. Thirteen steps worn hollow in the stone. And then the corridor ended at the Thorn Gate.

It looked, to a stranger's eye, like a door grown rather than built: black wood veined with living briars, thorns as long as a finger curling from its frame and interlacing across its face in a lattice tight enough to keep out light. No handle. No hinge visible. Only at the center, where the thorns parted for the span of a hand, a hollow shaped like something that might once have held a key.

Five years ago, on a night he did not let himself remember clearly, Hazel Dunmore had sealed this door with his own blood and his own oath, and he had walked away from what waited beyond it, and he had built this small life over the wound like a poultice.

The ritual was simple. He uncovered the lamp. He laid his palm flat against the coldest thorn, the one just left of center, and he listened.

Most nights the gate was silent — that deep, vegetative stillness of something sleeping with its roots deep in both worlds. Most nights he felt nothing but the thorn's chill seeping into his hand, and he would say, aloud, because the oath liked to be spoken: *"Still shut. Still mine."* And then he would climb the ladder, replace the sack, sleep badly, and sell goose fat in the morning.

Tonight the gate was warm.

Not warm the way a body is warm. Warm the way a forge is warm after the smith has gone home — heat without presence, heat that meant *something has been burning here*. The thorn under his palm pulsed faintly, once, twice, slow as a sleeper's heart, and far on the other side of the door, deeper than sound, Hazel felt something shift its weight.

His mouth had gone dry. He realized he had been holding his breath long enough for his vision to spark at the edges.

"Still shut," he said, and his voice came out thin. "Still mine."

The pulse slowed. Did not quite stop. He stood there a long while longer than the ritual required, listening to the dark breathe, telling himself that gates this old were moody things, that thorned doors dreamed, that five years of silence did not end on an ordinary Tuesday in autumn.

Then he climbed the ladder, replaced the sack, and told himself a second lie, which was that everything was fine.

* * *

It was well past midnight when the stranger came.

Hazel was awake in the narrow room above the shop — he slept little and lightly, another inheritance of five years ago — and the first he knew of anything was the sound of his street door latch lifting. Not breaking. Lifting. Opened by a hand that knew, impossibly, that the third board from the bottom of his door groaned unless you raised the latch fully before pulling, a detail he had never told anyone because no one had ever stayed long enough to need to know it.

He was down the stairs with the iron poker from the hearth before his mind caught up with his feet.

The man who half-fell across his threshold was tall, gray-bearded, dressed in the undyed wool of a carter, and he was bleeding from a wound under his shoulder blade that had soaked his whole left side black. His eyes found Hazel's in the dark with terrible precision, as if he had known exactly where Hazel would be standing.

"Door," the man said. "You keep the door."

"You've the wrong shop." Hazel's voice was flat and low, the voice of a man with nothing worth stealing. "Get out before I call the watch."

"There is no time left to be careful." The stranger took one step forward and his legs went from under him; Hazel caught him by reflex, and the man's weight sagged against him, and his blood soaked through Hazel's shirt, warm as the thorn had been warm. Up close he smelled of horses and river water and, underneath both, of cold iron and crushed green things.

Of the passage. Of the gate.

Every instinct Hazel owned screamed at him to shove the man out onto the cobbles and bolt the door and be a charm-seller again by morning. Instead he heard himself say, quietly, "Help me, then. Don't fall on the tray."

He got him into the back room, got him onto the cot, got the shirt off him. The wound was a hunter's work — a clean puncture made by a narrow blade, the kind the Order favored because it killed slowly enough for questioning. Around it, drawn on the skin in something that glittered faintly like ash mixed with spit, someone had written a binding sigil. Hazel recognized the hand of it. Everyone in Corvale's hidden corners recognized it. The Ashen Seal used it to pin a mage's power down while they carried him.

Which meant the man on his cot was a mage. Which meant that helping him was death by decree, no trial, no appeal, the pyre lit by noon.

"I'll cut that mark off you," Hazel heard himself say, "if your power doesn't flood out and break my windows. Nod if that's agreeable."

The man laughed — a cracked, astonished laugh — and nodded.

Hazel worked in silence: knife, salt, a knot of red thread he had sworn never to use for true mending. The sigil resisted, hissed, came away like a splinter of frozen light. The stranger shuddered, gasped, and lay stiller afterward, his breathing easier, color creeping back toward his jaw.

"The crown," he said.

"I don't discuss the crown. Nobody sane discusses the crown."

"He wears it." The man's hand closed around Hazel's wrist with shocking strength. His eyes were bright with fever and with something worse than fever. "Boy. Gatekeeper. Listen to me, because I have been running for nine days to reach you and I am nearly finished running. He wears it, and he is not in it anymore."

"Who—"

"Aldric. The king." The words came slow and deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a wall. "There is a thing called the Veilwraith. It comes from beyond doors like yours. Five winters ago it found a crack into this world, and it climbed, the way such things climb, and it reached the throne, and Aldric — the true Aldric — invited it in. An oath, freely given. Do you understand? It did not conquer us. We are being *eaten politely*."

Hazel's hands had gone still over the bandage. Beyond the back-room wall, beneath the floor, beneath the earth, he felt the gate pulse again — that slow forge-warmth — and this time he understood it, and understanding was infinitely worse than not.

"It feeds on bound magic," the stranger said. "On oaths. On every hedge-charm and hidden rite in this city, and the tighter Corvale forbids them, the richer the harvest grows. Your outlawed magic is its table. The Ashen Seal are its grooms. Every winter solstice it eats more, and the man wearing the king's face casts no shadow at certain angles, and the decrees have grown cruel in ways no frightened human king would invent, because cruelty is seasoning." He smiled, horribly. "I was the last of an order that kept maps. Yours is among them. I'm sorry about that. I truly am."

"Get out," Hazel whispered. It came out as less than a whisper, barely breath. "Whatever you think I am—"

"I know precisely what you are. You sealed the Thorn Gate five years ago and ran from what lies on the other side of it, and you have been hiding on top of it ever since like a man sleeping on a drum." The stranger fumbled weakly at his own shirt and drew out a fold of oilcloth, stained dark at one corner. "And now the thing beneath your floor is stirring, because its master on the throne has begun to call, and when the solstice moon rises — nineteen nights from tonight — it will answer, whether you seal it or not. Unless you open it first. Unless you go through."

He pressed the oilcloth bundle into Hazel's hands, folding Hazel's fingers closed around it with both of his own, and his grip was already failing.

"The map shows the gate," he said. "And it shows what the gate leads to. And at the end, boy, it shows the throne room. Everything is doors. It was always only ever doors."

His head fell back. His breathing lengthened, shallowed, and somewhere in the next hour — Hazel could not have said when, could not have said why he simply sat there holding the man's hand instead of fetching anything useful — it stopped.

Outside, Quill Lane slept on, innocent, bell-less, ordinary. Hazel sat in the dark of his back room with a dead stranger's blood drying on his hands and a paper parcel heavy on his knees, and above the smell of blood he could smell, faint and unmistakable, cold iron and crushed green things rising through his floorboards.

The gate was warm. Something on the throne was calling it home.

With fingers that did not feel like his own, Hazel unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay a map, folded and refolded a hundred times, drawn in ink gone brown with age — and pressed into the crease at its heart, unmistakable even in the lamplight, a smear of fresh blood in the shape of a crown.
