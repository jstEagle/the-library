## 2. A Map Pressed into Unwilling Hands

The stranger died at dawn, and he did it badly.

Hazel had known death before—had held his mother's hand while the fever took her, had buried a neighbor's cat with more ceremony than the law allowed for human dead in Corvale—but he had never watched a man die slowly enough to be *inconvenient*. That was a shameful thought, and he knew it even as it formed, and still it would not leave him. The bleeding man in his back room was going to die among the dried lavender bundles and the jars of beetle-wing pigment, and when the witch-hunters came—and they would come; blood on cobblestones always drew them like crows to a slaughterhouse—they would find Hazel Dunmore standing over a corpse that was not his own.

He knelt beside the pallet he'd dragged from the storeroom and pressed another cloth to the man's side. The cloth came away black. Not the red-black of ordinary blood but something darker, thinner, that smelled of cold iron and old rain. Hazel had seen wounds like this exactly once, five years ago, on the far side of a door he had sworn never to open again.

"Stop that," the man rasped. His hand, gray as candle wax, caught Hazel's wrist. "Save your strength. It's past mending."

"You don't know that."

"I know thorn-rot when it's eating me." The stranger laughed, and the laugh turned into a wet cough that spattered his chin. He was perhaps fifty, hollow-cheeked, dressed in the plain wool of a city clerk, though the boots were made for walking and the cloak had been mended by someone who knew field stitches. A courier's disguise over something else entirely. "You're the charm-seller. The one on Bellwether Lane."

"I sell charms," Hazel said carefully. "Lucky knots. Dream sachets. Nothing that works."

"Nothing that works." The man smiled with cracked lips. "That's what I told myself too, once. Before I took the oath." His eyes drifted to the ceiling beams, tracking something Hazel couldn't see. "Five years I've kept it shut. Five years of watching. Do you know how long five years is, when you're the only one who knows?"

Hazel's hands went still on the cloth. *Kept what shut.* The words landed in his chest like stones dropped down a well, and he heard the splash of them somewhere very deep.

Outside, Bellwether Lane was waking. The baker's boy clattered past with his cart. Somewhere a shutter banged. Ordinary sounds, the sounds Hazel had spent five years arranging his life around like furniture, so that no one would ever look twice at the narrow shop with the faded sign reading DUNMORE'S CHARMS — KNOTS & KEEPSAKES, and less than once at the man behind the counter who never aged quite right, whose eyes were the green of deep water rather than shallow glass.

"The crown," the stranger whispered. "You have to understand about the crown."

"I don't have to understand anything," Hazel said. "I have to get you out of my shop before—"

"The king is already gone."

The sentence fell into the room and lay there. Hazel found he had stopped breathing. "Gone where?"

"Not gone." The man's fingers tightened on Hazel's wrist with surprising strength, a drowning man's grip. "*Gone.* There's nothing under the crown anymore. Nothing wearing it but hunger. I've stood in the throne room, charm-seller. I've looked at him at close range. At certain angles—" He shuddered, or tried to; the shudder died halfway through his body. "At certain angles he casts no shadow. And the shadow is the true part."

"That's madness," Hazel said, because it was the safe thing to say, and because saying it aloud might make it untrue. "King Aldric has ruled for twenty years. Whatever you saw—"

"I saw what I saw. And I've seen what comes through the streets at night, gathering. Feeding." The man's gaze snapped back to Hazel's face, and there was such terrible clarity in it that Hazel's denial curdled in his throat. "It eats magic, do you understand? All the little magics your neighbors hide in their cellars—the charms, the blessings, the hedge-craft—it drinks them down, and every season it grows stronger, and every season the decrees grow crueler. The purges aren't policy. They're appetite."

*The purges aren't policy.* Hazel thought of the Edict of Ashes, three winters past, when forty households in the Tanner's Quarter had been burned out in a single night on suspicion of unlicensed craft. He'd told himself it was fear. Fear made sense. Kings feared what they couldn't control; that was practically the definition of a king.

But appetite—

"Who are you?" Hazel asked quietly.

The stranger studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Hazel's face seemed to decide something. "Corvus Hale," he said. "Last of the Order of the Gate."

The name went through Hazel like a blade finding the gap between ribs. The Order of the Gate. He hadn't heard those words spoken since the night he'd run, since the night he'd left his sister standing on the thorned path with her hand outstretched and her voice cracking as she called his name. Five years, and the words still fit his ears like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

"There is no Order," he said. "There hasn't been for a hundred years."

"No," Corvus agreed. His breathing was shallower now, each word costing more than the last. "And look what the world's become without one."

* * *

He lasted until the light through the back window turned from gray to gold.

In between, he talked—in fragments, in pieces that didn't fit together, some of it delirium and some of it, Hazel suspected with growing dread, the plainest truth Hazel had heard in years. He talked about the Thorn Gate beneath the city, sealed not with stone but with an oath older than the kingdom. He talked about wards that needed renewing every season, and how he'd been the only one left to renew them, and how this winter he'd been three days late and the gate had *tasted* him. He talked about a map.

"It's yours now," he said, near the end. "I chose you. Don't argue. I've been watching you for two years, Hazel Dunmore. You check the seal under your floor every night. You think nobody notices a man who walks the same circuit at midnight, but I notice everything. It's my whole cursed job."

Hazel's blood went cold. "You've been *watching* me?"

"Somebody had to. The gatekeeper fled and left the door half-latched, and somebody had to keep an eye on the latch." Corvus's mouth twisted—not in anger, Hazel realized, but in something closer to pity. "I'm not angry with you, boy. Running is the most human thing there is. But doors don't care why you ran. Doors only care whether they're shut."

"I don't know what you're talking about." Even to his own ears it sounded thin as paper.

"Mm." Corvus closed his eyes. "Get the map. Left boot. Before your hands shake too much to untie it."

Hazel should have refused. Every instinct honed across five careful years screamed at him to refuse—to put the dying man out of his mind, to sweep the bloodstains into the fire, to open his shop at nine o'clock and sell lucky knots to gullible clerks and be no one, forever. That was the bargain he'd made with himself the night he crossed back through: *invisible, harmless, small.* Small things survived.

Instead he knelt and unlaced the ruined boot with fingers that shook only a little, and drew out a roll of oilskin wrapped around something stiff.

Inside the oilskin was a map drawn on vellum gone soft with age, and pressed between its folds, a second thing: a sliver of black wood, thorned along one edge, that hummed against Hazel's fingertips like a struck bell. He snatched his hand back. The humming continued in his palm, faint and patient, as if it could wait.

"Careful with that," Corvus murmured. "It remembers being part of the door."

Hazel spread the map on the floorboards with the reverence he usually saved for dangerous things. It showed Corvale—or a Corvale beneath Corvale, rendered in ink that shifted color when he tilted it, brown to rust to a red like the blood drying on his floor. Streets he knew. Streets he didn't. And threaded through all of them, drawn in a line of tiny linked thorns, a route: from a point marked with a circle in the oldest quarter of the city—a point that sat directly beneath, he understood with a lurch, his own shop—all the way up through the warrens, the aqueducts, the foundations of the palace itself, to a chamber marked at the heart of the royal residence with a symbol he recognized from nightmares he'd had since childhood.

A crown, drawn inside a ring of thorns.

"It goes to the throne room," Hazel said stupidly.

"It goes everywhere the Veil reaches," Corvus said. "The gate below your feet is just the front door. The wraith doesn't live beyond it, charm-seller. It lives *here*. It's been leaking through for years, seeping up through the roots of the city, and it pooled where the power pools. Where else?" He coughed, long and awful. "The throne. The crown. The king's own oath, which is the strongest binding in Corvale—that's what it wears. That's what it *is*, now, mostly. Aldric invited it in. Or Aldric's grandfather did. The maps don't say. The maps only show where the rot runs."

"And what am I supposed to do with this?"

"Renew the seal. Find someone who remembers the rites—Ilex, if he still lives, the priest at the Undertow, he forgets things but his hands remember. And then—" Corvus's voice frayed. He gathered himself with visible effort. "Then go through, if it comes to that. Go through and cut the bond at its root. Before solstice. After solstice it won't matter what anyone does."

"Go *through*." Hazel sat back on his heels. His heart was slamming against his ribs like something trying to escape. "No. No, absolutely not. I don't—you have the wrong man. I sold charms. I sell *knots*. I'm not a gatekeeper, I'm not anything, I left that life—"

"I know you left it." Corvus opened his eyes, and they were clear and calm and unbearably kind. "That's why I chose you. A man who stayed would have already been eaten. A man who ran knows exactly what's out there, and exactly what it costs." His hand crawled across the blanket, slow as a spider, and closed over Hazel's where it rested on the map's edge. "Take it. Take the oath-key. Sworn hands only, boy. It's useless to anyone else, and worse than useless to the wrong ones."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's the qualification." The ghost of a smile. "Press your thumb to the seal. Bottom corner. Please. I can't die holding this. If they find it on my body, everything I've done is wasted, and the city goes dark by spring."

"They'll find it on *my* body instead."

"Then don't die." Corvus's grip tightened. "Swear it. Sworn hands. Please."

And here is the thing Hazel would turn over and over in the nights afterward, examining it like a counterfeit coin: he could have said no. He could have pried the old man's fingers loose, wrapped the map in oilskin, and dropped it into the river with the rest of his conscience. Instead he heard himself say, in a voice that sounded younger than he felt, "If I do this, you tell me how to undo it after. Whatever I swear, there has to be an end to it."

"There's always an end," Corvus said. "It's just rarely where you expect."

Hazel pressed his thumb to the bottom corner of the map, where a thorn-ring waited like a mouth.

The vellum warmed under his skin. Something slid up his arm—not pain, exactly, but recognition, the feeling of a lock acknowledging a key it had been waiting decades to test. The oath-key in its fold of cloth sang one clear note and went silent. On the map, the route of linked thorns brightened from rust-red to living green, and Hazel felt the whole hidden architecture of it settle into his mind like a weight he would carry now until someone took it from him, or until it took him.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you bastard. What did you just do to me?"

"Gave you the worst job in the world," Corvus said. His voice was barely a thread now. "Same as mine. Same as hers, before me. You'll want to hate me for it. Go ahead. Hate's easier to carry than guilt." His eyes were fixed on the ceiling again, on whatever only he could see. "She said you'd come back to it eventually. Your sister. She said, *he'll run home before he runs anywhere else.*"

The room tilted. Hazel gripped the edge of the pallet. "What did you say?"

But Corvus Hale, last of the Order of the Gate, had spent his final breaths, and what came out of him next was only air, and then not even that. His hand slipped from Hazel's wrist and lay open on the blanket, palm up, empty, the way hands look when they have finally set down something heavy.

Hazel sat with the body for a long time while the gold light climbed the wall. Outside, the baker's boy made his second pass. A woman laughed somewhere, high and careless. The city went on being a city, ignorant and bright, and Hazel knelt in his back room holding a dead man's map and understood that the last five years of his life had just quietly ended, the way a bridge ends—not with a crash, but with the moment your foot comes down and finds nothing underneath.

* * *

They came at noon.

Hazel had done what he could. He'd washed the floor with lye water, wrapped the body in sacking from the storeroom, and stowed it in the cellar nook behind the casks, where the cold would slow what he didn't want to think about. He'd burned Corvus's boots. He'd opened the shop at nine and sold a dream sachet to Mrs. Callow and a lucky knot to a nervous bridegroom, smiling his shopkeeper's smile, hands steady, voice pleasant, while the oath-key sat wrapped in oilskin inside the false bottom of his flour bin and hummed its patient note against the grain.

At noon, the bells of the Citadel began to ring wrong.

Not the hour-chime—something faster, arrhythmic, the pattern the city used for fire and plague and other emergencies that required people to stay indoors. Hazel's stomach dropped through the floorboards. He stepped to the door and looked up Bellwether Lane, and there they were: six riders in ash-gray cloaks coming down from the Fountain Square at a walk that was somehow worse than a gallop, unhurried, inevitable, the way winter comes down a hill. Witch-hunters. He knew the lead rider by reputation if not by sight—Commander Dree, they called her, the woman who had emptied the Tanner's Quarter. Behind the riders marched a dozen of the graycoat watch, and behind them, absurdly, a cart with a barrel of pitch.

They weren't looking for a corpse. They were looking for a street.

Hazel watched them halt at the head of the lane. Watched Commander Dree dismount and unfold a scrap of paper—a list, or a map, or a name. Watched her speak briefly to the graycoats, who fanned out with the smooth coordination of men who had done this many times. The first door they hammered on belonged to the Tallow family, who kept a lodger rumored to read fortunes. The screaming started almost immediately.

*They're sweeping the lane,* Hazel thought, ice spreading through his chest. *Door to door. Someone told them where the blood came from.*

Because that was the thing about Corvus Hale's death that Hazel had failed to think through in his grief and his panic: a man doesn't bleed like that from nothing. Corvus had been wounded somewhere else, and had run here, and had been followed, or tracked, or scented. The witch-hunters hadn't come for Hazel. They'd come following a trail of thorn-rot blood straight down Bellwether Lane, and every house between the fountain and his shop was paying for Hazel's silence.

Mrs. Tallow was dragged out into the street in her nightdress, still arguing, still insisting she was loyal, loyal to the crown, loyal to the king. Her lodger didn't argue at all. They carried him out limp.

Hazel stood in his doorway with his shopkeeper's smile nailed to his face and his heart hammering out a single thought, over and over, like a fist on a door: *not me, not me, not me.* And underneath it, quieter, in a voice that sounded horribly like Corvus Hale's: *doors only care whether they're shut.*

The graycoats reached the fourth house. Then the fifth. Then the sixth, which shared a wall with his.

Hazel stepped back inside and closed his door with a click that sounded, in the ringing hush of the lane, as loud as the Citadel bells. Through the gap in his shutters he watched them work. He watched a graycoat sergeant hold up a hand outside the Tallow house, sniffing the air like a hound, and turn—slowly, deliberately—toward the narrow shop with the faded sign.

DUNMORE'S CHARMS. KNOTS & KEEPSAKES.

His legs wanted to carry him to the cellar, to the tunnel he'd never finished digging, to anywhere. Instead they carried him to the flour bin. His hands, steady as they had not been all morning, lifted out the oilskin bundle, unwrapped it, and spread the map across the counter in the slanted noon light.

Think. Think like a hunted thing, because that is what you are now.

The hunters would search the shop. They would find the cellar, the sacking, the body—unless. Unless the body wasn't there to find. Unless the trail ended somewhere it made sense, somewhere that explained six houses searched and a corpse discovered in a drain or a ditch, somewhere that gave the graycoats their answer and sent them away satisfied.

Or unless they found the map first, and then none of it mattered, and neither did he.

Hazel looked down at the vellum, at the route of thorns threading from the circle beneath his feet up through the bones of the city to the crowned symbol at its heart, and for the first time he truly *read* it—not as a dying man's obsession but as what it was. Not a map of an escape. A map of an approach. Every secret way, every forgotten stair, every root-passage beneath Corvale, converging on the throne room like veins converging on a heart.

Corvus hadn't drawn this to flee the wraith. He'd drawn it to reach it.

And the wraith, if it was real, if any of this was real, would know that. Would feel this map the way a spider feels a fly testing the outermost strand.

*It wants the gate opened,* Corvus had said, or had he? Had those been the man's words or Hazel's own rising panic putting them in his mouth? Either way the certainty settled over him now, cold and complete: the thing on the throne had let Corvus run. Had let him bleed his way across the city to the one door it couldn't pass. Because a door needs a keeper, and the last keeper was dying, and dying men choose successors.

Hazel's thumb throbbed where it had touched the seal.

Boots crunched on the cobbles outside. Voices, low and official. The scrape of the Tallow house's door being forced.

Hazel folded the map along its old creases, wrapped it in oilskin, and did the only clever thing he could think of: he tucked it flat against his spine, beneath his shirt, bound there with a strip of linen the way smugglers bound contraband, and he picked up his shears and a bundle of lavender and stood at his workbench like a man with nothing in the world to hide.

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was the worst part. Three measured raps, courteous as a tax collector.

"Hazel Dunmore?" a woman's voice called through the door. "Witch-hunter Sera Vane, on the crown's business. Open, please."

Hazel looked at his hands—his father's hands, his sister's hands, a gatekeeper's hands pretending to be a shopkeeper's—and found, to his surprise, that they had stopped shaking altogether. The fear was still there, vast and cold as the sea. But underneath it, in the place where Corvus's map had settled like a swallowed stone, something else had begun to harden. Not courage. He wouldn't insult himself by calling it courage. Closer to the stubbornness of a knot that refuses, no matter how hard you pull, to slip.

He had run once. He had left his sister on a thorned path in the dark, and he had built five quiet years on top of that grave, and the years were over now, burned down to the ground with the rest of his careful life.

"One moment," Hazel called back, and reached for the latch.

Through the shutter-gap, at the edge of his vision, he saw the graycoat sergeant pause in the middle of the lane. The man was staring down at the cobblestones—at the faint, dark smear that led, drop by drop, from the fountain square to Hazel's door. Then the sergeant raised his head and looked directly at the shop, and his lips moved around a word Hazel couldn't hear but understood perfectly.

*Here.*
