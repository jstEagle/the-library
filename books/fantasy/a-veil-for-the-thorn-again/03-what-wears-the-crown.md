## 3. What Wears the Crown

The summons came at the hour when Corvale's bells had gone hoarse with cold, and Sera Vane dressed for court the way she dressed for a siege.

She laid out her commission coat on the chair back first — charcoal wool, silver braid, the sun-and-stake of the Order of the Purged Eye stitched at the breast — and looked at it for longer than she needed to. Eleven years she had worn that badge. Eleven years of kicking in charm-house doors and dragging weavers and midwives and one memorable beekeeper into the light for judgment, and in all that time she had never once stood before the king whose edicts made her work lawful. Witch-hunters did not attend court. They were the broom; the throne was the hand. The hand did not need to see the broom.

But the summons bore the royal seal, pressed so deep into the black wax that it had nearly torn through, and the seal itself was wrong in some way she could not name until she'd stared at it over her morning tea: the sun's rays on the old Aldric seal had numbered twelve. These numbered thirteen. She counted them three times to be sure. Her tea went cold while she counted.

*Thirteen rays. A counting error,* she told herself, and pinned on the badge anyway.

* * *

The Hall of Mirrored Bells had been built by an Aldric four generations dead, a man who — if the histories were honest, which they mostly weren't — had wanted a room where he could never be surprised. Every wall was polished bronze-silver, hung with bells of green glass that chimed when the drafts moved through, and the ceiling was vaulted so high that petitioners' voices fell upward instead of down, dissolving into echo before they reached the dais. It meant the king heard everything twice: once as speech, once as ghost.

Sera was led in with the third cohort of the morning — a grain factor, two shipwrights, a widow suing a guild — and arranged herself among the courtiers along the east wall, where the hunters' delegation stood like a row of nails driven into soft wood. Commander Haleth was there ahead of her, of course. Haleth was always ahead of her, the way frost is ahead of morning.

"Vane." He didn't look at her. His attention was fixed forward, on the dais, with the flat devotion of a man watching a fire he'd lit himself. "You'll wonder why we've been summoned."

"The letter didn't say."

"It doesn't need to say." A pause, filled by the green bells shivering somewhere above. "The Solstice Purge lists are being drawn. His Majesty wishes his instruments to be seen. To be *counted.*" Something in the word turned slightly, like a key not quite fitting. "Stand straight when he looks this way."

Sera stood straight. And then the trumpets sounded — thin, cold notes that made the glass bells answer in a chorus like distant screaming — and King Aldric came into his hall, and Sera Vane forgot every instruction she had ever been given about standing straight.

She had seen portraits. Every citizen of Corvale had seen portraits: Aldric the Vigilant, tall and spare, gray at the temples at forty, a man carved out of duty. The man who ascended the seven steps to the dais wore that same face the way a glove wears a hand. It fit. It moved where it should move. But something underneath had changed its posture, the way a house changes when the family within dies and strangers move in and put the furniture almost — but not quite — where it used to stand.

He was thinner. That was nothing; kings dieted strangely. He was paler. Also nothing; winter made corpses of everyone's complexion. It was the way he walked that snagged in her hunter's eye, the part of her trained across eleven years to read bodies the way scholars read scripture. The living body guards itself. It flinches from cold floors. It adjusts its balance without deciding to. This man descended the steps as though descending was something he had studied — each footfall placed with perfect, terrible correctness, no waste, no wobble, no life.

*He walks like something remembering how walking works,* she thought, and immediately despised the thought, because thoughts like that, unpruned, were the first rot in any institution. She had seen hunters go that way before — seeing sorcery in limping dogs and milk that soured early — and the Order burned such hunters' careers as efficiently as it burned charm-houses.

The king sat. The audience began.

* * *

For an hour she watched him do the ordinary violence of kingship, and it was the ordinariness that frightened her.

The grain factor pleaded a failed harvest; Aldric remitted his levy entirely, gently, and ordered his factor arrested for having mentioned the failure aloud. The widow's suit was settled in her favor with a generosity that drew murmurs of admiration — and then the guildmaster who had wronged her was sentenced to stand in the Pillory Square through three nights of the coming frost. Not death. Never death, anymore. The king had abolished capital punishment for magic crimes in the spring, and the city had called it mercy.

Sera had processed the paperwork on what that mercy meant. The condemned were taken to the palace under guard. No trial records followed them in. No records followed them out. Two hundred and eleven souls since spring, entered in the register as *remanded to the crown's care*, and the crown's care had a door that did not open again. Mercy, she had learned, could be a shape hunger wore.

Each decree the king issued today had that doubled quality — kindness in the mouth, cruelty in the teeth — as though someone had described justice to him secondhand and he was performing it from the description, getting the words right and the music wrong.

Then the light moved.

It was a small thing. Winter light in the Hall of Mirrored Bells came down in long amber lances from the clerestory, and as the morning aged those lances crawled across the floor like slow hands. Courtiers knew to drift with them or be blinded. The king did not drift; kings did not shift themselves for the sun. And so the light crossed him — crossed the dais, crossed his seated figure, crossed the pillar behind him — and in the moment that the lance lay full upon the floor at his left side, Sera saw the floor there was bare.

Every other body in the hall cast shadow. The grain factor's shadow trembled with his breathing. Haleth's shadow lay rigid behind him like a second commander. Even the banners threw shade. But where the king sat, the light simply stopped at his outline and continued beyond it unstretched, as though he were a hole cut cleanly out of the afternoon.

She blinked. The angle changed; a cloud crossed; when the light cleared, there was his shadow again, correct and proper, falling to his right.

Her heart was going hard against her badge. She made herself breathe the way she'd been taught on her first hunt: four counts in, six counts out, the body calmed so the eyes could be trusted. *Trusted.* That was the question, wasn't it. Whether her eyes could be trusted. Whether a woman who had spent eleven years finding guilt in other people's shadows could bear to find absence in her king's.

*Certain angles,* she thought, cataloguing it against her will, hunter-fashion, the observation filing itself where she could not unfile it. *When the light strikes low from the west. When it must pass close around him.* As if the shadow were a garment kept in reserve and donned only when the light demanded it. As if—

"Commander Haleth."

The king's voice carried through the hall without effort, and yet she felt it in her back teeth, a resonance slightly too deep for a human throat, a bell-note struck inside a word. The whole assembly stilled. The green glass bells overhead went silent, which was strange in itself; they answered every sound, and they did not answer him.

"Present, Majesty." Haleth strode to the center of the floor and knelt with the crisp totality of a man folding himself into an envelope.

"Your hunters have taken two hundred and nine." The number was wrong by two, Sera thought absurdly — the register said two hundred and eleven — and then hated herself for correcting arithmetic while her skin crawled. "The city grows cleaner. Tell me, Commander: does the city grow *quieter?*"

Haleth hesitated one half-second. Anyone else would have missed it. "Quieter, Majesty. Yes."

"And yet I hear still a humming beneath the stones. Like a hive. Like a choir holding its note." The king smiled, and the smile arrived on schedule and departed on schedule, a train running to time through empty country. "We will finish it, you and I. At solstice there will be a great gathering-in. Every tainted soul in Corvale, brought to the palace. My physicians believe a single treatment may suffice to end the taint in the city forever."

*Treatment.* The word dropped through Sera like a stone through pondwater, and she watched the ripples: *two hundred and eleven remanded to the crown's care, and what treatment has ever emptied a bed back into the world?*

"Your men will be honored at the gathering," the king continued, rising. "They have fed the— " a pause, fractional, like a needle skipping in a groove — "they have served the crown faithfully."

He turned from the hall, and as he turned, the western light caught him full and low, and for the space of one heartbeat Sera Vane saw the king of Corvale stand in a shaft of winter sun and throw no shadow at all — not faint, not shortened, but *absent*, the light lying on the flagstones behind him as innocent as if he had never stood there.

Then he was gone through the door behind the dais, and the bells remembered how to ring, and the hall exhaled into murmur, and everyone began saying the ordinary things people say after audiences: *generous, decisive, a strange light about him lately, don't you think?*

Sera said nothing. She stood very straight, as instructed, and understood that she had just been shown the reason for the summons. Not to be seen. Not to be counted.

To be *checked*. The way a craftsman checks a blade before trusting it. The king — whatever wore the king — had wanted his instruments assembled in one room where the light could fall on all of them.

* * *

She did not go to the barracks afterward. She told herself she was walking, only walking, the circuit hunters walked after court to clear their heads, down the Processional and past the Register House where the purge rolls were kept — and then she was inside the Register House, because her rank opened doors and because she had already decided, somewhere below decision, that she would not leave without looking.

Two hundred and eleven names. She copied none of them; she had a memory like a ledger and she knew its cost. She read them standing, in the dust-colored light of the archive, reading the occupations because occupations told truths titles concealed: *cooper's daughter, hedge-charmer, lamplighter (suspected), widow, widow, apprentice glover, hedge-charmer, hedge-charmer.* On and down. The Order had taught her that pattern meant a network. Standing here in the quiet, with the king's too-deep voice still ringing behind her teeth, she thought: *or it means a net, drawn slowly closed, and something at the center feeding.*

At the bottom of the final page, in fresh ink, dated yesterday, a new entry waited. Not a name. An annotation in the registrar's smallest hand:

*By order: rolls to continue through solstice. All entries thereafter to be delivered directly to the Privy Chamber. None to the Chancery. None to the Queen's former secretary. — marked urgent, thrice.*

The Queen had been dead eight years. Her secretary had died the following winter. Yet someone had thought to forbid the rolls to both of them, which meant someone feared what dead hands might do with the truth, or — colder thought — meant the order had been written by a hand that did not fully remember which of its enemies still lived.

Sera closed the roll with fingers that were not quite steady, and set it back exactly as she'd found it, spine-creases aligned, dust undisturbed, the way she'd been taught to leave a scene she might need to search again.

Outside, the Processional was lighting its lamps. Somewhere a child was singing a skipping-song about the Thorn Man who takes naughty charmers under the hill, and mothers were hushing it, because such songs were near-magic now, and near-magic was enough.

She walked home the long way, and she did her duty by herself the entire distance: *You imagined the shadow. You were tired. You have hunted so long you see hares in every hearth-rug. The king is the king, the Order holds the wall between Corvale and the wild dark, and a hunter who doubts the hand that holds her is worse than useless — she is a doorway.*

It was good reasoning. It had held for eleven years.

But duty, she reflected, stopping at her own door in the blue evening, watching her own shadow stretch long and honest and unquestionable across the snow — duty was a coat, and coats could be made to fit anything. She had seen enough men wear honor over rot to know the tailoring proved nothing.

What proved things was evidence. Angles. Light. Records.

That night she took out a clean sheet of paper, and instead of writing her report, she wrote at the top of the page, in the private shorthand she used for nothing she ever expected another soul to read:

*Thirteen rays on the seal. Twelve in the old cuts.*
*Walks like a memory of walking.*
*No shadow when the light comes low from the west. Twice observed.*
*"Feed the—" (unfinished).*
*Treatment.*

She looked at the five lines for a long time. Five small sentences. Enough to hang her career, if anyone found them. Not nearly enough to hang whatever sat on the throne, if she was wrong — and oh, how she hoped to be given the chance to be wrong.

Below them, after a silence in which the candle guttered and steadied, she added a sixth line:

*Find what came through, and when.*

Because that was the thought that had finally come to her in the archive, the one she had been circling all evening the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. Hunters knew their monsters. The Order's oldest manuals drew the shapes of them: the hollow-eyed Fetch, the Liar-of-Flesh, the things that slipped through boundaries where boundaries thinned. And every manual agreed on the first law of such things.

They did not arrive through walls. They arrived through doors.

Somewhere in Corvale, in these last years of quiet mercy and missing shadows, a door had opened. And Sera Vane, who had spent her life closing doors on the powerless, intended to find the one that had let something powerful in — even if it cost her the coat she stood in.

She banked the fire. She slept badly. And in the morning, the first rumor reached her over the breakfast plates, breathless and secondhand and impossible to trace: a charm-seller on Weft Street, a stranger bleeding out in his shop, witch-hunters swarming the quarter before dawn.

A dead man on Weft Street, and the hunters hunting not the killer but the shop itself.

Sera put down her cup, and reached for her coat.
