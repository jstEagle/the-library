## 5. Ashes on the Register

The order came at dawn, written in the commander's own hand, which meant it could not be refused.

*Charm-house suspected, Lantern Row, the sign of the copper bell. Burn it. Bring the keeper to the Register for questioning.*

Sera Vane read it twice by the light of her window, the paper cold in her fingers, and told herself that this was the work she had sworn to do. Ten years in the king's hunt. Ten years of registers and raids and the clean, bright logic of it: magic unbound is a fire in a dry field, and someone must carry the water. She had believed it once with her whole chest. She still believed most of it, most mornings.

But she had not slept well since the court assembly three weeks past — since the moment she had stood at a certain angle beneath the high windows of the throne room and seen that the king cast no shadow where every other soul in the hall did. She had told herself it was a trick of the light, a pillar blocking the sun, some flaw in her own tired eyes. She had told herself so many times that the telling had begun to sound like prayer: repeated not because it was true but because she needed it to be.

She dressed, buckled her commission seal at her belt, and went down into a city waking gray and cold.

* * *

Lantern Row was one of those streets Corvale pretended not to have — narrow, leaning, its upper stories so close together that neighbors could shake hands across the gap. The copper bell hung above a shop door gone green with weather, and beside it, painted in careful faded letters: *Charms & Small Mercies.*

"Small mercies," Sera murmured. Someone had a sense of humor. Or a sense of cover.

Her squad of six waited in the mist behind her, torches unlit but ready, because the protocol was always the same: surround, enter, search, register, burn. The burning was ceremonial as much as practical. A charm-house's stock had to be destroyed publicly, in the street, so the neighborhood would understand that the crown saw everything, forgave nothing, and kept its arithmetic exact.

She knocked first. Protocol said knock. It gave the guilty thirty seconds to surrender, and it gave the innocent time to open their own door like people rather than prisoners.

Footsteps. A bolt drawn back. The door opened on a woman perhaps sixty years old, small and round-shouldered, flour on her apron as though she'd been baking, which Sera would later learn she did every morning whether or not anyone bought bread from her.

"Yes?" the woman said. Her eyes went to the seal at Sera's belt and something behind them went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when it hears a wolf at the door but hopes stillness will pass for an empty room.

"Sera Vane, witch-hunter, second register." She held up the order. "By decree of His Majesty's hunt, this premises is suspected of harboring unlawful charms. You'll stand aside while we search."

The woman's hands folded over her floury apron. "I sell buttons," she said. "And thread. And sometimes bread."

"Then you've nothing to fear."

It was the oldest lie in the hunt's liturgy, and they both knew it. Everyone had something to fear. That was rather the point of the whole apparatus.

They went in.

* * *

The shop smelled of lavender and woodsmoke and, underneath it, faintly, of something green — the smell of growing things, of sap, which in a city where living magic was contraband hit Sera like a shout in a library. She kept her face still. Behind her, Corporal Drenn struck flint to the lamps and the interior bloomed into warm light, and Sera saw the shop entire.

Buttons in jars. Thread in ranked spools. Bundles of dried herbs labeled in a neat hand — *for sleep, for fever, for grief* — which were technically legal, herbs being herbs, until intent made them otherwise. A counter worn smooth by decades of elbows. And along the back wall, on shelves built low, at exactly the height a child could reach:

Toys.

Wooden horses with manes carved in flowing curls. Cloth dolls with embroidered faces, each smile stitched slightly different, no two alike. Little felt birds that — Sera picked one up and turned it in her fingers — had seeds sewn into their bellies, so that if a child buried one in the garden, the bird would become flowers by summer. A clever trick. A kind one. Not, strictly speaking, illegal.

But under the shelf, half-hidden in shadow, sat a row of dream-catchers woven from willow and thread, and these were another matter entirely. Sera lifted one down. Charms against nightmare were gray-market at best; charmed *for* dreams — woven to give a sleeping child good dreams, sweet ones, ones where lost mothers came back and fathers came home from the wars — that was working magic, plain and simple. Working magic in a home. Offered for sale. On Lantern Row, three streets from the hunt's own barracks.

"Drenn," she said quietly. "Back wall."

He came over, took the dream-catcher, weighed it in his palm the way he weighed everything, and grunted. "Woven tight. Old craft." He looked up at her. "That's a burning, Captain. No argument in it."

"No," Sera said. "No argument in it."

Except that there was, and it had started somewhere below her ribs and was spreading upward with every item they carried out into the street. Because here was what the registers never recorded: the toys were warm. Not magically — just warm, the way things are warm when they are loved, when many hands have held them. Some of the dolls' embroidery was mended, patched with mismatched thread by someone who could not bear to let a child's comfort die of a split seam. Whoever kept this shop did not make weapons. Did not make hexes, curses, love-spells, any of the things the law rightly feared. She made wooden horses and stitched smiles and sold sleep without terror to children who lived in a city where terror was the only honest currency.

"Ma'am." Drenn again, from the back room, his voice carefully flat. "You should see this."

The back room was a workshop. A bench under the window, tools laid out in soldierly order, shavings swept into a neat pile. And pinned to the wall above the bench, a child's drawing — crayon, the waxy cheap kind — of a stick-figure family standing outside this very shop, the copper bell rendered as a yellow circle, and beneath it in a child's enormous letters: *THANK YOU FOR MY DREAMS. I DONT HAVE THE BAD ONE NO MORE.*

*I don't have the bad one no more.*

Sera stood in front of the drawing for a long time. Behind her she heard the squad moving through the shop, gathering the stock into the burning-baskets, the wicker rustling like something alive being penned.

"Captain?" Drenn. "Orders?"

The order was already written. It was in her belt, in the commander's hand. *Burn it.* There was no line in the protocol for *but the toys are warm,* no column in the register for *a child sleeps without nightmares because of this crime.* The law was the law precisely so that a captain at dawn on a cold street did not have to decide anything at all. That was the mercy of it. That was the trap of it.

"Bring it all out," Sera said. "Front of the shop. We do it by the book."

* * *

They burned it in the street, because the book required the street.

The old woman — Maren Ottery, per the shop's registration, sixty-one years, widowed, no prior marks on the register in nineteen years of licensed trade — stood watching with her floury hands folded, and she did not weep, which was worse than weeping. She watched the dream-catchers go into the brazier first, then the herb bundles, then the dolls, and her face stayed composed as a closed shutter, and only her eyes moved, following each small burning thing down into the flames.

A crowd gathered, as crowds did. Mothers with shawls over their nightclothes. One boy, maybe seven, in a coat too big for him, who arrived at a dead run and stopped as if he'd run into glass when he saw the fire. Sera watched him watch a cloth doll — a rabbit, long-eared, one ear re-stitched in blue thread — curl and blacken and come apart into ash that the wind took eastward over the rooftops.

The boy did not cry either. He looked at Sera instead. Children always found the officer in charge; they had an instinct for it, the way dogs did. He looked at her with an expression she had no name for, an expression that was not hatred, because hatred requires a strength most seven-year-olds haven't got yet. It was the look of someone updating a ledger. *So this is what the crown is. So this is what it does. Noted.*

Sera had burned forty-one charm-houses in ten years. She had never once watched a child watch her do it.

"The keeper comes with us," she said, and her voice came out steady, thank all the quiet gods. "To the Register. Standard questioning."

Maren Ottery went between two troopers without resistance, pausing only at her own threshold to look back — not at the fire, Sera noticed, but at the boy. She mouthed something to him. Sera couldn't read it from that distance. She suspected it was *don't worry* or *be brave* or possibly *the blue tin, under the floor,* and she found she desperately hoped it was one of the first two.

The fire burned down. The crowd dispersed the way crowds do, reluctantly, in clusters, carrying the story home to a hundred kitchens. And Sera Vane stood in the ash-smell on Lantern Row holding her ledger, and understood that the next hour belonged to her alone.

* * *

The report was due by noon. Every raid produced one: date, location, items seized, items destroyed, disposition of persons. The register was the hunt's spine. Every entry signed and sealed. Every entry true — that was drilled into recruits before they ever carried a torch. *The register is the only thing between us and mere violence,* Commander Hale liked to say. *Anyone can burn a house. Only the hunt can say why, and be believed.*

Sera sat at her desk in the barracks annex with the form before her and her pen uncapped, and wrote:

*Raid conducted, Lantern Row, sign of the copper bell, per your order of this date. Premises searched. Unlawful charms discovered and destroyed by fire: eleven (11) dream-charms, four (4) herbal workings, assorted enchanted goods, total count thirty-seven (37) items.*

All true so far. Her pen hovered.

Here was the fork in the road, and she had been walking toward it for three weeks without admitting it — since the shadowless king, since the wrongness she had filed away as fatigue. Because there was a version of this report that was fully true, and it read: *Items destroyed included children's toys charmed for pleasant dreams. Keeper has no prior record. Evidence suggests no harmful workings on premises. Recommend review of raid criteria.*

And there was the version the commander expected, the version the machine wanted, the version that would keep Maren Ottery's questioning brief and her sentence light and Sera Vane's career smooth: *Dangerous enchantments destroyed. Keeper detained for full interrogation.*

The difference between the two versions was perhaps six lines of ink. The difference between the two versions was also, Sera understood with a clarity that felt like falling, the rest of her life.

If she wrote the true one, she became a problem. Problems got watched. Watched officers got assigned to ugly duties, and ugly duties had a way of ending careers or worse — she had seen it happen to a lieutenant named Corwin who had raised questions about a raid in the Tannery Quarter and now sold fish at the river market and did not meet anyone's eyes. If she wrote the false one — not false, she corrected herself, *incomplete*, the word mattered, incomplete was survivable, incomplete was merely silence — then she remained inside the machine, trusted, positioned, able to keep doing the small good the machine permitted. That was the argument for it. It was even a sincere argument. Half the compromises in the world were built on that exact sincere argument, and half the ruins too.

She thought about the boy's ledger-look. She thought about the stitched blue ear. She thought about the king's missing shadow, and how she had chosen, day after day, to call it fatigue.

Sera Vane wrote:

*Unlawful enchantments discovered and destroyed. Count and nature per standard schedule. Keeper detained for questioning. No anomalies noted.*

*No anomalies noted.*

She signed it. She pressed her commission seal into the wax, and the seal — the crowned tower of the hunt, the emblem she had been proud of at twenty-three — sank into the red like a thing drowning.

"There it is," said a voice behind her.

She had not heard the door. Commander Hale stood in the annex doorway, a thin man gone gray at the temples, with the patient eyes of someone who had read ten thousand reports and believed almost none of them at first glance. He crossed the room unhurried and looked down at the form, at the wet wax, at her.

"You hesitated," he said. Not accusing. Observing, the way he observed weather.

"I was confirming the counts, sir."

"Mm." He picked up the report, read it through, and set it down again with a softness that frightened her more than anger would have. "Thirty-seven items. Dream-charms among them, I'm told. Toys." He let the word sit. "You wrote *no anomalies noted.*"

"The items were destroyed per protocol. The keeper is in custody. The street is pacified. Sir."

Hale studied her for a long moment, and Sera realized, with a strange detachment, that whatever passed behind his eyes in that moment would decide more than her career. "When I was a young hunter," he said at last, "I burned a charm-house in Dockside. Found love-potions in it. Nasty things — coerced consent, ruined lives. Easy burning. Clean conscience." He straightened his cuffs. "Two doors down, unbeknownst to me, was a woman selling cradle-charms. Sleep blessings. My squad burned hers the same afternoon, off my own sergeant's initiative, and I signed the register without reading the item list. A baby died that winter. Colic, the physician said. Sleeplessness, and then fever, and then the grave." He looked at the report again. "I have read every item list since. Forty years of item lists. Do you know what I've learned, Captain?"

"No, sir."

"That the register doesn't record what we destroy. It records what we're willing to become." He tapped the page once, on the words *no anomalies noted,* and something in his expression was so old and so tired that Sera's breath caught. "This stays as written. But you'll take the Ottery questioning yourself, and you'll bring me her file personally, and you'll remember, every day you wear that seal, which version of today you buried."

He left. Sera sat alone in the annex with the ash-smell still in her hair and her heart going like a trapped bird, and understood that she had not been forgiven, or condemned, or even warned. She had been *kept*. Filed away, like a charm of uncertain nature, pending further examination.

* * *

She questioned Maren Ottery herself that evening, in the small room above the Register, and it was the easiest interrogation of her career, because the old woman confessed to everything and asked for nothing.

"Yes, I charmed the dream-catchers," Maren said, hands folded on the table, flour still ghosting her knuckles. "Willow and thread and a word over them, one word, the same my mother used. Yes, it's outlawed. Yes, I knew. Nineteen years licensed and I knew exactly what I was." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and level. "There's a girl on our row who screams in her sleep since her father was taken in the last purge. Screams every night. Her mother came to me with coins she didn't have. What would you have me tell her, Captain? *Sorry, the law prefers her screaming?*"

Sera had a hundred procedural questions prepared. She asked none of them.

"Why toys?" she heard herself ask instead. "Why the birds with the seeds?"

Something moved in the old woman's face — surprise, then a gentling. "Because a child who buries a bird and grows flowers learns the world isn't only taking," she said. "That's all. That's the whole spell. Everything else they say about us — the pacts, the thorns, the things beyond the gate — let the great houses fight over that. I only ever wanted the children to sleep."

*The things beyond the gate.* Sera turned the phrase over later, walking home through streets slick with early winter dark, past the palace where the windows burned gold and the king's banners stirred in a wind she could not feel. Rumors again. Charm-sellers' talk, smugglers' talk — whispers lately of a bleeding stranger on the south side, of a hidden gate, of a map. She'd dismissed it all as the usual folklore that grew in a city like mold in a wet wall.

But she was done, she realized, with dismissing things. She had falsified her first register today, and her commander had watched her do it, and the ground under her feet had shifted in a way that would never shift back. When the ground moves, a hunter looks for what else is moving with it.

In her pocket, her fingers found the scrap she'd taken from the shop without logging it — she didn't know why, except that it seemed wrong for it to burn. A corner of the child's drawing, torn loose in the search. Crayon-yellow circle. The copper bell.

Beneath it, in the enormous earnest letters she hadn't noticed before: *AND THE LADY SAID THE BELL WILL RING AGAIN SOMEDAY.*

Sera stopped under a lamp and read it three times.

Somewhere across the city, in a charm-shop on a quieter street, a bell she had never heard of hung waiting — and for the first time in ten years, Witch-Hunter Sera Vane began, quietly and against all her training, to wonder which side of the register she was really on.
