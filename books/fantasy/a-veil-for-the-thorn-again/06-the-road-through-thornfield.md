## 6. The Road Through Thornfield

They left Corvale before the bells finished ringing terce.

Hazel had chosen the hour for its ugliness—the grey sludge of mid-morning when the markets were loud and the watch was bored and no one looked twice at a charm-seller and his tinker cousin walking north with a handcart of scrap. Ugly hours kept you alive. He had learned that in another world, in another life, and the lesson had followed him across the Thorn Gate like a dog that would not be called home.

"Remind me," Bramble muttered, hunched under a coat three sizes too large for her narrow frame, "why we're pretending to be junk merchants instead of simply leaving like honest fugitives."

"Honest fugitives get stopped. Junk merchants get ignored." Hazel kept his eyes forward, on the mud and the cart wheels and nothing else. "Also, you *are* a junk merchant."

"I am an artist of small mechanisms who has fallen on hard times."

"You're a fae exile who sells clockwork beetles to children."

"And they *love* them," she said with dignity.

The Northgate road was thick with carts hauling winter turnips and barrels of salt fish, and they folded themselves into that traffic like two more dull threads in a coarse weave. Hazel had spent five years perfecting the art of being overlooked—a slouch here, a forgettable face there, charms of unremarkability stitched into the lining of his own coat, which was either deeply hypocritical or deeply sensible depending on how frightened he was. Today he was very frightened indeed, so he had layered three of them and still felt the itch between his shoulder blades, that animal certainty of eyes on his back.

The stranger's map lay against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, pressed flat beneath his shirt where it seemed to pulse faintly with each heartbeat. Or perhaps that was only his imagination. Perhaps it was only guilt. The dead man's blood had soaked through the parchment in a stain shaped like nothing at all and everything at once, and when Hazel closed his eyes he saw the man's cracked lips forming the same word over and over: *the crown, the crown, the crown.*

"They'll have found the body by now," Bramble said quietly. It wasn't a question. She had helped him bury it, after all, in the cellar beneath the shop, beneath the flagstone that also hid the stairs down to the gate. Two secrets stacked one atop the other like dishonest masonry.

"This morning. Maybe last night." Hazel guided the cart around a pothole. "Which means the witch-hunters will have torn my shop apart by noon, and by evening every hunter in the city will know a charm-seller named Dunmore left through the north road carrying something worth killing for."

"So we should hurry."

"We should hurry," he agreed, and did not hurry, because hurrying drew the eye, and the eye was the enemy. That was the whole rotten architecture of his life: move slowly enough to be invisible, care about nothing loudly enough to be believed. Five years of practice. Five years of being no one, and it had worked, right up until a bleeding stranger had collapsed through his door and made him someone worth hunting.

The city walls fell behind them by midday, and the land opened into the long grey shoulder of the Northmarch—hedgerows and fallow fields, then rougher pasture, then the first dark smudge of Thornfield Moor rising against the horizon like a bruise.

Bramble saw it before he said anything. Of course she did. She went quiet in the way she only went quiet when something genuinely troubled her, which was rare enough that Hazel noticed the silence the way you notice a missing bell in a familiar chime.

"You've gone stiff," she said finally.

"It's just a moor."

"It is not *just* a moor, Hazel Dunmore, and we both know it. Thornfield sits over one of the old root-veins. When the Gate was whole, this whole stretch of country hummed like a struck wire. Things grew here that had no business growing anywhere near a human city." Her amber eyes flicked to him, then away. "Things that were supposed to stay on the other side."

"The Gate is sealed."

"The Gate is sealed," she echoed. "Say it again, slower, and see if you believe yourself."

He didn't answer. The map, warm against his sternum, seemed to answer for him.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a drystone wall, a fire no larger than two cupped hands, because Bramble insisted that cold was a worse informant than any hunter. Hazel took first watch and sat with his back to the stones, watching the moor's edge dissolve into darkness, listening to the wind comb through the bracken.

It sounded almost like voices. It always had. Even as a boy—no. Not as a boy. He had never been a boy here. As a young man in the otherworld, walking the thorned hills beyond the Gate, he had heard the wind do this, and his sister had laughed at how he flinched.

*Maren,* he thought, and shut the thought away the way he shut away all thoughts of her, in the little stone room at the back of his mind where he kept everything he could not afford to carry.

"You're doing the thing," Bramble said from her bedroll, without opening her eyes.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes smooth as a pond and I can hear you locking doors inside your head." She rolled onto her side. "Whatever it is you're not thinking about—it travels lighter if you say it out loud."

"Nothing travels lighter than silence. That's rather the point of it."

"Mm." She closed her eyes again. "Five years, Hazel. You've been silent for five years. Has it made anything lighter?"

He fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch, flare, and die. Somewhere far off, a nightbird called—or something that imitated one badly—and he found himself counting the intervals between calls, an old habit, a sentry's habit, learned in a country where the dark things sang lullabies to lure the careless off the paths.

"I sealed the Gate," he said at last, because the silence had teeth tonight. "That's what I don't talk about. Not that I could seal it—that I *had* to. A gate doesn't get sealed for no reason, Bramble. You don't weld a door shut unless something came through it once. Or something might."

She was very still. "Something came through?"

"No." His hands were steady. He was proud of his hands. "Nothing came through. Nothing ever got the chance. But it *wanted* to. And I—" The twig had burned down to his fingers; he let it. "I decided the door stayed shut. Whatever was on the other side of it. Whoever."

The wind moved through the bracken, and this time it didn't sound like voices. It sounded like breathing.

Bramble said nothing more, but she didn't sleep either. He heard her turning over, once, twice, the small metallic whisper of the tools she slept with even now, and understood that she had heard exactly what he hadn't said: *whoever.* And that she was filing it away, patient as a jeweler, for the day she would demand the rest.

* * *

They crossed onto Thornfield proper the next afternoon, and the change came over the land so gradually that Hazel couldn't name the moment it happened—only that one step he was walking through rough grass and the next the ground had gone black and springy, webbed with roots, and the hedgerows were gone and in their place stood hawthorns, hundreds of them, gnarled and leafless though it was barely autumn, their branches hooked like fingers curled around a secret.

The birds had stopped.

He realized it the way you realize a room has gone silent—not the absence itself but the shape it leaves. No larks. No crows scavenging the moor's edge. Just wind, and the creak of the cart, and Bramble's breath gone shallow beside him.

"Hazel." Her voice was very low. "Look at the thorn trees."

"I see them."

"Do you? Look *properly.*"

He looked properly, and his stomach dropped through the black earth.

The hawthorns were arranged in lines. Not planted lines—nothing so innocent—but radiating ones, spokes of a wheel whose hub lay somewhere ahead of them, hidden in the moor's slow rise. And the branches weren't merely hooked. They pointed. Every tree on the western side of the track leaned its crooked limbs east, toward the hub, like a congregation turning to face an altar.

Or like a trap, closing.

"That's not natural growth," Bramble whispered. "That's *directed* growth. Something told these trees what shape to be." Her hand had found the little hammer at her belt, the one she used for clockwork and, when necessary, for skulls. "This is otherworld craft. This is *Gate-side* craft, Hazel, and it is standing in the middle of our world, which means—"

"Which means the seal is leaking." His mouth had gone dry. "I *sealed* it. I checked the wards myself, every season, every—"

"Yes, and I helped you, and I'm telling you that whatever is making those trees lean does not care about your wards." She grabbed the cart's handlebar. "Turn us around. Slowly. Nothing that runs gets to run."

But the moor had already decided.

It began with the smell—wet iron and crushed green stems, the scent of a hedge cut too deep—and then the ground itself moved. What Hazel had taken for root-mounds along the trackside unfolded, jointed and deliberate, and rose on legs like bundle of black briars, taller than a man, taller than two men, shapes woven entirely of thornwood and held together by something older and hungrier than sap. They had no faces. Where a face should be, the branches parted around a hollow, and in each hollow burned a light the color of frost on a grave.

Thorn-wights. He knew them instantly, the way you know a word from a language you swore you'd forgotten. Wardens of the deep thorn. Sentinels of the otherworld's border marches.

Creatures that belonged behind the Gate. Creatures that had never, in all the old stories, crossed it.

"Bramble," he said. "How many?"

"Four visible. Probably six." Her knuckles were white on the hammer. "And they're not looking at me."

They weren't. All four hollow faces, all four grave-lights, were fixed on him with an attention that felt like being read aloud.

"They want the map," Hazel breathed. "No—they want *me*. The map's just paper. Wights don't guard paper."

"Hazel—"

One of them moved. There was no transition, no wind-up, none of the clumsy telegraphing of a beast. One instant it stood forty feet away; the next it was twenty, thorn-legs folding and unfolding like a spider's nightmare, and the frost-light in its hollow face brightened, and Bramble screamed his name, and Hazel did the thing he had sworn on his sister's memory never to do again.

He reached for his magic.

Not the parlour tricks, not the stitched charms and bottled luck he sold to gullible Corvale clerks. The other thing. The thing under his skin, rooted deeper than his name, the wild green current of the otherworld that had come with him through the Gate and lain coiled and starving in his blood for five years while he starved it in turn. He had promised himself—promised *her*, in the only prayer he still knew—that he would never open that door inside himself again. Power was how the otherworld caught you. Power was the hook. He had watched it take better people than himself.

The wight was ten feet away and its thorn-arms were opening like a cage.

*I'm sorry, Maren,* he thought, absurdly, and let go.

It was like opening a vein. The power came up through him in a green flood, sweet and terrible, and for one dizzying heartbeat it felt like *home*, and that was the most frightening part of all. Then he shaped it—roughly, rustily, the way a man swings a sword he hasn't lifted since the war—and drove it into the ground between himself and the wight.

The moor answered.

Blackthorn erupted from the black earth in a wall eight feet high, a hedge thrown up in a single convulsion, and the charging wight hit it at full speed and shattered—not broke, *shattered*, bursting apart into a storm of spinning thorns that rained down hissing and steaming where they touched the living hedge. The other wights stopped. The grave-lights dimmed, flared, dimmed again, and then—this was the part Hazel would remember for the rest of his life, in whatever life he had left—they *bowed*. All three remaining shapes bent their branch-bodies low, the way courtiers bow to a passing lord, and withdrew backward into the moor without turning around, sinking into the root-webbed ground like stones dropped into water until there was nothing left but the hawthorns, leaning, and the silence, total now, absolute.

The green light guttered out of Hazel's hands. His knees followed it down.

He was aware of Bramble crouched beside him, saying words, her voice coming from a great distance. He was aware of the taste of sap in his mouth and the feeling that his skin had been turned inside out and hung on a line. Mostly he was aware of the hawthorns. Because as the power drained out of him, the trees were straightening—one by one, groaning like old ships, their crooked limbs swinging back toward neutral sky—and every one of them, as it settled, turned its crown briefly toward *him*.

As if acknowledging.

As if recognizing.

"Hazel." Bramble's face swam into focus above him, pale beneath the freckles, her amber eyes wide with something he had never seen in them before. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, worse: recognition of her own. "What did you just do? What *are* you? Because wights don't bow. Wights have never bowed to anyone in the whole history of both worlds except—"

"Don't." He pushed himself upright. The world tilted and steadied. "Don't say it."

"—except the thorn-blooded," she finished anyway, because Bramble had never in her long strange life left a sentence unfinished. "Except the old wardens' line. Except *Gatekeeper's* blood."

He got to his feet. He picked up the cart handle. His hands, he noted distantly, were still steady, and he hated them a little for it.

"The map says the route goes through the heart of the moor," he said. "Past the standing stones at Cael Hollow. We make the hollow by dark or we camp in the open, and after what just happened I'd rather not camp in the open."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He started forward, pulling the cart, and the wheels bit into the black springy earth. Behind them the hawthorns watched them go, straightening now, ordinary, innocent, lying.

Bramble fell into step beside him. For a long time neither spoke. Then, quietly, in the tone of someone setting a debt on a ledger where it would keep:

"When we reach somewhere with walls and a locked door, Hazel Dunmore, you are going to tell me why the wights bowed. You are going to tell me what you fled five years ago. And you are going to tell me who *Maren* is—yes, you said the name in your sleep two nights ago, don't make that face—because I have helped you lie to witch-hunters and lie to kings' men and lie to yourself, and I will help you lie to whatever else is coming. But I won't help you lie to *me*."

Ahead, the moor rose toward Cael Hollow, and the standing stones stood against the reddening sky like the fingers of a buried hand.

And on the tallest stone, where no bird had landed all day, something pale and folded sat waiting—patient as a letter, patient as a summons—watching them come.
