## 7. An Audience of Shadows

The summons came at dusk, delivered by a page who would not meet her eyes.

Sera Vane read the folded card twice, then a third time, as though repetition might soften its edges. *Her Majesty's pleasure. The King requires your presence. Alone.* No reason given. No charge named. Just that single word—*alone*—sitting on the parchment like a spider in the center of a web.

In five years of service, she had never been summoned alone. Witch-hunters were questioned in pairs, always, so that testimony could be weighed against testimony and no one's loyalty rested on a single tongue. It was procedure. It was protection. And it had been waived for her without explanation.

She dressed in her formal grays, checked her commission seal twice, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the corsetry.

*You are a hunter,* she reminded herself as the carriage rattled through streets going amber with lamplight. *Hunters do not tremble at being noticed. Being noticed is what we live for.*

But she knew, in the place where she kept the things she did not say aloud—not to her commander, not to her sister, not even to herself in the honest dark before sleep—that this summons was not a reward. Rewards came with fanfare, with commendations read aloud in the hall, with the warm weight of her commander's hand on her shoulder. This came with silence. And in Corvale, silence from the crown was how the crown ate you.

The palace rose out of the evening mist like something grown rather than built. She had thought that many times over the years and never examined the thought, because it was an absurd one: spires that curved where stone should be straight, buttresses that arched like ribs, windows narrow and tall as slitted eyes. Tonight, with fog coiling around its foundations, the palace looked less like architecture and more like the exposed root system of some vast, patient plant.

She was searched at three gates. At the third, a guard ran his hands along her sleeves and paused at her left forearm, where the hunter's brand sat beneath the cloth—a circle bisected by a blade, seared into her skin at her swearing-in.

"Hunter Vane," he said, and there was something strange in his voice. Pity, maybe. Or apology.

"Is there a problem?"

"No, my lady." He stepped back. "Go up. He's waiting."

*He's waiting.* As though the king had nothing else to occupy a winter evening but the interrogation of a mid-ranked witch-hunter with six years' service and no family name worth mentioning.

They took her up through corridors she did not know, past tapestries older than the dynasty, and left her outside a pair of doors carved with thorned branches so lifelike she half expected them to bleed when touched. A steward in livery the color of dried blood opened them without announcement.

The throne room was empty.

That was her first wrongness. Court should have been in session—evening court, the smaller session, but never empty. Aldric had been a king who disliked solitude; the old stewards said he filled his halls with petitioners because silence reminded him of his father's reign. Yet the chamber stretched away from her, forty yards of polished floor reflecting a hundred candles, and the only living thing in it was the figure on the throne at the far end.

Sera walked. Her boots made no sound on the stone, which was impossible—she could hear her own footsteps in these halls on any other night—and the silence pressed against her ears like deep water. Halfway down the room she realized the candle flames were all leaning toward the throne. Every flame in a hundred sconces, bent like grass in wind, though the air was perfectly still.

She stopped at the prescribed distance and knelt, head bowed, one knee to stone gone cold as riverbed pebbles.

"Rise, Hunter Vane."

The voice was Aldric's voice. She had heard it a dozen times from the gallery during public audiences—warm, a little rough, the voice of a man who had once, famously, laughed until he cried at a mummer's play about his own coronation. This was that voice. Exactly that voice.

Which was the second wrongness. Because it came out flat. Reproduced. Like a song played by someone who had heard it once through a wall.

She rose.

King Aldric sat with his hands folded on the armrests, watching her. He wore the winter crown—iron worked into holly leaves—and robes of state she'd last seen at the Remembrance Day ceremony. His face was the face from the coins and the portraits: long-jawed, gray-eyed, creased at the corners by genuine smiles that had once been common enough to be unremarkable.

And yet.

"Your report on the charm-house raid," the king said. "Ashen Lane. Read it to me."

Sera's stomach dropped through the floor. She had filed that report four days ago. She had falsified it four days ago—the first lie of her career, ink still fresh enough to smear if she licked her thumb—and she had felt, all week, the sensation of Commander Hale's eyes on her back as she wrote it. But the report itself was sealed. Filed. Buried in the registry under her seal and his countersignature.

"You have it before you, Majesty," she said carefully. "I submitted it Tuesday last."

"I know what I have before me." Something moved behind the king's eyes—not anger, nothing so human. Interest. The way a scholar leans toward a dissection. "Read it to me. Aloud. I wish to hear it in your voice."

There was no refusing. There was no *reasoning* with whatever this was. Sera drew a breath and recited from memory, because she had written those words and rewritten them and they were carved into her now:

"The premises at Ashen Lane contained no implements of forbidden craft. Household charms only. Dream-sleep tokens of a type commonly sold to children. No arrests were made. The structure was lost to fire before full inventory could be taken."

Silence. The candle flames leaned further.

"And the children's toys," the king said softly. "Describe them."

"Majesty—"

"*Describe them.*"

"A wooden horse," she said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Painted blue. A rag doll with button eyes. A tin whistle. Each one charmed—for good dreams, Majesty. Nothing more. The maker used no harmful craft. The children who owned them will dream badly tonight, and every night after, because their house burned down."

She hadn't meant to say that last part. It fell out of her like a stone from a crumbling wall, and she stood there in the terrible quiet waiting for it to kill her.

The king tilted his head. The motion was smooth, fluid, correct—and somehow wrong, the way a puppet's movements are correct. A fraction too graceful. A joint bending at an angle joints don't quite go.

"Dreams," he repeated. "Yes. Dreams are difficult to govern. Do you know what I dreamed last night, Hunter Vane?"

"No, Majesty."

"Neither do I." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a mask fits a face—precisely, completely, and hollow underneath. "I find I don't dream at all anymore. Isn't that restful?"

Every instinct Sera possessed was screaming at her to kneel, to agree, to laugh politely at the king's small joke the way courtiers had laughed at his jokes for years. Instead she heard herself ask:

"How long, Majesty?"

The smile did not waver. But the temperature of the room dropped—not a draft, nothing so explicable. The cold came up through the floor, out of the walls, as though the building itself had exhaled.

"How long since what?" the king asked pleasantly.

"Since Your Majesty slept. Since Your Majesty—" *cast a shadow*, she almost said, and stopped herself at the very brink, because she remembered now, she had been trying not to remember for weeks: the Remembrance Day ceremony, the king descending the cathedral steps at noon with the sun high behind him, and the long shadow that should have stretched before him simply *not there*. She had told herself it was the angle. She had told herself a great many things.

The king rose from the throne.

He descended the dais steps slowly, unhurried, robes whispering against stone that gave back no echo of his footfall either. Up close, in the leaning candlelight, she could see that his skin was flawless. Not healthy-flawless. *Unweathered*-flawless. A man of fifty with no line on his face that time had put there, as though someone had described wrinkles to an artist who had never seen one.

"You are wondering," the king said, stopping an arm's length away, "whether you are speaking to Aldric."

"Majesty, I would never presume—"

"Answer honestly. You've discovered a taste for honesty tonight. I could smell it on your report." He leaned in—not close enough to touch, but near enough that she caught his scent, and it was wrong too: cold stone and old smoke and something sweetly rotten underneath, like flowers pressed too long between the pages of a book. "So let us test it. A game, Hunter Vane. Kings love games. Answer me truly, and all is well."

"What game?" Her voice came out steady. She would remember later, with disbelief, that it came out steady.

"When I was nine years old," the king said, "there was a storm. My father kept me in the west tower through all of it, and I was frightened, and someone came to sit with me until morning. Who was it?"

Sera's blood turned to ice water.

This was the test. Of course it was. Questions only the true king could answer—a verification, a shibboleth, exactly the sort of thing a usurper or an impostor would need to survive. If the thing wearing Aldric's face answered rightly, then perhaps she was mad. Perhaps the shadows and the stillness and the leaning flames were all exhaustion and nerves, and tomorrow she would request a transfer to the border garrisons and think no more about it.

If it answered wrongly—

The king watched her with gray eyes that reflected the candlelight like glass reflects it: brightly, and with nothing behind the brightness.

"Well?" he prompted, courteous as a host offering wine. "You must know the story. Everyone knows the story. It's in all the chronicles. The loyal servant who comforted the frightened prince. Tell me, Hunter Vane—who was it?"

"That is not my place to answer, Majesty," Sera said. "The question was put to me."

Something flickered across the king's face. For one instant—one heartbeat—she saw beneath the mask. Not a monster. Worse than a monster: *hunger*. A vast, patient appetite looking out through the eye-holes of a stolen face, weighing her, tasting her fear the way a man tastes soup for salt.

Then the mask settled back into place, and Aldric smiled his flawless smile.

"His kennel-master," the king said. "A man called Dree. That is the chronicle's answer. It is wrong. It was a scullery girl named Maren, who was dismissed the following spring for telling the prince stories of the otherworld, and whom my father ruined so thoroughly that no record of her survives anywhere in Corvale." He spread his hands, apologetic, almost boyish. "You see my difficulty, Hunter Vane? I know the true answer. But no one alive can confirm it. So how am I to prove to you that I am your king?"

The floor seemed to tilt beneath her. Sera locked her knees.

Because that was not the answer of an impostor. An impostor would have said *Dree*. Any creature wearing the king's face, any thief of crowns, would give the chronicle's answer and pass the test and send her home reassured. Only the true Aldric—or something that had been *inside* Aldric so long it wore his memories like its own skin—could produce Maren.

Unless.

Unless it wanted her to believe. Unless the wrongness she had tasted was the trap itself, and this whole audience—the empty hall, the leaning flames, the confession of the scullery girl—was theater staged for an audience of one, designed to make her doubt everything except the thing on the throne.

*It lets me leave,* she thought wildly. *Whatever this is, it will let me walk out of here. Why? Why let me go?*

"Your Majesty," she managed, "I am a simple hunter. I hunt what the law names forbidden. I have found nothing tonight that the law names."

"No," the king agreed gently. "You haven't. Because the law is mine, and I have not yet named it." He returned to the throne and sat, folding his hands, becoming once more the portrait, the coin, the king. "You may go, Hunter Vane. Thank you for your candor regarding the toys. It was... nourishing."

*Nourishing.* The word followed her to the doors like a hand on the back of her neck.

At the threshold she stopped. She did not decide to stop; her body simply refused to carry her one step further without turning. She looked back down the length of the empty, candlelit hall.

"Your shadow, Majesty," she said. "On the cathedral steps. At Remembrance Day. I noticed it was—"

"Gone?" The king regarded her with mild, terrible patience. "Yes. I gave it away. Or it was taken—I confess I no longer recall which. Do you know what remains, Hunter Vane, when a man's shadow is taken?" He lifted one hand, and the hundred leaning flames straightened all at once, every candle in the hall standing rigid as soldiers at attention. "Neither does he. That is rather the point."

The doors closed behind her without being touched.

* * *

She did not remember the carriage ride home. She remembered fragments: the mist, the lamplight swimming, her own hands white-knuckled on the seat's edge, the sensation of having narrowly survived something that had decided, for reasons of its own, not to eat her.

In her rooms above the registry office, she lit no lamps. She sat in the dark at her little table with her commission seal in front of her—the wax-stamped disc, the circle bisected by a blade, the emblem she had sworn her life to at nineteen—and stared at it until her eyes burned.

*The law is mine, and I have not yet named it.*

She thought of the burned house on Ashen Lane. Of the blue wooden horse in the ash. Of Bramble—if that was even the creature's real name—chained in the cells below the registry, awaiting questioning, humming fae tunes through split lips while hunters walked overhead. Of Commander Hale watching her falsify her first report with an expression she had spent four days refusing to interpret.

Of the chronicle answer and the true answer. Of a scullery girl erased so completely that even the king who destroyed her had needed a stranger to confirm she'd existed.

*How am I to prove to you that I am your king?*

It had asked her the question as though it genuinely wanted to know. As though proof mattered to it. As though somewhere inside the hunger and the courtesy there was a thing that had once been a man, buried so deep under five years of something else that even it had lost the seam between them.

Or—as though it wanted her convinced. Wanted a hunter, one specific hunter, carrying a specific doubt back out into the city like a lit coal in her pocket.

Both possibilities ended the same way: with her knowing. With the knowledge sitting in her chest like swallowed glass, irreversible, incommunicable. Who could she tell? Hale, who reported directly to the palace? The registry, whose archives the thing on the throne could open with a word? There was no one above her whose loyalty ran deeper than their fear, and she had just seen what lived above everyone.

Terror wrapped in courtesy. That was what she had met tonight. And it had thanked her for her honesty, and let her keep her life, and both of those mercies were more frightening than any execution, because mercy implied *use*. Things discarded things they had no use for.

Near midnight, a knock at her door. She went cold, hand drifting to the knife under her cloak.

A slip of paper lay on the threshold—no messenger in sight, the corridor empty in both directions. She picked it up with two fingers, as though it might bite.

Three words, in a cramped, hurried hand she did not recognize:

*The gate remembers.*

She stood in her dark doorway for a long time, the paper trembling in her grip, listening to the city breathe around her—the bells, the distant dogs, the ordinary human noise of ten thousand people who did not know what wore their king.

Then she folded the note into her breast pocket, beside the seal she was beginning to suspect she'd soon have no right to wear, and began, very quietly and methodically, to plan how a witch-hunter might go about asking a prisoner questions her masters did not want asked.

Below the registry, in the dark, something hummed a fae tune—and waited.
