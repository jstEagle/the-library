## 8. The Inn at Hollowmere

The rain found them an hour before the inn did.

It came down the moor in grey sheets, turning the road to porridge and the world to a smear of heather and stone, and by the time the lights of Hollowmere swam out of the wet dark, Hazel had forgotten what it felt like to be dry. Bramble walked ahead of him with her hood up and her satchel clutched to her chest like a grumpy child, muttering in a language that predated the city of Corvale and most of its sins.

"There," she said, pointing a copper-tipped finger at the glow. "The Hollowmere. See the sign? The boar with the lantern in its tusks."

"I see it."

"Then walk faster, hedge-witch. My joints are older than this road, and twice as bitter."

The inn was low and broad-shouldered, built of black timber that had gone silver at the edges, with a stable leaning against one side like a drunk against a wall. Smoke curled from its chimney and light spilled yellow from its windows across the puddled yard, and to Hazel, soaked to the bone and three days out of Corvale with witch-hunters somewhere behind them, it looked like the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

He made himself stop at the threshold and look at it properly. That was the old discipline—the first thing he'd taught himself when he came through the gate five years ago and decided to become no one. *Look at every place like you might have to leave it burning behind you.* Note the doors. Note the windows. Note how many people are inside.

Two windows lit downstairs. A stable boy hauling water. One rider's horse already in the yard, a tall grey mare with a scholar's saddlebags, mud-flecked but well kept.

One stranger. He could handle one stranger.

* * *

Inside, the Hollowmere was all warmth and noise and the smell of onion stew and wet wool. A dozen farmers and drovers crowded the long tables, arguing about sheep prices over weak beer, and a fire big enough to roast an ox cracked in the hearth. The innkeeper, a broad woman with flour on her forearms, took their coins without looking closely at their faces—border folk had long ago learned not to look closely at anyone's face—and gave them a corner table near the wall, where Hazel could sit with his back to the stone and see the whole room.

Old habits. Old, necessary habits.

Bramble ordered stew for three before Hazel could stop her.

"We're two," he said.

"You're two," she said, "and I am a fae-tinkerer who has not eaten a hot meal since Corvale, which is a crime against my people. Besides." Her eyes, bright as new pennies, flicked once toward the far end of the room. "We may be three before the night is out."

He followed her glance. The grey mare's owner sat alone at a table by the window: a woman perhaps thirty, dressed in travelling clothes too fine for the moor and too worn for a court, with a stack of books bound in oilcloth beside her cup. She was writing—had been writing, Hazel realized, for some time, her pen moving steadily while the whole room roared around her. Dark hair pinned back carelessly. Ink on her fingers. When she looked up—and she looked up directly at him, without pretense, without the slow sideways approach people used when they were pretending not to stare—she smiled.

Hazel looked away first. It cost him something to do it.

"Hedge-witch," Bramble said quietly, and there was no teasing in her voice now. "You're staring at the wall like it insulted you."

"I'm not staring at anything."

"Mm. And I'm a duchess." She tore her bread in half and pushed the larger piece at him. "Eat. You've been wound tight since Thornfield, tighter than a crossbow left strung. Whatever is coiled in you, this inn is not going to spring it loose."

But something was coiled in him, and it had been coiled for years, and the fire and the stew-smell and the ordinary human racket of the place seemed designed to loosen it. Five years of being invisible. Five years of selling luck-knots and dream-charms to people who never learned his name, of keeping his head down and his magic buried so deep he sometimes dreamed it had died. Five years of *alone*, worn smooth as a river stone, so familiar he'd mistaken it for safety.

And now a woman with ink-stained fingers had smiled at him across a room, and his chest ached with a longing so sudden and stupid it frightened him more than the thorn-wights had.

*Don't,* he told himself. *You don't know her. You don't know why a lone traveller with a scholar's saddlebags is on this road in this season.*

He ate his stew. He did not look at her again.

She came to him anyway.

* * *

"Forgive the intrusion," she said, appearing at their table with her book under her arm and her cup in hand, "but you're the charm-seller from Corvale, aren't you? Hazel Dunmore."

Every muscle in Hazel's body went still. Beside him, Bramble's spoon paused halfway to her mouth.

"I'm sorry," the woman said, laughing softly at whatever she saw in his face. "That sounded like a threat. It wasn't meant to be. Odile Marchetti—I'm a scholar, late of the University at Vell. I was in Corvale three weeks ago, researching the old gate-cults, and I bought a luck-knot from your stall. Blue thread. You told me it was 'harmless as bread,' and I said that was exactly what worried me about it." She set her cup down. "I remember faces. It's a professional failing."

Hazel made himself breathe. Three weeks ago. A stall in the Tanners' Row market, hundreds of customers a day, and she remembered his face and his joke and the color of the thread. Either she had a scholar's memory or a hunter's, and both wore the same smile.

"That was me," he said carefully. "Small city. Lots of knots."

"Lots of knots," she agreed, eyes crinkling, "and one knot-seller who flinched when a customer mentioned the weather, because weather-talk is how hunters open conversations. I noticed that too. I notice everything; it's my other professional failing." She glanced at Bramble, inclined her head. "Madam. Your companion has the look of the elder folk about her, if you'll forgive the observation. I've read half the surviving texts on the Thorn Gate, and every one of them says the gatekeepers kept fae counsel."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. *Thorn Gate.* Said plainly, warmly, in a border inn full of strangers, loud enough for anyone to hear.

Bramble's spoon went down with a small, deliberate click. "Scholar," she said, "you have a remarkable talent for saying dangerous things in a friendly voice."

"And you have a remarkable talent for hearing them," Odile said pleasantly. She pulled out a chair uninvited and sat, arranging her book on the table. "So let me be plainer, since plainness seems to be valued here. I know what you are, Master Dunmore—or rather, I know what you're rumored to be. There are stories coming down the moor road. Stories about a charm-seller who fled Corvale with a dead man's map, pursued by the king's own hunters. Stories about thorn-wights walking on Thornfield, which haven't been seen on this side of the veil in a hundred years." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped, warm and urgent. "I've spent nine years studying the Thorn Gate from the outside. Nine years of reading secondhand accounts written by frightened monks. And now the gate is waking, and the man who can reach it is sitting in front of me drinking bad beer. Do you understand what I'm offering?"

"No," Hazel said. "Tell me."

"Passage. Safe passage. I know these borders better than any cartographer alive—the ward-stones, the toll-paths, the ways around the hunters' checkpoints. I'll take you to the gate myself. All I ask—" and here she smiled again, that open, disarming smile, "—is to come with you when you open it. To see it. To write down what's truly there. That's all I've ever wanted. Truth. The real thing, after a lifetime of copies."

For one treacherous moment, Hazel wanted it so badly his teeth hurt. Someone who knew. Someone who wasn't afraid of him, who spoke of the gate like a wonder instead of a crime, who looked at him—*him*, the invisible man, the charm-seller, the coward—as though he were the answer to nine years of patient searching. She was clever and warm and she had smiled at him first, and some starved part of him reached toward all of it like a hand toward a fire.

Then Bramble kicked him under the table, hard enough to bruise.

"What my companion means to say," Bramble said, "is that we are honored, and we will consider your generous offer while we finish our stew, which is getting cold."

"Of course." Odile rose, gathering her book, and her smile didn't waver. "Consider it overnight. We're all staying here tonight, I think—the rain won't lift till morning, and neither should anyone." She paused, and added, gently: "The rooms upstairs. Mine is the third door. If you decide you want safe passage, knock anytime." She touched two fingers to her brow, a scholar's salute, and returned to her table by the window.

Hazel watched her go. Watched her sit, dip her pen, resume writing as though nothing had happened—as though she hadn't just peeled his disguise off in a public room and offered him the thing he'd wanted for five years.

"Well," he said.

"Yes," said Bramble.

"She knew me. From one purchase. Three weeks ago."

"Mm."

"Bramble." He turned to face her fully. "How much do scholars make at the University at Vell?"

"Less than they deserve and more than they admit." She stirred her stew, not eating it. "And how much does a spy make, hedge-witch? For a name, a face, a route? Enough to buy a fine grey mare and books bound in oilcloth, I'd wager. Enough to sit alone in an inn writing letters by the window." Her penny-bright eyes lifted to his. "She named the Thorn Gate aloud in a room full of strangers. A true scholar of forbidden things learns to whisper. She wanted someone to hear."

"Or she's careless. Or brave. Or telling the truth."

"Or all three. The best lies are mostly true—that's what makes them work." Bramble finally ate a mouthful, chewing slowly. "Her boots, Hazel. Did you see her boots?"

"No."

"Travelled hard, but recently resoled. City cobbler's work—Corvale stitching, if I know leather, and I know leather. A university pays poorly. A crown pays well." She set down her spoon. "I'm not saying she's a hunter. Hunters smell of iron and certainty. But a spy? A spy smells of kindness. Kindness opens doors that fear cannot."

Hazel looked across the room. Odile had pushed the lamp aside; the firelight caught her profile as she wrote, unhurried, absorbed, ink on her fingers, exactly what she claimed to be. She felt his gaze and looked up, and this time she didn't smile—she simply held his eyes for a moment, steady and unafraid, then returned to her page.

*Trust me,* said everything about her. *I am exactly what I seem.*

That was the problem. Everyone was exactly what they seemed, right up until the moment they weren't. His sister had been exactly what she seemed, right up until the day he'd run and left her standing at the gate.

"I half-believe her," he admitted quietly. "That's the worst part. I want her to be true."

"I know," Bramble said, and her voice gentled. "Loneliness makes a lovely lockpick. It slips into you quiet, and turns, and before you know it the door's open and something's walked in wearing a friendly face." She covered his hand with hers, small and dry and strong. "So we do neither the stupid thing nor the cruel thing. We don't trust her, and we don't turn her away. We half-trust her. We let her guide us on roads we can verify, and we tell her nothing she could sell. Can you do that? Half-trust?"

Hazel exhaled. "Half-trust," he said. "Like half a map."

"Exactly like half a map. And we both know what half a map is worth." She patted his hand and stood, joints crackling. "Off to bed with you. And Hazel—if you go knocking on the third door tonight, knock with your wits, not your heart."

"I hadn't planned to knock at all."

"No," Bramble said. "But you'd thought about it. So had she."

* * *

He couldn't sleep.

The room above the inn was small and clean, rain drumming on the shutter, and Hazel lay on the narrow bed with the torn map beneath his shirt, against his skin, where it had lived since the ford. Half a map. Bramble's other half was sewn into her coat lining, and between them they held the way to the Thorn Gate, and somewhere south of here Sera Vane held Bramble's freedom and a prisoner who knew the wraith's name.

He turned the evening over and over, the way he turned charms in his hands, looking for the flaw. Odile Marchetti, who remembered a blue thread. Who named the gate aloud. Whose boots were resoled in Corvale stitch. Who had appeared on this road, in this season, at this exact inn, three days behind two fugitives—unless she'd been ahead of them all along, waiting, because the road to the gate ran through Hollowmere and only through Hollowmere, and anyone who knew the map would know that too.

Or she was exactly what she said. A lonely scholar who'd waited nine years for the gate to wake.

Both things could be true. That was the trap of it. He couldn't find the lie because there might not be one—not a whole lie, just stitches of truth sewn over a frame of intent, the way a good charm hid a working in harmless words.

Somewhere past midnight, he heard footsteps in the corridor. Soft ones, pausing outside his door. Not entering. Just... pausing. Then receding toward the third door, and a latch clicking shut.

He lay in the dark with his heart going like a hammer and understood, with terrible clarity, that he was not the only one in this inn deciding whom to trust.

* * *

Morning came grey and rinsed-clean, the rain gone, mist lying in the hollows like spilled milk. Hazel came downstairs with his pack strapped and found the common room half-empty, breakfast smoke in the air—and Odile at the door, cloaked and ready, holding her grey mare's reins, talking easily with the innkeeper as though she'd known her for years.

She saw him and smiled, and this time there was something almost shy in it, which was either genuine or the finest piece of craft he'd ever witnessed.

"The roads are clear," she said. "There's a path north through the fells that skips the checkpoint at Miller's Ford entirely—hunters stopped using it after the rockfall, but it's sound enough for horses and careful feet. If you're for the gate, that's your road. I leave within the hour, whether or not you ride with me." She hesitated, then added, quieter: "I meant what I said last night, Master Dunmore. I've been alone with this work a long time. I suspect you know what that's like."

He did. That was the knife of it.

Behind him, Bramble descended the stairs slowly, watching Odile with an expression carved from amber—stillness with something ancient caught inside it. Then the fae-tinkerer sighed, a sound like wind through dry reeds, and said:

"The fells path, then. And madam—you ride ahead of us, and you wait at each turning until we catch sight of you. And you tell us nothing of yourself that we cannot check."

Odile laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her. "Fair terms. Distrustful, but fair."

"Distrust keeps the living alive," Bramble said. "Trust is for afterward, if there is an afterward."

They saddled up in the yard under a sky the color of pewter. As Hazel swung onto the borrowed farm horse the innkeeper had sold them at a scandalous price, he caught Odile watching him—not the searching look from last night, but something softer, more curious, and for a heartbeat he let himself imagine that it was honest.

Then he remembered the footsteps outside his door at midnight, and the pause, and the click of the third door's latch, and he rode out of Hollowmere with his heart split cleanly down the middle: half of it hoping she was true, and half of it already mourning what it would cost him if she wasn't.

Three miles along the fells path, Odile reined in at the first turning and waited for them, as promised, silhouetted against the mist. And as they drew level with her, she pointed back the way they'd come—down into the hollow, where the road to Hollowmere lay silver and empty in the morning light.

"You should know," she said, conversationally, "that a company of the king's hunters passed through the village an hour before dawn. Six of them, and a woman leading them with a scarred jaw. They asked the innkeeper about a charm-seller and an old fae woman." She met Hazel's eyes, calm as still water. "I told her you were my hired guides, bound for Vell. She believed me—for now. But she'll be on this road behind us by noon, and the fells path only buys us until dusk."

Hazel's blood went cold. *A woman with a scarred jaw.* Sera Vane, riding not three hours behind them—with Bramble's freedom in her keeping and questions of her own.

"So," Odile said, gathering her reins, her smile gone sharp and bright as a blade drawn in sunlight. "Shall we ride?"
