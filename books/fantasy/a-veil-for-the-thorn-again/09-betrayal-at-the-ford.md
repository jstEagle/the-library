## 9. Betrayal at the Ford

The rain came in off the moor like a debt collector.

Hazel felt it first as a change in the wind's texture—thornfield weather had a smell to it, iron and wet peat and something older underneath, something that reminded him of a door he'd rather forget. He pulled his hood lower and watched the river come into view through the grey: the Hollowmere ford, where the water ran shallow over pale stones and the far bank dissolved into birch trees that had no business growing this far north.

Bramble crouched at the treeline beside him, copper goggles pushed up on her forehead, her mechanical fox whirring softly against her hip. She hadn't spoken in an hour. That was how he knew she was worried. Bramble filled silence the way other people filled rooms—with noise, with complaint, with the endless small percussion of her craft. When she went quiet, the quiet had weight.

"You're staring at the water," she said finally.

"I'm staring at the other side of the water."

"Same thing. Water's just the argument between them." She adjusted a dial on the fox's collar without looking at it. "You're thinking about Odile."

He was. He hated that he was. Three days since the inn at Hollowmere, three days of Odile's laugh and her ink-stained fingers and the way she'd quoted old gatekeeper verse like she'd been born to it, and Bramble's warning still sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. *She's a spy for the crown, Hazel. I've seen her handwriting on crown dispatches.* And yet he'd slept easier those three nights than he had in five years, and some traitorous part of him kept insisting that a person could be a spy and still mean the things she said over breakfast.

"People can be two things at once," he'd told Bramble.

"So can knives," she'd said.

Now, crouched in the rain with the ford ahead and the map—whole, intact, *still whole*—pressed flat inside his coat, Hazel tried to feel grateful instead of foolish. They were nearly through Thornfield. Another day's walk and they'd be past the moor, past the reach of the Corvale hunters, into hill country where the king's writ thinned to nothing.

"The crossing's shallow here," he said. "But we'll be exposed for a hundred yards. No cover once we're on the stones."

"Then we cross fast and argue later." Bramble's jaw tightened. "I don't like it. Wights don't hunt in daylight, but daylight doesn't stop men."

"Hunters won't have found us. Not this fast."

"You believe that?"

He wanted to say yes. What came out was: "I want to."

Bramble snorted. The fox's ears swiveled. And then the fox's ears swiveled again, toward the birches behind them, and its whole body went rigid as a drawn bow, and Hazel understood, in one cold drop of certainty, that the wanting had never mattered at all.

* * *

They came out of the trees on horseback, six of them, and the rain made their black coats gleam like oiled leather. At their head rode a woman in hunter's grey with a commission seal at her throat and a face that even at forty yards looked carved out of the same stone as her orders.

Hazel knew her by reputation only—Sera Vane, the commander's favored blade, the one who burned charm-houses clean and asked few questions. He'd imagined her crueler. There was nothing cruel in the way she sat her horse; there was something almost reluctant, a coiled hesitation, as if she were riding toward a thing she hadn't yet agreed to do.

It didn't matter. Reluctant blades cut all the same.

"Run," Bramble breathed.

They ran.

The ford took them ankle-deep, knee-deep, the current shoving at their legs like impatient hands. Behind them hooves hit the near bank, splashing, and a voice—Vane's voice, level as a ledger—called out: "Hazel Dunmore! By order of the crown, stand!"

He did not stand. He grabbed Bramble's wrist and hauled her forward through the water, and the world narrowed to the slap of rain and the scream of the fox mechanism spinning up to full power and the half-thought, repeating, *Odile, Odile, you fool, you absolute fool*—

Because there was no other way they'd been found. Not this fast, not this precisely, not at the one ford the torn map's route offered. Bramble had warned him. Bramble had shown him the handwriting. And he'd sat across from Odile Voss in the inn's yellow lamplight and let himself be lonely at her, let himself believe that being seen was the same as being safe.

A crossbow bolt hissed past his ear and buried itself in the shallows with a sound like a snapped bone.

"They're shooting!" Bramble shrieked. "At the *ford*, are they mad, someone'll see—"

"No one sees anything on Thornfield." Hazel's voice came out flat and strange to him. "That's why they chose it."

They reached the far bank. Birch trunks closed around them, white and wet, and for one breath—one single, stupid breath—Hazel thought they might make it.

Then the ground moved.

Not the ground. What grew from it. Thorns, long and black and wrong, erupting from the mossy soil in a tangle that shouldn't exist on this side of any gate, and through them came shapes that made Hazel's stomach drop through the soles of his boots. Thorn-wights. Three of them, four, unfolding out of the briars like spiders out of a web, twig-limbed and hollow-eyed, wearing the suggestion of faces the way a scarecrow wears a coat.

"Those aren't possible," Bramble whispered. "Those crossed the gate? Nothing crosses the gate. *We sealed it.*"

"I know what we sealed." His mouth had gone dry. "Something's letting them out."

The wights fanned through the birches, cutting off the slope, and behind them the hunters were dismounting at the ford's edge, and Hazel stood in the narrow space between two kinds of death and felt five years of careful, deliberate smallness crumble around him like wet paper.

There was no way through this without magic. He had sworn off magic. He had built a whole life—a shop, a sign, a name that meant nothing to anyone—on the foundation of never touching it again.

The wights lunged.

* * *

Later, he would not remember deciding. He would remember the moment before deciding: Bramble going down under the first wight, her goggles flying, the fox mechanism shrieking sparks, and the raw animal knowledge that he would burn every vow he owned before he watched her die on a rainy moor because of his sentimentality.

Magic came up through him like floodwater through a cellar door.

It wasn't the gentle stuff—the charm-craft, the luck-knots, the little warm workings he sold to superstitious grandmothers. This was the other kind, the deep kind, the kind that remembered the otherworld's grammar. He spoke a word he hadn't spoken in five years and the rain itself turned to needles around him, a whirling shell of stinging silver, and the nearest wight recoiled with a sound like green wood splitting.

"Dunmore!" Vane's voice, closer now, sharp with something that wasn't quite triumph. "Witchcraft! You've confirmed it yourself!"

"Yes!" he shouted back, absurdly, furiously. "Well done! Write it down!"

The second wight came low, along the ground, and he caught it with a working that unmade the water in its twig-body, shriveling it to drifting ash. The third circled wide—and that was when Hazel saw the child.

A boy. Eight, maybe nine, barefoot, soaked to the skin, frozen mid-flight on the slope above the ford where he'd clearly been doing whatever moor children did in the rain—fishing the shallows, following the horses' noise. Directly in the path of the circling wight. Directly in the path of the hunters' next volley.

Everything happened very quickly after that, and Hazel's body made most of the decisions.

He threw himself up the slope. The wight wheeled toward the movement—toward the easier prey—and Hazel got there first, arms closing around the boy, twisting to put his own back between the creature and the child, and the wight's claw raked down across his shoulder in a line of fire that he barely felt because he was already speaking the banishing words, the ones Father Ilex would have recognized, the ones that belonged to the old rites, and the wight came apart like a rotted fence in a gale.

The boy was screaming. Hazel held him anyway, held him until the screaming gentled into sobbing, and pressed him down behind a fallen birch. "Stay. Don't look. Whatever you hear, don't look."

Then he turned, shoulder bleeding, magic still crackling off him like static, and found Sera Vane ten feet away with her sword drawn and her crossbowmen flanking her—and found her not firing.

She was looking at the boy. At the hiding place. At Hazel, who had spent his one free moment not attacking her, not fleeing, but shielding a stranger's child with his own back.

Her sword point dipped an inch. Her eyes went somewhere distant, somewhere internal, and Hazel watched—actually watched—doubt move across her face like cloud-shadow over the moor.

"Commander!" one of the crossbowmen barked. "The witch is unguarded—"

"I can see that, Tallow." But she didn't give the order. The rain ticked against her grey shoulders. Somewhere downslope, Bramble screamed.

* * *

Hazel heard the scream and the world went red at the edges.

He left Vane standing. He left the boy. He crashed down through the birches to find Bramble on her knees between two hunters, her wrists already ringed in suppression-irons, the fox mechanism crushed under a boot heel, its glass eye staring up at the rain. A third hunter held her hair. She was fighting—not uselessly, she'd bitten someone, there was blood on somebody's chin—but she was fighting a losing arithmetic, and the arithmetic was about to finish.

"Bramble—"

"Don't you dare," she snarled at him, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "Don't you *dare* be stupid about this. Go. GO. The map matters more than me, you great soft-hearted idiot, the map is the only—"

A hunter struck her across the mouth. She went quiet. Not silent—never silent, even bleeding she was muttering curses in three languages—but quiet enough that Hazel heard, beneath everything, the sound of his own choice arriving.

He could fight six hunters. Maybe. With the deep magic, spending himself like a drunkard spends coin, and maybe win, and maybe kill men who were only following orders, and maybe the boy on the slope would see all of it, and maybe Vane would put a bolt through him while he worked. Or he could take the map and run, and hate himself in a new register for the rest of his life.

"Hazel." Bramble spat blood into the mud and looked at him with her bright, furious, clear-sighted eyes. "The gate. Think of the *gate*."

The hunters closed in around her. Vane came down the slope at a walk, sword sheathed now, and something in her expression had gone carefully blank—the blankness of a woman filing something away that she intended to examine later, alone, at length.

"Take the fae," she said. "Leave the witch. He's not going anywhere."

*Not going anywhere.* It wasn't mercy. It was arithmetic too—he was wounded, exhausted, marked. But Hazel heard the seam in it, the place where her order should have been *kill the witch* and wasn't, and he tucked that seam away next to the memory of her lowered sword-point, because he was learning, badly and late, that people could be two things at once.

The last thing Bramble said to him, as they dragged her toward the horses, was not a curse or a farewell. She twisted in their grip and shouted it across the rain, loud enough for the hunters, loud enough for the moor:

"HALF THE MAP IS STILL A LIE, HAZEL! CHECK THE FOLD! CHECK THE *FOLD*!"

Then the trees swallowed her, and the hoofbeats faded, and Hazel Dunmore stood alone in a dripping birch wood with blood running down his arm and fury sitting in his chest like a second heart.

* * *

He walked until dark. He didn't remember choosing a direction; his feet chose, following the map-route on instinct while his mind replayed it all in merciless loops. Odile's laugh. Odile's hands quoting verse. *So can knives.* The bolt in the shallows. Bramble's mouth bleeding. The boy's sobs behind the birch. Vane's sword-point dipping, dipping, not falling.

By full dark he'd found a hollow under a rock shelf, and he built no fire, and he took out the map with shaking hands.

Half the map. Half the map was still a lie. What had she meant?

He unfolded it by touch, by the faint grey light of the rain-thinned clouds, and examined the creases. The paper was old, old enough that the folds had become part of its structure—and there, along the deepest fold, he felt it before he saw it: a thickness. A second sheet, glued hair-fine against the first, invisible unless you knew to split the layers.

His heart was pounding as he worked a fingernail into the seam. The backing layer peeled away in a whisper.

And there it was—the true map, the whole map, the dead stranger's real legacy—showing not just the Thorn Gate but what lay beyond it. The thorned roads. The court of briars. And at the center, drawn in ink that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at it, a palace of black glass with a root running down from its throne-room floor, down and down, through the world's floor, into a darkness labeled in the old gatekeepers' script with a single word.

Hazel read the word. Then he read it again, because the first time his eyes had refused it.

*Veilwraith.*

Below it, smaller, in a different hand—a hurried hand, a dying hand—the stranger had written a final line, and the rain on the rock above sounded suddenly very loud:

*It knows your name. It has always known your name. It wants you to open the gate.*

Hazel sat in the dark with the map in his lap and the blood drying on his shoulder, and the guilt and the fury rose together in his throat until he couldn't tell which was which—guilt for Bramble, guilt for the boy, guilt for five years of hiding while something wore a king's face and fed; fury at Odile, at the hunters, at the dead man whose secrets had landed in his unwilling hands, at himself, most of all at himself, for every time he'd chosen the small safe life over the hard true one.

Somewhere south of him, Bramble was in irons. Somewhere north, a gate waited. Somewhere in Corvale, a shadowless king sat on a throne grown over a root.

And the thing beneath the throne, according to a dead man's hidden ink, had been waiting for *him*.

Hazel folded the true map along its new creases, tucked it against his heart, and stood up in the rain.

He was done running from both worlds. He was going back for the key.
