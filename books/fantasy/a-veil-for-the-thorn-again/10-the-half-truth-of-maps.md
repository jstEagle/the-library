## 10. The Half-Truth of Maps

They brought the prisoner in at dusk, when the lamps of the interrogation wing burned low and orange, and Sera Vane understood immediately why Commander Hesk had chosen her.

Not because she was the best questioner in the Order of the Crown's Vigil—though she was, or had been. Because the prisoner was fae, and fae unsettled everyone else. They saw the pointed ears beneath the wild grey hair and the copper rings on every finger, and their hands shook on their quills. Sera's hands did not shake. She told herself that was professionalism. She told herself a great many things that week.

"Name," she said, when the guards had chained the fae-tinkerer to the iron chair and withdrawn.

"Bramble, if you're asking nicely. *Prisoner 447* if you're not." The voice was dry as autumn leaves, with a lilt to it that didn't belong to any human tongue. Old eyes, the colour of river-stone, regarded her without fear. "You're the one from the ford. The one who hesitated."

"I don't hesitate."

"You do. I watched you do it. It was the most interesting thing that happened all day, and I was being shot at." Bramble tested the manacles, found them warded—the iron etched with suppression runes—and settled back with the air of someone making themselves comfortable for a long stay. "So. Ask your questions, witch-hunter. I've nothing to hide that you'd believe anyway."

Sera opened the folder. The report of the ambush at the ford lay on top: two witch-hunters dead, three wounded, the fugitive Hazel Dunmore escaped with half a map, one fae prisoner taken. Beneath it, the inventory of Bramble's effects—a satchel of tools that hummed faintly when touched, a pouch of iron filings that *moved*, a scrap of leather worked with a sigil the archive could not identify.

She had spent two days in the archive with that sigil. She had found it once, in a folio so old the vellum had gone the colour of old milk, in an illustration of a door. A door grown of thorns. The caption, in a dead hand, had read: *the gate and its keepers*.

The keepers were drawn as nine figures. Eight of them were crossed out.

"Where is Hazel Dunmore going?" she asked.

"To the end of a story that started before your Order had a name." Bramble smiled with too many teeth. "Ask me something you actually want to know. We haven't got time for the script."

*We haven't got time.* Sera wrote the date in her log and made her voice flat. "The prisoner will answer—"

"The prisoner has been answering since you walked in. You just haven't been listening." The old fae leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, and the lamplight caught something in those river-stone eyes that made Sera's spine prickle—not menace. Urgency. "You want to know what the map leads to. I'll tell you for free, girl, because you're the first one of your kind in five years to hesitate at a killing, and I'm sentimental. It leads to the Thorn Gate. And the Thorn Gate leads to the thing that's wearing your king."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

Sera did not move. She had been trained for this—prisoners lied, prisoners provoked, prisoners told you the moon was made of cheese if they thought it would buy them an hour. "The king is in Corvale."

"The king is in Corvale the way a coat is in a wardrobe." Bramble's voice dropped, and the lilt went out of it, leaving something old and tired and terribly certain. "You've seen it. Don't insult us both. You've stood at court and watched him, and you've seen the way the light goes through him wrong. You've seen him cast no shadow at the wrong hour, and you've told yourself it was the candles, the angle, your own tired eyes. Haven't you."

Sera said nothing. Her pen had stopped moving. She made it start again.

"His name," Bramble said, "is Vaelmourn."

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife. Sera had never heard it before, and yet some part of her, some animal part that predated her training and her commission and her careful, dutiful life, flinched from it as if from a smell of smoke in a crowded hall.

"What is it?"

"Now we're getting somewhere." The fae closed her eyes briefly, and when she opened them again they were steady. "It's old. Older than the Vigil, older than Corvale, older than the crown it wears. It was born from an oath—a king, a real one, centuries back, who was losing a war and made a bargain he didn't understand. He bound himself, and his heirs, and his whole line's luck, to something beyond the Thorn Gate, in exchange for victory. He won his war. And the thing he'd bound himself to came to collect, slow as rot, generation by generation, feeding on every oath sworn to the crown it wore. Every coronation vow. Every soldier's enlistment. Every magistrate's and hunter's and hangman's oath of service." The river-stone eyes fixed on her. "It doesn't eat magic, witch-hunter. That's the lie your Order tells itself. It eats *bound oaths*. Sworn loyalty. And you have been feeding it every day of your career."

Sera's hand was steady on the pen. She was rather proud of that. "The Vigil's oath is to the crown and the city."

"The crown *is* the city, is it? And who wears the crown?"

"King Aldric."

"Look me in the eye," Bramble said softly, "and tell me you believe that."

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight. Outside, Corvale's evening bells began to ring—the six o'clock, the same bells that had rung every evening of Sera's life, the sound she'd sworn her oath beneath at seventeen with her father's hand on her shoulder and her heart full of righteous fire. *To guard the city from the unbound.* She could still feel the wax of the commission seal cooling against her thumb.

"I believe the king has been ill," she said. "I believe his decrees have grown harsh because he's afraid."

"His decrees have grown harsh because decrees are how it feeds. Every new law that binds the city tighter is a meal. The purge of the charm-houses. The registries. The loyalty oaths they made every guild sign last spring—did you sign one, Vane? I'd wager you did. I'd wager you signed it in your own blood, because the new forms asked for it, and you thought it was administrative." Bramble shook her head slowly, rings clicking against the chair's iron arm. "You didn't sign a form. You rang a dinner bell."

* * *

They gave her until morning to finish the interrogation, which meant they gave her the night, which meant they gave her the prisoner, alone, in a room with one lamp and one door and her own thoughts for company. She suspected Hesk had done it deliberately. Hesk had been watching her since the charm-house—the one with the children's dream-charms, the one whose report she had falsified with her commander's eyes resting on the back of her neck like a hand. *A test,* she'd thought at the time. Perhaps it had been. Perhaps everything since had been.

She sat across from Bramble for a long while before she spoke again.

"If what you're saying is true," she said, "then the Vigil isn't guarding the city. It's the wraith's teeth."

"Now she's listening." Bramble rattled the chains, gesturing at the room with her chin. "Iron and runes, girl. You built all this—your Order, your wards, your little museum of confiscated charms—to cage the very people who could have told you what was happening. Five years ago, the gatekeepers were nine. Now they're none, and the door they kept shut is being pried at from the other side by the thing your oaths have fattened. You didn't fail the city. You were the failure, all of you, working beautifully, exactly as designed." The old voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "I don't blame you. You were a child when they swore you. That's how it works. That's how it has *always* worked. It doesn't take the strong ones first. It takes the sincere."

Sera stood, walked to the door, and stood with her hand on the iron ring for a long time without opening it.

"Why tell me?" she asked finally, without turning. "You could have lied. You could have wasted the night on riddles. Instead you gave me its name. Names have power—your people more than anyone know that. You've handed me a weapon. Why?"

"Because you hesitated at the ford," Bramble said. "Because you saw a hedge-witch throw himself over a child that wasn't his, under your crossbows, and something in you went *wrong*—went right, rather—and you haven't slept properly since. Because I'm old, and I've watched a hundred of your kind march past this cell, and you're the first one who looked at me like a person instead of a specimen." A pause. "And because the boy is walking into the dark with half a map and a guilt the size of a cathedral, and he's going to need someone on the inside of your walls when the solstice comes. Nine days, witch-hunter. Winter solstice. Ask yourself what a creature that feeds on oaths wants with the longest night of the year, and then ask yourself why your king has commanded every soul in Corvale to renew their loyalty oaths at the solstice feast."

The bells finished their sixth hour. In the silence after, Sera could almost hear it—that vast, patient hunger beneath the city, beneath the crown, beneath the wax and ribbon of every oath she'd ever sworn, licking its lips.

"Nine days," she repeated.

"Nine days," Bramble agreed. "And you, Sera Vane, are going to have to decide which side of the door you're standing on. Not today. Not tonight. But soon, and once, and forever after."

* * *

She did not go home. She walked.

Corvale at night was a city of lamps and shadows, and she had spent eleven years learning which shadows hid which sins. Now she walked it like a stranger. The registry office on Candlemaker's Row, where every charm-seller's name was inked and every unregistered talent was a hanging offence—*a feeding trough*. The guildhalls with their framed loyalty oaths in gold leaf—*place settings at its table*. The palace on its hill, lit gold against the dark, where she had stood a fortnight ago and watched the king answer questions only Aldric could have answered, and answer them *wrong*, so gracefully, so kindly, that she had doubted her own eyes rather than her sovereign.

*It let me live,* she thought, crossing the square below the palace walls. *It tested me, found me uncertain, and let me walk out. Why?*

And the answer came, cold and complete, and she stopped in the middle of the empty square to let it finish arriving.

Because uncertainty was enough. Because a hunter who doubted was still a hunter, still sworn, still feeding it with every day she stayed. Because it didn't need her loyalty. It only needed her *oath*—the word she'd given at seventeen, the bond she couldn't see a way out of, the chain it had fastened around her before she was old enough to know what chains were.

It hadn't let her live. It had kept her.

Sera Vane stood in the square below the palace of the thing that wore her king, and she began, very quietly, to laugh—because it was that or scream, and screaming would bring the watch.

* * *

The Vigil's charter hung in the entrance hall of the chapterhouse: the founding oath in letters of gold, signed by the first commander, countersigned by a king whose name the archive dated to two hundred years ago. Sera had walked past it every day for eleven years. She stopped before it now, at an hour when only the night-porter stirred, and read it all the way through for the first time since her induction.

*We swear ourselves, our hands and our judgement, bound to the crown and its heirs, unto death and beyond it.*

*Unto death and beyond it.*

*Beyond it.*

She thought of Bramble's voice: *It eats bound oaths. Sworn loyalty. And you have been feeding it every day of your career.*

She thought of the children's dream-charms, ash on the register. She thought of the king's shadowless face, and the moment beneath the courtesy when something had looked at her with her own sovereign's stolen eyes, and she had felt—she had never told anyone this—*pity*. Pity, for the thing on the throne. As if some small drowned part of the real man had looked out through the mask at her, and mouthed a word she hadn't been able to read.

She read it now, in her memory, and it resolved at last into a shape.

*Help.*

The night-porter coughed. "Late for you, Captain Vane."

"Working late, Tam." Her voice came out steady. She was rather proud of that, too, and this time the pride tasted like ash.

She climbed to her quarters. She took off her sword belt and hung it up. She took off her boots. And then she sat at her desk and opened the small lacquered case that held the one object a Vigil officer was permitted to keep on her person at all times, in life and in death, by charter, by oath, unto death and beyond it.

Her commission seal. Red wax and pressed brass, the crown-and-thorn sigil of the Order, worn smooth on one side from eleven years of her thumb.

She had signed the guild loyalty oath in blood last spring. She had renewed her commission in wax every midsummer. She had fed it, and fed it, and fed it, sincerely, diligently, exactly as designed—and the thing beneath the crown had grown one season stronger every year, and the charm-houses had burned, and the registries had thickened, and the city she loved had grown quiet in the way cities grow quiet under occupation.

Bramble had said she'd have to decide which side of the door she stood on. But standing there, seal in hand, Sera understood that the decision had already been made for her, long ago, by a frightened king and a hungry thing and a seventeen-year-old girl with her father's hand on her shoulder. The only choice left to her was whether to keep pretending the chain was a necklace.

The candle was right there. It had been burning the whole time.

She pressed the seal into the flame and held it there, and did not look away, and did not let go, even when the brass grew hot enough to brand her fingers, even when the wax ran down her wrist like blood, even when the sigil of the crown-and-thorn softened, sagged, and came apart into shapeless shining drops that hissed on her desk.

*Unto death and beyond it,* she thought, watching it die. *Well. Here is my beyond.*

When it was done she wrapped the ruined metal in a handkerchief, because even now—especially now—she would not leave it where the Order might find it and read her treason in its melted face. She sat back in the dark, sucking the burn on her thumb, and felt lighter and more terrified than she had since she was a child.

Nine days to the solstice. A hedge-witch with half a map somewhere in the moorlands. A fae in an iron chair below her feet who had given her the wraith's true name as freely as a gift.

And a feast, nine days hence, where every soul in Corvale would be commanded to kneel and swear.

Sera Vane looked at the candle smoke rising toward the ceiling, and made her first free decision in eleven years.

She was going to break a prisoner out of the Vigil's deepest cell—and then she was going to find Hazel Dunmore, and give him the one thing he didn't know he'd lost: the other half of the truth.

Because the map was torn in half. But so, she now understood, was everything else—and the wraith had counted on no one ever holding both pieces at once.
