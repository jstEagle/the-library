## 11. Below the City of Bells

The river remembered the old road even if the city did not.

Hazel knelt at the edge of the Miren where it slid beneath Corvale's southern wall, dark and slow as spilled ink, and let the cold climb through his knees into his bones. Above him, the bells of Vesper Row rang the ninth hour—Corvale's bells, a hundred of them, bronze throats that sang over rooftops and markets and the homes of people who had no idea what their city stood upon. He had heard those bells all his life. Tonight they sounded like a warning he had spent five years refusing to read.

*Go back,* said the sensible voice in his head, the one that had kept him alive through two witch-hunter regimes and one very bad winter. *You have half a map, no allies, a torn coat, and a bounty on your head with your own likeness drawn far too accurately. Go back to Thornfield. Disappear again.*

But Bramble was in a hunter's cell, or worse. And somewhere beneath this city lay the twin key—the other half of the only door that mattered—and the wraith wearing Aldric's crown was feeding on outlawed magic by the season, growing fat on every charm Hazel had ever sold in fear instead of honesty.

He slid into the water.

It took him to the waist before his feet found the submerged grate, and it took all his strength—not magic, never magic, not here—to wrench it wide enough for a man to pass. The current tried to keep him. The dark swallowed him whole. And then there were only stones under his hands, and the smell of centuries, and the sound of his own breathing echoing down a throat of brick older than any kingdom.

The catacombs began where the river ended.

* * *

Hazel had grown up on stories of the Undercorv, the buried first city, though everyone agreed it was mostly legend—a place smugglers invented to frighten each other, a place tax collectors could not follow. The stories were wrong about one thing: it was not mostly anything. It was entire. Streets without roofs, staircases descending into staircases, doorways carved with saints whose faces had been chiseled away by some pious generation that feared what it could not explain.

He walked by candlestub, counting turns the way the rumors had taught him. *Three arches past the weeping wall. Left where the water drips twice.* The smugglers' rhymes had sounded like nonsense when he'd heard them in dockside taverns, back when he still believed invisibility would save him. Now each line was a rope thrown across a chasm.

The dark below Corvale was not empty. That was the first thing that unsettled him.

Rats, yes. Drip-stone and rot, yes. But between the arches hung things that had no business surviving underground—sprigs of something pale and woody thrusting from cracks in the mortar, thin as wire, bare as winter. He told himself it was root-rot. Told himself a great many things as he went deeper, and the sprigs grew thicker, branching along the walls like veins, like writing, like—

Like thorn.

He stopped at the mouth of a long gallery and held up his light, and his stomach performed a slow, sick revolution.

The walls were not stone.

They had been stone once—or stone enough—but beneath the plaster, beneath the chisel marks, the whole gallery curved and ridged like the inside of a living thing. And from every joint, every seam where block met block, the pale wood grew: a lattice of thorn, leafless and patient, threaded through the foundations of Corvale like thread through cloth. Some of the branches were thick as his arm. One had grown straight up through a broken flagstone and out of sight, upward, toward the streets above. Toward the bells.

"Saints' teeth," Hazel whispered, and the whisper came back to him wrong—multiplied, as though the corridor itself repeated things it liked.

He knew what thorn meant. He had crossed a moor full of the stuff; he had watched wights unfold from it like spiders from silk; he had sealed a gate against everything it fed. Thorn belonged beyond the Veil. It did not belong in the bones of a human city.

Unless, said a thought he did not want, the city had been built *into* it. Unless Corvale had not been founded on rock at all, but on something rooted, and every bell tower and palace spire and witch-hunter's gallows stood planted in living wood that had been waiting, all these centuries, for someone to come back and knock.

His candle guttered. In the dark that rushed in behind it, for one heartbeat, he heard the lattice creak—slow, vast, like a ship shifting at anchor, like a sleeper turning over.

Then the flame steadied, and the catacombs were just catacombs again, and Hazel walked on with his skin crawling and his hand pressed flat against his charm-pouch out of pure habit. The little charms inside gave him no comfort. They had never given anyone comfort. They were lies sold by weight, and he was beginning to hate how well they'd worked—including on him.

* * *

The smuggler-priest could be found, the rumors said, where the drowned chapel rings.

Hazel found the chapel an hour later, and understood the rumor's joke at once: it did ring. Water fell somewhere above through a crack in the vault, drop by drop onto a bronze basin some enterprising smuggler had positioned years ago, and each strike sent a low, sweet note rolling through the flooded nave. A church with a bell that needed no hand and no congregation. It was the kind of clever, melancholy thing that made Hazel miss having someone to tell.

The chapel sat knee-deep in black water. Stumps of pillars rose around a shattered altar, and the saint-statues here had not been defaced—they had been *grown over*, thorn-lattice sheathing them like bark swallowing a fence post, until each figure was less a person than a suggestion of one. At the altar's foot, a lamp burned. Beside the lamp, a man sat on an overturned pew, mending a net.

He was younger than Hazel expected and older than he looked, the way sailors are—grey at the temples, forty maybe, sixty maybe, hands like knotted rope. He wore the collar of a priest of the Undivided Flame, the old faith, banned almost as long as magic had been. He did not look up from his netting.

"You're dripping on my floor," the priest said.

"It's already underwater."

"My floor," the priest agreed, "is very forgiving." He finished a knot, bit the thread, and raised his eyes. They were pale, nearly colourless, the eyes of a fish or a fortune-teller. "You're not a smuggler. Smugglers don't flinch when the water talks. You're not a hunter; hunters come in threes and never wet their boots. So you're either a fool or a pilgrim."

"I'm looking for Father Ilex."

"Mm. Everyone who finds me is looking for Father Ilex. Nobody ever looks for the man himself." He set aside the net. "Which are you? Fool or pilgrim?"

Hazel thought of Bramble in chains. Of the stranger dying in his back room, blood on his lips, pressing a folded map into Hazel's hands with the last of his strength. *It has to be someone who won't use it,* the man had whispered. *Or someone who'll finally have to.*

"A pilgrim," Hazel said. "I think. I'm told you remember the old rites. The rites of the gatekeepers."

Something moved behind the pale eyes—careful, quick, like a shutter closing. "I remember a great many things I was paid to forget. Sit down, pilgrim. You're making my floor nervous."

Hazel waded to the nearest dry-ish pillar and lowered himself onto its stump. The water soaked through to the bone anyway. He found he didn't care. He was so tired that tiredness had stopped being a sensation and become a landscape he simply lived in.

"The Thorn Gate," he said. "There's a key. Half of it—the iron half—came through with the map. But the map speaks of a twin. A key of green wood, made when the gate was first closed. I need to find it, or find someone who can remake it. They say you remember how such things were done."

For a long moment the only sound was the drowned bell, tolling its slow patient count. Then Father Ilex laughed, softly, without much humor in it.

"They say that, do they. In the taverns. Where you buy your rumors with bad coin and worse company." He stood, joints cracking, and waded to the altar. From beneath the broken stone he drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with a reverence that contradicted every irreverent word he'd spoken.

Inside lay a branch of thorn. Black-wooded, silver-thorned, perhaps a forearm long—and alive. As the oilcloth fell away, the thorns flexed, faintly, like fingers remembering a grip. Hazel felt the hairs on his arms rise. He knew that wood. He had bled on its cousin on Thornfield moor while wights came apart around him.

"That's—" he started.

"Greenwood key, unfinished," Ilex said. "The last of the order died before the forging was done. His apprentice—that would be me, if we're using honest words—was left with a stick, a memory, and a faith nobody wanted." He looked up, and now there was nothing flippant in his face at all. "I can finish it. The rites live here"—he tapped his temple—"because I was too stubborn to let them die quietly. But the key is the least of what you need to understand tonight, pilgrim. So before I decide whether you leave here carrying hope or carrying nothing, I'm going to show you something."

"What?"

"How deep your city goes." Ilex lifted the lamp. "Follow me. And whatever you hear—don't answer it."

* * *

Behind the chapel altar, a passage climbed. Hazel had expected to go deeper; instead they went up, along a winding stair cut through the same ridged, curving material he'd seen in the galleries—stone that wasn't stone—and the thorn lattice grew denser with every step, until it arched overhead like the ribs of a cathedral and the lamp threw shadows that moved a half-beat behind their sources.

"Careful now," Ilex murmured. "We're passing through the root."

"The root of what?"

"Of everything you've ever called home."

The stair opened onto a chamber that stole the breath clean out of Hazel's chest.

It was vast beyond reason—a hollow like the inside of a hill, lit by a soft grey luminescence that seeped from the wood itself. And filling that hollow, rising from darkness below and disappearing into darkness above, stood a single tree.

Not a trunk. A *trunk of trunks*—wide as a town square, its bark fused with stone, its branches splitting and splitting again until they became the beams of the vaults above, the pillars of the chapels, the frames of the doorways, the bones of every building Hazel had ever walked beneath. Corvale had not been built near this tree, or around it. Corvale had been built *out of it*, plank and rafter, street and sewer, a hundred thousand tons of masonry grafted onto living wood—and the wood had accepted the graft, the way a body accepts a splinter, patiently, while it decided whether to heal around it or reject it.

And through the grey light, Hazel saw the rest.

The thorns. Great hooked thorns, each the size of a scythe, curling from the trunk in slow spirals—and caught on some of them, impaled or entwined, were shapes. Old ones. Bones gone to lace. A helm crusted green with age. What might once have been a shield, split lengthwise like a dropped plate. Things carried down by time, or offered, or taken.

And moving among the roots at the hollow's edge—small, quick, wrong—things that were not quite wights and not quite trees. They paused as the lamplight touched them. Dozens of pale faces turned, all together, toward the two small warm creatures standing on the stair.

"Don't answer them," Ilex breathed.

Hazel hadn't spoken. He hadn't moved. But his heart was hammering loud enough to be an answer, and he understood suddenly what the priest had meant.

One of the small things drifted closer. Up close it resolved into something almost pitiable—a twig-limbed homunculus, a child's drawing of a person given three hundred years to practice. It tilted its head. Its face had no features except two shallow dents where eyes should be, and yet Hazel felt *seen*, thoroughly, the way he had not felt seen since the night a bleeding stranger chose his shop out of every shop in Corvale.

The thing raised a twig-finger. Pointed—at Hazel's chest, at the pouch where the half-map slept against his ribs.

Then it bowed. Slowly, formally, with terrible courtesy. And withdrew into the roots, and the others followed, and the hollow exhaled a wind that smelled of sap and snowfall.

Hazel discovered he was gripping the stair rail hard enough to splinter it. He made himself let go.

"What are they?" His voice came out scraped raw.

"Parishioners," Ilex said quietly. "What's left of the ones who tended the tree, once. Before the Veil closed. Before your order sealed the door and called the sealing mercy." He turned the lamp so its light fell across the great trunk, across the fused stone, across the bones hanging in the thorns. "Every founding story Corvale tells itself is a lie with nice manners. There was no brave settlement on empty hills. There was a tree that stands in both worlds at once, and people who lived in its shadow and called it holy. Then came the oath. Then came the closing. Then came men like us, building our bells on top of the wound and calling the ringing music."

Hazel stared up at the vaulting branches, at the streets he knew stacked layer upon layer overhead—Vesper Row, the fish market, the witch-hunters' barracks, the palace itself—and felt awe curdle slowly, steadily, into horror. Every step he had ever taken in Corvale had been a step on something alive. Every foundation stone he'd ever haggled beside was set in flesh that remembered being worshipped. The city had not been sleeping. It had been *waiting*. And now the wraith on the throne was feeding it outlawed magic season by season, and the thorn crept thicker through the catacombs, and the small courteous things bowed to a half-map in a charm-seller's pocket because they knew what he carried, and they knew what it would open.

"They pointed at me," Hazel said. "Down there. Why would they point at—"

"Because the tree dreams," Ilex said, "and lately it dreams of doors. And you, pilgrim, smell of keys." He wrapped the unfinished greenwood key back in its oilcloth with careful, loving hands. "So. Here is where we stand, you and I. I can finish your key—I have the rites, I have the wood, I have the grudge. Three nights' work, maybe four. But my price is not coin."

"Name it."

Ilex looked at him for a long moment, pale eyes unblinking, and the drowned bell tolled somewhere behind them, once, twice, patient as judgment.

"Confession," the priest said. "The real kind. Not the sort you sell to strangers with your charms and your careful smile—the sort that costs. Five years ago, Hazel Dunmore, you came *through* the Thorn Gate from the other side, alone, half-dead, and you sealed it behind you so thoroughly that even the order's survivors couldn't reopen it. I want to know why you ran. I want to know what you left on the other side of that door." He stepped closer, and the lamplight made his shadow climb the living walls, huge and branching. "Because the tree remembers you. Those parishioners didn't bow to your map. They bowed to *you*—to the man who broke an oath on the threshold. And whatever you're carrying in that chest of yours is heavier than the map, and it will kill you before the wraith does unless you set it down. So. Confession first. Key after."

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. The hollow breathed around them, vast and slow, and somewhere far above—muffled by stone and timber and living wood—the bells of Vesper Row rang the midnight hour, and the sound came down through the roots of the world like grief coming down through years.

"I don't—" he began.

"You do," said Father Ilex. "You've carried it since the moor. I watched you flinch every time the thorn touched you, and that's not fear, boy. That's recognition. Now sit down on my floor—it forgives, remember—and tell me what you left behind the gate."

And Hazel, who had lied to witch-hunters and charmed magistrates and smiled his way past five years of armed suspicion, found that the lie would not come. It sat in his throat like a stone, like a seed, like the first small hook of a thorn taking root—and above him the whole hidden city waited, breathing, to hear what he had done.
