## 12. The Priest Who Forgot His God

The smuggler-priest lived, according to the catacomb whispers, in the drowned chapel of Saint Verren, three levels beneath the Street of Bells where the city's oldest dead had been laid and then, in Corvale's usual fashion, forgotten about entirely.

Hazel found it by following the smell of tallow smoke down a stair that had been bricked over twice and unbricked twice more. The passage narrowed until his shoulders brushed both walls, until the thorn-grown stone around him pressed close enough that he could feel its slow pulse against his palms—because that was the thing he could not stop feeling now that he had seen it: the whole city was rooted in living wood. Every foundation stone he had ever walked past, every cellar floor he had ever swept, sat on branches as thick as cathedrals, still growing, still patient, reaching up through mortar and marble like veins through skin.

He had lived above this his entire life. He had sealed the Thorn Gate five years ago believing he was shutting out the otherworld—and all the while, the otherworld had been underneath him, holding him up, growing quietly toward everything he loved.

*It wants to be let back in,* Bramble had said once, before the ford, before the hunters, before half of everything went wrong. *That's the trouble with doors. They don't care which side you're standing on.*

Hazel's hands remembered how to shake at the thought, so he made them hold the lantern instead.

The stair ended at a door of black oak banded with iron gone orange with age. No lock. He pushed it open with two fingers and stepped into a chapel that had drowned and dried again: water-stains climbing the pillars like brown ivy, a font full of rainwater and coins, pews stacked against one wall to make room for something that looked less like an altar than a workbench—vises and hammers, crucibles and coils of wire, and hanging from the rafters on hooks, dozens upon dozens of keys. Iron keys, brass keys, bone-white keys carved from something Hazel did not want to identify, turning slowly in a draft he could not feel.

A man stood at the bench with his back turned, sleeves rolled, forearms grey with metal-dust, working something small and intricate under a jeweler's lens screwed into his eye socket. He wore a cassock that had been patched so many times it resembled a map of old wars. His hair was tonsured badly, by his own hand probably, with whatever blade had been nearest.

"Closed," the man said without looking up. "Mass is at dawn. Confession is never."

"I was told you remember the old rites," Hazel said. "The rites from before the Veiling."

The hammering stopped.

The man set down his tool with exaggerated gentleness, the way a person does when they are very angry or very afraid and have decided to be neither. Then he turned. He was perhaps sixty, with a face built for weathering—deep lines, pale eyes, and a scar across his throat that looked old enough to have been earned honestly.

"And who told you that?" he asked. "Because the last person who told me that was wearing the king's colors, and I spent four years in a cell below the Bastion learning exactly what I remembered."

"My name is Hazel Dunmore." He said it plainly, without the shop-voice, without the charm-seller's softness he wore like a second cloak. It felt strange and dangerous to say it plainly. "I keep the Thorn Gate. Or I did. The map says the key is broken, and the map's owner is dead, and I need someone who can forge the twin."

For a long moment the priest only looked at him. Then he laughed—one dry, cracked sound, like ice shifting on a pond.

"The Thorn Gate." He wiped his hands on his cassock, leaving grey streaks. "Five years I've been down here. Five years since they cut my orders out of the registers and burned my library and told the city I never existed. And now a hedge-witch walks into my chapel asking after the one door in Corvale I'd hoped to God was rusted shut forever." He crossed himself—old habit, half-mocking, half not. "Father Ilex. Formerly of the Order of the Threshold. Currently of nowhere. You'd better sit down, keeper. This is going to take a while, and my stools are terrible."

* * *

They talked through the night.

Ilex asked questions first, and they were good questions—the kind that had teeth. Where was the gate's western hinge-stone. What did the seal-tally read when Hazel last checked it. Which hand did the stranger use to draw the map. When Hazel answered correctly, again and again, something in the priest's posture loosened by degrees, the way a fist unclenches in sleep.

"You really kept it," Ilex said finally, wonder creeping into the dry voice. "All this time. Alone. In a city that hangs people for lesser magic than gatekeeping."

"It wasn't hard," Hazel said. "I'm good at being invisible."

"No one is good at it," Ilex said. "Some are just better at lying." He began clearing space on the bench, sweeping metal filings into a tray, setting out tools with the economy of long practice. "Show me the map."

Hazel unfolded the torn half he carried—the left side, the side with the gate's sigil and the route through Thornfield and the careful annotations in a dead man's bloodstained hand. Ilex bent over it under his lamp for a long time. When he straightened, his face had changed. The irony was gone out of it. What remained was older and much worse.

"This isn't a gatekeeper's map," he said quietly. "This is a *warden's* map. Do you understand the difference? Gatekeepers watch doors. Wardens were sworn to the otherworld itself—to the court beyond the thorns, to the oaths that bind both sides of the veil." His finger hovered over a symbol near the gate's sigil, a small interlocked ring Hazel had taken for decoration. "This mark means the bearer died in service. There hasn't been a warden's order in Corvale since before my grandfather. Whoever your bleeding stranger was, he was the last of something very old, and he chose *you* to carry it."

"He chose whoever was nearest," Hazel said. "I was nearest."

"Mm." Ilex gave him a look that saw far too much. "And the key. You know the problem with forging the twin?"

"The metal has to have been on both sides of the veil."

"The metal has to have been *oathed* on both sides of the veil," Ilex corrected. "Which means I need material from a warden's regalia, or—" he paused delicately, "—from a warden. Something that carried a binding oath across and back. Do you have such a thing?"

Hazel reached inside his shirt and drew out the cord he had worn for five years without ever quite letting himself touch the pendant on it. A thin sliver of white wood, no bigger than a finger joint, drilled and strung like a relic. It had come through the gate with him the night he fled. It had been pressed into his hand by someone screaming at him to go, to run, to *live*, and he had carried it ever since without knowing why, except that throwing it away had always been unthinkable, the way amputees are said to feel about the missing limb.

Ilex took it with both hands, reverently, the way he would take communion. He held it up to the lamp. His breath caught.

"Where did you get this?" His voice had gone strange. "This is heartwood. Warden's heartwood—from a tree grown *on the oath-line itself*. Keeper, do you know what this is? This is the rarest component for a threshold key that exists in either world. This alone—" He stopped. Looked up. Really looked at Hazel for the first time, past the charm-seller's disguise, past the careful face. "Someone gave you their own oath-token. Someone who was bound. Someone who meant for you to come back."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled by thirty feet of thorn-rooted stone, a cart rattled over the Street of Bells, and the sound came down faint and faraway, like grief heard through a wall.

"She didn't mean for me to come back," Hazel said.

His voice came out flat. He hadn't decided to say it. It simply arrived, the way floodwater arrives when the wall finally gives—not gradually, but all at once, everywhere.

"She meant for me to *go*. There's a difference." 

Ilex set the pendant down on a square of clean cloth with enormous care, as though it might bruise. Then he pulled the jeweler's lens out of his eye, folded his hands on the bench, and waited. He was very good at waiting. Hazel understood suddenly that this was the priest's true trade—not forging, not smuggling, but sitting with people while the things they had buried worked their way up like splinders rising through skin.

"That's the price," Ilex said gently. "You knew it when you walked in. Everyone who comes to me for the old rites knows. The rites require truth, keeper. Not because I'm cruel. Because the key won't bind to a lie. An oath forged around a falsehood holds for a day and shatters on the threshold, and takes the holder's hand off at the wrist. I've seen it happen." A pause. "So. Tell me about her."

* * *

The chapel was very quiet. The keys hung overhead, turning in their invisible draft, and Hazel stared at them because it was easier than staring at the priest, and easier than staring at the pendant, which lay on its cloth between them like an open wound.

"There were two of us," he said. "Me and Wren. My sister. Twins—we came through the veil together when we were children, refugees of a border feud nobody remembers the name of anymore. We grew up on the other side. In the thorn country. You learn things there that Corvale doesn't teach. How to walk a path that moves. How to bargain with a briar. How to listen to the gate."

"Wren," Ilex repeated softly. "Short for?"

"Hawthorn-ren. It's a warden's name. Our mother was a warden. Her mother before her." His mouth twisted. "The order didn't die out, Father. It just moved. Went quiet. Hid itself so thoroughly that eventually it hid even from itself. By the time I was born we were three people in a house of thorns keeping a door shut, and calling it farming."

"And five years ago?"

Five years ago. He had rehearsed this story for five years—the version where there was nothing to confess, the version where the guardian turned them away, the version where Wren stayed behind *by choice*, the version where he had done everything right and simply lost. He had told it to himself so many times in the dark hours behind his shop that it had worn smooth as river stone, and he reached for it now the way you reach for a banister in a burning house.

What came out instead was the truth, raw-edged and awful:

"We were attacked. The Veilwraith's creatures—wights, the same kind that ambushed us on Thornfield moor, but worse, older. They wanted the gate. Our mother was dead by then; she'd been dead two years, and Wren had taken up her oath, and I—" His hands were shaking now, properly shaking, and he let them. "I had never taken any oath at all. That was the arrangement. Mother bound Wren to the line and left me free, because someone had to be free, she said, someone had to be able to leave if it came to that. And I hated her for it. All my life I hated her for it, and I never once said so, and I never once refused it either."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Ilex did not move.

"They came at dusk. There were too many. Wren held the threshold—I can still see her standing in the doorway with mother's staff, light coming off her like heat off a forge—and she looked at me and said *go*. Not run. Go. Get to the other side. Get help. Find the wardens' remnants in Corvale, raise the alarm, bring back strength." His laugh came out broken. "There are no warden remnants in Corvale. She knew that. I knew that. It was a lie to make me move. And I moved, Father. That's the whole of it. I *moved*. I went through the gate and I let it seal behind me because sealing it was the only way to keep the wights from following, and I told myself it was strategy, I told myself I was protecting the city, I told myself every night for five years that I would find another way back, another door, some road that didn't require—" His voice failed and restarted. "—didn't require facing her. Facing what she became, holding that door alone for five years against the dark. Because I knew. Don't you understand? I *knew* what staying on the oath-line does to a person. I watched it take our mother apart year by year, and I ran rather than look at what it must have done to my sister, because if I looked, then I would have to admit that I left her. Not that I couldn't stay. That I *left*."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water.

Hazel found he was still standing. He had expected to collapse—he had imagined this confession for days, ever since Father Ilex's name surfaced in the catacombs, and in his imaginings he always ended up on his knees—but his body had apparently decided that kneeling was a luxury it could not afford yet. So he stood there in the drowned chapel, stripped of the charm-seller, stripped of the invisible man, stripped even of the careful survivor he had built out of five years of lies, and he was just a twin with half a soul and a shard of his sister's heartwood on a string.

"She's the guardian now," he said. "Isn't she. Whatever holds the far side of that door—it's her. It's been her the whole time."

Ilex exhaled slowly. "Heartwood is given, not taken," he said. "An oath-token passes willingly or not at all. If she pressed it into your hand as you fled, then she was already giving you the means to return. She was trusting you to come back *and* telling you how." His pale eyes were kind and pitiless at once. "The question the key will ask, keeper, is whether you can answer it honestly this time. Doors don't care about your guilt. They care about your truth. Guilt looks backward. Truth stands in the doorway and doesn't flinch."

"I flinched," Hazel said. "For five years."

"Then say that to the metal when I forge it. Say it exactly like that." Ilex picked up the pendant again, and this time he weighed it in his palm like a smith weighing stock, and the reverence was still there but it was joined by something practical and fierce. "This will make a fine twin. Better than fine. Heartwood remembers the oath that made it, and an oath remembered is half an oath renewed." He glanced up. "It will take me until tomorrow's dusk to cut and bind it. Until then—"

The chapel door banged open.

Hazel's knife was in his hand before his mind caught up—five years of habit, a blade of cold iron under his sleeve—and then he saw who stood in the doorway, breathing hard, hood thrown back, torchlight from the passage flickering over a face he had last seen leading an ambush at the river ford.

Sera Vane. Witch-hunter. Traitor to the crown, now, presumably, though her commission seal was conspicuously absent from her belt. Behind her, limping and scowling and unmistakably alive, came Bramble, wrists rope-burned, one eye swollen shut, carrying the other half of the map like a banner.

"We found the catacomb entrance the hunters missed," Sera said, and her voice shook only at the edges. "The palace announced the purge tonight. Solstice eve lists are already posted—two hundred names, Hazel. Charm-sellers, healers, children." She swallowed. "We have maybe two days. Less."

Ilex looked at the pendant in his hand, then at Hazel, and something grim settled over his features like frost.

"Well then," said the priest who had forgotten his god. "I suppose I'll be working through the night after all."
