## 13. Winter Court

The Great Hall of Corvale had been dressed for winter the way a corpse is dressed for burial.

Sera Vane stood at her post along the eastern wall, third position from the pillar, where she could see the dais and the doors and most of the thousand candles burning in their iron sconces. She had been given this position deliberately—she understood that now. Commander Rusk placed his hunters where they could watch the guests, and tonight every guest in the hall was, in some sense, a suspect. The solstice feast was tradition, and tradition was the one thing the crown still pretended to honor. So the lords and guildmasters and merchant princes of Corvale sat beneath banners of white and iron-grey, eating venison glazed with winter pears, drinking spiced wine from cups they kept close to their chests, and smiling at one another with the particular care of people who had all heard the rumors and none of them dared name them aloud.

Sera had heard the rumors too. She had helped write some of them.

The wine in her own cup had gone untouched for an hour. She turned it slowly by the stem, watching the candlelight swim in the dark red surface, and let her eyes do what they had been trained to do: move, catalog, return. The hall's great windows had been shuttered against the solstice dark, which meant no moonlight, no angles to test. She found she missed the angles. For three weeks now she had been watching the king at every opportunity, in every light the palace offered, waiting for the sun to fall across him the way it had that morning in the map room—waiting to see whether the shadow would be there or not.

It was never there. Not at any angle. Not in any light. Aldric of Corvale, the Iron King, the Restorer, moved through his own palace like a figure cut from dark paper and laid down upon the world, and no one else seemed to notice, and Sera had begun to doubt the architecture of her own sanity.

*You saw what you wanted to see,* Rusk had told her, the one time she had come close to saying it aloud. *You've been in the field too long. Hunters see shadows in every corner. That's the trade's sickness, Vane. Don't let it take root.*

But she had checked. She had checked the way she checked everything, with the patience of a woman setting snares. She had stood in the colonnade at midday and watched the king walk its length, his courtiers' shadows stretching and swinging beside them like loyal dogs, and his side of the marble clean as polished glass. She had watched him pass the great hearth in the council chamber, firelight throwing the shadows of eleven men and one absence up the wall. She had even, in a moment of desperation she was not proud of, held a candle behind her own hand in the dark of her quarters and reminded herself what a shadow was, how it worked, that it could not simply be *refused*.

The thing on the throne cast no shadow. And it had let her live after asking it a question only the true king could answer, and it had answered wrong, and it had smiled at her with Aldric's mouth, and she had walked out of that room with her knees soft as candle wax and her face arranged into perfect, stupid loyalty.

Tonight she was to stand in its hall and toast its health.

* * *

The trumpets sounded at the ninth bell, and the hall rose.

Sera rose with them, cup lifted, spine straight, the picture of the loyal hunter. The doors at the far end swung open and the king entered, and the hall's breath went out of it in a single collective sigh—the way it always did, because whatever else was true, the thing wearing Aldric knew how to fill a room. He was tall. He was broad through the shoulder. He wore the iron crown low over his brow, and his cloak was the grey of deep winter, and he walked with the measured, unhurried stride of a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry.

The courtiers bowed. The lords sank. The guildmasters dipped their heads. And Sera, watching from her pillar, watched the way she had taught herself to watch—not the face, which was a masterpiece, but the *edges* of him. The way the candle flames near the dais leaned very slightly toward him as he passed, as if drawn. The way the shadows of the candelabra behind the throne pooled and thickened around its base, gathering there like water finding a drain. The way the hounds of the Lord Marshal, quartered by the door for the feast, pressed themselves flat to the stones and would not look at him, ears back, a low whine in their throats that their masters hushed with anxious hands.

Animals knew. Children knew. That was the thing no one said. The purge lists had begun with the slums, with the charm-sellers and hedge-witches, the ones no one powerful would miss. But Sera had seen a fishmonger's daughter in the Shambles last month, no more than six years old, go silent and still as the king's procession passed, and hide her face in her mother's skirts, and *tremble*. Not at the soldiers. At the man on the horse.

The king reached the dais. He turned. He raised his own cup, and the hall raised theirs, and for a moment there was only the roar of a thousand voices crying *Long life to Aldric*, and Sera's mouth made the words while her mind stood apart from them like a woman watching her own reflection in dark water.

*Long life.* She almost laughed. Whatever sat on that throne did not live, and did not need her wishes to continue.

The feast began. Sera ate nothing. She had learned that much, at least: never eat in that hall, never drink, never let the thing on the dais have even the smallest claim on her body. She moved along the wall as the courses came and went, taking up her positions, watching the guests watch the king, and the king watch the guests, and beneath all of it the great silent arithmetic of fear being counted and recounted.

It was near midnight, when the plates were cleared and the musicians had gone quiet at a flick of the king's hand, that he stood.

The silence that fell was total. Even the fire in the great hearth seemed to draw in its breath.

"My lords. My ladies. Children of Corvale." The voice filled the hall without effort, warm as mulled wine, and that was always the worst part—the warmth. "Tonight is the longest night of the year. The night when the old world stands closest to the new, when the veil between what is and what might be wears thinnest. Our fathers kept this feast to drive back the dark. And I have kept faith with you, as you have kept faith with me."

He let the silence work. He was good at that. He always let the silence work.

"But I must speak plainly tonight, as a father speaks to his children when the sickness is in the house." He turned slowly upon the dais, and the candlelight slid across the iron crown, and Sera saw the Lord Marshal's knuckles whiten around his cup. "There is a poison among us. A taint. It seeps through the cracks of this city like damp through old mortar—in the cellars of the charm-sellers, in the whispered prayers of the hedge-witches, in the *sympathies* that certain of our citizens harbor for the lawless wilds beyond our walls. You have felt it. You have told me of it. Your courage in coming to me has been the salvation of this city."

Sera's stomach went cold. She had filed those reports. She knew which names were on which lists, because for three weeks her own hand had been adding to them—names she had verified, and names she had not, and names that Rusk had added over her objections because a full list was a useful list and the crown had stopped asking whether the useful and the true were the same thing.

"And so," the king said, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anything, because there was nothing in him for it to reach, "at tomorrow's dawn, the Winter Purge begins. Every tainted soul in Corvale will be gathered to the Palace Green. There will be no trials, for the taint is its own evidence. There will be no appeals, for mercy toward the tainted is itself a taint." He raised his cup toward the hall, toward the frozen faces, toward the guildmaster whose daughter had been arrested in October and the merchant whose son had fled across the moor and the old lord who had once, years ago, been seen speaking kindly to a fae tinkerer at a market fair. "I do this *for* you. Rejoice with me. The longest night is nearly over—and by spring, Corvale will be clean."

The hall did not rejoice. The hall did nothing. A thousand people stood in a silence so complete that Sera could hear the candles burning, the small wet hiss of a thousand wicks, and she thought: *this is what it sounds like when a city understands what it has crowned.*

Then someone began to cheer. A young lord near the dais, flushed with wine and ambition, banging his cup on the table, and after him his friends, and after them the ones who could not afford not to, and the applause spread through the hall like fire through a dry field, ugly and obedient, until the only people not clapping were the ones too old or too honest or too terrified—and Sera's own hands were moving, because she was a hunter in the king's livery standing in the king's hall, and the alternative to clapping was to be noticed, and to be noticed was to die, and she clapped, and she hated herself with a completeness that frightened her more than the throne did.

The king drank. The hall drank. The musicians, released, struck up something bright and foolish, and the feast resumed as if the air had not just been cut open, and Sera stood at her pillar with her palms still tingling from her own applause and understood that she had just helped murder a city.

*Not yet,* she told herself. *Nothing has happened yet. Dawn hasn't come. There is still a night between the sentence and the grave.*

She had spent three weeks telling herself versions of that sentence. *Not yet. Not proof. Not my place.* And every *not yet* had been a coin paid to her own cowardice, and the purse was nearly empty, and dawn was six hours away.

* * *

It was past the second bell when she saw it.

She had moved to the western wall, near the doors, on the pretext of checking the guard rotation—Rusk had gone to the privy or to report, and for a few minutes the eastern approach was hers alone. The king had withdrawn from the high table to a smaller chair near the hearth, the better, it was said, to receive the personal felicitations of his favorites. A queue of courtiers formed, bowing, murmuring, presenting their children to be blessed with a touch of the royal hand. Sera watched it the way she watched everything now, cataloging, and she told herself she was watching for threats, and she knew she was watching for something else.

A serving girl came past her with a tray of wine, and Sera stepped aside to let her pass, and in stepping aside her line of sight to the hearth shifted—just slightly, just enough.

The firelight caught the king full on.

And for one heartbeat—one single beat of the great clock above the doors—the face beneath the crown was not the king's.

It was the same face. That was the horror of it. The same heavy brow, the same grey-streaked beard, the same strong jaw. But it was *alive*. It was wet with sweat. The eyes were wide and human and drowning, and the mouth was open in a soundless scream, and the hands—she saw the hands, gripping the arms of the chair, the knuckles bloodless, the fingers bent like a man hauling against a rope that was dragging him under—and the eyes found hers across the hall, across forty feet of candlelight and applause and the bright idiot music, and they *fixed* on her, and they begged.

*Sera Vane,* those eyes said, in a voice no one else could hear. *You see me. In the name of whatever you still believe in—see me.*

Then the heartbeat passed. The firelight shifted as a log settled. The face smoothed over like water closing above a drowning man, and the king smiled at the next courtier in the queue, warm and gracious and hollow, and reached out to bless the man's small daughter, who flinched away and was hastily, furiously, dragged back by her collar.

Sera's whole body was shaking. She pressed her shoulder against the pillar and made herself breathe—in, out, slow, the way she had taught recruits to breathe after their first killing—and the hall swam and steadied, swam and steadied.

It was real. She had not wanted it to be real. She had spent three weeks building every alternative she could find—trick of the light, failing eyes, the sickness of the trade, Rusk's comfortable diagnosis—and now there was no alternative left. She had seen the true king's face, and it had been screaming, and no one in this hall of a thousand witnesses had seen it but her.

Which meant the thing that wore him had let her see. Or had not cared. Or—worse, far worse—had wanted a witness. The thought arrived cold and complete, the way her best deductions always did: *It showed me on purpose. It is telling me what it does to kings. It is telling me what it will do to me, if I am ever worth the trouble.*

And beneath the terror, rising through it like a blade through deep water, something else. Something that did not feel like fear at all.

Compassion. Pity. The raw, helpless ache of a woman looking at a man in a cage. She had spent her career learning to see through people—the smuggler's smile, the witch's innocence, the liar's steady eyes—and now the skill had found its true use, and what it showed her was a prisoner wearing a crown, alone in the most crowded room in the kingdom, screaming into a silence no one else could hear.

She knew what that silence was. She had lived inside it for three weeks.

*Tomorrow at dawn they will gather the tainted on the Palace Green.* The thought was calm now, terribly calm. *And the day after, and the day after, until the lists run out and they start making new ones. And I will stand in the ranks, or I will be on them, and either way the thing on the throne wins.*

She looked at the king by the fire. The eyes did not find hers again. The mask was perfect; perhaps it had only slipped the once, in a moment of the wraith's carelessness or the prisoner's desperation, and might never slip again. It did not matter. Once was enough. Once was a door, and Sera Vane had spent her whole life walking through doors other people were too afraid to open.

Rusk returned from the eastern wall, resplendent in his ceremonial grey, and paused beside her with a cup of wine he did not offer her. "A great night for the city," he said, watching the hall with satisfaction. "The purge lists are finalized. Your sector's names go to the guard captains at first light. You'll want to review them before dawn, Vane. There are—corrections. Names added. The crown is being thorough."

"Corrections," Sera said.

"Forty-one new names." He said it the way he said everything, flat and administrative, a man discussing grain shipments. "Some of them you'll know. Good people. That's how the taint works—it hides in good people. That's why we can't afford mercy." He glanced at her, and for a moment his eyes lingered, and she made her face into the smooth loyal mask she had been practicing for three weeks, and after a moment he nodded and moved on down the wall.

She stood a while longer. The feast roared around her, bright and doomed. By the fire, the king received his courtiers' bows and gave his hollow blessings, and the candles leaned toward him, and the shadows pooled at the throne's feet like something waiting to be fed.

Then Sera Vane pushed off from the pillar, straightened her hunter's grey, and walked—not toward the guard barracks, not toward the lists she was meant to review, but toward the small postern door in the hall's north corner, the one the servants used, the one no hunter had any business knowing.

She had until dawn. She had one night. And there was a prisoner in the Shambles—a fae tinkerer with thorn-scarred wrists, taken at the ford, who had spoken to her, in the dark of his cell, of a thing called a Veilwraith, and of a hedge-witch charm-seller who carried half a map to a door beneath the city.

She did not know yet what she was going to do. She only knew that tomorrow, when the trumpets sounded and the tainted were gathered on the green, she would not be standing in the ranks.

The postern door closed behind her, and the winter night took her in.
