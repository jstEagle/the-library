## 14. Two Halves Make a Door

The knock came at the third hour past midnight—three slow raps, then two quick ones, then a pause that stretched so long Hazel almost convinced himself he'd imagined it.

No one knew that rhythm. No one alive, anyway, except the man who had died in his back room with a map pressed into his hands and a secret pressed into his silence.

Hazel stood in the dark of his shop with a candle guttering between his fingers, watching the door as though will alone could hold it shut. Below the floorboards, beneath three layers of cellar stone and one of living thorn, the Thorn Gate hummed its low, patient note—the sound it had made every night for five years, the sound that meant *still sealed, still safe, still yours to guard*. Tonight it sounded different. Tonight it sounded like it was waiting.

The knock came again. Three slow. Two quick.

He opened the door on a woman half-drowned in shadow, hooded, snow gathering on her shoulders like the city itself had tried to bury her. Sera Vane. Witch-hunter. The same sharp face he'd last seen across the chaos of the ford, her blade half-raised, her eyes full of something that hadn't looked like hatred at all.

"You're either very brave," Hazel said quietly, "or very stupid."

"Both, probably." She glanced over her shoulder at the empty street, the shuttered windows, the bell tower counting out the hour against a sky the color of iron. "May I come in before someone decides I'm brave and stupid in public? Corpses are easier to explain indoors."

He should have shut the door. Every instinct honed through five years of careful invisibility told him to shut the door, bar it, salt it, and let Corvale swallow whatever trouble she carried. Instead he stepped aside, because Bramble had been gone nine days, because the map lay torn in halves on his worktable, and because when she'd hesitated at the ford—when she'd seen him throw himself between a hunter's blade and a child—he had known, with the terrible certainty of a charm-seller reading a cracked amulet, that something in her had already broken from the rest of them.

Sera Vane stepped into the charm-seller's quiet like a hawk stepping into a songbird's cage, all coiled awareness and barely leashed purpose. She pushed back her hood. There was a bruise along her jaw and a burn scar fresh on her left hand, the kind a seal leaves when it's torn from flesh rather than wax.

"I burned my commission," she said, before he could ask. "Three nights ago. They noticed."

* * *

She told it plainly, standing among his shelves of harmless charms—luck-knots and dream-bells and little woven stars that did nothing but look pretty, the only magic Corvale permitted because it wasn't magic at all. She told him about the register house she'd been ordered to burn, and the children's toys inside charmed for good dreams. About falsifying her first report while her commander watched, saying nothing, which was worse than any reprimand. About Bramble, held in the cells beneath the Hall of Judgement, questioned not for treason but for names—and about what Bramble had given her instead.

"The Veilwraith," Hazel said. The word still felt like a splinter working under the skin. "You believe it now."

"I interrogated a fae-tinkerer for six days." Her mouth twisted. "I expected lies. I got a history lesson instead. Bound oaths, hungry things wearing borrowed crowns, a king who hasn't cast a shadow since midsummer." She flexed her burned hand. "I've watched him at court, Dunmore. At certain angles, in certain light, there's nothing on the floor where his shadow should be. I told myself it was the candles. I told myself a great many things were candles."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in an outlaw charm-seller's shop at three in the morning asking him to mend a map." She reached into her coat and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, setting it on the worktable with the care owed to a relic or a wound. "And bringing you this."

Bramble's freedom had cost more than a seal. It had cost Sera a forged transfer order, a bribed gaoler, and a route out of the city through the coal-chutes beneath the Hall of Bells—a route she'd memorized years ago and never thought she'd use against her own. Bramble was waiting now in a smuggler's loft by the river, furious, singed, and demanding to see Hazel before dawn.

"He wouldn't tell me why," Sera said. "Only that you'd need him. And that—" she hesitated, and for the first time the witch-hunter looked uncertain, "—that the map has to be whole before you understand what you're carrying."

Hazel unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay the two halves of the stranger's map, torn clean down the middle the night Odile's betrayal scattered them at the ford. He had kept them separate ever since, wrapped apart, as if distance might dilute whatever they meant. Now he laid them side by side on the worktable, and his breath went shallow.

The tear ran straight through the heart of the drawing. On the left half: Corvale, rendered in fine ink, its streets and bells and palace towers—and beneath it, drawn in a different hand, older, the Thorn Gate with its seven-fold lock. On the right half: the otherworld, the thorned country he had fled, its roads winding toward a horizon marked with a single word in a language he had sworn never to read again.

Between the halves, where the tear crossed, lines that had never met now yearned toward each other. He could see it even unjoined: the gate's lock on one side, and on the other, faint as breath on glass, the answer to it.

"Mend it," Sera said. "Please. Before I lose my nerve and arrest us both."

He worked the way he'd been taught before he'd learned to be afraid—flour paste and thorn-ash sizing, a warm iron, strips of vellum laid crosswise over the wound. His hands remembered. That was the cruelty of hands; they kept faith with the past whether you wanted them to or not. As he pressed each strip flat, the shop around them seemed to lean closer, the candle flame standing unnaturally tall, and below the floorboards the Thorn Gate's hum deepened by a single tone, like a struck bell deciding to listen.

When it was done, the map lay whole, and Hazel understood, all at once and completely, why the stranger had died rather than surrender it.

* * *

The two halves were not two maps. They were one sentence, split so that neither half could speak it alone.

On the joined surface, new lines surfaced as the paste dried—lines that had been invisible on each fragment separately, visible only where the tear was healed. They spiraled outward from the Thorn Gate in both directions at once, threading through Corvale's foundations and into the thorned country beyond, until they converged on a figure drawn small and precise at the map's center.

A figure holding a key. A figure with one foot drawn in ink the color of this world, and one foot in silver that shimmered like the otherworld's light.

Below it, in the old tongue—the language of the gatekeepers' dead order, the language Hazel had spent five years refusing to read—ran a single line. He read it despite himself. Some part of him had always been going to read it.

*The door knows its keeper. Only blood of both worlds may turn the key.*

"That's you," Sera said quietly. It wasn't a question. She was looking at the figure, then at him, with the expression of a woman assembling a horror out of ordinary pieces. "The stranger. The map. The gate under your shop. You're not just its neighbor, Dunmore. You're its—"

"Don't say it." His voice came out rough. "Five years ago I sealed that gate and swore I'd never touch it again. I didn't do it because I was chosen. I did it because I was *running*, and the gate was the fastest way to stop being followed."

"But the map says—"

"The map says what it says." He stared at the silver-footed figure, at the spiral lines binding both worlds together like stitches through a wound, and felt the old shame rise in his throat, hot and familiar. Half his blood belonged here, to a hedge-witch mother buried outside Corvale's walls. The other half belonged there, to a father he'd never speak of, in a country of thorns he'd abandoned along with everything else. He had spent his whole life making himself small enough to fit in neither place. Apparently the door had been keeping count.

Then he saw the rest of it. The thing he'd missed because grief was shouting too loudly in his ears.

The spiral lines didn't end at the gate. One of them continued—thin, deliberate, unmistakable—from the gate's lock, up through the map's rendering of Corvale, through the palace foundations, through the throne room itself, and terminated at the crown.

The Veilwraith's crown.

"No," Hazel whispered.

"What?" Sera leaned in. "What is it?"

He turned the candle toward the line, traced it with a finger that shook slightly and hated itself for shaking. "This thread. It runs from the gate to the throne. The wraith isn't just feeding on the city's magic—it's anchored. To the gate. To the *lock*." He looked up at her, and the dread beneath five years of routine finally broke the surface and showed its face. "It can't open the gate itself. Whatever oath binds it, whatever rules it broke to wear a king, it doesn't have the blood. It needs someone who does."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere above, snow ticked against the shutters. Below, the Thorn Gate hummed its patient note, and for the first time Hazel heard what had been wrong with it all these months. It wasn't humming to itself.

It was humming *toward* something. Calling. Or answering a call.

"It wants me to open it," he said. "That's why the wraith hunts witch-folk and burns charm-houses and purges anyone with a drop of the old blood. Not just to feed. To *drive*. To leave no one else. So that when the solstice comes, the only creature in either world carrying both worlds' blood is standing exactly where it wants him—with a key in reach and no way out but through."

Sera had gone pale. "The purge announcement. Solstice eve. It's not a purge, it's a—"

"A funnel." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle like the map had been. "Every road leads to the gate. Every hunt, every decree, every burning. It's been herding me for five years, Sera. Maybe longer. Maybe since the day I sealed it. Maybe the stranger didn't find me by accident. Maybe the *map* found me."

"Then don't open it."

"And let it keep the king? Let it bleed the city dry until there's nothing left worth ruling?" He pressed his palms flat against the mended map, feeling the vellum's warmth, the faint pulse beneath it that might have been imagination and might have been the gate answering his touch. "Bramble always said a seal isn't peace. It's just a slower war. I chose the slow war because I was a coward. I'm done choosing it."

"That's exactly what it wants you to say."

"I know." The words tasted like ash and honesty. "That's the trap, isn't it? It doesn't need to force me. It just needs me to decide that walking through that door is my own idea." He met her eyes. "So we make it our idea instead. All three of us. We go through the gate on our terms, with your knowledge of the palace wards and Bramble's knowledge of the otherworld and whatever I've got that passes for courage. If it wants me at that door, then it's going to get me there—accompanied, armed, and thoroughly inconvenient."

Sera studied him for a long moment. Outside, the bell tower began to toll the fourth hour, each stroke falling through the frozen air like a stone dropped down a well.

"You understand," she said at last, "that this is precisely the kind of plan that gets everyone killed."

"I'm a charm-seller. Everything I sell is luck. I'm due some."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first he'd ever seen there. "Bramble's waiting by the river. He said—if you'll forgive the phrasing—that if you weren't ready by dawn he'd come drag you out by your ridiculous ears."

"That sounds like him." Hazel began gathering the map, the key-tools, the few charms that were not harmless at all, hidden behind false shelf-backs for five years against a night he'd prayed would never come. His hands were steady now. Steadier than they'd been since the ford. Fear, he was discovering, hardened into resolve the way water hardens into ice—all it needed was a cold enough truth.

At the door, Sera paused, hood raised, snow-light framing her like a saint in a window nobody prayed to anymore.

"Dunmore. The line on the map. From the gate to the crown." Her voice dropped. "If the wraith is anchored to the lock, and you're the only one who can turn the key—then when you open that gate, you won't just be opening a door. You'll be holding its leash. For one moment, maybe two."

He hadn't thought of that. The realization settled over him slowly, vast and cold, and beneath the fear he felt something else stir—something dangerously close to purpose.

"Then we'd best make sure," Hazel said, "that in that moment, I'm the one holding it."

They stepped out together into the snow, two traitors to two worlds, and behind them in the dark beneath the floorboards, the Thorn Gate fell silent for the first time in five years—

—as if it, too, had heard the plan, and approved.
