## 15. The Price of Keys

The forge beneath the chapel had not been lit in thirty years, and when Father Ilex coaxed it awake with a word that was half prayer and half profanity, the whole room seemed to remember itself. Shadows leaned back from the walls. The thorn-rooted pillars overhead—those pale, fibrous columns Hazel still could not look at without his stomach turning—seemed to shiver, as if the city's living bones felt the heat.

"Sit," Ilex said, without turning from the anvil. "Or pace. You're the pacing sort."

Hazel paced. He had been pacing for six hours, since before midnight, while the old priest worked the twin key out of a bar of grave-iron and a coil of Hazel's own hair and a sliver of thorn-wood Bramble had carried against his heart for five years. Sera sat on a fallen pew with her sword across her knees, sharpening it in slow, even strokes, though there was nothing left in Corvale for her to cut. Bramble dozed in the corner with his bandaged wrists folded in his lap, and even in sleep he looked like a man listening for boots on stairs.

"You'll ruin the edge," Hazel said, to say something.

"It's not the edge I'm keeping sharp." Sera did not look up. "It's my hands. They've spent four years learning to point at people like you. They need reminding which way is wrong."

* * *

Near dawn, Ilex held the key up to the forge-light, and Hazel forgot how to breathe.

It was small—no longer than his thumb—and ugly, a knotted black thing like a wasp's nest crossed with a bird's skull. But it moved. That was the horror and the wonder of it: the key turned slowly over in the priest's palm of its own accord, its three prongs flexing like fingers remembering a grip, and where the light touched it, it drank the light and gave something else back—a color Hazel had no name for, the color of the hour between dusk and true dark, the color of the otherworld's sky.

"It wants to be used," Ilex said. His voice was rough with smoke and age. "That's the nature of keys. They don't care what they open. Only that they open."

"Then we won't let it care," Hazel said.

Ilex laughed, a dry rustle. "You sound like your sister."

Hazel stopped pacing. The words landed in him like a stone dropped down a well—he heard them fall and fall and never heard them strike bottom. "You never met her."

"No. But you've spoken her name in your sleep twice tonight, boy, and I've been sitting close enough to hear it." The priest set the key down on a square of altar cloth with the tenderness of a man laying an infant in a cradle. "Wren. Isn't that what you called her?"

"Wren," Hazel said. It came out broken. He hadn't said it aloud in five years—not to Bramble, not to anyone—and now it hung in the forge-smoke between them, small and bright and unbearable.

Ilex wiped his hands on his cassock and came to stand before him. Up close the priest smelled of iron and incense and, faintly, of green things—the smell of the thorn that ran through the city's foundations, through everything, through Hazel himself if he was honest, because he had crossed that gate once and come back and no one ever comes all the way back.

"I asked for your confession," Ilex said. "You gave it. All of it. The guardian at the gate, the choice you made, the sister you left standing watch. Do you know why I demanded it?"

"Because the key needs truth to bind it. You said."

"That too." The old man's eyes were pale as river-washed stones. "But mostly because a man who has confessed can be trusted with a door. A man who hasn't will only use it to run again." He pressed the key into Hazel's hand and closed Hazel's fingers over it with both of his own, gnarled and furnace-hot. "Don't run this time."

The key pulsed once against Hazel's palm, warm as a sleeping bird.

* * *

They came at moonset.

Sera heard them first—she was always hearing things first, a habit of four years' hunting that she could not unlearn any more than Hazel could unlearn flinching at bells—and she rose from the pew without a word, hand raised, and the room went still as held breath.

Boots. Many. On the chapel floor above them, moving with the deliberate spread of men who have been told exactly where to go and exactly what they will find.

"The smuggler's tunnel?" Hazel whispered.

"Flooded since autumn. I told you."

"The crypt stair?"

"Leads up into the nave. Into them." Sera drew her sword without a whisper of steel; she'd oiled it hours ago, knowing. Her face in the forge-light was calm in a way that frightened him more than panic would have. "There's the bell-rope shaft. Narrow. We could squeeze through, maybe two of us, maybe not three, and they'd be behind us in minutes anyway."

Bramble was already awake, already on his feet, his ruined wrists tucked against his chest. "Then we don't run up," he said. "We run down. The catacombs under the undercroft—they connect to the old processional way, and the processional way runs straight to the Thorn Gate. Ilex showed me the first night."

"And how long does that buy us?" Sera asked.

"Minutes," Bramble said. "Maybe ten."

"Ten is enough," said Father Ilex.

They all turned. The priest had walked past them to the foot of the crypt stair, and he stood there now with his back to them, looking up into the dark where the boots were gathering, and he was doing something with his hands—tracing signs in the air, slow and precise, signs so old that Hazel felt them in his teeth rather than saw them with his eyes.

"What are you doing?" Hazel asked, though he already knew. He knew the shape of warding when he saw it; he had been born knowing it, the way some children are born knowing how to swim.

"Holding the door," Ilex said mildly. "That's rather the family trade, isn't it? Doors."

"You can't hold a whole company of witch-hunters. Not alone. Not—" Hazel heard his own voice climb and hated it. "Not with hedge-craft. It would burn you out. It would burn you out in minutes."

"Yes," said Father Ilex pleasantly. "I imagine it will."

"No." Hazel stepped forward. "No, we find another way, there's always another—"

"Hazel." The priest turned, and his face was lit from below by the dying forge, and it was the face of a man who had made peace with something decades ago and had only been waiting for the moment to arrive. "I am seventy-one years old. I buried my god thirty years ago and kept saying his mass anyway, because the words were beautiful and someone ought to say them. I have spent my life tending a faith whose object was gone, and do you know what I learned?" He smiled, and it transformed him, made him young for one heartbeat. "The tending was the faith. The object was never the point."

Above them, a boot found the first step of the crypt stair.

"They will come down those stairs," Ilex said, "and I will stand here, and I will hold the ward until the last of you is through the processional door, and then I will hold it a little longer, because that is what wards are for. And then—" he shrugged, a small, almost embarrassed motion, "—then I will finally get to meet whatever it was I've been praying to all these years. I confess I'm curious."

"There has to be—"

"Boy." The old man's voice cracked like a whip, and then gentled just as fast. "Five years ago you left your sister behind because you could not bear to spend yourself. I know. You told me. You've been paying for it every day since, in the coin of small cowardices." He took Hazel's shoulder, and his grip was iron despite his years. "Do not make me into another debt. Make me into a reason. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you walk through that gate tomorrow with your spine straight or your head bowed."

The boots were louder now. Somewhere above, a man's voice, low and commanding: *the undercroft, check the undercroft.*

Ilex released him and turned back to the stair. He lifted his arms, and the air around him began to shimmer like heat off summer stone.

"Bramble," he said, without looking around, "take them down. Now."

* * *

Hazel went. That was the thing he would carry afterward, the thing that would sit in his chest like a swallowed coal for the rest of his life: he went. Sera caught his arm and pulled, and Bramble pushed from behind, and the three of them stumbled through the hidden door in the undercroft wall and down into the processional dark, and behind them the light of the ward bloomed up the stairwell like a second dawn, white and gold and terrible.

He heard the hunters shout. He heard the ward answer—a sound like a cathedral bell struck underwater, vast and mournful. He heard Father Ilex begin, incredibly, to sing. Some old rite in a dead tongue, his voice thin and cracked and absolutely steady, rising over the clash of steel on unseen air.

And then the processional door swung shut behind them, and the song was cut to silence, and there was only the dark, and the three of them running.

* * *

The old processional way ran beneath the city like a vein beneath skin. It had been built—no, Hazel corrected himself, watching the walls slide past in the light of Sera's shuttered lantern—it had been *grown*, corridors of pale thorn-wood polished smooth by centuries of bare feet, arched overhead in ribs like the inside of some enormous gentle beast. Once it had connected the palace to the gate, so that kings could walk between worlds in ceremony and safety. Now it connected a smuggler's crypt to a sealed door, and the only feet upon it were theirs.

Bramble led, limping, one hand trailing the wall. Sera followed with the lantern. Hazel came last with the key clenched in his fist, and he did not let himself think about the singing, or the silence after the singing, or the coal in his chest.

"He knew," Sera said quietly, after a long time. "He knew they were coming. He'd known since yesterday—that's why he finished the key when he did. Why he rushed the binding."

"He bought us the night," Hazel said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "That's what he'd say. He'd say it like a joke."

"He'd say it exactly like a joke," Bramble agreed softly, "and then he'd pour you a drink and refuse to let you toast anything solemn."

They ran on. Or walked fast—running was impossible in the narrow dark, and grief made their legs heavy—but it felt like running, the walls streaming past, the lantern swinging shadows, the key pulsing against Hazel's palm like a second heartbeat that was not his own.

Twice they passed side passages, and twice the key grew cold in his hand, and Bramble shook his head: *wrong way.* The third passage made the key burn, and they took it, and the floor began to slope downward, and the air changed—thicker, greener, tasting of rain on hot stone and something sweeter underneath, something like overripe fruit, like honey left too long in the sun.

The otherworld's breath, seeping through a sealed door.

"We're close," Hazel whispered.

* * *

They came up out of the processional way into the old gate-chamber, and Hazel stopped as he always stopped, as he had stopped every night for five years, though he had never entered by this door before—only crept down through his own cellar, alone, in the smallest hours, to check the seals and tell himself the world was safe and hate himself a little more each time.

The Thorn Gate filled the far wall.

It was not a door, not truly. It was a wound in the world shaped like a door—an archway of living thorn grown dense and black as iron, woven so tight that no light passed through it, and across its face the old seals lay like scars: bands of silvered charm-work, Hazel's own work, laid down night after patient night five years ago, layered and cross-hatched and reinforced until the arch was more scar than thorn. Through the gaps between the bands, if you looked too long—and Hazel had always looked too long—you could see movement. Slow, green, patient movement. The otherworld pressing its face against the glass.

Tonight the movement was faster.

"Solstice," Bramble murmured. He had gone gray around the mouth. "The veil thins. It knows we're coming. It's been waiting five years for us to come."

"Not five years," Hazel said. "Longer than that. It's been waiting since the oath was sworn." He walked toward the gate, and the key rose in his hand without his willing it, straining toward the thorn like a compass needle finding north at last. "Since before any of us. Since a king was afraid and made a bargain instead of asking for help."

Sera planted the lantern on the chamber floor and unsheathed her sword, and this time the steel sang freely, ringing off the thorn walls. "How long do we have?"

Hazel tilted his head, listening—to the city above, to the distant bells that never rang in Corvale anymore except by decree, to the pulse of the key. "The solstice moon rises within the hour. The gate will only take the key at full rise. Before that, we wait. After that—" he looked at the scarred arch, at the slow green pressure behind it, at everything on the other side that he had run from and was about to walk into willingly, "—after that, we open it."

"And on the other side," Sera said, "your sister."

"My sister," Hazel said. The coal in his chest shifted, and for the first time it hurt in a way that felt less like burning and more like warmth. "And the guardian. And whatever's left of the road to the wraith's root. And every debt I owe, all coming due at once."

Bramble lowered himself onto a fallen block of thorn-stone with a grunt, cradling his bound wrists. "Then rest," he said. "All of you. Grief and fear are heavy packs. Set them down for an hour, or you'll arrive at the gate already spent." He closed his eyes. Within moments—for fae tire strangely and wake strangely—his breathing slowed.

Sera did not sit. She stood facing the stair they had come by, sword drawn, listening to the city that had been hers to hunt and was now hers to defend, and Hazel watched her and understood that she would stand like that until the moon rose, and that no power in either world could move her.

He sat down beside the lantern instead, across from the gate, and opened his hand, and looked at the key.

Small. Ugly. Alive. Made of grave-iron and his own hair and a sliver of heart-wood, forged by a dead man's final morning. It turned slowly in his palm, prongs flexing, wanting the lock.

"Ilex said keys don't care what they open," Hazel said quietly, to the key, to the gate, to the priest who could no longer hear him. "Only that they open. So I'm going to care enough for both of us, old man. I'm going to care so much it counts for everyone who wouldn't."

Above them, muffled by earth and thorn and stone, the bells of Corvale began to ring—wrong, arrhythmic, the Veilwraith's solstice peal summoning its purge, calling the fearful to the feast. The sound bled down through the living rock, and the thorn walls trembled, and behind the gate's scarred seal the green pressure quickened like a heartbeat answering a drum.

Hazel got to his feet. The key burned in his fist like a coal finally given leave to become fire.

Through a fissure in the chamber's high ceiling, he could see a sliver of sky, and in it, climbing slow and enormous and white, the solstice moon began to rise.
