## 16. Beyond the Veil

The gate did not open so much as *admit* them.

Hazel had expected a door swinging wide, a rush of wild air, the otherworld spilling through like water through a cracked dam. Instead the Thorn Gate simply grew quiet—the thorns along its arch drawing back one by one, like a fist uncurling finger by finger—and the space between its branches became a passage of deep green shadow that smelled of rain on old stone.

Sera went first, because she insisted, her hunter's blade drawn and her face set in the expression Hazel was beginning to recognize as her version of courage: jaw tight, eyes moving, refusing to be impressed by anything. Bramble followed, his copper tools chiming softly at his belt, muttering wards under his breath in a language that made the thorns shiver. Hazel came last, and he paused with one hand on the gate's living wood, feeling its heartbeat against his palm—slow, vast, patient as roots.

*You know me,* he thought at it. *You've always known me.*

The gate said nothing. Gates never did. But it let him pass, and that was answer enough.

Then the world turned over, and they were through.

* * *

The otherworld at dusk looked exactly as Hazel remembered it, which was the cruelest thing about it.

The sky hung low and violet, stitched with stars too large and too near, as if the night were a tapestry someone had hung carelessly close overhead. The land rolled away from the gate in hills of black grass silvered by moonglow, and everywhere—everywhere—the thorns. They grew in great winding hedgerows taller than cathedrals, their branches braided into arches and spirals and staircases that led nowhere, blooming with white flowers that opened as the three travelers passed, then closed behind them like eyes deciding not to watch.

"It's beautiful," Sera said, and her voice had gone strange. She kept touching things—the grass, a thorn, the air itself—as if checking whether they were real. "It's horrible and beautiful."

"That's the otherworld for you," Bramble said. "It never lets you have just the one feeling." He glanced at Hazel, and there was something careful in the old fae's face, something he'd been carrying since the gate. "You remember the way?"

"I remember everything," Hazel said. "That's the problem."

Because he did. Five years since he'd crossed back through this gate with thorn-scratches down his arms and a scream lodged in his throat, five years of telling himself he'd had no choice, and every step now was a step his feet remembered better than his mind wanted them to. The path wound between two hedgerows toward the low dark line of the Watchwood, and at the edge of the Watchwood stood the standing stone where—

Where she had told him to run.

He stopped walking. Sera noticed first; hunters noticed everything. "What is it?"

"Nothing. Keep moving."

"Hazel." Bramble's voice, quiet. "We're not doing this again. You don't get to carry it silently all the way to the wraith's root and let it fester. Say it or don't, but walk either way."

Hazel walked. He didn't say it. But the word *run* echoed in him anyway, in her voice, young and fierce and terrified, *Run, Hazel, I'll hold it, run*—and he had run. That was the whole of it. That was the shape of the thing he'd pressed down for five years until it fit inside him without showing, the way a charm-seller learns to make magic invisible.

They made camp that night in the hollow of a fallen hedge-giant, its trunk hollowed into a room of smooth grey wood. Sera took first watch because she didn't trust anything, including sleep. Bramble curled up like a fox. Hazel lay staring at the impossible stars through a gap in the branches and did not sleep at all.

Somewhere out in the black grass, something was walking. Not toward them. Just walking. Keeping pace with the camp, patient as the tide, stopping when they stopped. It had been doing it since sundown.

"Bramble," Hazel whispered.

"I hear it." The old fae didn't open his eyes. "Been hearing it for hours. It's not hunting us."

"How do you know?"

"Because if it were hunting us, we'd already be dead." Bramble opened one eye, luminous in the dark. "In here, that's not fatalism. That's arithmetic."

* * *

They reached the river on the second day—or what the map called a river, though it ran silver and slow and uphill in places, and fish the color of stained glass watched them pass with frank, unhurried interest. On the far bank rose the Thornharrow, the fortress-hedge where the gatekeepers of the old order had kept their court, and where the guardian of the crossing had held her watch for as long as anyone could remember.

Hazel had told Sera and Bramble almost nothing about the guardian. He'd told them there was one, that it would test them, that it had reason to hate him. He had not told them why. Some debts you pay in private or not at all.

The bridge over the silver river was grown from white thorn, arched high as a rainbow, and halfway across it the guardian came to meet them.

She came out of the air itself, the way the powerful ones do—not walking so much as arriving gradually, the violet sky bending around her until she stood at the bridge's crown with the light behind her. She was tall, robed in living bramble that flowered and fruited as it moved, and her face was hidden beneath a helm woven of blackthorn, its branches closed over her features like fingers laced over a mouth.

Sera's blade cleared its sheath. Bramble's hand found his tools. Hazel didn't move at all, because some part of him had known, from the first night of footsteps in the grass, from the moment the gate had admitted them so gently—he had known who waited here, and knowing had not made it bearable.

"Who crosses the Veil?" The voice was the voice of hedges in wind, but under it, threaded through it, was another voice—a human voice, worn thin by years.

"The charm-seller of Corvale," Hazel said. His own voice came out steady, which surprised him. "And two who come under his protection."

"Charm-seller." A pause. The helm tilted, birdlike. "I knew a liar by that name once."

"You knew a coward by that name," Hazel said. "The liar came later."

The guardian laughed, and the sound broke something open in his chest, because he knew that laugh. He had grown up under that laugh, in a cottage at the edge of the Watchwood, before the oaths and the gates and the crown across the Veil. He had spent five years making himself forget the exact sound of it, and here it was, unchanged, and forgetting had accomplished nothing at all.

"Take off the helm," he said quietly. "Please. You know I know. You've known since the gate."

The thorn-fingers unlaced slowly. Branch by branch the blackthorn helm drew back, and beneath it was a face Hazel had last seen younger than his own memory of it—younger, because grief keeps people frozen at the age you lost them—and older, impossibly older, weathered by a vigil that had lasted far past any natural span. Her hair had gone the white of hawthorn blossom. Her eyes were still her eyes: brown, sharp, and full of a love so old and so angry it looked, at first glance, exactly like hatred.

"Hello, Hazel," said Rowan Dunmore.

* * *

Sera had a hundred questions. Hazel could see them queuing behind her teeth—*your sister? your sister is the guardian? your sister has been here five years?*—and to her lasting credit she asked none of them aloud. She sheathed her blade and stepped back to stand beside Bramble, giving the bridge to the two of them, which was the kindest tactical retreat Hazel had ever witnessed.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said. It came out wrong, all wrong, an accusation instead of a prayer. "Bramble told me—I asked, after, I asked everyone—the wights took the crossing, and you were holding it alone, and no one—"

"No one came back to check." Rowan's voice was very even. "No. They didn't."

"I came back. I—" His hands were shaking. He shoved them into his coat. "I stood on the near side of the gate for three days. I couldn't—"

"Three days." She nodded slowly, and each nod was a small cold thing. "Three days, and then you sealed the gate from your side and built yourself a little shop and sold little charms to frightened people, and you called it penance."

"It wasn't penance. It was hiding. I know the difference now."

"Do you?" She came down the bridge toward him, and the bramble robe parted around her steps, and up close he could see what the long watch had done: thorn-scars lacing her forearms like lacework, a streak of bark grown into the skin at her temple where the otherworld had begun, patiently, to claim her. "Because from where I stood, brother, it looked like this: the wraith's host poured through the crossing, and I held the ward, and I screamed at you to run and bring help, and you ran, and the help never came. And when the host finally broke and I lay bleeding on this bridge thinking *he'll come back, he always comes back, he's coming back*—the gate stayed shut. For five years. Do you know what I did in those five years, Hazel? Do you want to know?"

"No," he said honestly. "But tell me anyway."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, maybe, or the ghost of the girl who used to laugh at his honesty. "I held the crossing," she said. "Alone. Because that was my oath, and unlike some people, I keep mine. And the otherworld watched me do it, and the otherworld is not cruel, whatever your city thinks—it is only *thorough*. It saw a keeper who would not leave her post, and it began to make her suited to the post. Bark. Bloom. Years that don't pass so much as accumulate." She lifted her hand, and a white flower opened along her knuckle. "Another decade, and there wouldn't have been a Rowan left to meet you. Only the guardian. Only the post."

The wind moved through the hedgerows, and the whole world sighed.

"I'm sorry," Hazel said.

"I know."

"I should have come back. I should have torn the gate open with my bare hands. I was afraid—"

"Yes."

"—not of the wight-host. Of failing. If I came back and you were dead, then it was real, and if I stayed away, some part of me could keep believing you'd walked out of the Watchwood someday and knocked on my shop door and called me a fool for worrying." His voice cracked clean through. "I let you become a story I hadn't finished. That's what I did, Rowan. I made you into an unfinished story because finishing it hurt."

For a long moment she only looked at him. Behind her the violet sky burned low, and the white flowers along the bridge opened and closed like breathing.

"There it is," she said softly. "Five years, and the truth finally crosses the Veil before you do." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the starlight caught in her eyes. "Do you know why I let you think I might be dead? Why I never sent word through the gate, though I could have—though the gate whispers both ways for a keeper?"

"Why?"

"Because as long as you believed it, you'd stay in Corvale. Safe. Small. Hidden." Her mouth twisted. "I was angry, Hazel. I was so angry that I chose your safety as a weapon. I let you live half a life because you'd cost me most of mine. That was my debt, and I've carried it as long as you've carried yours." She exhaled, and it shivered the flowers. "So. We are both cowards, and we are both liars, and we are both sorry. Shall we call it even and save the world, or would you rather stand on this bridge apologizing until the solstice takes Corvale without us?"

A laugh tore out of him, ragged and wet and real—the first true laugh since the stranger had died in his back room. "Even," he managed. "Gods. Even."

She embraced him then, thorns and bark and hawthorn-blossom hair and all, and her arms were strong around him, and somewhere in the middle of it he stopped being thirty-one and became eight years old again for exactly four seconds, which was four seconds more than he'd had in five years.

When she released him, her face was the guardian's face again—but warmer now, the way a house is warm when someone has just lit the fire.

* * *

"Now," she said, turning to Sera and Bramble, who had the decency to look only mildly astonished. "The passage beyond. You should understand what you're asking. Past the Thornharrow lies the deep thorn, and in the deep thorn, the root-lair where the thing wearing your king's crown keeps its heart. My watch ends at the boundary; I cannot go with you. What I can do is open the way—and tell you what the way costs."

"Costs," Sera repeated flatly. "Of course it costs. Everything in this story costs."

"Everything true does," Rowan said. "The passage opens for debt paid. That is the law under the Veil—older than kings, older than the wraith. No one walks the deep thorn owing more than they admit." Her eyes came back to Hazel. "Yours is the largest, brother. The gate will weigh it. So before we reach the boundary, you must accept what you owe—not say sorry, not feel sorry. *Accept* it. Name it. Take it into your hands and carry it the rest of the way. Can you do that?"

Hazel looked at his sister—at the bark at her temple, at the five years written on her in thorn-script—and understood that this was the test, and that it had begun the moment she removed the helm, and that it would not end until the wraith was ash.

He thought of the shop. The sign. The small safe life, the charms that did nothing, the invisibility he'd mistaken for peace. He thought of the stranger's blood on his floorboards, and the map, and the words *the king is already gone.* He thought of Father Ilex falling with the ward blazing in his fists, buying them the night with his life. He thought of Bramble bound in thorns in a palace corridor, alive, waiting. He thought of Sera burning her commission seal in a gutter and choosing truth over everything she'd been.

Everyone had paid. Everyone had named their debt and carried it forward. It was his turn, and it had been his turn for five years.

"I owe Rowan five years of my presence," he said, and his voice did not shake, or rather it shook and he let it. "I owed her a rescue and gave her an absence. I owed the gatekeepers my service and gave them a locked door. I owe Corvale the truth of its king and have paid it in trinkets. I owe my sister—" his throat closed; he forced it open— "I owe my sister the rest of my life, however much of it she'll take, and I accept that. All of it. Every year of it. I stop running tonight."

The bridge hummed. Along its whole white length, the thorn-flowers opened at once, thousands of them, a silent applause of petals, and the silver river brightened as if a cloud had moved off a mirror.

Rowan smiled, and it was the smile from the cottage, from before everything. "Well," she said. "That'll do."

She raised her hand, and the Thornharrow's far wall split open along a seam no eye could find, revealing a road of black earth descending into country where the thorns grew dense as storm-clouds and red as old wounds.

"One more thing," Rowan said, and the warmth in her voice cooled into warning. "The wraith knows you're coming. It has known since the ford—it has been *counting* on you, Hazel, on your blood, on your guilt, on the gate answering your hand. Whatever it offers you at the root—and it will offer, it always offers—remember that the sweetest lie it owns is the one shaped exactly like what you want most." Her eyes held his. "Don't kneel. Whatever it shows you. Don't. Kneel."

Beyond the seam, far down in the red-dark of the deep thorn, something vast shifted in its sleep—and Hazel felt, faint as a pulse through the soles of his boots, the wraith's hunger turn toward them like a head.

"Come on, then," he said. "Let's go introduce ourselves."
