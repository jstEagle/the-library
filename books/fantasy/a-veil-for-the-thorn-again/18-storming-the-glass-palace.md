## 18. Storming the Glass Palace

The palace guard did not believe in shadows. That was the first thing Sera Vane understood as she stood before them in the frost-lit courtyard of the Glass Palace, her commission seal burned to slag in her pocket, thirty halberds leveled at her chest and Commander Rusk's voice still ringing in her memory like a struck bell: *Bring me the traitor Vane, dead or breathing.*

They believed in orders. They believed in pay and pensions and the king's word made law. Shadows were for children and charlatans.

So she gave them something they could not dismiss.

"Form a line," she said, "and watch."

She had chosen the hour with a conspirator's care—the changing of the morning watch, when the sun climbed low and long over the eastern towers and every man in the courtyard threw a shadow that stretched twenty feet across the flagstones like spilled ink. She stood at the center of it, hands open, empty, her witch-hunter's coat unbuttoned so they could see she carried no hidden blade.

"You've all seen His Majesty at court," she said. "You've knelt to him. You've sworn your oaths to him." She let the word *oath* hang there; Bramble's voice was still in her ear from three nights of interrogation-turned-conversation: *It feeds on bound oaths, girl. Every vow you swear to it is a thread in its web.* "So swear nothing now. Only look."

"Vane," said Captain Ossler, an old man who had taught her swordwork a lifetime ago, "you're either mad or bought, and I'll have you in irons either way—"

"The king casts no shadow."

The words dropped into the courtyard like a stone into a well. Somewhere above, a gull cried against the pale sky.

"At certain angles," Sera went on, walking slowly along the line of leveled halberds as though they were fence posts and not weapons, "at certain hours, he casts none at all. I have watched him for months. I told myself it was trickery of the light, glass and mirrors, some court illusion. Then I found this." She drew from her breast pocket a sheet of vellum—a copy, painstakingly inked by Father Ilex's own hand before his death, of the ward-diagrams Sera had memorized from the palace archives. "These are the wards on the throne room. Drawn by the royal masons two hundred years ago, when Aldric's grandfather built the Glass Palace. Look at what they were built to contain."

Ossler did not take the page. But a young guardsman near the end of the line did—young Pell, whose sister Sera had once pulled out of a burning charm-house, whose eyes had never quite forgiven her for the smoke—and his face, as he read, went the color of tallow.

"This says—" Pell's voice cracked. "Captain, this says the wards are for *holding something in*."

"They were renewed eleven years ago," Sera said quietly. "By decree. In secret. The same year the purges began. The same year the king stopped eating at public table. The same year, if any of you care to check the parish rolls, that Aldric's shadow stopped appearing in the stained glass of the Coronation Chapel—because glass remembers light, gentlemen, and light remembers what stands in it."

She let the silence do its work. Thirty men stared at her, and behind their eyes she could see the small treasons of memory assembling themselves: the king who no longer slept, the decrees that grew crueler each season, the purge lists posted on solstice eve with names that included children. The things they had each swallowed because swallowing was duty.

"His Majesty is ill," Ossler said at last, but his voice had lost its iron.

"Yes," Sera said. "Ill in a way no physician can name. And tonight, while we stand here arguing, the thing wearing his crown intends to open a door beneath this city and drown both worlds in what comes through it. I am not asking you to betray your king. I am asking you to *rescue* him."

She turned and pointed one gloved finger up at the high window of the throne room, where the winter sun struck full.

"And if you doubt me—look. Now. While the light is right."

Thirty faces turned. Thirty pairs of eyes found the window, and the silhouette within it: the tall figure standing at the throne room's heart, motionless as a mannequin, arms at its sides.

And around that figure, the sunlight fell clean to the floor. No shadow followed it. No shadow touched it at all.

Pell dropped his halberd. It rang on the stone like a funeral bell, and one by one, in ones and twos and then in a long clattering chorus, the rest followed.

* * *

Hazel felt the gate-wards break from half a mile away—a shudder through the thorn beneath the streets, like a great beast rolling over in its sleep—and knew Sera had done her work.

"Now," Bramble said, and kicked open the postern door.

They came into the Glass Palace not through its front but through its bones: the servants' warrens and coal-chutes that Hazel had spent two days mapping from Father Ilex's smugglers' routes, passages where the walls were wrong in a way only Hazel could feel. Because the walls were not stone. They had never been stone. Under the plaster and gilt, Corvale's palace was grown from living thorn, black and patient, and it had been growing toward the throne room for eleven years.

He could feel it reaching for him now. Whispers in the grain. *Open the gate, keeper. Open it wide.*

"Hazel." Bramble caught his sleeve. The fae-tinkerer's copper eyes were bright and hard in the torchlight. "Stay with me. This place eats dreamers."

"I'm not dreaming. I'm furious." He gripped the twin key under his shirt, warm against his sternum, and led on.

They broke from the warrens into the grand corridor of the west wing and found it transformed. Where last week there had been marble and portraits of dead kings, now thorn had erupted through the floor in ridges and buttresses, black vines thick as a man's waist, white flowers opening along them like watching eyes. Courtiers' abandoned shoes lay scattered. A single glove. Somewhere ahead, glass shattered and did not stop shattering.

"The wraith knows we're coming," Hazel breathed.

"No," Bramble said grimly, drawing a slender blade of green-gold metal from his coat. "Worse. It's been *waiting* for us. There's a difference, and you'd best learn it fast."

The first of the thorn-wights came out of a side passage like a spider from a wound—limbs of braided briar, a face like a knot in wood with a single hollow where an eye should be. Hazel's hand was moving before thought, magic rising off his skin in a cold blue wash, and he caught the creature mid-leap and *unmade* the binding holding it together, and it burst into drifting ash that smelled, horribly, of roses.

Three more came. Then six. The corridor became a running battle, Bramble's blade singing in arcs that left trails of sap-light, Hazel tearing holes in the thorn-walls to pass through, sealing them behind. His oath-breaking magic poured out of him freely now—he had stopped apologizing for it somewhere on Thornfield moor—but each working cost him, and he could feel the otherworld leaning close through every spell, hungry and familiar, like a house he'd fled calling his name.

They reached the antechamber of the throne room as the great doors began to open—not pushed, but *bloomed*, petals of glass peeling back—and beyond them Hazel saw the throne, and the tall figure upon it, and the vast root-mass rising behind the dais like a black tree crowned in the king's own banners.

And between them and the doors, the corridor filled.

Not with wights. With thorn itself. The walls convulsed, the floor split, and a wall of living briar rose across the antechamber, ten feet high, dense as iron, flowering white. Behind it, more growth thundered inward from every direction. Cutting through would take an hour they did not have. The solstice moon would rise within the hour, and the Veilwraith needed only Hazel's blood on the gate to end everything.

"It's penning us," Hazel said. "Like livestock."

"Aye." Bramble rolled his shoulders, and for the first time since Hazel had known him, the fae-tinkerer looked tired—not old, for the fae did not age, but *worn*, like a tool used well past its warranty. "And there's one way through a pen, lad, and it's over the top, and someone has to hold the fence down while the rest go over."

Hazel turned. "Bramble—"

"Don't." The tinkerer smiled, and it was the crooked, wicked smile of five years of friendship, of a man who had demanded Hazel's worst truth and given him his own in return: the exile, the debt, the shame. "I sealed that gate with you once. Sealing's done. Opening's the work now, and opening needs a keeper, not a tinkerer. Go."

"Bramble, there has to be another—"

"There's always another way. That's my whole trade." He pressed something into Hazel's hand—small, cold, a tinkerer's knot of copper wire shaped like a bramble crown. "For your sister. Tell her the fae remember her watch. Now *run*, Hazel Dunmore, and don't you dare look back, because I'll want to make a joke about it later and I can't make jokes at cowards."

Then Bramble walked into the thorn.

He did not fight it. That was the terrible grace of it. He walked in singing—an old fae road-song, nonsense syllables and bright melody—and the briar closed over him like water, and the whole mass of it *shuddered*, every vine in the antechamber straining toward him, toward the ancient fae-blood that was richer fare than any hedge-witch. The wall of thorn bowed inward, groaning, its attention fixed, its hunger fixed—

—and the way beyond it stood briefly, terribly clear.

Hazel ran. Through the blooming doors, across the shattered mosaic floor, the key hot against his chest, the whispers rising to a roar—*keeper, keeper, come home*—and he did not look back. He heard Bramble's song continue behind him, steady and unhurried, and then he heard it stop.

The silence after was worse than any scream.

* * *

Sera came up the grand stair with forty guardsmen at her back and found the west wing breathing.

There was no other word for it. The walls flexed. The sconces bent like grass. And everywhere the black thorn had grown, and along its length hung shapes that made her stomach drop: guardsmen, courtiers, servants, cocooned in briar, alive—she could see the rise and fall of chests—but bound, their faces slack with dreaming.

"Pell," she said. "Take ten men. Cut down everyone you can reach. Blades below the flowers—the flowers are eyes, don't touch them. The rest, with me."

They fought through the antechamber and found the battlefield already over: a wall of thorn collapsed inward around a single point, and at its center, bound in a lattice of black briar, a small figure with copper-bright hair and a face like a fox caught mid-laugh.

Bramble's eyes opened as Sera cut the first vine away.

"Took you long enough," he rasped. His voice was wrecked, but the wit in it survived. "Careful with the left arm, love. It's the only honest one I've got."

"We're getting you out."

"No, you're not." His hand closed on her wrist, shockingly strong. "Listen. The wraith's roots run under the throne—it's drawn most of its strength up top, which means the gate below is thinning. Hazel's down there alone. You have to get to him." He coughed, and the sound was wet. "I'm fae, huntress. Thorns that bind a fae bind deep. Cut me loose and I'm a dead weight on your sword-arm. Leave me bound and I live till spring, maybe longer. Faeries keep." The crooked smile again, weaker now. "Go turn the guard. Go save your king. And when you see that stubborn hedge-witch—"

"What?"

"Tell him the fence held."

Above them, through the broken ceiling, the sun reached the throne room floor—and Sera saw, cast huge across the mosaic, the shadows of the bound courtiers, the fallen guards, her own kneeling shape.

And beside hers, thin and gray and trembling, the shadow of the thing on the throne flickering like a candle about to gutter—as if, somewhere far beneath the glass and the gold, the true king had heard a door begin to open, and was fighting toward the light.

Sera stood, drew her blade, and turned toward the stair that led down into the dark.

"Hold on, Your Majesty," she said. "We're coming."

* * *

Below, in the dark beneath both worlds, Hazel Dunmore set his hand against the Thorn Gate, felt the twin key burn, and heard—faint, from very far away, carried on a wind that smelled of roses and copper—his sister's voice saying his name.

The gate began to open.
