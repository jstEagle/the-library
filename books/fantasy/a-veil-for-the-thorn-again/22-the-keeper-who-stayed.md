## 22. The Keeper Who Stayed

The solstice moon had come and gone, and the thorns had learned to bloom.

Hazel noticed it first on the mornings he climbed the stair from the cellar, when the light through the grate fell across the Thorn Gate and caught on things that had no business being there: white flowers no bigger than his thumbnail, opening along the black briar-wood of the doorframe like a slow exhalation. The first time he saw one, he had stood with his hand on the banister and his heart in his throat, certain it was a symptom, a rot, the beginning of some new hunger. He had fetched Bramble from the workshop at the top of the stairs, and Bramble had looked at it for a long moment through his brass spectacles and said, in the tone he saved for Hazel's more spectacular acts of ignorance:

"It's a door, you great soft fool. Doors are meant to be opened. You've finally opened one properly, and it's *grateful*."

Hazel had touched the flower then. It had been warm.

That had been three months ago. Now, on a bright spring morning with the bells of Corvale ringing the hour and the smell of bread drifting down from the street, Hazel stood in the cellar beneath his shop and did what he had done every morning since the wraith unraveled: he tended the gate.

It was not the work he had imagined for himself. He had imagined, in the years of hiding, that if he ever touched the Thorn Gate again it would be in terror, or desperation, or the cold arithmetic of sacrifice. He had imagined sealing it. Breaking it. Dying in front of it. He had never once imagined *sweeping* it. But that was the truth of a living bridge: it was less like a fortress and more like a garden, less like a wound and more like a hearth. The thorns along the arch had opinions. They leaned toward the light and away from iron. They grew an inch too far over the lintel if he neglected them, and they drooped, visibly drooped, when the crossing was quiet for too long.

"Greedy," Hazel told them, brushing fallen petals from the threshold with a broom of hazel-twigs that Bramble had charmed to never lose its bristles. "You had a whole otherworld to yourself for five years. Now you sulk because a Tuesday goes by without visitors."

The thorns rustled. He had decided, somewhere in the second month, that it was laughter.

He had decided a great many things in three months. That was what belonging did to a person, he was learning. When you stopped spending all your strength on being unseen, all that hoarded attention had to go somewhere, and it went into noticing. He noticed that the catacombs below the city, the ones grown through with living thorn, had begun to put out green. He noticed that Father Ilex's old smuggler-routes now carried pilgrims instead of contraband, and that the smuggler-priest's memory had a shrine at the crossing point, candles and river-stones and ribbons, because the city had decided it needed someone to thank and Ilex was not there to argue the point. He noticed that when he walked up his own street in daylight, in his own coat, with his own face, people nodded to him. Not the wary nod of neighbors to a stranger they'd rather not know. The other kind.

The bell above the shop door rang, and he heard the particular shuffle of feet he'd been waiting for.

"Up here," he called. "Or down here, rather. Mind the third stair; it still thinks it's a thorn."

Sera Vane came down into the cellar the way she came everywhere now: openly, her hunter's coat traded for a grey traveling cloak, her commission seal—what remained of it, a scorched disc of brass she kept on a cord—hanging where anyone could see it. She had refused a new seal from the council for two months. Last week she had finally accepted one, and Hazel had seen her turn it over in her fingers for a long time before she put it on, like a woman learning to hold something without expecting it to burn her.

"You're talking to the door again," she said.

"The door talks back."

"The door rustles. There's a difference, and it matters, because the last man who claimed the thorns talked to him ended up being a Veilwraith cultist, and I had to write the report." She said it dryly, but she stopped at the threshold the way she always did, a half-step back from the arch, her eyes moving over the gate with the old hunter's assessment—*what's wrong with this picture*—before she caught herself and let it go. Three months of practice, and still the reflex lived. Hazel understood. He had his own reflexes. He still checked the street for watchers before unlocking the shop, though there was nothing left in Corvale to outlaw what he was.

"News from the palace?" he asked.

"News." She handed him a folded paper, council-weight, heavy stock. "The first session of the joint court. Both worlds' delegates, actually in the same room, actually not throwing furniture. Aldric opened it himself." A pause, and something gentler came into her voice, the way it always did around the king's name now. "He's thin. He's grey. He laughs at things that aren't funny and then looks surprised at himself. But he's *there*. Every hour of every day, he's there, and he signs everything, and yesterday he asked me—" she stopped, shook her head. "He asked me to thank you. Again. He says he doesn't remember the years under the wraith, only the last moments, when it came apart. He says the last thing he felt before it let him go was someone telling it the truth about itself. He wants to know what truth does that."

Hazel folded the paper and put it in his apron pocket, next to the twine and the dried rowan. "It stops being hungry," he said. "That's all it ever was. A hunger that forgot what it was hungry *for*." He had thought about it a great deal, in the quiet nights, about the oath at the root of it all—some king, centuries dead, so afraid of losing his people that he had invited a thing to hold the walls for him, and the thing had held, and held, and held, until holding was all it knew how to be. Power was never stolen. It was invited. Bramble had said that in the root-lair, and Hazel had built his whole victory on it, and now he built his mornings on it too.

"Anyway," Sera said, and the dryness came back, but it was a different dryness now, an armor she wore loosely. "I didn't come about the council. I came because the crossing queue is out the door. Again. There's a fae delegation of some kind—Bramble's kin, I think, the ones who disowned him, come to see what he's built—and a merchant from the Winter Court who wants a permit, and a grandmother from the moor villages who insists her grandson wandered through the Gate on a dare and I am *not* paid enough to sort out which side of the door he belongs to."

"Is he fae?"

"Half. Like you."

"Then he belongs to both," Hazel said, "and both belong to him. That's the whole law. I wrote it on the sign."

"Your sign," Sera said, "is a piece of driftwood with paint on it."

"My sign," Hazel agreed, "is a piece of driftwood with paint on it, and it is *binding*."

* * *

He went up the stairs two at a time, the way he never had in the years of hiding, when he had learned to move through Corvale like water through cracks—silent, shapeless, apologetic. The shop smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and, faintly, of the otherworld, a green smell like rain on stone that had crept up through the floorboards and never left. The charm shelves stood where they had always stood, but they were honest now. No more "lucky buttons" that were only buttons. The little dream-charms for children hung beside charms that actually worked, each one tagged in his own hand with what it did and what it cost, because the new law required disclosure and because, more than the law, he required it. He had spent too many years being a small lie. He was done with small lies.

The queue stretched out the door and down the street into the sun: a knot of tall, bright-eyed fae in Bramble's family colors, looking around Corvale with the expressions of cats in a new house; the merchant, sweating under a hat with too many feathers; the grandmother, who had brought her own chair.

And at the front of the queue, apart from all of them, standing where the sunlight came through the shop window and laid her shadow long across the floorboards—

Wren.

His sister had a shadow. That was the thing he still could not get used to, the thing that caught in his chest every single time. Five years she had stood watch in the thorned otherworld, transformed by her long vigil into something half of the briar, and when he had come back to her in the deep of the gate's heart and spoken her name, the thorns had let go of her the way a fist lets go—slowly, finger by finger, reluctant even in mercy. But she had come back to a shape, and the shape had a shadow, and the shadow moved when she moved, and every morning Hazel saw it and had to press his hand flat against something until the moment passed.

"You're staring again," Wren said. She didn't look up from the charm she was examining—a simple one, a knot of rowan for safe travel, the first kind he had ever learned to make, back when they were children in the otherworld and their mother had put one around each of their necks.

"You're early. The crossing doesn't open till noon."

"The crossing," Wren said, "is a door in a cellar, and I have been standing in doorways for five years. I think I've earned the right to be on either side of one whenever I like." She set the charm down and looked at him then, and her eyes were her own eyes, brown and sharp and tired in the way of someone who has slept a very long time and is not yet done being tired. "Besides. I wanted to see it before the crowd. The sign."

Hazel followed her gaze to the window.

There were two signs now. The old one—*H. DUNMORE, CHARM-SELLER, FINE WORK, FAIR PRICES*—hung in its familiar place, sun-faded, the paint he had touched up every year of his hiding so that the shop would look ordinary, permanent, unremarkable. And beside it, on a plank of driftwood Bramble had pulled from the otherworld's shore, in letters Hazel had painted himself, slowly, on an afternoon when his hand would not stop shaking:

*GATEKEEPER.*

"It's crooked," Wren said.

"It's *rustic*."

"It's crooked, and the K is backwards, and I can see the place where you repainted the E because you spelled it wrong the first time."

"I did not spell it wrong. Bramble distracted me."

"Bramble was in the room for four seconds and said one word, which was *tea*."

"Then it was a very distracting word." Hazel came and stood beside her at the window, and together they looked out at the street—at the queue, at the fae delegation peering into the window with open curiosity, at a pair of city children who had stopped to stare at the tall bright strangers and were being sternly hauled along by a mother who then, Hazel noticed, smiled at him. At him. At the gatekeeper with the crooked sign. "I keep thinking," he said, quieter, "that I spent five years perfecting the art of not being looked at. And now I've hung a sign that says *look here*. And it turns out that was all it ever took. A plank of wood and some paint. I could have belonged the whole time."

"No, you couldn't." Wren said it simply, without cruelty, the way she had always said true things. "The city wasn't ready, and neither were you. Belonging that costs you your truth isn't belonging; it's just a bigger hiding place." She touched the window glass, over the word GATEKEEPER, and her shadow touched it too. "You had to open the gate before you could hang the sign. Not the other way around."

Below them, faintly, through the floor, the thorns rustled.

"They agree with me," Wren said.

"You can't possibly know that."

"I was a thorn for five years, Hazel. I know exactly what they sound like when they agree." She turned from the window, and something in her face shifted—still light, still her, but with the weight of purpose under it, the weight she had carried so long it had grown into her bones. "I didn't come early to mock your spelling. I came because I've decided. I'm staying on this side. For a while, at least. There are others like me—watchers, wardens, the ones the old gate left standing post when it went dark. Bramble's found three already. They don't know what they are now that the vigil's over. They need someone who's been it." She looked at him. "And I need to learn how to be a person with a shadow again, and I'd rather do it where my brother can see me do it badly."

Hazel's throat closed. He had imagined this moment, or feared it, in a hundred small ways since the gate opened: that she would go back, that the otherworld would take her again, that the five years would prove to have made her more thorn than sister. He had not let himself imagine this.

"You can have the room above the workshop," he said, when he could speak. "It's dusty. Bramble's been storing clock parts in it."

"I'll evict the clocks."

"He'll fight you. He's very attached to the clocks."

"I was a briar-warden for five years, Hazel. I have evicted worse than clocks."

The noon bells began to ring, and Hazel went to the cellar door and opened it, and the queue began to come down—Sera marshaling them with her old hunter's efficiency, softened now into something more like a steward's patience; Bramble appearing from the workshop with his spectacles pushed up into his hair and his expression, upon seeing his estranged kin in the queue, cycling rapidly through horror, dignity, and a very poorly concealed delight. The grandmother came down with her chair. The half-blood boy from the moor, found at last asleep in a hedgerow on the far side, was carried through on his grandmother's back, and when he woke in the lamplight of the cellar and saw the flowering thorns arching over him, he did not cry. He reached up and touched one of the white blossoms, and it was warm, and he laughed.

Hazel stood at the threshold, one foot in each world, and turned the key that was not a key—it was a word now, a word Father Ilex had forged in his last night alive, a word that meant *both*—and the Thorn Gate opened the way it was always meant to open: not a wound, not a secret, not a door slammed against the dark, but a bridge, tended, lit, and loved.

On the far side, the otherworld's morning was coming up green.

He hung the CLOSED sign on the shop door at dusk, when the last of the queue had crossed and the candles in Ilex's shrine had been trimmed, and he stood a moment in the empty shop with his hand flat on the counter, feeling the whole day settle into him like sediment into still water. Through the window, Corvale's lamps were coming on one by one, and beyond the rooftops, faint as a memory, the moor hills of the other world caught the same dying light. Two skies. One evening.

Sera had left a note on the counter in her square, unsentimental hand: *Council session moved to Thursday. The king is sending a gift for the shop. He wouldn't say what. I'm as worried as you are. — S.*

Hazel smiled, folded it, and put it in the drawer with the others—the drawer that had once held nothing, because a man with no correspondents needed no drawer, and now held notes from a huntress, a tinkerer, a king, a priest's shrine-keepers, and a delegation of the Winter Court whose permit request he still hadn't finished reading.

He was blowing out the lamps when he heard it: a knock at the shop door, soft, twice, in a rhythm he knew. Through the window he could see the street, empty, lamplit, ordinary. But the knock came again, and this time, beneath the floor, through the boards, up from the cellar where the gate stood in its arch of blooming thorns, he felt the rustle—and it was not laughter this time.

It was a summons. Gentle, patient, and unmistakably meant for him.

Something on the far side of the bridge, in the green dark of the other world, wanted the gatekeeper—and it had knocked on *both* doors.

Hazel looked at the crooked sign, at the word he had finally earned the right to wear, and reached for his coat.
