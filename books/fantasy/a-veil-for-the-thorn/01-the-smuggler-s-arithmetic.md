## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

There is a particular arithmetic to smuggling in the Thornfells, and it goes like this: everything costs. The rope costs. The mule's feed costs. The silence of the man who saw you load your wagons at midnight costs double what his loyalty would have cost in summer, because winter makes honest men greedy and greedy men honest. And above all of it, sitting at the top of the ledger like a fat spider, is the mountain itself, which charges rent in the only currency it accepts—attention.

You pay attention, or you pay with your life.

I was paying attention on the fourth morning out of Greyhollow, which is why I was the only one who noticed that the goat was lying to us.

"Vesper." Doss Marrow stood hip-shot against the lead wagon, chewing a strip of smoked elk and watching the pass ahead with the squint of a man who trusted nothing he couldn't drink. "What's the holdup?"

"The holdup," I said, "is that Half-Ear says the drift ahead is crusty all the way through, and I'm deciding whether to believe him."

Half-Ear was not, technically, part of our caravan. He was a white mountain goat with a torn left ear and the build of a cathedral gargoyle, and he had been pacing us since the second morning, appearing on ridgelines like a disapproving landlord. The other men thought he was following us for the salt in our pack grain. This was true as far as it went. It did not go nearly far enough.

"Since when do you speak goat?" asked Pell, the youngest of the drivers, all elbows and opinions.

"Since always. It's not difficult." I scratched Half-Ear under the jaw where he liked it, which bought me three seconds of his undivided attention, which is more than any man in that caravan had earned from me. "He says the snow bridge over the Kettle Gap froze thin this year and refroze crooked. Says there's a hollow underneath big enough to swallow the middle wagon."

"And you trust a goat's word over my own eyes?" Doss said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. Ten years I'd worked for Doss, and in ten years he had never once asked me directly how I knew the things I knew. That was the deal between us, unspoken and therefore unbreakable. He didn't ask; I didn't leave.

"Your eyes are telling you the drift looks solid because it hasn't collapsed yet," I said. "His ears are telling him there's water running under it. Goats don't lie about snow, Doss. They've got no reason. It's not like anyone's ever cheated one out of a fair price for wool."

That got a laugh from the drivers, which was fine. Laughter was good cover. I'd learned early that the trick to hiding an extraordinary thing is to stand next to a ridiculous one. Let them think I had a knack for animals, a shepherd's intuition, a way with beasts like some folk had a way with cards. *Knack* was a small, safe word. Nobody burns a hut down over a knack.

The truth was larger and much more dangerous, and it lived in my mouth the way a knife lives in a drawer: I could understand them. All of them. Not read minds—that's the story drunkards tell—but hear their speech whole, the speech every beast has spoken since before the first king, the speech my grandmother sang into my ear as lullabies when lullabies in the King's Tongue would have drawn the wrong kind of listeners to our door. Wolf-speech, crow-speech, the slow grey grammar of bears, the chittering ledgers of rats. Fluent in all of it, since before I could write my own name.

Fluent in everything, as my grandmother used to say, except people. She said it like a joke. Grandmothers make their cruelest statements sound like jokes so you can't argue with them.

We went around by the ridge trail instead of the gap, which cost us half a day, which cost Doss a fee he grumbled about, and we were two miles past the Kettle Gap when we heard the ice let go behind us—a sound like a giant clearing his throat—and nobody laughed after that, and nobody asked me anything either. But Pell looked at me differently for the rest of the afternoon, the way you look at a door in a house you've lived in all your life that you've suddenly realized you've never once seen open.

I hate being looked at that way. I'd sooner be looked at not at all.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers in Greyhollow would say about me, if Greyhollow kept ledgers on people instead of just cargo, which it mostly doesn't, which is why I live there:

Vesper Winterbourne. Thirty-one years, or thirty-two; the winter I was born was hard on records. Orphan, which is true. Guide, which is also true, in the sense that a knife is a tool for spreading butter. Came down out of the high Thornfells ten years ago with nothing but a coat, a grandmother's songs, and an accent she'd already taught herself to flatten. Took up guiding contraband through passes the crown's surveyors declared impassable, which suited everyone, because contraband pays better than honesty and impassable means uninspected.

Ten years of invisible living. You manage it the way you manage anything long-term: in small daily payments. Don't sing where you can be heard, because the old songs carry. Don't linger at the beast-pens talking to nothing, or talking to what looks like nothing. When the wolves come down near the town in a hungry winter, don't be the one who walks out alone to meet them and comes back with the herd intact and a story about luck. Pocket exactly what you need—no more—and pay your debts promptly, because forgotten people owe nothing and are owed nothing, and that is the whole architecture of safety.

It sounds lonely, written out that way. Good. It was.

I want to be clear that I chose it. Choosing it is the part people never understand, the ones who cluck over hermits and spinsters and say things like *she must have been hurt once.* Of course I was hurt. Everyone is hurt; it's practically citizenship. But I had grown up fluent in a truth that would get me burned or bled or, worse, *used*, and I had watched my grandmother die quiet and unmourned and safe in her bed at seventy, and I had done the arithmetic. A small life, paid for daily in silence, buys more years than a large one bought with a gift. Every beast on the mountain knows this. Beasts don't perform. Only people insist on being seen, and look how that goes for us.

So: ten years of small payments. Salt grain to goats. A wide berth given to the wardens' post at the toll road. Never more than two cups of anything at the Sable Ewe. And once a season, when the moon was dark and the trails were mine alone, a long walk up to the high meadows to sit with the wolves—not to do anything, just to *be among voices that understood me without requiring me to explain myself*—which is the closest thing to confession I've ever practiced, and even that I rationed, because the wolves began to ask when I was coming back to stay, and asking questions like that is how attachments start, and attachments are the first thing they take off you when they take everything else.

My grandmother's word for the gift was *the listening*. Her rule for it was simple: hear everything, answer little, belong nowhere. I had followed the rule for ten years without breaking it once.

I want that noted somewhere official, whatever passes for official in a story like mine. I followed the rules. Whatever came of it later, I followed every single rule, right up until the night the rules stopped mattering.

But that was weeks away yet. On the fifth day of that caravan I still believed in arithmetic.

* * *

The cargo, since you're wondering, was salted river-eel, forty bolts of Greyhollow loomcloth dyed in colors the tariff men called seditious, and six locked crates Doss wouldn't name and I didn't ask about, because the sixth rule of invisibility is that plausible deniability weighs nothing and carries everything. The crates rode in the middle wagon, the one Half-Ear had nearly saved from the Kettle Gap, and whatever was in them shifted sometimes, at night, with a sound too deliberate to be settling cargo.

On the sixth day, the weather closed.

Snow-blind is a word that undersells it. The world didn't go white; it went *absent*. Sky and slope shook hands and became one seamless nothing, and you walked inside a bowl of milk, and the only thing that kept a caravan moving was the guide's memory of the mountain's bones—the place where the trail bent left below a rock shaped like a snapped tooth, the hollow wind-note the pass made at the notch we called the Old Woman's Chimney. Memory and nerve. Everything else was taken.

"Vesper." Doss again, close beside my shoulder now, because even Doss's nerve had margins. "How far to shelter?"

"Two hours. Maybe three. The bothy at Cold Larder."

"You're sure it's still standing? Last I heard the roof went in the autumn storms."

"It's standing. The martens denning in the loft told me the east wall held."

Silence. Then, carefully: "Martens."

"Pest problem," I said. "They chatter about it constantly. You learn things when vermin infest your barns." I kept walking. Behind us I heard Pell snort, and someone tell him to shut it, and boots crunching, and the wind taking all of it away.

Doss let it go, the way he'd let it go for ten years. But I felt the question move in him the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee. He was a patient man, Doss Marrow, but patience is just arithmetic too, and somewhere behind his eyes a column was summing.

Half-Ear found us an hour later—or we found him, though I've never been sure the distinction means anything to a goat—standing square in the middle of the trail like a milestone with opinions.

"He says stop," I told the caravan, and the caravan, having witnessed the Kettle Gap, stopped.

"What now?" Doss said.

I listened. The wind here carried the flat milk-white roar of the blind, and under it, thin as a hair, something else. Half-Ear stamped and turned his head south, torn ear swiveling like a weathervane.

"Bells," I said. "South of us. Downwind, so they'll have heard nothing. Wardens on the toll road, most likely, checking the pass before it closes."

"Then we go around."

"We *can't* go around. There's no around, not in blind conditions, not with wagons. We wait here, quiet, until they pass, and then we take the shelf trail while the wind covers us."

"How long?"

I cocked my head. Half-Ear chewed philosophically. "He thinks less than an hour. He says they travel badly in the blind—they whip their horses and the horses hate them for it, and the horses' hooves ring different on the cut stone. He finds wardens very easy to track." A pause. "He says they smell of iron and fear."

"Does he now," Doss said, and gave me a long, level look across the milk-white air, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach that I had made an error. Nothing large. Just a small daily payment missed—one sentence too specific, one detail too true. Martens reporting on walls. Goats cataloguing the emotional lives of cavalry horses. The column summing.

An hour later the bells faded northward, and we moved, and we made Cold Larder by full dark, and the bothy stood, and the fire I built was very ordinary and very carefully casual, and if Doss noticed that I'd known precisely which of the three hearthstones drew best, he didn't say so. Not that night.

* * *

We delivered the caravan to the buyers at Thornfoot Landing on the ninth day, and it went clean, which in my trade is the exception worth noting: nine days, no losses, no contact, no blood. The buyers haggled. Doss haggled back. The eel sold, the cloth sold, the six unnamed crates sold for a figure that made Doss's eyebrows perform a brief, restrained dance, and then we were paid, and the paying-out happened in the back room of a warehouse that smelled of tallow and eel brine, and this is the part I want you to watch closely, because it's the smallest moment in the story and it's the hinge of everything.

Doss counted out shares. The drivers got theirs. Pell got his, plus a little extra, which Doss did openly, which meant it was a message to the others about the value of not panicking at the Kettle Gap. And then Doss counted my share, and paused, and looked at the coins in his palm a beat too long.

"Ten years," he said, "you've been my best guide. Better than best. And in ten years I've never asked you a certain question."

"Don't ask it, Doss."

"I'm asking whether it's a thing I need to worry about."

Everything in me said: laugh. Make a joke about martens. Stand next to the ridiculous thing. Pay the small daily payment, buy another invisible month, another invisible year.

But I was tired. Nine days in the blind does that, and nine years of blind before it, and my grandmother had been dead eleven winters and no one alive knew her songs, and Half-Ear the philosopher goat was the creature on this earth who best understood my situation, and he was, I cannot stress this enough, a goat.

"No," I said. "It's nothing you need to worry about. It's a knack, and it keeps your cargo alive, and it stays in the mountains where it belongs. That's all it will ever be."

Doss looked at me for a long moment. Then he set the coins in my hand, all of them, exact, and closed my fingers over them with his own rough ones.

"That's good," he said. "Because a woman like you, Vesper—useful the way you're useful—you want to stay *small*. Small things last." He smiled, and it reached almost halfway to his eyes. "Get some sleep. There's a wool run in three weeks, if you want it."

Three weeks. Later I would count those weeks the way misers count coins, and understand that I spent nearly all of them.

* * *

I walked home to Greyhollow alone, which took two days, and which I preferred. Company is a tax; solitude is merely a fee, and cheaper.

The high trail dropped toward the town on the second evening, and I came over the shoulder of Raven's Rest in the blue hour—that hour the old books call *the door-left-open*, when the light goes dim blue and the world's hinges show—and I stopped, because the town below was doing what Greyhollow always did: smoking its chimneys, arguing in its taverns, pretending the mountains weren't slowly eating its foundations. Lamplighters working the low streets. Gulls screaming over the fish-sheds. Ordinary. My ordinary.

And on the wind, thin and wrong and out of place as a knife in a bread basket, came a sound I hadn't heard in eleven years.

Not words. Not quite. A child's voice, high and cracked with cold, making syllables that no human throat should know—syllables with roots in them, syllables that grew—and every gull over the fish-sheds went silent at once, mid-scream, and turned.

All of them. Hundreds. Turning their heads toward the lower town in perfect unison, like a field of pale flowers finding the sun.

I stood on the shoulder of Raven's Rest with my heart going like a fist on a door, and I listened to a language I had sworn was dead, sung by a voice I didn't know, in a town that had been safe for ten years because I had paid for its safety daily, in small coin, without fail.

The arithmetic had changed. Somewhere below me, in my ordinary town, in the blue hour, the ledger had opened to a new page, and the first entry was already written.

I started down.
