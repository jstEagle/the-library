## 3. What the Ravens Brought

The trouble with ravens is that they gossip like washerwomen and charge by the secret.

I'd known Quill for six years—since the winter I pulled a fishhook out of his foot and he decided that constituted a marriage contract. He lived on my roof ridge, ate half my breakfast, and considered himself my business partner in everything except the parts where I told him to mind his own feathers.

So when he came down through the chimney smoke that morning with his neck feathers standing up like a man who'd seen a ghost and been robbed by it, I knew before he opened his beak that the quiet life was over.

"Vesper." He said it the way he always did, two syllables dragged out long, *Vesss-per*, as if tasting something gone slightly off. "Bad news from the south."

"There's no other kind of news," I said, splitting kindling on the porch while Ember watched from the window with his chin on his fists. It had been four days since the boy arrived, and he had settled into my cabin the way water settles into a boot—thoroughly, inconveniently, and without any apparent intention of leaving. "What is it?"

"Roads closed. The king's roads. All of them." Quill hopped along the rail, agitated. "Garrisons at every pass. No one crosses into the lowlands without papers, and the papers—" Here he did something ravens almost never do. He hesitated. "The papers ask if you've heard voices. Animal voices. And what you said when you answered."

My axe kept moving. That was the trick of fear: keep your hands busy so your face has nothing to do. "Border closures happen every few years. Some minister wants to look busy."

"Not this one." Quill's eye was black and flat and pitiless, the way only a friend's eye can be. "They're burning villages, Vesss-per. Down past Thornfell's knees. Little places, the kind nobody maps. They burn them and post wardens in the ashes, and the wardens wear grey and don't talk except to each other, and their horses—" His feathers flattened. "Their horses don't answer anymore. You call to them and there's nothing behind the eyes. Nothing."

The axe bit deep into the block and stuck. I left it there.

*Speakers.* The word came up out of my grandmother's stories like a stone from a riverbed, worn smooth by decades of telling. *When they come for speakers, girl, they won't come asking politely. They'll come with fire and a ledger, and every beast that ever loved you will be evidence.*

I'd always assumed she was being dramatic. Grandmothers are permitted drama; it's practically their trade.

"How many villages?" I asked.

"Three that I know of. Probably more. The crows from the lowlands aren't talking much—they're scared, and crow fear travels faster than crow news." Quill hopped closer, lowering his voice as though the whole mountain weren't already listening. "There's more. They have a list. Names. Not just speakers—people who *shelter* speakers. People who feed them. People who once, ten years ago, were seen talking to a goat on a mountainside."

The goat in question had been named Bartholomew and had been extorting me for dried apples, but I doubted the warden's ledger would appreciate the nuance.

"Well," I said lightly, because lightness is the last coat of paint you put over a rotting beam. "Good thing I'm not on any lists."

Quill looked at me the way he looked at a snare he'd already stepped around twice.

* * *

From the roof ridge, an accounting:

The woman feeds me. This is the arrangement. She pretends it is charity; I pretend I cannot hunt, which is a lie so old it has grown moss. In exchange for her dried fish and her grudging conversation, I bring her the world. Weather three days out. Which smugglers are lying. Where the carrion is good. It is a fair trade, though she would haggle even with a god, and probably win.

Now I bring her fire-news, and she chops wood like the wood owes her money, and the small human sits in the window watching her with those eyes that are wrong for a child. Old eyes. Eyes like wells.

I do not like the small human. This is not sentiment; it is arithmetic. Before him, the woman was a closed door. Useful, warm on one side, predictable. Since him, she keeps looking south, and women who look south stop feeding ravens properly, and then where am I?

Also—and I would deny this under torture—the small human talks in his sleep, and the things he says make my crop go cold. Last night he spoke words I know. Old words. Words the mountains used before we birds borrowed language from everywhere else. My grandmother's grandmother claimed to remember them. I had hoped they were extinct.

Extinct things should stay extinct. Everything that comes back comes back hungry.

* * *

Ember didn't speak—not in any tongue I could parse, not yet—but he communicated plenty. By the second day he'd rearranged my entire kitchen to his own logic, which placed the honey jar at eye level and everything else beneath consideration. By the third day he'd learned to split kindling badly and proudly. By the fourth, he'd found my charcoal stubs and the back of an old flour sack, and had begun to draw.

Children draw. I knew this in theory. They draw suns with faces and dogs with too many legs and families as a row of lollipops. So when I leaned over his shoulder that evening expecting lollipops, I got instead a map.

Not a child's map. A *surveyor's* map, done in a firm hand that no five-year-old owns, with contour lines and elevation marks and a winding road rendered in strokes so confident they might have been traced. The road ran south through the Thornfell passes, down toward the lowlands, toward—

"Tolden," I read aloud, before I could stop myself. The capital. The name sat in my mouth like a coin I'd swallowed years ago and never digested.

Ember looked up at me. Then he took the charcoal and wrote beneath the map, in careful letters he clearly hadn't learned from me: **TOLDEN**. And below that, after a moment of fierce concentration: **HOME**.

"You've never been there," I said. "You can't have been. You turned up half-frozen on my doorstep from the north, and Tolden is nine hundred miles south, and no child walks nine hundred miles."

He tapped the map. Then he flipped the flour sack over and drew again, faster now, charcoal squeaking, and what emerged made the hair on my arms stand up and take a collective step backward.

A room. A vast room drawn in perspective no child should understand—vaulted ceiling, pillars carved with thornwork, ranks of empty benches, and at the far end, elevated on a dais, a throne. And on the throne, a figure. Small in the drawing, but detailed with horrible precision: a man slumped forward, hands gripping the armrests, and around his head the artist had scribbled a crown of branching lines that spread beyond the crown itself, down his neck, across his shoulders, rooting him to the chair like a tree grown through a fence.

Ember pressed his finger to the crowned figure's chest. Then to his own chest. Then back again.

"No," I said.

He nodded.

"No. Whatever you think you're showing me, whatever dream or fever or—"

He picked up the charcoal and wrote, slowly, pressing hard enough to snap the stub twice: **HE IS SAD. HE FORGOT ME.**

And then, because the gods apparently felt the scene lacked punch, Quill came down the chimney in a clatter of soot and panic and said, "Riders on the low road. Grey cloaks. Six of them, heading for Greyhollow, and they're asking questions at the tollhouse."

* * *

Interlude, from the eaves:

Let the record show I gave fair warning. Riders, grey cloaks, six, low road—I delivered the intelligence promptly and at no charge, which for me constitutes heroism.

The woman went very still, which among humans is either wisdom or the beginning of stupidity, and in her case history suggests both arrive together. She banked the fire. She fed the small human bread and the last of the honey without being asked, which frightened me more than the riders did. Generosity in a haggler is a symptom. Something terminal is coming.

She asked me to watch the road. I watched the road. I will report on the road. But first, since no one is listening, let me say what I saw in the flour-sack drawing before she rolled it up and hid it under the floorboard with her grandmother's book.

I saw the throne room. I have never seen the throne room. No bird of my acquaintance has, and those who claim otherwise are jackdaws, who lie professionally. Yet I knew it the way you know a face from a story told before you hatched. The thornwork pillars. The benches. The thing on the throne with its roots in the chair.

The small human drew it from memory.

Memory of what? He is the size of a loaf of bread and the age of a spring thaw. Whose memory lives in him, wearing him like a coat?

I am a raven. We are comfortable with mysteries—we nest in them. But there are two kinds of mystery: the kind that is a door, and the kind that is a mouth. I looked at that drawing and I felt teeth.

* * *

I spent that night doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for four days.

Option one: send Ember away alone. Discard immediately; he'd last a day and a half, and the day and a half would be cruel.

Option two: take him down the mountain myself, to some town far from Greyhollow, and lose him in a crowd. Except the crowds were exactly where the burning was happening, and my face was apparently on a list, and every mile south was a mile toward the throne room he insisted on drawing.

Option three: stay. Hide him. Wait out the winter and hope the wardens' attention drifted like snow off a roof.

I stood at the window with Quill on the sill and watched the low road until dawn, and around the coldest hour I admitted the truth I'd been chewing without swallowing: option three wasn't an option. Wardens don't drift. Ledgers don't forget. Somewhere in a grey file there was a line about a woman and a goat, and lines become columns, and columns become pyres.

"You could leave," I told Quill. "Fly south ahead of all this. Warm country. Dead sheep, easy living."

"I could," he agreed. "So could you. There's a whole ocean east, and ships, and ships have rats, and rats are terrible conversationalists but excellent eating."

"I don't have papers. Papers ask questions. My answers would get us both—"

"—burned, yes, I follow politics." He preened, buying time. Ravens preen when they're thinking; it's our version of pacing. "Vesss-per. Ask yourself why you haven't thrown the small human out already. You've had four days. You're not soft—you once charged a dying man rent. So why is he still here?"

Because of the lullabies. Because when Ember slept, he murmured in the old tongue, the forbidden one, the one my grandmother sang into my hair when the fever was high and everyone said she was mad—a language of rounded stones and long vowels, a language that sounded less like speech than like the ground deciding to talk. Because hearing it come out of a child's mouth was like hearing my grandmother's voice from the grave, and I am not strong enough to evict a ghost.

"Sentimental weakness," I said.

"Mm," said Quill, who had watched me bury my grandmother's book under the floorboards and had never once mentioned it, which is the deepest loyalty a raven offers.

Toward morning, Ember woke and padded out to the window in his blanket, and stood between us, and looked down the mountain at the thin grey thread of the low road. Then he reached up and took my hand. Not clinging—claiming. There's a difference, and children know it instinctively, and it terrifies adults precisely because of that.

"They'll come here," I said to no one, to the boy, to the mountains. "Because of me, if not because of you. And when they come, everyone in Greyhollow who ever traded me a favor becomes a name in their ledger."

Ember squeezed my hand. Then he pointed at the peaks above the cabin—at Thornfell itself, white and indifferent in the early light—and said one word in the old tongue. I knew it. God help me, I knew it. My grandmother had sung it a hundred times.

It meant *stay*. Or possibly *refuge*. Or possibly, given how the old language worked, both at once: *the place that holds you when nothing else will.*

"That's not an answer," I said. "That's a mountain."

He nodded, patient, certain, as though the distinction were mine to sort out.

* * *

Final interlude, from the chimney top, where I keep the best view and the worst manners:

By midmorning the woman had made her decision, though she'd have denied it under oath. I know the signs. She sharpened the axe. She counted her coins and hid them in three places instead of one. She took the grandmother's book from under the floorboard and read it by the fire with her lips moving, learning again a language she pretended to have forgotten, and the small human lay against her side correcting her pronunciation in a whisper, and neither of them acknowledged that this was happening, because acknowledgment makes things real and reality is expensive.

Below, in Greyhollow, the tollhouse lantern burned late. Questions were being asked. Names were being written down.

I give it a week. Perhaps less. The grey riders move like weather, and weather does not negotiate.

The woman thinks she is choosing to protect the child. The child thinks he is choosing to protect her. I think they are both walking into a story older than either of them, the kind of story where the opening chapter is written in someone else's blood, and I have decided—against every instinct, against the warm south and its dead sheep—to stay and see how it ends.

Someone has to witness. It may as well be the one who charges by the secret.

Besides. If it all goes to ash, I want front-row seating.

If it doesn't—if the small human is what the drawings suggest—then I want to be able to say I knew him when he was merely strange, and fed him honey, and was not afraid.

I was afraid, of course. I am a raven. Fear is half our diet.

But I stayed anyway. Write that down.
