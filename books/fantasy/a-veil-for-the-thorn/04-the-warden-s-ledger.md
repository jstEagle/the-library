## 4. The Warden's Ledger

There is a particular pleasure in a clean ledger that I have never found in anything else. Not in prayer—though I was raised to it and can still recite the Litanies of the Sealed Order without stumbling. Not in the company of other wardens, who are mostly men who joined because they liked the uniform's silhouette. A ledger does not flatter you. It does not weep when you write a name into it. It simply holds what is true, in columns, and waits for you to be honest enough to read it.

I have been honest for eleven years.

The report from Greyhollow arrived on the morning post with a coffee ring stamped across the corner, which told me the courier had set his cup on official correspondence and that nobody between him and me had cared enough to object. Sloppiness travels downhill like water. I made a note of the courier's seal number anyway. Small disciplines keep large ones sharp.

The report itself was routine, which is to say it was wrong, and I could tell it was wrong before I finished the first page.

*Anomalous livestock behavior reported near Greyhollow township. Goats refusing standard herding routes without visible cause. Two incidents of draft animals halting on the north pass road, unresponsive to whip. Local handlers attribute to weather. No speakers identified. No further action recommended.*

No further action recommended. I read that line three times, the way one rereads a sentence in a contract that has been drafted specifically so you will not notice it.

Consider what the report actually said, beneath its own words. Goats are stupid. This is not an insult; it is their great virtue, and the reason we domesticated them instead of the reverse. A goat does not refuse a route *without visible cause*. A goat refuses a route because there is a wolf behind the rocks, or ice under the snow, or a man with a stick it has learned to fear. When goats refuse a road *together*, *consistently*, in a manner that experienced handlers cannot explain, then either the mountains around Greyhollow have grown something new to fear—

—or someone is telling them things.

* * *

Let me explain how the Hollow Wardens understand the world, since you will otherwise mistake us for simple butchers, and while I have killed my share of people, I resent being called simple.

Ten years ago, King Aldric sealed the borders and founded our Order with a single edict: *the land will be governed by law, not by whisper.* The old ways—the beast-speakers, the hedge-cunning, the women who sang to rivers and claimed the rivers sang back—were declared instruments of sedition. The reasoning was published openly and I have never once heard anyone refute it: a kingdom cannot obey two voices. If the king commands the roads closed and a shepherdess commands the wolves to open them, then the kingdom has two kings, and only one of them was crowned. The other was simply *trusted*. And trust, unlike coronation, requires no accountability, keeps no records, and answers to no succession. It is power without a ledger.

We do not hate the speakers. That is the first thing outsiders get wrong about us, and they get it wrong because hatred is easier to imagine than procedure. I have interrogated forty-one speakers in my career. I remember all of their names, because I wrote them down, because everything goes in the ledger. What I felt for most of them was closer to pity. They were born with a leak in them—a channel through which authority flows around the law instead of through it—and the kingdom cannot survive leaks the way a ship cannot survive them. Not because any single leak sinks it. Because enough of them, unchecked, make the whole vessel's purpose meaningless.

So: mercy is a leak, and leaks are sealed. That is the entire doctrine. Everything else—the hounds, the burning, the questions asked in rooms without windows—is plumbing.

You may find this monstrous. I did too, once, briefly, at nineteen, standing in a courtyard in Vellmarsh watching my instructor demonstrate the hollowing of a hound. Then he asked me a question I have never managed to answer: *If your mother lay dying and the village speaker could save her by asking the beasts to carry word to a physician faster than any rider—would you refuse, because the proper petition channels take four days?*

Everyone would say yes. Everyone lies. The point is that a kingdom is made of ten thousand dying mothers and ten thousand speakers willing to help them, and every act of private mercy is a thread pulled from public order, and eventually you are holding a rope with no cloth left. Better to grieve once, at the sealing, than endlessly, in the ruin.

I believed this the way I believe in load-bearing walls: not with passion, but with a complete unwillingness to remove one and see what happens.

Which brings me back to the goats.

* * *

I laid the Greyhollow report beside the previous quarter's reports and did what no one in this building ever does: I read backward.

The archive room of Warden Headquarters occupies the basement of a customs house in the capital, which tells you exactly how much the Crown values memory. It smells of mildew and lamp oil and, faintly, of the tallow candles they used before oil, as though the past itself were rendered down and stored here in vats. I am the only officer who requests access. The clerks have stopped hiding their surprise. Surprise, like everything else, can be filed.

Here is what eleven years of reports said about Greyhollow and the surrounding Thornfell approaches:

Year one after the Sealing: fourteen speaker arrests in the region. Standard. The Thornfells had always been thick with the old blood; mountain folk keep old habits the way stone keeps cold.

Year three: six arrests. Declining, consistent with suppression curves elsewhere. The model predicted continued decline.

Year five: two arrests, both elderly, both surrendered voluntarily. Also consistent. By year seven: zero arrests, and here is where any competent analyst should have paused, because zero is not what suppression curves produce. Suppression curves approach zero asymptotically. They never reach it. There are always holdouts, always grandmothers teaching lullabies with teeth in them, always some fool boy whistling crows off the crops. Zero means either extinction or concealment. Extinction is rare. Concealment is common.

And the reports for years seven through ten contained, buried in the livestock sections, the same recurring phrase in different hands: *animals behaving oddly.* Sheep moving at night in formation. Ravens gathering on the pass road "as if waiting." A mule that refused a certain valley and starved itself rather than enter it. Each incident filed separately, each dismissed as weather or superstition or bad feed, none of them ever cross-referenced.

Because cross-referencing is my habit and not the institution's. The Wardens are very good at catching speakers. We are terrible at noticing patterns, because patterns require admitting that the world is still talking when we have ordered it to be silent.

I spread eleven years of livestock anomalies across the archive table and weighted the corners with ink bottles, and I looked at the shape they made, and the shape pointed at Greyhollow.

Not at a speaker in Greyhollow. At Greyhollow itself—at the town as a node, a place where something had been quietly working for years. And now, this winter, the goats had begun refusing the north pass road entirely.

Something was moving through those mountains. Something the animals knew about before the humans did. Animals always know first; that is the whole problem with them and the whole reason for the Order.

I requested the personnel file for the Greyhollow garrison. Two wardens stationed there, both rated adequate, both with complaint records I found instructive: each had filed a request for transfer out of Greyhollow citing "atmosphere." Not danger. *Atmosphere.* Men who could not articulate why a town unsettled them, which meant the town was unsettling them correctly, below the level of articulation. Good instincts, poor vocabulary. I noted both names.

Then I sat with the ledger open before me and performed the exercise I perform whenever the data asks for more than procedure: I imagined the counterargument.

Perhaps it was weather. Perhaps the passes were icing differently this year. Perhaps a new wolf pack had moved into the high country and the goats were sensibly avoiding it. All plausible. All checkable. But here was the fact that no weather explained: over eleven years, as the anomaly reports accumulated, the *arrest* numbers had gone to true zero in a region historically dense with speakers. The old blood had not fled—the smuggling traffic through Greyhollow was well documented and nobody flees through their own escape routes—they had gone *quiet*. Perfectly, uniformly quiet, in a way that frightened people are never quiet.

Frightened people run. Only organized people hide.

And organization, among speakers, requires coordination, and coordination with the old gift requires—

Well. It required what the archives delicately called a *confluence*. A strong one. The kind that had not been recorded since before the Sealing. The kind the old texts suggested could call across species lines, which the texts described in terms I had always assumed were embroidery, because the alternative was to believe that somewhere in my jurisdiction a human being could raise a voice that every animal within earshot would answer like soldiers answering a horn.

I am a methodical woman. I want it understood that I did not leap to the frightening conclusion. I walked to it, in good boots, checking the ground.

* * *

The request for investigative authority went up the chain that afternoon, written in my own hand, because dictation leaves tone in the margins where subordinates read it.

*Requesting leave to investigate anomalous animal behavior, Greyhollow region, Thornfell approaches. Rationale: sustained concealment pattern inconsistent with suppression models. Suspected confluence-level speaker activity. Proposed method: discreet entry, ledger audit of local contacts, deployment of hollowed assets as needed. Estimated duration: one month. Requesting two field wardens and a full hound team.*

Captain-General Voss read it standing at his window, which is where he reads everything, as if documents might behave better when observed against the sky. He is sixty, broad as a door, and he got that way carrying out the early purges personally, back when the Order still pretended its work was surgical.

"Confluence," he said. "That's a word from the archives."

"The archives are where the words live, Captain-General. The mountains are where they're happening."

He grunted. He has never liked me and has never once let it interfere with his judgment, which is the highest compliment available in this profession. "Greyhollow is a smuggler town. Smugglers are the Crown's pressure valve. You go in loudly, you collapse the valve, and half the contraband reroutes to the coast where we have no eyes. Discreet?"

"Discreet," I confirmed. "I'll go in as an assessor of toll-road contracts. Nobody hides from an auditor. They're too bored."

"You'll burn whatever you find."

"If I find it. Yes." I said it evenly, and I want to record that I said it evenly, because later—much later—people would claim I hesitated, that some part of me already knew. I did not hesitate. I want that in the record. Whatever else became of me, on that day I was still the woman whose ledger balanced.

He signed. His seal came down on the wax like a verdict, which is what seals are.

"One thing," he said as I turned to go, and there was something in his voice I had not heard before, and would spend a long time afterward trying to classify. Not doubt. Not quite. More like a man checking a load-bearing wall out of habit, in a building he knows is sound. "The old texts. The confluences. What happened to them? The ones before the Sealing. Why did the practice die out, if it was as powerful as the archives claim?"

"It didn't die out, Captain-General. We killed it. That was rather the point of us."

"Yes," he said. "Of course." And he turned back to his window, and I left, and neither of us said anything else, and I have replayed that exchange perhaps four hundred times since, searching it for the crack. There was nothing to find. It was two professionals confirming a schedule.

The crack was elsewhere. The cracks are always elsewhere. That is what ledgers are for.

* * *

Preparations took six days. I will not bore you with the requisitions, except to note one item, because it matters to what follows.

The hounds.

People imagine the hollowed beasts as monsters—red-eyed, slavering, wrong. The reality is worse and simpler: they look like excellent dogs. Glossy coats, perfect obedience, no fear of fire or blood or their own deaths. What is missing is invisible unless you know to look for it, and what is missing is *them*. A normal dog wants things. Food, approval, the squirrel, the other dog's dinner. A hollowed hound wants nothing. It executes. Watching one work is like watching a sentence with all the meaning removed but the grammar intact.

I have hollowed eleven animals myself, early in my career, before the practice centralized. I was good at it. The technique requires no cruelty during the act—that surprised me too—it requires only that you speak to the animal in the old cadence, the cadence the speakers use, and instead of asking, you *empty*. You take the wanting out. It takes less than a minute. The animal does not struggle, because struggling is a thing the wanting does, and the wanting is already gone.

I mention this so you understand: I knew precisely what I was bringing to Greyhollow. Six hounds, hollowed, crated for transport. Tools. Leaks, sealed.

On the fifth night, packing my field kit, I opened the crate to inspect them—one last inventory, because inventories are prayers for people like me—and the lead hound looked up at me.

I want to be careful here, because what happened next lasted perhaps two seconds and I have built entire nights of sleeplessness on those two seconds, and I would like to report them accurately rather than dramatically.

The hound looked at me. Its eyes tracked. Its breathing was even. Every measurable function was nominal. And I stood there holding the lantern, and I thought—not felt, *thought*, the way one thinks a mathematical error has occurred—*there is nothing looking back.*

It was not that the hound hated me, or feared me, or resented me. It is that the creature before me was a surface. Somewhere inside the procedure, in the minute when I or someone like me spoke the emptying words, everything that looks back had been taken out, and what remained was a shape that ate and ran and killed on command, and I had spent eleven years calling that mercy for the kingdom, and in the lamplight of a customs-house cellar, for the first time, I asked myself who the animal had been before, whether it had had a name, whether anyone had scratched its ears, and whether the kingdom's arithmetic had ever once included a column for it.

Then the moment passed, the way such moments do, and I closed the crate, and I logged the inspection in my ledger, and my hand did not shake, because I am a professional, and professionals do not shake.

But I did something I had never done before. On the journey north—I will get to the journey, the roads were frozen and miserable and the details are irrelevant—on the journey north, riding at the head of six crates of silence, I began, for my own purposes, a second ledger.

Not the official one. A private one, kept in the back pages of my field notebook, in a cipher of my own devising, beginning with a question I could not file anywhere:

*What, exactly, are we keeping the kingdom safe from? And who decided? And where is their ledger?*

I did not have answers. I had boots, and a road, and orders, and the comfortable weight of procedure. I rode toward Greyhollow believing every wall would hold.

The mountains, of course, had other architecture in mind.
