## 5. Old Roads, New Prices

Doss Marrow's office smelled the way it always did: tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular staleness of a man who counted money in a room with no windows because windows let in witnesses. I'd been coming here for ten years, and in all that time the only thing that had ever changed was the price of everything.

"Sit," he said, without looking up from his ledger.

I stayed standing. It was a small rebellion, but a smuggler learns to take her victories where she finds them.

He finished his arithmetic—Doss believed in finishing his arithmetic before acknowledging that other people existed—and then he looked at me over the top of the ledger the way a fishmonger looks at a cod he's deciding whether to gut now or leave overnight.

"You brought a child into Greyhollow," he said.

"I found a child on my doorstep during a whiteout. There's a difference, and the difference is that I didn't have a choice."

"There's always a choice, Vess. You could've turned him out." He said it mildly, testing me, the way he tested everyone. "You didn't."

"No. I didn't."

"And now every raven between here and the capital is carrying your name south like it's worth coin." He closed the ledger and laced his fingers over it. Doss had hands like knotted rope, and when he laced them, it meant the conversation was about to become expensive. "King Aldric sealed the borders last week. Did you hear?"

"I heard."

"Burning villages that shelter speakers. Not arresting them. Burning them. With the speakers inside, mostly, though I'm told they give you the option to run first, out into the snow, which is generous of them." He said *generous* the way other men said *plague*. "The Hollow Wardens don't take bribes. Do you understand what that means? In thirty years of moving contraband through Thornfell, I have never once met an authority that couldn't be bought. It's the one honest thing about power. And these Wardens—" he tapped the ledger once, sharply, "—these Wardens are honest. Which makes them the most dangerous thing to come down that mountain since the mountain itself."

I kept my face still. Behind me, in the corner where Doss kept a crate of cheap brandy he never drank, something scratched. A mouse, probably. Or not probably. Ember's habit of talking to things had spread through the town like damp through plaster, and lately I couldn't walk three steps without some creature giving me a look I understood entirely against my will.

"You want me to stop sheltering him," I said.

"I want you to be smart, which is different, and which is why I'm offering you a way out instead of a knife." He stood, went to the brandy crate after all, and poured two fingers into two cups, which was itself alarming, because Doss Marrow did not pour for free. "There's a caravan going south in three days. Wool, salt, and one mute boy packed in among the bales like any other cargo. They'll take him as far as Lowmarsh. From there, someone else moves him on. He disappears into the low country, and nobody in Greyhollow ever saw him, and neither did you."

I looked at the cup he held out to me and didn't take it. "And what does this cost me?"

"Nothing." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd seen all winter, and I'd spent the winter looking at wolves' teeth. "It costs you nothing. That's the beauty of it. The price isn't yours."

* * *

Here is the thing about Doss Marrow that took me ten years to learn, and that I learned too late to matter: he was not a cruel man. Cruelty requires imagination, and Doss had none. What he had was arithmetic so complete it had eaten whatever heart he'd started with, the way ivy eats a wall—not maliciously, just thoroughly, until you couldn't tell where the building ended and the strangling began.

So when he said *the price isn't yours*, I already knew whose it was. I just made him say it anyway, because I wanted to watch him do it.

"The buyers," he said, settling back behind his desk, "are from the capital. Not Wardens—worse, or better, depending on how you look at it. Scholars. Rich ones. The kind who collect things the Crown has declared don't exist. They've been asking after beast-speakers for years, quietly, through channels I don't even know the bottom of. And now there's a boy in my town who speaks a language older than the kingdom, and every animal within a mile of him sits up and listens like he's the morning bell." He spread his hands. "Vess, they'll pay more for that boy than I make in five years of wool and salt. More than you'd make in twenty. Enough that you never have to guide another caravan through a whiteout again. Enough that you can buy a cabin somewhere warm and green and far from anything with a Warden in it, and live out your whole invisible life in comfort."

The fire popped. Somewhere under the floorboards, the scratching had stopped, which meant the thing that had been scratching was listening, which meant Ember's influence had reached even into Doss's walls, and I found that oddly comforting.

"They're not buying him to hurt him," Doss added, and I'll grant him this: he actually believed it would help. "Scholars feed their acquisitions. They house them. The boy eats better than either of us for the rest of his life. It's the kindest arithmetic on the table, Vess, and I'm offering it to you because you're the closest thing I have to family, which is a sentence I'll deny saying if you repeat it."

I thought about the cabin. I thought about warmth that wasn't rationed, and green that wasn't pine, and a life where no one knew my name well enough to sell it.

Then I thought about Ember sitting on my floor three nights ago, drawing the throne room of a palace he'd never seen, his charcoal stub moving with the certainty of a man remembering rather than a boy imagining. I thought about how he'd paused at the throne and drawn a crown on it, and then drawn a second shape around the crown, thorned and patient, and looked up at me with eyes too old for his face and said, in the tongue my grandmother had sung me to sleep with, *it knows I'm gone. It wants its piece back.*

"Doss," I said. "Do you know what happens to a splinter when you pull it out of a wound?"

He frowned. "What kind of question—"

"A splinter comes out clean, or it takes flesh with it. This boy isn't cargo. He's not even a boy, exactly—he's a piece of something that got cut away from something worse, and if your rich scholars carry him south in a wool bale, they aren't rescuing him. They're moving the wound. And wounds moved wrong fester." I finally took the brandy, not because I wanted it but because my hand needed something to do besides shake. "No."

"Vess—"

"No. Find another answer."

"This *is* the other answer. The first answer is the Wardens come up the pass in a fortnight, maybe less, and burn Greyhollow down around our ears because we harbored him, and they find him anyway, because he talks to animals and animals talk back and the Wardens have ways of listening I don't want described to me. There is no third door, girl. There's the door south and the door into the fire."

"Then I'll build a third door."

"With what? You're one woman with a gift you've spent ten years pretending you don't have. That's not a door. That's a crack in the wall, and cracks get sealed."

I set the brandy down untouched. "Thank you for the drink."

"Vesper." He said my full name, which he almost never did, and for a moment the ivy parted and I saw the man underneath, old and tired and genuinely frightened, and I hated him a little for making me see it. "Ten years I've kept you fed and forgotten. Ten years. Don't make those years worthless by dying stupid."

"They were never worthless," I said. "But they were never free either. Everything has a price, right? Isn't that your gospel?" I pulled my coat tight. "Well. Here's mine."

* * *

My route maps lived in a tin box under the floorboards of my cabin, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Ten years of work. Every pass, every blind ravine, every goat-track and frozen waterfall crossing between Greyhollow and the low country—the knowledge that had kept me invisible and alive, that had bought my bread and paid for my silence, that no one else in Greyhollow possessed entire. Doss had pieces. His runners had pieces. Only I had the whole map, and only in my head and on that paper.

Ember watched from the corner while I pried up the boards. He'd taken to sitting very still when adults did serious things, folding himself small the way children learn to when they've learned that being noticed is dangerous. But his eyes tracked every movement, and the firelight caught something in them that wasn't fear. It was attention. The same attention the goats gave him, and the crows, and once—a moment I still hadn't decided how to feel about—a wolf, on the ridge above my cabin, standing in the blue dusk like a sentry reporting for duty.

"What are you doing?" he asked. He spoke rarely aloud; the old tongue came easier to him, and human words seemed to cost him something, like a man speaking a second language through a mouthful of stones.

"Housekeeping," I said.

I carried the tin box to the hearth. Inside: eleven maps, inked and re-inked, corrected through ten winters of avalanche and rockfall and one memorable spring flood that had erased half the western trail. My life's work. My invisibility, rendered in iron-gall ink.

Doss wanted the boy sold south. Fine. But Doss also had runners, and runners talk, and half of them owed debts to men who'd sell their own mothers for Warden favor. If the maps survived me, then sooner or later someone would move through my passes without me—Wardens, buyers, worse—and the mountains I loved would become roads for the thing wearing the king.

You can't sell what doesn't exist.

I fed the maps to the fire one at a time. Not all at once—I'm not a barbarian—but properly, each one held until the corners blackened and curled and the ink lifted off the paper in little ghost-smears before the flame took it all. Eleven maps. It took most of an hour. The cabin filled with the smell of burning iron-gall, sharp and mineral, like lightning had gotten into the room.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Ember crept forward and sat cross-legged on the hearthstone beside me, close enough that I could feel the heat on both of us, and watched the western trail map curl and die.

"That was your way out," he said.

"It was everyone's way out. That was the problem."

"Are you sad?"

I considered lying to him. He was six, maybe seven; lies should have worked. But he had a way of waiting for answers, absolutely still, the way winter waits, that made lying feel like throwing a stone down a well and hearing it land on glass.

"Yes," I said. "But sad is cheaper than dead, and cheaper than you sold to some scholar's cabinet like a pressed flower. Sad I can afford."

He nodded slowly, accepting this with the terrible gravity of children, and then he reached out and touched the last unburned corner of the last map—the high northern passes, the ones above the tree line where nothing human was supposed to go—and said something in the old tongue, soft, almost apologetic. Asking the fire's pardon, maybe. Or the paper's. Asking the road's.

Every candle in the cabin guttered at once, and outside, the wind changed direction mid-gust, and I felt the words go through me like cold water through cloth. My grandmother's lullabies had always sounded like weather. His sounded like the weather answering back.

When the last scrap of the last map was ash, I swept the hearth, buried the ashes in the cold corner of the fireplace where they'd never be found, and put the floorboard back.

"Well," I said, to Ember, to the cabin, to whatever had been listening from the walls and the rafters and the dark beyond the shuttered window. "That's that. No way back to invisible."

Ember looked up at me, and for the first time since he'd arrived half-frozen on my doorstep, he smiled. It transformed his whole face, made him briefly, actually, a child.

"The mountains are glad," he said.

"The mountains can mind their own business."

"They say you're theirs now."

"I'm nobody's—"

A knock at the door cut me off. Three slow strikes, deliberate, spaced like a man counting. Nobody knocked like that in Greyhollow. Smugglers knocked fast and quiet, twice, like apology. That knock belonged to someone who had never once needed permission to enter anywhere.

Ember went rigid beside me. The fire shrank flat in the hearth, and from the ridge above the cabin came a sound I'd never heard a wolf make in ten years of living under their ridgeline: a long, low note, sustained, like a horn. Like a warning.

I put my hand on the poker and looked at the door, and the door looked back.

"Who is it?" I called.

Silence. Then, in a voice like dry paper, polite as a tax collector:

"Warden-Captain Ilvane. We should talk about the boy."
