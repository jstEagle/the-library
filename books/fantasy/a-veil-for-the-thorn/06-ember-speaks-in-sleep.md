## 6. Ember Speaks in Sleep

I was old before I was small.

That is the first thing I have to tell you, and I don't know who *you* is yet. Maybe the woman with the careful hands. Maybe the fire. Maybe nobody, because nobody is listening, which has been true for a very long time and I am used to it the way a stone is used to rain.

I remember being vast.

Not big — vast. There is a difference, though it took me dying once to learn it. Big is a bear. Big is a mountain, if you want to stretch the word. Vast is when your edges are somewhere else and you don't miss them because everywhere you end, something else begins, and all of it is you anyway. I was the winter talking to itself. I was ten thousand small warm hearts beating in the same long sentence. I was a river that had opinions about the moon.

I was many voices at once, and none of them argued, which should have warned me. Voices that never argue are either dead or about to be.

* * *

Here is how it was, before. I'll say it quickly, like pulling off a bandage, because if I say it slowly I will feel every tooth of it.

There was a war. Not the kind men have, with flags and mud and songs afterward. The older kind. The Devouring, they called it later, when there were laters. Something was eating the world — not chewing, not swallowing, just *eating*, the way rot eats, without appetite or manners. The beasts fought it. I fought it. We were the same fight seen from different windows.

And we were losing. Slowly, politely, the way ice loses to spring.

Then came the man with the sad eyes and the good spine. Aldric. I knew him before he was king, back when he was only a general who talked to his horse as if it might answer, which it might have; horses in those days were more conversational. He stood on a black ridge where the fighting had stripped everything down to bone and root, and he looked at what was left of the world, and he did not look away. Most people looked away. Kings especially.

"Give me the power to end this," he said. To us. To me. To the whole parliament of fang and feather and root. "And I will pay whatever it costs."

He meant it. That was the tragedy of him. Men who mean their promises are so much easier to break.

We made the bargain. The forest grew a crown — thorn and memory and law braided together — and we set it on his head, and it held him the way a riverbed holds a river: shaped him, carried him, kept him from flooding. It was supposed to be temporary. Write that down. Underline it in whatever ink you keep. *Temporary.* A tourniquet, not a marriage. You take a tourniquet off when the wound closes. Ten years. Twenty, if the wound was deep. But someone has to remember the taking-off.

So they cut a piece out of me — out of him, out of both of us, we were already braided by then — the piece that remembers mercy. The piece that would have said *enough* at the right time. You can't leave that part inside a king holding a tourniquet; it would nag him loose too soon. Better to store it somewhere safe. Better to make it small and warm and portable.

They made me into a boy.

They cut me apart with hands I loved. That's the part I can't get around. Not enemies' hands. Not wardens' hands, iron-gloved and indifferent. The oldest hands — roots and wings and one pair of human palms, his own, pressed flat over his heart like a man swearing an oath. Everyone who took a piece of me apologized while they did it. The wolf wept. The crow sang the mourning-song *while cutting*. Do you understand what that does to a person, being divided by love? Being amputated gently, with apologies, by everyone you trust?

It teaches you that love has edges. That's all. Just that.

* * *

Now I am small, and the world has gotten enormous again, the way it does when you shrink. The woman's cabin is a country to me now. The fire is a sun with a bad temper. Her name is Vesper, which I know means evening star, and she moves through her own house like a guest, quiet on her own floors, apologizing to her own chair. I know that walk. I wore it for ten years under a crown.

She doesn't know what I am. She knows *something* — she heard me speak to the goat, to the ravens, to the snow itself, and her face did the thing faces do: hunger and terror sharing one plate. She has my language. Not all of it, but the bones of it. Her grandmother sang it to her, forbidden lullabies, and she thought she'd forgotten, but you don't forget a mother tongue. You only bury it shallow.

Tonight she gave me soup and a blanket that smells of cedar and smoke, and she banked the fire, and she watched me until she thought I slept. Then she sat by the window a long time, listening to the storm interrogate the shutters, and I felt her worry from across the room. Worry has a texture. Hers is like wool — scratchy, warm, pulled tight over something soft.

I slept. Or I fell. From up here in a boy's body, sleep feels like falling off the edge of myself into the deep water where I used to live. And in the water, I dreamed the old dreams, because pieces of me are still down there, still vast, still arguing in ten thousand voices.

* * *

Dream the first: the coronation.

I am standing on the black ridge and also kneeling on it and also circling above it as a hawk, and the forest walks forward on roots that groan like ship-timber, carrying its own crown in its branches. Thorn and memory and law. It is beautiful the way a verdict is beautiful. Aldric kneels. His neck is bare and terribly human, and I want to warn him — I am trying to warn him, I am shouting from inside the dream with no mouth — *remember the taking-off, write it on your skin, tattoo the word TEMPORARY across your ribs where the mirror can scold you* —

But the crown comes down, and the sound it makes settling is the sound of a door closing in a house with no other doors.

And I feel it happen to me too, because we are braided now. Something reaches into my chest with fingers made of patience and takes the piece of me that says *enough*. And here is the terrible, funny, awful thing: it doesn't hurt. Being cut apart doesn't hurt at all. What hurts is watching them carry the piece away and knowing it's me and being unable to miss myself properly, because missing yourself requires a self, and mine has a hole in it shaped like mercy.

The wolf carries me. The crow sings. The hands are gentle. Everyone apologizes.

*Wake up,* I try to say. *Someone has to remember the taking-off.*

Nobody wakes up. Not for ten years.

* * *

Dream the second: the queen.

I wasn't supposed to keep her. She wasn't part of the bargain; she was just the woman who loved the man under the crown, and the crown found her inconvenient the way a lock finds a key inconvenient. So it took her face out of him early, first amputation, rehearsal for me. Folded her away like a letter no one dares reread.

But I was the mercy-piece, and mercy keeps things. That's the whole job. So I hid her inside myself, her laugh, her way of entering a room like weather, the specific gray of her eyes when Aldric was being brave instead of sensible. I kept her the way a riverbed keeps the shape of water after the water goes.

I still have her. She is the only luggage I carried out of being vast.

When I heal things — and I can, I've learned, though it costs — I pay with memories. Every mending spends something. Last week, or last month, time is porridge now, I healed the goat's cracked hoof and lost the smell of my first morning, which was a smell like cold stone and pine and beginning. Fine. Worth it. The goat walked without limping. Good trade.

But I have decided the queen stays. Whatever else gets spent, she stays. A person has to keep one thing.

* * *

Dream the third is not a dream. Dreams have walls. This one doesn't.

Something is looking for me.

I feel it the way a splinter feels the magnet — a pull along my seams, patient and vegetative and old. The Crown. It has been ruling ten years without its mercy, and the land is going gray at the edges, villages burning, speakers hunted, the beasts falling silent one valley at a time, and somewhere under all that ruin the Crown has finally noticed the hole in its own chest. It wants its piece back. It wants *me* home.

And the worst part — the part I will not tell Vesper yet, because she has worry enough to knit into sweaters — is that some thin, frightened filament of me wants to go back. Being vast was easy. Being one voice among thousands means never having to be the only one awake. Out here I am small and singular and responsible, and responsibility turns out to weigh more than mountains did.

No. Get behind me, filament. I didn't crawl out of a king's chest through ten years and one burned smuggler town to come home to a parasite just because loneliness is loud.

* * *

I wake up falling, which is embarrassing, and I wake up crying, which is worse, because the body I'm wearing cries in a child's register, thin and squeaky, utterly without dignity. In my vast days grief could shake mountains. Now it sounds like a kettle. Grief is too large for this body. It doesn't fit. It comes out sideways, in sobs and hiccups and words I don't choose.

And the words come out in the old tongue, because grief speaks its mother language.

Vesper is there instantly — she sleeps badly, I've noticed; she listens even while pretending not to, a professional habit from her smuggling life — and she gathers me up with those careful hands, and she says something low and ordinary and human, *"shh, you're safe, you're here,"* the standard liturgy, and it helps exactly as much as liturgy helps, which is a little, which is enough.

But I am still half in the deep water, and the deep water is honest in ways waking-me is too polite to be, and I look at her face — sharp chin, storm-gray attention, the buried lullaby still living behind her eyes like a coal under ash — and I recognize her. Not from now. From *before*. From the ridge, from the coronation, from the long story written in the abbey murals I haven't seen yet but will.

I know that line. The speaker-line. The family that taught the beasts language and taught the forest to make bargains and wrote *temporary* where everyone could read it, before the word got buried. Her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother stood where I'm dreaming and kept the ledger of the taking-off, and the ledger was lost, or burned, or hidden, and the Crown spent ten years hunting every page of it.

Every page except the one sleeping in front of me.

"*Ysolde,*" I say. My voice cracks on it, old syllables in a young throat, and the fire leans toward us, and outside in the dark I hear the snow itself pause to listen. "*Ysolde vael Thornfell.* First speaker. Keeper of the unbinding."

Vesper goes very still. Stillness in her isn't calm; it's a drawn bow.

"That's not my name," she says carefully, in the tone of a woman checking whether the floor is load-bearing.

"It is," I say, because the deep water doesn't lie. "You forgot. They made everyone forget. But the mountains didn't." I press my small cold hand flat against her sternum, right over the coal. "You're the last page, Vesper. I've been looking for you for ten years."

Outside, far to the south, so far it should be beyond hearing, something crowned and patient turns its head in a frozen throne room — and begins, for the first time in a decade, to be afraid.
