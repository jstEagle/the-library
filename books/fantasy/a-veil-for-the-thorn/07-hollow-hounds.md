## 7. Hollow Hounds

The dogs stopped barking on a Tuesday.

I want you to understand what that means in Greyhollow. Dogs here bark at everything—gulls, gulls especially, the tide, the tax collector, the concept of Tuesday itself. A Greyhollow dog that isn't barking is a dog that's dead or dreaming. So when I stepped out of Ember's room at dawn and the whole town lay under a silence thick as wet wool, I stood on my own doorstep for a long moment and listened to the nothing.

"You hear it too," Ember said behind me. Not a question. The boy had taken to sleeping with his boots on, which told me more about his dreams than any of his maps did.

"Hear what? It's quiet."

"Exactly." He said the word like it tasted wrong. "Quiet is a mouth closed around something."

Ten years old, maybe eleven, and he talked like my grandmother's ghost wearing a child's skin. Some mornings I loved him for it. Most mornings it scared me half to death, which is a specific kind of love, if you're wondering. The kind that keeps you up checking door latches.

I'd sent word to Doss Marrow three days prior: *Wardens coming, get your people off the ledgers.* He'd sent back a note that said only *Which wardens?* As if there were kinds. As if it mattered whether the knife was iron or steel when it was already at your throat.

* * *

They came up the coast road just past midmorning, and the first thing I saw wasn't them. It was their hounds.

Six of them, big mastiff-crosses bred in the kennels outside the capital, and they moved wrong. That's the only way I can say it. Dogs move like water finding its level—all loose hips and wandering noses, every part of them going somewhere slightly different than the rest. These moved like furniture being carried. Legs in perfect time, heads locked forward, and their eyes—

Their eyes were the worst part. I've looked into a lot of animal eyes in my life. There's always somebody home. Even a chicken has somebody home, and God knows chickens are not deep thinkers. But these dogs looked at me the way a window looks at you. Glass with weather behind it.

And when I reached out—the way I reach out without meaning to, the way I've been reaching out since I was four years old and my grandmother clapped a hand over my mouth and said *never where anyone can see*—there was nothing to touch.

You know how you talk to someone across a crowded room by catching their eye? Imagine catching an eye and finding no one behind it. Imagine shouting into a house and hearing your own voice come back changed, flattened, owned by something else. My gift slid off those hounds like rain off slate. Worse than silence. Silence still listens. This was absence wearing fur.

"They're hollowed," Ember whispered. His hand found mine, small and cold. "Someone took their voices out and left the shapes."

"Took them *out*," I said. "Where do voices go?"

He didn't answer, but he looked north, toward the Thornfell passes, and I decided I didn't want the answer badly enough to press a frightened child about it. There are mercies we do ourselves.

Behind the hounds came the Wardens. Twelve of them in grey-and-white, moving with the same terrible economy as their beasts, and at their head rode a woman on a dun mare. She sat her saddle like she'd been poured into it. Her face was the kind of face that makes you check whether you've committed a crime recently, just in case.

Warden-Captain Sera Ilvane. I didn't know her name yet. I would learn it later, the way you learn the name of a storm after it's taken your roof.

* * *

Here's the arithmetic they don't teach you about hiding. You spend ten years being invisible, and invisibility starts to feel like safety instead of what it actually is, which is a debt accruing interest. Every year unseen is another year the bill grows. And then one morning the collector rides into town with hollow dogs, and you find out what your quiet life actually cost: everyone who helped you keep it.

The Wardens worked methodically. They didn't kick down doors—they knocked, politely, and asked questions in reasonable voices while their glass-eyed hounds stood in the street smelling for something. Smelling for Ember, I realized. Or for me. For whatever scent a speaker leaves on a household, some residue of all that listening.

I watched from Marta Quill's bakery, where I'd installed myself with a loaf I had no intention of eating, because Marta's front window commands the square and Marta herself had gone suspiciously pale when I asked to borrow it.

"They came through Saltmere yesterday," she said, not looking at me, kneading dough that didn't need kneading. "Burned the tannery row. Old Fenwick tried to argue. They—" She stopped kneading. Started again. "His dog wouldn't stop howling afterward. Wouldn't eat. Just howled at the empty house until one of the greycoats took it away."

"Took it where?"

"I didn't ask. You don't ask." She said it like a prayer she hated. *You don't ask, you don't ask, you don't ask.*

That was the whole catechism of Greyhollow, and I'd recited it faithfully for ten years, and watching those hounds stand motionless in the snow I understood finally what the catechism bought us. Nothing. It bought us nothing. The bill comes due regardless; the only question is who's standing next to you when it does.

Ember was supposed to be at the Widow Crane's, two streets over, tucked in a root cellar with a false wall. Instead he appeared beside me in the bakery, silent as smoke, because apparently the boy had decided instructions were more of a suggestion.

"How did you—" I began.

"The rats told me," he said simply. "They said the grey men were asking about a woman who talks to animals and a boy who talks to everything else. They said it like a joke. Rats have jokes now. I'm still deciding how I feel about that."

"We need to go. Now. Out the back."

"Not yet." He was watching the square with an intensity that made my chest hurt. "Look at the captain. Look at her hands."

Ilvane had dismounted and was speaking with the harbormaster, and yes—I saw it. Her hands were shaking. Fine tremors, quickly stilled, but there. And when one of her hounds turned its empty head toward a crying child in the crowd, Ilvane's jaw tightened in a way I recognized from mirrors. That was not the face of a woman enjoying her work. That was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she couldn't make come out even.

It didn't matter. Sympathy doesn't stop a burning. I caught Ember's collar and steered him through the kitchen, past Marta—who pressed a sack of day-old rolls into his arms without being asked, because that's the other thing about Greyhollow, we're stingy with trust and reckless with bread—and out into the alley behind the smokehouse.

* * *

We almost made it clean. I want that on the record. We were three streets from the treeline, moving through the fish-gut alleys where even Warden hounds presumably balked, when the hound found us anyway.

It came around the corner ahead of us and simply stood there. No growl. No hackles. It just stood, the way a wall stands, and turned its head, and somewhere behind those glass eyes a bell rang that I couldn't hear.

Then it opened its mouth and made a sound.

Not a bark. A voice. A thin, borrowed, secondhand voice, like wind through a dead man's flute, and it said—in the old tongue, the tongue of my grandmother's lullabies, the tongue Ember spoke in his sleep—one word.

"*There.*"

My blood went to ice water. Because that voice hadn't come from the dog. It had come *through* the dog, the way sound comes through a pipe, and whatever was on the other end of the pipe knew the oldest language in the world and used it to point at a child.

"Run," I said, and for once in my careful, calculating, invisible life, I didn't think past the word.

We ran. Up the goat-track behind the tannery, through the alder scrub, Ember's hand locked in mine and his breath coming in ragged white flags. Behind us I heard the town bell start ringing—not the alarm pattern, the fire pattern—and I heard the particular crackle-and-roar that no one ever mistakes for anything else, and I heard screaming, human screaming, and I kept running, because that's the thing about survivor's guilt nobody tells you: it doesn't feel like guilt yet. In the moment it feels like legs. It feels like air in your lungs and ground under your feet and the animal certainty that living is the only job you have.

The guilt comes later. Guilt is patient. Guilt walks.

A warden cut us off at the ridge path—one man, young, his crossbow coming up with the smooth mechanical confidence of someone who's done this before—and I did the stupid brave thing I'd sworn off doing for ten years. I reached for the horse behind him.

Nothing. Hollowed too. Of course. Of course they'd hollow the horses.

But the warden hesitated. One heartbeat, looking at me—a woman, a smuggler, a nobody—and at the child beside me, and in that heartbeat Ember stepped forward and said something in the old tongue, three syllables, soft as an apology.

The warden went gray. He lowered the crossbow like it weighed a hundred pounds. Later—much later, in another life—I would learn what Ember had said to him. For now I grabbed the boy's hand again and we went over the ridge and into the trees, and the last thing I saw of Greyhollow was the smoke standing up straight in the cold air, six columns of it, like the town raising its fingers to say *we were here.*

Marta was in one of those columns. I know because I counted the streets from the ridge, because counting is what I do instead of weeping, and hers was the third.

* * *

We climbed until dark. Past the tree line, past the scree fields, up into the high passes where the wind speaks a language older than the old tongue and doesn't care who hears it. I found a hollow beneath an overhang, got a fire going small enough to hide, and sat with Ember between my knees and my coat around both of us, listening to the valley burn below.

He cried. Quietly, the way children cry when they've learned crying costs something. I held him and did my own crying inward, where it could be converted directly into anger, which is the family recipe.

"I did this," I said finally. "I brought you to Greyhollow. I thought—ten years, Vesper, ten years invisible, one more winter won't matter—"

"You hid me," Ember said. "Like the king hides things. Cutting pieces off so the rest can survive." He tilted his head back to look at me. "It's not the same. But it rhymes."

"Did you just tell me my mercy rhymes with the Thorn Crown?"

"Somebody should." He wiped his nose on my sleeve, which I permitted because I am weak. "Vesper. The hound that spoke. The voice through it."

"What about it?"

"It wasn't the Crown. Not exactly. It was—" He frowned, hunting for words in a language that had names for things like *grief-of-mountains* and *the-smell-of-yesterday's-rain* but apparently nothing simple. "When they hollow a beast, the voice doesn't die. Voices don't die. They get *kept*. Stored. Like grain in a silo. And the Crown... the Crown eats from the silo."

I sat with that. Somewhere below us, Greyhollow's church bell melted or fell or simply gave up, and the ringing stopped, and the silence that replaced it was the honest kind, the empty kind, nothing wearing anything.

"So every hound they send," I said slowly, "is carrying stolen voices. Including—"

"Including ones like yours. Like mine. Speaker-voices, from the villages they burned." His small hand closed around my finger. "The Crown is building an army out of everything it took away."

Well. There it was. Ten years of careful arithmetic, and the sum total was this: a burned town, a dead baker, a stolen child, and an enemy that had weaponized silence itself. I had spent a decade fluent in everything except trust, and now trust was the only currency left, and I was broke, and the mountains were cold, and the road south ran through a thousand miles of kingdom that wanted us dead.

I should have despaired. Instead—and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one luxury I never smuggled—I felt something click into place, some gear tooth engaging after years of spinning free. All those years of hiding had been a holding pattern. Waiting for a reason. And reasons, like debts, come due eventually.

Below us, the fires would burn till dawn. Above us, the stars wheeled on, indifferent and beautiful, and somewhere in the black crags a wolf began to sing—not a hollowed thing, a real wolf, a voice with somebody home—and I listened to it the way you listen to a promise.

"Ember," I said. "Do the wolves know the old roads? The speaker-roads, the ones my grandmother sang about?"

He was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had that vastness in it again, that echo of being old and many, and it raised the hair on my arms.

"Vesper Winterbourne," he said, using my name like a key fits a lock, "the wolves have been waiting for you since before you were born. And tomorrow, they're going to take us to a parliament."

"A parliament of what?"

He smiled in the firelight, and for the first time since the blue hour he'd frozen on my doorstep, it wasn't a child's smile at all.

"Crows," he said.
