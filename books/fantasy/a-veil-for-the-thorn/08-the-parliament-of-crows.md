## 8. The Parliament of Crows

There is a particular kind of silence that follows the burning of a town you lived in for ten years. It isn't quiet — quiet is just an absence of noise. This was a presence, a heavy wet blanket of sound laid over everything, and Ember and I walked through it for two days without either of us saying a word.

Not that Ember spoke much anyway. Not in any tongue I could answer.

We climbed. The pass out of Greyhollow wasn't a road so much as a rumor — a smuggler's path I'd kept off every map Doss ever paid me to draw, switchbacking up through pine and scree until the trees gave up and the wind took over their job of trying to kill us. I'd walked it a hundred times with contraband on my back and arithmetic in my head. Now I carried a child instead of silk, and the only arithmetic was how many days of dried mutton we had left, divided by how many days until the Wardens found our trail, which was not a division that came out in our favor.

Ember rode my shoulders when the slope got steep, his small hands knotted in my hair, and once — only once — he pressed his cheek against the top of my head and made a sound. Not words. A low hum, like something enormous remembering it used to be enormous.

Every bird within half a mile went silent.

I didn't ask him what he'd said. There are conversations you don't start when your legs are already tired.

* * *

On the third night we found the hollow my grandmother called the Listening Place, though she never told me its real name, if it had one. A bowl of granite ringed by black pines, snow drifted deep against the stones, and in the center a flat rock like a table worn smooth by weather or by centuries of feet. She brought me here when I was nine and made me sit still for four hours while nothing happened, and then a fox walked out of the treeline, looked at her, looked at me, and left again.

"Congratulations," she'd said. "You've been ignored by parliament. It's an honor most humans never earn."

I hadn't been back since she died. Coming back now felt like wearing her coat — too big in some places, exactly right in others, smelling faintly of someone who knew what they were doing.

"We're going to have company," I told Ember. He was crouched at the edge of the table-stone, tracing the old marks carved into it — claw-grooves, tooth-marks, glyphs older than any alphabet the lowlands claimed to have invented. "And I should warn you, the etiquette here is brutal. No flattery. No lies. If you're hungry, you say you're hungry. If you're afraid—" I exhaled. "You say that too."

He looked up at me with those eyes that were wrong for a child's face, too old and too patient, and he nodded like a king agreeing to terms.

The crows arrived first, because crows are always first; it's the whole basis of their economy.

They came down out of the darkening sky in ragged dozens, folding onto the pines and the rocks and one audacious individual onto the table itself, and the sound they made settling in was like a library catching fire. Then, all at once, silence — the deliberate kind, the kind that has weight and intent.

"They know you," Ember said suddenly, in plain Common, his voice rusty as an unused hinge. His eyes were fixed on the largest crow, a scarred old bird perched dead center on the table like a magistrate. "She says you stopped coming."

"The crow said that?"

"The crow says many things." He tilted his head, listening. "She says you owe the mountain a debt."

"Of course she does." I sat down on the cold stone before my knees decided the matter for me. "Hello, Magpie."

Her name wasn't actually Magpie. Crows find names undignified unless they choose them themselves, and this one had chosen hers forty years ago from a dead smuggler's vocabulary, and my grandmother had honored it, and honoring it meant I did too, even though calling a crow Magpie struck me as the avian equivalent of naming a fish Dog.

*You bring a strange cub,* Magpie said, in the way crows speak — not sound exactly, but meaning arriving fully formed behind your eyes, with a texture like gravel and old paper. *It smells of the king's house. Of thorns.*

"He's not the king's," I said. "He's the king's. Both things. It's complicated."

*Crows do not do complicated,* said another voice, deeper, from the treeline.

The wolves came down through the snow without disturbing it, which is a thing wolves can do when they want to remind you they're better at existing than you are. Seven of them, grey and winter-thin, and at their head a female with one ear torn to lace — Nettle, who had known my grandmother longer than I'd been alive, and who had once, memorably, saved my life during a whiteout by sitting on me until I stopped trying to walk anywhere stupid.

*Little speaker,* Nettle said, and there was no warmth in it, but there was no frost either, which from Nettle is practically an embrace. *You smell of smoke and dead men.*

"Greyhollow burned."

*We saw.* Her yellow eyes flicked toward Ember. *We smelled the Wardens' beasts on the wind. Hollow things. Things with no voice left in them.* A shudder ran through the pack — wolves feel the loss of voice the way sailors feel the loss of wind. *What walks behind you, child-of-the-old-tongue?*

Ember answered her in the old language, three soft syllables, and every wolf in the circle lay down.

I mean it. All seven, at once, bellies to the snow, ears flat. Nettle last, fighting it, her lip curling back over her teeth even as her legs folded under her — fury and obedience occupying the same body, which is a state I recognized intimately from my own.

"What did you say to them?" I whispered.

"I said who I am," Ember said. "Or part of who I am. The part that remembers being the mountain's."

"That seems like an unfair advantage in negotiations."

*It is,* said a voice like a landslide deciding to be polite about it.

Osha arrived the way Osha always arrives, which is to say the night simply developed a bear in it. She came up out of the dark beyond the firelight, eight hundred pounds of grizzled patience, and settled herself across the table-stone's approach like a wall that had opinions. In summer she'd have been fishing; in winter, denned and dreaming. That she was awake and here meant the council had already been talking among themselves for days. The animals always knew things before I did. It was the great humiliation of my gift that the beasts gossiped worse than smugglers.

*So,* Osha said. *The granddaughter comes back after ten years, dragging a piece of the king, with the Crown's hounds burning towns behind her.* She breathed, long and slow, and the breath fogged the air between us. *Your grandmother would have led with an apology.*

"My grandmother also died owing half the valley grudges, so I'll take her example with a grain of salt."

Something moved through the assembled animals that might have been laughter. With crows it's a clicking sound. With wolves, a huff through the nose. With Osha it's a tectonic event.

*Good,* the bear rumbled. *She feared you'd turn out soft.*

*Enough,* Magpie snapped, gravel-voiced. *Business. The cub. What is it?*

And so I told them. All of it — the boy at my door in the blue hour, the language in his mouth that matched my grandmother's forbidden lullabies note for note, the maps of places he'd never seen, the Hollow Wardens and their voiceless beasts, Greyhollow burning behind us because I'd made the mistake of taking him in. I told it plainly, because lying to this audience is not merely rude but logistically impossible. Crows can taste falsehood. I don't know how. Nobody knows how. Don't ask a crow; it will cost you a shiny object and your dignity.

When I finished, the silence went on long enough that I started counting my remaining regrets to fill it.

Then Nettle stood, shook the snow from her coat, and said, *The Wardens will come into the mountains. They always come. Their beasts track by absence — by the holes where voices used to be. And the mountains are full of holes now, little speaker. Every winter there are fewer of us who speak, and more of them who have been emptied. You are asking us to shelter the very thing those hounds are hunting, in the last place where we still have numbers.*

"Yes."

*And when they come — and they will come — you are asking us to spend our lives against them.*

"No," I said. "I'm asking you to shelter him. What you spend is your own affair."

*Spoken like a smuggler,* Magpie observed dryly. *Everything has a price and everyone pretends otherwise.*

*I have a different question,* Osha said.

Of course she did. Bears are the philosophers of the parliament — slow to speak, impossible to answer.

*The cub is a piece of Aldric. Cut away. You told us this yourself.* She leaned forward, and her shadow fell across the whole circle. *Which means the rest of him is still sitting in that palace, wearing a crown of thorns, letting his hands do terrible things while his conscience shivers in the snow on a mountain. Your grandmother's generation swore an oath, girl. The first speakers crowned that king to end the Devouring War, and they swore — all of them, beast and human alike — to unmake the crown when the war was done. And then they died, and their children forgot, and the oath passed down thin and thinner until all that was left of it was lullabies and one stubborn woman teaching her granddaughter forbidden syllables by firelight.*

The fire cracked. Ember's hand found mine, small and cold.

*So here is our price,* Osha said. *Not blood. Not service. An oath. Shelter for the cub, passage through our territories, our teeth and claws between him and the hollow hounds — in exchange, you swear to finish it. To go to the throne room where your grandmother never managed to go. To unbind the king. To end what the first speakers began and were too mortal to finish.*

"That's a big oath," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to.

*Yes,* Osha agreed placidly. *That is generally true of worthwhile ones.*

I looked around the circle. Magpie preening, one eye on me like an auditor. Nettle watching with that terrible wolf patience, the kind that can wait out glaciers. The younger wolves pretending not to listen and missing nothing. The crows packed along the branches like a jury that had already voted. And Ember beside me, watching me with an expression I couldn't read — hope, maybe, or pity, or both braided together, because he knew something I didn't yet. He'd known it since the door. Maybe he'd known it before either of us was born, back when he was old and vast and many-voiced.

"You all knew I'd come here," I said slowly. "Didn't you. Before I decided to come. You've been waiting."

*Ten years,* Magpie said.

*Since your grandmother's funeral,* Nettle said. *We argued about you for three days.*

*The crows wanted to give up on you,* Osha rumbled.

*The crows give up on everyone,* Nettle returned. *It's why nobody trusts them with anything important.*

*We trust them with news,* said one of the young wolves.

*News is not important,* said Nettle. *News is merely urgent.*

And there it was — the loneliness I'd been carrying for ten years, fluent in every tongue except trust, hoarding my coin and my silence and my invisibility like a woman saving up for a retirement from her own life — turned inside out and held up to the firelight. They hadn't forgotten me. They'd been *waiting* for me, arguing about me, keeping my seat warm at a table I thought had been struck from every map. The animals had known me better than I knew myself, which is the specific indignity of being loved by creatures who cannot lie: they see the shape of you underneath all your careful arithmetic.

I laughed. It came out rough, half a sob wearing a disguise, and Ember squeezed my hand.

"All right," I said. "All right. I'll swear it."

*Stand,* Osha commanded.

I stood. My legs had gone stiff with cold and something else. The snow had begun to fall again, fat and slow, catching in the crows' feathers, silvering the wolves' backs, and the whole parliament watched me with the stillness of a world holding its breath.

*Say it in the old tongue,* Magpie said. *Common is for buying and selling. This is not commerce.*

So I closed my eyes and reached for the lullabies — the forbidden syllables my grandmother fed me like contraband, verse by verse, telling me they were nothing, telling me to forget them, hiding a kingdom's whole unfinished business inside a child's bedtime. I found the oath-forms among them, the cadences that were built for promising, and I spoke.

*I, Vesper Winterbourne, of Greyhollow and of nowhere, speaker by accident and by inheritance, swear before stone and feather and fur: I will finish what the first speakers began. I will carry the cub Ember to the seat of the thorn-crowned king. I will speak the unbinding, whatever it costs, whoever it unmakes — even if the one it unmakes is me. Witnessed under winter sky, witnessed by the parliament, witnessed by the mountain that listens.*

The moment the last word left my mouth, the mountain listened back.

I don't know how else to describe it. A pressure, vast and slow and geological, turning its attention toward the hollow like an eye the size of a valley opening beneath the snow. The fire guttered. Every animal bowed its head — even Magpie, even Osha, even Ember, though his bow was less submission than recognition, the way you nod to an old colleague across a crowded room.

Then it passed, or sank back, or settled in to wait, and the ordinary night resumed, and the parliament began making its arrangements with the brisk efficiency of people — animals — who had expected this outcome all along.

*The denning caves at Wolf-Winter are empty,* Nettle said. *The pack will take you in. The cub may sleep in the middle. He smells of old things, and old things keep warmth.*

*My ravens will fly ahead of you and behind,* Magpie said. *Wardens move like men who believe no one is watching. We will correct that belief hourly.*

*And I,* said Osha, rising, shaking off snow like a minor avalanche, *will walk with you as far as the high passes. Slowly. I am old, and you are carrying something fragile, and fragile things break when hurried.* She regarded me for a long moment with those small, ancient, unhurried eyes. *Your grandmother swore this same oath once, girl. Did you know that?*

The ground tilted slightly. "...No."

*She swore it the year you were born. She was going to go south the following spring.* Osha turned and began to walk into the dark, her voice rumbling back over her shoulder like distant weather. *Then she had you. And she decided the oath could wait until you were grown, and then she decided it could wait until you were ready, and then she ran out of decisions. Mortals do that. It's why we let you make the promises — someone has to, before the waiting eats the world.*

She paused at the treeline, a black shape against blacker pines.

*Don't run out of decisions, Vesper Winterbourne. The crown has been patient for three hundred years. Be quicker than that.*

* * *

We walked north the next morning behind Nettle's pack, Ember riding pick-a-back, the crows streaming overhead like a banner nobody in the lowlands would ever understand. Somewhere behind us, Wardens with voiceless hounds were reading our trail out of the snow. Somewhere ahead of us was a frozen abbey, a blizzard, a traitor, a grave, and a throne room I had promised, under the oldest sky and in the oldest words I knew, to stand inside.

But that morning there was only snow and wolves and a child humming fragments of a king's stolen memory against my neck, and for the first time in ten years, I was not alone, and not invisible, and not — whatever the price turned out to be — sorry.

Ember tugged my hair. "Vesper," he said. "Nettle wants to know if you snore."

"Tell Nettle," I said, "that bears snore, and she shares a den with a bear, so her standards need adjusting."

Behind us, far to the south, a column of smoke still rose from where Greyhollow had been. Ahead of us, the peaks stood white and silent and listening.

I'd sworn the oath. Now the mountains intended to collect.
