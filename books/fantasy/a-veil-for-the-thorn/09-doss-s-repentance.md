## 9. Doss's Repentance

There is a particular smell a town makes when it burns in winter, and I will spare you the poetry of it. Wet ash. Melted snow over char. The sweetish reek of a granary going up, which is the worst part, because grain means somebody planned on a spring.

I smelled it from three miles south, where I'd been sitting on my mule like a coward with a ledger, and by the time I turned back it was too late to be anything but a witness. That's the arithmetic of Doss Marrow: always counting, always arriving after the sums are done.

Greyhollow burned for most of a day. The Hollow Wardens didn't loot, didn't rape, didn't even shout much. They worked like clerks. They went door to door with a list, and if your name was on it they took you into the square, and if it wasn't they gave you ten minutes to walk out with what you could carry. Ten minutes. You'd be amazed — no, you wouldn't, you're a hard reader or you wouldn't have come this far — you'd be amazed what a person decides to carry when the clock's running. Old Tam Bricket carried his dead wife's boots. Not her letters, not her ring. Boots. A woman named Ferro stood in her doorway holding a cat that had been dead two years, taxidermied, and she set it down gently on the step like it was sleeping, and she walked out with nothing else at all.

I watched from the tree line with maybe forty others who'd had the sense or the cowardice — same coin, different faces — to run early. And here's the thing I can't put down, the stone in my boot that I've been walking on ever since:

I knew.

Not the day of. But weeks before. Warden scouts buying provisions in pairs, asking after speakers the way you ask after weather. A sealed order riding south under escort that should have ridden north. My network told me things, that was its whole purpose, and the thing it told me was *run*, and instead of shouting it from the rooftops of Greyhollow I sat down and did sums. How many of my routes would burn. How many contacts were replaceable. What it would cost me personally.

The answer, for those keeping score at home, was everything except me. That's how I came out of Greyhollow. With me.

* * *

You don't find Vesper Winterbourne unless she wants finding, and even then she'll make you work for it.

I'd spent eleven days doing just that, working, following a trail through the high Thornfell passes that only existed because I'd cut half of it myself over twenty years of smuggling. She was good. Better than I'd taught her, which stung less than you'd think, because mostly I hadn't taught her anything; I'd hired her gift and pointed it at goats and looked away whenever she did whatever it was she did, because looking too close felt like reading another man's letters.

She traveled high and cold, off the roads, moving at night more than day, and the boy rode wrapped against her back like a parcel of contraband. Twice I found camps where she'd cooked nothing, eaten cold, banked no fire. Third time I found a camp where something enormous had slept beside them — pressed snow, a wall of fur-warmth in the drift — and I decided right there that following Vesper Winterbourne was a profession with a very short career ladder.

But I followed anyway. Because of the boots. Because of Ferro's cat. Because every night when I closed my eyes I saw the square in Greyhollow, and old Marta Kess standing on the scaffold platform with her chin up like she was waiting for a coach, and I heard the sound the Wardens' hollowed hounds made when they were loosed, which is no sound at all, which is the whole horror of it — a hunting beast with its voice scooped out comes at you like silence given teeth.

I'm not going to pretend I became a hero on that mountain. What I became was colder, and tireder, and honest in the specific way a man gets honest when he has nothing left to protect except the truth about himself.

* * *

I caught up to them properly on the fourteenth day, or rather I stopped bothering to catch up and let them catch me, which is a smuggler's oldest trick and didn't fool her for a second.

She had a knife at my throat before I heard her move. I'd like to tell you I heard the crunch of snow, some warning, some chance. Nothing. One breath I was chewing jerky behind a boulder, the next there was a blade resting under my jaw light as a lover's finger, and a voice like a frozen rope creaking.

"You're loud, Doss."

"I'm many things," I said, careful not to move my lips much, "but quiet was never one of them. It's why I went into management."

The knife didn't move. Her eyes did — flicking past me, checking the slope, checking the treeline, checking everywhere I might have brought friends. I let her check. There was nobody. There was only me, my mule Bess (who, I should note, has better survival instincts than I do and was already walking away), and two wardens' worth of equipment bundled in oilcloth that I'd taken off two dead men an hour earlier.

Her gaze came back to the bundle. To the grey-and-silver livery showing through the oilcloth. Back to me.

"Where did you get those?"

"Uptrail. Two of Ilvane's people, tracking you. They were very professional." I paused. "They were also very dead now, so I suppose the professionalism didn't take."

"You killed them."

It wasn't quite a question, but I answered it like one, because a man in my position owes exactness. "Crossbow. Both, before they knew I was there, which I learned from watching you people do it to each other for years. Then I took their papers." I fished the folded dispatch out of my coat with two fingers, slow, the way you move around a wild animal, and held it out. "Warden-Captain Ilvane has your route. Half of it. She's guessed the rest."

Vesper didn't take the paper. The boy did — the mute one, Ember, sliding down off her back with that uncanny stillness children have when they've seen too much. He couldn't have been more than eight. He unfolded the dispatch with careful fingers, read it — read it, mind you, fluently, which told me things about what this child actually was that I'd been refusing to think about — and then looked up at Vesper and made a small gesture, palm open, fingers closing once.

Whatever language that was, she understood it. "He says you're telling the truth," she said. "Or he says you smell honest. His signals aren't subtle."

"I'll take either. Honesty of any variety is new territory for me."

"Why are you here, Doss?"

And there it was. The question I'd climbed fourteen days of frozen mountain to avoid answering, delivered flat as a bill of sale. I opened my mouth to give her the practical answer — the dispatch, the danger, the tactical value of a man who knows the passes — and what came out instead was worse and truer.

"Marta Kess kept a stall next to my mother's," I said. "When we were young. Before my mother died and I discovered that other people's misfortunes could be purchased wholesale." The wind came down the pass and I let it, because it gave me somewhere to look. "I knew the Wardens were coming. Weeks. I ran sums, Vesper. I'm good at sums. I decided Greyhollow was an acceptable loss because none of the losses were mine." I finally looked at her. "Then they were all mine. So. Here I am."

She was silent long enough that snow accumulated on both our shoulders. Behind her the boy watched me with eyes the color of banked coals, and I had the distinct uncomfortable sensation of being counted, weighed, and entered into a ledger far older than any I kept.

"You want forgiveness," she said at last.

"No." I meant it. That surprised me most of all. "Forgiveness is a discount, and I don't haggle down from full price. I want a job."

"A job."

"There's two dead wardens back uptrail whose absence will be noticed within the day. There's a captain behind them who reads trails like scripture. And there is exactly one man alive who knows the beast-roads through the Thornfell well enough to move ahead of her instead of behind her, and he's standing here asking for employment at the worst possible rate of pay, which is none." I shrugged, and the knife at my throat finally lowered — not away, just down, like a drawn curtain rather than a withdrawn verdict. "I'm not asking to be trusted, Vesper. Trust takes years and I've spent mine. I'm asking to be *used*. Point me at labor. I'll carry, dig, lie, freeze, and if it comes to it, die, and the only thing I ask in return is that when you write the account of this — and you will, someone will, the ones who survive always get written down — you write that Doss Marrow paid what he owed, at cost, no markup."

Ember said something then. Soft syllables, that old tongue, the one that made the hair on my arms stand up because it sounded less like speech and more like weather deciding something. Vesper listened the way you listen to a judge.

"He says you grieve correctly," she translated, and her voice had gone strange. "That most men grieve themselves. You grieve the town." A beat. "He says that's rare enough to be useful."

"Tell him flattery gets a man nowhere in my organization."

"He's eight."

"Then he's precocious, and I stand corrected."

* * *

She let me carry supplies and nothing else. I want that on the record, because later accounts — if there are later accounts — will inflate it. She did not forgive me. She did not welcome me. She assigned me, the way you assign a mule, and I understood the honor precisely because it wasn't one.

The terms were spoken that first evening over a fire so small it barely qualified, built in a hollow she'd dug beneath a granite shelf. Three rules.

One: I walked point during the day and slept apart at night, downwind, because if the Wardens took me I'd talk — everyone talks, she said, without malice, as simple fact — and she wanted the hours between my talking and their acting.

Two: I touched the boy never. Not to lift him, not to steady him, not to ruffle his hair no matter how the paternal instinct seized me. He'd been handled enough by people with plans for him. I gathered that referred to a king and something wearing a crown, though I didn't know it yet, and she didn't say it, and the not-saying filled the firelight like a third traveler.

Three: I lied to her never again. Not about distances, not about danger, not about small comforts. "I can survive bad news," she said. "What I can't survive is finding out you rounded it down to keep me comfortable. That's what you did to Greyhollow."

I won't claim I slept that night. I lay downwind with Bess breathing warm against my back and I did the only kind of arithmetic that had ever done me any good, which is the kind where you count what you've destroyed. Twenty years of routes. Forty names in my book, and I went through them one by one, marking alive, fled, burned. When I got to the end the total was seventeen. Seventeen out of forty. I fell asleep doing that sum and woke with frost in my beard and the number unchanged, which is the nature of such numbers.

* * *

We moved north by east along a shelf road I'd cut with two dead men in '09, back when the Thornfell's smuggler economy was a gentleman's arrangement between people too poor to betray each other profitably. Vesper walked like she owned the mountain's grudges personally. The boy rode her back and watched everything, and I mean everything — he watched the way old priests watch, like the world was text he'd read before and was verifying against memory.

On the second day the ravens found us.

I know, I know. Ravens follow travelers, carrion-birds, nothing uncanny. But these came down out of the white in a loose wheel, six of them, and they didn't beg food. They landed in a row on a dead pine like a delegation, and one of them — big fellow, scarred cere, feathers gone bronze at the edges — fixed Ember with one eye and croaked three notes that were almost words.

Ember answered. Full voice, that ancient tongue, and the raven cocked its head and answered back, and the two of them held a conversation of perhaps a minute while Vesper stood absolutely still and I stood absolutely stiller, because I have survived twenty years in the smuggling trade by knowing when a thing is above my pay grade.

Afterward the ravens lifted, wheeled once, and went north. Fast. Purposeful. Like couriers.

"What," I said, "in the name of every god I've ever shortchanged, was that."

"Quill," Vesper said. "The big one. He's been carrying messages since before the town burned." She adjusted the boy's weight on her back. "He says there's a column on our trail. Wardens, eight of them, plus hounds. Two days back, moving fast."

"Two days." I did the math automatically, the old reflex. "With the shelf road and the ice-fall detour, that's—"

"Four days if they guess wrong. One if they guess right." She looked at me, and there it was — the thing I'd asked for and dreaded equally. Need. She needed what I knew. "You said you wanted a job, Doss. Here's the job. Tell me where they'll guess wrong."

So I did. I spread my twenty years out on the snow like a merchant laying out cloth, every shortcut and chokepoint and place where the mountain forgives a mistake or doesn't, and I watched her take it in the way she took in everything — completely, silently, with no expression wasted. And when I finished she nodded once and said, "Good," and that single syllable warmed me more than the fire had, which tells you exactly how far a man like me has fallen and how little light he needs.

* * *

Ilvane's column gained on us anyway. Good captains do.

By the fifth day we could see their sign — the terrible clean sign of hollowed beasts, tracks straight as survey lines, none of the wandering and scent-marking of living animals. Just purpose, printed in snow. We crossed their morning camp once, abandoned two hours prior, and I found the stake where a hound had been tied and stared at the ground around it. No droppings. No scattered feed. No bones from a thrown scrap.

"They don't eat?" I asked.

"They don't live," Vesper said. "There's nothing left in them to speak to. Believe me, I've tried." Something crossed her face when she said it, grief wearing anger's coat, and I had the wisdom — newly acquired, expensive — to say nothing.

That night she changed our route based on my counsel, taking the long way around the Sorrow Shelf through a crevasse field I'd only crossed twice, both times drunk, which I did not mention until after we were through it, at which point mentioning seemed redundant.

And on the sixth day, in a narrow defile below the Widow's Teeth, the job I'd asked for arrived at last in its true costume.

They'd split the column. Ilvane was smart enough to send climbers along the ridge while the main body tracked the valley floor, and the two climbers — scouts, fast movers, the dangerous kind — came down on us from above while we were boxed in the defile with ice walls on three sides. I heard the scrape of a boot on stone before anyone else, because guilt sharpens the ears, and I didn't call out a warning.

I threw the warning away and acted on it instead.

Here's what I did, plainly, because plainly is all I have left: I stepped out of cover into the open defile, raised my crossbow, and shot the first scout through the chest as he came around the rock. The second got a bolt off — took me through the meat of the left shoulder, a wound I'd rate as educational — and then closed with a hatchet, and we went down together into the snow, and I got my belt knife into him twice before he got his hands around my throat once, and then Vesper was there, and it ended, and the defile was quiet again except for my own disgraceful breathing.

She knelt over me, checked the shoulder wound with hands that missed nothing, and her face was unreadable as a customs seal.

"You could have called out," she said. "Warned us. Let us handle it."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

I looked past her at the boy, who was watching me from the shadow of the ice wall with those coal-colored eyes, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that I wanted to answer him more than her.

"Because calling out spends other people's lives to save your own comfort," I said. "I did that arithmetic in Greyhollow. I'm retiring it."

Vesper studied me for a long moment. Snow ticked down from the ice walls. Somewhere above, a raven called once, three notes, almost words.

"The wound needs stitching," she said finally. "You'll slow us down. You'll complain constantly. You'll probably talk in your sleep and compromise us all." She pulled out needle and gut thread, coarse as rigging line, because everything about Vesper Winterbourne was built for function and nothing whatsoever for comfort. "You're carrying the tent from now on. And the salt pork."

"That's it?"

"That's it. You carry supplies and nothing else." She threaded the needle. "This is going to hurt."

"It usually does," I said, "in my experience, when it's done properly."

She stitched me there in the defile with the two wardens cooling beside us, and I bit down on a strap of leather and did not cry out, partly from toughness, mostly from the discovery — brand new, still warm, fragile as an ember in wet grass — that a man can bear a great deal of pain gladly, if it's the pain of paying what he owes.

* * *

That night, downwind as agreed, I wrote in my head the account I'd asked her to someday let me be part of. Not the heroic version. The true one.

*Doss Marrow, smuggler, sold forty neighbors for the price of his own skin, and bought back seventeen of them at the cost of everything else. Terms of payment: supplies carried, complaints issued, lies retired. Balance outstanding.*

Still owing. Still walking.

Above us, on the ridge against the last grey light, the raven called again — and this time I'd swear on my mother's grave, which is the only collateral I have left, that the mountain itself answered. A low sound, deep under the snow, like a word being remembered.

I decided not to mention it. Some news, I'm learning, keeps better unrounded.
