## 10. The Frozen Abbey

We found the abbey on the ninth day of walking, which is the sort of arithmetic that makes a person start believing in divine intervention out of sheer spite.

It sat in a fold of the mountain like a coin dropped in snow—a grey shell of a building with one tower still standing and the rest shrugged into a drift that had been accumulating since before my grandmother learned to swear. The wolves found it first, naturally. Osha's pack ranged ahead of us the way they'd done since the Parliament, and when I crested the ridge behind them, three of the younger ones were already digging at a doorway half-buried to its lintel, tails going like metronomes set to *triumph*.

"There better not be anything dead in there," I told them.

The youngest—Fen, all elbows and enthusiasm even for a wolf—looked back at me with an expression I can only describe as *offended*. Through the gift, his meaning arrived as a wash of scent-impressions: cold stone, old incense, mouse, mouse, more mouse, nothing larger than mouse. Wolves don't lie. It's one of their few redeeming qualities and they know it, which makes them insufferable about everything else.

"Fine," I said. "Good work. Don't let it go to your head."

Ember had gone very still beside me. He did that sometimes—stopped mid-stride the way water stops when it finds a hole in the ice, all of him pouring toward something only he could see. He was looking at the abbey's broken face, at the empty sockets where windows had been, and whatever he saw there made him pull his hood tighter.

"You know this place," I said. Not a question. I'd stopped pretending my questions were questions somewhere around day four.

He nodded once. Then he walked past me, down through the snow, and put his small mittened hand flat against the buried door as if greeting an old dog.

I've learned not to ask what he greets. Mostly because the answers frighten me, and partly because he answers in that language—the old tongue, the lullaby language—and every time he speaks it, something in my chest pulls tight like a stitch in a wound that never quite closed. My grandmother sang me fragments of it over a cookfire thirty years ago, her voice dropped low enough that the flames seemed to lean in. She said it was the language the world spoke before people taught it to lie. She also said if anyone ever asked where I'd heard it, I should say I hadn't. Grandmothers are excellent at handing down both gifts and curses, often in the same breath.

Doss caught up as we were clearing the door. He'd taken to hanging back fifty paces since the pass, carrying the supplies and nothing else, exactly as ordered, and radiating guilt the way a wet dog radiates smell. He looked at the abbey, then at me.

"Consecrated ground," he said. "Bad luck, sleeping in it."

"You're a smuggler, Doss."

"I'm a *superstitious* smuggler."

"Then sleep outside." I shouldered the door—it groaned open on hinges that had rusted into sculpture—and stepped into the dark. "Besides, whatever god lived here has been evicted. The rent's gone unpaid for centuries."

Behind me, I heard him mutter something that was either a prayer or an insult, possibly both. With Doss it was always hard to say.

* * *

Inside, the abbey was a cathedral of blue.

That's what the light did in there. Snowlight came down through the collapsed roof in long slanted columns, and the stone drank it and gave it back changed—everything washed in that deep hour-of-dusk blue that makes even ruins look like they're holding their breath. Our footsteps echoed wrong, too loud, as though the building remembered being full of voices and resented the comparison.

And the walls were painted.

Murals, hundreds of years old, running the length of the nave in a procession of figures: people, animals, mountains. The paint had faded to ghost-colors, ochre and iron-red and a green so pale it was almost a memory of green, but the shapes held. I walked the line of them slowly while the wolves spread out below, sniffing along the bases of the walls, and Doss got a fire going in a hearth some prior squatter had thoughtfully not demolished.

The story the walls told went like this:

First panel: a land under teeth. Figures fleeing across a landscape rendered in jagged black strokes, pursued by shapes I didn't have names for—vast, many-limbed, drawn with a kind of loving horror. Above them, animals and humans ran together, mixed, indistinguishable. Speakers and beasts, fleeing the same hunger.

Second panel: the speakers standing. Human figures with animals at their shoulders—wolves, crows, bears, stags—each figure shown with lines running from their mouths to the mouths of the beasts, speech made visible as thread. A parliament painted on plaster. My grandmother's councils, frozen mid-session.

Third panel: the crowning.

A king, kneeling. Around him the speakers stood in a ring, and above his head they raised—not a crown of gold, not metal at all, but a circlet of thorned branches, woven, living. And here the painter had done something that stopped me flat: the threads of speech ran from the speakers' mouths not just to the animals, but *into the crown itself*, binding king and branch and beast into one knot of language.

A bargain, painted by someone who'd been in the room.

"The Devouring War," I said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice confirm the room wasn't entirely dead. "Maren's ashes-and-paperwork version. The thing that ate the north until the first speakers crowned a king to stop it."

"Cheerful décor," Doss offered from the fire. He'd wedged himself against the far wall with his bedroll between him and the murals, which I chose to interpret as respect rather than cowardice. With Doss you could choose your interpretation freely; he provided no evidence either way.

But it was the fourth panel that mattered, and the fourth panel was wrong.

Or rather, it was unfinished. The plaster there had crumbled—a whole section fallen away into rubble on the floor, leaving a gap in the story right after the crowning. Whatever came next—the reign, the bargain's terms, the ending—was dust. The wall simply stopped, the way histories do when nobody's left alive to finish them.

Ember was standing in front of it.

He'd shed his mittens. His bare hand hovered an inch from the painted king's bowed head, and the blue light moved on his face like water, and I felt the hair rise on my arms before I understood why. The wolves had gone silent. All of them, all at once, the way a forest goes silent—not absence of sound but *attention*, the whole room leaning in.

"Ember," I said carefully. "Don't—"

He touched the wall.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because I've turned it over so many times I risk polishing it into something it wasn't. There was no thunder. No light show. The mountain did not shake. What happened was quieter than that and considerably worse.

The paint *moved*.

Not much. Not dramatically. But where Ember's fingers rested, color seeped back into the faded figures like blood returning to a numb limb—the ochre deepening, the green remembering itself—and the crumbling gap in the fourth panel began, gently, horribly, to fill. Plaster regrew the way frost grows, in slow feathering branches, and on that new surface images surfaced as if rising through water.

A throne room. I knew it instantly, the way you know your own face in a bad mirror—I'd seen it in Ember's drawings, night after night, in shelters and hollows across nine days of winter. High vaulted ceiling. Long hall. And on the throne, a man with a crown of living thorns, the branches grown down past his temples, into his jaw, threading through him like roots through soil.

Around the painted throne, the speakers' circle again—but this time the figures were fading, thinning, their speech-threads fraying and snapping one by one. And the crown grew thicker with each thread that broke.

"It was supposed to end," I heard myself say. My voice came out flat, factual, the voice I use for prices and distances, because the alternative voice would have been screaming. "The crown. It was meant to be temporary. A tourniquet, not a—"

Ember finished the sentence for me, in the old tongue, without turning around.

I don't speak it fluently. I have lullabies and fragments and a grandmother's careful omissions. But I didn't need fluency for that. Some words carry their meaning the way a blade carries its edge. What he said translated itself somewhere below language, straight into the marrow:

*A season. It was lent for a season.*

The mural kept growing under his hand. Now it showed the king trying to remove the crown—painted small, in a corner, a figure hunched over his own reflection in a mirror, hands bloody from the thorns. Then the crown alone on the throne, vast now, the man beneath it diminished to a shape barely distinguishable from the chair. Then wardens—hollow-eyed, marching, villages burning in miniature, each flame a fleck of red the painter could not possibly have known to include and yet there it was, fresh as yesterday.

History correcting itself in real time. That's the phrase that came to me later, lying awake under three blankets with a wolf against my back for warmth. Somewhere in the last ten years, the record had been doctored—or burned, or sealed in Maren Ostwick's contraband boxes—and here was a boy with a splinter of a king's conscience in his chest, restoring the minutes of the meeting.

The final image formed at the edge of the new plaster, and it was small, almost an afterthought: a woman with dark hair standing before the throne, a child at her side, and above them the crown cracking like river ice in spring.

Ember took his hand away.

The colors stayed.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. Even Doss, who could talk through a funeral and frequently had, sat with his mouth open, staring at a wall that now told a story four hundred years newer than it had at supper.

Finally he said, weakly, "Is that supposed to happen?"

"No," I said.

"Because if abbeys do that regularly, I've been robbing the wrong buildings for twenty years."

"Doss."

"Right. Shutting up." He shut up for nearly a full minute, which for Doss was practically monastic discipline. Then: "The lad drew that throne room. On the trail. Night before last, in the dirt, with a stick. Same hall, same chair, same—" He gestured at the mural, at the thorn-rooted king. "Same nightmare."

"He doesn't have nightmares," I said. "He has memories. They're worse."

Ember had finally turned from the wall. In the blue light his face was the oldest thing in the room, older than the abbey, older than the paint, and he looked at me with an expression I'd started to dread—the one that meant he was about to translate something vast into the small vocabulary of a seven-year-old body.

He pointed at the fourth panel. At the fading speakers, their threads snapping. Then he pointed at himself. Then, with terrible gentleness, at me.

"I know," I said. "You're the missing piece. You've mentioned."

He shook his head. He pointed again—himself, me, then the painted woman and child before the cracking crown—and this time he spoke Common, haltingly, the words dragged up from wherever he keeps them.

"*Both.* Both go. Crown wants... wants its piece back. Wants the *first speaker's* line." His hand flattened against his own chest, over the sternum, where I imagined a shard of a king's amputated conscience sitting like a swallowed coal. "It knows we're close. It's been... reaching. Since the pass. Since the wolves." His brow furrowed, hunting for the word, and what he found made my stomach drop through the floor and keep falling. "*Listening.*"

The wolves stirred. Osha herself—she'd come partway with us, a mountain of a bear needs a reason to cross a valley and apparently we qualified—rose from where she'd been drowsing near the door and fixed her small dark eyes on the south wall, on the direction of the capital, of the palace, of the thing wearing Aldric like a coat.

Through the gift, her meaning arrived simple and enormous, the way bear-meanings always do:

*Something is listening. So we will be loud.*

I laughed. It came out cracked and slightly unhinged, and it felt wonderful.

"Yes," I told her. "Loud. First thing tomorrow, we move, and we move fast, and if the Crown wants to listen—" I looked at the mural, at the small painted woman with the child at her side, at the crown cracking above them like spring ice. "—it can hear exactly what's coming."

Doss cleared his throat. "And what *is* coming? Asking for inventory purposes."

"A king," I said, "who owes a debt four hundred years overdue. And a boy who remembers the terms."

Outside, the wind picked up, moaning through the abbey's broken windows—and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the southern passes, came the belling of hounds.

Hollow ones. Voiceless, and therefore tireless, and therefore closing.

Osha's ears swiveled. Fen growled low, a sound like a rockslide deciding.

So much for a full night's sleep.
