## 11. Ilvane's Doubt

The smuggler's name was Ferren Cobb, and he had the eyes of a man who had already decided how his story would end and was merely waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

I sat across from him in the interrogation tent at the edge of the burned village—*the secured settlement*, the dispatches insisted, as though a word could unburn a roof—and watched him bleed from a split lip he'd earned resisting arrest, which was to say, from a warden's baton applied with more enthusiasm than the regulations permitted. I noted the injury in my ledger. I always note the injuries. A ledger that only records what the Crown wants recorded isn't a ledger; it's a prayer.

"Name," I said.

"Ferren Cobb." His voice was steady. That was the first wrong thing. Men in his position are usually negotiating by now—offering names, routes, the location of a cache in exchange for a cell instead of a rope. This one sat with his hands cuffed to the table as if they were a formality he'd outgrown.

"Occupation."

"Roads," he said. "I keep roads open."

"The Crown keeps the roads."

"The Crown closes them." He smiled through the blood. "Somebody's got to do the opposite."

I have been a Warden-Captain for eleven years. I have interrogated poachers, traitors, a bishop, and one very confused man who had genuinely believed the royal seal on his forgery was real because he'd made it so well. I know the taxonomy of the guilty. Most of them want something: leniency, vengeance, an audience. Cobb wanted nothing I could see, and that made him either a fanatic or a man with something he considered more important than his own neck.

Both categories, in my experience, tend to know things.

"Your caravan was carrying a woman and a child," I said. "The woman is called Winterbourne. The child has no name in our records. Tell me about them."

"Never saw them."

"You fed them at your own table two nights before we burned—before we *secured* your associate's inn. Three witnesses."

"Then I guess you don't need me."

"We don't," I agreed. "The rope is already priced. But there is a difference between hanging as a smuggler and hanging as a traitor, and only one of those lets your family keep the house."

There it was. A flicker. Not fear—something older than fear. Grief, maybe, arriving early, the way a man flinches before the blow he's already accepted. He looked at his cuffed hands for a long moment, and when he looked up, he'd made some private arithmetic and the total had surprised even him.

"You'll write it down exactly as I say it?" he asked. "Word for word?"

"I keep exact records."

"Good." He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "Because my grandmother told me this story, and her grandmother told her, and none of us ever thought it would matter, because who burns a whole country over a *story*?"

I uncapped my ink.

* * *

The story, as Cobb told it—and I set it down word for word, because he made me swear, and because exactness is the only piety I have ever practiced—was this:

Before the kingdom, before the roads and the tolls and the Wardens, the mountains were not empty. They were *inhabited*—not by people, Cobb said, not the way we mean it, but by voices. Every peak had one, every river, every old tree with roots deep enough to have opinions. The first people to settle the valleys learned to speak with them. Not commanded them—Cobb was very insistent on this distinction, leaning on it like a man leaning on a fence that was the only thing between him and a drop. *Spoke with.* The old tongue, he called it. The listening language.

And then the Devouring War came.

No one remembers what the Devouring was. Cobb didn't, and neither did his grandmother, and neither, he suspected, did anyone, because that was rather the point. Something had eaten the land itself—not the crops, not the herds, but the *meaning* of things. Fields that forgot how to grow. Rivers that ran but forgot they were supposed to reach the sea. People who woke up one morning and couldn't remember why they loved their children, and went through the motions anyway, hollow, polite, terrible.

The first speakers—the beast-speakers, the listeners—fought it the only way they knew: by lending the land their own voices, their own memories, their own names, until there was almost nothing left of them. And when even that wasn't enough, they did the thing Cobb's family had whispered about for six generations with the lamps turned low.

They made a crown.

Not a symbol. A *thing*. They wove it from everything they had left—their oaths, their grief, their memory of justice—and they set it on the head of a king, and they told it: *hold the line. Hold it until the Devouring is dead. And then let go.*

"And then?" I asked.

Cobb looked at me with something close to pity.

"Captains," he said, "when did the Crown ever let go of anything?"

* * *

I finished the interrogation at dusk. Cobb gave me nothing else of use—no routes, no names, no cache locations. He'd spent his one coin on a story, and he knew it, and he sat back afterward with the particular peace of a man who has paid a debt.

"Your family," I said, before they took him away. "The house. You understand I can't—"

"I know what you can do." He paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the ash-grey evening. "You want to do one thing for me, Captain? Read your own orders. All the way down. To the bottom of the paper."

They marched him out. I sat alone with my lamp and my ledger and the transcript, and I told myself the unease in my chest was the cold.

It was not the cold.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about me, if you are to understand what I did next, which I still have not entirely forgiven myself for, and which I would do again before breakfast.

I believe in order.

Not the word—everyone believes in the word; the word is cheap as a blessing. I mean the thing itself. I grew up in a border town where the law arrived once a year, in the person of a tax assessor who was also a drunk, and I watched what the gaps did to people. My father was killed in a dispute over a field boundary that no court had ever ruled on, because the nearest court was four hundred miles away, because the kingdom had never bothered. He died over a strip of mud, and the man who killed him died three years later over a different strip of mud, and I was eleven years old, and I decided—I *vowed*—that I would spend my life making sure the map and the ground agreed with each other.

The Wardens were the only institution I ever found that meant it. We didn't take bribes. We didn't take sides. We took *records*. Every order signed, every action logged, every chain of command documented down to the last link. Other soldiers joked that we worshipped paperwork. They were not wrong, exactly. Paperwork is what justice looks like when you can't afford to trust anyone's word, including your own.

So when a dying smuggler told me to read my own orders to the bottom of the paper, my first instinct—the correct, professional, eleven-years-honed instinct—was to do precisely that.

The problem was that the bottom of the paper did not exist.

* * *

I want to be careful here, because this is the part I have replayed a thousand times, and each time I find a new door I should have opened sooner.

The Warden directives come from the Crown. That is the whole architecture. The Crown issues a sealed directive; the directive is logged; the log is archived. I have personally handled hundreds of them. They bear the royal seal, the royal cipher, the royal signature—or rather, the royal *sign*, because in eleven years I had never once seen the king's hand. Directives arrive by warden-courier, sealed in black wax, and the courier waits while you read and signs your receipt, and then the courier leaves, and that is the entire transaction.

I had always assumed the king's absence from the process was efficiency. Kings are busy. The realm is large. A man cannot sign every order in his own hand, and a good king surrounds himself with instruments that function without his constant attention. This is what I believed. I want that on the record. I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you: not as a conclusion, but as a thing you never had reason to think about at all.

That night, in my tent, I took out every directive I had ever been issued that I was legally permitted to retain—thirty-one of them, spanning six years—and I laid them out on my field desk in chronological order, and I did the thing no one in the history of the Hollow Wardens, as far as I could determine, had ever thought to do.

I read them *together*.

* * *

Individually, they were unremarkable. That is their genius, and I use the word with respect, the way you'd respect a well-made trap. Each directive concerned a single matter: a village to be searched, a speaker to be detained, an archive to be sealed. Each was precise, proportionate in its language, and scrupulous about procedure. Whoever wrote them understood regulation the way a surgeon understands anatomy. If you read one, you slept well.

Read together, they were a sentence being spelled out over six years, and the sentence was: *there are no more speakers, and there never were, and anyone who says otherwise was never born.*

The burnings began in year four. The first directives had ordered *detention*. Then *isolation*. Then, in a directive whose procedural language did not change by a single syllable, the objective became *removal*, and the definition of removal was left as an exercise for the reader, and we all did the reading, and we all did the arithmetic, and we filed our reports, and the reports came back stamped *received*, and nothing in the machine ever objected.

Because here is the thing I found, the thing that made me sit down on my cot in full uniform at two in the morning like a recruit with heatstroke:

The directives had no author.

Not *no named author*—directives are sealed; anonymity is procedure. I mean no *origin*. I pulled the receipt chains. Every directive I'd ever received had been handed to me by a courier. I pulled the couriers' assignment rosters. Every courier had been assigned by a regional dispatch office. I pulled the dispatch offices' authorization ledgers. Every office had received its instructions from the Office of the Royal Seal.

I had never heard of the Office of the Royal Seal.

Neither had any of the three senior clerks I queried—carefully, hypothetically, over the course of a week, in the tone of a captain doing a routine audit. The Office of the Royal Seal appeared in the authorization ledgers of every dispatch office in the northern provinces. It appeared in no organizational chart. It appeared in no budget. It appeared in no directory of the civil service, and I checked four editions, including one from before the king's coronation. It had no head. It had no address. When I wrote to it—once, formally, requesting clarification of a procedural point—the reply arrived in two days, in perfect regulation format, answering my question completely and correctly.

The reply had no seal.

An office named for a seal that does not use one. I laughed when I saw that. I sat in my tent and laughed until my adjutant asked if I was well, and I said I was conducting an audit, and he believed me, because why wouldn't he? I am a woman who believes in paperwork. I am the last person anyone would suspect of noticing that the paperwork has no floor beneath it.

* * *

There was one more place to look. I want to say I hesitated, that I weighed the risk like a professional. I did not. I had passed the point where the vertigo takes you—the moment when you realize the staircase you've been descending your whole life is attached to nothing—and past vertigo there is only a kind of terrible clarity. If my orders came from nowhere, then everything I had done came from nowhere. Every village. Every ledger entry. Every *secured settlement*. Either I found the bottom of the paper, or I had to conclude that I had spent eleven years as the instrument of a ghost, and I could not live with the second thing, so I went looking for the first.

The sealed royal archive beneath the Warden citadel at Karst is supposed to require a writ signed by the king himself. I had no writ. What I had was eleven years of flawless service, a reputation for procedural rigor so absolute that no one questioned what I was doing in the sub-basement at midnight with a lamp and a crowbar, and the observation—made over years, filed away without thought, the way you file everything until the day you need it—that the lock on the archive door was older than the citadel and had been oiled recently by someone who did not want to break it.

Inside: the banned records. Not burned, as the directives claimed. *Filed.* Shelf after shelf of them, in perfect order, catalogued in a hand I did not recognize. Someone had been collecting the evidence of the Crown's own crimes and preserving it with the tenderness of an archivist. That frightened me more than anything else I found, and I found a great deal.

I found the Devouring War. Not the legend—Cobb's story was a child's drawing next to this. I found requisition rolls from the first speakers' settlements. I found a treaty, written in three scripts, two of them dead, one of them the language the child in Greyhollow had been reported speaking. I found the coronation record of King Aldric, and appended to it, in the same dead script, a document that the catalogue described with a single word in the old tongue.

I had the catalogue's glossary. The word translated as: *temporary*.

And beneath the coronation record, sealed separately, marked with the cipher of the Office of the Royal Seal—the office that does not exist, the office that answers letters without a seal—I found the first directive. The founding one. The one from which, I now understood, all the others descended like water finding every crack in a wall.

It was not signed by a king.

It was not signed by a man at all. The mark at its foot was pressed into the wax the way a branch is pressed into soft earth, and it was a crown of thorns, drawn from life, and around it the wax had curled and blackened as though the document had been *growing*.

The directive was dated eleven years ago. Three days after Aldric's coronation.

I stood in that cold archive with the lamp guttering and read my whole career backwards. Every order I had ever obeyed had flowed from that one document, through offices that existed on no chart, signed by a hand that was not a hand. The chain of command did not lead to the throne. It led *through* it—the way a rope leads through a pulley. The throne was not the source. The throne was the point where the force turned.

Cobb had told me a story about a crown that was told to let go.

I had spent my life believing in the map. And the map, it turned out, had been drawn by the thing it described.

* * *

I did what I always do with evidence. I copied it. Exact records—word for word, mark for mark, the blackened wax rendered in careful ink. Then I locked the archive, oiled the lock, and walked back through the sleeping citadel past the portraits of warden-captains dead and honorable, and I felt their painted eyes on me, and for the first time in eleven years I could not tell whether I was the law or merely its glove.

In my tent, I opened my ledger to a fresh page. At the top, in my precise hand, I wrote the date and the words: *Audit of command origin, ongoing.*

Below that, I wrote the question I could not put down, the one that had been forming since a bleeding smuggler told me to read to the bottom of the paper:

*If the orders do not come from the king, who—or what—gave the order to find the child?*

Because that was in the directives too. The recent ones. The ones that had sent me to Greyhollow. Priority absolute: a speaker-child, small, dark-haired, traveling with a woman. Every resource. Every warden. *Removal.*

The Crown did not hunt smugglers with priority absolute. The Crown did not hunt *anyone* with priority absolute. Whatever that child was, the thing at the top of my chain of command wanted him the way a body wants a missing piece of itself.

And somewhere in the high passes, in this killing cold, a woman who kept roads open was carrying him north.

I closed the ledger. I sat for a long time, listening to the wind worry the tent ropes, and I understood that I was no longer standing where I had stood that morning, and that there was no path back to it, and that the only direction left was the one the smuggler had, in his way, already pointed me: down through the paper, past the seal, to whatever was holding the pen.

In the morning, I would request a transfer of my command south, citing the pursuit. In the morning, I would lie with a straight face to men I had served beside for a decade, because the truth was no longer safe inside the walls of the thing I had given my life to.

And if I found the child first—if I reached him before the directives did—I would have to decide what a woman does when she discovers that her whole life has been an entry in someone else's ledger.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, doing the arithmetic.

The total surprised even me.
