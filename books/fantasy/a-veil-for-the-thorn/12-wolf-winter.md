## 12. Wolf-Winter

The blizzard came down on the ninth hour of the ninth day of our climb, which even I—a woman who makes her living reading omens in animal spoor—had to admit had a certain theatrical timing. One moment we were picking our way along a ridge with a sky like pewter overhead; the next, the world turned to white static and the wind began trying to peel my face off like the skin from an orange.

"Osha's den," I shouted at Doss, who was hauling the supply sled like a man doing penance, which he was. "Two miles north. If we don't make it before dark, we're meat."

Ember tugged my sleeve and shouted something back. The wind ate it. But the wolves heard. They always heard him.

They came out of the white like an apology—six grey shapes materializing at Osha's silent command, pressing their flanks against Ember's legs, breaking the wind for him without being asked. The pack had adopted him the way mountains adopt rivers: completely, and without any expectation that he'd stay where they put him.

We made the den by last light. It was less a cave than a wound in the mountain's flank, deep enough to hold twenty wolves, two humans, one repentant smuggler, and—if you counted him separately, which I was beginning to—the fragment of a king's soul wearing a child's boots.

I counted us lucky. That should have been my first warning. Luck, in my experience, is just misfortune saving up.

* * *

Living with wolves for nine days teaches you things about yourself you'd rather not learn. For instance: I snore, because Rill the yearling informed me of it on day two, loudly, in front of everyone. For another: Doss Marrow, who once cheated a garrison quartermaster out of forty crowns and talked his way past a Warden checkpoint with nothing but a ham and a sob story, cannot abide being licked by wolves. He sat rigid in the corner of the den like a man attending his own funeral, while pups used him as furniture.

"You could relax," I told him, passing him dried elk. "They've decided you're pack."

"They decided I'm *theirs*, is what they decided." He brushed at his sleeve where a pup named Sorrel had left a generous amount of slobber. "There's a difference, Vesper. Theirs gets eaten if the winter goes bad."

"Warm," I said. "You're learning to be warm."

He grunted and let Sorrel climb into his lap anyway, which told me more about his repentance than the two wardens he'd killed ever had.

The den smelled of musk and snow and crushed pine, a smell I knew from my grandmother's winter stories—she'd claimed she spent a whole season among wolves once, back when speakers still gathered openly. I'd thought those were fables when I was young. Sitting there, wrapped in a pile of warm fur while the storm screamed outside, I understood she'd been leaving me instructions. She'd been leaving me instructions my whole life, and I'd read them as bedtime stories because instructions are frightening and bedtime stories are not.

Ember spent those days with the pack. Not playing—or not only playing—but listening. He'd lie with his ear against a wolf's ribs and his lips would move, practicing syllables, and the wolves would answer in their body-language way, and somewhere between them a conversation happened that I could feel at the edge of my gift like heat from a fire in the next room. My grandmother's tongue lived in him the way water lives in snow. In me it was more like a coin saved too long: real, but worn nearly smooth.

On the fourth day, Osha brought me a frozen ptarmigan, dropped it in front of me, and looked at me with yellow eyes full of patience.

"I know what you want," I said. The bear matriarch had made me swear an oath in the Parliament of Crows, and oaths sworn over crow-feathers and old bones have a weight that doesn't lift. *Finish what the first speakers started.* "It's coming. We're walking toward it as fast as legs allow."

Osha huffed through her nose. In bear, that can mean many things. This one meant: *Not fast enough.*

She wasn't wrong. Every day the storm pinned us, I felt the capital pulling at Ember like a tide. He dreamed louder. He woke speaking words that weren't his. And sometimes, watching him sleep, I caught a look on his small face that belonged to no child—an expression of such old, specific grief that it stopped my breath. A king's grief, wearing a boy's mouth.

* * *

The cub began dying on the fifth night.

Her name—I say her name; the pack called her something like *Small-River-Running-Under-Ice*, which is a mouthful, so Ember shortened it to Ice, and Ice stuck even among humans—she was the runt of the litter, born late, wrong side of the season. On the fifth night she stopped nursing. By morning she lay in the deepest hollow of the den breathing in shallow hiccups, and her mother stood over her making a sound I hope I never hear again: the low, continuous keening of an animal that understands death perfectly and has no word to argue with it.

My gift rose up in me and found nothing to grip. That's the truth of beast-speech nobody romanticizes: you can talk all you like, but you cannot talk a body into living. I knelt beside Ice and told her mother everything true—that the cold had gotten into her blood, that her heart was stuttering like a tired bird—and the she-wolf looked at me with eyes that said, *Then fix it,* and I had never felt more useless in ten years of careful invisibility.

Ember knelt down across from me.

"No," I said, before he'd even done anything. Because I already knew. Don't ask me how—I knew the way you know a floor is rotten before your foot goes through. "Ember. Look at me. No."

He looked at me. His eyes were calm in a way no seven-year-old's eyes are calm, and behind the calm something vast was already moving, gathering itself like weather.

"She is pack," he said. Simple as arithmetic.

"She is a wolf. Wolves die. It's winter. It's awful and it's *winter.*"

"The old tongue doesn't distinguish," he said, and there was something in his voice that wasn't the boy, some echo of lecture halls or councils or whatever passed for them before kingdoms. "That was its whole nature. What is spoken for one is spoken for all. Vesper—" and here the vastness receded and he was just a child again, scared, stubborn, "—I can feel her going out. Like a lamp. You'd stop it. Anyone would."

"Not at this price."

"You don't know the price."

"I know *you* don't." I grabbed his shoulder. "Every time you reach for that thing inside you, something comes back missing. You told me. After the pass, after the ravens—you lost the name of a river. You lost the taste of something you couldn't describe. Small things, so far. But it's not small things forever, Ember. It's a purse with a hole in it, and eventually you reach in for gold and come out with nothing."

The she-wolf keened. Ice hiccupped a breath and didn't finish it properly.

And I saw the exact moment I lost—not to the power in him, which frightened me, but to the boy underneath it, who had watched a town burn for his sake and had decided, privately and permanently, that he would never again be the reason something died if his two hands could prevent it.

"I'm sorry," he said, and he genuinely was. "Hold my hand? So I know where home is."

God help me, I held his hand.

* * *

What he did next, I will set down as plainly as I can, because plainness is all I have against it.

He laid both palms on the cub's ribs and he spoke.

The old tongue doesn't sound like language when he uses it. It sounds like the memory of language—like hearing, from far off, the first conversation anyone ever had around the first fire. The syllables went through the den and every wolf present lowered its head, and Osha, at the mouth of the cave, made a sound like a landslide very far away, and outside, impossibly, the blizzard itself seemed to lean in and listen. The words weren't loud. They were *heavy*. They fell into the world the way stones fall into deep water, and the ripples went out and out.

Warmth came up through the den floor—from the mountain itself, I'd swear it. Ice's heartbeat steadied under Ember's palms, found its rhythm, doubled, settled. Color flooded back into her gums. Her tail moved—one weak flick, then another, like a flag remembering what wind was.

The pack's joy when she scrambled up onto her feet was total and unembarrassed. Her mother knocked Ember flat licking his face, and Doss laughed, actually laughed, and for perhaps a quarter of an hour we were simply people and animals celebrating a small life pulled back from the edge, and I let myself forget the bill.

Then Ember sat up, and I watched him pay it.

He blinked, slow, like a man surfacing from deep water. He looked around the den. He looked at me. And his face did something terrible—it went searching.

"My mother," he said. "Vesper. What did my mother look like?"

The den went quiet in a way dens shouldn't be able to go quiet, with twenty animals in it.

"You've never had a mother," I said carefully. "You came out of a king's head. You told me. You *are* the part of him that remembers—"

"I know that." Impatient, frightened. "But I kept one. Out of everything he cut away, I kept the queen's face. It was mine. It was the thing I chose to keep." His hands were shaking now. He pressed them against his own temples as if he could hold the shape of a memory in by force. "Brown hair. There was brown hair. And she—she sang, or she didn't sing, she hummed—Vesper, I had it this morning. I hummed it to Ice yesterday. What color were her eyes?"

"Ember—"

"*What color were her eyes?*"

And the honest answer was: I didn't know. I'd never known. The queen existed in my world as a rumor and a portrait's worth of gossip, and the boy who carried her actual living face—carried it like a candle through ten years of darkness—had just spent it. Spent it the way you spend anything precious: on love, in winter, without hesitation.

He understood before I said anything. Children always understand first; it's adults who need the long way around.

"It's gone," he said. Flat. Testing the word. "I bought a wolf with it."

"You saved a wolf with it," I said, "and you'll never get her face back, and you did it anyway, and I am so angry at you I can't see straight, and you are the bravest person I have ever met, and those are all the same sentence."

He cried then. Not the way children cry—with noise and demand—but the way old men cry, silently, appalled at themselves for grieving something they gave away gladly. The pack gathered around him. Wolves do not understand regret, but they understand grief, and they pressed against him from all sides until he was buried in warm fur, and Ice—the little fool, the beautiful little fool—climbed onto his chest, put her chin on his throat, and stayed there.

Doss found me at the back of the den later, staring at the fire.

"There has to be a way to give it back," he said quietly. "When this Crown comes off him. All the pieces go home, right? He said so. The memories disperse back to—"

"And then what's left of him?" I didn't mean to snap. I did it anyway. "He's not a jar someone spilled, Doss. Whatever goes back to Aldric, it leaves *him*. Everything he spends makes him smaller, and he keeps spending, because nobody taught him—he was never a person long enough for anybody to teach him—that you're allowed to keep something for yourself."

Doss was quiet a while. The fire popped. Somewhere in the den, a wolf dreamed and twitched.

"He learned it from somebody," Doss said finally. "This past month. Somebody who gives and gives and calls it staying invisible." He had the decency not to look at me when he said it. "Maybe you ought to teach him the other thing before you teach him the old tongue."

I hated him thoroughly and correctly for a full minute. Then I got up, crossed the den, wedged myself into the pile of wolves beside Ember, and did the only thing I could think of to do.

"Her eyes were grey," I lied gently, into his hair. "Storm-grey, like yours. And she hummed off-key, and the court was too polite to tell her, and the king loved her for it."

"That's not how it was," Ember mumbled.

"No," I said. "But it's how it is now. That one's yours. Spend it wisely."

He slept. I lay awake counting costs, which is the smuggler's prayer, and for the first time in ten years I hated the arithmetic of my own trade—because I had finally met something I wanted to keep whole, and I knew exactly what the road ahead intended to charge for it.

On the eighth day, the storm broke. On the ninth, Rill trotted to the den mouth at dawn, stiff-legged, growling low—and when I followed her gaze down the white slope, I saw why.

There were lights in the valley below us. Too orderly for campfires. Too many for hunters.

Lanterns. Warden lanterns, climbing.
