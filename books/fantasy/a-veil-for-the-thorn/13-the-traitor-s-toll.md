## 13. The Traitor's Toll

I have been lied to by smugglers, cheated by merchants, robbed by soldiers, and threatened by priests of three separate faiths who couldn't agree on which god was doing the threatening. I thought I knew every shape betrayal came in.

Then I learned it comes in fur.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and the mountains hate impatience almost as much as they hate warmth.

* * *

The ninth day of wolf-winter broke clear and cruel, the kind of cold that makes your eyelashes snap when you blink. We were camped in a hollow below Osha's den, nine days snowbound with the pack, and I had learned three things in that time: wolves snore in harmony, cubs have no concept of personal space, and Ember had become something between a guest and a small god to them.

He sat in the snow that morning with the healed cub draped across his lap like a stole, teaching it—there's no other word for it—the old tongue. The cub would chirp back a mangled syllable and Ember would laugh, this bright, startled laugh that didn't belong to anyone's memory but his own. It was the only thing he'd kept whole since healing the animal. Everything else he'd paid out like coins from a purse that never refilled. He'd forgotten his mother's face. He'd forgotten the taste of honey. He'd forgotten, for two terrible hours, my name, and then it had come back to him on its own, which he swore meant something, though he wouldn't say what.

"You're smiling," Doss said beside me. He'd been sharpening the same knife for three days. It was sharp enough to split a sunbeam. He just needed his hands busy while he waited for me to forgive him, and I hadn't, so the knife got sharper.

"I'm planning," I said.

"Planning looks different. Planning has eyebrows."

"Then I'm smiling at a child and a wolf pup in the snow, Doss, because if I don't smile at something I'm going to scream loud enough to start an avalanche."

"Fair arithmetic." He sheathed the knife. "Vesper. About Greyhollow—"

"No."

"You haven't let me finish a single sentence about it."

"Because every version of the sentence ends with you saying you did what you had to do, and I've heard that sermon from men with better churches." I stood and brushed snow off my knees. "You carried supplies. You can keep carrying supplies. That's the whole liturgy."

Osha's shadow fell over us both before he could answer—which may have been deliberate; the bear matriarch had a sense of timing that bordered on theatrical. She lowered her great head to my level, breath steaming, and spoke in the rumbling cadence of her kind.

*The young one is gone.*

*Which one?* I asked, though I already felt the answer forming, cold in my chest, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

*Fennik. My sister's last son.* Her voice carried no anger yet, which frightened me more than anger would have. Anger is a decision. What she had was worse: the flat bewilderment of a mother whose world has stopped adding up. *He went to check the elk trails yesterday. He has not come home.*

*Wolves could have—*

*No wolves took him. His scent goes down the eastern slope and meets a smell I do not like. Iron. Oil. Men.* She looked at me, and in her small dark eyes I saw the question she was too proud to ask directly: *What are your kind worth, speaker?*

I had no good answer. I never do.

"We move today," I said aloud, to the camp, to anyone. "Today, now, packs on. Ilvane found us."

* * *

We climbed.

That's most of what the next six hours were: climbing, in loose order, up a spine of rock the wolves called the Old Man's Ribs, toward the high pass that would put the pack's territory and its teeth between us and whatever Fennik had sold. Osha led with two of her grown sons. The wolf alpha—a scarred female I'd privately named Duchess, because she had the manners of minor royalty—took the rear with four of hers. Maren Ostwick, the scholar, surprised everyone including herself by keeping pace without complaint, clutching her box of banned records to her chest like an infant. Ember rode pick-a-back on my shoulders when the drifts got deep, his fingers laced under my chin, humming fragments of the old tongue that made the snow itself seem to listen.

Doss brought up the middle, carrying more than his share and pretending it weighed nothing, watching our backtrail with the patience of a man who had spent thirty years reading mountain passes the way scholars read manuscripts.

It was Doss who saw them first.

"Contact," he said, very quietly, in the smuggler's cant we used around Ember. "Below. Two points. Maybe three hundred yards and closing slow. They're not hurrying."

"They don't need to hurry," I said. I could feel it through the stone under my boots—the wrongness of shod feet on old rock, and worse, the silence where animal voices should have been. The hollowed hounds. Nothing left inside them to speak to. Walking voids wearing fur. "They know exactly where we're going. There's one pass out of these ribs, and they're betting we'll take it."

"So we don't take it."

"There is no other way down before dark, and after dark the temperature drops hard enough to kill Maren and maybe Ember." I hated saying it. I said it anyway. Strategy is mostly saying things you hate.

Osha rumbled agreement from ahead, grim as a landslide. Then she went still, nostrils working, and turned her head east—and there he was.

Fennik came up the slope below us at a bear's easy amble, and behind him, spaced like chess pieces, came men in grey-and-white, and behind *them*, low shapes moving wrong. No breath-plume of thought. No flicker of want or fear or hunger. Just motion.

*Fennik!* Osha's roar shook ice from the rocks above. *Brother-son! What have you done?*

The young bear stopped. And here is the part I will carry until I die, so I might as well describe it accurately: he looked *confused*. Not guilty. Confused. Like a man waking in a strange bed trying to remember how much he drank. They had bought him with meat—I could taste it in the wind off him, cured pork and dried fish, a winter's feast for a young male who'd never known a hungry year—and they had talked to him, and here was the poison in it: they hadn't lied. Not exactly. A warden had crouched in the snow and told him, in careful, broken beast-speech, that the humans with his aunt were bringing ruin down on every creature in the Thornfell. That the king's law would burn dens and salt streams. That helping us stop them was *protection*. Mercy, Ilvane's regime called it. A leak, sealed with kindness instead of cruelty.

Betrayal doesn't need malice. It needs a story simple enough to swallow and meat to wash it down.

*They said—* Fennik began, and Osha hit him.

Not to kill. To teach. One blow of her forepaw that sent him tumbling twenty feet downslope, and the sound she made wasn't a roar, it was grief with volume. The wardens used the noise—used the *moment*—and the hollowed hounds broke cover at a dead run.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I've decided that's a mercy. Fast things leave shallow graves.

The pass narrowed fifty yards above us into a throat of stone—the Old Man's Gullet, Duchess called it—with a sheer drop on the left and a wall on the right. Beyond it, the high plateau and safety. Behind it, us. Anyone who has ever read a map or a battle knows what comes next. Someone stands in the throat.

Doss was moving before I finished understanding the geometry.

"Take the boy up," he said. Not loudly. Almost gently. He unslung the supply pack, dropped everything but two coils of rope, a hatchet, and the long-barreled smuggler's arquebus he'd cleaned every night by firelight and told us was for hunting. "There's a scree shelf halfway up the right wall. I pull it down behind me, nothing bigger than a goat gets through for a day at least."

"Doss—"

"Vesper Winterbourne." For the first time since Greyhollow burned, he looked me full in the face, and there was no smuggler's grin anywhere in it. "I sold a child to save my routes. You burned the routes. The town burned anyway. I have been carrying that arithmetic up this mountain for weeks and it does not balance and it never will." He racked the arquebus's lever. "Let me carry something that weighs less."

Behind him, the hounds came on, silent as falling snow, and the wardens behind *them* raised their hooked polearms, and there was no time for anything true, so I said the false thing, the kind thing:

"You come down that scree yourself when it's done. That's the job. Carrying supplies. You're hired."

His grin came back—one flash of it, crooked, the old Doss, the Doss who'd taught me which passes froze first and which men froze faster. "Worst employer I ever had," he said, and turned uphill into the throat of stone.

We ran. Osha drove us, literally drove us, shoulder and growl, Maren sobbing with effort, Duchess's wolves flowing past us like water finding its level. At the mouth of the Gullet I looked back once—I want it recorded that I looked back once, and that looking cost us nothing but nearly cost me everything, because what I saw I have not managed to unsee since.

Doss had pulled half the shelf already. Rock thundered down, plugging the lower throat, and the first two hounds went through it like it wasn't there—because to them it barely was, hollow things don't tire, don't fear, don't calculate odds—and Doss stood on the last ledge with the arquebus braced on a boulder, and he shot the lead hound through whatever passed for its heart, and reloaded, hands steady, while the second closed the distance, and the third, and the warden behind them shouting commands in that flat administrative voice they use for killing, and Doss fired again, and missed, or hit, it doesn't matter—

The scree went.

All of it, the whole shelf, the charge he'd rigged in the night while we slept, because of course he'd rigged it, because Doss Marrow never took a job he hadn't already priced his own exit into. Half the mountainside came down into the Gullet and took the pass, the hounds, and Doss Marrow with it, and the last thing the mountain showed me of him was nothing at all, just white dust rising like breath in the cold, like a man exhaling after holding it too long.

Ember's hand found mine in the dark of the climb that followed. He didn't say anything. He'd run out of memories to spend and knew better than to offer comfort in a currency he didn't have. He just held on, small and warm and alive, which was the entire point, which was the whole freight Doss had died carrying.

* * *

We made the high plateau by dusk and collapsed among the rocks like puppets with cut strings. Osha posted her sons. Duchess set her wolves in a ring. Maren cried quietly into her record box. And I sat apart, because there is a particular solitude reserved for people someone else has just died for, and I have earned it more than once.

Osha came to me. She didn't loom, which from Osha is a courtesy. She folded herself down onto the stone, a mountain arranging itself to sit, and was quiet for a long time.

*Fennik lives,* she said at last. *I did not kill him. My sister's line ends with him either way—dead by my paw or living as a traitor's whelp. I chose the harder mercy. I am telling you so you know I understand what you did with your smuggler. Choosing the harder mercy.* Her breath fogged the air between us. *Now hear me, Vesper Winterbourne. The wardens did not find us by accident, and they did not buy Fennik by chance. They knew a young male, ambitious, untested. They knew meat was the coin. That is not luck. That is study. Someone in grey understands us the way you understand us.*

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the altitude.

*Ilvane,* I said.

*Perhaps. Or perhaps whoever holds Ilvane's leash. Either way—they will try to buy another of mine. Or one of Duchess's. Or yours.* Her eyes held mine, patient and terrible. *Anger is a den with one entrance, speaker. Easy to defend, impossible to leave. So. Are you going to sit in it, or are you going to build something with two ways out?*

I looked west, where the dust had long since settled over the Gullet, and east, where far below us the lights of a warden camp pricked the dark like a constellation that had fallen and refused to admit it, and I did what I have always done instead of screaming, which is arithmetic.

Two hounds confirmed dead. Perhaps five remaining. Wardens: eight, maybe ten, plus whatever Fennik's information had already bought them—our numbers, our route, our destination, which meant they knew we were headed for the capital, which meant they'd send word ahead along roads faster than ours. Which meant the pass we'd fought for was worth less than the time it bought, unless we used the time to stop being predictable.

"They expect a fleeing smuggler," I said aloud, to Osha, to Duchess, to Ember sitting wrapped in the healed cub's fur with his too-old eyes fixed on me. "So from tonight, we stop fleeing. We go down the beast-roads—the deep ones, the ones under their maps—and we stop running *from* the capital and start running *into* it, on our schedule, not theirs."

Ember stirred. When he spoke, it was in the old tongue first, and then, laboriously, in mine, because he'd been rationing even that: "Under the city. The rats remember." A pause. "Vesper. The wardens' leader. The woman."

"What about her?"

"She isn't theirs." His brow creased, a child wrestling with a sum too large for the page. "When I look at her—when I taste her trail in the wind—it splits. Two smells. One is the Crown. One is..." he searched, frustrated, "...one is a door, half open."

Osha rumbled something that in any language translates roughly as *interesting.*

I looked at the fallen constellation of warden fires below us, and at the pass that had cost Doss Marrow everything to close, and understood that we hadn't bought safety. We'd bought a head start on a war, and the first move belonged to whoever was cleverest, not strongest.

"Bear," I said to Osha. "Wolf. Cub. Scholar. We climb down tomorrow. And somewhere between here and the capital, we're going to find out whether that door in Sera Ilvane opens—or whether I have to break it down."

Above us, the peaks stood silent against the stars, keeping their own counsel, listening the way stone listens.

Waiting, I thought, for someone to give them a reason to stop.
