## 14. Descent into the Capital

The rats found us before we found the road.

They came up out of a storm grate in ones and twos, then in dozens, sitting in the slush at the edge of the lamplight with their whiskers working, and I felt them the way you feel a crowd go quiet in a tavern when a fight's about to start. Not hostile. *Interested.*

"They've been following us since the river," Ember said. He said it the way children say things that should terrify adults—flatly, factually, already bored of it. "Since the bridge. They want to know if you're her."

"Her who?"

"The one the old ones talk about. The first speaker's daughter-line." He shrugged, small shoulders under an oversized coat Doss had left behind, coat and all. "I told them no. I told them you're just a smuggler."

"And what did they say to that?"

Ember's mouth twitched. It was the closest thing to a smile he'd managed since the pass where Doss died, and it hurt worse than crying would have. "They laughed."

* * *

We came down off the beast-roads on the fourth night out of the high country, and I want to be honest about what those roads are, because the histories call them something else. The scholars—the few who were allowed to keep their books—call them drainage tunnels, old aqueducts, the bones of some pre-kingdom engineering nobody remembers building. That's a lie of omission so large it deserves its own chapter.

The roads were built by speakers. Before Aldric, before the Devouring War, before anyone thought to put a crown on anything but a head of wheat. The beasts dug them—not rats alone, though rats did most of the fine work—and they were made for feet smaller and braver than mine, which is why we spent four days walking bent nearly double through passages that smelled of cold stone and older air, following a parliament of rodents who chattered directions ahead of us like outriders clearing a path.

Ember talked to them constantly. Not in words, mostly—it wasn't conversation the way I have conversations with a mule or a crow. It was more like watching two people who share a childhood speak entirely in references. He'd make a sound low in his throat and forty rats would change direction as one animal, and I'd feel the shape of what he'd said without catching any of it, the way you can hear music through a wall and know it's a lullaby without knowing the song.

It should have made me jealous. For ten years my gift had been the largest thing about me, the secret that shaped every day of my life, and here was a child of maybe eight winters who wore the same fluency like skin. But it didn't. What it made me feel was *homesick*, which is a stupid thing to feel walking through a hole in the ground beneath the capital of a kingdom that wants me dead, but there it was. Watching him with the rats was like hearing my grandmother's voice again, coming from somewhere I couldn't follow.

On the third day underground, the eldest rat—an enormous grey male with a torn ear and the unhurried manner of a creature who has outlived every reasonable expectation—stopped the whole column and spoke to *me*. Directly. In images, because rats don't waste breath on grammar: the city above us, but wrong. Smaller. Wooden walls where stone now stood. A river running clean. And over all of it, laid across the sky like a signature, a feeling I can only describe as *before*.

"He says the city had another name," Ember translated quietly. "He remembers it. His mother's mother's mother remembered it, all the way back." The boy frowned, working at something. "He says names are how the Crown eats places. If nothing has a name, nothing can be bound."

I looked at the old rat for a long moment. He looked back with the patience of geological time.

"Ask him," I said, "if he'll tell me the name."

Ember asked. The rat made a sound like dry leaves. Ember listened, and his face did something complicated.

"He says yes. But not here. Names are heavy. He says we should carry it down to where the others keep the ashes."

*The others.* I filed that away. "And who keeps the ashes?"

"The ones who remember," Ember said, as if that explained everything, which, infuriatingly, it did.

* * *

We surfaced after midnight on the fifth night, through a grate in the floor of a tannery that had gone out of business so thoroughly that even the smell had given up. Above us, the capital of King Aldric the Just, the Beloved, the Shepherd of the Realm, lay under curfew like a corpse under a sheet.

I'd seen cities. Greyhollow was a town, but I'd run caravans through Corvane and the port at Saltmere, and I knew how cities breathed—how the noise never quite stopped, how the dark was never fully dark. This city didn't breathe. It held its breath. Every window shuttered, every street empty except for pairs of Wardens walking their rounds with those hollowed hounds pacing beside them, and the silence they carried moved ahead of them like a bow wave. You could track them by the absence of cats.

"Vesper." Ember's hand found mine in the dark. Small, cold, gripping hard. "It smells wrong here."

"I know."

"No. Wrong like—" he struggled, and the old tongue came out of him in a rush, syllables that made the rats in the walls go still. Then, back in the trade-tongue: "Like the Thornfell before the first binding. Like somewhere the Crown has already been."

I stood in that dead tannery, in the heart of the enemy's power, and understood that we hadn't come to the Crown's territory. We'd come to its *feeding ground*. Ten years of stories about the king sealed behind his gates, the Wardens keeping order, the borders closed for our protection—and underneath all of it, this. A city that had forgotten how to make noise. A name eaten so long ago even the rats kept it hidden.

"Foreign country," I murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing. Come on. Let's find the people who keep the ashes."

Finding them was the rats' work. The old grey led us through back lanes with the confidence of a landlord, past a Warden patrol pressed flat against a wall while the hollowed hounds' silence slid over us like cold water—I felt for the beasts inside them and found nothing, just that terrible smooth emptiness, and had to breathe through the panic of it—and finally down a stair into a cellar beneath a chandler's shop that hadn't sold a candle in years.

The door at the bottom was oak, iron-banded, and utterly unremarkable, which is exactly why it was remarkable. Everything else in this city performed obedience. This door just wanted to be ignored.

The old rat sat down in front of it and waited.

"You're joking," I said.

He was not joking. After a moment, from inside, came a voice—muffled, irritated, human: "If that's Ostwick's cat again, I swear on every banned volume in this city—"

Then a pause. A longer pause. The sound of someone going very still.

Because the rat had spoken back. One word, in the old tongue, pitched to carry through oak and iron. And whatever it meant, it opened the door.

* * *

The man who opened it was sixty if he was a day, thin as a candle-end, with ink stains to the elbow and the haunted look of a scholar who has spent years being right at volumes no one appreciates. Behind him: shelves. Actual shelves, floor to ceiling, crammed with books, scrolls, loose sheaves tied with string—a library in a cellar, lit by two lamps turned so low they were more memory than light.

He looked at the rat. At Ember. At me. At the rat again.

"Well," he said faintly. "Tobias said you'd come. Tobias has been saying it for eleven years, mind, so forgive me if my faith wavered." He stepped back and held the door wide. "Come in before the street sees you. Names inside are spoken once and never written. House rules."

"Tobias?" I said, looking at the rat.

"My colleague," the scholar said, with complete sincerity. "Senior archivist, actually. He was cataloguing the palace cellars when the purges started and elected to remain. In a reduced capacity." He bowed slightly to the rat, who inclined his head with the graciousness of minor royalty. "Maren Ostwick. Formerly Royal Archivist, Third Chair. Currently a chandler. Of nothing."

"Vesper," I said. "Of nowhere in particular."

"Mm. The mountains disagree, I think." He shut the door, shot three bolts, and turned. His eyes went to Ember and stayed there, and everything casual fell off his face like snow off a roof. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you're *real*. We thought—you have to understand, we thought the fragment was a metaphor. A poetic conceit in the sealed annals. 'The king cut away his conscience and hid it in the world.' Everyone assumed allegory." He crouched slowly, bringing himself level with the boy, hands visibly kept still. "How much do you remember?"

Ember considered him with the gravity he gave everything. "Enough. Less than I had. I paid some for a wolf cub." A pause. "I don't remember my mother's face anymore. It wasn't really my mother."

Something crossed Ostwick's face—grief and fury holding hands. "No," he said gently. "It wasn't. It was Her Majesty's. Queen Isolde. The annals say the Crown took her from him twice—once in body, once in memory. The second theft was larger." He rose, joints cracking, and looked at me. "You've brought him through the passes in winter, past the Wardens, into the capital itself. Either you're the bravest woman alive or you haven't yet understood what you're carrying."

"Both," I said. "Frequently simultaneously."

That startled a laugh out of him—short, rusty, like a hinge unused for years. "Good. The frightened ones break; the brave ones die gloriously and uselessly. The ones who laugh in cellars might actually manage something." He gestured deeper into the stacks. "Sit. There's tea, of a sort. And then, Speaker Winterbourne, you and I are going to talk about what the Crown actually is—because I promise you, whatever the mountains told you, the truth is worse, and stranger, and it ends in that throne room whether we like it or not."

Behind us, Tobias the rat climbed a shelf, settled into a gap between banned volumes like a cat into a windowsill, and began, very quietly, to tell the room the city's true name.

I didn't understand the word. But every book on the shelves seemed to lean toward it.

* * *

Ostwick's tea tasted like bark and regret, and I drank two cups.

While he poured, I took stock of the cellar properly, because ten years of smuggling teaches you to inventory a space before you trust it. Two exits—one behind a false shelf, one through the old coal chute. Food for perhaps six people for a month. Weapons: one crossbow, disassembled, wrapped in oilcloth like something shameful. And paper. Everywhere, paper, in a kingdom where paper with the wrong words on it got villages burned.

"How many of you?" I asked.

"Nine. Was twelve." He said it the way you report weather. "Brother Callum went out for bread in autumn and didn't come back. The Wardens don't burn people in the capital—they've learned better. People simply stop arriving places. The city forgets them efficiently enough on its own." He handed Ember a cup heavily watered and honeyed, having apparently decided the boy's tolerance for regret-tea was limited. "Which brings us to the question I've been circling since the door opened. How much has the mountain told you? Or rather—" he corrected himself, glancing at Ember, "—what has the child been able to carry of it?"

"Tell me your version first," I said. "I want to see where yours has holes. Mine will too. Between them we might get a whole map."

He liked that. I could tell—he had the look of a man who'd spent years talking only to colleagues who already agreed with him, and here was someone treating his knowledge like cargo to be weighed rather than scripture to be received.

"The Devouring War," he began. "Before the kingdom, there were the speaker-councils—the beasts and the first humans governing together, the way your grandmother's songs remember. And then came the Devourers. Not beasts. Something else—hungry in a way hunger doesn't usually work, eating *meaning*, eating names, eating the past out of people's heads. The war against them lasted three generations and ended, according to every public record, with King Aldric's grandfather driving them back into the deep places." He set down his cup. "According to the sealed records—which I copied, before the copying became treason—the war never ended. It was *bargained* to a halt. The Devourers agreed to stop feeding on the realm. In exchange, they would be fed. Regularly. From a single source."

My stomach dropped ahead of my understanding, the way it does on stairs in the dark. "Aldric."

"Aldric. Voluntarily. That's the part the archives make unmistakable—he wasn't conquered, he wasn't deceived, or not entirely. He sat down with the thing his grandfather had fought and he *negotiated*. His memories, his conscience, his capacity to grieve—all of it, portion by portion, to buy the realm peace. The Crown isn't a parasite that took him, Speaker. It's a contract he signed. And the Hollow Wardens—" his voice roughened, "—are the enforcement arm of the feeding schedule. Villages that shelter speakers get burned because speakers can hear the truth of what the king has become. The Crown cannot abide witnesses."

The cellar was very quiet. On the shelf, Tobias had stopped grooming and was watching us with his head cocked, and I realized the old rat already knew all of this. Had probably known it for years, in the way of beasts, and had simply been waiting for the humans to catch up.

"Ember," I said carefully. "Is that how it feels? From the inside?"

The boy stared into his tea. When he answered, his voice was very small and very old at the same time, which is a trick only children and the ancient can manage.

"It feels like being loved," he said. "That's the worst part. The Crown loves him. It takes care of him. It keeps him safe and warm and it tells him he's good, and every time it takes another piece it says *this is so you don't have to hurt anymore*." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It's not a monster. Monsters are easier. It's kindness that won't stop."

I reached over and pulled him against my side, and he let me, stiffly at first and then not.

"So we unbind him," I said to Ostwick. "The mountain councils believe it can be done—the old tongue, spoken in the throne room, through the fragment. That's why we came. That's why any of this happened."

"Yes," Ostwick said. "And that is where I must ruin your evening, because unbinding requires reaching the throne room, and the throne room sits behind nine gates, four hundred guards, and a king who hasn't been seen in public in three years except as a silhouette at a window." He rubbed his eyes. "There is one opening. In nineteen days, the court holds the Feast of the Long Night—the anniversary of the bargain, celebrated as a triumph. Every noble house attends. The kitchens take on temporary staff from the outer districts because no servant of the inner court will work that night—they say the palace *weeps* that night, though officially it's superstition." He looked up. "Temporary staff. With papers. Papers I can forge, because forging documents was the least of my crimes before they took my chair."

I looked at Ember. Ember looked at the rats along the shelves, dozens of them now, silent and attentive, an audience of small grey historians.

"Nineteen days," I said.

"Nineteen days," Ostwick agreed. Then he paused, head tilting, listening to something I couldn't hear—and his face changed.

"Speaker," he said slowly. "Tobias asks—politely—whether you know that you've been followed since the river bridge. Not by us. By something with no smell, that walks on two legs, and that lost you at the tannery but is now asking the street-cats where you went." A beat. "The street-cats are telling it nothing. They're fond of Tobias. But it will find this district by morning regardless, because it doesn't need informants. It needs only patience."

I set down my cup. Outside, far above, the curfew bells rang the third hour, and the sound fell through the city like stones through water.

"Then we'd best decide," I said, "whether we run, or whether we spend nineteen days turning this cellar into the Crown's worst mistake."

From the shelves, in the old tongue, the rats answered as one.

They voted to stay.
