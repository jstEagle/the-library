## 16. Audience Denied

The plan had exactly one good idea in it, which by resistance standards made it a masterpiece.

Maren Ostwick laid it out on her cellar floor with a stolen guest list and a candle stub for a pointer. "The Feast of the First Frost," she said. "Every noble house in the capital under one roof, the king presiding, and—here is the miracle—the kitchens hire extra hands from outside the palace walls. Dishwashers. Scullery maids. Carters. Nobody looks at a dishwasher. Nobody has ever looked at a dishwasher."

"I've been looked at plenty," I said. "Usually while running."

"Then you'll be invisible," she said, with the serene confidence of a woman who had spent thirty years in archives and believed all problems were filing errors.

The plan was this: Maren's cell had forged service papers for three of us. I would go in as a carter delivering winter wine—carter being the one disguise that required me to know how to handle mules, which I did, and to be surly, which came naturally. Ember would stay behind with the rats, who had volunteered to guard him in the way rats volunteer for anything: by showing up uninvited and eating things. Once inside, I would mark the servants' stair to the royal wing, confirm where Aldric kept his private chambers, and slip out before the toast. The actual confrontation of the king would come later, with more numbers and fewer chandeliers.

Reconnaissance. That was all tonight was supposed to be. I want that written down somewhere, carved into something durable: *the plan was reconnaissance.*

* * *

The capital in winter smells like coal smoke and ambition. The palace squats above the city like a fat spider that has decided the web is beneath its dignity, all pale stone and black iron, banners hanging stiff as frozen laundry. The Thorn Crown's sigil—a circlet of briars, elegant if you didn't look too long at how the thorns curved inward—flew over every gate.

I hate cities. Everything in them talks and none of it says anything useful. Horses complain about their shoes, dogs recite the same four grievances on loop, pigeons are pigeons. But the rats—Maren's rats, the old-blood ones who remembered the city before it had a name—they'd taught me the underways, and I came up out of a storm drain two streets from the service gate feeling like I'd cheated at a game I hadn't agreed to play.

The forged papers worked. Of course they worked; Maren had forged them herself, and the woman could forge a confession from a saint. A bored gate officer glanced at my face for less time than he spent glancing at my mule, waved me through, and went back to warming his hands over a brazier.

Inside, the palace was worse than outside. Warmth everywhere, wasted warmth, fires burning in rooms nobody sat in. Servants flowed through the corridors in a practiced current, and I let myself be carried along in it, head down, hands red from the cold, exactly as forgettable as my papers said I was.

I found the servants' stair in twenty minutes. Marked it. Found the wine cellar, delivered my casks, endured a cellar-master who talked about sediment the way other men talk about religion. All of it smooth. All of it easy.

Which should have been my first warning. Nothing in my life has ever gone smoothly except directly into disaster.

I was in the east gallery—a long corridor hung with portraits of dead kings, doing my best impression of a carter who was lost and knew it—when the doors at both ends opened at once, and six Hollow Wardens walked in.

Not toward me. Toward each other, meeting in the middle, forming a loose line across the gallery with the casual geometry of people who had done this a thousand times. And at their center, gray-cloaked and still as a held breath, stood Warden-Captain Sera Ilvane.

I had never seen her face. I had heard her name whispered through half the north like a curse you say quietly so it doesn't hear you. I knew her work: Greyhollow burned, the pass villages burned, the ledger of speakers sealed with wax and ash. I knew her hounds—hollowed beasts with nothing behind the eyes, the one kind of animal I couldn't reach because there was no one left home to talk to.

She looked at me the way an auditor looks at a column of numbers that doesn't add up.

"Carter," she said.

"Captain." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Somewhere under my ribs my heart was trying to dig its way out through my spine.

"You're not on the feast roster."

"Deliveries run late. Roads run bad." I shrugged. "Winter doesn't care whose party it ruins."

Something flickered across her face—not amusement, not quite. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow professional executing competently. Then she took one step forward, and the wardens behind her shifted, and the whole gallery got very small.

"Turn out your pockets."

And here is the thing about carrying contraband through a smuggler's life: you develop habits. I had a knife in my boot, a lockpick set sewn into my sleeve, and—because I am apparently incapable of learning—in my coat pocket, folded small, a scrap of parchment with the servants' stair marked on it in my own hand.

She found the parchment first.

I watched her unfold it. Watched her eyes move across the little inked line, the tiny X. Watched her understand what it was, and what it meant, and what I was.

The gallery went quiet enough that I could hear the feast below us, distant and golden, laughter filtered through floors of stone.

"Well," Ilvane said.

* * *

Here is what I expected: irons, a cell, questions asked with tools. Here is what happened instead.

She dismissed her wardens.

"Continue to the west posts," she told them. "I'll rejoin after inspection." They went, boots ringing on stone, obedient as their hounds—and I noticed, watching them go, that they obeyed her the way hollowed animals obey. Perfectly. Without a single word passing between them that wasn't an order. Even her own soldiers had been made quiet.

When the doors closed she turned back to me, and up close I saw what ten years of hunting does to a person's face when the person is still, against all odds, awake inside it. There were lines around her eyes that hadn't come from squinting at ledgers.

"The servants' stair to the royal wing," she said. "You were going to him."

I said nothing. Silence costs nothing and buys time.

"You're the speaker." Not a question. "Greyhollow. The woman who moves contraband through Thornfell. We had a file. It was thin. Animals behaving strangely around a carter who was never quite where the manifests said." She held up the parchment between two fingers. "This is thicker than your file."

"If you already know," I said, "why are we having this conversation instead of the arrest?"

"Because I've read the sealed archives." She said it flatly, like reporting weather. "Because three weeks ago I interrogated a smuggler who told me a story about an abbey in the high passes, and I cross-referenced it, and the story was true, and it was classified by an authority that does not appear in any chain of command I have served for eleven years." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Because last month I burned a village, and when I gave the order I felt nothing, and when I checked whether I should have felt something, I couldn't find the place where the feeling used to live."

Well. That wasn't in any file either.

"You're telling me this why?" I asked. "Softening me up?"

"I'm telling you because you're going to tell me the truth, and then I'm going to decide what to do with you." She folded the parchment and put it away—in her own pocket, not as evidence. "You have until the second course ends. The king toasts at the third. After that, this corridor fills with guards again."

"And if I don't feel like talking?"

"Then I do my duty," she said, "and we both find out which of us regrets it more."

There was a bench along the wall beneath a portrait of some dead king with a weak chin. She nodded at it. I sat, mostly because my knees had opinions. She remained standing, hands clasped behind her back, a soldier giving audience to the enemy—which, I was beginning to suspect, was exactly how she saw it, though not in the direction I'd assumed.

"Ask," I said.

"What is the child?"

The question landed harder than an accusation would have. I looked up at her. "You know about Ember."

"I know a fragment of the king's memory walks around in a boy's body. I know my orders regarding him changed six months ago from *observe* to *recover*, and I know the order didn't come from the Lord Marshal, or the Chancellor, or the King's Own Secretary, because I checked all three seals and none of them matched." A pause. "So. What is he?"

"He's a piece of Aldric," I said. "The piece that could say no."

I told her. All of it—or as much as I understood of it, which after the abbey murals and Maren's ashes was more than most living people could claim. The Devouring War, the land eating itself, the first speakers crowning a king and binding a guardian to him so the realm would have a shepherd instead of a graveyard. The bargain meant to be temporary. The crown that forgot it was a lease and started acting like a deed. Ten years ago, Aldric—whether in weakness or terror or both—let it cut his conscience out of him and bury the piece somewhere it could grow legs and run.

"That's why the Wardens burn speakers," I said. "It's not heresy. It's surgery. Every beast-speaker is a thread the Crown can't control, a voice it can't own. It's been cutting them out of the realm the same way it cut itself out of the king."

Ilvane was very still. When she finally spoke, her voice had a crack in it she didn't try to hide. "I burned a village in March. Harrowmere. Forty-one souls. The report listed seven speakers recovered for questioning." She stopped. Started again. "There were no seven speakers. There was a girl who sang to goats. I signed the report anyway."

"Yes," I said. "That's what it does. It doesn't make you evil, Captain. It makes you efficient. Evil takes effort. Efficiency just takes paperwork."

Something almost like a laugh escaped her, and died immediately, and I think the dying hurt her more than the laugh would have.

"The abbey," she said. "The murals. The crown was meant to end."

"Meant to be handed back. Meant to be *finished*." I stood up, slowly, keeping my hands visible, an old smuggler's courtesy. "Your masters' orders don't come from any chain of command because the thing giving them isn't in the command. It's in the throne. It's been wearing your king for ten years, Captain, and everything you've done in his name—every village, every ledger, every hound you hollowed—you did for a weed wearing a crown."

The silence stretched long enough for the feast below to reach some crescendo, applause bleeding faintly up through the stones.

Then Sera Ilvane reached into her cloak, and every muscle in my body prepared to die in a hallway, and she drew out—not a weapon—a ring of keys. Iron and brass, heavy, worn bright at the edges from use.

"Warden-Captain's keys," she said. "They open the royal wing. They open the archive vaults. They open the cells under the east tower, where we keep the ones we question." She held them out. "Take them."

I didn't move. "Why?"

"Because I have spent eleven years believing I was holding a wall together," she said, "and you've just shown me I was the wall." Her hand didn't waver. "I want it back, Speaker. All of it. The king, the realm, whatever's left of my own name when this is done. And I can't get it from inside the uniform, because the uniform doesn't answer to me anymore—it answers to *that*." Her jaw tightened. "So take the keys. And take this."

From her breast pocket she drew a folded document, sealed with the Warden crest, and pressed it into my hands alongside the keys. "My confession. Signed, dated, witnessed by no one but me. Every order I executed, every village, every name I could recover. If your scholars are building a case, build it with mine in it. I won't hide behind orders. Orders were the problem."

Below us, faintly, a herald's voice rose—the second course ending, right on schedule.

"You understand," I said, tucking keys and confession away, "that if this goes wrong, they'll call you a traitor. Not a defector. A traitor. There's no coming back from this door."

"I burned Harrowmere," Sera Ilvane said simply. "I stopped having a door to come back through in March. I'm only now finding the one that leads out."

She turned toward the far doors, then paused.

"The third-course toast," she said. "The king will speak. He always speaks the same words—I've heard them five years running. *'To the realm, whole and guarded.'*" Her mouth twisted. "I thought it was patriotism. Now I think it's the leash talking to itself." She looked back at me once, gray eyes level. "West post, second landing, there's a warden change at the top of the hour. Ninety seconds, no more. Use it."

"Captain." I stopped her with the only honest thing I had. "Harrowmere. The girl who sang to goats. Give me her name. If we win—if the mountains take up the watch—she gets remembered. Properly. By everyone."

For the first time since the doors had opened, Sera Ilvane's composure broke—not into tears, but into something rawer, a soldier given permission at last to grieve on duty.

"Lirra," she said. "Lirra Ostvale. She was nine." And she straightened her cloak, became Warden-Captain again from the boots up, and walked out through the doors into the golden noise of the feast, carrying her horror like a standard.

* * *

I went down the servants' stair at the top of the hour, through ninety seconds of empty corridor, past a sleeping guard dog who woke, looked at me, and—bless its unhollowed heart—decided it had seen nothing. At the bottom of the stair, in the dark where the rat-road began, a familiar shape detached itself from the shadows: small, solemn, eyes catching the lamplight like coals.

Ember. Who was supposed to be safe in Maren's cellar with the rats standing guard, which tells you exactly how much rats can be trusted with guard duty.

He looked up at me, and his face did something complicated—fear and relief and a strange, old sorrow all fighting for space on nine years of features.

"You met her," he said. Not asking. He'd felt it somehow, the way he felt storms before they arrived. "The wolf-woman with the empty dogs."

"She's not with them anymore," I said. "She's with us."

Ember considered this with the gravity of someone deciding the fate of kingdoms, which, technically, he was.

"She cried inside," he said at last. "The whole time she talked to you. Wolves cry like that too, when they've bitten the wrong thing." He slipped his small cold hand into mine. "Come home now? The rats ate the emergency cheese."

We went down into the dark together, a smuggler with a captain's keys in her pocket and a dead girl's name in her throat, while overhead the false king raised his glass to a realm whole and guarded—and somewhere beneath the throne, patient as roots, the Thorn Crown felt its missing pieces moving closer, and began, very quietly, to grow.
