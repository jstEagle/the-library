## 17. The Thing Wearing the Crown

*Vesper*

There is a particular smell to a palace at night, and it is not wealth. Wealth smells of beeswax and orange peel and someone else's labor. A palace at night smells of fear that has been stored so long it has gone to vinegar — centuries of servants holding their breath in doorways, all of it soaked into the stone like smoke into a mourner's coat.

The rats had not lied about the smell. The rats had also not lied about the gap beneath the wine cellar's east wall, the one wide enough for a woman with regrets and a child with no business being anywhere near this place. They'd led us through three miles of under-city dark, chattering the whole way in their quick gray voices — *the city was ours before it had a name, tall-thing, we remember when your kind slept in mud and owed us rent* — and I had paid them in dried fish and flattery, which is the traditional currency of my people.

Then up. Through a disused cistern, a servant stair that Maren's scholars swore hadn't been walked since Aldric's grandmother held court, and finally into the belly of the palace itself: a corridor lined with portraits of dead Winterkings, each one painted with the same expression of a man who has just smelled something bad and been told it is tradition.

Ember pressed close against my side. In the tunnels he'd been almost bright — the old tongue humming out of him in little sparks, making the rats laugh, making even me forget for whole minutes what we were walking toward. Now his hand in mine was cold as a latch.

"He's awake," Ember whispered. "He's always awake now."

I knew who *he* was. I wished I didn't.

"Then we stay ahead of him," I said, which was the sort of thing a confident person says, and neither of us believed it, and that made it almost honest.

Ilvane's keys had gotten us the first three doors. Ilvane's confession — written in her own square, unflinching hand, naming every village, every fire — sat folded against my heart, heavier than any coin I'd ever carried. And Ilvane's map of the feast-night rotations was the reason two smugglers' ghosts could stand in a portrait gallery forty feet from the throne room while the whole court gorged itself two floors down on venison and lies.

All we had to do was cross the gallery, slip the antechamber, and reach the small council stair that let out beside the throne room's private door, where tomorrow morning — not tonight, Maren had insisted, *tomorrow*, when the Wardens changed shift — we would do whatever it is you do to unmake a king's cage.

Tonight was only reconnaissance. Walk soft. Look hard. Leave.

I have never once in my life completed a job that simple.

* * *

*Ember*

The palace is full of the King.

That is the wrong way to say it. There are no words in the trade-tongue for the right way, so I will try the old ones, the ones Grandmother-Vesper's songs used, the ones that taste of snow on stone.

*Kaelun veth. Kaelun veth imar.* The root remembers. The root remembers even cut away from the tree.

I am the root remembering.

When we came up out of the dark I felt him the way you feel a fire in the next room — not heat yet, but the light of it moving on the walls. He is everywhere here. Ten years of him soaked down through the floors like the vinegar-fear Vesper talks about. Every portrait of him in this hallway is wrong by a little more than the last one. The painters kept painting his face and the Crown kept moving underneath it, patient as ivy, and eventually the painters stopped being invited.

Vesper thinks we are going to look at the throne room tonight. She is wrong and I have not told her because telling her would make it true sooner.

The Crown knows I'm in the building.

It has known since the cistern. It has been pulling on me ever since, gently, the way you pull a thread you don't want to snap — *come home, come home, little piece, we were a whole thing once* — and I have been pretending not to feel it, which is the family skill, apparently. Vesper hid her voice for ten years. I have been hiding my screaming for six months. We are very alike. Neither of us has said so.

"Ember." Her hand tightens on mine. "You went somewhere."

"I'm here," I say, and it is mostly true.

We move along the gallery between the dead kings. My feet know how to be quiet here. That frightens me more than anything else tonight — my feet have never been in this palace, and they walk its stones like a boy walking home.

At the end of the gallery there is a window, and below the window there is a courtyard, and crossing the courtyard below us, alone, in the blue hour between feast and sleep, comes the King.

* * *

*Vesper*

I saw him from the gallery window, and I will spend the rest of my life unable to unsee it, so let me do it justice.

King Aldric Winterborne — the man whose face was on every wall of every town we'd burned through, stamped on warrants, carved over doors, printed on the lists of the condemned — walked across his own courtyard like a man crossing ice he did not trust. Not slowly. That I could have borne. Slow is grief or age or caution. This was worse: he moved in segments. Left foot, then a pause where a decision should have been, then right foot, as if something were consulting him about each step and finding his opinion interesting but not binding.

His shoulders were magnificent. His coat was worth more than Greyhollow. None of it was his.

And then he passed beneath a lantern, and he looked up — not at me, not at anything, just up, the way a plant leans — and his eyes flickered.

I want to tell you they glowed green, because glow is dramatic and manageable and belongs in stories. They didn't glow. They flickered, guttering, the way a candle does when something green and living has been pushed too close to the flame and hasn't caught yet but intends to. For half a heartbeat the man behind them surfaced — a face mid-forties, ordinary tiredness, a crease between the brows like a man doing sums he doesn't like — and he *saw* us. Or almost saw us. His mouth opened.

The flicker closed over him like water over a dropped coin.

"Vesper." Ember's voice came out thin. "He tried to say something. I heard it. Down the bond. He tried to—" The boy swallowed. "*Help.*"

Every instinct I own told me to run. I have spent ten years running from things smaller than a king, and I am still here, which proves the instinct works. But Ember had gone rigid against the windowsill, and when I looked down at him I saw his eyes doing it too — flickering, green-edged, answering — and I understood with a clarity I would pay a great deal to have missed:

The Crown wasn't looking for us.

It was reaching. And my boy was the hand-shaped hole it had been reaching toward for ten years.

* * *

*Ember*

Here is what nobody wrote in the murals, not even in the Abbey, not even Maren's box of ashes:

The Crown loved him.

That is the horror of it. I have touched its attention now, felt it turn toward me across the courtyard, and it is not hunger the way wolves are hungry, clean and seasonal. It is not cruelty the way the Wardens are cruel, which is really just fear wearing boots. It is *love*. It was made from love — some first speaker's desperate love for a war-torn world, poured into thorn and vow and laid on a young king's brow: *let nothing harm you, let nothing sway you, let you stand forever.*

Forever was the mistake. Love without an ending becomes a fence. Love without an ending becomes a cage, and then a crown, and then a thing that walks your body across courtyards while you do sums behind your own eyes trying to buy back one word.

He wanted to say *help*. I felt the shape of it rise in him and get pressed flat, gentle as snowfall. And the Crown soothed him afterward. I felt that too. It stroked his mind the way you stroke a frightened animal — *hush, hush, I have you, nothing will hurt you, nothing will change you, nothing will touch you, ever again* — and it meant every word, and that is why we cannot reason with it. You cannot argue with love. You can only outgrow it or break it.

It turned to me fully then, across the night air, and it said — not in words, in certainty —

*Come home.*

And every cold thing I have felt since the day I was cut apart rose in me at once, because home was a throne room, home was being a piece, home was wholeness the way a ledger is whole, everything entered and nothing alive.

"No," I said. Out loud. To a window.

Vesper's head snapped around. "Ember—"

"It asked me to come home," I told her, and my voice cracked down the middle like river ice, "and I said no," and then, because I am apparently my mother's son whoever my mother was, I added, "politely."

Something down in the courtyard went very quiet.

* * *

*Vesper*

There is a sound a palace makes when it notices you. Not alarms — we weren't there yet. Just a stillness, spreading outward from the courtyard like frost across glass: dogs ceasing to pace, sentries forgetting the sentence they were speaking, every hollowed beast in the building lifting its empty head at once and pointing it at my boy.

The hollowed ones. Ilvane's voiceless tools. Even from the gallery I felt them turning, and my gift reached for them out of pure habit and found nothing — the familiar sickening blank, mouths with the speaker scooped out. But they moved now with purpose they'd never shown, and their purpose wore Ember's name.

"Change of plan," I said. "Reconnaissance is over."

"The throne room—"

"is tomorrow, like Maren said, and only if we're stupid enough to come back, which we're going to discuss never." I took his face in my hands. His eyes were still green at the rims. "Look at me. Whatever it's saying to you—"

"It's not saying anything anymore." His chin came up, and there — there was the boy who'd healed a wolf-cub at the cost of his own memories, stubborn as a mountain goat and twice as sure-footed. "It stopped asking and started counting."

"Counting what?"

"How long until it stops needing to ask."

Down in the courtyard, the King raised his arm, segment by segment, and pointed at our window.

I grabbed Ember and ran.

* * *

*Ember*

We ran the gallery of dead kings with their wrong-painted faces, and behind us came a sound I will carry however long I stay small: the King's voice, used by someone else, calling down corridors in perfect courtly tones — *Wardens to the east wing, guests in the house* — polite as a dinner bell.

He fights it. I know that now the way you know a thing in your bones. Every order the Crown gives in his voice, he is somewhere inside doing sums, keeping count, keeping himself, ten years of *not yet, not yet, hold, hold* — and nobody knows. Ilvane didn't know. The scholars only guessed. The whole kingdom burns villages for the crime of hearing what I hear, and the prisoner in the palace has been screaming the whole time in a language only beasts and smuggled children speak.

Vesper shoves open a service door and we drop into darkness, down stairs older than the kings, toward the rat-roads and the cistern and Maren's cellar full of saved ash.

Halfway down she stops, listening, and I feel it too — the pulling has changed. All night it has been a thread, gentle, patient. Now it is a hook. The Crown has stopped courting me.

"Ember." Vesper's voice is very steady, which is how I know she's terrified; I've learned her arithmetic. "Talk to me."

"It's done waiting," I say. "Tomorrow morning, when we come for the throne room — it knows. It'll be ready. It wants us to come." I swallow. "It wants *me* to come. And Vesper — if I get close enough, I don't know that I can say no again. When it loves you, you feel it. How whole you'd be."

She is quiet a moment. Below us, faintly, the rats are already chattering our path clear — *down, tall-thing, down, quick-thing, the gray roads remember* — and torchlight begins to bloom on the stairs above.

Then Vesper takes my hand the way she did the first night, in the blue hour, at the cabin door, when taking me in was already costing her something, and she says:

"Then we don't get close. We get close *first*. Tomorrow we stop sneaking past the thing wearing the king—" and I watch the last of her fear harden into something with edges, "—and we go take its throne room away from it."

Above us, the first Warden boots hit the top of the stair.

*Down,* sing the rats. *Quick-thing. Tall-thing. The mountains are listening.*

We go.
