## 18. Throne of Thorns

The doors of the throne room were older than the kingdom and knew it.

They had been grown, not built—blackthorn wood fused into a single seamless sheet, and when Ilvane pressed her stolen keys into the lock, I felt the tree remember being alive. It remembered rain. It remembered the hands that had bent its branches instead of cutting them, and it opened for us with a groan that sounded, in the old tongue, like *finally*.

"Chatty door," Ember whispered. His hand was cold in mine. Ten years I'd spent teaching myself to speak less, and here was a child who hadn't spoken aloud in his whole small life except in a language dead kingdoms dreamed in, telling jokes about architecture.

"Doors have opinions," I said. "Most of them are about who knocks."

Ilvane went first, sword drawn but lowered, because she was still learning what defection looked like from the inside and kept checking her posture in the dark glass of the windows. Behind us came Maren with her box of ashes and her scholar's stubbornness, and behind her, padding silent as an apology, two of Osha's wolves—who had followed us down through the beast-roads despite every instinct screaming at them to stay in their mountains, because Ember had asked, and nobody had ever taught them how to refuse him.

The throne room was vast and frozen. Not metaphorically. The great hearths along either wall burned with orange light that gave no heat, and frost furred the banners of Aldric's line where they hung from the clerestory, each one stiff as a shroud. Our breath plumed. Somewhere high above, ice ticked against glass like a visitor who would not be sent away.

And at the far end, on a throne that had once been plain oak and was now something else—something with thorns running through its grain like veins through a wrist—sat the King of Thornfell.

I had expected a monster. I had prepared myself for a monster. What I saw was worse: a tired man in his middle years, silver at the temples, hands folded neatly on his knees, sitting very still in the way of someone who has practiced stillness so long it has replaced everything else. His eyes were open. They were brown, ordinary, human—and threaded through them, slow as sap, moved something green.

He wasn't asleep. He wasn't awake. He was *occupied*.

"He can see us," Ember said suddenly, and his grip crushed my fingers. "Part of him. The part that's me."

"Then let's give him something worth seeing," I said, and hated how steady my voice sounded when my heart was trying to climb out through my ribs.

We crossed the floor. Our footsteps echoed off stone that swallowed sound the way the Crown swallowed kings. Halfway down the hall, the braziers guttered, all at once, as if the room inhaled—and the temperature dropped so sharply that frost bloomed across my eyelashes, and I heard it.

Not words. Not yet. A presence, vast and vegetative and patient, unfolding through the room like roots through soil, filling every crack, and when it finally spoke, it did not use the air. It used my own bones.

***You brought my piece back to me,*** it said. ***Little speaker. Clever, stubborn little speaker. You have carried what is mine across my own mountains, past my own wardens, into my own house. I will grant that you do everything the hard way.***

"It's a family trait," I said. "The hard way. We're famous for it."

Ember made a small shocked sound that might have been a laugh. Good. Let the boy laugh while he could.

Ilvane stepped forward, and I'll give her this: she didn't flinch. She planted herself between the throne and the child she'd hunted across half a kingdom, dropped to one knee in the formal posture of a warden's oath, and then—deliberately, watching the thing wear the king's face—rose back to her feet without completing it.

"Sera Ilvane," she said, voice carrying in the frozen air, "Captain of the Hollow Wardens, testifies before this court that the orders she has executed for ten years originate from no lawful authority. That she has burned villages on the command of a crown, not a king. That the man on that throne—" her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it, "—is not present."

***She is present,*** the Crown said, amused. ***She is more present than she has ever been. Shall I show her? Shall I show her ten years of mercy, efficiently applied?***

"You showed us a ledger," Ilvane said. "I read the ledger. I know exactly what your mercy costs per village."

For a moment—just a moment—the green in the king's eyes flickered, and something human surfaced in them, drowning. His mouth opened. What came out was a whisper, cracked and desperate:

"*Run.*"

Then the thorns on the armrests flexed, black and glistening, and his face smoothed back into terrible calm.

My grandmother's lullabies had a verse for this. Of course they did. My grandmother had a verse for everything—she'd sung the whole forbidden liturgy into me before I could walk, disguised as nonsense rhymes about goats and weather, and I'd spent ten years pretending I couldn't remember any of it. But I remembered. Standing on the frozen flagstones of a usurped throne room, with frost climbing my boots and a puppet king watching me with stolen eyes, I finally understood why she'd taught me the unbinding first, before anything else. Before the beast-speech. Before the councils.

Because someone in our line was always going to end up here.

I set Ember behind me. I put my hand flat against the frozen stone, where the oldest roots of the thing ran deepest, and I began to speak.

The old tongue doesn't work like ours does. There's no subject and object, no *I* and *it*—everything is relationship, everything is *between*, and to unbind something you have to name every thread of the binding and call each one by what it truly is. So I named them. The bargain-thread, spun from war's ending. The fear-thread, spun from a young king's desperation. The hunger-thread, spun from something older than both, that had worn the shape of a solution until the solution wore the throne. And I felt them, under my words—the way you feel a knot loosen under your fingers—and the frost on the banners began to weep, and the king's body shuddered like a house settling—

And the Crown spoke to me.

Not through my bones this time. Through my heart. Which is a much softer place to live, if you're a parasite with good manners.

***What did you lose, Vesper Winterbourne?*** it asked, gentle as snowfall. ***Shall I count? Your mother, who sang off-key. Your father, who never came back from the pass. Your grandmother, who died alone teaching a child secrets instead of holding her hand. Shall I return them?***

The hall filled. Not with ghosts—with *mornings*. I smelled my mother's bread. I heard my father's boots on the cabin porch, the particular double-scuff of his left heel. My grandmother's lap was warm beneath me and her lullabies made sense now, all of them, every verse explained, and there was a place at a table where I fit, and voices that said my name the way you say something you're glad to have found—

*Belonging,* the Crown murmured. ***Voice. Family. You have been fluent in everything except trust for ten years, girl. I can teach you the last language. I only ask one small thing. Leave the threads knotted. Walk away. Take the boy. Raise him. Be loved. Only leave me my throne.***

My hand lifted from the stone.

I want to tell you I fought it. I want to tell you some heroic part of me raged against the lie. What actually happened was quieter and much more shameful: my hand simply lifted, because grief is patient and I was so tired, and the vision was *warm*, and I had been cold for a decade.

It was Ember who broke it.

He stepped out from behind me—I felt him go the way you feel a blanket slide off in the night—and walked forward on those absurdly small legs into the center of the frozen hall, until he stood directly before the throne, looking up at the man whose missing piece he was.

"No," he said. In the old tongue, it was a word with enormous weight: a refusal that also meant *I see you*, and also meant *enough.*

Then he turned to look back at me, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling, and I understood with a horror beyond anything the Crown had shown me what he was about to do.

***If a piece must fill the space,*** Ember said to the Crown, in the formal cadences of the first speakers' covenant, ***then let the piece be willing. Let it be me. I was cut from him; I am the correct size. Take me back and let the old man go free.***

"No," I said. It came out very quietly. Then again, louder, tearing free of the warmth and the visions and ten years of practiced silence: "*No.*"

***He offers himself freely,*** the Crown said, and there was something almost tender in it, which is the most frightening register a predator owns. ***Freely given binds deepest. It is the same bargain, improved. One child against a kingdom's peace. Even your scholars' records say the first crown was made this way.***

"Yes," I said. "And look how that turned out."

I crossed the frozen floor and knelt in front of Ember, and took his face in my two cold hands, and looked at him—at the boy I'd pulled half-dead from a blizzard, who'd mapped throne rooms he'd never seen and healed wolf cubs at the cost of his own memories, who'd carried a king's conscience around in a body too small for it and had never once, in all that winter, used it as an excuse to be cruel.

"Listen to me," I said. "You are not a piece. Do you hear me? You are not a piece of anyone. Whatever you were cut from—you grew. You became *Ember*. And Ember doesn't get spent. Ember doesn't get *used* to patch up bargains other people made before he was born." My voice broke and I let it. "You wanted to help? Then help me do this *together*. Not as payment. As partnership."

He searched my face. Behind him the Crown's presence coiled, patient, certain, and somewhere inside the king's borrowed body a drowned man watched us through a crack in his own eyes.

"Together," Ember said. In the old tongue the word meant something larger: *two who speak as one throat.* "How?"

"You don't stand aside and let me save you," I said. "And I don't stand over you and speak for you. We speak *through* each other. My voice. Your truth. Both throats, one breath."

I put my hand on his chest, over his heart, and I took the deepest breath of my life, and this time when I spoke the unbinding, I didn't speak it *to* the binding or *at* it.

I spoke it through him.

His truth came up through my mouth like water finding an old channel: the memory of being vast, of being loved, of being many voices singing in one mind; the terror of the knife of love that cut him free; the long small years of being lost and cold and mute among people who feared what they couldn't hear. And woven through my words, his wordless knowing answered every thread I named—not *this thread is fear,* but *I remember whose fear it was;* not *this thread is hunger,* but *I remember when it was only appetite, before it learned to make deals.*

The frost screamed off the walls. The banners sagged, shedding decades of ice. The thorns on the throne writhed like things set alight, and the Crown rose—it rose out of the king like smoke leaving a ruined house, vast and black-green and flowering into shapes meant to awe, offering, pleading, promising in a hundred voices, my mother's voice among them, and I held Ember's heartbeat under my palm and kept speaking, and he kept speaking, and we were one throat, one breath, and the old tongue does not recognize a lie told in partnership—

The Crown shattered.

Not like glass. Like memory. It came apart into everything it had ever taken, a blizzard of moments and mornings and borrowed years, and the last thing it said, in a voice like roots growing, was not a threat at all.

***At last,*** it said. ***Someone who would rather share a burden than trade for a blessing. Perhaps the war is truly over.***

And then it was gone—out through the high windows, into the ground, into the mountains waiting beyond the walls, dispersing like a debt finally paid—and the man on the throne collapsed forward onto his knees in the wreckage of frost and thorn-wood, gasping, mortal, diminished, weeping without sound.

Ember swayed. I caught him before he fell.

For a moment we just stood there in the thawing hall, the boy and I, holding each other up, breathing the same ragged breath, and I laughed—a wet, cracked, ridiculous sound—because we'd done it, we'd actually done it, and he was still *him*, I could feel it, he hadn't been consumed, he was still small and stubborn and his own—

Then his eyes rolled back, and the flood came.

Because the Crown hadn't only scattered into the land. It had scattered into *him*—into the piece that was always meant to receive it—and everything King Aldric had amputated ten years ago came pouring home into a nine-year-old body: ten years of atrocities witnessed from inside a cage, ten years of a queen's face slowly forgotten, ten years of a kingdom's grief compressed into a single small skull—

"Ember!" I shook him. "Ember, stay with me—"

His lips moved. The old tongue poured out of him, faster and faster, syllables stacking on syllables, a language carrying more than any one life should hold—and his eyes opened, and for one terrible instant they weren't a child's eyes at all.

They were deciding.

*Whole or separate. King or boy. Return and be restored, or remain and be diminished.*

The hall held its breath. Ilvane's sword-arm trembled. On the floor, the freed king lifted his ruined face to watch what his own severed conscience would choose.

And Ember—my Ember, the boy who healed cubs and drew maps and chose, always, the harder kindness—looked at the vastness inside himself, and looked at me, and smiled.

"I know which one I am," he said. In our tongue, this time. Small. Hoarse. His.

* * *

He closed his eyes, and let it go.

I felt it leave him—not violently, but the way a river reaches the sea and stops needing to be a river. The surplus poured out of him and out through the walls, out into the sleeping capital, out beyond it into the frozen fields and the high white passes, soaking into root and river and stone, and the mountains themselves seemed to lean closer to receive it. What stayed behind in that small body was exactly enough: a boy's memories, a boy's griefs, a boy's fierce and particular love.

He weighed nothing at all when he finally went limp in my arms.

* * *

The next hours passed in a blur of torchlight and shouting and the strange quiet of a palace discovering it had a living king again. Maren wept over her box of ashes as though the archives had personally apologized. Ilvane stood guard at the throne room doors having apparently decided that defecting included defending, and the wolves slept in front of the dead hearths like they'd paid for the privilege.

And Aldric—mortal, diminished, gray-faced Aldric—knelt on the floor of his own thawing throne room and asked me, in a voice scraped raw, whether his queen had died knowing he'd forgotten her face.

"She isn't yours to grieve anymore," I told him. "None of it is. That's what freedom costs. You pay in ownership."

He nodded slowly, taking the sentence like a sentence, which was the first kingly thing I ever saw him do.

But it was Ember, waking at dusk in a bed too large for him, who said the thing I carried out of that palace and have carried ever since. I was sitting watch beside him, and he surfaced from sleep just enough to find my hand.

"The mountains took it," he mumbled. "All of it. Every memory. They're going to *remember for* everyone, Vesper. Nobody has to forget anymore."

"Go to sleep, troublemaker."

"They're going to answer," he said, already sinking under. "Next spring. When you climb up and speak to them. They're going to answer *back*."

I sat in the lamplight a long time after that, listening to a child breathe, in a city that no longer belonged to a crown, and wondered what in the world I would say to a mountain that talked.
