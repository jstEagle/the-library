## 21. Spring Thaw, Open Doors

The mud in Greyhollow arrived before spring did, the way gossip always arrives before the truth—everywhere at once, tracking into every corner of your life whether you invited it or not. By the time the snowmelt finished its slow confession down from the Thornfell, the town had decided two things: that I was a hero, and that I was insufferable about it.

I was, for the record, insufferable about exactly none of it. The hero business was Doss's posthumous invention, polished by survivors who needed the story to have a shape. In their version, I strode out of the burning town with the child-king's conscience on my hip and a wolf army at my back, and I did not once slip on the ice, swear in three languages including one meant for goats, or cry into my scarf where nobody could see. History is a smuggler too. It moves what pays best and leaves the rest in the pass.

What actually happened was smaller and stranger: the town burned, half of it anyway, and then the war ended, and then people came back, because people always come back to places they've sworn off. Greyhollow rebuilt itself the way it did everything—badly, stubbornly, with stolen timber and arguments about property lines. By midsummer the new rooftops went up crooked and bright, cedar shingles still smelling of sap, and someone nailed a sign over the tavern door that read THE VEILED THORN, which was either an homage to my grandmother's old lullabies or a joke at my expense, and knowing Greyhollow, both.

My cabin survived. Of course it did. It had spent ten years being invisible, and invisibility, like any craft, improves with practice. The fire had come up the valley, licked at the fence posts, and moved along, bored. I took it as a professional compliment.

* * *

The school started the way most of my disasters start: with a goat.

Pietra—you'll remember her, the suspiciously intelligent mountain goat of Chapter One of my life, though she'd dispute the word *suspiciously*, preferring *thoroughly*—had taken to following a pack of children around the reconstruction sites, correcting their footing on loose scaffolds with head-butts of maternal precision. Their mothers found this charming until Pietra began herding the children away from the flooded culvert three days before anyone knew it would flood. After that, the mothers found it unnerving, which is the correct response to a goat who reads weather.

"She wants them taught," said Tam Wicker, the cooper's widow, standing in my doorway one morning with her daughter's hand in hers and the expression of a woman about to make a problem mine. "That's what Quill says. He says she's been saying it since thaw."

Quill, perched on my eave in the insolent posture he'd perfected over a decade of freeloading, confirmed this with a caw that translated roughly to: *I have been saying it since thaw, yes, and also you owe me bacon.*

"I'm not a teacher," I said.

"You're the only speaker left who talks to everything instead of commanding it," Tam said. "Osha's wolves talk about you like you're weather. Useful weather." She held out her daughter, who was six, gap-toothed, and staring at me with the naked hunger children have when they've heard adults whisper the word *magic*. "Start with her."

And that was how, at forty-one years of age, after a decade of engineering my own erasure with the diligence of a master craftsman, I came to be standing in the ruined chapel—the abbey was too far for small legs, and besides, the chapel had good light and worse acoustics, which keeps everyone honest—teaching eleven children and one goat the first verse of the oldest lullaby in the world.

*Ash to ash, thorn to tree,
what was broken remembers me.
Sleep, the mountain says, sleep.*

They were terrible at it. Children are always terrible at first, because the old tongue isn't learned so much as remembered, and remembering is work. The sounds sit sideways in a human throat; you have to let your breath go soft and low, down where the voice stops being yours alone. Little Sera Wicker—named, she told me proudly, for the Warden-Captain who now spent her days testifying in the capital and sending Greyhollow long letters of apology she hadn't earned yet—got the second line right on the fourth try, and when she did, every bird within a hundred yards went quiet.

Then, one heartbeat later, they all answered.

It wasn't dramatic. That's the thing nobody tells you. No thunderclap, no glowing runes. Just the finches and the jackdaws and old Quill on his eave, singing the line back in their own voices, slightly wrong, entirely willing—and Pietra humming it through her nose like she'd known it all along, which, knowing Pietra, she had.

Sera looked at me, eyes enormous. "Did I do that?"

"No," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "*We* did. That's rather the point."

I taught them for three mornings a week after that. Word got around, as things do in Greyhollow, meaning within a day everyone knew and within a week everyone pretended they'd always approved. We added a fox kit whose den had been dug out by the flood, two raven fledglings Quill grudgingly sponsored, a badger who never spoke but attended every lesson and stared at me like an examiner. Mothers brought bread. Fathers stood at the back pretending not to listen and getting the verses stuck in their heads anyway, and I'd catch them humming *thorn to tree* over their carpentry with the sheepish look of men caught praying.

Openly. That was the word that kept catching in my chest like a splinter. Ten years of teaching nothing to no one, of trading my voice for safe passage and calling the silence peace. And now a child sang in the street and nobody flinched. A warden's name sat on a child without fear. The door of the chapel stayed open all day because there was no longer anything to hide behind it.

I kept waiting for the trap. Old habits. You spend a decade fluent in everything except trust, you don't lose the accent just because the country changes.

* * *

Ember came in autumn, riding pillion behind Maren Ostwick on a borrowed mule, and I heard him before I saw him—which is to say I heard the mule complain, in the resigned tones of an animal ferrying the most important person in the realm and fully aware it deserved better compensation.

Maren had adopted him in the summer, paperwork and all, because apparently even the end of kings requires signatures, and Maren, who had spent a lifetime believing in the redemptive power of documentation, approached the adoption rolls the way other people approach pilgrimages. She wrote to me weekly now. Her letters were four pages long, footnoted, and contained more warmth than she could manage aloud, which made them perfect.

Ember had grown. Not much—he was still small for his years, still carrying whatever weight ten years of being a king's amputated conscience leaves in a body—but he'd grown *into* himself, if that makes sense. The hollowed look was gone. He ate with both hands now. He laughed at things that weren't ancient griefs.

He also drew constantly. Maren said he filled a sketchbook a month: mountains mostly, and doorways, and hands. Always hands.

"Vesper!" he shouted before the mule had even stopped, and launched himself off its back with the total faith of a boy who knows someone will catch him. I caught him. Obviously. I'd have caught him out of the sky.

"Careful," I said into his hair. "You're famous now. If you break, the whole realm depreciates."

"Maren says I'm not famous, I'm *significant*, and significant is a burden you carry quietly." He pulled back and grinned at me, and there it was again, that flicker of something older than his face—Aldric's grin, the one from before, worn briefly by the piece of him that got to keep being kind. "She also says I can stay a whole week."

"A whole week," I agreed. "Provided you attend school and set a good example, which will be difficult, as you're the worst speller I know."

"In the old tongue?"

"In every tongue. It's almost a talent."

Maren climbed down more slowly, accepted my hug with the stiff formality of a woman who'd been raised to believe affection should be cited properly, and then looked at my chapel with the expression of a scholar finding a manuscript she'd only ever seen banned. "You're really doing it," she said. "In the open. Do you know how many records we have of open instruction? Three. All fragmentary. All—" She stopped herself, breathed, and smiled the smile of a woman giving herself permission. "All less than this."

* * *

He showed me the drawings on the second night, sitting cross-legged on my floor by the fire, while outside Pietra and Quill argued about acorns in the rising dusk.

"They're not dreams anymore," he said. "Maren helped me understand. When the Crown broke, everything Aldric cut away came back to me, and then I gave most of it to the land. But some things I kept." He turned the page. "On purpose."

The drawing was a woman. Ink and berry-stain, done with the unsteady confidence of a child who draws what he sees, not what he thinks he sees. Dark hair going grey at the temples. Laugh lines. A queen's circlet drawn as a simple band of flowers, because that's how he'd chosen to remember it—not the crown, the garden.

"The queen," I said quietly. "Aldric's memory of her."

"My mother," Ember said, without hesitation and without drama, and I understood that this sentence had cost him something enormous last winter and cost him nothing now, because he had done the arithmetic of grief already and gotten the answer he wanted. "The wolves' healer asked me, during Wolf-Winter. She said a memory kept is a debt paid forward. So I kept her face and gave away the war councils." He shrugged, nine years old and wiser than the archives. "Fair trade. Nobody needs the war councils. Everybody needs a mother."

He had eleven drawings of her. Cooking, he said, though Aldric had probably never watched her cook—it was a guess, a reconstruction, love filling in the gaps with ordinary light. Reading by a window. Dancing with a man whose face Ember had deliberately left blank. "That one's for him," he explained. "If he ever earns it back, he can draw himself in."

I thought of Aldric in the capital—diminished, mortal, spending his days signing dissolutions and abdications and letters of apology, weeping over testimony, slowly learning to be a man instead of a throne. Ilvane's letters mentioned him sometimes, always obliquely: *the former king attended the hearings today. He listened. He didn't defend.* I wondered if anyone had told him a child was keeping his wife's face safe for him, holding it like a coat at a funeral, waiting to see if he'd grown enough to wear the grief.

"He visits," Ember said, reading my silence the way he read everything. "Once a season. Maren supervises." A pause. "He's learning the lullaby. He's very bad at it."

"Worse than me?" This from the doorway, where Maren had appeared with tea and scholarly precision. "Because Vesper, I regret to inform you, hums it like a woman threatening a landslide."

"I do *not*—"

"*Thorn to tree,*" Maren intoned, in a flat archival monotone that was, admittedly, worse than anything I'd ever produced. Ember laughed so hard he fell over, and Pietra stuck her head through the door to see what was funny, and Quill landed on the windowsill to demand inclusion, and for a moment my cabin—the same cabin where I'd once counted coins to stay forgotten—held more voices than it had walls.

Warmth earned, guarded, permitted. I'd have written that down if I'd been the kind of person who wrote things down. Instead I just let it happen, which was harder.

* * *

On Ember's last night, we walked up behind the chapel to the flat stone I'd begun using for lessons with the older students—the ones ready to learn that the old tongue has grammar the way rivers have currents, felt rather than memorized. The autumn stars were out in force, sharp and cold, and the peaks wore their first dusting of white like old men trying on new hats.

Ember stood on the stone and looked north for a long time.

"They're louder this year," he said.

"I know."

"Is that good?"

I considered lying, decided against it—he'd given away enough lies for one lifetime, most of them belonging to other people. "I don't know yet. They've only been *awake* since the unbinding. A year is nothing to a mountain. They might be stretching. They might be practicing." I hesitated. "Or they might be deciding what kind of guardians they want to be. Osha says the high packs have stopped answering the council stone. Not refusing. Just... listening to something else first."

"Listening upward," Ember said.

"Listening upward," I agreed.

He nodded slowly, and then he did the thing he does that frightens and comforts me in equal measure: he reached out and put his palm flat against the stone, the way he'd touched the mural in the frozen abbey, and he listened with whatever it is he listens with—that splinter of a king's conscience, that boy who chose himself at the edge of annihilation.

After a minute, he lifted his hand and looked at me, and his eyes had gone wide and dark and very old.

"Vesper," he said. "Next spring. On the anniversary. You should climb to the council stone and greet them properly. Like your grandmother did." A pause, and then, softer, with a small boy's certainty and something underneath it vast as snowfall:

"Because this time, they're going to answer."
