## 22. The Mountains Answer

A year ago to the day, I spoke a language older than kingdoms into a frozen throne room, and a crown made of thorns came apart like a lie told too many times. A year is not long enough for the hands to stop shaking. It *is* long enough for the shaking to become something you can carry in one pocket alongside your knife and your coin, which is the smuggler's definition of healing.

I climbed the high road before dawn.

Not the smuggler's road—I burned those maps, and besides, the mountain has grown new paths since then, or perhaps uncovered old ones, the way an old woman uncovers dishes she'd forgotten she owned. This path went up past the goat terraces, past the shrine where someone had left winter apples and a comb for Osha, who has opinions about grooming that would embarrass a duchess, up through the last of the snow-rotted pines to the council stone.

The council stone is older than Greyhollow, older than the kingdom, older probably than the concept of being embarrassed by anything. It sits in a saddle between two shoulders of Thornfell like a table where somebody very large once negotiated something very important and then forgot to come back for the second meeting. My grandmother brought me here when I was seven. She put her hand flat on the frost-slick rock and said, *Greet them properly, girl, even if they never answer. Manners cost nothing and mountains remember everything.*

They never answered. Not her, not me, not any speaker in the songs. The peaks took up their guardianship at the unbinding—every beast from here to the capital confirmed it, chattering about roots waking and stones dreaming—but the mountains themselves kept a silence that was older than their silence had been before. I had made my peace with it the way you make peace with a deaf god: politely, and without expectation.

I brought offerings anyway. Smuggler's habit. You never approach anyone empty-handed, whether they wear a crown or fur or ten thousand feet of granite.

Bread baked with the good butter. Salt. A lock of Ember's hair he'd insisted on cutting himself, which meant it looked like a fight he'd lost, and a drawing he'd made of me standing on this exact spot—he'd drawn the mountain behind me with a face, which either showed remarkable artistic vision or a worrying amount of literal truth. And for Quill, who refused to be left behind and rode my shoulder the whole climb complaining about altitude in the tone of a dowager forced to holiday in Scotland: half the dried liver in my pack.

"You spoil him," I told the mountain, nodding at Quill as he crammed his crop.

*You spoil everything you love,* the wind seemed to say, but wind says whatever you're afraid of, so I didn't count it.

I put my palm on the stone. Frost bit my skin. Below me Greyhollow was a scatter of woodsmoke in the blue hour, roofs I'd helped re-lay in spring, the new schoolhouse with its shutters still crooked because Maren keeps ordering books instead of carpenters, and honestly, fair. Somewhere down there children were waking up to lessons in the old tongue, human children and a fox kit and a badger cub who bites when bored, all of them learning words that would have gotten us all burned two winters ago. Somewhere further south, a diminished king was planting an orchard with his own two mortal hands, because he said ruling taught him nothing so useful as gardening, and Ilvane—who commands no one now but commands herself beautifully—walked his orchard rows every week with a ledger of names, reading the dead back into the record where the Crown had erased them.

And somewhere between here and there, in the high passes, wolves sang the old tongue now. Badly. With enthusiasm. Like children mangling a hymn.

I took a breath that hurt with cold and spoke the greeting.

It's a simple thing, the morning greeting. Grandmother's version ran: *Old ones, steep ones, keepers of the long memory — we who wake and die have not forgotten you.* Six words in the old tongue, each one shaped in the mouth like river stones. I said it the way she taught me, thumb pressed to the rock, voice pitched low the way you speak to something sleeping, or grieving, or both.

The valley held its breath.

Then the mountain answered.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that moment and words are already wearing it smooth. It was not an echo. Echoes come back thinner than what you send. This came back *larger*. The greeting returned to me in the old tongue, but deepened, slowed, layered—a hundred voices folded into one, the way a choir is one voice made of throats—and beneath the words themselves there was a pressure, a weight, like standing under a waterfall of meaning. The syllables arrived in my ears and simultaneously in my bones, in the soles of my feet, in the fillings of my teeth.

And I understood it, the way you understand your own name.

*We who sleep and wake, keepers of the short memory — you who were not forgotten have not been forgotten.*

It isn't a translation you can make clean. Old tongue doesn't translate; it *agrees* to mean approximately what you need. But the shape of it, the intent: they knew me. They knew my grandmother, whose name they pronounced inside the greeting like a small bell rung once. They knew Ember. They knew Aldric and his orchard and his weeping, and Ilvane and her ledger, and Doss Marrow buried under the cairn at the traitor's pass with a wolf-pack honor guard that visits him more faithfully than most men get visited by the living. They knew the young bear who took the wardens' meat, too—the mountain mentioned her without hatred, only sorrow, and noted that she carries water for the pack now, every day, in payment, and that payment is how trust gets rebuilt, which is a lesson some kingdoms could stand to learn.

They had been listening the whole time. All year. All century. All the slow ages while kings rose like frosts and melted.

"Say something else," I whispered, when I could speak at all. "Please."

The answer rolled through the stone under my hand, patient as snowfall:

*Ask.*

So I asked the question I'd carried up the mountain like a stone in my boot. *How long? How long will you keep the vigil?*

Because here is the thing nobody had said out loud, though Ember had come close once, drawing at my kitchen table with his tongue between his teeth. The Crown was a bargain made to end the Devouring War. The bargain failed—or rather, succeeded too well, the way cages succeed. And now the guardianship had passed to the mountains. To *something*. And bargains, I've learned, are just futures with the fine print not yet read.

The mountain's answer came slow, and it did not come as words first. It came as sensation: the feel of ten thousand years of patience, the patience of a thing that measures time in uplift and erosion, in the leisurely collapse of empires into topsoil.

*Until the small ones no longer need guarding from each other. Then we will go back to sleep, and be only beautiful.*

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out cracked and half a sob, but I laughed, there on the roof of the world, and the vibration of it traveled down through my palm into the stone, and I felt—distinctly, unmistakably—the mountain *feel* it. Amusement. Dry as scree. Ten thousand feet of granite doing the equivalent of a raised eyebrow.

"You'll be waiting a while," I said. "We're a stubborn lot."

*Yes,* said the mountain, with what I can only describe as fondness. *We chose our students poorly and our heirs well.*

* * *

Quill tugged my ear. He does that when there's something he wants said aloud that he doesn't want attributed to him, an arrangement we've had since the parliament of crows, when he voted for me three times under false pretenses and has felt vaguely guilty ever since.

"Go on then," I said.

He hopped to the council stone, spread his wings, and addressed the peak directly in the formal register of crow-council, which sounds like a hinge that needs oiling but is apparently ancient diplomatic protocol. What he said, roughly rendered: the winged folk wished it recorded that they had served as messengers throughout the vigil, that their fees had been paid mostly in liver, and that they requested, going forward, consideration of *other* currencies, specifically shiny ones.

The mountain laughed.

I don't know how else to write it. Rock does not laugh the way throats laugh. It laughs the way avalanches begin—one grain, then another, a shiver of joy moving through the mass—and the whole slope below the council stone trembled with something that was absolutely not an earthquake, whatever the Greyhollow seismophobes claim, and snow slid off the upper cornices in soft white sheets, and every bird within five miles went up at once, a black confetti of delight, and Quill preened like a cardinal who'd just gotten a cardinal promoted to pope.

*Tell the feathered diplomat,* the mountain said, *that rivers polish stones without fee, and that shininess is available free at any streambed, and that wisdom is knowing the difference between price and value.*

"He'll pretend to find that profound," I warned them. "He'll repeat it at every council until we all memorize it against our will."

*Good,* said the mountain. *That is how sayings survive.*

* * *

I stayed until full sun. We talked—or the closest thing to talking that a woman and a mountain have managed in the history of either. I told it about the schoolhouse, and it told me about the glacier, and how the glacier remembered the first speakers, and how the first speakers had asked exactly the question I'd asked, and how the answer then had been the same: *until you no longer need us.* I asked what happened if the realm needed guarding from the mountains someday, and got a silence so vast and so gently amused that I understood I'd just asked the ocean whether it worried about offending rain.

Near the end—it let me feel the nearness of the end, the way a good host signals last call—I asked the question underneath all my questions. The one I hadn't asked in the throne room. The one I hadn't asked Ember, or Maren, or myself, in a year of not asking.

*Why me?*

There were speakers before me. Better trained, better trusted, better loved. Grandmother died for the old tongue and never heard so much as a pebble clear its throat. Why did the unbinding pass through *my* mouth? Why did the mountains choose my generation, my town, my ragged caravan of a child and a crow and a repentant smuggler and a defecting warden and a bear matriarch with grooming standards?

The answer came slowly, and it was not the answer I expected, and it broke me open in the way that only true things can.

*We did not choose you. You were never chosen. That was the Crown's word and the Crown's error—choosing, selecting, excising the unchosen parts. We do not choose. We listen, and we answer whoever speaks. Your grandmother spoke. We were too asleep to answer, and her granddaughter grew up fluent in everything except trust, which is the fluency we needed. You were not chosen, Vesper Winterbourne. You arrived.*

I sat on the council stone with the sun on my face and cried like something washed clean. Quill stood on my knee and pretended, out of mercy, to be interested in a distant cloud.

Arrived. Not chosen. Ten years I'd built my life on the arithmetic of staying invisible—small thefts of coin, smaller thefts of safety, a talent hidden like contraband. And all along the door had simply been unlocked, and the only toll was the courage to knock. Grandmother knew. *Greet them properly, even if they never answer.* She wasn't teaching me manners. She was leaving the door ajar.

* * *

On the way down I stopped at the cairn. Someone had left fresh juniper on it—Osha's work, by the size of the paw print in the mud beside it. I added a smooth grey stone from my pocket, picked up on the summit without quite knowing why, and I understood it now.

"Doss," I said, "you absolute menace. The mountain talks. You'd have tried to sell it something."

Wind moved through the pines. It might have been agreement. It might have been wind. Up here those are increasingly the same thing.

Greyhollow was loud with morning by the time I reached the terraces. The schoolhouse door stood open, and I could hear Ember's voice—he visits twice a month now, rides up with Maren's couriers, draws on every flat surface including, once, the forehead of a sleeping badger—teaching the six-word greeting to a roomful of children who chanted it back wrong in eleven different ways. Wrong, and alive, and theirs.

I stood in the doorway with frost melting off my coat and listened.

And from the heights above the town, faint but unmistakable, the mountain repeated the greeting back to them—*we who sleep and wake, keepers of the short memory*—and the children went silent, and then erupted, and one small fox kit looked straight at me with her amber eyes wide, and said:

"It answered! Teacher, it *answered!* Does it always?"

"No," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me. "But it's listening. Always start from that."

She nodded gravely, the way children accept enormous cosmologies before lunch, and turned back to the board.

Outside, the peaks stood white against a hard blue sky, keeping their patient watch, and I swear on my grandmother's grave and Doss's cairn and every map I ever burned—that morning, for just a moment, the sunlight on Thornfell's eastern face moved like a slow blink.

The world was listening.

All we had to do was keep speaking.
