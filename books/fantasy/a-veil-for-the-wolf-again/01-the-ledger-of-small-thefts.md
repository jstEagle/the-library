## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Emrys Joubert found the discrepancy.

She sat in the back room of the Guild of Impossible Things, at a desk older than the city walls, with a ledger open before her so heavy it took both hands to turn a page. The paper was vellum the color of old teeth, and the ink—her ink, mixed fresh every month from gall and ash and one drop of something the Guild's alchemist refused to name—had that faint silver sheen that meant it would hold a memory without letting it leak.

Around her, the guildhouse slept. She could hear the building settling, beams ticking like slow clocks, and somewhere below, the cellar door swinging on its hinge because no one else ever bothered to latch it. The Guild of Impossible Things did not sleep well as an institution. It slept like a drunk: heavily, badly, and with no memory in the morning of where it had put anything.

Which was why they kept her.

Emrys dipped her pen and wrote, in a hand so small and precise it looked printed:

*Item 447. One lullaby, bottled. Seller: the widow Anselm of Tanner's Row. Buyer: the house of Vaire, for their youngest, who has not slept a whole night since his christening. Price: six years of the widow's grief for her drowned son, weighed and found acceptable. Note: grief was stale; buyer overpaid. Flag for goodwill adjustment.*

She blotted it, turned the page, and ran her finger down the column of entries until she reached the one bothering her.

*Item 441. One debt, discharged in shadows.*

The entry read clean enough. A moneylender named Ostrec had owed a blood-debt to the family of a man he'd ruined, and rather than pay in coin or flesh, he'd paid in shadow—the specific shadow he cast at noon on midsummer's day, the one hour of his life when he stood tallest. The family had accepted. The ledger recorded the transfer, the notarization, the sealing wax.

What it didn't record was where the shadow had *gone*.

Emrys checked the receiving entries. Nothing. Checked the vault manifests. Nothing. Somewhere in the Guild's possession—or already sold, or already lost—was a man's noon-shadow, and nobody had written down where it lived.

That was how things went wrong. Not with thefts and alarms, but with an unwritten line. A memory unaccounted for was a door left open, and doors left open were how the world got drafty.

"Where are you," she murmured to the shadow, and pulled a fresh sheet toward her to begin a reconciliation note.

The knock came at the guildhouse front door at half past midnight, which meant it wasn't a client. Clients came by day, through the painted door with the veil hung across it, escorted, searched, made to sign things. A knock at half past midnight was either trouble or desperation, and after eleven years behind this desk Emrys had learned the two often wore the same boots.

She waited. The knock came again—three blows, evenly spaced, patient. Whoever it was knew someone was awake. The candlelight always leaked under the back room door; she'd never bothered to stop it.

She closed the ledger, locked it in the iron box beneath the floorboard, and went to answer it herself, because the other residents of the guildhouse included a forger who screamed at moths and two brothers who could only be trusted near sharp objects one at a time.

* * *

The man on the doorstep was rich. That was the first thing. Rich the way people are rich who have never once had to think about the cost of anything: fur-lined cloak beaded with rain, boots that had never been resoled, a signet ring he'd had the poor sense to wear openly to a den of thieves. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, and he stood in the rain like a man who had forgotten weather existed until it touched him.

"You're closed," he said.

"The veil is up," Emrys agreed. "We're closed."

"I'll pay for the inconvenience." He said it without arrogance, which somehow made it worse. It was simply a fact of his life, like gravity. "My name is Aldous Renne. I'm steward to the crown."

Emrys kept her face still. Rain dripped off the eaves between them, threading silver in the dark. Behind her, inside the guildhouse, someone laughed in their sleep—a thin, unhappy sound.

"We don't serve the crown," she said.

"The crown doesn't know I'm here." He glanced past her shoulder into the dark hallway, and she stepped sideways, closing the sightline. "I need something taken. Something small. Small for you."

"Everything is small until we're holding it."

"A memory," he said. "A childhood. The king's childhood."

The rain filled the silence for three full drops before Emrys said, "No."

"You haven't heard the—"

"No." She said it again, gently, the way you'd close a door on a child's fingers. "Whatever your reasons—and I'm sure they're good ones, stewards always have good ones—the answer is the same. We do not take from the king. We do not take from the royal line. It's not negotiable, it's not a matter of price, and if you say the word *discreetly* I will have you escorted to the river gate and remembered there, fondly, by no one."

Renne's jaw tightened. Rain ran off his chin. "You don't understand what I'm asking."

"I understand perfectly. You want us to steal a man's childhood out of his head." She leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, and let him see her clearly for the first time—small woman, plain dress, ink stain on the side of one hand like a birthmark. "There are rules, Master Renne. Three of them, and they're carved over the door you knocked on, though the veil hides them from those who aren't meant to read. First: never steal what can't be replaced. Second: never steal from someone who can't consent to the loss. Third: never keep what you wouldn't give back."

"And the king's childhood breaks all three."

"It breaks them beautifully," Emrys said. "A childhood is the load-bearing wall of a person. You take it out and the man comes down, whatever age he is. And the king—" she paused, choosing the words with a thief's care, giving him none of what she actually thought, "—the king consents to nothing. Kings don't. They're told afterward that it rained yesterday, and they nod, because everyone around them is paid to agree."

Something flickered across the steward's face then. She'd catalogued it before, in other faces, other decades: the look of a man carrying a secret too large for his body. It aged people. It bent them slowly, like wet timber.

"He wouldn't miss it," Renne said quietly.

"Everyone misses it. That's the cruelty of the trade. The missing is the wound; the memory is just the knife."

He looked at her for a long moment, rain pooling at his expensive feet, and Emrys saw him decide—not to leave, but to stop pretending. It happened in the shoulders first. People lied with their spines straight.

"There are gaps," he said. "In him. In the king. Days that don't connect. Years, now. He greets ambassadors twice, delighted both times, as if for the first time. He signs documents he signed the day before. His physicians call it melancholy. His confessor calls it grace." Renne's voice dropped. "I've served this crown thirty-one years. I know what a man looks like when he is *wearing out*. And last week, at the autumn court, the king looked at me—looked straight at me, Emrys Joubert, though I'd never given him my name—and asked me why the stars had stopped coming out."

The rain fell. Somewhere across the rooftops, a watch bell rang the third hour, muffled and wrong, as if the sound itself had gotten lost.

"That's not my work," Emrys said. "And it isn't any work the Guild has done. We keep accounts. If a theft had been made from the king's head, it would be written here, in this house, in ink that doesn't fade. There is no such entry. I balance these books myself. Every night. There is no such entry."

"Then where did he go?" Renne asked. Simply. Terribly. The question of a man who had run out of better questions.

And Emrys—who had answers the way other people had coins, who could produce a fact for any occasion the way a merchant produces change—found she had nothing.

"I don't know," she said.

It was, she would think later, the second most expensive sentence of her life.

Renne nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something rather than failed to. He reached into his cloak and produced a card—heavy stock, sealed with plain black wax, no crest. "If you learn anything. Anything at all. Day or night. The veil won't stop me."

He turned into the rain. Emrys watched him go down the street, hunched now, smaller than he'd arrived, a rich man diminished by a poor man's knowledge: that some things cannot be bought back.

She closed the door. Stood with her hand flat against the wood for a moment, listening to the building breathe around her.

Then she went back to her desk, unlocked the iron box, and opened the ledger not to the current page but to the very beginning—to the founding entries, sixty years old, in handwriting that predated hers by three keepers. She had read these pages a hundred times. She read them again, slower, hunting for the shape of a hole: an entry that should exist and didn't, a gap in the numbering, a year with fewer trades than the year before it.

Nothing. The books were perfect. Sixty years of impossible commerce, balanced to the copper token, and not one line missing.

That was the problem, she realized, sitting back in the chair as the candle finally guttered and died. The books were *perfect*. Perfectly balanced, perfectly complete, perfectly consistent—in the way a portrait is consistent, flat and finished and silent. Real ledgers breathed. Real ledgers had smudges, corrections, crossed-out lines, the honest mess of hands at work. Hers had none. As if somewhere, sometime, someone had gone through sixty years of entries and made them *agree*.

As if the books themselves had been edited.

Emrys lit a new candle from the stub of the old one, and in the fresh flame she caught her own reflection doubled in the ledger's brass corner-fittings—two small dark women, facing each other across an inch of metal, neither one moving.

Outside, high above the rainclouds, the stars were going out. She did not see it yet. No one in Vhalen did, not tonight, not while the lamps still burned their ordinary gold.

But the king had noticed. And kings, whatever else they were, noticed first.

She wrote one final line before bed, in her smallest hand, at the bottom of a blank page she dated simply *tomorrow*, because some instinct older than her training told her the entry belonged there:

*Item 448. One inquiry, declined. Client: the crown's own shadow. Subject: the king's childhood. Status: unresolved. Note: the ledger shows no theft. The ledger is lying.*
