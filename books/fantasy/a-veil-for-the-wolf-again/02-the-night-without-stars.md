## 2. The Night Without Stars

The candle had burned down to its last thumb-width when the stars went out.

Emrys did not see it happen. She was hunched over the Guild's ledger with her sleeves pushed back and her pen moving in small, precise strokes, cataloguing the week's impossible trades: one bottled lullaby, purchased from a weaver's widow who could no longer bear to hum it; one debt paid in shadows, three years' worth, collected from a moneylender in the Salt Quarter whose shadow had grown fat and disobedient; one memory of a first kiss, sold twice by the same fool, which meant somewhere in the city two people believed they had been loved first and best.

She liked this hour. The guildhouse above her—*her* guildhouse, though no deed said so and no neighbor knew her face—was quiet as a held breath. Down here in the counting room the world reduced itself to ink and candlelight and arithmetic, and arithmetic never lied to you. People lied. Memories lied, especially the stolen ones, which arrived already grieving. But a ledger balanced was a ledger balanced, and for twenty years that had been enough to keep the dark out.

Her rule sat stitched into the ledger's flyleaf in her own careful hand: *Never steal a memory you cannot give back.*

It was the only commandment she kept. It was, she sometimes thought at hours like this, the only thing about her worth keeping.

The candle guttered.

Not the way candles gutter when a door opens—no push of air, no shiver of flame leaning into a draft. It simply *crouched*, the way an animal crouches, and the light in the room went strange and thin, and Emrys looked up from the ledger because twenty years of thieving had taught her that light always announces what it is afraid of.

Through the counting room's high slit of a window she saw the sky.

Or rather, she saw where the sky should have been. She stood so fast her chair went over backward and cracked against the flagstones, and she didn't care, because the night over Vael Morath was empty. Not clouded. Not fogged. *Empty.* Every star gone at once, all ten thousand of them, snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, as if some enormous hand had come down over the world like a lid.

She had seen the sky without stars before. Everyone had, on overcast nights, on smoke-heavy nights. But there was a difference between a sky that hid its stars and a sky that had been *robbed* of them, and the difference was in the blackness itself. This black had texture. It pressed on the window like water pressing on the far side of glass.

"The wards," she said aloud, to no one, and then, more quietly: "Someone took the wards."

Because that was the thing every child in Vael Morath learned before they learned their letters: the border wards were strung from starlight. Nine great anchor-stones ringing the kingdom, each one fed by a fixed star, each thread of the warding woven bright and invisible between them, holding out everything the founders had agreed to name *the outside*. You couldn't see the threads. You weren't supposed to be able to. But every thief in the city knew they were there the way a fish knows water—by the shape of what they excluded—and Emrys, standing at her window with her heart going hard and slow, felt them *go*.

It was not a sound. It was the absence of a sound she had never consciously heard, the way a tooth's absence aches. Somewhere beyond the hills, nine threads cut taut for four hundred years let go all at once, like strings snipped by scissors, and she felt the snap travel through the stone floor and up through her boot soles and settle in her teeth.

Then the lamps turned blue.

All of them. In the counting room, in the hall above, in the lane outside the shuttered window—the whole city's flame went from gold to a pale, cold blue in the space of a breath, the color of ice over deep water, the color of the light that came off certain memories when you lifted them out of a dying mind. Emrys had seen that blue exactly twice before. Both times, someone had died shortly after.

She did not scream. Screaming was for people who existed. Instead she did the only sensible thing a thief does when the world breaks: she checked her ledger, because if something had changed, the change would be written down. That was the whole faith she lived by. *If it happened, it can be recorded. If it can be recorded, it can be understood. If it can be understood, it can be survived.*

The ledger lay open to the current page, the week's trades neat beneath her hand.

And below the last entry, in ink still wet enough to shine, in handwriting that made her stomach drop through the floor—

There was a new line.

*Received, in trade: one night, complete. Paid out: one life, entire.*

Dated tomorrow.

---

She read it eleven times. On the eleventh reading her hands had stopped shaking, which was worse than the shaking, because the shaking had at least been hers.

The handwriting was hers. She would have staked every memory in her hoard on it—the particular forward lean of the letters, the way she crossed her *t*'s with a single impatient stroke, the tiny hook she put on the descender of a *y*. No forger alive could match it, because she had trained herself out of every recognizable habit years ago and then trained herself *back into* these, deliberately, so that her own hand would be the one thing in the world she could trust.

The entry was dated tomorrow. The ink was wet tonight.

Emrys Joubert had spent twenty years making herself unremarkable. She paid her taxes through intermediaries who paid other intermediaries. She owned nothing under her own name. Her neighbors in the dyer's row house she rented thought the rooms above the shop were occupied by a widowed cousin who traveled for cloth—she had built that cousin memory by memory, a fiction with better provenance than most real people. The Guild knew her as Ledger-Keeper Fourth Rank, a functionary, a person who counted other people's thefts and committed none. Even Vess, who saw further into a soul than any living thief, saw only a competent, colorless woman with tidy books.

Invisible. That had been the whole architecture of her survival, laid brick by brick since she was old enough to understand that being remembered was dangerous.

And now the sky had been emptied, and her own hand had written tomorrow's date, and the entry read like a bill of sale for a life.

"Paid out," she murmured. "One life, entire."

Whose life? Hers? Someone else's? Ledgers did not do poetry. Ledgers recorded trades, and every trade had two sides, and somewhere in the city or beyond it there was a counter-entry waiting to be found. That was the thought she caught hold of, the rope thrown to a drowning woman: *find the counter-entry.* If her book said one life paid out, then some other book said one life received, and books could be traced, and things that could be traced could be fought.

She was reaching for the archive keys when the bells began.

Vael Morath's watch-bells rang the hours, and everyone knew their voices: the cracked tenor of Saint Ferren's, the double-bronze of the Palace tower, the flat iron tongue of the Customs house that sailors cursed in three languages. What began now was none of those. It came from the walls—from the ward-towers on the old circuit, long decommissioned, bells that had not sounded since her grandmother's grandmother's time. They rang in a pattern with no hour in it, no number, just a rising and falling wail, and after a few seconds Emrys understood what she was hearing, because the pattern was older than the city and every child once knew it the way children know the sound of their mother's footsteps.

They were ringing *unstrung*. They were ringing the fall of the wards.

Above her, through the floorboards, she heard the dyer's household wake. Footsteps. A child crying, then stopping abruptly, in the wrong way—not comforted but *confused*, as if it had forgotten midway through why it was crying. Doors opened along the lane. Voices rose, overlapped, and Emrys stood very still in her blue-lit counting room and listened to a city begin to panic, and made herself think instead of feel, because feeling was a luxury with a price tag and she had never once been able to afford it.

Think. The wards fell tonight. The stars went out tonight. The lamps burn blue. And in my ledger, in my hand, dated tomorrow: *one night received, one life paid out.*

What traded a night? What bought a whole life?

Only one kind of buyer had that much currency. She had never dealt with it—no thief had, not openly, not in living memory—but every Guild member absorbed its existence with their first cup of tea in the counting room, the way you absorb that the sea is salt. There were things in the world that ate memory the way fire ate wood. The Guild's oldest ledgers, the ones chained shut in the vault below, referred to them only ever as *the hungry*, and even those entries were sparse, and even those entries had been redacted.

Redacted. By whom? By what authority? The Guild answered to nothing but its own rules, and Emrys had copied those rules out herself as an apprentice, all of them, and nowhere among them was a rule permitting erasure. Yet the old ledgers had holes in them. Clean holes. Professional ones.

She had noticed years ago and told herself it was decay, or modesty, or the simple rot of very old paper. Tonight, standing in blue light under an empty sky with her own tomorrow already written down, she stopped telling herself stories.

The bells wailed on. The child upstairs had begun crying again, or perhaps a different child, or perhaps the same one crying for the second first time.

Emrys blew out her candle—it barely mattered now, the lamp burned blue whether she wanted it or not—and took down the ledger, and wrapped it in oilcloth with the care other people gave to infants. Then she unlocked the drawer where she kept the things she would need if she ever had to run: coin under four false names, a knife with no maker's mark, a ring of keys to doors in six districts, and the copper token she'd carried for nineteen years without knowing why—a worn disc stamped with a crown, too soft to be currency, too deliberate to be trash. She'd won it in a card game the night she first came to Vael Morath, off a drunk old man who had wept when he lost it and couldn't say why.

She had meant to return it. That was the rule: never take what you can't give back. But he'd died before spring, and the token stayed in her drawer like an unpaid debt, and tonight, for no reason her mind could defend, her hand went past the coin and the knife and closed around the copper first.

The crown stamped into it was crude, ancient, wrong somehow—the circlet drawn with too many points, eleven or twelve, where the king's crown bore seven. Under her thumb it felt warm. Nothing in that drawer had been warm in nineteen years.

Outside, the bells climbed toward a pitch that hurt. Down the lane someone shouted a name over and over—*Mara, Mara, Mara*—the way you shout when the person who answers is the only proof you have that you exist.

And the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, felt heavier than a book should. Heavier the way a confession is heavier. Because there was a fourth fact she had not yet allowed herself, and it sat in her chest like swallowed ice:

The entry was in her hand. Which meant either she had written it and forgotten—and Emrys Joubert forgetting was a catastrophe on the order of the sea forgetting salt—or something had reached into her night, into her locked and warded counting room, into the one place in the world built entirely out of her own caution, and used her.

Both possibilities ended the same way. The invisible life was over. Whatever came next, someone out there in the starless dark already knew her name—the true one, the one she hadn't spoken aloud in nineteen years, the one not even the Guild had.

She looked up at the blue-lit ceiling, as if the answer might be written there, as if the missing stars might leave an outline of themselves behind the way pressed flowers leave ghosts in old books.

"Tomorrow," she said to the empty sky, and her voice did not shake, and she hated how steady it was, how practiced, how much it sounded like a ledger-keeper's voice instead of a person's. "Then I have until tomorrow."

The bells broke off mid-wail, all together, all at once, and in the ringing silence afterward Emrys heard—clearly, unmistakably, from the direction of the dead ward-towers—a voice. Thin with distance. Patient as interest accruing.

It called her name. The true one.

And beneath her arm, the ledger grew warm through the oilcloth, as if the wet ink of tomorrow's entry were drying at last—as if the transaction were already closing, and the night had merely come early to collect.
