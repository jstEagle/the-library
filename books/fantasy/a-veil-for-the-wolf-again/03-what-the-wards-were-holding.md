## 3. What the Wards Were Holding

The wards had been dead for three days when I finally stopped pretending I wasn't going to cross them.

I told myself it was professional curiosity. A ledger-keeper's diligence. The Guild paid me to know where every impossible thing in the city stood, and a collapsed border was, by any accounting, an impossible thing standing in the wrong place. But the truth was simpler and uglier: I had read my own handwriting dated tomorrow, and there are only two kinds of people who can live with that. The kind who burn the ledger and run.

And the kind who walk toward it.

I packed like a thief, which is to say lightly and with lies. Bread, cheese, a flask of plum brandy for warmth or courage depending on the hour. My lockpicks. Three blank memory-vials, glass blown thick enough to hold a screaming soul without cracking. And the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, because I had learned by then that the book and I were bound together in some way I didn't yet understand—where one of us went, the other followed.

The city thinned as I rode north. First the merchant quarters with their shutters drawn, then the tanneries, then nothing but frost-bitten fields and the occasional shrine to saints whose names had been worn off their own statues. The lamps of Vhalen burned blue behind me all night, a cold stain on the horizon like a bruise on the sky's throat.

I passed no one on the road. That should have been my first warning. The borderlands were poor, but they were never empty—carters, pilgrims, smugglers who knew the gaps in the ward-lines better than the wardens did. Instead I rode through a silence so complete I could hear my own heartbeat arguing with the horse's hooves.

Near dusk on the second day, I found the first village.

It sat in a shallow valley where a river used to run—the riverbed was cracked clay now, though no drought had touched the green hills above it. Smoke still rose from chimneys. Doors stood open. Laundry hung on lines, stiff with frost, forgotten mid-task.

The people were there too. That was the worst part. They hadn't fled, hadn't died, hadn't been slaughtered. They were *there*, walking their streets, sitting on their stoops, tending fires they seemed surprised to find burning.

A woman stood at a well with a bucket in both hands, staring into the water. She'd been crying—I could see the salt tracks on her face—but she cried the way weather happens, without thought behind it.

"Hello," I said, dismounting.

She looked up. Her eyes were brown and ordinary and utterly vacant, and when she spoke her voice was pleasant and empty as a rented room. "Hello."

"What's your name?"

She frowned. Not in confusion—in *effort*. I watched her reach for something inside herself and find the shelf bare. "I had one," she said slowly. "I'm nearly sure I had one. It was..." Her mouth worked. "It started soft. Like the beginning of a prayer." She shook her head, and the effort collapsed, and her face went smooth again. "Is it important? Names?"

"Yes," I said. "Yes, it's very important."

"That's good," she said, relieved. "I thought maybe it wasn't. I keep thinking things are important and then I can't remember why." She smiled at me, warm and vacant, and held out the bucket. "Would you like water? I don't remember if I've offered you any yet. I might have offered already."

I took the bucket because refusing felt like cruelty. The water was clean and cold. While I drank, she watched me with the mild interest of someone watching birds.

"You're a stranger," she said. "Strangers have names. Could I borrow yours until mine comes back?"

My name. Emrys Joubert. Ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, third of that name in the ledgers, keeper of the rule that had kept me alive and invisible for eleven years: *never steal a memory you cannot give back.*

"It's Emrys," I said.

"Emrys," she repeated, tasting it. Then her face clouded, and I watched the word slide off her like rain off wax. By the time she said, "Thank you," she had already lost it. Already lost *me*.

I mounted up with my hands shaking. Behind me she returned to her well, to her bucket, to her beautiful empty patience, waiting for a name that was never coming back.

The second village was worse because it was larger, and the third was worse than that because I began to understand the pattern.

It wasn't violence. There were no soldiers, no burn marks, no broken doors. Whatever had come through the fallen wards hadn't attacked these people. It had *edited* them. And not crudely—it hadn't taken everything. They could speak. They could work. They could feel the dim ghosts of love; I saw a man rocking an infant with real tenderness, humming a lullaby he no longer knew the words to, making up sounds to fill the shape the words had left.

What they'd lost was themselves. Their names. Their histories. The particular weight of being *someone* rather than anyone. They were people reduced to their functions, and functions, I knew from my trade, are the last thing memory surrenders. You forget your mother's face before you forget how to knead bread.

I was looking at the aftermath of a theft so vast it made my whole career look like a child picking pockets at a fair.

And I recognized the technique. That was the thing that turned my blood to ice water. The selective taking, the careful preservation of useful skills, the hollowing-out that left a body walking and working and wanting nothing—it was *craft*. It was the same craft the Guild taught, scaled up past reason, the way a forest fire is the same thing as a candleflame.

Someone had done to four villages what I did to one mind at a time. Someone with my training, or older than my training. Or—

Or something that had been eating this land's remembering since before the Guild had a name.

I camped that night in a hay barn rather than sleep among the hollowed villagers, and I did not sleep much anyway. Every time I closed my eyes I saw the woman at the well reaching for her name and finding the shelf bare. And beneath the fear, quieter and more terrible, sat a question I couldn't put down:

*Who keeps their ledger?*

Every thief keeps accounts. Even the wildest of us, even the ones who claim they steal for art, track what they've taken somewhere—in a head, in a book, in scars. Four villages of stolen selves meant four villages' worth of entries in some ledger I had never seen.

And my own book had an entry in my own hand, dated tomorrow, that I didn't remember writing.

I reached the ward-line at noon on the fourth day, and I understood immediately why the maps called it the Veil.

In life, the border wards had been a lattice of woven light, tall as cathedral spires, humming along the whole northern edge of the kingdom—a wall made of agreement, the old texts said, the settled consensus of a nation that here is the border and beyond it is elsewhere. You couldn't see through it so much as believe through it. Crossing legally meant walking an archway where the light parted like a curtain acknowledging you.

It was dead now. All of it, as far as I could see in either direction, dead as cut strings.

The spires still stood—great pillars of gray crystal gone dark and foggy, like eyes filmed with cataract. Frost crawled up them in patterns that almost spelled letters and refused to. Where the light-curtain should have shimmered between them there was only air, and the air felt wrong, thin, like a room where a fire has just gone out. Sound behaved strangely. My horse's hooves made no echo. When I spoke aloud—"Just checking"—my voice fell flat a foot from my lips.

I left the horse tethered at the last farmstead, whose occupants had smiled at me with those same gentle vacant faces, and I walked through the gap alone.

The moment I crossed, the world changed texture.

It's hard to describe to anyone who hasn't handled raw memory for a living. Inside the wards, a mind sits in its own house. Outside—or rather, here, where the wards had *been*—the house had no walls. Memory moved like weather. I could feel it happening around me, feel thoughts being pulled gently out of the landscape the way heat pulls out of a stone at dusk. Somewhere ahead, something was drinking.

Not feeding. *Drinking.* Slowly. Patiently. With a connoisseur's palate.

I walked a mile through fields where the crops grew in perfect rows and nobody had harvested them, because harvesting requires deciding today matters enough to act on tomorrow, and tomorrow had been taken. I walked past a chapel whose bell hung silent, past a milestone whose carved distance had been worn smooth—not by weather, I realized, stopping cold. By touching. Hundreds of hands, running over the numbers until they vanished, because the numbers were the last thing each passing villager had tried to hold onto.

At the top of the next hill stood the warden's station.

Warden stations were supposed to be garrisons—twenty men, signal beacons, a chain of command reaching back to the capital. This one was a ruin of routine. The beacon tower stood unlit. In the yard, a dozen soldiers in the king's gray sat or stood in attitudes of interrupted duty, their weapons stacked neatly, their faces wearing the same soft vacancy I'd seen in the villages. One was trying to write a report. I watched him for a long time. He dipped his pen, wrote a single word, stared at it, dipped his pen, wrote the same word again. The page held the word forty times over:

*who*

*who*

*who*

But in the watchroom at the top of the tower, someone was still fighting.

I heard him before I saw him—a voice reciting, hoarse and rhythmic, the way a man recites when reciting is the only rope keeping him from falling. I climbed the stairs quietly, hand on my knife out of habit, and found him in the beacon room: an old warden in a gray coat gone to rags, chained by his own hand to the beacon mechanism, a length of rope knotted around his wrist and the ironwork. His lips were cracked and bleeding. His eyes, when they found me, were the first eyes in four days that still had anyone home behind them.

"Name," he rasped, before I could speak. "Give me a name. Any name. Say it and make me say it back. Quickly."

"Emrys," I said.

"*Emrys.*" He said it like a man swallowing medicine. "Emrys. Emrys. Good. Strong name. Holds well." He sagged against the rope. "Mine's Aldous Verren. Warden-Commander, Third Veil Station. Write it down. Do you have paper? Write it down, girl, before I lose it too."

I had paper. I wrote *Aldous Verren, Warden-Commander, Third Veil Station* while he watched the letters like a starving man watches bread.

"How long have you been up here?" I asked.

"I don't know. Days. Weeks. Time goes strange when you're holding on." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "You want to know how I kept myself? Rope and repetition. Chained myself to the beacon so my body couldn't wander off and leave me. Recited my name, my mother's name, my sister's name, the names of every man in my garrison, over and over. A name spoken is a name anchored. Old warden's trick." His gaze drifted to the window, down to his soldiers in the yard. "Didn't work for them. They wouldn't do the exercise. Called it superstition." His voice broke. "Bram was thirty years in the service. He called it superstition."

"What happened here?" I asked. "When the wards fell. What came through?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh something—whether I was safe to tell, whether telling would cost him the last of himself.

"You're a thief," he said finally. Not an accusation. An identification. "I can smell the vials on you. Guild?"

"Ledger-keeper," I said.

"Worse," he muttered. "Fine. Fine. You crossed the dead Veil alone, so you're either brave or doomed, and it makes no difference to me which." He pulled himself upright, rope creaking. "Nothing came through. That's what I need you to understand. We were trained for invasion. Tunnels, ladders, sorcery, armies. We drilled for it every season of my life. And when the Veil went down, I stood on that wall ready to die defending the kingdom, and what came through the gap was—" his hands opened, empty— "*nothing*. A wind that smelled like the inside of an empty house. And Bram looked at me and asked me who I was. Asked *me*. Thirty years, and he'd lost the word *warden* before he lost the word *bread*."

"The villages—"

"Aren't damaged. That's the horror of it. They're *harvested*. Clean work. Selective. Everything taken, nothing wasted." He gripped my wrist suddenly, hard, with the strength of desperation. "I've served the crown forty years. I know what the Veil was. Everyone thinks it kept enemies out. It never kept anything out. It was a *lid*, girl. A lid on a pot. And whatever's underneath has been sipping at the edges for generations—little leaks, little losses, the sort of forgetting folk blame on age—and the lid held it polite. Held it *rationed*." His breath whistled. "Three nights ago the lid came off. And it isn't rampaging. Rampaging is for animals. It's dining."

My skin crawled. Because I knew about bargains. I knew about lids. I had spent my life balancing books full of impossible trades, and every instinct I owned was telling me that the Veil had never been a defense at all—that it had been a *term of contract*, and somewhere in a ledger I was sworn to keep, the terms were written down.

"Why are you still alive?" I asked softly. "Why does it leave you?"

"Because I'm bitter, I think." He grinned with bloody teeth. "Memory tastes worse with vinegar in it. Or—" and here his face changed, went grave and strange— "or because it wants a witness. Some things like to be witnessed. Some things *insist* on it."

He coughed then, a deep wet sound, and I saw the blood on his lips and understood the rope around his wrist had been holding more than his body. He was dying. Whatever the drinking-wind had taken from everyone else, it had taken differently from him—taken slowly, while he fought, and the fight itself had spent him.

"There's something you have to carry," he said. "I'd give it to a soldier, but my soldiers are gone. I'd give it to a priest, but the priests forgot God's name two villages back. So it goes to a thief, and may the saints forgive the arrangement."

"With pleasure," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it, and appreciated it anyway.

He fumbled at his collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small copper token, stamped with the sigil of the Wardens-General: a tower under seven stars. Except the stars had been scratched out—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven deliberate gouges, old ones, decades old by the look of the wear.

"This was mine, and before me it was my commander's, and before him his, back and back," Verren said. "Every warden of the Veil carries one. We're told it's a badge of office. We're told it means the crown sees us." His hand closed around it, knuckles white. "We're lied to. It's a *key*. And it opens something under the palace, and every warden who ever learned that died of learning it, quiet-like, except the ones who gouged out the stars first so they'd forget why it mattered."

"Then why keep handing it down?"

"Because somebody has to remember," he whispered. "Somebody always has to remember. That's the whole cursed job. That's the whole cursed *job*." He pressed the token into my palm and folded my fingers over it, and his hands were freezing. "Listen to me. Listen. It's going to try to take your name soon—you crossed the line, it's already tasting you, you'll feel it tonight, a pulling, like a hook behind your navel. Don't fight it with anger. Anger burns hot and fast. Fight it the way I did. Repetition. Rote. Say your name till it's a hammer, say it till it's boring, boring is armor—"

"I will."

"And when you get to the capital—when you stand in front of the throne—" His grip tightened. His eyes were going glassy at the edges, the vacancy creeping in like frost across a window, and he knew it, and he was racing it. "When you stand in front of the throne, remember that the man sitting on it is not—"

He stopped.

His face didn't go slack all at once. It went slack the way a lamp goes out—flickering, guttering, one last flare of the person he was, and in that flare he spent his final strength on six words, forcing them out through whatever was closing over him like water:

"The crown remembers. Make it confess."

Then Aldous Verren, Warden-Commander of the Third Veil Station, forty years in the service, looked at me with kind, empty eyes and said, politely, "I'm sorry—do I know you? It's terribly rude, but I seem to have forgotten. Would you like tea? I think there's tea."

I stayed with him an hour. I gave him tea. I wrote his name on paper and read it to him twice, and both times it slid off him like rain off wax, and both times he thanked me warmly, the way the woman at the well had thanked me, and I understood that I was watching the exact shape of my own future if I failed.

Before I left, I stole one thing from him. Just one. Against my own rule, because I could never give it back—he was past receiving—but I told myself the rule was already broken, the whole world's rules were broken, and besides, someone had to remember.

I took his memory of his sister. Small, warm, worn smooth by forty years of revisiting: a girl on a dock, laughing, holding up a fish too big for her arms, calling him a name only she used.

I sealed it in a vial and carried it down the stairs past his soldiers, past the man writing *who* forty times, out through the harvested fields to where my horse waited.

That night, camped in the ruins of the Veil with the copper token heavy in my pocket and the stars overhead burning black as burnt-out coals, I felt the pull he'd promised me—a gentle hook behind my navel, a whisper at the edge of my mind suggesting, reasonably, that names were such a fuss, such an bother, really, and wouldn't it be restful to set mine down just for a moment—

"Emrys," I said aloud. "Emrys Joubert. Emrys Joubert. Emrys Joubert."

Boring is armor. I said it until dawn.

And when the sun rose I turned south, toward the capital, toward the palace, toward the throne where a man who was not a man sat wearing a kingdom like a stolen coat—and I understood, with the clarity that comes after terror, exactly where I had to go next.

Because the token was a key, and keys belong to locks, and I was a ledger-keeper, and somewhere in the Guild's great vault of redacted pages was the entry that explained why every warden's badge bore seven stars that someone, sixty years ago, had been desperate enough to scratch away.

The crown remembers, the dying man had said.

Well. So did I.

For now.
