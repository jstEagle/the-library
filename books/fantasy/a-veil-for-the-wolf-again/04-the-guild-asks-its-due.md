## 4. The Guild Asks Its Due

The summons came the way the Guild's summons always came: quietly, and too late to matter.

Emrys found it at dawn, folded into her ledger where no one could have put it without her noticing—*no one should have been able to put it there without her noticing*—a slip of grey vellum bearing the seal of the Guildmaster's office. A wax wolf, veiled. The old mark. It had not been used in years, because it was only used for matters the Guild wished to pretend had never happened.

She stood in her doorway a long time, holding it, while the city behind her went about its morning as if nothing were wrong. That was the strangest part of the night without stars: how completely the morning refused to acknowledge it. Bakers had baked. Fishwives argued over eels. Children ran hoops down the Tallow Steps, shrieking, their shadows blue-edged from lamps that still burned wrong—a cold, drowned color, like moonlight through deep water.

But the sky was empty. She had checked. Every hour through the small hours she had leaned out her window and looked up into that vast unmarked black, waiting for even one star to remember itself, and none had. The sky was a page with all its writing scraped away. And she was a woman who kept ledgers for a living. She knew what a scraped page meant. It meant someone had wanted the words gone badly enough to ruin the page for them.

The summons said: *Come before the veil is lifted. Come alone. Come having forgotten nothing.*

That last line was the one that frightened her. Guildmaster Vess did not waste ink on reassurances. If she wrote *having forgotten nothing*, it was because someone else would have preferred Emrys to arrive empty.

* * *

The guildhouse of the Guild of Impossible Things sat on Cindermarrow Lane like a lie told so often it had become architecture: narrow, unremarkable, three floors of shuttered windows above a shopfront that sold, officially, rare books and unofficially, nothing at all. The door was hung with a veil of grey silk, older than the city's oldest quarter, and beneath it the threshold hummed against Emrys's senses like a struck tuning fork. Wards. Old ones. Older than the border wards that had fallen two nights ago—and unlike those, these still held.

Inside, the air smelled of candlewax, dust, and the particular ozone tang of stored memory, which collectors described as *rain on hot slate* and which Emrys privately thought smelled more like the moment just before crying.

The clerks' hall was nearly deserted. She passed the long tables where journeymen usually bent over trade-books, cataloguing the day's impossible commerce—a bottled lullaby here, a debt paid in shadows there—and found only one junior scribe, a boy named Pell who still flinched when spoken to. He was stacking ledgers with the frantic tidiness of someone who has been told to clear a room.

"Everyone's been sent out?" Emrys asked.

Pell didn't look up. "Guildmaster's orders. Whole house emptied since midnight." He hesitated, then, quieter: "They took the archive carts out the back. Six of them. I've never seen more than two leave at once."

"Where did they go?"

"I wasn't told." He said it in the flat tone of a man who had decided not to know, which in the Guild was a survival skill and sometimes an art form. Then his eyes flicked up, briefly, and there was something in them—not quite warning, not quite pity. "Ledger-keeper Joubert. Your copy. You keep your own copy, don't you? Of the master ledger?"

"You know I do."

"Don't bring it," he whispered. "Whatever happens in there—don't let her see what's written in yours."

Before Emrys could ask what in the nine hells that meant, the inner door opened on its own, the way it did when Vess wanted you to feel invited rather than summoned, and the grey light beyond was already waiting.

* * *

Vess received her in the Veiled Room, the chamber where the Guild conducted the business it never admitted to conducting. No windows. A single table of black oak, older than the guildhouse around it, its surface scarred by rings where countless candles had burned through the nights of countless bad decisions. Two chairs. Vess occupied one; the other sat empty, angled toward the door like a courtesy or a trap.

The Guildmaster was a small woman made almost entirely of angles and patience, silver-haired, dressed in clerk's grey with no ornament except the signet of office—a wolf, unveiled. Her hands rested flat on the table, and Emrys noticed, with the part of her mind that never stopped taking inventory, that those hands were perfectly still. Vess's hands were never still. They moved when she talked, counted when she listened, drummed when she lied. Stillness meant she had rehearsed this.

"Sit, Emrys."

"Guildmaster."

"You received my summons."

"I received your summons." Emrys sat but kept her spine straight, her hands visible, her ledger-weight absent from her coat in obedience to a boy's whisper she hadn't yet decided whether to trust. "I also received a night without stars, dead wards along the whole northern march, and an entry in my own book dated tomorrow. Forgive me if I skip the pleasantries."

Something flickered across Vess's face—there and gone, quick as a pickpocket. "You read the entry."

"I wrote the entry. Apparently. In a hand that's mine and isn't." Emrys leaned forward. "Entry four thousand eleven. *The crown remembers. Return what was borrowed.* Dated tomorrow. And underneath, in the margin, a number I don't recognize and can't stop recognizing: sixty-one."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as it cooled, counting seconds neither of them wanted counted.

"You crossed the wards," Vess said at last. Not a question.

"I walked the northern road to Hallowmere and back. Or to where Hallowmere was." Emrys kept her voice level, but the images rose anyway: the village standing intact and utterly vacant, doors open, meals rotting on tables, a loom half-threaded with wool gone stiff in the damp. People who had forgotten their own names wandering the fields like ghosts who hadn't been told they'd died. The dying warden at the boundary stone, pressing the copper token into her palm, blood on his lips, whispering it like a prayer or a curse: *the crown remembers.* "Three hundred souls, Guildmaster. Maybe more. Nobody dead. Everybody *gone*. Their names, their mothers' faces, the words for bread and grief—all of it lifted clean out of them. What did that?"

Vess looked at her for a long moment, and Emrys watched the Guildmaster make a decision the way she'd seen her make a hundred others: completely, and with visible cost.

"What do you know of the founding bargain?" Vess asked.

"The Guild trades in impossibilities. We brokered the peace after the Succession Wars—the memory-for-memory accords, the tithe of forgetting, all of it standard history."

"Standard history." Vess almost smiled. It was not a warm expression. "Yes. That is precisely what it was designed to be. Standard. Unexamined. A story with its seams turned inward." She drew a breath. "Every copy of the master ledger contains the full record of the Guild's dealings with the crown. Every copy but one entry. Entry sixty-one. Redacted in every transcription for sixty years, in every archive, every clerk's hand-copy, every backup sealed in salt and iron. Do you understand the expense of that? Sixty years of scribes burning midnight oil to make certain that one line stayed blank?"

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "Except mine."

"Except yours." Vess turned her signet ring once, twice—a tell, finally, the stillness cracking. "Your copy was made the year you were indentured. You were twelve. You copied the master ledger yourself, line by line, as all initiates do. And entry sixty-one came through your hand *unredacted*, because the redaction is not ink, Emrys. It is a working. A forgetting laid over paper. And it failed for you. It has failed for you every day for twenty-two years, and until two nights ago, we did not know why."

"Why?" The word came out smaller than Emrys intended.

"That," said Vess, "is precisely what we cannot afford to learn. Which brings me to the reason you are here." She reached beneath the table and set something on the oak between them: a second ledger, bound in the same oxblood leather as Emrys's own, but heavier, older, its corners cased in iron. "This is the true master. The first hand. And I am going to show you entry sixty-one—one time, at this table, under conditions—because a Guildmaster who hides the fire from the person standing in the smoke is a fool, and I have been many things but never that."

She opened the book.

Emrys had spent her life among records. She knew every trick of concealment the Guild taught and several it didn't: palimpsests, sympathetic inks, memories knotted into margins. But she had never seen a redaction like this. The page was not blotted or scraped. The space where entry sixty-one should have been simply *wasn't*—not blank, not black, but a place her eyes slid off of, a hole in the reading the way Hallowmere was a hole in the world. Except—

Except that now, looking directly, letting her thief's attention settle into the gap like a lockpick finding the pin, she could feel its shape. The way a missing tooth aches. The way a stolen memory leaves a wound in the mind that remembers being wounded even when it can't remember why.

And the shape of the gap was familiar. Horribly, intimately familiar. It was the same shape as the silence where her childhood should have been. The same dimensions as the absence where a mother's face should have lived.

"Read it," Vess said quietly. "Once. Then we will discuss what you are to forget."

* * *

Later, Emrys would try to reconstruct exactly what she had seen in those few seconds, and she would find that memory, treacherous thing, had already begun smoothing the edges. But some fragments held like shrapnel:

A date. Sixty-one years past, in the reign of the old king—the *true* king, though the entry's phrasing made her stomach drop through the floor: *in the last year of the king's natural life.*

A transaction. Not a trade. A *surrender*. The Guild of Impossible Things, acting as broker, receiving custody of something on behalf of the crown. The item column did not say *object*. It said *name*—and then a word that her mind refused to carry out of the room, a word that dissolved off the page even as she read it, leaving only the burn of having read it.

A price. Paid not in coin but in remembering. *The realm shall keep its peace, and the crown shall keep its hunger fed, and the Guild shall hold the key in trust.* Fed. The word *fed* sat in the entry like a fly in amber.

And at the bottom, in the founder's own hand, the clause that made everything else make sense and nothing else bearable: *Let no copy speak it. Let no keeper know it. Let the ledger-keeper who copies this page forget it by morning—for her own sake most of all.*

"For her own sake," Emrys repeated aloud. Her voice sounded far away, like someone speaking through the veil on the guildhouse door. "You've known. All these years. You've known what the crown is, what it eats, what happened to Hallowmere—and you've spent sixty years making sure nobody knows, including the one person whose own book won't stay quiet about it."

"I have spent sixty years keeping a truce," Vess said. "The alternative to that truce was a war the realm could not survive. You saw the borderlands, Emrys. That was the entity *hungry*—starving, its feeding interrupted for the first time in six decades. Now imagine it *fed*, and *free*, and furious. Imagine what it did to the queen's court in the year it was bound, except unbound, and everywhere, and awake." She closed the master ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "The stars going dark, the wards falling—that is not an attack. It is an appetite waking up. And the only thing standing between it and the whole world is the bargain this Guild brokered, and the silence that maintains it."

"So you'll maintain the silence. Over three hundred hollow people. Over a sky with nothing left in it."

"I will do what keeps the most people whole," Vess said, "which is the job. It is not a noble job. It was never meant to be." She straightened, and the Guildmaster looked out from behind her eyes again, patient and angular and immovable. "Which brings us to the last matter. You have read what you have read. Under the terms of the founding bargain, no keeper may retain knowledge of entry sixty-one. The working will be applied tonight. You will sleep, and wake, and the shape of the gap will be smooth, and you will go back to balancing impossible trades and never think of it again."

"No."

It was out of her mouth before she had decided to say it. Vess raised one silver eyebrow—the closest thing to surprise she permitted herself.

"No," Emrys said again, steadier. "You want me to forget. Fine. That's the rule, and rules are the only thing holding this profession together—I've built my whole life on them. But I'm owed the choice. Because here's what I know, Guildmaster, that you apparently don't, or won't say: the redaction doesn't work on me. It *never worked on me*. Twenty-two years, and my copy stayed clean. So whatever forgetting you lay on me tonight, I'll wake up tomorrow with a ledger on my shelf that says otherwise. Again. And I'll stand in this room again. And you'll have spent your working for nothing, except teaching me that when the sky falls, the Guild's first instinct is to blind its own."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind Vess's composure that looked almost like grief.

"You think I don't know that?" the Guildmaster said softly. "Why do you imagine your copy exists at all, child? Why do you imagine a twelve-year-old initiate was given the master ledger to copy alone, unsupervised, on that particular year?" She shook her head slowly. "There is so much you don't remember asking. Go home, Emrys. Sleep. Let the working take what it takes. And if tomorrow you find yourself unable to stay away from entry sixty-one—" her eyes dropped, briefly, to Emrys's empty coat pocket, where the copper token sat hidden against her ribs, and Emrys's blood went cold, because Vess could not have known about the token, could not have, and yet— "then perhaps it was never the redaction that was failing."

Emrys walked out through the empty clerks' hall, past Pell stacking his ledgers like a man building a wall against a flood, out under the veiled door and the starless sky. The lamps still burned blue. The city still pretended.

She did not go home. She walked until the guildhouse was blocks behind her, then stopped in the mouth of an alley and took out the copper token, turning it in the drowned light. On one side, the royal arms. On the other, worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had worried it for years, a single engraved word in the old court hand:

*Amaranth.*

Not a word she knew. Not a word she'd ever learned.

And yet her hand, entirely without instruction, had already begun memorizing how to spell it.
