## 5. A Thief Who Steals Herself

I did not sleep.

Sleep is a ledger too—a settling of accounts between the day and the dark—and I could not bear to close the books on what Vess had ordered me to forget. So instead I sat in my rooms above the guildhouse with every candle I owned lit, and I laid the ledgers out across my floor like a spread of tarot cards, and I began to hunt a ghost through sixty years of ink.

The Guild of Impossible Things keeps its records in triplicate. One copy for the Master's vault, one for the archive beneath the counting-house, one—the working copy—for whoever holds the ledger-keeper's post. The bargain with the crown appeared in all three. In all three, it had been redacted: not torn out, not burned, but *unwritten*, the way only a memory-thief can unwrite a thing. Someone had reached into the paper itself and lifted the entry out whole, leaving behind that smooth grey absence I knew too well from the other side of the trade. A theft leaves a shape. Even when you take everything—even the remembering of the taking—you leave the outline of where the thing used to sit.

Three copies. Three identical absences. Which meant three thieves, or one thief working three times, or—

Or one thief who had done it once, and the theft had been copied forward like a stain pressed into wet cloth.

I turned pages until my fingers were grey with handling. The redaction sat in the ledger's deep past, in the reign of the old king's grandfather, and beside the absence someone—Vess, or Vess's predecessor—had written only: *Terms as agreed. See sealed annex.* There was no sealed annex. There never is. That is what a sealed annex means in Guild accounting: a thing we have agreed not to know.

But here was the thought that kept me at the candles long past midnight, the thought with teeth:

Redactions are trades. Nothing in this house is taken without something given back—that was my rule, my one rule, the line I had drawn in myself so long ago I'd forgotten drawing it. *Never steal a memory you can't give back.* And a redaction of this size, an entry spanning years of Guild history, would have required a vessel. Memory does not vanish. It goes somewhere. It goes into someone.

I looked down at the three grey absences spread across my floorboards, and then, slowly, because I am a coward before I am anything else, I looked at my own hands.

*Where did it go?*

* * *

The Guild teaches its children anatomy before it teaches them theft. You cannot lift a memory if you don't know the shelves it sits on, and so by twelve I could recite the architecture of a mind the way a mason recites load-bearing walls. Early childhood lives shallow and bright, near the surface, easy to reach and dangerous to disturb—it is all foundation stone. Deeper lie the load-bearing memories: first thefts, first griefs, the moments that hold up the rest of the house. Take those carelessly and the whole structure sags. I had seen it happen. I had seen a thief named Aldery pull one afternoon out of his own head—one afternoon, one—and spend the rest of his life unable to remember why he flinched at church bells.

So I knew how to read a mind the way I read a ledger. And sitting there among my candles, I did the thing I had never once done in nineteen years of professional forgetting.

I opened my own books.

Memory-tasting is delicate work even on strangers. On yourself it is like trying to see the back of your own head in a mirror made of water. But I had a trick—I had always had a trick, though I'd never asked myself where I learned it. I set a candle before me and watched the flame, and I let the flame be the present, and I walked backward along its light the way you walk backward down a corridor, hand trailing the wall.

My childhood should have been there. Everyone's is. Even the unhappiest childhood is a crowded room: a mother's voice, a street's name, a smell of bread or smoke or river-mud, some face bent over your cradle. Mine was—

Mine was a room with the furniture gone.

Not empty. Empty would have been something. It was *furnished with absence*. I reached for my mother and found the shape a mother leaves: the height of a shoulder I had rested against, the register of a voice pitched low over a fever, the specific callus on a thumb that wipes your cheek. All the impressions of her. None of her. No name. No face. No moment. It was like knowing a song by the silence after it ends.

I sat very still. The candle flame leaned and righted itself.

*All right,* I told myself, in the tone I used with frightened clients. *All right. Grief does this. Time does this. Minds shed.*

Minds shed the details. Minds do not shed the *person*.

I went deeper, walking backward down the corridor, and came to the place where my first theft should have been. Every thief remembers their first. It is our shared scripture, the story told over and over in the common room until the newest recruit can recite their elders' first thefts better than their own. Corvane, they said, stole his sister's fear of the dark and spent forty years trying to give it back. Old Maren lifted her father's dying words and wore them like a stone around her neck. Every thief has one. Every thief remembers the room, the victim, the price, the taste of it—the way a first theft tastes of copper and candle-smoke and shame.

I reached for mine.

And the corridor ended.

There was no wall, no door, no locked room. There was simply a place where the corridor stopped being a corridor, smooth and grey and seamless, and beyond it nothing—not darkness, which is a thing, but *nothing*, which is the theft-shaped absence I had been staring at all night in three ledgers on my floor.

I knew that absence. I had felt it under my fingers a hundred times, in a hundred minds, the particular texture of a professional job. Amateurs tear. Professionals leave a clean edge. This was the cleanest edge I had ever touched, and it was inside my own skull, and it was shaped exactly—*exactly*, down to the dimensions, down to the weight—like the missing entry in the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things.

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the guildhouse clock counted three, indifferent as arithmetic.

Someone had edited me.

Not robbed me—robbery is violence, robbery leaves bruises and broken shelving. This was *editing*. This was a thief of enormous skill reaching into the house of my mind and removing two rooms entire: my mother, and my first theft. Removing them so cleanly that I had lived nineteen years without ever noticing the floor plan was wrong. Without noticing that I was a woman with no origin, a ledger-keeper whose earliest entry was blank, a thief who could not remember becoming one.

And the worst of it—the part that made me put my hand flat on the floorboards to feel something solid—was that I recognized the craftsmanship.

Because I knew only one person alive who worked that clean.

I had watched Vess take a traitor's confession once, years ago, in the vault below the counting-house. She had done it almost tenderly, the way you close a book you've finished reading. And afterward the traitor had sat blinking in his chair, whole and calm and missing eleven years, asking pleasantly what everyone was looking at him for.

I had admired it. Gods forgive me, I had stood in the corner holding the lamp and I had *admired* it.

* * *

Dawn came up grey over the rooftops, and grey suited me fine. I dressed like a woman going to market, plain wool, hood, the face of nobody, and I went out into a city that was still pretending the night without stars hadn't happened. The lamps had burned blue till morning; now they flickered uncertainly, as if unsure whether daylight was real. People moved fast and spoke little. At the corner of Tallow Row a fishwife was arguing with her own daughter about a name—*that's not your name, girl, I named you myself*—and the daughter's face was smooth and polite and utterly blank, and the mother was crying without any sound at all, the way you cry when you've already begun to understand that shouting won't help.

I walked past. I told myself I couldn't help her. I told myself I had my own accounts to settle. Both things were true, and neither one made my legs feel less like running.

The archive beneath the counting-house opens to ledger-keepers at any hour; that privilege is older than I am and I had never once abused it. I went down the spiral stair into the cold, past shelves of trades going back two centuries—bottled lullabies, debts paid in shadows, a widow's grief exchanged for a stranger's courage—and I found the intake registers for the year of my apprenticeship.

Here is what no one tells you about investigating yourself: every document is a mirror. I looked up my own intake record expecting the ordinary dryness of Guild paperwork, and there I was, *Emrys Joubert, age nine, admitted by*, and the admitting officer's name had been inked over in the old style—not crossed out, which leaves a trace, but washed, dissolved, the letters lifted off the page like stains drawn out of linen with salt.

Nine years old. Admitted by no one. From nowhere.

I pulled the registers for the surrounding years. Every child the Guild took in had a provenance: an orphanage, a debt, a parent who sold them honestly or dishonestly, a street, a parish, a *something*. I traced the column with my finger, down and down, and my row sat in the middle of it like a missing tooth—everyone else accounted for, everyone else *from* somewhere, and me a gap with a name attached.

A hole shaped exactly like the missing entry. Again. The same craftsman's edge.

I stood in the cold archive with my finger on my own blankness and I understood, finally, the thing I had been circling all night. It wasn't just that someone had edited me. It was the *pattern* of it. My mother, gone. My first theft, gone. My origin, gone. And in the ledgers, the crown-bargain, gone—gone in the same hand, with the same tenderness, the same clean edges.

Two entries. Two vessels. Or rather—

One entry, and one vessel, and the vessel was me.

The bargain with the crown had needed somewhere to live when they unwrote it. Not paper. Paper can be re-read. They had needed a living archive, something that would carry the stolen years without knowing it carried them, something loyal and blind and close at hand. They had needed a ledger-keeper who would never open her own books.

They had needed a thief who steals herself and never feels the knife.

I laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, and echoed off the shelves of impossible trades, and I clapped my hand over my mouth like a child. Because here was the joke, the beautiful, obscene joke of it: I had built my whole life on one rule. *Never steal a memory you can't give back.* I had prided myself on it. I had refused a client the king's childhood over it. And all the while I was the Guild's greatest violation of its own ethics, walking around in plain clothes, keeping their books, balancing their accounts—holding sixty years of the darkest bargain in the kingdom's history somewhere behind my eyes, in rooms I couldn't find, in a house whose floor plan had been redrawn while I slept.

*You are the sealed annex.*

That was why Vess had summoned me last night. Not because I'd discovered the redaction—she'd known I would eventually, it was practically my job—but because the redaction was *me*, and the stars going out meant the terms were changing, and she had needed to look me in the eye and order me to forget what I read, knowing full well that the most important part of what I'd read was already inside me, patient as buried coin.

She hadn't ordered me to forget the truth. She'd ordered me to keep doing it.

I climbed the spiral stair on legs that belonged to someone else. Above, the city was waking into its second starless day, and the blue lamps were dying one by one in the morning fog, and somewhere out there a princess was keeping a hollow king company through another looped and borrowed day. I had thought my invisible life was ending because the wards fell. Now I understood it had ended decades ago, at age nine, in a room I couldn't remember, at the hands of someone whose craft I had spent my whole career admiring.

There was one more thing to check. One last account to audit. I went back to my rooms, sat before the cold hearth, and did the hardest thing I have ever done in a life suddenly full of hard things.

I tasted my own grief.

I went looking for the mourning. Because a girl who loses her mother—even a girl who doesn't know she has lost her, even a girl whose mother has been unwritten—leaves a residue. Grief is sticky. It gets into the walls. Somewhere in me there should be an unexplained sorrow, a nameless ache, the way Aldery flinched at bells.

I found it immediately. Of course I did. It was everywhere. It was the loneliness I had called a temperament. It was the reason I kept candles burning all night in an empty room. It was the ache under every quiet evening, the sense—lifelong, familiar, worn smooth as a river stone—that I had lost something enormous and been forbidden to turn around and look at it.

I sat with my nameless grief in my lap like a foundling, and outside the window the fog thickened, and far away toward the palace a bell began to ring with the ragged, urgent rhythm that means *the court summons, the court summons, come at once.*

They had left me the grief. They had taken everything else and left me the grief, because grief is what keeps a vessel from asking questions. A woman with an unexplained sorrow doesn't dig. She just burns candles against the dark and calls it her nature.

Well. I had audited my own soul, and the books did not balance, and I was done being a vessel.

I took down my hood, and I looked at my face in the black glass of the window—a face with no mother behind it, no first theft, no beginning—and I said aloud, to the woman there, in a voice that shook:

"Then we go get ourselves back."

The bell rang on. And beneath its summons, faint but unmistakable, I heard the second bell—the one rung only when the Guildmaster wants a thief brought in, not sent out.

Vess knew I'd been in the archive. Vess always knew.

I had perhaps an hour before her people came to finish the edit. An hour to decide which I trusted less: the woman who had unmade me, or the thing wearing the crown that had made her do it.

I blew out the candles—all of them, every one, for the first time since the stars went dark—and stepped into the fog.
