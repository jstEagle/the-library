## 6. The King's Empty Eyes

The palace hired a memory-taster twice a year, in spring and autumn, and had done so for longer than anyone could remember—which was precisely the kind of sentence that made Emrys's teeth ache these days.

She went in under the name Wren Calloway, third daughter of a wine-merchant house in the low city, apprenticed to the tasting guild at fourteen. The papers were flawless because she had written them herself, and then she had stolen them from herself: walked into the records office of the Tasters' Charter with a borrowed face and lifted the ink of her own forgery out of the ledger and set it back down again, so that the entry read *Wren Calloway, admitted* in a hand three decades old. A small theft. A perfect one. No one missed what they never knew they'd had.

The gate guards barely looked at her. That was the trouble with palaces, Emrys thought as she passed beneath the portcullis with her tasting kit—silver spoons, glass vials stoppered with wax, a case lined in velvet worn smooth by other women's hands—trouble being perhaps the wrong word; it was the *gift* of palaces. They were built on ritual, and ritual was just memory wearing livery. Nobody questioned the taster who came when the taster always came.

But she felt the wrongness before she was through the first courtyard.

It was subtle. The fountains ran, but the water sounded rehearsed. The gardeners trimmed hedges whose shapes had been identical for years—she could see the ghost-lines where the shears had passed ten thousand times, each cut laid over the last like brushstrokes varnishing a painting. Two chambermaids crossed the colonnade laughing, and Emrys caught the laugh's edge and held it the way you hold a note of music, and found it was the same laugh, syllable for syllable, that a serving girl had given her in the outer ward. Different girl. Same laugh.

The whole palace was laughing with one mouth.

She stopped by a pillar and pretended to adjust the buckles on her kit, and let her craft open like a second pair of eyelids.

Every place has a taste. Taverns taste of spilled beer and arguments and someone's first kiss against a wall that still remembers. Churches taste of candle-fat and hope thinned over centuries. This place tasted of nothing. Not emptiness—Emrys knew emptiness, had stood in houses where grief had scraped the walls bare—but of *repetition*. Of a day played so many times it had worn grooves into the stone, and everything living here slid along those grooves like a coin circling a wishing well, going around and around, never falling in.

The wards had fallen two nights ago. The stars were still gone. And the heart of the kingdom tasted like a song sung past all meaning.

*Someone has been drinking this place dry*, she thought. *For years.*

"Madam Taster?" A steward had materialized at her elbow, grey-liveried, pleasant-faced, his eyes the flat blue of glazed pottery. "Their Majesties will receive you after the morning meal. You'll take the king's cup first, as always."

"As always," Emrys agreed, and smiled, and followed him, counting doors.

* * *

They brought her to the sunlit gallery where the king took his breakfast, and Emrys saw him and forgot, for three full seconds, every plan she had.

He was not what she had steeled herself to find. She had expected something wrong—a puppet-thing, a waxwork, some horror wearing majesty like an ill-fitting coat. Instead he was a handsome man in his late forties, silver coming in at the temples of his chestnut hair, dressed simply in a wool doublet the color of river stones. He was laughing. He looked up as she entered and his whole face lit, warm as a hearth, and he said:

"The taster! Good. I'm starving, and Isolde says I mustn't eat until you've done your work, though between us—" he leaned toward her conspiratorially, "—I think she enjoys watching me suffer."

And there was the princess, seated at the far end of the table with a cup of tea going cold beside her, watching her father with an expression Emrys couldn't read at any distance.

"Your Majesty honors me," Emrys said, and bowed, and opened her kit.

Here is what a memory-taster actually does, since the court had long ago forgotten even that much, which told Emrys something about how deep the rot ran: she does not taste food. Food is the excuse. What she tastes is the residue—the way strong experiences soak into objects the way wine soaks into oak. A king's breakfast knife carries the flavor of his mornings. If those mornings turned bitter—if fear crept into them, or poison, or despair—it would show in the metal before it ever showed in the man. The taster reads the king's life sideways, through its sediment, and reports anything spoiled.

Emrys lifted the butter-knife and read it, and had to keep her face still through sheer professional discipline.

The knife remembered a morning. It remembered *this* morning—this exact morning, this joke about Isolde and suffering, this quality of light through the east windows, this precise arrangement of honey and bread. And it remembered the same morning yesterday. And the day before. Three hundred and eleven times, the knife reported in its dumb metallic way, the same breakfast, the same joke, the same light. The blade's memory had been wiped clean so many times it had stopped recording new things altogether. It recorded only the groove.

She set it down. Took up the spoon. Same. The goblet: same. The salt cellar carried a fainter echo, and she chased it, and found that the faintest layers went back further—ten years, twenty—and that somewhere around thirty years back the sediment simply *stopped*, like a river cutting off mid-valley, and below that line lay something dark and smooth and hungry-smelling that she did not want to touch with her bare gift.

Thirty years. The wards fell two nights ago, but whatever happened here happened thirty years ago, and it had been happening ever since, patiently, one wiped morning at a time.

"Is everything to Your Majesty's satisfaction?" she asked aloud, because the silence had stretched.

"Oh, splendidly." He tore his bread and offered her half, absurdly, a king feeding his taster. "You know, you have a kind face. Has anyone told you? You remind me of—"

He stopped.

Emrys watched it happen. Watched the warmth stay perfectly intact in his face while everything behind it went looking for something that wasn't there. His eyes moved slightly left, tracking a thought down a corridor that ended in a blank wall. He blinked. The smile returned, seamless, untroubled.

"—of no one," he finished pleasantly. "Of no one at all. Forgive me, my mind wanders in the mornings."

"It's the honey," said Princess Isolde, without looking up from her tea. "Papa believes honey improves the mind. Papa is wrong about this."

"I am *never* wrong about honey," the king said with tremendous dignity, and the moment folded itself away as if it had never existed, and the groove closed over it smoothly as water.

Emrys worked the rest of the reading in silence. Every object said the same thing. She packed her kit with steady hands and asked leave to go, and got four steps toward the door before a voice said, quietly:

"A word, madam Taster. In the orangery."

Not a question. Emrys turned. Princess Isolde had risen; her father was already absorbed in his bread and his loop, smiling at the window as though the light were an old friend he met every single morning for the first time. The princess's face gave away nothing at all—she had her father's warmth stored somewhere out of reach, Emrys thought, banked like coins in a wall.

"Certainly, Highness," Emrys said, and followed her into ruin or revelation, whichever came first.

* * *

The orangery was a long hall of white iron and glass, full of orange trees in tubs and the smell of green growing things, which was the first honest smell the palace had offered her. Sunlight came down in slats. Isolde walked to the middle of it, stopped among the trees, and did not turn around.

"How far back does your charter go?" she asked.

"Highness?"

"The Tasters' Charter. Your guild. How old is it?"

"Four hundred years, Highness. More or less."

"Mm." Isolde touched an orange leaf, absently, the way people touch things when their hands need occupation. "Then you'll know the rites. The old ones. So you'll understand me when I say this plainly: my father has eaten the same breakfast for thirty-one years, and yesterday I watched a guard forget his own mother's face on the walls, and two nights ago every star in the sky went out at once, and you are the first person to walk into this palace in a decade whose presence doesn't taste like the rest of them."

She turned. Her eyes were her father's color but nothing of his warmth—they were winter versions of the same brown, and they went through Emrys like a spear through wet paper.

"You're not a taster," Isolde said. "You're something else wearing a taster. I don't know what. I watched you read the butter-knife and then read the *air above the butter-knife*, which is not in any charter I've studied, and I've studied all of them, because I have had thirty-one years and very little else to do."

The sensible thing—the thing that had kept Emrys invisible for fifteen years—was to deny it. To bow, to apologize, to offer some plausible taster's explanation, to let the princess's suspicion die of politeness.

Instead Emrys heard herself say: "How long have you known your father is hollow?"

Something flickered in Isolde's face—not surprise. Relief, maybe. The terrible relief of a person who has been alone with a weight for so long that another pair of hands under it feels like danger and mercy in the same breath.

"Since I was nine," Isolde said. "I cut my hand in the orchard. Ran to him crying, the way children do. He picked me up, and he kissed my forehead, and he said exactly the right thing, *exactly*, the perfect thing a father should say." Her voice stayed level. Her hands didn't; they had gone still on the orange leaf, gripping it. "And then he said it again. Word for word. With the same pause in the same place. Like a musician repeating a phrase. I stood there bleeding in his arms while my father recited comfort at me, and he never noticed, because noticing requires remembering the first time." She released the leaf. "I told no one. There was no one to tell. Do you know what it is to be the only person in a palace of six hundred souls who knows the king is dead?"

"He's not dead," Emrys said quietly. "I've read dead things. Dead things are quiet. Whatever is in your father isn't empty, Highness. It's *fed*. Something has been eating this court's days for thirty years and leaving the shapes behind. Your father isn't a corpse. He's a—"

"A coat," Isolde said. "Something is wearing him."

They looked at each other across the orange trees, thief and princess, both of them fluent in the language of pretending.

"Why didn't you raise the alarm?" Emrys asked. "You caught me reading him. One word to the steward and I'd be in a cell by now—or worse. Why warn me off and then invite me here?"

"Because you're the first person in thirty-one years to look at my father and be *frightened for him* instead of merely fond." Isolde stepped closer. Sunlight striped her face. "Everyone else sees a beloved king having a pleasant morning. You saw the bars of the cage. And I am tired, madam whoever-you-are. I have kept this court running on ritual and rote since I was sixteen—signing his decrees in his style, holding the feasts at the right hours, feeding the groove so the machine keeps turning—because I believed that if the pattern ever broke, whatever wears him would notice us. Would notice *me*. And I do not know what happens to a kingdom when the thing in the crown stops pretending."

"And now?"

"Now the stars are out and the border wards are ash and forgetting is walking up the high road toward my city." Her jaw tightened. "So the calculus has changed. Keeping the pattern is no longer enough. Someone has broken the world's remembering, and I think it started in this room, thirty-one years ago, and I think you know more than you've said, because when you read the air above that knife you flinched at something thirty years deep, and flinching means recognizing."

Emrys said nothing. The copper token sat in her pocket like a small hot coal—the dying warden's gift, *the crown remembers*—and she thought about ledgers with redacted lines, about holes shaped like mothers, about a Guild that had traded something unspeakable for peace and edited everyone who might remember the price.

"I can't give you answers yet," she said at last. "I can give you honesty about that much: I don't have them. But I know how to look for them, and looking is my trade."

"What is your trade?" Isolde asked. "Truly."

Emrys weighed it. Fifteen years of invisibility pressed down on one side of the scale. On the other side lay a night without stars and a hole in her own head and a princess who had spent her whole life being the only one who remembered.

"I steal memories," Emrys said. "Professionally. And recently I've learned that someone stole mine—including, I believe, whatever I once knew about what happened to your father. Which makes us, Highness, two women robbed by the same thief."

Isolde absorbed that the way she seemed to absorb everything—completely, immediately, without wasted motion. Then: "The royal vaults sit beneath the west wing. Behind the third seal there is a room the stewards' maps mark as 'flooding, disused.' It is not flooding. My grandfather sealed it, and his father before him, and none of the keys I inherited fit it. Whatever happened thirty-one years ago, part of the answer is down there, and I cannot open it, and neither can anyone in this palace, because no one in this palace can remember why it's locked."

"And you want me to steal my way in."

"I want you to restore my father," Isolde said, and for the first time her voice cracked, one hairline fracture across all that control. "Even one true memory. One minute of him knowing me. Give me that, and you can have every vault and every secret in this palace, and my silence, and such protection as a crown can give—even a hollow one."

Through the glass roof, the sky hung starless and pale, an eye rolled back. Somewhere beyond the walls, Emrys knew, the forgetting was walking closer, patient as tide. And here in a room full of orange trees stood the first person in fifteen years who had seen through her and offered a hand instead of a knife.

"One condition," Emrys said. "When I go down to that vault, I go alone. And whatever I find—you'll get the truth of it, all of it. Even if the truth is that your father can't be saved."

Isolde held out her hand. Emrys took it. The princess's grip was cold and did not shake at all.

"Then we understand each other, thief," Isolde said. "Come back tonight, after the court sleeps. The guards change at the third bell, and the third bell," a thin, bleak smile, "is the one thing in this palace that has never once rung late."

Emrys bowed and turned for the door—and paused, because the king had drifted into the orangery doorway behind her, unhurried, pleasant, a man out for his morning stroll through the same morning he had taken for eleven thousand days.

"Lovely flowers," he remarked to no one, gazing at the orange trees. Then his eyes found Emrys, warm and kind and bottomless, and he tilted his head like a bird considering something bright.

"You know," he said, in exactly the voice he had used over breakfast, "you remind me of someone."

And this time, before the groove could close, before the smile could reset—he frowned, very slightly, at something far behind his own eyes, and whispered:

"Why won't you let me remember her?"
