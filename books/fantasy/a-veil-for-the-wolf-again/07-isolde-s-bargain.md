## 7. Isolde's Bargain

I had known for eleven years that my father was not my father.

Not in the way children suspect it—in the feverish, guilty way of a girl who wakes at night and thinks *this man is too kind, too smooth, no one is that kind*—but in the way one knows a portrait is not a person. The thing that wore his face loved me. I never doubted that. It loved me the way a hearth loves a room: evenly, warmly, without ever once asking what the room wanted.

His loop had grown shorter over the years. When I was small, he lived whole weeks before the day reset—a hunt, a council, a feast, the long walk through the rose garden where he would stop and look at the sky as if waiting for something to be written there. Now he lived a single day. He woke at dawn believing it was the morning after my mother's funeral, every morning, forever. He grieved her freshly each sunrise. And each sunrise I sat with him while he wept for a woman whose name he could no longer pronounce correctly, and I told him she had gone somewhere gentle, and he believed me, because he had no reason left not to.

The court believed nothing and everything. That is the trick I learned by fifteen: courts do not need truth, they need rhythm. So I gave them rhythm. The king takes his broth at eight. The king reviews the eastern petition on Tuesdays. The king smiles at the ambassador from Vell and says the word *prosperity* twice. I wrote it all down—not in diaries, diaries can be found, but in the bodies of servants, in the choreography of a hundred small ceremonies until the palace could run for a week without my father saying anything at all beyond *yes*, *good*, *how fine*. Chancellor Maren knew. Old Halvard, captain of the inner guard, knew. They had both stopped sleeping well years ago, but they stayed, because someone had to stay, and I had made myself the someone so thoroughly that I no longer remembered choosing it.

Then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and the lamps burned blue like drowned things, and I understood that rhythm was about to fail me.

* * *

I caught her on the third night, in the Long Gallery, with her hand hovering an inch above my father's temple.

She did not see me at first. She stood very still in the blue lamplight, thin as a candle-snuffer, dressed in the gray livery of a court taster though no taster I had ever hired had eyes like hers—eyes that moved the way accountants' hands move, tallying. My father sat in his chair by the window where he always sat at this hour, watching a sky with no stars in it, and his face wore its evening expression, mild grief folding into mild peace, and the woman's fingers were spread just above his hair like a diviner's over water.

"Step back," I said.

She stepped back. That surprised me. Thieves, in my experience, ran or lied. She did neither; she simply lifted her hand away as if it were a courtesy, and turned, and looked at me with the particular blankness of someone deciding how much of herself to show.

"You're Emrys Joubert," I said. "The memory-taster. You came with the Guild's credentials three days ago."

"I did."

"You've tasted six members of my household since then. My chamberlain. Two maids. The under-captain." I walked toward her slowly, letting my heels sound against the stone, because sound was a weapon I owned and she did not. "You are supposed to taste for tampering. For foreign memories. Instead you sit with the king at night and read him like a ledger."

"He doesn't mind," she said. Then, hearing it, she winced. "That was—"

"Cruel? Yes. Accurate? Also yes." I stopped an arm's length from her. Up close she smelled of candle smoke and cold air, the smell of someone who had been climbing through places she shouldn't. "How much do you know?"

"That depends," said Emrys Joubert, "on how much you already know. Because you didn't call the guards. You sent them away before you came down here—I watched you do it. A princess who protects a thief from her own guard already knows something worth protecting."

We looked at each other across the length of my father's looping grief, and I felt the strange vertigo of recognition—the feeling of meeting someone who has also been carrying a secret so long it has changed her posture. She carried hers like a weight on the shoulders. I carried mine like a stone behind the ribs. Between us, the king sighed in his sleep and murmured my mother's name, wrong, the way he always said it now, vowels softened as if the name were melting.

"Come," I said. "Not here. Walls in this palace have been listening longer than either of us has been alive."

* * *

My mother's old sewing room had been sealed for eleven years. I had unsealed it myself, quietly, the year I turned sixteen, and filled it with the only honest archive in the kingdom: every anomaly, every reset, every hour my father had repeated himself, recorded in my own cramped hand across forty notebooks hidden in the cavities of a disused loom. It was the one room in the palace where I did not have to perform certainty.

Emrys stood among the shelves of notebooks and read their spines the way other people read scripture.

"Eleven years," she said. "You've been keeping records for eleven years."

"Someone had to. The court forgets between resets—it's subtle, they don't lose days, they lose *edges*. An argument with the king becomes a warm impression. A decision becomes a feeling that it was always so. If I didn't write it down, even I would start to believe the loop was life." I pulled the fourth notebook from the loom's hollow and set it in her hands. "Read the entry from the winter I was nine. The first one."

She read. Her lips moved slightly, a habit of the very literate. "*The king asked me today whether I had eaten, and then asked again, four minutes later, and both times his face was exactly the same face, like two prints from one stamp. I checked the stamp theory by dropping a spoon. He flinched identically both times.*"

"I was testing him," I said. "Nine years old, hiding behind a screen, dropping spoons to see if my father was real." I laughed, and it came out wrong, and I let it. "He wasn't. Or he was, and whatever is real about him is folded up very small inside there, and has been for a long time. Longer than me. Halvard remembers a campaign in the north when my father was young—he came back from it different, Halvard says. Quieter. Like a house after the family has moved out."

"The wards fell the same night the stars went dark," Emrys said slowly. "And your father loops on a single day. And something ate the sky." She closed the notebook with exaggerated care, the care of someone handling evidence. "Princess, I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to understand that I ask it professionally, not cruelly. Do you know what your father is?"

"No," I said. "But I know what he isn't. He isn't the man who taught me to ride. He isn't the man who cried at my mother's wedding. Whatever sits on the throne wears him the way the crown sits on his head—perfectly fitted, and utterly indifferent." I took the notebook back. "Now you. You read him tonight. Tasters read surface tremors, residue, the shape of what's been taken. What did you find?"

For a long moment she said nothing. The blue lamplight through the shuttered window gave her face a drowned cast. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its professional smoothness, and what was underneath was younger and much more tired.

"There's a hole in him," she said. "Not emptiness—emptiness I'd know, I work with the emptied. This is a *cut*. Something was removed cleanly, at the root, decades ago, and the wound was cauterized with craft so fine it might be holy. Around the cut, everything heals wrong. His days grow back like scar tissue, the same day, over and over, because the day he lost is the day he can't get past." She looked up at me. "Your father didn't lose his memory, Princess. Someone performed surgery on his soul and took the keystone out. Everything else is the building settling around the gap."

I had waited eleven years for someone to say those words. I had imagined relief arriving like a tide. Instead what arrived was rage—cold, precise, eleven years deep—and I had to turn to the window and put my hand flat against the cold glass before I trusted my voice.

"Who," I said.

"I don't know yet. But I know where the answer is." She hesitated, and I heard her choose honesty over safety, which is a rarer act than courage. "There's a bargain. Old one. The Guild's ledgers carry a redaction sixty years wide, and redactions that size aren't made to hide shame. They're made to hide *terms*. Your kingdom is inside those terms, Princess. I think your father was the price—or the collateral—or the vessel. I don't know which. Yet."

"And the vaults," I said.

Her stillness told me everything before her words did. "You know about the vaults."

"I know my mother went down twice a year, alone, and came up looking like she'd argued with God and lost. I know the third stair from the bottom sings a note no stair should sing. I know that when the wards fell, every lock in that vault opened itself and then locked again, fast, like something down there *flinched*." I turned to face her fully. "Here is my offer, thief. Hear all of it before you speak."

* * *

I laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only currency I had never debased.

"You will have access to the royal vaults—all of them, including my mother's sealed rooms, including whatever the crown keeps below the crown. I will give you my authority in writing, and if the Guild objects, they may bring their objections to me and learn what eleven years of managing a haunted court teaches a woman about negotiation." I held up one finger. "In exchange, you will restore one true memory to my father. One. Not the whole man—I am not fool enough to demand a miracle. One moment. One hour of my actual father, awake behind his own eyes, who knows me, who knows what was done to him, who can tell me—" my voice cracked and I let it, deliberately, because I wanted her to see the cost of what she was being paid in— "who can tell me *who did this to him*, so that I know whether to burn them or bury them or kneel to them."

"One true memory," Emrys repeated. "Do you understand what you're asking? The cut in him isn't a lock I can pick. It's an absence. To restore a memory into that absence I'd have to find the original—which means finding whoever holds it, or finding the thing that took it. Either road goes through the redaction. Through the bargain. Through—" she stopped.

"Through what?"

"Through the crown," she said quietly. "Maybe through the thing wearing it."

"Good," I said.

She stared at me. "Good?"

"You keep pausing before that word as if it should frighten me. Let me tell you what frightens me, Emrys Joubert." I crossed to the loom and drew out another notebook—my oldest, the one with the water-stained cover, the one I had written at nine years old with a shaking hand. "What frightens me is dying as the custodian of a lie. What frightens me is my guards forgetting my face mid-shift, which happened yesterday, to Halvard's own sergeant, who looked at me and called me 'the little queen' and then burst into tears because he didn't know why he was crying. What frightens me is the city forgetting its own name while I stand in a palace running on rote and ritual like a music box winding down." I set the notebook in her hands beside the other. "Whatever wears my father's crown, whatever bargained with your Guild, whatever ate the stars—it has been eating *us* for sixty years, patiently, politely, one memory at a time. And everyone who could have stopped it chose instead to keep the peace. Well. The peace is over. The wards are down. There is no truce left to protect, only people left to save."

She looked down at the two notebooks, mine at nine and mine at twenty, the same handwriting grown steadier and no less frightened. When she raised her head, something had shifted in her face—the accountant's blankness cracking along a fault line I recognized, because I had lived on that fault line for eleven years.

"If I go into those vaults," she said, "and find what I think I'll find, it won't stay buried. Whatever the bargain was, digging it up ends it. There's no version of this where the kingdom gets its memories back and everything else stays comfortable. Comfortable is what the bargain bought."

"Comfortable," I said, "is a word for people who still have something left to lose gently. Look outside, thief. The lamps are blue. The stars are gone. My father lives one day on a wheel. Take the vaults. Take my seal. Take anything I have." I extended my hand. "Bring me back one true hour of my father, and I will spend the rest of my life paying for it."

She took my hand. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested, and colder, and it shook—just once, just slightly, the shake of someone stepping off a ledge.

"One condition of mine," she said. "When I go down, you stay above. Not because I doubt your nerve. Because if what's down there offers people what they want most—and I have reason to believe it does—you're the one person in this palace who cannot afford to be tempted."

"And you can?" I asked.

Her laugh was short and entirely without humor. "Princess, I'm a memory-thief with holes in her own past. Temptation and I are old acquaintances." She gathered the notebooks, tucked them inside her livery against her heart like stolen letters, and moved toward the door. At the threshold she paused, silhouetted against the blue-lit corridor, and said, without turning:

"The third stair. Your mother's rooms. I'll start there tonight."

"How do you know about my mother's rooms?"

She glanced back, and for a heartbeat her eyes were not an accountant's at all but something older and more afraid.

"Because," she said, "when I touched your father tonight, the residue on the cut—the craft that made it—it was signed. Not with a name. With a sigil. And I've seen that sigil once before, in a place I don't remember visiting, next to a word I don't remember reading." Her hand tightened on the doorframe. "It was your mother's sigil, Princess. The queen cut your father. And I think I'm the one who helped her do it."

She was gone before I could speak, her footsteps fading down the corridor toward the stairs that sang, and I stood alone in my mother's sewing room with the loom gaping empty and the blue light crawling across the floor, understanding at last that the story I had been keeping records of for eleven years had never once been about my father—

and that the woman who had just promised me a miracle had just confessed, in the same breath, to being part of the crime.
