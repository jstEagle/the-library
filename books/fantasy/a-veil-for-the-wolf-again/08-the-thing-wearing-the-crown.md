## 8. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The royal vaults sat beneath the palace like a heart beneath a ribcage, and Emrys went down to them the way a thief goes anywhere: quietly, with her hands empty and her mind full of other people's doors.

Isolde's key was not a key at all but a sentence—a line of the old liturgy that the guards at the third landing had been chanting so long they no longer heard it. Emrys spoke it in the princess's cadence, learned in one afternoon of watching Isolde's mouth, and the door drank the words from the air and opened for her as if it had been waiting sixty years to be asked.

Beyond it, the dark was older than the palace above. It smelled of cold stone and tallow and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a locket worn against skin until the metal forgets it was ever anything else.

Emrys lit no lamp. A memory-thief learns early that light is a debt; everything you illuminate sees you back. Instead she walked by touch, fingertips trailing the wall, counting turns the way she counted entries in a ledger—left, left, down, right—and let the dark have its look at her.

*You are expected,* said nothing, in no direction at all.

She stopped. Breathed. The Guild trained its thieves against fear the way farriers train horses: patiently, repeatedly, until the animal learns that spooking costs more than standing still. She stood still.

"I'm always expected somewhere," she said. "It's the price of being good at your work."

The dark did not laugh, but she felt it consider laughing, the way you feel weather change behind a closed window.

The stairs ended at a chamber that should not have existed under a palace of this age—the stone too smooth, the proportions wrong, the ceiling arched in a style the kingdom had never built in. In the center, on a plinth of black glass, sat the crown.

It was smaller than she expected. That was the first cruelty of it. Coins and songs had made it vast, a wheel of gold wide as a wagon, blazing with the wealth of nine generations. This thing on the plinth was a modest circlet, the kind a minor lord might wear to a wedding he resented attending. Its gold was dull. Its stones were cloudy, like eyes going blind.

And it was warm. She could feel the warmth from six feet away, radiating off it like a hearth seen through a doorway on a winter night.

Emrys had broken into banks, monasteries, one lighthouse, and the private reliquary of a saint who turned out to be three con artists in a long coat. She knew every species of trap: the poisoned lock, the weight-sensitive floor, the curse that waited politely for greed to finish walking before it bit. She catalogued this room and found none of them.

That was the second cruelty. There were no traps because none were needed. Nothing stolen could be kept here. Everything in this room already belonged to the thing wearing the crown.

"You're the vault," she said aloud. "Aren't you. The whole room is you."

*Everything is me,* said her own voice, gently, from everywhere. *You simply haven't audited lately.*

Her hand went to the knife at her hip out of pure reflex, and then she laughed at herself—a short, ugly sound in all that smooth stone—because there was nothing here to cut.

*Careful,* said the voice, with what felt like real solicitude. *You'll cut yourself on an edge that isn't there. You've done it before.*

"When?"

Silence. But it was the silence of a ledger held shut, and she understood suddenly why Vess's redacted entry had made her hands shake. Some silences are empty. This one was full.

She approached the plinth. Up close, the crown's dullness resolved into movement—not tarnish but motion, slow as sediment, as if the gold were a surface of some very deep liquid pretending to be metal. The cloudy stones shifted their cloudiness when she wasn't looking directly at them.

Every craft-lesson Corvane's generation had ever written agreed on one point, underlined twice: *never touch the bound thing.* And every lesson also acknowledged, in smaller print, the problem that bound things cannot be unbound by people who keep their hands in their pockets.

Isolde wanted one true memory restored to her father. To restore, Emrys had to read. To read the crown's contents, she had to touch.

"Tell me your terms first," she said. "I'm a ledger-keeper. I don't sign blind."

*Ah.* The warmth brightened, pleased. *There she is. The girl who counts. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else who comes down here wants something simple. Power. Long life. Their dead mother's face. They reach for me like children reaching for a stove. You want the accounting. I can respect the accounting.*

"Terms," Emrys repeated.

*Very well.* Her own voice, but pitched lower now, the way hers went when she was tired and honest. *Touch me, and you may read what I hold. All of it. Sixty years of a king's days, and the days beneath the days. Read freely. Take freely. My only term is this: when you have read, you stop. You climb those stairs, you close that door, and you do not dig further. No Corvane. No redacted entries. No questions about what the Guild traded, or for whom, or why.*

"And if I agree?"

*Then I give you a gift on top. Freely. No theft required.* The warmth pulsed, almost tender. *I will return what was taken from you. Your mother's face. Your first morning. The name you had before you chose another one. Every gap in you, filled to the brim. You will walk out of here whole, Emrys Joubert, and you will finally know what everyone else has always known about themselves and you have had to guess.*

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled through sixty feet of stone, the palace went on repeating its single day.

"That's not a gift," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "That's a bribe."

*All gifts are bribes,* said the thing wearing the crown, reasonably. *The only question is whether the price is worth paying. Most people never get offered a price worth paying. I am offering you yours.*

She looked at the crown. The crown did not look back, exactly—but it attended, the way a well attends to a dropped coin.

Here is what no one tells you about having holes in your past: you do not feel them as absence. You feel them as a strange competence. You are very good at surviving without knowing why. You flinch from kindness without knowing what taught you to expect its opposite. You keep ledgers of other people's memories because your own refuse to balance, and you tell yourself it's discipline, and some nights you almost believe it.

Emrys thought of her mother—or of the place where her mother should have been, a room in her mind swept clean so thoroughly that even the dust had been itemized and removed. She thought of the entry dated tomorrow. She thought of Vess saying *forget what you read* in a voice that had done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more.

She thought: *I could be whole.*

And then, underneath that thought, quieter, in a voice that sounded less like hers than the entity's did: *whole according to whose inventory?*

"No," she said.

*You haven't touched me yet,* said the crown, amused. *You're refusing in advance. That's either wisdom or cowardice, and I've had sixty years to learn the difference, and I still can't tell.*

"It's bookkeeping." She stepped closer. The warmth climbed her face like sun through glass. "You're offering to fill my gaps with whatever you choose to put there. I'd never know which memories were mine and which were yours, wearing mine like a coat. That's not wholeness. That's the deepest theft there is, and I'd be the willing mark." She flexed her fingers. "I steal memories I can give back. That's my rule. What you're offering, nobody gives back."

Silence again. Then the temperature of the voice changed—not warmer, but closer, the way a whisper is closer than a shout across the same distance.

*Emrys.*

Not Joubert. Not the Guild name, not the street name, not any of the seven aliases stitched into her various papers. The word that followed was short, old, and hers—the name someone had called across a yard when she was small enough to turn around when called. She had not heard it in twenty years. She had not known, until this moment, that she had been missing it, because the missing of it had been edited out along with everything attached.

Her whole body went cold around the one warm point where the crown's heat touched her face.

*That name,* said the thing gently, *is the first thing I will give back. Say yes, and you will remember who gave it to you, and where, and what the sky was doing, and why you stopped answering to it. I keep excellent records. Better than yours. Better than the Guild's. I have had sixty years and an entire kingdom to practice.*

"How," she managed, "do you know that name."

*I ate the person who used it,* said the crown, with terrible mildness. *Memories don't die with their owners, little thief. They just change custody.*

Something in Emrys went very still and very clear, the way water goes still and clear over something deep. Fear was still there—fear was a professional tool, fear kept her alive—but beneath the fear, a colder thing assembled itself, entry by entry, the way she balanced a ledger at candlelight: *this thing knows my true name. It has eaten the king. It has eaten, possibly, my mother. It is offering me back my own life at the price of stopping, and it is confident I will take the offer eventually, because it has watched a hundred thieves stand where I'm standing, and it knows the arithmetic of longing better than any of them.*

Good, said the cold thing. Let it be confident. Confidence is a weakness you can invoice later.

"I'm going to touch you now," Emrys said.

*Yes,* said her own voice, satisfied, certain she meant surrender. *Do.*

She reached out and laid two fingers on the rim of the crown.

The world inverted.

Not darkness—she had been braced for darkness—but reading. It hit her the way a flooded archive hits an open door: sixty years of mornings, the same morning, ten thousand copies of the same gray dawn through the same east window, the same breakfast tasted ten thousand times, the same daughter greeted with the same love and the same blankness behind it, page after identical page after identical page, and beneath the repetition, deeper, pressed flat under the weight of all that sameness like flowers in a book—

—a different man. Thin, sharp-eyed, laughing at his own coronation because the crown was too big and kept sliding. A queen with ink-stained fingers adjusting it for him, scolding him gently, her mouth forming a word that the memory itself flinched away from, a word burned out of the record like a name scraped from a stone—

Emrys gasped and pulled her hand back. The chamber reassembled itself around her: plinth, stone, arches, dark.

*There,* said the crown, softly, triumphantly. *Now you've seen him. Now you understand what I am holding. Every day of him, preserved perfectly. Isn't it beautiful? I am the most faithful keeper this kingdom has ever had. And all I ask—all I have ever asked—is to be left alone with what I keep.*

"You didn't show me that for free," Emrys said. Her fingers were shaking. She watched them shake with detached professional interest. "Nothing you do is free. Why show me the king?"

*Because you'll go looking anyway—you're already looking, I can taste it—and I would rather you found a locked door than a broken one.* A pause, delicate as a knife being set down. *And because I want you to understand the shape of the bargain, Emrys. The Guild traded me this kingdom's remembering, and in exchange I keep my hunger leashed. One kingdom, fed slowly, forever, instead of everything, devoured at once. Your masters signed that in blood and then redacted it in shame. If you dig—if you speak certain syllables, if you follow certain copper tokens to certain ruined chapels—then the truce ends. Not because I break it. Because *you* will. And when it ends, I will not be leashed anymore, and I will not be modest anymore, and the last stars will go out not one by one but all together, like candles at the end of a party.*

The warmth receded slightly, leaving something almost like respect.

*So. Final offer, and I will not make it twice. Stop digging, and keep everything you are. Dig, and lose everything you might have been. Choose.*

Emrys stood in the dark with her own heartbeat loud in her ears and the ghost of a laughing king pressed flat behind her eyes, and she found—to her own surprise—that the choice had already been made, sometime between the ledger dated tomorrow and the warden's dying whisper, sometime maybe thirty years ago in a chapel she couldn't remember, by a child she couldn't remember being.

Someone had edited her once already. She was not going to hold the pen for the second draft.

"You keep saying *dig*," she said, backing toward the stairs, "like it's a threat. But you've met thieves before. You must know what we do with threats."

*And what is that?*

"We write them down." She reached the bottom step. "In plain ink."

The silence that followed her up the stairs was not empty either. It was the silence of something deciding, very slowly, whether to be patient.

Behind her, in the vault that was not a vault, the crown sat on its plinth, warm as a hearth, patient as winter, and began—very quietly, in a voice like a girl's, in a voice like a thief's—to practice a name.

Emrys did not hear it. But she felt it at the base of her skull all the way up, all the way out, all the way across the sleeping city, and by the time she reached the river district, gray with false dawn, she knew exactly one thing with ledger-perfect certainty:

There was one man alive older than the bargain, and she had to find Corvane before the thing wearing the crown finished learning how to say her name.
