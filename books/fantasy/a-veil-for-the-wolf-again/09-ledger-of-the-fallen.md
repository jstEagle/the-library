## 9. Ledger of the Fallen

I ran from the palace the way I'd learned to run from everything: quietly, without looking back, with my hands full of other people's secrets.

The thing in the vault had spoken to me in my own voice. That was the part I couldn't shake. Not a mimicry—mimicry I could have dismissed, the way you dismiss an echo. It had used my voice the way I use a lockpick: familiarly, expertly, like it had been practicing for years. *Come back, Emrys,* it had said, in the exact tone my mother might have used, if I'd ever had a mother, if I'd ever known what that tone sounded like. *Everything you lost is still here. Everything you never had is waiting.*

I had run anyway. My legs had decided before my mind caught up, and I was grateful to them.

The city outside the palace walls was worse than the city I'd slipped into at dusk. The lamps still burned blue—the wrong blue, the blue of deep water where nothing has eyes—and the streets had gone strange in the way streets go strange when people stop being sure of them. A baker stood in his doorway at midnight, flour to his elbows, staring at his own hands as though he'd never seen them work. Two guardsmen walked their rounds past the same corner three times, each pass with slightly less purpose, until on the fourth they simply stopped and stood, and one of them asked the other, gently, almost politely, "Were we going somewhere?"

The other didn't answer. He'd forgotten how questions worked.

I kept to the shadows and the rooftops where I could, because a memory-thief learns early that crowds are dangerous when forgetting is in the air. Forgetfulness moves through a crowd like smoke through a keyhole. It finds the cracks. And every person it touched left a little residue behind, a faint cold smudge in the air, and I could feel those smudges accumulating around me like frost forming on a window.

By the time I reached the Tanners' Reach—the crooked district where the Guild's retired went to be old and bitter and conveniently unfindable—I had counted eleven people who no longer knew their own names. One of them was singing. She sang beautifully. She just didn't know what any of the words meant anymore.

* * *

Corvane lived above a shuttered dye-house at the end of a street that smelled of old salt and older regret. Sixty years ago he had been the finest thief the Guild of Impossible Things ever produced, which is a sentence I'd heard Vess say exactly twice, both times while drunk, both times followed by a long silence and another drink. Corvane had retired before I joined. Retired, or been retired; with thieves of his generation the distinction was mostly academic.

He was expecting me. Of course he was. Men like Corvane are always expecting something; it's how they stay alive.

"You're dripping riverwater on my stairs," he said when I climbed through his window instead of knocking on his door, because some courtesies between thieves are non-negotiable. "And you've got vault-dust on your sleeves. Palace vaults." He squinted at me across the candlelight. He was small now, shrunken into himself the way old trees shrink, but his eyes were still thief's eyes—quick, amused, and utterly without mercy. "Sit down, girl. You look like someone who's met the crown."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards. "How did you—"

"Because you flinched." He poured two cups of something amber and pushed one across the table. "Everyone who meets it flinches. Now sit, drink, and tell me what it said to you. Take your time. We have until dawn, probably less."

So I told him. The redacted entry. The hole in my own ledger. The king on his loop, warm and hollow, saying the same kind words to the same morning over and over. Isolde's face when she caught me reading him—not alarm, not fear, but the terrible tired recognition of someone who has known a truth so long it has worn smooth as a river stone. And then the vaults, and the crown, and the voice.

"It spoke in my voice," I finished. "It knew things about me I don't know myself. It offered to give me back my mother."

Corvane listened without moving. When I was done he drank his cup in one swallow and refilled it, and for a long moment the only sound was the liquid and, far off, a bell tolling the hour wrong—one stroke too many, as though even the bells were beginning to forget how time went.

"Your mother," he said at last. "You don't remember her."

"I don't remember having one. There's a gap. There are gaps." I turned my cup without drinking. "Corvane, I came here because you're the only person alive who's older than the bargain. Vess said—"

"Vess says a great many things." His mouth twisted. "Vess was not yet born when the ink dried. I was there. I carried the pen." He set the cup down with a click that sounded final, like a lock engaging. "You want to know what the Guild traded? Then understand first what the crown is, because the bargain only makes sense once you know what we were bargaining *with*."

* * *

He got up, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the wall behind his bed. The plaster there was covered in hanging things—dried herbs, copper charms, strings of knots in patterns I half-recognized from ward-craft. He pulled aside a knot-charm older than the others, and behind it, set into the stone, was a cavity. From the cavity he took a ledger.

Not a Guild ledger. Smaller, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its corners rounded by decades of handling. But I knew the format instantly, because I'd spent ten years keeping ledgers in that exact format: columns for the taken, columns for the given, columns for the price. It was a working ledger. A thief's ledger.

"This is mine," Corvane said. "From before. Before the Guild made rules about what could be traded and what couldn't. Back then we were just thieves with steady hands, and the things we stole weren't always memories, and nobody had thought to write down that stealing a man's grief might leave him open to worse things than grief." He laid it on the table between us like a card player laying down a losing hand. "Read the entry dated the ninth of Hearthmonth. Any year. The year doesn't matter. It happened on the ninth of Hearthmonth, and it has happened on the ninth of Hearthmonth every year since, and that is the whole of the bargain."

I read.

The handwriting was young Corvane's—sharp, confident, already beautiful. The entry was short. Thieves' entries usually are; poetry is for people who can afford the extra ink.

*Taken: one (1) king. Given: sixty years of quiet. Price: to be collected annually.*

"That's not—" I stopped. Started again. "Kings aren't memories. You can't take a king."

"We didn't take him all at once." Corvane sat back down heavily. "That's the part nobody understands, because it's the part that makes it monstrous instead of merely sad. You don't steal a mountain in one haul. You quarry it. A memory here—a childhood summer, a first love, a father's funeral. A name, later. A face. The sound of his own children laughing. We took him apart the way you'd take apart a house brick by brick, and every year on the ninth of Hearthmonth we took one more brick, and the entity in the crown took *him*, and in exchange it kept its hunger leashed. Sixty years of peace. No devouring. No villages waking up empty-headed. No stars going out." He looked at me, and his old thief's eyes were finally, fully tired. "It was a good deal, Emrys. That's what I need you to understand. It was a *good deal*. Everyone involved believed that. The queen believed it most of all."

"The queen," I said. The word came out of me strangely, as if someone else had put it in my mouth. "What queen?"

Corvane went very still.

It was a particular kind of stillness, and I recognized it, because it was a trick I used myself: the stillness of a man deciding, in real time, how much of the truth a person can carry. I'd seen Vess do it. I'd done it to clients a hundred times, weighing what they could hold without breaking.

"Don't," I said. "Don't weigh me. I've spent tonight talking to a thing that knows my name better than I do. Whatever you're deciding whether to tell me—I can hold it."

"You can't," he said, not unkindly. "Nobody could. That's rather the point of the design." But he sighed, and rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked twenty years older than he had when I climbed through the window. "The queen. Yes. All right. Here is the shape of it, girl, and may whatever gods are still listening forgive us all.

"There was a queen, sixty years ago, married to a good king of a prosperous realm. And there was a thing beneath the world—or beyond it, or inside it; the old texts disagree and I've stopped caring—which ate remembering. Not minds. Remembering. The difference matters. It didn't want to kill anyone. It wanted everyone to forget, because a forgotten thing is a defenseless thing, and a world that forgets itself is a world it can wear like a glove. It came up through the borderlands first. Villages went quiet. Wards fell. Stars began going out one at a time, because stars are the oldest memories the sky has, and it started with those.

"And the queen—" Corvane's voice caught, and he cleared it roughly, angry at himself. "The queen found out what it was. Found its name, somehow, in a way no one has managed since. And she learned the second thing, the terrible thing, the thing that made everything after possible: that a named thing can be bound. Named and bound and *crowned*. She couldn't kill it. Nothing kills it. But she could shut it in iron and gold and set it on her husband's head, where its hunger would press against the strongest will in the kingdom and be held there, contained, starving slowly, for as long as the binding held."

"So the crown isn't a symbol," I said slowly. "It's a cage."

"It's a cage with a tenant. And tenants get hungry, Emrys, even caged ones, especially caged ones. Starving doesn't kill it. Starving only makes it patient. So the queen faced a choice, the choice every jailer faces: watch it starve forever, or feed it just enough to keep it docile." He spread his hands. "She chose to feed it. Small meals. Carefully measured. Memories, brought to it on schedule, portioned out by the only people in the world with the craft to harvest them safely." He tapped the grey ledger with one finger. "Us. The Guild. We were founded for this, girl—did you think we collect impossible things out of whimsy? Every rule we have, every restriction, every entry in every ledger, exists because of this one bargain. We became the crown's pantry. And in exchange, the realm got sixty years of forgetting nothing. Sixty years of stars."

"But the king," I said. "The real king. If the entity is wearing him—"

"The entity *is* wearing him. Has been, more or less, from the start. The binding needed an anchor—a living will strong enough to hold it in place. The queen used her husband. And the feeding, year after year, ate the anchor along with the prisoner. That was the flaw in her design, and I think she knew it, and I think she did it anyway, because the alternative was the end of the world and a dead husband is a smaller grief than that." Corvane's voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something very heavy across very thin ice. "The man on the throne now is the last scrap of him. A loop. A day repeated. The entity keeps him warm and kind because a contented anchor holds better than a screaming one. Everything else—the man, the father, the king—he went into the well a brick at a time, on the ninth of Hearthmonth, for sixty years."

I thought of the king's hands. Warm, gentle hands, resting on the arm of his throne. I thought of Princess Isolde's face, and the way she had watched her father say the same kindness to the same morning, and I understood suddenly why she hadn't raised the alarm when she caught me reading him. She wasn't protecting a secret. She was protecting the loop. Because the loop was all that was left of him, and she had been keeping it running alone, for years, with ritual and rote and a court that never knew.

"The wards," I said. "They fell the night the stars went dark. All at once. If the bargain is annual—if the feeding is scheduled—"

"Then something broke the schedule." Corvane leaned forward, and the candlelight made his shadow loom huge on the wall behind him, bigger than the man, big enough to fill the room. "Either the entity found a way to stop being fed and started taking, or someone stopped bringing the meals. Either way, the truce is over, and everything the bargain held back for sixty years is coming due at once. With interest." He fixed me with those quick, merciless eyes. "Now. The question I've been circling since you climbed through my window. The entity spoke to you in your own voice and offered you your mother. It doesn't make offers like that to strangers, girl. It makes offers to *assets*. So you tell me: what are you, that the crown wants to keep?"

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying since the vaults, sitting in my chest like swallowed ice.

"I don't know," I said. "But I think it's in my ledger. There's an entry I don't remember writing. Dated tomorrow. And there are gaps in me, Corvane—gaps shaped like answers. Someone edited me the way Vess edits ledgers, and I think—" My voice failed, steadied. "I think whoever did it was protecting the bargain. And I think they used me to do it."

Corvane was silent for a long moment. Then he reached across the table and closed his old spotted hand over mine, and his grip was still a thief's grip, precise and strong.

"Then there's one more thing you need to hear," he said, "and I'll warn you now, it's the worst of them. Because the bargain didn't begin with me, and it didn't begin with Vess, and it didn't even begin with the queen. It began with the first theft. The founding theft. The one the whole edifice is built on top of, the way a house is built on its cellar." His eyes held mine, and in them I saw pity, which frightened me more than anything the entity had said. "Every copy of that entry was destroyed, girl. Every ledger, every record, every memory of everyone who witnessed it. Except one. The Guild left exactly one copy in the world, sealed and hidden, because a binding needs a lock, and locks need keys, and you don't destroy the key to the cage that's holding the end of the world."

The candle flame bent, though there was no draft.

"They hid it in a person," Corvane said softly. "In a child too young to know what she was carrying. They stole her memory of the theft, sealed the theft itself inside her where no one would think to look, and wiped everyone who might remember her doing it." His hand tightened on mine. "Emrys. Ask yourself why a woman with no mother and no first theft has spent her whole life unable to stop taking memories. Ask yourself what you were built to hold."

Outside, far away toward the palace, a bell tolled the hour wrong again—two strokes too many now—and somewhere below us in the Tanners' Reach, the singing woman had stopped mid-verse, and did not begin again.
