## 10. Stars Going Out One by One

Isolde had learned to measure the kingdom's health by small things: the weight of the bread at breakfast, the pitch of the bells at noon, the number of times her father's steward said *as it was yesterday* in a single hour. Lately all three measures had gone wrong at once. The bread came out of the ovens pale and half-remembered, as if the bakers had forgotten halfway through what they were making. The bells rang a fraction late, then a fraction early, as though even bronze could lose count. And the steward—good, gray Martel, who had served since her grandmother's reign—said *as it was yesterday* forty-one times before supper, each repetition a little more hollow than the last.

Tonight she stood on the east balcony of the high tower with her private diaries stacked at her feet, and watched the last star in the sky go out.

It did not fall. It did not dim. It simply stopped, the way a clock stops, the way a heart stops—one moment present, the next moment a hole where presence used to be. Isolde felt the absence land somewhere behind her ribs, and understood with terrible clarity that the sky above Vhalenmere was now a ceiling painted black by something very patient.

Below her, the city's lamps burned blue.

They had been burning blue for six days, ever since the border wards fell like cut strings—she'd felt them go, everyone had, a shudder through the stones—and ever since, the forgetting had crept inward from the edges of the realm like frost across a window. First the villages past the Marches, where the wardens sent word in shaking hands: whole towns awake and walking and unable to say their own names. Then the river towns. Then the outer districts of the capital itself, where children woke asking their mothers who they were, and mothers opened their mouths and found nothing there to answer with.

The court called it a plague. The priests called it a test. The Guild, when her messengers went asking, called it nothing at all, because the guildhouse doors were shut and veiled and no one answered, which frightened Isolde more than any of it.

She knew better than all of them. She had known for years that her father was a shell wearing a crown, that the thing behind his warm eyes ate memory the way other things ate meat. She had kept the kingdom running on ritual and rote precisely so that no one would look too closely at the king and notice he repeated the same day like a song stuck in a groove. Ritual was armor. Rote was scaffolding. As long as everyone did everything the same way every day, no one had room to wonder why the king never aged, never erred, never changed.

But ritual required remembering, and remembering was exactly what was being taken.

"Your Highness." Captain Sera Vane's voice from the doorway, clipped as always. "The night watch is assembled."

Isolde turned. Sera had served her for eleven years, had pulled her from a horse's hooves at fourteen, had once held the princess's hair back while she wept over a dead falcon. Her face was weathered, honest, framed by iron-gray hair cropped short for the helm.

"I don't recall dismissing you," Isolde said mildly.

Sera blinked. "Dismissed—from where, Highness?"

"The balcony. You stood here an hour ago and told me the watch was changing."

Something flickered across the captain's face—not guilt, not confusion exactly. Confusion would have been better. It was the look of a woman reaching into a drawer and finding it empty and not being able to say whether the drawer had ever held anything at all.

"I—" Sera's hand drifted toward her sword hilt, the old reflex, the body remembering what the mind had lost. "I came up just now. The stairs. I remember the stairs."

"You remember the stairs," Isolde said quietly. "Do you remember my name?"

"Of course." A pause. The pause lasted two seconds too long. "Highness."

"That isn't my name."

"It's what one calls—" Sera stopped. Isolde watched, with a horror she refused to let reach her face, as the captain searched herself and found the search coming back empty. Eleven years. A dead falcon. Horse's hooves. All of it gone or going, scooped out like the flesh of a fruit, the shape left standing but hollow.

"The watch," Sera said at last, grasping for procedure the way a drowning woman grasps for rope. "The night watch is assembled in the lower yard. They're waiting for inspection."

"Then let's not keep them."

Isolde swept past her without touching her. Touching, she had learned, made it worse—or made it more real, which amounted to the same thing.

* * *

The lower yard held thirty guards in formation, torchlight sliding off their breastplates, and Isolde walked the line the way her father had taught her to walk a line when she was nine years old: slowly, meeting every eye.

She met nineteen eyes that knew her.

By the eleventh man, she noticed the pattern. The older guards—the ones who'd served longest, who had the most years of her face filed away—still straightened when she passed, still murmured *Highness* with the easy warmth of habit. The younger ones looked at her the way one looks at a stranger in borrowed finery: respectful, wary, blank. And the youngest of all, a boy named Pell who had joined the watch only last spring, actually stepped back when she paused before him, hand going to his spear.

"Easy," she said. "You know me."

"Do I?" He said it honestly, without malice, and that honesty was the worst part. "Forgive me, my lady. You have the look of someone I ought to know."

"I am Princess Isolde Vhalen. Daughter of the king. You swore your oath to my father's throne eight months ago, in this yard, in the rain."

Pell's brow furrowed. Behind him, another guard shifted. Somewhere in the formation, a whisper started and died.

"He wouldn't remember the rain," said Sergeant Odo, gruff, fifty, scarred down one cheek—a face Isolde had trusted since she was a child. "None of us would. That's the trouble, Highness, isn't it? We're losing—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "There are gaps. In the ledgers of us. My wife's maiden name. The name of the village I was born in. I know I *had* these things. I can feel the shapes of them, like footprints in snow after the snow's stopped falling. But the walker's gone."

"How long?" Isolde asked him. "How long have the gaps been growing?"

"For some of the men, days. For me—" Odo's scarred face worked. "Since the stars went dark. Since the wards fell. It's faster now. Whatever it is, it's feeding faster."

*Feeding.* Yes. She thought of her father in his chair by the window, repeating his single golden day, and understood at last what fed him. Not devotion. Not prayer. Memory itself, siphoned from a whole kingdom, poured down whatever hidden throat connected the crown to the world. The wards had been damming that current for sixty years, thinning the flow, rationing the entity its meals so the realm could forget gently, gradually, one small thing per person per year—the name of a childhood friend, the smell of a grandmother's kitchen—losses so gentle no one ever noticed the ledger shrinking.

Now the dam was broken, and the flood ran backward, and everything was pouring into the crown at once.

"New orders," Isolde said, and her voice carried across the yard because she made it carry, because a voice was a tool like any other and hers had been trained for exactly this. "From tonight, every member of the watch will write down ten memories. Ten true things about themselves. Their mother's face. Their first love. The worst day and the best day. They will read them aloud at every change of shift until they can recite them blind. If a man finds a gap in himself, he reports it to his sergeant immediately. No shame in it. Shame wastes time."

"And if the writing goes too?" Odo asked. "If we wake tomorrow and our own hands' work looks like another man's?"

Isolde had no answer for that. So she gave him the only truth she had.

"Then we write it again," she said. "And again. And again. Memory is a wall built one stone at a time, Sergeant, and I intend to keep laying stones until either the wall holds or my arms give out."

Nineteen faces believed her. Eleven did not know who she was well enough to believe anything. She dismissed them anyway, and stood alone in the emptying yard, and listened to the blue lamps hum.

* * *

Her study smelled of tallow and old paper, and it was the only room in the palace where she felt like herself—which was, she realized as she barred the door, precisely because it was full of proof of herself. Fifteen years of diaries lined the shelves, bound in calfskin, written in her own slanting hand. Every day since her twelfth birthday. Every fear, every fury, every secret joy. Her mother's death, recorded in ink gone brown at the edges. Her first taste of statecraft, age fifteen, talking a grain merchant out of ruinous prices while her father smiled his warm hollow smile beside her. The night she'd first suspected what the king really was, and vomited into a chamber pot afterward, and written *I will not be afraid of my own father* seventeen times until the words stopped shaking.

She took down the first volume and sat with it unopened for a long moment.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape. Writing preserved memory—but only for those who could read. The forgetting was spreading inward, district by district, and sooner or later it would reach the palace, reach this room, reach her. A diary in the hands of a woman who couldn't remember writing it was just paper. Ink meant nothing to a mind that couldn't anchor the words to a self.

Unless the words were anchored somewhere deeper.

Corvane's name surfaced in her thoughts unbidden—a retired thief, her spies had told her, the oldest living member of the Guild, a man who supposedly remembered the bargain. She didn't know yet how to find him. But she knew the principle he'd have taught, because she'd deduced it herself over years of watching her father loop: memory held in the mind could be stolen, but memory burned into the mind—repeated, ritualized, worn into the grain like a path across grass—was far harder to take. The entity fed easily on what lay loose. It struggled with what had become structure.

So she would make herself into structure.

She opened the first diary to the first page and began to read aloud.

*"Twelfth day of the Harvest Month. Mother let me hold the seal today. It's heavier than it looks, warm from her hand..."*

She read it twice. Then she closed her eyes and recited it from memory, checking every word against the page. Then she did it again with her eyes open, then shut, then open. Twelve times. Twenty. Until the words stopped being words and became a groove, a liturgy, something her mouth could do without her mind's permission.

Then the next entry. And the next.

Hours passed. Candles died and were replaced. Her voice went raw and dropped to a rasp, and she kept reading, kept reciting, page after page, year after year, driving her own life into herself like nails into oak. Her mother's laugh. The falcon's death. The taste of the rebellion wine she'd drunk at sixteen and regretted at seventeen. The exact weight of the royal seal, warm from her mother's hand.

Somewhere past midnight she came to the entry she had been circling all along, the one dated the night she'd first understood her father was empty, and she read it aloud, and her voice cracked on the seventeenth repetition of *I will not be afraid of my own father*, and she read it a eighteenth time anyway, and a nineteenth, until the crack sealed over and the sentence became bedrock.

Let the thing in the crown come for her. Let it reach into her and find not loose treasure but load-bearing stone. It could take her memories if it could pry them out—but she would leave nothing lying loose. She would be all keystone, all foundation, a fortress with no unlocked doors.

Dawn found her hoarse, candle-greased, hollow-eyed, and more solidly *herself* than she had ever been. She had burned forty volumes into her mind in a single night, and her skull ached like a fist had been at it, and she would do it again tonight, and every night, for as many nights as the war lasted.

A knock at the door. Martel's voice, careful: "Highness. There's a woman below. She says she's a memory-taster come for the court, papers from the Guild. But—" a hesitation, and Isolde braced herself, because Martel's hesitations had grown longer all week, "—the strange thing is, she asked for you by a description no one gave her. She said the princess would have ink on her fingers and fire in her eyes. And Highness... she says the stars aren't dead. She says they're *taken*. And she says she knows how to steal them back."

Isolde rose. Her legs were stiff; her mind was granite. On her desk lay the newest volume, its first page still blank, and she dipped a pen and wrote the date and one line before she answered:

*A thief has come. Good. Thieves know what things are worth.*

"Show her up," Isolde said.
