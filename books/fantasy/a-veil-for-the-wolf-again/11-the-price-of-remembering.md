## 11. The Price of Remembering

Corvane lived at the top of a house that had forgotten how to be a house.

That was the only way Emrys could describe it as she climbed the stairs in the dark—stairs that seemed to lose count of themselves, so that she passed the same cracked landing three times before the fourth attempt carried her higher instead of sideways. The building sat at the crooked end of Tallow Row, where the city's forgetting had come early and settled deep. Paint peeled from the walls in long curls that looked, in the blue light of the guttering lamps, like shed skin. A child's chalk drawing faded on the stairwell wall: a sun, a stick figure, a word beneath it that no longer had letters.

She knocked on the door with the copper token pressed flat against her palm, because Corvane had told her to, thirty years ago, when she'd been a girl with quick fingers and a Guild badge too big for her coat. *If you ever need me and can't remember why,* he'd said, laughing, *knock with metal and I'll know it's trouble.*

The door opened before her knuckles fell a third time.

Corvane was old. Not elderly the way the ledgers said—he was, by the Guild's count, ninety-one—but old the way furniture is old, the way rivers are old, worn down to something essential and unlovely and utterly himself. He was thin as a bundle of kindling, wrapped in a robe that might once have been Guild grey, and his eyes were the pale, washed-out color of a sky after rain. Memory-thieves went pale-eyed in the end. They gave too much away over the years, and the color went with it.

"You're late," he said. "By about eleven years."

"I've been busy staying invisible."

"Invisible." He snorted and turned back into the room, leaving the door open behind him, which from Corvane was an embrace. "I taught you better than that. Invisible is what things are when nobody's looking. You were never invisible, girl. You were *unread*. There's a difference, and the difference is going to save your life or spend it."

The room inside was small and ferociously tidy. One bed, one chair, one table. And shelves—shelves covering every wall from floor to ceiling, crowded not with books but with objects. A rusted bell. A doll missing one eye. A birdcage with the door wired shut from the outside. Hundreds of them, thousands, each one tagged with a scrap of paper bearing a date and a few words in a cramped hand. Emrys knew what they were. Every thief did. Anchors. Physical moorings for memories too heavy or too dangerous to carry loose in the mind.

"You've been *collecting*," she said.

"I've been *keeping*," Corvane corrected. "There's a difference there too, and it matters even more." He lowered himself into the chair by the cold hearth with the care of a man handling his own bones. "Sit. Tell me what Vess wouldn't."

So she told him. The wards falling like cut strings on the night the stars went out. The villages beyond the border where people had forgotten their own names, their children's faces, the way to work a field they'd farmed for forty years. The dying warden and the copper token and the words *the crown remembers*. The redacted entry that existed in every copy of the Guild ledger except hers. The hole in her own past shaped exactly like a first theft—a mother who wasn't anywhere, a childhood with its center cut out clean. The king, warm and kind and hollow, living the same day on a loop while his daughter held the court together with ritual and rote. The thing in the vaults that spoke in her own voice and knew her real name.

Corvane listened without moving. When she finished, he was quiet for a long time, long enough that the blue lamp-light shifted through two shades of wrong.

"Sixty years," he said finally. "I wondered if anyone would ever pull that thread. I wondered if I'd have to do it myself, at my age, with these hands." He held them up—trembling, spotted, steady only when he made them be. "You understand what you're asking, Emrys? You want to steal from yourself. The deepest theft there is. The one we don't teach, because the ones who learn it usually don't survive the lesson."

"The entity offered to give me back everything I've lost," she said. "My mother. My name. My whole self. All I had to do was stop digging—and swallow the queen's secret forever. I'm going to take the name out of my own head, Corvane. The one I stole when I was nine years old and didn't know what it was. But to use it—to speak the rite—I have to reach it without unraveling. And I don't know how. Nobody alive knows how. Except you."

"Nobody alive," he agreed mildly. "Which puts me in an awkward position, since I'm mostly dead already and just haven't informed my body." He pushed himself up, crossed to the shelves, and ran a finger along the tags until he found what he wanted: a small tin whistle, dented green with age. "First lesson. Sit down properly. No, on the floor—cross-legged, like a child. Deep craft starts where all theft starts. On your knees in someone else's house. Tonight the house is yours."

Emrys sat. The floorboards were cold through her trousers. Above them, through the gaps in the shutters, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"Ordinary theft," Corvane began, pacing now, slow as a tide, "is taking a memory out of someone else. You slip in along their attention, find the thread, cut it, roll it up, and carry it out. It costs you a little of yourself every time—that's the toll, that's why our eyes go pale—but the memory stays whole. It keeps its shape. It remembers being loved." He stopped. "Stealing from yourself is different. Do you know why?"

"Because there's no distance. You're thief and mark both."

"Because there's no distance," he confirmed, "and because the memory doesn't want to leave. A stranger's memory comes away easy—it barely knows you. Your own memory has grown roots into everything else you are. Pull it fast and you tear. Pull it whole and you take half your mind with it. That's the unraveling. That's what emptied the villages past the wards—not stolen *from* them, but torn *out*, ragged, by something that didn't care about the shape of the wound." His voice went flat and hard. "The crown doesn't steal, girl. It devours. We are going to teach you to do the opposite of devouring."

He tossed her the whistle. She caught it.

"What am I holding?"

"A memory anchor. Mine. Inside that whistle lives the last hour of a summer afternoon in 1189, on the estuary mudflats, with my sister Wren. She was teaching me to whistle gull-calls so I could cheat the fishmongers' cries. I was seven. She was twelve." He said it evenly, the way a man reads coordinates off a map. "It is the oldest thing I own. Older than my name, which I've given away twice. Older than my hands. When I die, it will be the last of me that was ever truly me."

"Corvane—"

"Lesson one," he said, over her, relentless. "*Anchoring.* Before you cut anything out of yourself, you must give it somewhere to live outside you. An object that was present when the memory was made. The object holds the edges. You hold the middle. Together the memory survives the surgery intact." He tapped the shelf of tagged things. "Every piece here is somebody's saved life. Mine, mostly, at this point. Now choose. What are you taking out of yourself tonight?"

Emrys looked down at the whistle in her palm and understood, with a slow cold turning in her stomach, exactly what he intended.

"No," she said. "Absolutely not. Pick something else. Anything else. Take a demonstration from me—you said yourself I'm the one who needs to learn—"

"You'll take plenty from yourself before this is done. Tonight you watch." He lowered himself back into the chair, and for a moment the tremble left his hands entirely, and he looked, briefly, like the man who had trained half the Guild's finest thieves and terrified the other half. "Wren died in 1196. Fever, the winter the river froze. I've kept this afternoon for eighty-one years because it was the day I loved her most, and I thought—" his mouth twisted, wry, "—I thought keeping it would keep her. Foolishness. Sentiment. The exact foolishness that makes deep craft dangerous. So we will use it. Best lessons cost the teacher something, Emrys. Otherwise the student doesn't believe you."

"There has to be another way to demonstrate anchoring."

"There isn't. Watch closely, because I'm only doing this once, and because the next pair of hands that opens this memory should be yours." He nodded at the whistle. "Hold it against your sternum. Both hands. Feel it?"

She pressed the cool tin to her breastbone. Beneath her palms she felt—faintly, unmistakably—a pulse that was not her own. Quick, light, skipping like a stone across water. A child's heartbeat, stored.

"Now I go in," Corvane said quietly. "Watch my eyes. Don't follow me yet. Just watch what the face does, so you know what to expect when it's your face."

And Emrys watched a master work.

It did not look like much. That was the horror and the beauty of it. Corvane closed his eyes, breathed once, long and slow, and went still—stiller than a man could sit, stiller than the chair beneath him. His breathing slowed until she couldn't see it. His face smoothed. And around him, the room *leaned*. The candle flames bent toward him though there was no draft. The dust motes hung motionless. Even the blue light from the window seemed to pause, listening.

When he spoke, his voice came from very far away, from under water, from 1189.

"Mudflats. Low tide. Wren's got mud to her knees and she doesn't care. She whistles and a real gull answers her, and she laughs like the sound is a physical thing she's throwing." A breath. "There. I've found the edges. Object first—the whistle in my fist, salt-wet, the dent from when I dropped it on rocks last spring. Anchor to the object. Then the frame—sky, tide-line, the smell of the flats at ebb, low sun. Build the box. *Then* the middle." His brow furrowed, the only strain showing. "The middle is the hard part. The middle is her laugh. The laugh wants to stay. The laugh has roots in every year since. Cut around it. Slow. Slow. Leave the roots a little blood—they'll heal into whatever's next—"

His hand, resting on the arm of the chair, began to shake.

"Corvane." Emrys started forward.

"*Don't touch me.*" Sharp as a whipcrack, eyes still closed. "You break my concentration and the tear takes us both. Sit. *Watch.*"

She sat. She watched an old man bleed out eighty-one years of love through a wound the width of a whistle, and he did it cleanly, precisely, with a craftsmanship that made her want to weep. She felt the moment the memory came free—a soft internal click she heard more than saw, like a lock turning in a distant room—and then Corvane opened his eyes, exhaled, and held out his hand.

"Whistle," he rasped.

She put it in his palm. He closed his fingers around it, and the faint second pulse beneath the tin steadied, settled, went quiet. Saved. Whole. Edges anchored, middle intact, roots trimmed but living.

"It worked," she whispered. "You did it. It's out of you and it's *whole*—"

"Yes." He smiled. It was a terrible smile, gentle and final. "Now the second half of the lesson. Ask me my sister's name."

"What?"

"Ask me."

"Corvane, what was your sister's name?"

He looked at her with those washed-out eyes, and there was nothing behind them where the answer should have been. Not a blank, exactly. Worse than a blank—a smooth place, healed over, like skin grown across an old wound. He searched for it genuinely, patiently, the way you search for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found nothing, and was not distressed, because he did not know enough to be distressed.

"I had a sister?" he said pleasantly. "Did I? How nice."

The bottom dropped out of Emrys's world.

"No. No, no—Corvane, Wren, her name was Wren, she taught you gull-calls, the estuary, 1189, you *kept it*, you kept it eighty-one years—"

"Did I?" He turned the whistle over in his fingers, curious, a man examining a stranger's trinket. "It's a nice whistle. Old. Someone's tagged it." He squinted at the paper scrap. "Can't read the writing. My eyes aren't what they were." He looked up at her, mild and empty and smiling. "You seem upset, dear. Come sit by the fire. Well. By where the fire would be, if I'd any coal."

Emrys stood in the middle of the small fierce room and shook. Because she understood now—understood what he had done, what the lesson really was. Anchoring preserved the memory. It did not preserve the man. He had cut the afternoon out whole and perfect, and the space it left behind had healed smooth, and everything connected to it had gone with it—not just the afternoon but the knowing, the loving, the eighty-one years of deliberate keeping. He had demonstrated the deep craft flawlessly.

And the price was that Corvane no longer knew why any of it mattered.

"That's the tuition," she said hoarsely. "That's what it costs. Not the memory. Everything hanging from it."

"Sorry?" He blinked at her, courteous. "You'll have to speak up."

She crossed the room on legs that didn't feel like hers and took the whistle gently from his hand. He let it go without a flicker of resistance. She hung it back on its hook among the hundreds of other saved lives, and her fingers brushed tag after tag—dates, names, fragments of other people's afternoons—and she thought: *this is what I'm walking toward. This smooth-healed emptiness. This pleasant, polite nothing.*

Unless.

"Corvane. I'm going to ask you some questions. Answer as best you can."

"Happy to."

"Why did you knock copper on doors?"

"So I'd know it was trouble." Instant. Certain.

"Why do thieves' eyes go pale?"

"We give too much away. The color goes with it." Instant. Certain.

"How do you anchor a memory to an object?"

"Object present at the making. Hold the edges. Trim the roots, leave a little blood." Instant. Certain. He recited it like a catechism, fluently, meaninglessly—and Emrys felt the shape of hope move through her grief like a fish under ice. The craft remained. The *knowledge* remained, rooted deeper than any single memory, woven through everything he was. He had lost Wren. He had not lost the skill that cost him Wren.

The skill survived. Only the reason didn't.

"Last question," she said, and her voice broke on it. "Are you happy?"

Corvane considered this with genuine attention, head tilted, an old man weighing a curious gift. Outside, the starless night pressed at the shutters. Somewhere down Tallow Row, a woman was calling a name into the dark—her own, maybe, or a child's—and getting no answer.

"I think so," he said at last, smiling. "I seem to have had a good life. There are an awful lot of whistles."

Emrys sat down on the floor beside his chair, the way she had as a girl, and stayed until the blue lamps burned low. She memorized him—the washed-out eyes, the trembling capable hands, the smile with nothing and everything behind it—because someone had to, and because she was beginning to understand that remembering was not a service she performed for coin anymore. It was the whole of the work. It was the only thing left worth stealing.

Near dawn, his breathing changed.

She knew death when she heard it; she'd sat with enough of the Guild's spent thieves. She took his hand. It tightened around hers with surprising strength, and for one instant the emptiness in his eyes flickered—something surfacing, some last root the surgery hadn't reached, some reflex older than memory—and his lips moved.

"Wren," he said clearly. Wonderingly. Like a man greeting morning.

Then he smiled, and did not know at what, and let go.

* * *

She buried him at first light, behind the house, under the one tree on Tallow Row that still had leaves. No name for the marker—she didn't dare carve one; names were being eaten out of the air itself now, and she would not feed his grave to the dark. Instead she took the green tin whistle from its shelf, pressed it once to her sternum, feeling the small stored heartbeat skip beneath the metal, and buried it in his hands.

*Someone will whistle gull-calls again someday,* she thought. *And it will mean you, even if no one knows it.*

Then she climbed the stairs that couldn't count, and stepped out into a city gone quieter in a single night—shop signs blanker, faces vaguer, a beggar on the corner opening his mouth for alms and producing no words at all—and unrolled the scrap of paper she'd taken from Corvane's table, the one he'd been writing when she arrived, in a hand suddenly shaky, dated yesterday:

*If the girl comes asking about the deep craft, tell her the chapel on the Salt Road. Tell her the token knows the door. And tell her—*

The rest was a smear of ink where his pen had stopped.

But Emrys had read the first line, and the copper token in her pocket had begun, very faintly, to warm.
