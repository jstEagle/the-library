## 12. What I Stole First

The copper token pulled me north like a fishhook through the sternum.

I had tried to leave it behind twice. The first time I set it on Corvane's table, and when I turned back from the door it was in my pocket, warm as a held coin. The second time I buried it under a loose stone in the Guildhouse courtyard, and by the time I reached the gate my hand was closed around it again, and I could not remember reaching for it. That was the thing about tokens pressed by dying wardens: they did not ask. They chose.

So I walked.

North out of the capital, past the last functioning ward-stone, whose carved sigils had gone smooth and blank as river pebbles. Past the first village where a woman stood in her doorway and asked me, politely, who I was, and then asked me again, and then stopped asking because she had forgotten there was such a thing as asking. Past fields where scarecrows hung unremarked because no one remembered what they were meant to frighten away—or that anything needed frightening at all.

The forgetting moved like weather now. I felt it at the edges of myself, a soft erosion, the way a river works at a bank without hurrying. Names went first. Then faces. Then the reasons for things. I kept my own name by repeating it under my breath like a prayer with no god attached—*Emrys Joubert, Emrys Joubert*—and kept my purpose by holding the token so tightly its edge printed a crescent into my palm. Pain is an excellent ledger. It records itself.

The chapel appeared on the third evening, exactly where the memory-shaped hole in my life said it should be, which was the cruelest part. I had no memory of this place. But I had a *shape* of one, a negative space I'd carried since Vess showed me the redacted page—a hollow with chapel-sized walls—and my feet fit it perfectly.

It sat in a fold of hills where the road gave up pretending to go anywhere. Grey stone, roof fallen in, windows long since emptied of their glass so that the sunset poured through them unhindered, laying red panes of light across the floor as if the building still wore its colors somewhere invisible. Ivy had taken the east wall. A bell tower leaned like a drunk against the sky, its bell missing, its rope rotted to a fringe.

And on the door, hanging from a nail rusted flat with age, a veil.

Small. White. Child-sized work, knotted badly, the kind of charm a thief's guild teaches its foundlings in their first year: *a veil for the wolf*, we called them, though none of us knew why. You hang it over a door you never want opened twice. I knew the knot. My hands knew the knot. My hands were shaking.

"You've been waiting," I said to the door, and heard how young my voice sounded, and hated it.

The token in my palm grew hot—not painfully, but insistently, the way a sleeping animal shifts toward warmth. I lifted it. In the low sun I could see, for the first time, the fine engraving I'd taken for wear: not a crest, not a sigil, but a single word in old court script, worn nearly to nothing.

*Amaureth.*

I didn't know the word. And yet some locked room deep in me rattled at the sound of it, the way a dog stirs at a whistle pitched too high for human ears.

I broke the veil. It seemed important to do it deliberately—to choose, for once, instead of being chosen for. I hooked my finger through the rotten cord and pulled, and the little white knot came apart into threads, and the door swung inward on a darkness that smelled of dust and candle smoke and, beneath both, something older: cold iron and dried roses.

Inside, the chapel had been stripped bare except for the altar, and except for what lay on the altar.

A box. Lead-lined, seal-crusted, small enough to hold a loaf of bread or a human heart. I knew that kind of box. Every memory-thief does. We call them *cradles*. You use them when a memory is too dangerous to carry loose in a head—too heavy, too sharp, too alive. You seal it in lead and wax and you bury it, and you pray whoever finds it has better sense than to open it.

There was a card pinned to the lid under the wax. The handwriting was round and careful, a child's best script, learned from a guild tutor who rapped knuckles with a ruler.

*I stole this. I don't remember stealing it. If I come back, I'm sorry. —E.J.*

I stood in the ruined chapel while the light died through the empty windows, reading my own apology to myself, and understood that I had written it standing exactly here, in this exact pool of failing sun, decades ago, and then walked out and let someone take even the walking-out from me.

"Well," I said to the child I'd been, "you came back after all."

I broke the seal.

* * *

Memories don't enter gently when they've been caged that long. They come out like floodwater.

I was seven years old, and small even for seven, with the quick light fingers the Guild prizes and the quiet way of moving that means a child learned early that being noticed was dangerous. The chapel was full then—full of candles, full of voices, full of grief. A funeral. I knew funerals were good hunting; grieving people's minds stand open like doors in summer.

But this was no commoner's funeral. The woman in the coffin wore white, and the crown beside her wore gold, and half the court had come to weep beautifully where the right people could see.

She was the queen. Dead of nothing anyone would name aloud. Young. And the whole chapel hummed with a secret so loud it rang like a struck bell—I could hear it the way I could always hear things other thieves couldn't, the loud thoughts, the ones too big to keep.

I slipped along the wall in my borrowed acolyte's robe, doing what I'd been sent to do: lifting trinkets, rings, a silver psalter, whatever came loose. Small thefts. Safe thefts. My rule already lived in me even then, though I hadn't yet found words for it—*never take what you can't give back.*

Then I passed the open coffin, and the queen's eyes were closed, and her mind was not.

That's the truth no guild master teaches, because most thieves never learn it: the dead hold their memories a little while, the way a bell holds its note. And hers were screaming.

I didn't mean to reach in. I was seven. I saw a mind in agony and my hands did what my hands have always done—they helped themselves. I touched two fingers to her cold brow, the way Corvane taught me forty years later in a different lifetime, and I *took*.

I took everything.

Not a trinket. Not a lullaby. I took the whole sealed vault of her—the binding she had performed in this same chapel a year before her death, kneeling on these stones, cutting her palm on a chalice's rim. I took the words of the rite, the old words, the name-rite, older than the kingdom. I took the thing she had bound: a hunger wearing no shape, that had drifted down between the stars and begun to eat the world's remembering one village at a time, until a queen with more courage than sense caught it in a circle of her own blood and nailed it into a crown of gold with words of binding, trading its freedom for its restraint.

I took the price she'd agreed to pay: that the binding would hold only as long as no living tongue spoke the thing's true name. So she'd had the name cut out of every record, every mind, every witness—including, at the end, her own. She had hired the Guild to make her forget her own great work, because a secret that exists in no one cannot be spoken, and a name that is never spoken keeps its prisoner chained.

And I took the name itself. Amaureth. The word engraved on the copper token, the word that had been sitting in a lead cradle on an altar for forty years because a seven-year-old thief stole the lock along with the door.

The queen had made herself the key and thrown the key away. And I had picked the thrown-away key out of the gutter of her dying mind and pocketed it, because my hands were quick and my heart was soft and no one had ever taught me that some doors are locked for everyone's sake.

The vision shifted. I was seven again, stumbling out of the chapel with a stolen mind roaring inside my skull like a sea, too much, far too much, the name burning holes in me. I remembered—through the memory's own eyes—the Guildmaster of that day, young then, Vess's predecessor or perhaps Vess herself with fewer grey hairs, going white when she read what I carried. Remembered the decision made over my head in whispers. *Too dangerous to destroy. Too dangerous to keep awake. Seal it. Seal the girl's taking of it. Let her be a lock that doesn't know it's a lock.*

They hadn't erased my first theft to protect me.

They'd erased it to protect the world—from *me*.

* * *

I came back to myself on the chapel floor with dirt in my mouth and the lead cradle dented where my fingers had gripped it, and the last red light gone from the windows, and the stars—absent, all of them absent, the sky a black lid—pressing down like a held breath.

I lay there a long time.

Here is what I had believed, walking north: that I was a thief hunting the truth, that the truth was a thing outside me, in vaults and ledgers and palaces, and I would find it and carry it home like any other job.

Here is what was true: I was not carrying the key to the disaster. I *was* the key. Sixty years ago a dead queen's final mercy had passed through my childish hands, and the Guild had welded it shut inside me and posted me, all unknowing, as the lock on the world's worst door. Every starless night, every blank-faced village, every guard who forgot his own mother—it had all been waiting on me. On whether the lock would ever, ever turn.

And someone had started turning it. The wards falling, the entry dated tomorrow in my own handwriting, the entity speaking to me in my own voice from the vaults, knowing my real name. Amaureth wasn't reaching for the crown anymore.

It was reaching for its key.

I got up. My knees complained; I let them. I took the copper token from my pocket and looked at the worn word a long moment—*Amaureth*, the name I now held whole and blazing in the recovered memory, the name that must never be said—and I understood, finally, why a dying warden had spent his last strength pressing it into a stranger's hand.

He hadn't known what it was. He'd only known the crown remembers, and that remembering had to go somewhere, and I had been walking toward this chapel since before he was born.

I wrapped the cradle in my cloak. I did not hang a new veil on the door. Some doors, I decided, deserved to stay broken open.

Then I turned south, toward the capital, toward the Guildhouse, toward Vess—who had ordered me to forget what I read, who had known, who had *all* known, for sixty years—and I began to rehearse in my head the conversation that was coming, and the word I would not say, and the question I would:

*Who else knows I'm the lock?*

Because there was one more thing in the queen's memory, one detail I had turned over and over on the dark road like a stone with a sharp edge: the binding had a clause. The queen had built it in, one mercy inside another. The rite could be undone—Amaureth could be unbound forever, cast out beyond the stars it came from—but only by a living voice that held the name, spoken in the presence of the crown, in the words she alone had written.

Only me. Only ever me.

And tomorrow's date was already written in my ledger, in my own hand, and I did not remember writing it.

The stars stayed dark all the way home.
