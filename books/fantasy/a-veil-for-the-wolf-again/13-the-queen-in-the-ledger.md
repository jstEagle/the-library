## 13. The Queen in the Ledger

The memory did not open so much as it opened *me*.

I had expected the sealed thing in my skull to unfold like a letter—paper, ink, a clean line of revelation I could read and set down. That is not how stolen memories behave. They behave like wolves. When I finally unpicked the last of Corvane's knots—his knots, his craft, paid for with the last warm hour of his life—the memory came up through me like water through a drowned man's lungs, and for a long moment I could not tell which of us was drowning.

I was six years old again.

Not my six years—I had no six years; I had learned that much already, standing in the ruins of my own past with a hole in it shaped like a queen—but *a* six-year-old's, small and cold and kneeling on chapel stone that smelled of tallow and old blood. Through the child's eyes I saw the world at knee height: candle stubs guttering in their dishes, a woman's hem dragging past, grey wool gone silver at the edge where it had been mended too many times. The woman knelt across from me. Her hands were shaking.

I knew her face before the memory gave it to me. Of course I did. Every soul in Vhalen knew her face from the coins, from the stained glass, from the great painted panel above the palace doors that tourists pointed at and children were taught to bow toward.

Queen Maerlinde. The Last Light. She who held the dark at the border for forty years and then simply—vanished from history, between one reign and the next, unmentioned, unmourned, as if the kingdom had agreed all at once to forget her.

We had. That was rather the point.

*Hold out your hands,* she said to the child, and her voice was nothing like the voice on the coins. It was hoarse. It was afraid. It was the voice of someone who has run out of every other option and is now bargaining with a thief who still had milk teeth. *Whatever you see, whatever I give you—you will keep it safe. You will never speak it. You will never give it back. Do you understand? Some things are safer forgotten than kept.*

And the child—the child was me, or had been once, before the Guild scraped the name off and wrote *Emrys Joubert* in its place—nodded, because children always nod, because children want so badly to be trusted with something heavy.

Then the queen leaned forward and pressed her forehead to mine, and she *gave*.

* * *

Memory does not transfer whole. It pours. And what poured into that small kneeling child was sixty years of watching.

I saw through the queen's eyes now, and the queen's eyes had seen everything.

I saw her young, crowned too early, standing on the northern wall while the sky above the border rippled like the skin of a struck drum. I saw the first star go out—not fall, not dim, but be *eaten*, its light drawn away in a long silent thread toward something crouched at the edge of the world that had no shape until you looked at it, and then had every shape at once. I saw her learn its name. That was the horror at the center of it, the thing the memory had been sealed against: names are leashes, and she had spent eleven years of her reign doing nothing but listening, in dream and divination and desperate scholarship, until she heard the thing say itself in its sleep.

It had a name. A true one. And a name, spoken rightly, could bind it—or loose it.

I saw her build the rite. I saw her carry it up the mountain to the crown-forges alone, because she trusted no one else with the words, and forge into the circlet of state a cage shaped like a bargain: the entity would wear the crown, and the crown would wear it, and so long as the binding held it would feed only on what the realm consented to forget—the dead's old griefs, the wars' stale shame, the slow sediment of a nation's unwanted past. A kingdom must forget something to stay sane. She made its forgetting into a meal, and the meal into a chain.

I saw it work. God help me, I saw it work beautifully. Forty years of peace. The stars burned. The wards sang.

And then I saw her begin to die.

Not quickly. Memory-thieves know the signs; we live by them. The queen was being eaten from the inside—not by the entity, which could not reach past its leash, but by the *binding*, which required an anchor, a living mind that held the name and fed it to the rite night after night. The anchor burns. That is the price written into any cage built from a word. She had known it when she forged the crown. She had simply assumed she would have more time.

She did not. And there was no heir who could hold the name—her son was kind and shallow and would have spoken it within a year out of curiosity—and if the anchor failed with the name still loose in the world, the entity would tear free of the crown and finish what it had started at the border.

So the queen did the only clever, terrible, unforgivable thing left.

She called the Guild.

* * *

I watched myself watch her, there in the ruined chapel, and I understood at last why the Guild had come to a border village at all, why a Guild broker had stood in a tallow-smelling chapel and offered a child coin for a memory no child should have been able to carry.

Because the Guild had not come for the queen's secret. The Guild had come to take it *apart*.

The memory showed me the negotiation—I saw it over the child's shoulder, the way you see your own past, half-blind and too close. The Guild's man was soft-spoken, grey-gloved, and he laid out the terms with the gentle cruelty of a surgeon describing an amputation. The queen would surrender the name—not destroy it, never destroy it, names cannot be destroyed—to the Guild's keeping. The Guild would seal it inside a living vault, a thief of unusual talent, a child whose gift for holding was so rare she'd been flagged in the ledgers since birth. The child would carry the name without knowing it, buried under seals even she couldn't open. The queen would surrender her own memory of the binding—every detail, the rite, the forging, the eleven years of listening—so that no living mind held the whole truth in one place. Scattered, the knowledge would be safe. Safe from the entity, which hunted names through dreams. Safe from kings, who might be tempted. Safe from everyone.

*And when I die,* the queen said, *who anchors the crown?*

The grey gloves folded. *The Guild will maintain the truce. Your successors need never know there is anything to anchor. The entity feeds on consented forgetting; the Guild will arrange for the consenting. There will be peace, Majesty. There will be stars.*

*I am asking who holds the leash.*

*A ledger,* said the Guild's man, *is stronger than a hand. Hands fail. Ledgers endure.*

I wanted to scream at her. I wanted to scream across sixty years: *Don't sign it, don't trust them, look at their faces*—but I had looked at their faces, hadn't I, for twenty years, every morning over candlelight and cold tea, and I had believed exactly what she believed: that the Guild was a strange, gray, necessary thing, a broom that swept up the world's dangerous remembering. I had believed it because believing it was easier than asking who held the broom.

The queen signed. I watched her sign. Her hand shook worse than it had when she'd knelt to the child, and when it was done she looked at me—at the small, hollow-eyed girl the Guild had brought as a vessel—and something in her face broke and reformed into tenderness.

*You'll remember none of this,* she told me softly. *That is the mercy of it. You'll grow up light. Whatever they took from you to make room—they chose well. Forget me, little one. It's the kindest work you'll ever do.*

And then, because the sealing required a thief and the only thief present was six years old and already gifted, the Guild put my own small hands to work. I watched myself steal from the queen. I watched my tiny fingers hover at her temple, watched the silver thread of her eleven years come away like gossamer, watched her smile as it left her—smile, because forgetting was the mercy, because she *asked* for it, because she was so tired—

The memory ended where the seals began. But I was still kneeling on the chapel floor of my own skull, and there was one thing more, one thing the queen had pressed into the child's hands after the theft, one thing the Guild's man had not seen because he had turned to pack his instruments:

A copper token, stamped with a crown, worn smooth by a thumb.

*If ever the chain slips,* she whispered, *if ever the stars go out—find this place. The rite is written where I wrote everything. Under the altar. In my hand. Only the one who stole it can read it, because only the one who stole it knows what was taken.*

Only the one who stole it can end it.

Me.

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor of Corvane's back room with my cheeks wet and my hands closed around the copper token hard enough to print its crown into my palm.

For a long time I didn't move. Outside, Vhalen went on forgetting itself—a cart rattled past with no driver who remembered where he was going, a neighbor's voice rose and fell in a conversation that had lost its nouns—and I lay there in the dust of a dead man's house, holding the oldest guilt in the kingdom in my two hands.

Every thread led back to me. Not metaphorically. *Literally.* The wards fell because the binding weakened, and the binding weakened because the anchor-rite had been scattered, and the scattering had passed through my hands. The entity wore the crown because a queen had caged it there, and the queen's sacrifice had been erased by the Guild, and the erasure had been carried—indefinitely, perfectly, unknowingly—by me. I was not the lock. I was worse. I was the lock, the key, and the courier who had delivered both into the wrong cellar and forgotten the address.

And the redaction—Vess's precious redaction, the entry blacked out in every ledger but mine—now I understood that too. The Guild hadn't hidden the bargain to protect the realm. They'd hidden it to protect the *arrangement*. Sixty years of feeding the entity the nation's consented forgetting, sixty years of the Guild deciding what a kingdom should be allowed to lose, sixty years of power dressed up as custodianship. The queen had signed a leash. The Guild had turned it into a revenue stream. And when the entity grew hungry enough to start taking what wasn't consensual—when the stars began going out—it wasn't betrayal of the bargain. It was the bargain, working as designed, with the Guild's fingerprints sanded off every copy of the page.

Except mine. Except the one they'd given to the vessel, because the vessel was supposed to be a vault, and vaults aren't meant to be opened.

They just hadn't counted on the vault learning to pick locks.

I sat up. My body ached the way it aches after grief or fever, every joint reporting in. Corvane's chair sat empty by the window where he'd died smiling and nameless, and I found I couldn't look at it yet, so I looked at the token instead. Copper, cheap, the stamp of a crown that any mint in Vhalen could produce by the thousand. But the queen had said *under the altar*, and the altar of this chapel had been cracked down the middle for as long as the chapel had been ruined, and I had walked past it twice without thinking, because I hadn't yet been the person who knew to think.

I got up. My legs held. That surprised me a little.

Under the altar, in a cavity behind a loose foundation stone that fit my fingers as though it had been waiting sixty years for exactly these fingers, I found a thin sheet of lead, rolled tight, inscribed in a hand that trembled only at the beginning and steadied toward the end. The queen's hand. The rite of unbinding, complete—the name's true spelling rendered not in letters but in a notation of silences, gaps, the shapes of the pauses around a word, because she had understood better than anyone alive that the dangerous part of a name is not saying it but *stopping*. Instructions for the vessel. Warnings. And at the bottom, in smaller script, almost shy:

*To the one who keeps this: I am sorry for what was taken from you to make room. If you are reading this, then the mercy failed, and you have grown into the kind of person who opens sealed things. Good. Sealed things are how we got here. Finish it, and then—forget me properly, at last. Not because the Guild orders it. Because you choose what deserves remembering, and I would rather be your choice than their erasure.*

I read it three times. Then I rolled the lead sheet closed and tucked it against my heart, where thieves keep the things they intend to keep.

The guilt was still there. It would always be there—sixty years of a queen's sacrifice sitting in a child's skull like a stone at the bottom of a well, and me walking around above it complaining about my loneliness. But guilt is a currency like anything else, and I had spent my whole life learning what currencies could buy. This one bought clarity. Every thread led back to me, yes. Which meant every thread ran *through* me, and threads that run through a thief's hands can be cut, spliced, rewoven.

The entity had a name. I had carried it since I was six years old. Tonight I intended to learn how to say it—and tomorrow I would walk it down into the dark beneath the palace and introduce it to its cage.

I blew out Corvane's candle, out of some thief's superstition about leaving a lit flame in a dead man's house, and stepped out into a starless street.

I had gone perhaps fifty paces when I saw them: three figures at the far corner, grey-cloaked, moving with the particular unhurried certainty of people who know exactly where their quarry will be. Guild retrieval. Vess hadn't waited for my answer after all.

I turned and ran, and behind me, faint and courteous as a dinner invitation, came the sound of a bell being rung backward.
