## 14. Vess Closes the Book

The Guildhouse knew I was coming before I did.

I felt it in the door—the veil over the threshold shivering as I crossed it, tasting my intentions the way a spider tastes its web. Sixty years of quiet craft woven into that hanging cloth, and tonight it read me like an open ledger. *She knows. She knows and she's come home anyway.*

I let it taste me. There was no point hiding from a building that had raised me.

Inside, the long hall was lit for company. That was the first wrongness. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its hours by candle-stub and thrift; we were thieves, not courtiers. But every sconce along the corridor burned full, and the air smelled of Vess's imported smokeleaf, sweet and heavy as a confession.

The second wrongness was the silence. No apprentices scrubbing ink from their fingers. No old Corvane's chair creaking in the corner—though Corvane was beyond chairs now, nameless and smiling in his grave behind the tannery row. No one at all. Just the hall stretching ahead of me like a throat.

Vess sat at the head table with the master ledger open before her, her hands folded on its covers as if in prayer. She had not changed since I'd last seen her—she never changed, which should have been my first clue about what she was willing to pay to stay exactly herself—but tonight there was something new in her face. Not anger. Something worse.

Relief.

"Emrys." My name in her mouth sounded like a key turning. "You read it."

Not a question. I stopped three tables away, close enough to see the ledger, far enough to reach the side door if this went the way I suspected it would go. "You left it unredacted in my copy," I said. "In *my* copy only. That wasn't sloppiness."

"No." She rose, smoothing her coat the way she did before difficult negotiations, and I realized with a cold turn in my stomach that she was treating this as a negotiation. "It was an inheritance. Sit down, child."

"I'm not your child."

"You've been mine since you were nine years old and stole your own name out of your mother's mouth to stop yourself crying for her." She said it gently, and that gentleness was the cruelest thing she could have done, because it was true. "Sit. We have perhaps an hour before the others return from the field houses, and what I have to say must be said once, plainly, and then never again."

I didn't sit. I'd learned that much from watching her: never take the seat offered across a bargaining table until you know what's under it.

So Vess sat alone, and spoke to me standing, like a priest to a stubborn congregation.

* * *

"The bargain is older than me," she began. "Older than my predecessor. It was struck in the reign of the king who is still, officially, on the throne—a man who has now worn the crown for sixty years without aging a day, and no one in this city has ever thought to find that strange, because the price of noticing was written into the terms."

"The entity," I said. "The thing in the crown."

"The *tenant*," Vess corrected, and her voice did not waver, and I hated how steady it was. "It came out of the dark between stars when the sky was still full of them. It does not eat flesh. It eats remembering—the way a fire eats a forest, all at once or slowly, but always completely. The queen bound it into the crown because a crown can be contained. A kingdom cannot. And the binding required a payment: memory. Ours. Everyone's. A tithe of forgetting, collected nightly, so small no one would miss it—and so constant that over sixty years it has hollowed out more than any single feast ever could."

"And the Guild brokered it." My hands were steady. I made them steady. "Our Guild. Thieves. We sold the whole realm's past for peace."

"We *negotiated* it," Vess said, with the faint, terrible pride of a woman describing her finest work. "Do you understand what the alternative was? Do you understand what stood outside those border wards before they fell? I have read the accounts, Emrys. Whole valleys where the rivers forgot how to run downhill. Children born without faces because no one remembered faces. The entity unbound is not a disaster. It is an *editing*. Of everything. Forever."

"And so we became its clerks."

"We became its *wardens*." She leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the grey at her temples, the deep lines around her mouth—lines carved by sixty years of carrying this. "Every redaction in every ledger. Every thief sent to quietly steal back a rumor, a suspicion, a song that remembered too much. You think the Guild steals memories, child? The Guild is a dam. We hold back the flood, and we have held it for sixty years, and you—" her finger came up, pointing at me across the candle-smoke, "—you are standing in the spillway asking why the water is wet."

"The wards fell," I said. "The stars went out. Your dam is broken, Vess. Whatever truce you protected, it's already ending."

Something moved behind her eyes then—something I had never seen in forty years of reading faces professionally. Fear, yes, but underneath it, older: *grief.* The grief of a woman who has spent six decades telling herself a story and has just heard it crack.

"That is why you must not speak the name," she said quietly.

And there it was. The heart of it, laid bare on the table between us like a stolen watch.

* * *

"You found it in the ledger," Vess said. "The entry. The queen's rite. Her name-rite—the words that bind, and the name they bind. You read it, and you understood it, because you are the finest thief this Guild has produced in three generations, and because—" she paused, choosing the words like a surgeon choosing blades, "—because part of it was already yours. Was always yours. Do you know why the redaction failed in your copy? Why your ledger alone kept the truth?"

I said nothing. My heart said it for me, loud enough that I half-feared she could hear it.

"Because you wrote the original," Vess said. "Because you were there. Because the first theft you ever made—the one someone cut out of you, the hole shaped like a missing entry—you stole the entity's true name from the dying queen's mind, and the ledger recorded it, and ledgers do not forget what their keepers refuse to. Ink remembers, Emrys. Even when we bleed it white."

The room tilted. I gripped the edge of a table and the wood was real and cold and that was the only thing keeping me upright.

"You knew," I managed. "You've known what I am since—"

"Since the night they brought you in, nine years old, with a gap in your skull where your childhood used to be and a copper token clutched in your fist." Vess's voice softened, and I wished to God it hadn't. "We didn't cut you, child. We found you already cut. Someone sealed that theft inside you so deep that even you couldn't find it—and the Guild let it stay buried, because a lock works best when it doesn't know it's a lock."

"A lock."

"The name cannot be spoken safely by anyone who merely *knows* it. Knowing is not enough—it must be carried, carried in a vessel strong enough to hold it without speaking it, without dreaming it, without dying and letting it spill. You are that vessel. You have been since you were a child kneeling in a ruined chapel with a queen's last breath in your hands." She spread her hands, palms up, an offering or a surrender. "Sixty years the Guild has guarded the redaction. Sixty years we have kept the world from learning the name, because the moment it is spoken aloud—by anyone, anywhere, in fear or fury or foolish hope—the truce ends. The tenant wakes fully. The binding frays. And everything the queen bought with her life, everything we have paid in forgetting to preserve, burns in a single night."

"So the plan is forever," I said. "Forever forgetting. Forever feeding it. Forever pretending the king is a man."

"The plan is *peace*."

"The plan is a slow amputation and calling the bleeding mercy!" My voice cracked off the stone walls and I didn't care. "Vess, I've *seen* what's past the wards. Villages where people stand in their own doorways trying to remember their children's names. A warden died holding a copper token because it was the last thing he could still recognize. The dam isn't holding. It hasn't been holding for years. You've just been standing on it refusing to look down."

For a long moment neither of us moved. The candles burned. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled over a starless sky.

Then Vess closed the master ledger, softly, the way one closes a coffin, and when she looked up at me her face had gone smooth and final as a signed contract.

"I had hoped you would choose wisdom," she said. "I truly had. So let me make you the offer I was instructed to make, and let us both pretend it is generous."

* * *

"Partnership," she said. "You and I, keepers of the redaction together. Full access to the archives. Your hoard restored—all of it, every stolen memory the Guild holds in trust, including the ones taken from you. Your mother's face. Your first winter. Everything in the hole." She watched me flinch and pressed the blade home with terrible kindness. "Yes. We know what's in the hole. We've always known. Say yes, Emrys, and you'll have yourself back entire—and the world keeps its slow, gentle forgetting, and no one else has to die tonight."

"And if I say no?"

"Oblivion." No hesitation. No apology. "There is a craft for it. Corvane taught you the edges of it before he died—I know he did, he was sentimental that way. A thief can steal from herself until nothing remains worth stealing. The Guild refined that craft long ago. You would not suffer. You would simply... stop being a problem. A girl with no past, no name, no memory of ledgers or crowns or names-that-must-not-be-spoken. Sweeping floors in some field house, content, empty, safe." She smiled, and it was the smile she wore at funerals. "Most thieves, offered this choice, weep and say yes. It is not a cruelty, Emrys. It is the kindest door in the building."

I looked at her—at the woman who had fed me, trained me, taught me to balance a ledger to the last copper and to never steal what I couldn't give back—and I finally understood that she believed every word. That was the horror of it. She wasn't lying. She had simply spent so long guarding the dam that she'd forgotten there was anything on either side of it worth drowning.

"You're asking me to choose between being your partner and being your victim," I said. "Those aren't choices, Vess. They're two ways of closing the same book."

"The book *must* be closed."

"Then I'm choosing a third thing."

Her eyes narrowed. "There is no third thing."

"There's always a third thing. That's the first rule of theft." I took a step backward, toward the side door, and saw her register the movement—saw her hand drift, casual as a yawn, toward the bell-cord that would bring the wardens up from the lower floors. "You want to know what I learned from the queen's memory, Vess? From the real one, the one in my head, not your redacted copies?"

"Don't."

"The binding doesn't end when the name is spoken." Another step. The door was ten feet away. Eight. "That's the lie you've been guarding for sixty years. The name spoken in *fear* breaks the truce—that part's true. But the queen left a rite. A full rite. Speak the name as she spoke it—as a *gift*, freely given, poured through a willing vessel—and the binding doesn't fray. It *completes*. It locks the thing out of the crown and out of the world, forever, and the only price is everything the vessel carries."

Vess had gone very still. "You can't possibly—"

"I'm the vessel," I said. "You told me so yourself. You built your whole peace on top of me and never once asked what I'd want to do about it."

"*Emrys.*" Her voice dropped into the register she used for breaking thieves' wills, low and resonant and absolute. "If you attempt this, you will not fail heroically. You will succeed *partially*. You will crack the binding halfway and unleash something that makes the fallen wards look like a spilled cup of tea. You will kill everyone you have ever met, and worse—you will make them *forget they were killed*. Is that your justice? Is that your grand third choice?"

"It's mine," I said. "That's the point of it."

My hand found the door.

"Partnership or oblivion," Vess said, rising at last, and for one naked instant I saw her not as the Guildmaster but as an old woman alone in a burning house, insisting the fire was warmth. "Choose, child. Please. For once in your life, choose the door I'm holding open."

"I did," I said. "You just couldn't see it."

I pulled the door and ran.

* * *

Behind me the bell began to ring—deep bronze strokes rolling up through the Guildhouse bones, the alarm I'd heard only twice in my life, both times for fires. The corridors erupted. Boots on stairs. Voices calling positions in the old cant: *west wall, roof line, seal the yard.*

They knew me. That was the worst of it. Every warden in the building had been trained by me or trained by someone I'd trained; they knew my habits, my shortcuts, my favorite exits. The Guildhouse wasn't a building to me. It was a hand, and it was closing.

So I did the one thing no warden chases well: I stopped running like a thief.

I turned and walked—walked, chin up, hands visible—straight through the kitchen, nodding to the two young wardens who froze there with drawn knives and wide eyes. "*Emrys?*" one breathed, and I put a finger to my lips like we were children sharing a secret, and I walked out through the scullery door into the rain-slick yard while they stood blinking at each other, uncertain whether the intruder they'd been ordered to catch was the woman who'd taught them to catch intruders.

By the time the confusion broke, I was over the tannery wall and into the warren of streets below, where the lamps burned blue and the fog came up off the river thick as wool.

But they wouldn't stay confused. Vess would see to that. Within the hour my face would be on every board in the capital—not as a criminal, worse, as a *risk*, a walking redaction, a girl-shaped hole in the world's safety. The Guild had hunted kings' secrets for sixty years. It would hunt me with everything it had, and it would hunt gently, sorrowfully, with genuine love in every net it threw.

I pressed my hand against the copper token in my pocket—the dead warden's gift, warm as a heartbeat—and let the ruined chapel rise in my mind like a lighthouse.

Because here was what Vess had missed, in all her sixty years of careful bookkeeping: she'd told me the rite required a willing vessel. She'd told me the price was everything the vessel carried.

She'd assumed I'd flinch at the price.

But I had spent my whole life stealing memories I could never give back, hoarding other people's yesterdays in a vault behind my ribs, and I had learned the one lesson the Guild never meant to teach: *nothing carried is heavier than nothing kept.*

The chapel lay south, past the districts where the street signs had begun to blank themselves. I could hear, faintly, from somewhere near the river, a crowd chanting a single sentence over and over like a drumbeat against the dark—the princess's work, it had to be, the king's last words repeated as a ward. They were still fighting. The city was still fighting.

Then so was I.

I pulled my hood up against the blue-lit rain and ran south, toward the chapel, toward the name in my mouth, while behind me the Guildhouse bells rang and rang and did not stop.
