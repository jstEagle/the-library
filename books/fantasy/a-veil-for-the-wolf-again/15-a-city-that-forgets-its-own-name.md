## 15. A City That Forgets Its Own Name

The first word to go was *bread*.

Isolde noticed it at dawn, in the kitchens below the east wing, where she had gone to count heads because counting heads was something she could still do. The baker's boy stood before his tray with his mouth working like a fish's, pointing at the loaves, and when Isolde asked him what was wrong he said, "I can't— it's the—" and his hand made a shape in the air, round and warm, a shape that had once meant bread.

By noon there were forty-seven words missing from the palace. By dusk, no one was counting anymore, because no one remembered that counting had been the point.

She kept her own tally anyway, scratched into the margin of her father's old hunting almanac with a stylus pressed so hard it tore the paper. *Bread. Bell. Brother.* She wrote them as they fell, each one a small death she witnessed alone. The words did not vanish all at once; they went the way stars had gone, quietly, one by one, so that you only noticed the dark after it had settled over everything.

*They are eating us from the edges inward,* she thought. *First the borders. Then the villages. Now the words themselves.*

Her guards still knew her face. That was the mercy of the morning, at least. But she had watched Ser Aldric pause at her door two days ago, brow furrowed, hand resting on his sword hilt for three full seconds before recognition crawled back into his eyes — and she had seen what lived behind those seconds. Not doubt. Not suspicion. Just an open, patient blankness, like a room with the furniture carried out.

The court had thinned to perhaps sixty souls who still functioned: the ones who chanted, the ones who held ritual like rope over a ravine. The rest wandered the corridors with pleasant, empty smiles, doing tasks they no longer understood, folding linens into shapes that meant nothing, watering plants that had died weeks ago.

Isolde climbed the west stair to the bell tower because the bell tower was high and because climbing it was part of the litany now — *I climb, therefore I am not yet forgotten* — and from its parapet she could see the city.

What she saw made her grip the stone until her knuckles whitened.

The street signs were blank.

Not defaced, not weathered, not painted over. *Blank* — smooth rectangles of wood and iron where letters had been, as if the words had been lifted off them like wet ink blotted away. And below, in the squares, she could see people standing before those signs, mouths moving, hands gesturing, asking one another questions that no longer had vocabulary to hold them.

A woman in the fish market was weeping without sound. Beside her, a man held up a child and turned slowly in a circle, as though looking for someone whose name he had just realized he'd never known.

*This is how a city dies,* Isolde thought. *Not in fire. In silence.*

* * *

She found the High Cantor in the chapel of the Moonlit Vigil, kneeling before an altar he no longer seemed able to address. Old Maren had served the palace for fifty years; his voice had once been called the finest in the realm, a voice that could make grown soldiers weep at evening prayers. Now he knelt with his lips moving through the opening words of the Litany of Dawn and stopping, always stopping, at the same place — a gap in the prayer like a missing tooth.

"The third line," he said when he heard her boots on the stone. He didn't turn around. "Highness, I've lost the third line. It was about the light. It was about how the light comes back." His shoulders shook. "It was about how the light *always* comes back, and I can't remember how we said it."

Isolde knelt beside him. Up close she could see the forgetting working in him like frost working through a windowpane — beautiful, in a terrible way, spreading patterns across something that used to be clear.

"Maren," she said. "Look at me."

He turned. His eyes were still his own. Mostly.

"I need you to do something harder than remembering," she said. "I need you to *hold*. Can you hold?"

"Whatever Your Highness requires."

"No." She took his face in both hands, felt the papery skin, the tremor beneath. "Not whatever I require. This specifically. There is a sentence. My father's sentence. You will teach it to everyone who can still learn, and you will make them say it morning, noon, and night, until their throats are raw and their children dream in it. Do you understand?"

"What sentence, Highness?"

And here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she had burned into herself in the long nights after the stars went out, pressing it into her own mind like a seal into wax, over and over, because she had understood — earlier than anyone, maybe even earlier than Emrys Joubert — that the entity fed on memory, but it could not eat what was being said *now*, aloud, by many mouths at once. A spoken word belonged to the moment. It was too hot, too alive, too communal to swallow. Memory was a storehouse. Speech was a fire.

The entity could raid storehouses. It could not put out every fire.

"My father," Isolde said, "is not my father. Has not been for longer than either of us knew. But the man he was — the true king, the one who wore this crown first — spoke last words before the thing took him. Words no one heard, because they were stolen along with everything else." She had recovered them weeks ago, in the vaults, from a thief with tired eyes and a ledger full of sins — words pulled out of the deep places where devoured memories went to be caged. "But they were kept. They survived. And they are these."

She leaned close, and she gave him the sentence.

Maren's eyes went wide. His lips formed it silently once, twice, testing the weight of it. And then something happened in his face that Isolde would carry for the rest of her life, however long that proved to be: the frost receded. Not everywhere, not forever, but around that one sentence, the windowpane cleared, and behind it she saw the old cantor whole and burning.

"That's—" he whispered. "That's a king's voice. I can hear it. How can I hear it?"

"Because it's true," Isolde said. "Truth holds better than anything. Now get up. We have work."

* * *

They gathered in the great hall at sundown, the sixty who remained, and Isolde stood them in rows on the cold mosaic floor — servants and seneschals, knights and kitchen girls, the chamberlain whose name she had to prompt him for and the scullery boy who still, stubbornly, knew his own.

She stood before them in her father's — in the *king's* — plain traveling cloak, no crown, no regalia, because crowns were the problem and she wanted nothing between her people and her face.

"You have lost things," she began. "Names. Words. Faces you loved. Some of you have lost the knowledge of what you've lost, which is the cruelest theft of all." Murmurs ran down the rows. A woman near the back began to cry. "I will not lie to you. I cannot give back what has been taken. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. But I will tell you what I know, and then I will ask something terrible of you, and you may refuse, and I will love you for refusing just as I would love you for agreeing."

Silence. Even the weeping woman stilled.

"I know what is eating us. I know why the stars went out. I know why the wards fell and the villages went quiet and the signs went blank. It is old, and it is hungry, and it lives above us in the crown my father wears — the shell of my father — and it feeds on remembering the way we feed on bread." She let that land. Let them feel the horror of it fully, because she had learned that horror, named plainly, was easier to carry than horror unnamed. "It has eaten a kingdom's worth of memory already. It will eat ours too, down to our names, down to the words in our mouths, unless we become something it cannot swallow."

The chamberlain raised a trembling hand. "Highness — forgive me — but what can flesh do against a thing like that?"

"*This,*" Isolde said, and she taught them the sentence.

She said it once, alone, her voice ringing off the vaulted stone. Then she said it again and beckoned them in, and Maren — blessed, half-erased Maren — picked it up on the second pass and carried it, his ruined-and-restored voice wrapping around hers, and then the front row caught it, and then the second row, and by the fourth repetition the great hall was full of it, sixty voices speaking a dead king's last words in unison, and the sound was —

The sound was a wall.

Isolde felt it happen. Felt the sentence stop being language and become architecture. Each voice laid a stone; each repetition mortared it. And somewhere far above, in the crown, in the dark, something vast turned its attention toward the palace and found — for the first time in sixty years — a door it could not open.

The candles guttered. The blue flicker that had haunted the palace lamps since the night the stars vanished flared, strained, and steadied back to gold.

Sixty people felt it. Sixty people kept chanting anyway, tears streaming down faces, voices cracking, because they could feel it too — the thing outside the wall, pressing, pressing, and failing.

When Isolde finally raised her hand and let them stop, the silence afterward was not empty. It was full, the way a drawn bow is full.

"Morning, noon, and night," she told them, hoarse. "In the halls, in the kitchens, in your beds. Teach it to anyone who can still learn. If you meet someone who has lost their words, do not grieve and move on — take their hands and put the sentence into their mouths. It is a rope. Hold the rope."

Old Maren stepped forward, and in a voice that had not sounded like that in decades, led them once more.

* * *

Three days later, the chanting reached the streets.

It spread the way rumors spread, the way fires spread — a servant's sister in the Tanner's Quarter, a knight's old comrade at the river gate. People came to the palace gates asking not for food or justice but for *the words*, and Isolde ordered the gates opened and the sentence given freely, to any mouth that could hold it. Her chamberlain protested — crowds, disease, panic — and she looked at him until he remembered himself, or enough of himself, and bowed.

On the fifth day she walked the capital incognito, hood up, four guards who still knew her name trailing at a distance, and she saw what the sentence had built.

The Tanner's Quarter rang with it at dawn, a hundred rough voices rising from doorways and rooftops. At the fish market — the same square where the woman had wept without sound — vendors had painted the sentence on their stalls in crude letters, some misspelled, and the misspellings made Isolde laugh until she cried, because a misspelled truth was still a truth, and the errors proved that living mouths were carrying it, not stone. Children skipped rope to it. A drunk sang it badly in a tavern and the whole room corrected him, gently, together.

And in the squares, the blank signs remained blank — but beneath them, someone had chalked the sentence over and over until the walls were white with it, and the people who had forgotten where they lived stood before those walls and read, and wept, and remembered *something*, even if only the feeling of belonging to a voice larger than their own.

It was not victory. Isolde knew that. The city was still bleeding words; that morning she had heard a mother call her child by a name that dissolved halfway out of her mouth, and the child had answered anyway, answered to the tone, because tone was older than language and the entity had not yet learned to steal it. Whole districts still drifted. The palace itself grew quieter each day, more of its corridors filling with pleasant, hollow-eyed wanderers.

But the sentence held. The sentence *spread*. And each dawn, when the bells — the ones that still remembered they were bells — rang out over the capital, they rang over a city that answered.

*We are not a storehouse,* Isolde thought, watching the sunrise catch ten thousand chimneys, ten thousand voices rising like smoke. *You cannot raid us. We are a fire, and we are teaching each other to burn.*

* * *

On the seventh night, the thing in the crown spoke to her.

She was alone in her chambers, adding new fallen words to the almanac's margins — *harvest, harbor, her* — when the candlelight went blue and the air pressure changed, the way it does before a storm or a confession. A voice filled the room. It used her father's warmth. It used her own cadence. It used, horribly, the voice of her nurse from when she was six, singing the lullaby whose melody she could no longer recall.

*Princess,* it said. *You are spending yourself like a miser's last coin.*

"You don't get to talk to me," Isolde said aloud, and kept writing.

*I could give it all back. Every word. Bread and bell and brother. Your nurse's lullaby. Your father's face, true and laughing. One bargain. You know how bargains work — your Guild wrote the book on them.*

She set down the stylus. Her hand was steady. She was surprised by that.

"My father's last words," she said, "were a refusal. I've read them. I chant them. And so does everyone in this city, morning, noon, and night." She rose and faced the blue-lit dark. "So here is mine: no. Find another door."

The silence stretched. Then, softly, almost tenderly:

*There is a thief coming down, little princess. Down beneath us, to the well. When she arrives — and she will arrive — you will want to be holding your rope very tightly indeed.*

The candleflame snapped back to gold. The presence withdrew like a tide going out, leaving the room ordinary and cold.

Isolde stood a long moment, heart hammering, the almanac open on the desk. *A thief coming down.* She knew exactly which thief. The one with tired eyes and a ledger full of sins, the one who had pulled a dead king's last words out of the dark and handed them to a stranger because it was right.

Emrys Joubert was going to the well. Alone. Into the place where the stolen stars hung caged and the taken memories circled like fish in black water.

Isolde snuffed the candle, slung the king's plain cloak over her shoulders, and went to wake the sixty.

If the thief was walking into the dark, then the dark was about to hear a kingdom's worth of voices at its back — and Isolde intended to lead every one of them down.
