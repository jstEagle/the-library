## 16. The Long Way Down

The queen's memory did not give directions the way a map does. It gave them the way a fever does—sudden, burning, and impossible to argue with.

I stood in the palace's east cellar among barrels of salted eel and candle-stubs, and the memory rose in me like water finding a crack: *down, and left where the stone is warm, and down again until the stone forgets to be stone.* I pressed my palm against the wall and felt it—warmth, faint as a sleeping animal's breath, seeping through granite that had no business being anything but cold.

"You're certain about this," Isolde said behind me. She had insisted on coming as far as the cellar, wrapped in a servant's cloak that fooled no one, her jaw set in the particular way it set when she was losing an argument with herself.

"About which part?" I asked. "The part where I climb into the thing's throat, or the part where I do it alone?"

"Both."

"Then no." I checked my kit one last time by feel: the copper token warm in its pouch, three vials of blank ink for writing on nothing, Corvane's knife—not for cutting flesh but for cutting *loose*, though he'd never gotten the chance to teach me what that meant in practice. He'd spent his last lesson dying nameless and smiling, and I had carried both the technique and the grief of it down every corridor since. "But certain isn't what this is. This is the only door left, and doors don't care if you're ready."

Isolde looked at me for a long moment. In the blue light of the failing lamps her face seemed carved from the same stuff as the city outside—beautiful, cracking, refusing.

"When you find him," she said, "when you find whatever is left of my father under all of it—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, quickly and at cost. "Tell him Isolde kept the candles lit. He'll know what it means. Or he would have, once."

"I will."

"And Emrys." She stepped closer. "If the thing offers you something down there—and it will; things like that always have a price on the shelf—it will offer it in your own voice. Don't trust any voice you recognize. Not even mine."

I almost laughed. Almost. "Noted, Your Highness."

She held out her hand then, and I took it, and we stood like that for one breath—two women who had known each other eleven days and trusted each other further than either had trusted anyone in years—before she let go first and turned back up the stairs without looking at me. That was mercy, of a kind. She knew I couldn't afford to watch her go.

* * *

The warm seam in the wall opened when I pressed the copper token flat against it.

Not dramatically. No grinding of ancient gears, no gout of dust. The stone simply stopped agreeing to be a wall, the way a lie stops holding when you press it in the right place, and there was a corridor beyond that had never been built so much as *remembered*—rough-hewn, older than the palace above it, older than the dynasty, sloping down at an angle that made my inner ear file a quiet complaint.

The queen's memory walked with me. It wasn't a voice anymore; it had become more like weather, a pressure system of knowing that moved through me in gusts. When the passage forked, the pressure pulled left. When the air grew thick enough to chew, it eased, telling me I was still on the grain of the truth. Once, passing a niche where someone centuries dead had left a stub of tallow candle, the memory flared so hard I staggered—the queen had stood in this exact spot, young and terrified, with a stolen name folded up inside her like a letter she didn't dare read—and for a moment I was two people in one body, a girl from sixty years ago and a woman from now, both of us descending toward the same hunger with the same terrible cargo.

*I'm sorry,* I told her, the way I'd told her every hour since the chapel. *I'm sorry I took it from you.*

She didn't answer. The dead rarely do, and besides, she hadn't been dead yet, in the memory. She'd been standing exactly where I stood, doing exactly what I was doing, and look how that had ended: bound entity, hollowed king, dark sky, a kingdom dissolving into forgetting one syllable at a time. If there was a lesson in it, it was that good intentions descend very well and come back up badly.

I counted my steps instead of thinking. Four hundred steps down, the walls began to sweat. Six hundred, and the warmth became heat, and the heat began to have texture—not fire-heat but body-heat, the close damp warmth of a mouth. Eight hundred steps, and I heard it.

Singing.

No—that wasn't right either. It was the sound a memory makes when it's being eaten. I'd heard it once before, small-scale, the night I'd watched a drunk in the Saltmarket forget his daughter's face mid-sentence; his words had gone soft and vowel-less, melting off the bone of meaning. This was that sound multiplied past counting, layered past harmony, a cathedral choir of everything the crown had ever swallowed, still singing because songs are the last thing memories lose.

My hands were shaking. I noticed it the way you notice rain starting—distantly, academically—and kept walking, because the alternative was standing in the dark listening to the world be digested, and I had not come eight hundred steps to develop a new fear. I had plenty. They were organized.

* * *

The well was not a well.

That was my first thought, absurdly pedantic, standing on the lip of a cavern that had no business existing beneath a palace built on river clay: *someone should update the ledgers, because this is mislabeled.* A well implies water, depth, a narrow throat. This was a vastness. The cavern opened around and below me like the inside of a bell, and every surface of it—walls, floor, the impossible ceiling far overhead—was covered in light.

Stars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Caged stars hung in the dark like lanterns in a festival gone wrong, each one dimmed to a coal of itself, each one wrapped in coils of something that was not rope and not shadow but *absence given tension*—the same stuff, I understood with a lurch, as the redaction in the Guild ledger, the same stuff as the hole where my mother should have been. The entity hadn't just eaten the sky. It had eaten the sky's remembering, and here was the pantry.

And between the stars, drifting slow as snowfall through deep water, everything else.

Memories. Devoured ones, whole ones, fragments wearing other fragments like borrowed coats. I saw a wedding dance turning endlessly on itself, bride and groom blurred to two smudges of joy. I saw a harvest festival, a ship launching, a dog running through wheat with its ears back. I saw languages I didn't know and griefs I recognized anyway. Whole villages' worth of ordinary Tuesdays, floating in the dark, waiting to be unmade into fuel.

The awe hit me first, and I want to be honest about that, because what came after shames the awe and I'd rather record them in order. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I am a thief; beauty is my trade's raw material and I am not easily stunned. Every stolen moment I had ever catalogued—a bottled lullaby, a debt paid in shadows, a soldier's last sight of home—was a single note of this chord. The Guild had taught me that memories were currency. Down here I learned they were also *light*, and I had spent my whole life trading candleflames while this ocean burned.

Then the vertigo came, and it came with teeth.

Because the cavern was vast, yes—but it was also *shaped*. The drift of the memories followed currents, and the currents spiraled inward, down toward a center I could not see, and my eyes kept trying to follow them and my body kept insisting that following them meant falling, and there was no floor within a hundred feet of me except the narrow ledge the queen's memory had delivered me to, barely wide enough for two feet and a decision.

I knelt. It seemed wise. Great revelations and I have historically gotten along better at kneeling height.

"That's far enough."

The voice came from everywhere, which was the first warning. The second was that it was mine.

I turned, slowly, one hand on Corvane's knife, and found myself looking at the well's center after all—or rather, the center had come to me. It stood at the edge of the ledge where there had been nothing: a figure made of the same absence-coils that bound the stars, wearing shape the way a puppet wears strings, and it had my face. My face as I'd seen it last in a cracked mirror in the Saltmarket—tired, sharp-eyed, candlelit—with an expression I had never worn in my life: serene. Welcoming. A shopkeeper's smile.

"You've brought it back to me," the thing said, in my voice, and it looked at the copper token in my hand with something that might have been longing, if hunger can be called that when it's had sixty years to refine itself. "After all this time. Little thief. Do you know how long I have waited for someone to carry my name back down to me?"

"It's not your name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "It's the queen's leash. She stole it fair and square, and I stole it from her, and theft transfers title. Ask any Guildman."

The thing laughed, and my own laugh rang back at me off the walls of the dark, and somewhere above us a star flickered in its cage like a struck match.

"Clever," it said. "Vess said you'd be clever. Vess said many things." It drifted closer, and the cold of it reached me before its shape did, a cold like the space where a tooth used to be. "But you didn't come all this way to talk about names. You came because you felt it, didn't you? The moment you crossed my threshold. The pull." Its smile widened by a degree, patient as geology. "Look at them, Emrys. Look at what I keep. Everything lost, everything forgotten, everyone's mother and everyone's Tuesday, all of it safe, all of it *here*. You could wander these waters for a hundred years and never run out of wonder. And somewhere in all of it—"

It gestured, a small courteous motion, a maître d' indicating a table.

"—is you."

I looked. I hadn't meant to. That was the trap of it, and I think some part of me knew it was a trap and looked anyway, because I am a thief and thieves are constitutionally incapable of leaving a locked box unexamined.

Out in the drift, maybe forty feet from the ledge, moving with the slow current: a girl.

Seven years old, maybe eight. Barefoot. Dark hair cut short and crooked, the way hair gets cut when a child does it herself with a stolen knife. She wore a coat too big for her with one button missing, and she was laughing at something—laughing the whole-bodied, graceless laugh of a child who has not yet learned what laughter costs—and she was holding hands with a woman whose face I could not see, whose face my eyes slid off of like rain off glass, because that face lived in the hole inside me, the edited place, the gap shaped exactly like a mother.

I knew the coat. I knew the laugh. I had heard that laugh in dreams I'd told myself were inventions, and here it was, archived, catalogued, *kept*—my own childhood self, floating in the belly of the thing that ate the sky, filed away among the devoured things like a pressed flower in a monster's book.

"No," I said. My voice cracked on it, and I hated that, and the thing heard the crack and its smile deepened, drinking it.

"Yes," it said gently, in my voice, with my gentleness. "You see now. You were always mine, little thief. The Guild only borrowed you. Come down. Come into the water. Everything they took from you is here, and everything you never got to have is here too—I keep those as well; I am very thorough—and you need never again be a woman made of gaps. You need only stop climbing back up."

The current turned the girl, slowly, and for half a heartbeat her laughing face pointed at me across the dark, and her eyes—

Her eyes found mine. Seven years old and dead sixty years and drowned in a well of forgetting, and her eyes found mine, and the laugh stopped.

She raised one small hand. Not beckoning.

*Warning.*

And every star in the cavern went dark at once, all together, like a held breath—

—and in the black, very close, much closer than the thing had been, something else spoke. Not in my voice. In hers. In the queen's. In a voice like a name being spoken for the first time in sixty years, it said:

"*Emrys Joubert. Say it now, or we are both lost.*"
