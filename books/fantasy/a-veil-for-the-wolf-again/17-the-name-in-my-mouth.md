## 17. The Name in My Mouth

The well did not look like a well. It looked like a wound in the shape of a circle, a place where the world had been worried at by teeth too patient to leave marks.

Emrys stood at its edge on a ledge of black stone that had no business existing beneath the palace—no chisel had ever touched it, no mason had squared it, and yet it bore her weight as though it had been waiting for exactly her, exactly here, for sixty years and one long descent. Below her, where water should have been, there was light. Not starlight. Not lamplight. The light of everything that had ever been remembered and then taken: a slow-churning sea of it, gold and rust and river-green, faces surfacing and sinking, voices rising in fragments—a woman laughing at a window, a boy counting coppers, a prayer half-finished, a name called across a field at dusk.

And above it all, turning in slow orbit like moons around a drowned sun, hung the stars.

Every star in the sky, caged. They burned down there in the well of devoured memory, dimmed and struggling, like fireflies in a jar with the lid screwed tight. Emrys counted eleven before she stopped, because counting them felt like a violation she couldn't take back.

*You came.*

The voice was her own.

That was the worst of it—the thing beneath the crown did not speak in thunder or in whispers or in the cold harmonies of old stories. It spoke in Emrys Joubert's voice, with her exact cadence, the slight catch she got in her throat when she was frightened and trying not to show it. It stood at the well's far edge now, wearing a shape it had clearly assembled from stolen parts: her height, her build, her dark hair, but the face smoothed over like wax left near a flame, features suggested rather than committed. A sketch of her. A thief's rough draft of a thief.

"You know my name," Emrys said. Her voice echoed strangely off the black stone, and the echo came back wrong—slowed, as if the well were tasting it.

*I knew it before you did,* the entity said, pleased with itself, pleased with her. *I watched your mother choose it. I held that moment in my waters for thirty years. Would you like it back?*

"I'd like you to answer for what you've taken."

*Taken.* The thing tilted its smooth head, and the gesture was so perfectly hers—so exactly the way Emrys tilted her own head when reading a suspicious ledger entry—that her stomach turned. *I have taken nothing. Everything in this well was given. Freely forgotten, freely surrendered, freely lost to grief or time or drink. I am only the keeper of what remembering could not hold. Your Guild understood that. They wrote the bargain in their fine hand and took their tithe. Why do you think they are guilty, little ledger-keeper, and I am not?*

"Because you ate the king."

The word *ate* dropped into the space between them and the well went briefly, terribly still—all those golden memories pausing mid-swirl, listening.

Then the entity laughed, and it was Emrys's laugh, and she would never again hear her own laugh without hearing this.

*The king gave himself,* it said. *Slowly, over years. Every night a little more of him slid down into me, and every morning he woke emptier and kinder, because kindness is what fills the spaces where the self used to be. He was the gentlest meal I have ever been given. His daughter set the table for him every day and called it court.*

"Don't." Emrys's hand closed around the copper token in her pocket, the warden's gift, worn smooth. "Don't talk about her."

*Why not? You want to know her. You want to know anyone. Do you know what you are, Emrys Joubert?* It took a step along the ledge, unhurried, and where its feet touched the stone the surface rippled like the skin of milk. *You are a house with every room locked and the keys thrown into me. You have spent your whole life stealing other people's interiors because yours was robbed before you could walk. You have no mother's face. No first theft. No reason you became what you are. You are a story with the opening torn out—and I am holding the torn pages.*

Emrys's breath came shallow. She made herself stand still. Made herself do the thing she did best: observe, catalogue, wait.

*Here is my final offer,* the entity said. *It is the only one. I will not make another.*

It opened its wax-smooth hands, and between them the well's light gathered, rose, condensed—and became a door.

Not a real door. A doorway of remembering, standing upright on nothing, and through it Emrys saw a kitchen. A small kitchen with a crooked shelf and a blue jar that held wooden spoons, and a woman at the table with flour on her forearms, humming, and the woman looked up—

—and Emrys knew her.

Not recognized her. *Knew* her, the way you know your own heartbeat, the way your body knows the shape of a room it grew in. The woman's face arrived in Emrys's chest like a blow and stayed there, burning. Her mother. Her mother's face, which had been stolen from her before she had words to hold it, and here it was, offered back, whole, humming, alive.

"There is no record of her," Emrys said, and hated how her voice shook. "I searched every archive. There's no grave, no ledger, no—"

*Because I hold her,* the entity said gently, cruelly, in her own voice. *She is not dead. Dead things sink differently than taken things. She is here, in the first rooms of me, kept soft. And behind her is the rest: your first theft. Your childhood. The reason you flinched, just now, at the word mother. I will give you all of it. Every room unlocked. You will walk out of here whole, Emrys Joubert, a complete person, for the first time in your life.*

"And in exchange."

*You swallow the name.* Its smile was her smile. *You already carry it—you've carried it since you were small enough to be carried. The queen hid it in you because a child is the one vault no thief robs. All I ask is that you never speak it. Never write it. Let it sleep in you until you die of old age in some quiet town, with your mother's face hanging by your bed. One name, swallowed, against an entire life restored. That is not even a bargain. That is mercy.*

The doorway stood open. The humming continued, low and domestic and unbearably sweet. Flour on her forearms. A blue jar. Emrys could smell bread now—bread, actually smell it, rising up out of the memory like heat from an oven, and somewhere under her ribs the sealed thing stirred, the thing she had carried all her life without knowing she carried it, pressing upward toward the light of that kitchen like a plant toward a window.

*Take it,* her own voice whispered. *You have given everything to everyone else's memories. Take one back. Take yours. What is one name against a mother? Against yourself? Isolde will lose a father who has been gone for decades anyway. The city will forget either way—it forgets, that is what cities do. But you. You could be someone. You could finally be someone.*

Emrys stepped forward.

She told herself afterward—I told myself, in the ledgers I keep now, in ink that does not fade—that she was circling. That she was buying time, watching the entity's edges, doing thief's work. And perhaps some sliver of that was true. But most of her, most of her in that moment was simply a woman looking at her mother's face for the first time since infancy, and if the entity had pushed her then, if it had pressed even slightly, she would have walked through that doorway and swallowed the name and damned the stars and the king and the waking world for the sound of one woman humming.

The entity knew that. That is why it did not push. It simply let her look, and poured the smell of bread thicker into the air, and waited with the patience of a thing that had eaten a kingdom one evening at a time.

Her foot found the edge of the doorway. The light of it washed up her legs, warm as a hearth. Inside, her mother turned toward the sound of footsteps and began, slowly, to smile—

*"—his last words,"* said a voice from the tunnel behind her, ragged, cracked, human, *"are these, and I make you swear them—"*

Emrys froze.

Isolde came down the black stair with a torch in one hand and her skirts hiked in the other, and behind her, stumbling, exhausted, weeping without shame, came two dozen courtiers, guardsmen, scullery hands—the remnant of her court, the ones she had gathered in the unraveling capital when names were sliding off street signs and prayers were dissolving mid-word. Their faces were smudged and hollowed with sleeplessness. Half of them looked like people who had forgotten why they were following her and were following anyway, because her voice was the last fixed point in a dissolving world.

And they were chanting.

*"—tell the wolf the veil is thin, tell him the shepherd slept—"*

The words hit the well and the well *screamed*. Not aloud—the scream was in the memories, the golden sea below convulsing, faces shattering and reforming, the caged stars jerking in their orbit like startled birds. The entity's assembled body rippled, its borrowed face sliding.

*Stop,* it said, in Emrys's voice, and then, losing grip, in a hundred other voices at once—the warden's, the Guildmaster's, a child's, the dead queen's—*stop, stop, stop—*

*"Tell the wolf the veil is thin,"* Isolde shouted, louder, planting herself on the ledge three paces from Emrys, torchlight jumping across the black stone, *"tell him the shepherd slept, tell him the shepherd SLEPT—"*

And the court took it up, two dozen cracked voices, then more, and Emrys understood with a lurch what the princess had done: these were the true king's last words, the ones he had spoken into his daughter's ear before the crown finished eating him, the sentence he had trusted her to carry—and Isolde had spent the fall of the city drilling them into every survivor she could find, making them chant it like a ward, because a sentence repeated by enough living mouths is a fortress, and the entity could not digest what the living refused to surrender.

The kitchen doorway flickered.

The humming stuttered. Her mother's smile thinned, stretched, went glassy—and Emrys saw, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, that the figure in the doorway was not her mother. Could not be her mother. It was the well's idea of a mother, built from a thousand stolen kitchens, wearing a face pulled from the entity's waters—and the real woman, if she still existed anywhere, was deeper down, sunk among the first rooms, beyond any doorway the thing would ever willingly open.

Because the entity did not give anything back. It had never once, in sixty years, given anything back. That was the whole of its nature. Even its mercy was a mouth.

*Choose,* it hissed, and the word came from everywhere, from the stone, from the sea, from inside her own skull, in her own voice, desperate now, *choose now, thief, the door is closing, your mother, your SELF, choose—*

Emrys reached into the doorway.

Her mother's eyes met hers—one instant, one unbearable instant of eye contact, warm and brown and utterly empty, a puppet's warmth—and Emrys closed her hand not on the offered hand but on the memory itself, the whole golden thread of it, and *pulled*, the way she had pulled a thousand memories from a thousand sleeping minds, the craft of her lifetime concentrated into one tearing motion.

The doorway shattered. The false mother dissolved into a spray of light that fell back into the well, and Emrys staggered back onto the black stone with the thread of memory burning in her fist—not the whole of her mother, but the true fragment, the real one the entity had used as bait, ripped free and hers now, held like a live coal.

Silence. The chanting faltered.

Then the entity screamed, and this time it screamed out loud, and the sound was every voice it had ever eaten screaming at once, and the well rose.

Golden memory lifted off the surface in waves, coiling upward, gathering into a column, into a shape, into something vast and crowned and furious that filled the cavern from wall to wall—and the black ledge beneath Emrys's feet began, very softly, to crack.

Isolde caught Emrys's arm. Torchlight, terror, iron. "Tell me you have it," the princess said. "Tell me you didn't trade us for a kitchen."

Emrys opened her fist. The stolen fragment glimmered between their faces—her mother's true laugh, three seconds of it, all she would ever recover—and above them the crowned shape drew breath drawn from sixty years of stolen breath, and the caged stars guttered like candles in wind.

"I have something better," Emrys said, and put her other hand into her pocket, around the copper token, and thought of the chapel, and the seal, and the name. "Get your people behind me. And whatever you see me become in the next few minutes—"

The crack in the ledge widened. Light poured up through it, gold and hungry.

"—don't let go of the words."

Below them, in a hundred devoured voices, the thing wearing the crown began to laugh.
