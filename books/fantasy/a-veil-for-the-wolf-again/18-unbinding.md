## 18. Unbinding

The name sat in my mouth like a coal.

Not burning—worse than burning. It was *patient*. It had waited sixty years in a sealed chapel and thirty more in the hollow of my own skull, behind a door I'd nailed shut with a child's hands, and now that I had finally pried it loose it lay on my tongue with the terrible calm of a thing that knew it would be spoken eventually. Everything is spoken eventually. That is the first law of ledgers, and I am a ledger-keeper before I am anything else.

The well breathed beneath me.

I stood at its rim on a ledge of black stone that might have been palace foundation or might have been something older—the bones of the hill before anyone thought to build a crown on it. Below me the devoured memories turned in slow spirals, thousands upon thousands of them, glowing faintly the way deep water glows when light has given up reaching it. Stars down there. Actual stars. I could see them through the murk: caged constellations drifting like lantern-fish, the whole night sky of the world swallowed and kept. The Shepherd's Wain turned slowly near the bottom. The Sisters burned side by side, still holding hands after all this time.

And among them, small and bright and unbearably familiar, a child's memory shaped like a kitchen. Bread smell. A woman humming off-key. My mother—or the shape where my mother should have been, the erased past the entity had peeled out of me and kept, the way a miser keeps the first coin he ever stole.

*Take it back,* said the entity, in my own voice, from everywhere. *That is all I have ever offered, Emrys Joubert. Not power. Not peace. Just the pieces of yourself that other people took. I am the only one who has never taken from you.*

"That's not true," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "You're the reason they took. You're the market everything theft feeds."

*Ledger-keeper.* The voice smiled. I could hear it smiling, the way you can hear rain deciding to fall. *You balance books for thieves. You know better than anyone that every entry has two sides. Give me the name back and I will give you your mother's face. Your first snowfall. The sound of your own laughter before you learned to sell it. You will walk out of here whole.*

And I nearly did.

God help me, I nearly did. Because the offer wasn't a lie—that was the horror of it. The entity could do exactly what it promised; it held my childhood in its gullet like a pearl, and it would have handed it over gleaming. I felt my mouth begin to shape the bargain. I felt the name shift, ready to be swallowed instead of spoken, pressed down into the vault of me where it would sit forever, and in exchange—

A voice came down the stairwell.

It was thin. It cracked on the second word. It was a young woman who had been awake for three days and had watched her guards forget her face mid-shift, and she was chanting anyway, each syllable placed like a stone in a wall:

*"The wolf is fed. The veil is hung. The king goes gentle into the dark—"*

Isolde.

She came around the spiral stair with her dress torn at the hem and her eyes streaming—not weeping, I understood later, but straining against the dark, because she had burned her diaries into her memory deliberately and the effort of holding so much written truth in an unwritten mind had scorched her. Behind her came perhaps twenty courtiers, servants, two priests of minor gods, a scullery boy. All of them chanting. All of them holding the sentence like a rope thrown down a well.

*The king goes gentle into the dark.*

The words hit the entity's voice and broke it apart. My own voice—its voice—stuttered, frayed, went quiet. And in that sudden silence I heard the well below me make a sound I will describe as accurately as I can: it sounded like a very large animal realizing, all at once, that it had been caught.

"Emrys." Isolde reached the ledge, breathless, gripping my arm hard enough to bruise. Her eyes were fierce and terrified and absolutely certain. "Whatever it offered you—it lies by telling the truth. My father told me that once. When he was still him." She looked at the well, and her chin came up. "Finish it."

*Princess,* said the entity, and now its voice was hers too, her father's warmth poured through her timbre, *you were four years old when I first wore his smile for you. You loved me before you loved him. Come, let me—*

"Don't," Isolde said quietly. "Don't you dare wear him at me." And she began the chant again, louder, and the ragged crowd behind her caught it, and the well shuddered.

I knelt at the rim.

The queen's rite was simple. That was the thing Corvane had tried to tell me at the end, nameless and smiling on the floor of his own empty house: the deep craft looks like complexity from outside, but inside it is always the same shape. A vessel. A pouring. A sealing word. The queen had bound a god with nothing but herself and her grief and a name she'd torn out of its throat. She'd had no Guild, no ledger, no copper tokens. She'd had less than I had.

But she hadn't had an audience of twenty people chanting a dead king's last words. And she hadn't had a hoard.

I opened myself.

Every thief knows how to open a memory to take it. Almost none know how to open themselves to give—and what giving costs. Corvane had shown me with the last of his sister, dissolving even as he demonstrated, dying glad, and I had sworn I would find another way. There was no other way. There was only the way, and the way ran through me.

I reached into my hoard.

Understand what a memory-thief's hoard is. It is not a bag of trinkets. It is a second life built out of other people's first ones—a cathedral of borrowed rooms, every one furnished with someone else's love and loss. A bottled lullaby from a midwife in Harrowfen. A debt paid in shadows by a man who owed me his daughter's fever breaking. Sixty years of the city's small miracles, catalogued and cross-referenced and kept safe, because that was my rule, my one rule, the line I had drawn in the candle-smoke of a hundred nights: *never steal a memory you can't give back.*

Tonight I was going to break the rule in the largest possible way. Tonight every debt came due at once.

I found the first thread and pulled.

The lullaby went first. I felt it leave me—not painfully, that was the strange mercy of it, it left the way a bird leaves an open hand—and I sent it down into the well, and somewhere below a woman sixty years dead remembered her mother's voice. Then the shadows, then the fever, then the wedding a baker had sold me to pay a tax he couldn't name, then the storm a fisherman traded for calm water, then, then, then—I stopped counting. The ledger in my head tore itself open page by page and poured. Each memory fell into the well and became a small light rising, and the rising lights found their owners' descendants and neighbors and strangers in the city above, and I felt—distantly, through stone and soil and the roots of the palace—the whole capital flinch and gasp as remembering came back to it like blood to a numb limb.

The entity screamed.

It was not a sound. It was an *un*-sound, a hole in the air, the noise a star would make if you asked it to be a cage. *Stop,* it said in my voice, in Vess's voice, in the king's warm hollow voice, in my mother's—no, not my mother's, it didn't have my mother, it had only the shape, and the shape was already dimming—*stop, you are unmaking the truce, you are unmaking everything, the wards, the borders, the—*

"The wards were you," I said. My mouth was full of coal and light. "The bargain was you. The peace was just you, eating slowly." I pulled harder. "I'm not unmaking anything. I'm doing what the queen did. I'm closing the account."

*You are one woman—*

"I'm a ledger-keeper," I said, "and I am calling in every debt."

Then I opened the sealed room.

My own erased past. The mother-shaped hole. The first theft, the chapel, the queen's terrified holy face, the name I had carried unknowing since I was seven years old—all of it, the entity's hostage treasure, the price it had offered me minutes ago. I took hold of it the way Corvane taught me: gently, gently, the way you lift a sleeping animal, knowing it may wake and bite.

And I gave it away too.

Not to the well. To the binding. Because that was the secret in the queen's rite that no one had understood for sixty years, the thing the redaction had hidden in every copy of every ledger except mine: the seal needed a vessel, and the vessel needed to be *full*, and the queen had used her own life because she had nothing else. I had something else. I had a hoard. I had a stolen childhood and a borrowed city and sixty years of impossible trades, and I poured all of it into the space between the entity and the world, and I spoke the name.

I won't write the name here. Some entries stay redacted, and this one stays redacted by me, on purpose, forever. But I said it. I said it aloud, in the queen's cadence, with the sealing word after it, and I made myself the door it closed against.

The sensation is difficult to render honestly. It was like being a riverbed while the river changes course. It was like every book in a library closing at once. The entity did not die—things like it don't die—but it *ended*, in the way a story ends, the way a bargain ends, its vast attention folding inward and inward and inward until it was small enough to fit inside a circlet of gold, and then smaller, and then it was only metal again, cold and dumb, a crown and nothing more.

The stars came home.

I felt them go up through the stone, up through the palace, up through the ruined sky—one, then ten, then all of them, the whole caged constellation of the world's night released in a single silent detonation of light. Through the well-shaft far above I saw blue lamplight gutter and turn gold. Somewhere beyond the walls, I knew, the border wards were knitting themselves back together out of pure returned memory, villages waking to their own names, warden-tokens warming in dead men's pockets.

The chant above me faltered, changed, dissolved into weeping and laughing and the scullery boy saying *the stars, look, look, the stars* over and over like a prayer he was inventing on the spot.

I stayed kneeling. I took inventory, because that is what I am.

Almost nothing. That was what remained. I reached into myself and found the shelves bare, the rooms unfurnished, the cathedral of my hoard gutted to its stones. No lullaby. No shadows. No mother—no memory of a mother, which amounts to the same thing. Even my own past, the erased years, had gone into the seal; I had spent my childhood buying the world's morning. The Guild's books, when I got back to them, would show a single entry under my name, dated tonight, and the entry would read: *paid in full.*

Except.

There was one thing left. One small warm weight in the emptied vault of me, one item I had not catalogued and could not account for, sitting there like a coin someone had slipped into my pocket without my noticing. I reached for it carefully.

A man's face. Alive and laughing. Sunlight on gray temples, a crooked front tooth, eyes crinkled with some joke he was about to tell—a real man, a true king, decades younger than the shell I'd tasted in the throne room, laughing at something I couldn't hear anymore because the rest of the memory had gone into the binding and only the face remained, orphaned, inexplicable, mine.

I didn't remember stealing it. I didn't remember when or from whom or why. By every rule I had ever kept, it should go back—into the well, into the world, paid out like all the rest.

I closed my hand around it, in the way one closes a hand around a living thing.

"Not this one," I said aloud, to no one, to the ledger, to whatever force keeps accounts when accountants are gone. "This one I keep."

Isolde's hand found my shoulder. Above us the shaft filled with honest gold light, and far overhead, faint through miles of stone, the whole sky was burning with returned stars.

"Emrys," she said, and her voice shook. "The king. Something's happening to my father. He's—he's *awake*, and he's asking for—"

She stopped. Swallowed.

"He's asking for someone who remembers him."
