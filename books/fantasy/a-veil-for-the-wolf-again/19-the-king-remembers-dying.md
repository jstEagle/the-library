## 19. The King Remembers Dying

The stars came back at dawn.

Isolde stood on the eastern balcony of the palace with her father's hand in hers and watched them gutter out one by one as the light rose, each one folding itself away like a candle snuffed by a careful servant, and she thought: *he is still breathing.*

She had not let herself think it before. For eleven years she had kept the court running on ritual and rote, on the same breakfast at the same hour, the same blessing at the same bell, the same day folded and refolded like a letter read so many times its creases had gone to cloth — because the king her father was not in the king her father, and if anyone looked too closely at his eyes they would see what she saw: nothing looking back. A warmth without a person behind it. A lamp lit in an empty house.

But now the stars were returning, one by one, and the thing that had worn the crown was locked out of it, and Emrys Joubert — memory-thief, ledger-keeper, the woman Isolde should have arrested six days ago and instead had led by the hand into the vaults like a conspirator — had paid whatever price such magic asked. Isolde did not know the price yet. She suspected she would learn it soon, and that it would be terrible.

For now there was only this: beneath her palm, her father's chest rose. Fell. Rose again. And when she turned to look at him — really look, for the first time in eleven years — someone looked back.

"Isolde," he said.

His voice was wrong. That was the first thing. It was not the warm, measured voice of the king who opened parliament and blessed the harvest; it was thin, and cracked, and old, older than any king's voice should be, the voice of a man waking from a fever that has burned through years of him. He blinked against the dawn light as though he had never seen the sun before, or as though he had seen it last yesterday and could not account for the decades between.

"How long," he said, "have I been gone?"

Isolde's knees nearly went. She gripped the balcony rail and made herself answer, because he deserved an answer and she had promised herself, years ago, in the dark of her private chapel, that whatever else she failed at, she would never fail him in the small honest things.

"Eleven years," she said. "Father. You've been gone eleven years."

He looked at his own hands then, turning them over slowly, as though checking whether they were still his. They were an old man's hands — they always had been; he had been sixty-one when the crown took him — but he examined them with the particular horror of a man who has just been told how much time has passed while he wasn't watching.

"I remember dying," he said quietly.

* * *

She got him inside. She dismissed the guards herself — her guards now, in every way that mattered, men and women who had spent the last week chanting his true last words in the courtyards like a rosary against forgetting, and who would have knelt to him if she'd allowed it, which she did not, because she could see what kneeling did to him: he flinched from reverence the way a man flinches from a blow he doesn't understand.

They ended up in her private study, the small room with the false panel and the hidden diaries she had burned into her memory rather than trust to paper. He sat in her chair because he was too unsteady to stand, wrapped in a blanket she fetched with her own hands, and he held a cup of wine without drinking it, and he looked around the room as if memorizing it.

"You've been keeping things running," he said. Not a question.

"The court runs on habit," Isolde said. "I fed it habits. The same meals. The same processions. The same words at the same hours. People don't notice a king who repeats himself if everyone around him repeats himself too." She heard how it sounded even as she said it — eleven years compressed into a single flat sentence, all the terror and grief and grinding vigilance of it pressed down into something that fit in a teacup — and she stopped, because her throat had closed.

Her father watched her with an expression she had forgotten existed: the look he used to give her when she came to him as a girl with a problem she'd already solved but wanted him to know about. Pride, and sorrow, and the particular tenderness of a parent recognizing their child's loneliness and knowing it is partly their fault.

"You were nine years old when it happened," he said. "You noticed. Didn't you? Before anyone else."

"On the third day." Her voice came out steady. She was surprised by that. "You called me 'little star' at breakfast. You'd never done that before. And you asked Cook for honeyed pears, and you hated honeyed pears." She laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and brief. "Everyone said I was imagining it. The physicians said grief. You'd been ill that winter, they said, the crown-wearing changes a man, they said." She set her jaw. "I knew. From the third day, I knew something was wearing my father."

"Yes," he said softly. "So did I. On the third day."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, in the courtyard, the morning bells rang out the hour, and for once the sound was only bells.

"It's still in me," he said. "Not — wearing me. But I can feel where it was. Like a tooth pulled. The socket aches." He touched his temple, just above the ear, where the crown would sit. "It ate everything, Isolde. Everything it took, to make room for itself. My wedding day. Your mother's face. The wars, the treaties, my coronation, your first steps — I have your first steps back now, do you understand, I can see them, the red shoes, you fell into the dog's water bowl and laughed until you hiccupped—" His voice broke and he mastered it, the way kings master things, badly and at cost. "It gave them back. When it left. Or the thief did. Someone poured everything back into the hole it left, and I am drowning in forty years of my own life like a man raised from deep water."

"And yet," Isolde said, very carefully, "you said you remember dying."

* * *

He told her standing up, in the end. He couldn't tell it sitting down; some part of him insisted on dignity for this part, and she understood that too, and let him have it.

"There was no illness," he said. "That winter. There was a bargain. Do you know what the Guild of Impossible Things is?"

"A thieves' guild with pretensions," Isolde said. "I've learned more in six days than in twenty years of tutors."

"It's older than the crown. Older than my line." He paced to the window, blanket and all, a king pacing in his nightclothes, and she loved him so fiercely for the absurdity of it that she had to look away. "My grandfather brokered a truce with it. A thing lives under the palace — lived, lives, I don't know what tense applies — a thing that eats memory. Whole cities, once. It wore the kingdom like a coat and picked the pockets of generations. And my grandfather bought peace. Every ruler since has renewed the terms without knowing the terms. We paid in forgetting. Small forgettings, spread across the realm, so no one ever felt the bleeding. A village forgets a flood. A mother forgets a stillborn child. A soldier forgets the worst hour of the worst battle. The Guild collected. The crown signed. And the entity slept, fat and patient, under the roots of my house."

"And then?"

"And then I grew curious." He smiled, and it was the most terrible smile she had ever seen on a human face, because it was entirely self-directed. "I found a ledger entry I wasn't meant to find. I asked questions. And the Guild — not out of malice, Isolde, that's the poison of it, I believe they truly believed they were saving us — they brought me down to it. To negotiate. Man to monster. And it was *so tired*, it said, so hungry, and the bargain was straining, and it needed a vessel inside the wards, a crown to live in, a body to wear—"

"Father." She crossed the room and took his hands. They were shaking. "What did it cost?"

"My name," he said simply. "Not the word. The thing itself. Who I was underneath the titles. It drank that first, and everything else followed down the drain of it, and I remember—" His grip tightened on hers, hard enough to hurt, hard enough that she didn't care. "I remember the moment it finished. I was still conscious. Some scrap of me was still conscious, watching from somewhere behind my own eyes, and I thought, *this is what dying is,* and then I thought, *no, worse, because the body goes on,* and then there were eleven years of nothing, and now I am here, and I remember all of it, and I need you to understand what I am about to ask of you."

"Anything," Isolde said. "You know it's anything."

"Don't say that yet." He drew a breath, and she saw him gather himself the way he used to gather himself before addressing the assembled lords — spine straightening, chin lifting, the old architecture of authority rising one more time out of ruins. "No one must know how I died. No one must know what I agreed to. No one must know the crown was hollow, or why, or what the Guild traded, or that your whole reign has been a puppeteer's performance conducted by a frightened child." His eyes were bright and fierce and utterly clear. "Let them believe I was ill. Let them believe the illness passed and returned and finally took me, as illnesses do. Let them believe you are simply my heir, crowned in grief like every heir since the founding. Because if they know the truth — if the truth gets into songs, into sermons, into the mouths of lords with grudges — then the bargain isn't over, Isolde. The truth becomes the next debt. And someone will try to collect."

* * *

He had one hour. Emrys had warned her — sent up a note in a clerk's plain hand, unsigned, saying only *one hour, perhaps less, spend none of it on goodbyes you can say later.* Isolde had spent the first half hour on the balcony, holding his hand, watching stars go out, and she regretted nothing, and she regretted everything, and there was no time to sort which.

In the last twenty minutes, he became ordinary again.

That was the gift of it, she realized afterward. Not the state secrets, not the warnings, not even the apology — though he gave her that too, formally, kneeling as best his old knees allowed, asking pardon for eleven years of absence, and she hauled him up off the floor and told him never to kneel to her again while she lived. No: the gift was the ordinariness. He asked about her gardens, which he had never seen. He asked whether she still bit her nails (she did not; she lied). He remembered the dog's water bowl and the red shoes and he told her a story about her mother that made Isolde laugh and then cry and then laugh again, all in the space of a minute, because her mother had been dead fourteen years and this was the first time anyone alive had spoken of her as a person rather than a portrait.

"She sang badly," her father said, delighted. "Your mother could not carry a tune in a bucket. And every festival, without fail, she insisted on leading the hymn, and every priest in the capital developed sudden deafness, and your grandfather pretended to weep with devotion to cover the sound. And she knew. She knew exactly how bad she was. She did it anyway." He shook his head, marveling. "That was the whole secret of her, Isolde. She did things anyway."

"I'll remember that," Isolde said.

"No," he said gently. "You won't have to. That's the point of telling you. Remembering shouldn't be work, little star. Not for you. You've carried enough."

The light in the room had changed without either of them noticing — the long gold of late afternoon sliding toward the copper of evening — and when Isolde looked at her father again, she saw that the clarity in his face had begun to soften at the edges, like ink touched by rain.

"Is it time?" she asked. Her voice was very small.

"I think it's time." He sat back down in her chair, in his blanket, and arranged himself with care, and asked her to open the window, and she did, and the evening air came in cold and clean off the mountains, smelling of woodsmoke and the first thaw. Below, in the city, lamps were being lit — real lamplight, warm and yellow, the blue burning gone — and somewhere a child was singing a skipping song, off-key, gloriously off-key, and her father listened to it with his eyes closed and smiled.

"That," he said. "Remember that. Not the politics. Not the bargain. The child singing badly. Your mother's legacy."

"Father—"

"Isolde." His eyes opened, and they were clear to the last, clear and kind and tired beyond all measuring. "You were a better king than I ever was. Eleven years old and holding the world together with string. Don't let anyone — including yourself — tell you otherwise." He reached up and touched her cheek with two fingers, the way he had when she was small and frightened of storms. "Now get the physicians. Make it proper. Grief needs witnesses."

She got three steps to the door before he spoke once more, so quietly she almost missed it.

"Thank you for knowing. On the third day. Thank you for knowing me."

She ran for the physicians, and she was fast, faster than she'd ever been, and it was not fast enough, and when she came back into the room with white-robed men trailing behind her, her father was still in the chair with his face turned to the window, and he was smiling, and there was nothing behind the smile at all — not the entity's warm emptiness, but the simple, final emptiness of a man who has finished.

The physicians said his heart. An old heart, they said, after such illness. It happens, they said, sometimes, suddenly, peacefully.

"Yes," Isolde said, and her voice did not shake at all. "Peacefully. Suddenly. He was himself at the end. That is what you will write, and what you will say, for the rest of your lives."

They bowed. They believed her. Kings' words are heavy; they press belief into people like a seal into wax.

* * *

The coronation was nine days later, because the realm needed a coronation the way a wound needs stitching — quickly, cleanly, without admiring the damage.

Isolde chose the smallest ceremony the law allowed. She was crowned in the old chapel rather than the great cathedral, before the court and the commons both, because she wanted witnesses, hundreds of them, thousands — because she had learned from a week of chanting in courtyards that shared words are the strongest ward there is, and she intended to be crowned inside a wall of them. When the archpriest asked the traditional question — *who presents this sovereign to the people?* — the answer roared up from the crowd in ten thousand throats, the sentence they had chanted through the dark week, her father's true last words, which were also, though only four people alive knew it, the first words of the queen's binding rite:

*"We remember, and we are not afraid."*

The crown weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it. All those years, all that hunger, all the devouring — and when Emrys Joubert had unbound it, the thing had left the gold as empty as a shed skin. Isolde set it on her own head with her own hands, refusing the archpriest's help, because she wanted the full weight of the gesture even if the metal gave her nothing, and she felt the cold band settle above her ears, right where her father had said the socket ached, and she thought: *you will never wear anything else. I will carry you myself. And I will know exactly what you are.*

Afterward, alone at last in her study — her study, her father's chair pushed respectfully to the wall, though she had moved it back to the desk because it seemed cowardly to hide it — she found the note waiting on her blotter. Unsigned. Clerk's hand. The same hand as before.

*The Guild's ledgers are being rewritten tonight,* it said. *Plain ink, no redactions. Your realm is owed sixty years of accounts, and Vess is paying. Come and watch, if you like. Thieves rarely invite royalty twice.*

And beneath that, smaller, in different ink, hurried:

*One more thing. I kept something of his. I'm not sorry. If you want to hear how he laughed, come to the guildhouse when the veil is new.*

Isolde read it three times. Then she folded it along its existing creases, opened the hidden panel, and laid it beside the ash-marks where her diaries had been — not because she would ever need to read it again. She had burned it into her memory with the rest, every word, in the old way, the way Corvane had taught a thief to teach herself.

Outside the window, the stars were all present and accounted for, burning in their ancient stations, and beneath them the city glittered, and somewhere in that city a woman with almost no memories left was hanging a fresh veil over a door.

Isolde put on her father's cloak — it fit her badly, and she would wear it anyway, she would wear it for years — and went down to the stables to ride out and meet the new age, carrying the only truth in the kingdom that belonged to her alone.
