## 20. Settling Accounts

The guildhouse smelled of smoke and old paper when I came back to it.

Not the good smell—the one I'd loved for fifteen years, candlewax and vellum and the faint iron tang of bottled memory. This was the other kind of smoke, the kind that came from ledgers burned in a hurry by people who thought fire could erase a debt. The great doors hung open on their hinges. Someone had torn down the veil that covered them; its frayed hem still swung from one nail like a hanged man's coat.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the cold morning air move through me. I was lighter than I had ever been. My hoard was gone—poured into the binding beneath the palace, every stolen year and borrowed face spent in a single act of unmaking. What remained of me fit inside my own skull with room to spare, and the room was strange. It echoed.

"Emrys."

Vess stood at the foot of the main stair, and she looked old.

I had never let myself see it before. Guildmasters are like kings that way—you assume the office makes them ageless, that the weight they carry is distributed somehow across the institution instead of settling into their bones. But Vess had held this house together for sixty years on a lie written in redacted ink, and now the lie was out, and the stars were back, and the king was dead, and every thief under her roof knew exactly what their comfortable lives had been built on.

She wore her grey traveling cloak, not her master's robes. That told me more than anything she could have said.

"You're leaving," I said.

"I'm deciding whether to leave." Her voice was steady, but her hands were folded too tightly, knuckles white. "It depends on what you intend to do with us."

*Us.* Not *me*. Even now, even stripped, she spoke as the house.

I walked past her into the hall. The long counting table where I'd balanced ten thousand small thefts was littered with abandoned work—a half-catalogued lullaby in its bottle, weeping condensation; a debt paid in shadows that had begun to leak across the wood like spilled ink. The candles had burned down to stubs. Nobody had tended them. For the first time in living memory, nobody had tended the ledgers.

"They'll come back," Vess said behind me. "Some of them. They always do. A guild is not its master, Emrys. It's its books."

"Then we should look at the books."

* * *

They gathered slowly through the morning, the way people gather at a funeral when they're not sure whose funeral it is.

Corvane's empty chair sat at the head of the counting table, and no one moved it. I had buried him myself, three days ago, in a nameless grave outside the walls—nameless because he had given his last memory away to teach me how to steal from myself, and died smiling, not knowing his own name or mine or his sister's face. The Guild had sent no one else. They were afraid of him, or ashamed of him, or both. I put a copper token on the chair so he'd have a seat anyway. He would have appreciated the accounting of it.

Thirty-one thieves came. Out of ninety-four. Some had fled the city entirely when the wards fell and the truth came up out of the ground like a flood. Some were simply afraid of me—I understood that. I had walked into the dark under the palace carrying nothing but a name in my mouth and come back having unmade a god. People don't know where to put their eyes when they look at you after something like that.

Sera Tallow sat near the front, arms crossed, chin up, daring anyone to say she'd considered running. Old Maren from the acquisitions floor, who had taught me bottlework. The Ferrow twins, who dealt exclusively in impossible trades and had once sold a man Tuesday. And in the back, hood up, the ones who wouldn't meet my eyes: the brokers who had handled crown contracts, the archivists who had maintained the redaction, the quiet middle ranks who had known—or suspected—and chosen not to know harder.

Vess took her place at the table's end. Not the head. She left the head empty, and I understood that too: the head belonged to Corvane now, or to the idea of him, the last honest thief the Guild had produced before the bargain rotted us all.

"The realm knows," I said, without preamble. There was no gentle way into this, and I had decided, somewhere between the well and the surface, that gentleness was another form of redaction. "Princess Isolde has read the ledgers—all of them, including the pages your masters spent sixty years cutting out. She knows the Guild brokered the trade. She knows what was traded. She knows that while her father screamed behind his own eyes for thirty years, this house collected its fees and called itself honorable."

Silence. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in the wind.

"So here is what happens now," I went on. "Not exile. Not trials. I've seen enough of devouring things to know that punishment is just hunger wearing a nicer coat." I set my hand flat on the oldest ledger, the one I'd carried out of the burning archive two nights ago, singed at the corners but whole. "What happens is that we settle accounts. In plain ink. Every entry, every fee, every debt—entered honestly, signed, and witnessed. Starting with ours."

I opened the book to the page I had marked.

The redaction was still there, sixty years of it, a black bar of censored silence running down the margin like a scar. I had spent years assuming such bars were ordinary—that every ledger kept its secrets, that some trades were simply too dangerous to record plainly. Corvane had shown me otherwise before he died. *A redaction isn't protection,* he'd said, with his sister's laugh still warm somewhere behind his eyes. *It's a debt the writer was too cowardly to sign.*

I took out my knife, the thin one, the one made for cutting memories loose without spilling them, and I scraped the black bar away.

Underneath, the original entry survived, as original entries always do if you know where ink hides. My own hand hadn't written it—it predated me by decades—but I recognized the style. Every keeper learns the house hand. It read:

*Bargain struck with the Crown Entity (designation withheld per contract). Terms: the Guild secures the kingdom's collective memory against total consumption. In exchange, the Entity feeds at a measured rate—one district's remembering per generation, drawn slow, drawn quiet—and the Guild receives exclusive license over all memory-trade within the realm. Signed: Guildmaster Aldous Vane. Witnessed: [seven names, struck through].*

Seven witnesses. I looked around the table and did the arithmetic aloud, because arithmetic is crueler than accusation.

"Aldous Vane was Vess's predecessor's predecessor. Which means everyone in this room learned the craft inside a house built on this page." I let that sit. "We weren't victims. We were beneficiaries. The peace the kingdom enjoyed—the wards, the borders, the long safe years—we didn't earn them. We rented them out. And the rent was paid in other people's names."

Maren began, quietly, to cry. She was seventy, and she had bottle-fed lullabies to sick children her whole career, and I think until this moment she had believed the bargain was someone else's sin.

"That's why the entity grew," Sera said. Her voice was flat, working hard to be. "Measured feeding. We gave it permission to eat slowly instead of starving. We made it *sustainable*."

"We made it *ours*," I said. "Yes."

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was Guildmaster again, or trying to be. "And your terms?"

* * *

I had written them the night before, sitting on the chapel steps where I'd made my first theft, with the returned stars wheeling overhead and only one memory in my keeping. I read them out plainly, because plainness was the whole point.

"One. The redactions come out of every surviving ledger. Where an original can't be recovered, we write the truth fresh and mark it as reconstruction, dated and signed. No more black bars. Ever. If a trade is too shameful to record, it's too shameful to make.

"Two. The Guild enters its own debt to the realm in plain ink, and pays it. Sixty years of fees collected under a false peace, converted at fair value, delivered to the crown—not to Isolde's treasury, mind, but to the restoration. The forgotten villages. The nameless wards. The people who lost themselves so our margins stayed healthy. We stole from them. We give back to them. That's not charity; that's bookkeeping.

"Three. New terms of trade. We take no memory we cannot return. That rule existed before—my rule, my private discipline—and it becomes the house rule, enforced, audited, non-negotiable. And we take no memory from anyone who cannot consent. No more buying grief off the desperate. No more harvesting the dying because they can't object.

"Four." I paused here, because the fourth term was mine alone, and saying it aloud cost something. "Every thief in this house surrenders their personal hoard to the restoration fund. Voluntarily. Today. What you keep is what you lived. Nothing you took."

That one landed hardest. A hoard is a thief's life insurance, retirement, and identity all bound in glass and leather. I watched three thieves in the back row stand up at once.

One of them—Halric, a broker, smooth-faced, careful—stopped at the door. "You're asking us to become beggars," he said.

"I'm asking you to stop being creditors," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everyone you lent against."

He looked at me a long moment. Then he looked at the ledger, at the scraped-clean page, at the seven struck-through names, and something in his face performed a small, unwilling arithmetic of its own. He pulled the stopper from the first bottle in his coat and set it on the counting table. Then the second. Then the third.

He didn't say anything. Neither did I. Some settlements happen in ink, and some happen in glass, and the second kind are worth more.

In the end, twenty-two stayed. Nine walked out into the grey morning, and I let them go without a word, because justice without cruelty also means justice without pursuit. The door stood open behind them. So did the road.

* * *

The rewriting took eleven days.

I worked at the counting table with the shutters open, because I wanted daylight on the pages—wanted the sun to see what we wrote, the way a witness sees. Thieves who had never touched a ledger in their lives learned the house hand. Sera turned out to have a beautiful, ruthless script. Maren catalogued the surrendered hoards with trembling care, giving each returned memory its proper entry: *one mother's voice, returned to the miller's daughter, Ward of Ashen Lane; one summer, entire, returned to a soldier who bought it to forget his brother.*

The debt column grew enormous. That was the point. Vess watched the figures climb day after day, and on the fifth day she brought me tea without being asked, and on the eighth she sat down across from me and said, "Your rule. Never steal a memory you can't give back. Where did it come from? You couldn't have known about the bargain when you made it."

I thought about lying to her. It would have been easy; lying to Vess had been a professional skill of mine for years.

"I think it came from the queen," I said. "From the memory I stole when I was six and sealed away. She bound the thing into the crown to save the world, and then she made sure no one—including herself—could ever be tempted to undo it. I never met her. But I've been keeping her rule my whole life without knowing whose it was." I turned the page. "Maybe that's what memory is, in the end. Rules that outlive the people who needed them."

Vess was quiet for a while. Then she said, "When the debt is entered in full, I'll resign."

"No," I said. "You'll stay. Someone has to teach the new ones the house hand, and you're the best scribe in the building, and penance served with competence is worth ten times penance served with tears." I glanced up. "Besides, I'm going to need a deputy who remembers what we were, so we never manage to be it again."

She stared at me. "You're offering me a place. After everything."

"I'm offering you work," I said. "The place is yours if you earn it back, same as anyone. That's the new terms, Vess. Nobody inherits trust here anymore. Everybody signs for it."

On the eleventh day, I wrote the final entry myself, in plain ink, in my own hand, and read it aloud to the full hall so that every thief present became a witness:

*Debt of the Guild of Impossible Things to the Crown and Commons of the Realm, entered this day in full and without redaction: sixty years of unlawful gain, converted and payable to the restoration of the forgotten. Cause of debt: a bargain struck in cowardice and kept in comfort. Status: acknowledged. Repayment: begun. Witnessed by all assembled, and by the stars, which are returned.*

Then I signed it. And beneath my signature, after a moment's hesitation, Vess added hers.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when the last of them drifted out and I found myself alone with the ledgers and the copper token on Corvane's chair. I picked it up. It was warm, though the hall was cold, though that was probably just my hand.

Isolde's messenger arrived as I was banking the candles—a palace girl, breathless, with a note sealed in grey wax. I broke it and read it twice.

*Emrys — The restoration funds arrived. The first village has its names back; they sang all night, badly. Come tomorrow. There is something in my father's rooms I found while sorting them, and I do not know what to do with it, and I think perhaps it belongs to you. — I.*

Something belonging to me. From the true king's rooms. I stood a long time in the empty hall, turning the note over, and outside the open doors the stars were coming out one by one, steady and accounted for, every light in its proper column.

Whatever waited in those rooms, I had a feeling it would change the shape of the final line in my ledger.

I blew out the candles and reached for my cloak.
