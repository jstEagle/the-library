## 21. One Memory Kept

The hoard had filled a cellar once.

Emrys remembered that the way she remembered things now—deliberately, with effort, the way one remembers a story told by someone else rather than lived. The cellar beneath the guildhouse had been hers for eleven years, warded in chalk and salt and three languages nobody spoke anymore, and it had been *full*. Shelves to the ceiling. Jars of glass-dark memory stacked like preserves. A lifetime of other people's Tuesdays, other people's weddings, other people's griefs, each one catalogued in her careful ledger hand: *acquired, held, unclaimed.*

Now she stood in the middle of it and could hear her own footsteps echo.

She had poured everything into the binding at the well of devoured memories—every stolen face, every borrowed summer, every secret she'd ever lifted from a stranger's sleeping mind. She had felt them leave her like water leaving a broken jug, hundreds of lives streaming out of her and into the rite, and when the last of them went she had knelt on cold stone in the dark under the palace and been, for one vertiginous moment, no one at all. A ledger with every page torn out. A thief with nothing left to fence.

The stars had come back anyway. One by one, like lamps being lit down a very long street.

So now there was this: sorting.

* * *

She worked by candlelight because she always worked by candlelight, though the Guild's treasury could have bought her a chandelier of everburning witchfire and Vess—stripped, subdued, newly reasonable—would not have dared refuse her anything. Habit was a kind of architecture. When you have lost the shape of your own past, you build your days out of ritual instead, and Emrys had always been good at ritual.

The remaining inventory fit on two shelves.

That was the part that made her throat close, when she let herself notice it. Two shelves, where once there had been forty. Most of what remained wasn't even stolen goods in any meaningful sense—it was residue. Fragments too small or too frayed to have answered the rite's call. A sliver of someone's first snowfall, thin as onion skin. Half a lullaby in a language neither singer nor child had understood. The smell of bread from a bakery that had burned down thirty years before she was born, kept whole and warm and impossible in a jar.

She took each one down, turned it in her hands the way a jeweler turns a stone, and asked it the only question that mattered now:

*Whose were you? And do they still live?*

Her rule had never changed, not in eleven years, not through any of it: *never steal a memory you can't give back.* For most of her career that rule had been an abstraction, a line in a code of conduct that mostly existed so that thieves could feel honorable about not being honorable. Now it was arithmetic. The binding at the well had taken her hoard and spent it—but not all debts are paid in full, and some of these fragments, she suspected, had simply slipped through the rite's fingers like coins through a torn pocket. Unspent. Still owed.

Which meant work. Which meant, eventually, walking out into a city that was slowly remembering how to remember, and finding the people these pieces belonged to—or their children, or their children's children—and giving back what her hands had once taken.

It would take years. That thought, oddly, was the first thing since the well that had made her feel something other than hollow. Years. She would need years, and a reason to fill them, and hands that still remembered the craft. She had all three.

She wrote as she sorted, in a fresh ledger with clean pages, because the old ledgers were gone—surrendered to Vess's custody as part of the settling of accounts, every redaction unmade in plain ink, the Guild's sixty-year debt to the realm written out in full where any member could read it and be ashamed. Let them read it. Shame was a teacher. Vess, to her credit, had flinched only once, and had signed the amended ledgers with a steady hand, and had said, quietly, at the door: *"Your mother would have—"*

And stopped.

Because Emrys had no mother anymore. Not in any sense that mattered. The entity's final temptation had been exactly that—a restored life, a woman's face and voice, a childhood un-erased—and Emrys had refused it, and in refusing had fed her own past into the fire along with everything else. Whatever girl had once stood in a ruined chapel and made her first theft, whatever woman had raised her or failed to raise her, they were ink in the binding now. Spent. The world got to keep its remembering, and Emrys got to keep—

She paused, jar in hand, candle guttering.

What *did* she get to keep?

* * *

It took her three nights to finish the inventory, and it was on the third night, near the bottom of the second shelf, that she found it.

At first she took it for nothing—a small memory, unusually clear, which should have been her first warning. Residue is frayed. Residue is gauze. This was sharp as the day it was cut, and it did not feel like anyone else's leavings. It felt *warm.*

She uncorked it with the care of long habit, let it rise against her palm, and tasted the edge of it the way she'd been taught before she could write her own name.

Sunlight. That was the first thing. Real sunlight, not the amber haze memory usually wore, but the hard white gold of a southern afternoon, the kind that makes dust in the air look deliberate. Stone underfoot, sun-warmed. The sound of a courtyard—water in a fountain, boots, laughter that started somewhere deep in a chest. And then a face, turning toward her, young still, though the eyes were already old the way royal eyes go old early—

Emrys sat down. Her legs simply declined to hold her.

She knew that face.

Not from portraits—the portraits in the palace galleries showed a king in his prime, stern and gilded, a king who had never existed quite that way. Not from the shell that had worn the crown for sixty years, repeating its single golden day on a loop while the kingdom starved for remembering. From none of the ways she should have known it.

From *before.* From the hole in her ledger where an entry should have been.

Because here was the arithmetic that had been nagging at her for three days, the sum she hadn't wanted to run: the binding had taken her hoard. All of it. Everything stolen. But this memory was stolen—she knew theft when she held it, knew the particular grain of a thing taken from a living mind rather than grown in one—and yet it had survived. It had sat on her shelf through the rite like a guest who declines to leave, and when she looked closer, turning it in the light of her trained sight, she saw why.

There was a seal on it. Old work. A thief's knot, tied in the style of fifty years ago, elegant and absolute—a seal designed not to hide the memory but to *protect* it. To keep anything, even a queen's great binding rite, from spending it.

She knew that knot. She had seen it once, in the recovered ruin of her first theft, in the chapel where a dying queen had arranged for a child to steal the one thing that could someday unmake a god.

The queen had sealed this memory herself. Had taken it—no, had *commissioned* it taken—from her husband's living mind, in the last bright year before the crown ate him. Had sealed it against loss, against time, against the very entity that was even then closing its jaws around the throne. And then she had hidden it in the hoard of a child-thief who wouldn't understand what she carried for another six decades, in the one place in the world the entity could never look: inside a girl edited so thoroughly that even the girl didn't know what she was holding.

A vault disguised as an absence.

Emrys laughed—one short, cracked sound in the empty cellar, startling herself. Clever. The queen had been so *clever*, and so tired, and had trusted a stranger's child with the last true piece of the man she loved, and had died never knowing whether the trust was well placed.

Well placed, Your Majesty. Eventually. Give me sixty years and I'll get there.

She brought the memory to the light again and let herself look, properly this time, not skimming but reading, the way she'd read a thousand minds through a thousand keyholes. And the memory opened like a book.

The courtyard. The fountain. The king—young, dark-haired, sleeves rolled like any tradesman's, arguing cheerfully with a falconer about the merits of a particular bird. He lost the argument. He laughed at losing it, a big unguarded laugh, head back, entirely unaware that anyone might be watching, because he was a king only in documents that afternoon and otherwise just a man who loved a stubborn hawk.

And then he noticed the small dark-haired child at the edge of the court—the child she had been—and instead of summoning guards he crouched down to her height and said something the memory's edges had worn away, something she couldn't retrieve, but his face said it plainly enough: *Hello, little shadow. Are you lost?*

And the child—who was already, even then, a thief, already already—had reached up without fear and touched his sleeve, and he had smiled as if that were a perfectly ordinary thing, as if children touched kings' sleeves every day, as if the world were soft.

That was all. Thirty seconds of sunlight. But Emrys sat with it in her cupped hands for a long time after the vision faded, and her face was wet, and she couldn't have said precisely when that had started.

* * *

Here is the thing about a thief's inventory: everything in it is accounted for, or it isn't, and if it isn't, that is either an error or a decision.

Emrys had spent her whole adult life among errors. This was not one. The seal told her that much—someone had *meant* this memory to sit outside the ledger's rules, outside the rite's reach, outside every system of debt and return she had built her honor upon. Someone had meant it to be found by exactly one person, at exactly this hour, in exactly the state she was in: emptied, unmoored, holding nothing of her own and wondering what a person is when the taking and the giving are both done.

Someone had meant her to find it and ask the question she was already asking:

*Does this belong to anyone? Can it be given back?*

The honest answer was no. The man in the memory was dead—not the shell, not the loop, but him, the laughing man; Isolde had sat with him for his one lucid hour and buried him the next morning with a private word and a public mask. There was no one to return it to. His daughter had her own memories of him, worn and partial; this one, sealed fresh, was the only place in the world where he still moved and breathed and laughed at a falconer with his whole chest.

And her rule—her one rule, the spine of her—

*Never steal a memory you can't give back.*

Emrys turned the rule over the way she'd turned the jar. It had never said *give back everything.* It had said don't take what you can't return. And she had returned nearly everything—more than everything; she'd paid the Guild's debt with her own past thrown in as interest. The account was balanced. The books were clean. What remained on these two shelves was hers to steward, not to surrender, and stewardship sometimes meant keeping.

Besides—and here was the thought that finally tipped her, quiet and warm as the memory itself—if she gave this one back, no one would remember what he was.

Isolde carried the truth of his death, and would carry it alone, and Emrys would not add to that weight. But Isolde had known her father as a father: stern at councils, gentle at bedtime, a man in fragments across a daughter's years. Isolde had never met *this* man—this sunlit, laughing stranger who was kind to small thieves in courtyards. This version of him existed nowhere else. If Emrys let it go—if she dissolved it into the general fund of the world, spent it on some abstraction called closure—then the true king, the whole living man, would be entirely gone. The entity would have won a small final victory: not even one unguarded afternoon surviving it.

She was not going to permit that.

Emrys rose, fetched pen and ink, and opened her new ledger to a fresh page. Her hand, at least, had survived everything; it was still the same careful hand, the one Vess used to say could thread a needle blindfolded.

She wrote:

*Item: one (1) memory, sealed provenance, contents: H.M. Aldric IV, in life, thirty seconds, sunlit, laughing.*
*Acquired: prior to records. By order of Queen Maren, sealed against loss.*
*Status: debt discharged elsewhere. No lawful claimant.*
*Disposition:—*

She stopped. Pen hovering.

Every instinct of the craft said to write *held in trust*—the neutral phrase, the accountant's hedge, the words that committed her to nothing. It was what a careful thief would write. It was what she had written ten thousand times.

She thought about the child in the memory, touching a king's sleeve. About a queen sealing love into a stranger's cellar because she had no safer place in all her kingdom. About the sentence she'd spoken to her new apprentice-to-be, weeks ago, half-joking: *someone has to remember for the ones who can't.*

She wrote:

*Disposition: kept.*

Then, after a moment, in smaller letters, the truth underneath the truth:

*This is not a debt. This is a promise. He was real. I was there. I will carry it.*

She blotted the page, closed the book, and set the memory back on its shelf—but on the top shelf now, alone, where the candlelight would reach it every night she came down here. The little jar caught the flame and threw one small gold spark against the stone wall, like a star returning, like a lamp lit at the start of a long street.

Emrys stood a while longer in the doorway, looking back at it.

Somewhere upstairs, the guildhouse settled and sighed, and beyond it the city dreamed under whole constellations, and tomorrow there would be returns to make—fragments to carry home to strangers, decades of small restitutions, a life's work assembled out of what was left. She found she was almost eager for it. Almost someone again.

But tonight, one memory stayed.

She blew out the candle and climbed toward the daylight, and behind her in the dark the sealed jar sat on its shelf like a heart in a reliquary—holding, against all odds, thirty seconds of a king laughing in the sun.

She did not hear the soft tread on the stairs above her, or see the figure waiting in the doorway of the guildhall, wearing traveling clothes and a princess's posture, with a question that would change everything.

But Isolde had come, and Isolde was waiting.
