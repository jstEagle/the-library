## 22. A Veil for the Wolf Again

The stars came back in spring.

Not all at once—the sky was shy about it, the way the city was shy about everything those first months. One night there would be a single bright point above the eastern hills that everyone swore had not been there before, and the next week two more, and by the thaw there were enough of them that children began arguing about which ones were new and which had always been there and everyone had simply forgotten to look up. Emrys never joined those arguments. She knew exactly how many stars were new. She had counted them out of a well beneath the palace, one memory at a time, and paid for each one with something she would never get back.

But that was winter's arithmetic, and this was spring, and tonight the guildhouse door stood open to let the smell of rain in, and the wolf on the old veil had faded to a gray ghost of itself, threads gone thin as breath.

"You're staring at it again," said Vess from the ledger room, in the tone she used for entries she disapproved of.

"I'm deciding," Emrys said.

"Deciding what?"

Emrys lifted the veil off its hooks. It came away lighter than she remembered, as if some of its weight had simply given up over the years. "Whether we need a new one."

Vess appeared in the doorway, drying her hands on a cloth, and looked at the ruined fabric without expression. The Guildmaster had changed since winter—not softened, exactly. Emrys doubted Vess could soften any more than a blade could. But something had been filed down in her, some edge she had used for sixty years to hold the redaction shut between her teeth. She signed her name now in plain ink where once she'd have written nothing at all, and every time she did it, Emrys caught the small wince of a woman touching an old scar.

"Hang whatever you like," Vess said. "It's your guildhouse now. That was the agreement."

"It's our guildhouse."

"It's *yours*." Vess set the cloth down with great precision. "I brokered a bargain that cost a kingdom its memory. You broke it open and paid the bill yourself. If you want my opinion on drapery, I'll give it, but don't mistake courtesy for co-ownership." A pause. The lamplight made her face very old and very honest. "And I will spend the rest of my life being grateful that you didn't take partnership or oblivion when I offered them. Neither would have fit you."

She went back to the ledgers. Emrys folded the old veil and laid it on the table, where it sat like something dead that had once been loved.

* * *

The girl arrived at dusk, three days later, in the company of the rain.

Emrys saw her first from the window: small, dark-haired, standing in the street outside the guildhouse with her hood down and her face turned up to the wet sky, watching the stars come out through gaps in the cloud. Not counting them. Just looking. That was rarer than you'd think, these days. Half the capital still flinched from the open sky, the way a beaten dog flinches from a raised hand. They had spent a season under nothing, after all. They remembered—or half-remembered, or had been told—that the dark above had once been empty, and empty in a way that meant something was eating.

The girl knocked properly. Three knocks, evenly spaced, no hesitation. Emrys noted that before she noted anything else.

"We're closed to trade," Emrys said through the door. "We only do returns now."

"I know." The voice was steady. "I'm not here to trade. I'm here because of the notice. The one in the square. *The Guild of Impossible Things seeks apprentices. No experience required. Bring nothing you cannot afford to lose.*"

"That last part is a warning, not a challenge."

"I know that too." A pause, and then, quieter: "My brother forgot me."

Emrys opened the door.

The girl stood dripping on the step, eleven or twelve, with a burn scar along her jaw that she did not try to hide and eyes that did not move past Emrys's shoulder into the house the way most visitors' did—hunting for treasure. She looked at Emrys the way people look at doors they've decided to walk through.

"During the forgetting," the girl said. "He's sixteen. He woke up one morning last winter and didn't know my name. He knows it again now, mostly. Some days he doesn't. The physicians say the far memories come back slowest." Her chin lifted. "I want to learn to fix things like that. The notice says someone has to remember for the ones who can't."

"That isn't what the notice says."

"It's what it means. I read it four times."

Emrys stood in the doorway a long moment, rain ticking against the lintel behind her, and felt the shape of her own history turn over in her hands like a card being offered facedown. This was how it had started for her too, though she no longer had the memory of it—only Corvane's secondhand account, told in his last lucid hour: *a child on a chapel steps, stealing without knowing it was theft.* She had built a life out of the hole where that memory should have been. She knew better than anyone alive what it cost to enter this trade missing pieces.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Wren."

"And if I taught you to steal a memory, Wren, what would be the first thing you'd take?"

The girl didn't answer fast, and Emrys liked her for that. Most applicants had an answer ready—a wrong done to them, a secret coveted. Wren looked down at her own wet shoes and thought.

"Nothing," she said finally. "You said you only do returns now. So I'd take something someone lost and carry it home to them. And I wouldn't look inside on the way, even though I'd want to." She glanced up. "Would I want to?"

"Always," Emrys said. "Come in. Dry your boots. We'll start with ledgers."

* * *

The ledger room smelled of candle wax and ink and, faintly, of the lavender Emrys still burned out of habit for a mother she could not remember having. Shelves lined every wall now, and the shelves were full—but not with stolen goods. With returns. Bottled lullabies awaiting delivery. A debt paid in shadows, repaid in dawn-light, the transaction recorded in Emrys's careful hand. The Guild of Impossible Things had spent decades acquiring; it would spend the next decade giving back, and the work was slower than theft, and harder, and Emrys found she loved it in a way that frightened her slightly, the way all good things frightened her now. She had learned what the world did with unguarded treasures.

Wren sat across the table, copying entries in a hand that was already better than Emrys's had been at twelve—or so she assumed it had been at twelve. There was no record. There were so many things there was no record of.

"Bottled lullaby," Wren read aloud. "Acquired from a fiddler in Lowmarsh, owed to a widow in Candlewick Row. What does a bottled lullaby look like?"

"Like light through honey, if honey could hum. Careful with that entry—the fiddler sold it twice, so the provenance page matters more than the description."

"And this one—" Wren paused, frowning at the older ink, browned with age. "This entry is torn out. Half of it. There's just a stub left and someone wrote *returned in full, see master ledger* underneath."

Emrys's hand stilled on her pen.

Some nights, in the deep shelves of the archive, she still came across the redaction scars. Sixty years of ledgers, and every copy carried the same wound—except hers, and hers had been healed differently: not cut away but filled in, after the unbinding, line by line, in her own hand, reconstructing from Corvane's testimony and the queen's recovered memory everything the Guild had paid a kingdom to forget. *The crown remembers,* the dying warden had whispered, and oh, it had. It had remembered everything, and it had made the world pay for the privilege.

"The old Guild traded badly," Emrys said. "Someone cleaned up the mess by cutting it out of the books. It took a long time to write it back in."

"Did they find who did it? Who tore the pages?"

"They found her." Emrys dipped her pen. "She's writing the new entries now. Copy the stub exactly as it is—we don't restore damaged records, Wren. We annotate them. A scar tells the truth twice: once about the wound and once about the healing. Never sand that off."

Wren wrote that down, which pleased Emrys more than any cleverness could have. Then the girl sat back, chewed her pen, and asked the question Emrys had been waiting for since the doorstep—the question every apprentice asked eventually, usually on the third or fourth evening, when the ledgers stopped being mysterious and started being legible.

"Why do you keep them so carefully?" Wren gestured at the shelves, the tables, the thousands of pages. "You're a thief with almost no memories. Corvane's journal says—I read it, I'm sorry—you gave up everything you'd stolen. Your whole hoard. Your own past, half of it. So why does someone with almost nothing keep such careful accounts of everyone else's things?"

Emrys set down her pen.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the leaded window she could see the courtyard, the wet cobbles shining, and above the roofline a scatter of stars—more every month, the astronomers said, though the count had slowed lately, as if the sky were nearly full again. Somewhere across the city, Princess Isolde—Queen Isolde now, crowned in a ceremony so quiet most citizens mistook it for a funeral—was sitting with her father's last words written on cards around her chamber, keeping them warm the way one keeps an ember. Somewhere, a boy was waking up who would know his sister's name today, and not know why he'd ever forgotten it, and eat his breakfast without wondering.

That was the shape of the victory: not triumph, but restoration. Small returns, delivered daily.

"Because I know what it costs," Emrys said, "when nobody keeps them."

She turned the ledger toward Wren and tapped an entry near the front—one of the few in a different hand, older, blunter, belonging to a man who had died smiling and nameless on a floor covered in chalk circles. *Taught the deep craft. Paid with the memory of his sister, Maren, age nine, laughing with a mouthful of blackberries. Worth it.*

"He learned to steal from himself, to show me how it's done," Emrys said. "The lesson killed him, in a way. He forgot his own name before the end. But I have his sister's laugh written down here, word for word, exactly as he described it to me. So somewhere in the world, Maren laughing at nine years old with blackberries on her face still exists—even though Corvane is gone, even though he couldn't carry her out with him." She closed the book gently. "Memories don't survive being forgotten, Wren. They survive being *kept*. Someone has to do the keeping. That's the whole trade, if you do it honestly. Not taking. Keeping. For the ones who can't."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Rain dripped from the eaves in a slow, irregular rhythm, like a ledger being written by the roof.

"My brother," she said at last. "When he forgets me—on the bad days—he's not sad. That's the worst part. He just looks at me like I'm a stranger who won't leave, and I can tell there's nothing behind his eyes reaching for me. Nothing lost. Just absent." Her voice held steady, but her hands had curled into fists in her lap. "If I learn this craft properly. Truly properly. Could I steal back what he lost? Even if the forgetting wasn't stolen from him—just—fell out? Can you return something to a person who doesn't know it's missing?"

Emrys thought of a king who had repeated the same kind day for sixty years, hollow and warm and smiling, while his daughter ran the court on ritual and rote. She thought of the hour he had gotten back—old, terrified, himself—and the thanks in his eyes, and the way Isolde had held his hand and promised to tell no one, and had kept that promise so fiercely that Emrys sometimes wondered whether the promise was heavier than the throne.

She thought of the one memory she had kept.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "The far memories come back slowest. But I know someone who is trying to find out, at the palace, with better resources than ours. And I know that the trying matters, even when it fails. Even when it fails, Wren, the person knows somebody came looking for them. That gets kept too. It goes somewhere."

"Where?"

"That," Emrys said, "is a lesson for your second year."

* * *

They hung the veil on the first clear night of summer.

Emrys had made it herself over six weeks, working by lamplight after the ledgers were balanced—which meant late, which meant the apprenticeship of her fingers had to make up for what the archives could not teach her, since the memory of learning to sew had gone into the well with everything else. Her hands remembered anyway. Hands were loyal that way. They kept what the mind surrendered.

Deep blue velvet, dense as a night sky. And scattered across it, stitched in silver thread at slightly uneven intervals—she had counted them three times to be sure—two hundred and forty-one stars. Every one the entity had eaten. Every one she had poured back out of the well beneath the palace, spending her hoard coin by coin until the binding sealed and the sky refilled and the world, blinking, remembered what it had been looking at.

At the center, larger than the rest, worked in gold: a wolf. Not snarling. Not fleeing. Just standing, head high, facing outward from the door instead of inward—guarding the street from the guildhouse, rather than the other way around. A thief's emblem inverted. It had taken her four attempts to get the muzzle right.

Wren held the ladder while Emrys hooked the veil above the door, and the two younger apprentices—fisher twins from the coast who could hold a grudge like a memory and were learning to hold memories like a grace instead—passed up the pins and the hammer and offered commentary that was mostly unhelpful.

"It's crooked," said one twin.

"It's level," said the other. "The doorframe's crooked."

"The frame has been crooked for two hundred years. We don't restore frames either. We annotate them." Emrys stepped down, wiped her palms, and looked up.

The veil caught the lamplight from the windows and threw it back silvered, the stars glinting like the real ones beginning to emerge overhead, and for a moment the door seemed less like an entrance than a threshold between two skies—the kept one and the keeping one—and Emrys thought that was about right. That was the whole Guild now, hanging on a doorframe that listed slightly to the east. Two hundred and forty-one reasons never again to confuse acquisition with stewardship.

Vess came out to look, arms crossed, saying nothing, which from Vess was a eulogy of praise. After a while she said, "Your stitching is terrible," and went back inside, and Emrys smiled at the door for longer than dignity allowed.

Later—when the apprentices were abed and the guildhouse had settled into its nighttime creaks, when the lamps were banked and the ledgers locked and the streets outside washed quiet under the returned stars—Emrys climbed to her room and took the small box from under the floorboard.

One memory. Deliberately kept. The single theft she would never return.

She lay back on her bed and opened it the way she had opened it a thousand times since winter, carefully, the way you open a letter from someone dead.

A man's face, alive and laughing. Not the shell-king's looped smile, not the crown's borrowed warmth—a real laugh, creased and startled and delighted, the laugh of a man remembering what it was to be alive for one borrowed hour in a body grown strange to him. Sunlight on gray hair. His daughter's hand in his. The particular joy of a person who had gotten himself back long enough to be grateful, and had spent his gratitude not on rage or grief but on this: *tell no one what I was made to forget. Let me keep it, even going out. Let it be mine.*

He had asked her—no, he hadn't asked; Isolde had arranged it, fierce and dry-eyed, in the corridor outside his chamber—whether anyone would remember what he truly was. Not the shell. Not the entity's puppet. The man: Aldric the Quiet, who had loved his gardens and his daughter and his kingdom in that order, and who had died at eighty-nine in a bed that smelled of rosemary, one lucid hour richer than he'd been in sixty years.

*Someone should remember him as he was,* Isolde had said. *Not the stories. Him.*

So Emrys had kept one theft. His face, alive and laughing. She carried it the way a lamp carries flame—carefully, aware of the wind, aware of what it cost to keep anything burning in this world.

Tomorrow there would be ledgers to balance. Returns to deliver: a lullaby to Candlewick Row, a wedding-day to a widower in the Copper Quarter, a mother's voice to a fisherwoman's son. Wren would ask a dozen sharp questions and copy every stub exactly as it stood, scars and all. Vess would sign her name in plain ink and wince, and heal, slowly, the way kingdoms heal, the way brothers forget and relearn their sisters' names. The stars would burn above it all, kept now by everyone and no one, common as daylight, free as rain.

Emrys closed the memory box and slid it back beneath the floorboard, and lay a while longer in the dark, listening to the guildhouse settle, holding the laugh of a dead king the way you hold an ember through a long night: not to use it. Just so the world would have one more light in it come morning.

*Someone has to remember for the ones who can't.*

She slept, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was its own kind of peace—and woke before dawn to Wren knocking softly at her door, breathless, saying that the square was full of people, that something had happened to the stars, that Emrys needed to come and see.

Emrys dressed quickly, heart steady, mind already turning pages like a ledger opening to a fresh entry—and followed the girl down into the blue hour, out beneath the veil of two hundred and forty-one kept lights, toward whatever the sky had decided to do next.
