## 5. Thieves of Geography

The ledgers lived in the counting house's deepest vault, behind a door that only opened for three keys and one regret. Lena had spent eleven years learning the shape of that regret — the way Magister Corvane paused each night before locking up, his hand flat against the iron as though apologizing to it — and so she knew exactly which hour the door would stand unattended: the quarter-bell past midnight, when Corvane went down alone to reconcile the day's impossibilities and liked, in his private ritual of penance, to do it without witnesses.

She had never stolen anything in her life. Her hands shook so badly fitting the copied key into her sleeve that she nearly dropped it twice on the stairs.

*You owe him everything,* said the voice that had governed her since she was small enough to be carried. *He bought your life.*

*And now he means to spend a child's,* she answered, and turned the key.

Inside, the old ledgers breathed dust and tallow-smoke. She had mapped this room from memory a hundred times without ever entering it: the shelf of tongues dead before kingdoms, the ledger bound in grey vellum whose pages were said to be made from the skin of a folded country. She took it, and its two companions, and wrapped them in oilcloth against the rain still hammering the city, and then stood a moment in the dark listening to her own heart argue with itself.

It was Ash who settled the argument. He was waiting where she'd left him, in the linen closet off the scriptorium, curled around his compass like it was the last warm thing in the world. When she opened the door he looked up at her with those eyes the color of weather coming, and said nothing at all — because he didn't need to. Unmapped things recognized one another. That was the whole of their covenant already.

"Time to go," she whispered, in the dead tongue, the only word she'd learned that mattered: *with me.*

His hand found hers. Small. Cold. Certain.

* * *

Ash did not understand theft. In Vael — in the Vael that lived behind his eyes, whole and singing — taking what another held was a wound in the fabric of things, and the fabric remembered. But he understood doors, and Lena was opening them all, one after another, doors the Guild had locked against him, and if these were doors made of paper instead of stone, well. A door was a door. His compass agreed; the needle lay quiet against his palm for the first time since the storm, pointing not at any wall but at the woman beside him, steady as a heartbeat.

They went down through the Guild's bones. He felt the building thinking about them — old buildings did that here, muttered and shifted like sleeping animals — but the building's thoughts were slow, and they were quick, and by the time an alarm bell began somewhere above them, thin and furious as a wasp in a bottle, they were already out into the rain-washed streets of Lowmarsh, where the city began to do the thing Ash loved and feared about it: fold.

Streets here did not go where streets should. An alley between a chandler's and a dye-house might open onto a stair that climbed into yesterday, or loop back on itself until a walker came out where they started, older by an afternoon. The Guild called these the creases and pretended to have surveyed them. The people who lived in them called them home.

Lena moved through them like someone reading aloud a book she'd memorized in childhood. Left at the wall with no door in it. Right where the cobbles forgot themselves. Ash watched her and felt something loosen in his chest that had been knotted since the night the water took him. *She knows the way even where there is no way.* That was a Vaelish thing to be. That was almost a Vaelish word.

Behind them, bells. Ahead, a bridge that wasn't on any chart, slung between two rooftops over a street that had drowned itself out of spite a century ago. And on the bridge, arms crossed, waiting as though she'd been expecting them for years, stood a woman in a witch's patched grey cloak with a toll-lantern swinging from a crooked staff.

"Toll," said the woman.

Lena stopped. "Sable."

"You know my name." The witch's smile had edges. "That's flattering, cartographer, seeing as the Guild struck it from every record they keep. Disgraced bridge-toll witch. Toll-taker of a bridge nobody crosses. I hear they say I charged a duke his shadow and let a beggar pass for free." Her lantern swung toward Ash, and the light inside it wasn't firelight; it moved like water remembering. "I also hear the Guild is offering more gold than exists for a wet little boy and whoever's carrying him."

"We have no gold."

"Good." Sable stepped aside from the middle of the bridge. "Gold bores me. Crossing costs something true instead. A memory you won't miss. A lie you're fond of. One honest fear." Her eyes went from Lena to Ash and back, and softened by a degree, which Ash suspected was the most she ever gave away. "For him? Nothing. Children running from cages cross free. House rule. I invented it tonight, but rules have to start somewhere."

"And for me?"

"For you—" Sable tilted her head, listening to something in the lantern. "Interesting. Your toll's already been paid. By someone. Long ago." She frowned at her own lantern as if it had contradicted her. "Go on, then. Before the bells find us."

Ash crossed the bridge holding Sable's sleeve, because bridges between worlds deserved respect, and halfway across he heard the lantern whisper in a language older than kingdoms — his language — and the witch went very still, and when they reached the far side she was following them, and no one asked her not to.

* * *

By dawn they were four, though the fourth arrived sideways.

The smuggler kept a warehouse on a street that appeared only during fog, which meant that in Lowmarsh it appeared most mornings, and inside it he kept the things he'd smuggled: half a sunset in a jar, a staircase that went down in every direction, and — so the stories swore, and the stories were right — a river, sold to him by a bankrupt municipality and stored in forty barrels marked OLIVE OIL, BEST QUALITY.

His name was Ferrin Croft, and he was awake because guilt kept merchant's hours.

"They're saying it in every market square," he told them, pouring tea that tasted faintly of the river he'd once sold, which was to say of everywhere it had been. "Guild proclamations, nailed up before midnight. Not a bounty in coin — they've learned better than that. They're paying in *amendments.*" He slid a crumpled proclamation across the table, and Lena read it twice because the first reading refused to be believed.

*To whosoever delivers the child:* it read, in the Guild's beautiful merciless hand, *the Guild will amend one fact of your life. A debt forgiven. A death undone. A name restored to the records of the world.*

Lena's throat closed. She looked up and found Sable watching her, and Ferrin watching her, and Ash watching her with the terrible fluency of children who understand adults better than adults wish.

"They know," she said quietly. "Corvane knows what I am. Or isn't. He's telling the whole city: bring him the boy, and you can become real."

"That's not how I'd read it," Ferrin said. "I'd read it as: they're scared. You don't pay in miracles unless you've run out of everything cheaper."

Outside, the fog thickened until the windows went white, and in the white Ash heard — or dreamed he heard — the city calling his country's name in a thousand borrowed voices. Bells ringing in rhythms Vael had used for festivals. Gulls crying syllables of the old tongue. The erased place pressing against the world's skin from underneath, the way a word presses against the teeth before it is spoken.

He put his compass on the table between them all. The needle spun once, twice, and settled — pointing at each of them in turn, around the circle, like a candle being passed.

"What's it doing?" Sable asked.

Lena translated the question into the dead tongue. Ash considered his answer carefully, because it was the first sentence he would say to his family — and that was what they were, he realized; that was what this strange table of thieves and witches and smugglers was becoming — and he wanted the sentence to be a true one.

"It is counting," he said. "In my country, maps were not drawn. They were *grown.* A map was a living thing, made of everyone who loved the place it showed. My compass is trying to grow a new map." He touched the glass above the needle, gently, the way you touch a bird. "It has four points now. It needs more."

Ferrin laughed, a short startled sound, and refilled the cups. Sable hung her toll-lantern on a hook as if hanging up her coat, staying. And Lena reached across the table and turned the proclamation face-down, so the Guild's beautiful merciless handwriting looked at the wood instead of at them.

"Then we'd best be worth mapping," she said.

But later, while the others slept in the warehouse's impossible rooms — Ferrin snoring beside a barrel that gurgled like a brook, Sabe dreaming with her lantern lit — Lena stood at the fog-white window with the grey vellum ledger open on her knees, and by candlelight read the page she had stolen the whole world to steal.

It was a census. The last census of Vael, taken the winter before the folding, in ink that still gleamed like wet slate. Thousands of names, column after column, each one followed by a small notation in the margin. *Paid. Paid. Paid.* The price of survival, entered line by line.

Her finger moved down the final page, where the infant entries were kept, births recorded in a hurried hand — and stopped.

There were two names written there in the same ink, the same night, one above the other. Two infants born in the last hours of a dying country. The first name was smudged, water-damaged, half-legible: *A—h.*

The second was not smudged at all. It was clear as a bell, and it was hers.

*Lena Winterbourne. Smuggled out. Toll unpaid.*

Someone had carried her across a bridge, and someone had paid for her passage in something true — and the ledger knew whose name was missing from the world, and why, and it had been waiting eleven years in the dark for her to come and read it.

Behind her, in the doorway, Ash stood wrapped in a blanket, watching her face.

"Lena," he said, in her language, his first word of it, chosen carefully. Then, in his own, the word he had been carrying since the storm: "*Vael.*"

The compass, on the table, pointed at them both.
