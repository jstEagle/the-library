## 8. Corvane's Arithmetic

They had run out of city.

Lena felt it in her feet before she understood it in her mind — the particular ache of walking streets that no longer agreed to be walked, the cobbles beneath them going soft and uncertain as old bread. Behind them, the folded alleys of the smuggler's quarter had begun to close like a hand curling shut. Ahead, where Sable swore a bridge stood not three years ago, there was only fog and the smell of cold iron.

"They're folding it behind us," Sable said. She had her witch-marks drawn on both palms now, ink to the wrist, and they were smoking faintly, which Lena did not think was good. "The Guild's cartomancers. They're rolling the map up from the edges."

"Then we go faster," said Ferren, the river-seller, shifting Ash higher onto his back. The boy rode him like a burr rides wool, silent, his compass held against Ferren's shoulder blade as though he could feed its bearing directly into the man's spine.

"There is no faster," Sable snapped. "There's only *where*, and we're nearly out of that too."

The fog ahead thickened, then parted — not the way fog parts, but the way a curtain parts when someone behind it decides you've waited long enough.

Guild hunters stood in the street. Six of them, in grey traveling cloaks with the Guild's sigil stitched at the shoulder: an open hand holding a closed eye. They did not draw weapons. They did not need to. Behind them, at the far end of the street, more figures moved through the mist, and above them all, impossibly, hung the counting house itself — its stone facade, its tall lit windows — projected against the fog like a memory of a building, or a promise of one.

"Ah," said Ferren quietly. "Well. It was a good week."

Lena put herself between the hunters and Ash without deciding to. Her body simply did it, the way water finds low ground.

And then the hunters parted, and Magister Corvane walked through them.

He looked exactly as he always had: a tall, dry man in charcoal wool, silver at the temples, spectacles he didn't need because he liked the way they made people wait for him to remove them. He removed them now. He polished them on his sleeve with the unhurried care of a man who had never once been late for anything, including this.

"Lena," he said.

She had prepared, in the long weeks of flight, a great many things to say to him. Every one of them left her at once. What remained was the oldest thing, the child-thing, the thing she hated herself for still carrying: *You came.*

"You found us," she said instead.

"I never lost you." He tucked the spectacles away. "I taught you to hide, girl. Do you imagine I'd teach anyone a trick I couldn't undo?"

* * *

They stood in the dead street while the fog breathed around them. Sable's marks had stopped smoking; whatever Corvane had done to the space, it wasn't hostile yet, merely *closed*, like a ledger whose covers had been brought together. Ferren set Ash down slowly, keeping a hand on the boy's shoulder. Ash stared at Corvane with the flat, unblinking attention he gave to weather and to doors.

"You're not here to arrest us," Lena said.

"No."

"To kill us?"

"Don't be dramatic. I'm here to do arithmetic." He glanced down the ruined street, at the soft cobbles, the fog-wrapped absence of bridge. "Do you know what happens if you reach the seam? Truly? You've read the ledgers you stole — the old ones, the ones written before the Guild learned discretion. So you know what Vael was. A country of perhaps two million souls, folded out of the world in a single night, in exchange for deliverance from the Hungering War. A bargain struck by desperate people with something worse than desperation. And what did they leave behind?" His eyes went to Ash. "Collateral. A key. One child, kept outside the lock so the door could be opened again, should the world ever prove worth re-entering."

"He's not a key," Lena said. "He's a boy. His name is Ash."

"Ash," Corvane repeated, tasting it. "A name made of what's left after burning. Fitting. Did he choose it, or did you?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Here is my arithmetic, Lena, and I want you to actually do it, because you are the finest cartographer I have ever trained and I refuse to believe you haven't already done it in the dark hours and refused to look at the sum."

He began to walk, slowly, and — because the street was his now, folded to his will — the street walked with him, fog receding, revealing a wide square that had not existed a moment before. In its center stood a fountain, dry, its basin cracked. It was not any fountain Lena knew. Its carvings were of wolves wearing crowns of wheat.

Her breath caught. She had drawn this fountain once, from a half-translated ledger, as an exercise. She had thought it invention.

"Vael returns," Corvane said, "and everything erased comes back. Two million people. Their fields, their rivers, their language, their dead. A whole nation resurrected from a bargain kept faithfully for three hundred years. Magnificent. Worth any price, the masters say." He turned to face her fully. "But the world did not stand still inside the fold, girl. The space Vael vacated did not stay empty. Cities grew there. Roads. Harbors. Marrowfield. Coldharbour Reach. The entire delta province of Sereth, forty thousand souls, built on soil that used to be Vaelen wheat country. When the fold opens, the world must reconcile its books. Two lands cannot occupy one ground. And the world, in my long experience of its bookkeeping, does not split the difference."

"You don't know that," Sable said sharply.

"I know the ledgers. The bargain's original terms survive in the First Folio. *That which was given shall be returned; that which grew in its place shall be given in turn.*" He let the old formula hang in the fog. "Vael comes back, and everything born on Vaelen ground since goes. Not killed — unmade. Never-had-been. The mothers in Sereth will wake to empty cradles and no memory of why they're grieving." He took off one glove, finger by finger, a habit from every hard lesson he'd ever delivered. "Forty thousand in the delta alone. Perhaps two hundred thousand across the reclaimed territory. Against two million returned." He spread his bare hands. "There is your arithmetic. I have done it every night for twenty years. It always comes out the same. The many outweigh the few. Even the Guild's masters flinched from it — they wanted the trade route, the silk, the salt-plums, the impossible wines. I am the one who read to the bottom of the page."

Lena's mouth was dry. "And me?"

Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was kind.

"You are the elegant solution," he said. "You see it, don't you. You were born in Vael. Smuggled out in the last hour, an infant in a chart-tube, which is why no parish register holds you, why no tax roll, why even the Guild's great census — which misses nothing, Lena, nothing — has no line with your name on it. You are not unmapped. You are *pre-mapped*. Filed under a country that isn't there." He stepped closer. "When the fold opens, the world will come looking for its own. It will find you, and it will take you back, and it will call it restoration, and you will be Vaelen again, whole, named, home — and Ash will walk through the door beside you and the reconciliation completes cleanly, key and collateral both returned, and perhaps — *perhaps* — the terms can be renegotiated from inside, by Vaelens themselves, who might choose a gentler arithmetic than ours."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the masters spend the boy alone, badly, and the reconciliation runs wild, and I genuinely do not know what breaks." He said it plainly, without threat. That was the terrible thing about Corvane: he had never needed threats. "I am offering you the only version of this that ends with anyone choosing anything."

* * *

The fog had gone very still. Somewhere in it, the projected counting house glowed like a lantern.

"You bought my survival," Lena said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "In the flood year. You told me once. You said you'd paid a price for me no one else would have paid."

"I did."

"So this is the collection."

"This is the truth," Corvane said. "I have never asked you for repayment. I am asking you now for something better than repayment. I'm asking you to be what I trained you to be — the person who reads to the bottom of the page — and then to act accordingly." He extended his hand, palm up, and in it lay a small grey card, Guild-issue, blank. "This is a restoration writ. Your name goes on it. Winterbourne — your mother's name, the one she gave the ferryman who carried you out. I've known it for twenty years, Lena. I could have given it to you any day. I kept it because I was waiting for you to be able to carry it." His voice dropped, and for one moment the magister fell away and there was only the man who had found a half-drowned orphan and taught her to draw coastlines so she would always know where the edges of things were. "Come home. Both meanings of the word. Bring the boy. Save two million people and yourself in the same motion. There is no cleaner sum in all the world's accounts."

Behind her, Lena heard Ferren shift. Heard Sable's breath go ragged. Heard nothing at all from Ash — and that silence pulled at her more than any argument.

She looked at the boy. He was watching her with those eyes the color of shallow water over slate, and in them was no fear and no pleading, only a question offered without pressure, the way he offered everything: *What will you do?*

And Lena understood, finally, the shape of the trap. Not the hunters, not the folded street, not the writ in Corvane's palm. The trap was that he was right. That the arithmetic was sound. That two million outweighed two hundred thousand, that her life weighed almost nothing against either column, that any decent person would step through that door.

Any decent person. But she had spent her whole life learning that she was not a person the world's records accounted for, and she had learned it in the arms of the found family standing behind her, and she had promised a feral child in a storm-soaked doorway one thing only: *I will not spend you.*

"Keep the writ," she said.

Corvane's hand did not waver. "Lena—"

"My name isn't in your ledger," she said. "It never was. It's in *this* one." She reached back without looking, and Ash's small cold fingers found hers. "And I'm not surrendering him. Not to the Guild. Not to Vael. Not to arithmetic."

For a long moment Corvane simply looked at her, and she watched twenty years of careful love and careful accounting war behind his eyes.

Then, distantly, through the fog, came a sound none of them expected: a bell. Not the Guild's bell. A bell of green bronze, ringing a note that made the cracked fountain hum in sympathy — and Ash's compass, in the boy's pocket, began to spin.

"The seam," Ash said, in his dead tongue, and for once Lena understood him without the ledgers. "It's opening on its own."

Corvane went white. "That's impossible. The key is standing right here—"

"No," Sable whispered, staring past them all into the fog, where a second light had appeared, gold and wrong. "*Someone else has a key.*"
