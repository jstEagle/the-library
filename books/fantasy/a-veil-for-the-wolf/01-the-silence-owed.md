## 1. The Silence Owed

There are three rules to walking the Thornfell passes, and I learned all of them the expensive way. One: never trust a road that's too easy, because easy roads are where the mountain kills you out of boredom. Two: never trust a client who prays more than they pay. Three: never, under any circumstances, trust your own god to do anything useful.

I'd kept rule three for ten years without much effort. Vael was a silent god — that was the whole of his portfolio, near as anyone could tell. Silence, stillness, the held breath between lightning and thunder. His temples were ruins before my grandmother was born, his priesthood a rumor, and his faithful were mostly people like me: mercenaries, smugglers, and other professionals who appreciated a deity that didn't ask for anything. You swore the oath, he marked your wrist with a thin gray line like an old scar, and in exchange you got... nothing visible. No miracles. No voices. Just a quiet so complete it felt almost like company.

I used to tell myself that was the point. That silence *was* the blessing. That while every other god in the pantheon was busy demanding blood and gold and firstborn sons, mine had the good manners to leave me alone.

It's amazing what a decade of not looking too closely will let you believe.

* * *

The pilgrim caravan was eleven souls and forty-one mules, which tells you something about their priorities. They came up from the lowlands under a banner of the Dawnmother — sun-yellow cloth gone gray at the edges — bound for the high shrine at Cael's Rest, where the faithful believed the Dawnmother's light pooled thickest come midwinter. Nine days through the passes if the weather held. Twelve if it didn't. I quoted them for twelve and prayed to nothing in particular for nine.

Their leader was a fat, gentle man named Brother Aldous who sweated through his vestments even in mountain cold and called me "Mistress Reyes" like it was a title instead of a warning label. He had a habit of praying aloud at every switchback, thanking the Dawnmother for the view, the mules, the crisp air, the fact that nobody had died yet. By day four I was ready to push him off a cliff just to give him something new to be grateful for.

"You don't pray," he observed on the fifth morning, watching me check the mule harnesses. Not accusing. Curious, the way children are curious about scars.

"I'm spoken for."

"Ah." He nodded sagely, as though this explained everything. "The silent one. Vael." He said the name carefully, the way you'd handle a knife you weren't sure was sharp. "Forgive me, Mistress Reyes, but the theologians say he died. Long ago. That his silence is only... absence."

"Theologians say a lot of things from down where it's warm."

"And what do you say?"

I tested a buckle that didn't need testing. "I say he doesn't answer, and hasn't in ten years, and a god who doesn't answer is either dead or very well-mannered. Either way, he's never once gotten in my way."

Aldous smiled the smile of a man who pitied you and knew better than to show it. "Perhaps silence isn't absence. Perhaps it's patience."

"Perhaps it's a long walk off a short cliff," I said, and swung up into the lead.

Here's the thing I didn't say, the thing I hadn't said to anyone in years: the mark on my wrist had been fading. Slowly, the way ice thins over a stream — one week it's solid, the next there's a hairline crack and you're in the water. The gray line that Vael's oath had burned into me at nineteen was paler now. Thinner. Some mornings I woke and had to hunt for it among the other scars, and some mornings I found myself pressing my thumb against it like a bruise, waiting for something. A warmth. A weight. Anything.

Nothing ever came. And I told myself that was fine. That was the deal. That was the *silence owed*, and I paid it gladly, because the alternative was admitting I'd given ten years of my life to a god who might not exist anymore.

You can survive a lot in the Thornfell on that kind of arithmetic. What you can't survive is the day the arithmetic changes.

* * *

It changed on the seventh day, at a shrine called Hollowstone.

Hollowstone wasn't much — a shelf of granite overhung by a lip of rock, with a fire-blackened hollow where travelers had left offerings since before the Thornfell had roads. There was no idol. No altar stone. Just the hollow, and the habit. Even the smuggler routes bent slightly to pass it, because even men who trusted nothing else trusted the old waypoints. You left a coin or a candle stub or, if you were broke, a honest curse, and you moved on. The shrine had never answered anyone in living memory. It was furniture. It was geology.

We made camp a hundred yards below it, in a fold of the trail sheltered from the wind. Supper was barley and salt pork, and Aldous was leading the evening prayers when the youngest pilgrim — a girl named Sefa, maybe sixteen, traveling with her mother — went into convulsions.

I've seen fevers. I've seen mountain sickness, bad water, poisoned meat, and one memorable case of a man who'd eaten snow laced with wyrm-spoor. This wasn't any of those. Sefa arched like a drawn bow, her eyes rolled white, and she screamed — not pain, exactly. Something worse. The scream of someone being *listened to* after years of shouting into a well.

Her mother grabbed her. Aldous grabbed his prayer beads and started invoking the Dawnmother at speed. And I stood there with my hand on my knife doing absolutely nothing useful, because whatever this was, steel wouldn't help.

"Sefa." Her mother was crying now. "Sefa, sweetheart, stay with me—"

And then the girl stopped screaming, went limp, and said — in a voice that was hers but layered, hers with something underneath it like bedrock under soil:

"*She hears.*"

The camp went quiet enough to hear the wind worrying the tent ropes.

"She has heard every prayer you ever sent up and could not keep them all, and now—" The girl's head turned. Not the way heads turn. The neck moved wrong, smooth and slow, like a key rotating in a lock. "—and now the doors are opening, and she must choose, and choosing is *hunger*—"

"Aldous," I said quietly. "What is she saying?"

He had gone the color of tallow. "That's not — those aren't words of the Dawnmother's liturgy. That's older. That's *old-tongue*." He crossed himself with the sun-sign, twice. "Mistress Reyes, I studied the old rites as a novitiate. That is a voice from before the Concord. That should not be possible. The gods have been *silent* since the Concord—"

Above us, Hollowstone caught fire.

Not kindled. Not lit. The hollow simply *ignited* — a column of white-gold flame standing straight up out of black granite in the middle of a snowfield, casting shadows that fell the wrong direction, toward the flame instead of away from it. The heat hit our camp like an opened oven door. Two mules screamed and snapped their lead lines. Somewhere behind me, a pilgrim began reciting the Litany of Morning in a high, cracked voice, faster and faster, like a man trying to outrun something.

Sefa sat up. Her eyes were still white. She was smiling, and I will carry that smile to whatever grave is generous enough to take me.

"Mistress Reyes," she said, in her own voice again, small and terrified and *aware*. "It's warm. Why is it warm? Mother, why am I — I feel *heard*. I feel so *heard*—"

Her mother lunged for her. Aldous shouted something about profanity and miracle in the same breath. And I did the mercenary thing, the sensible thing, the thing I'd done for ten years: I started calculating exits.

Then the mark on my wrist *burned*.

Ten years of nothing, and suddenly the old scar-line was a live coal, searing up my forearm, and through the pain came something I had no framework for — a pressure, a presence, vast and patient and *straining*, like a whale sounding against the underside of ice. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep two mountains away.

Vael.

Not dead. Not absent. *Awake*, or waking, or — gods help me — *answering*.

"Sefa!" I was moving before I decided to, shoving through the panic, grabbing the girl's shoulders. Her skin was hot as a hearthstone. "Listen to me. Whatever's talking through you — you tell it to get *out*. You hear me? You're not a door. You're a girl. Tell it—"

The white flame above us flared, and the world went strange and slow.

I remember the heat bending. I remember the snow around our camp steaming, then not melting — *unmelting*, frost crawling backward up the tent poles. I remember Sefa's mother reaching for her daughter and the air between them going glassy, golden, thick as honey.

And I remember the miracle.

Because that's what it was — there's no other word. The fever broke. Not eased. *Broke*, like a wave against rock, and Sefa gasped in a full clean breath, color flooding back into her face, and every ache and chill and shadow of the climb lifted off the whole caravan at once. Old Brother Hesk's bad hip stopped hurting. The mules calmed. For one shining moment everyone in that camp stood in a warmth like summer noon in the dead of winter, and eleven people simultaneously understood that they had been *seen*, truly seen, by something immense and burning-bright, and that they were loved—

—and then the moment ended, and Brother Aldous was dead.

No wound. No warning. He simply folded, mid-prayer, like a puppet with cut strings, and where he knelt the snow did not melt but turned to fine gray ash in a perfect circle around him. The white flame above Hollowstone guttered out as if snuffed between finger and thumb.

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind stopped.

I knelt there with Sefa's mother sobbing into my shoulder and the girl herself staring at her own hands, healed and holy and hollowed, and I looked down at my wrist.

The mark had gone dark. Not faded — *dark*, a black line like a vein of midnight under my skin, and it was pulsing. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like a heartbeat.

Like something buried under the mountain, counting.

* * *

They buried Aldous at dawn in the ash-circle, because the ground there was soft as tilled earth, which none of us wanted to think about too hard. The caravan wanted to turn back. I talked them up the pass instead — farther from the lowlands, higher into country I knew, where the smuggler roads ran and the Ashen Court's hunters were less likely to look. I told them it was for safety. It was partly for safety.

Mostly it was because I needed time to think, and thinking requires distance, and distance requires movement, and if I stopped moving I would have to sit with the knowledge that my god had just answered a stranger's prayer through a burning rock and killed a fat kind man in the same breath, and that the mark I'd carried like a marriage ring for ten years was beating under my skin like a second heart.

Sefa found me at the first rest halt. She walked up quiet as a cat and stood beside me looking out at the white peaks, and after a while she said, without preamble:

"It touched me. When it answered. I could feel it deciding." Her hands were wrapped around a mug of tea she wasn't drinking. "It was like — like being chosen for something. And it looked at everyone, Mistress Reyes. Everyone in the camp. But it spent longest on *you*."

My stomach dropped somewhere below the snowline. "Why would it spend longest on me?"

"Because of your wrist." She said it simply, the way children say terrible things. "When it looked at you, the voice underneath got *quiet*. Careful. Like when you're walking past a sleeping dog." She finally sipped her tea. "It knows what you are."

"What am I, then?"

She shrugged, sixteen and unbothered. "Didn't say. But it was scared of you. Or scared *for* you. Those feel the same, sometimes."

She wandered back to her mother, and I stood in the cold and flexed my hand and watched the black line pulse, and thought about ten years of silence, and how I'd told myself silence was a gift.

Below us, far down in the foothills, a thread of smoke rose from the border town of Greyhollow. Too much smoke. Too many fires, all at once, in a town that size.

Somewhere down there, I suspected, people were already asking questions about answered prayers. And the Ashen Court always sent its hunters where the answers were.

I pulled my glove on over the mark, took the lead rope, and started walking.

The mountain, for the first time in ten years, felt like it was holding its breath.
