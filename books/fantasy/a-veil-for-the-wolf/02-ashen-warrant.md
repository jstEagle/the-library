## 2. Ashen Warrant

Greyhollow smelled like most border towns did — wet wool, tallow smoke, and the particular desperation of people who lived close enough to the mountains to hear them and far enough to pretend they were safe. I'd been coming through it for ten years, long enough to know which innkeeper watered her beer and which constable could be bought with coin and which needed to be bought with flattery first.

None of that knowledge helped me on the morning the god-hunters rode in.

I was sitting in the common room of the Drowned Lamb, doing what I do best, which is nothing at all in a way that makes people underestimate me. A mercenary between contracts is a piece of furniture. You learn to be a chair. The Ashen Court's riders came through the east gate in a column of eight, grey cloaks dusted white from the pass, and every chair in Greyhollow turned its head at once.

I knew the livery. Everyone who worked the Thornfell roads knew it. The Court didn't come down for smugglers — we were beneath their dignity, small sins for small folk. They came down when prayer got *answered*, and somebody had told them about the shrine on the pilgrim road.

Three days ago, that shrine had burned cold fire and given a dying woman one more breath of life before taking her clean out of the world, along with two others. I'd watched it happen. I'd felt the mark on my wrist do absolutely nothing, the way it had done nothing for a decade, and I'd told myself that was fine. That silence was just silence. That gods were like distant kings: real enough to swear to, too far away to matter.

The god-hunters dismounted in front of the Drowned Lamb because it was the largest building on the square, not because they had information. That's how they work. They arrive like weather and let guilt make people talk.

I finished my beer, paid with exact change, and left by the kitchen door.

* * *

Here is the first rule of surviving in the passes, and I want it carved somewhere official: *you don't touch marked goods.* Smugglers deal in contraband of every kind — untaxed brandy, unlicensed steel, fugitives with warrants — but the moment something is touched by divine attention, you drop it like a hot coal and you keep walking. Mercenary neutrality isn't an ideal. It's arithmetic. Ten years in the Thornfell, and the arithmetic had kept me breathing while better fighters than me went into the ground.

So when I heard a child crying under the bridge behind the tannery, the correct move was to keep walking. I want that on the record. The correct move was very clearly to keep walking.

I stopped walking.

She was maybe nine, curled into the gap between the bridge stones where the tanners' dogs couldn't reach, and she had the look I'd learned to read in war camps — the stillness of someone who has decided that being invisible is a profession. Her hands were wrapped in rag strips, but the rags were scorched through, and underneath them her palms glowed faintly, like coals banked under ash. Shrine-light. Miracle-residue. The mark of answered prayer, written on her skin where everyone could see it.

"You're the one from the shrine," I said. "The little girl whose mother prayed."

Her eyes came up and pinned me, feral and exhausted. "They're killing everyone who was there."

"I know."

"Then why are you talking to me?"

Good question. I've asked it of myself several times since, usually around two in the morning. What I said then was: "Because you're loud enough to get us both killed. Move over."

She made room in the stones, and I sat down in the filth of Greyhollow's tannery row beside a marked child, and that was the moment my decade of careful neutrality developed its first crack. I didn't even hear it break. That's the thing about fractures. You never feel the first one. You only notice when the whole structure comes down.

* * *

The executions started at noon, in the market square, because the Ashen Court believes in public works.

I watched from the upper window of Marisol's counting-house, where I'd stashed Wren — she'd told me her name eventually, in the tone of someone handing over a hostage — behind a false wall meant for unpaid debts of the non-magical variety. From three streets away I could see the grey cloaks arranged in their precise semicircle, and I could see the townsfolk of Greyhollow arranged around them in a much less precise circle, the way crowds always are: straining forward and leaning back at the same time.

There were seven of them from the shrine caravan. Survivors of the miracle. People who had merely *witnessed* it got a branding and confiscation. People the light had touched — healed, marked, changed — got rope.

One of them was a carter named Aldous Fell whom I'd shared a fire with on the pilgrim road. He'd complained about his knees and his wife and the price of feed in that order, and he'd been funny about all three. He stood on the scaffold looking for someone in the crowd, and I hope to every silent god he wasn't looking for me, because I was three streets away behind glass, being a chair.

The Court's reader — a thin man whose voice carried without effort, the way things carry when they're backed by institutional murder — read the warrant. *By authority of the Ashen Court, in the matter of unlawful divine manifestation.* Unlawful. As if prayer were a licensing issue. As if the old gods answering after a century of silence were a violation of code four, subsection twelve.

Aldous Fell died well, if you're keeping score. He didn't beg. The woman after him begged, and the Court hanged her anyway, because the warrant doesn't have a mercy clause. The third was a boy of perhaps sixteen with burns on his neck in the shape of a hand, and when they brought him up the steps, I looked away from the window and found my own reflection staring back at me, and my wrist — the oath-mark, ten years quiet — gave one single pulse of heat, like a heart remembering it had work to do.

That was new. That was very much not fine.

"He's not going to be the last," said Marisol from the doorway.

She leaned there with a cup of something expensive, watching me watch the executions, and her face had the flat calm she wore when the numbers were bad. Marisol Veyra runs half the smuggling in the Thornfell out of a counting-house full of ledgers, and she reads people the way she reads manifests — quickly, ruthlessly, and always looking for what's hidden between the lines.

"There are two more marked survivors unaccounted for," she went on. "The Court knows it. They'll turn this town inside out until they find them." She took a sip. "Which brings me to the child behind my wall."

"She's no trouble."

"Oscar." Just my name, in the voice she used for inventory. "You've been my best guide for eight years because you don't take sides. You take contracts. There's a difference, and the difference is that guides with contracts live to be old guides. That girl has the light of an awakened shrine baked into her hands. Every hunter between here and the coast can smell it. You're not hiding a child anymore. You're carrying a lit fuse."

"The Court hanged a sixteen-year-old today."

"The Court hanged forty people in Coldspring the year your oath was fresh, and you drank through it and kept walking, because you understood the arrangement." She set the cup down on the sill, and for a moment her composure slipped, just a hair, and I saw something old and raw underneath. "I understand wanting to spit in their eye. Believe me. But you don't know what's waking up out there, and neither do I, and until one of us does, the smart play is to be nobody. Walk away, Oscar. Take the north road. Let the Court have its warrant and the child have her luck or lack of it."

"When have you ever known me to take the smart play?"

"Never. That's what frightens me."

She left the cup on the sill. It was still warm. Everything in Marisol's house was warm and comfortable and safe, right up until it wasn't, and I've had years since to wonder how long she'd already been making other arrangements when she stood in that doorway telling me to walk away. At the time I just thought she was tired. So was I. Tired people make the mistakes they were always going to make anyway, and comfort just decides the hour.

I went downstairs to the false wall.

* * *

Wren was sitting cross-legged in the dark with her blistered hands held out in front of her like an offering, watching the glow. Up close, in lamplight, it wasn't ugly. It was the color of the shrine-fire, gold gone silver at the edges, and it moved when she breathed.

"It's getting brighter," she said. "Every day it's brighter."

"That's a problem."

"You think I don't know that?" The ferocity cracked, and underneath was just a kid. "My mother prayed so hard. She prayed so hard, and it worked, and now everything that happens is because it worked." She looked at me with an expression far too old for her face. "Is that my fault?"

I crouched down to her level, which is a thing I'd sworn off doing with clients, because clients you kneel for are clients you'll bleed for, and I said, "No. Whatever else is true, that part isn't yours."

She studied me the way children study adults deciding whether to trust them, which is more honestly than adults ever do it. Then: "You have a mark too."

It wasn't a question. My sleeve had ridden up when I crouched, and there it was — the oath-scar, a thin grey band circling my wrist like a shackle grown into the bone. It hadn't glowed in ten years. It wasn't glowing now. But it was warm, and Wren's shrine-light flickered toward it like a candle flame leaning into a draft.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A mistake I made when I was young," I said. "Get your boots on. We're leaving tonight."

"The hunters—"

"—are watching the gates and the roads, which is why we're not using either. There's a smuggler's path out of the drainage tunnels that comes up in the high passes. It's miserable, it's cold, and it smells like the underside of Greyhollow, which is considerably worse than the top side. But nobody's waiting for us there, because nobody smart would take it." I stood and pulled my pack from behind the false panel. "Which has always been my defining quality."

She almost smiled. Kids will forgive you anything if you're honest about how bad the plan is.

We went up through the counting-house in the dark hours, past Marisol's office, where a lamp still burned. Through the open door I saw her at her desk with a ledger open and a courier's satchel beside it, and I raised a hand to knock, to say *something* — goodbye, thanks, don't sell me to the Ashen Court — and she looked up.

For one heartbeat neither of us spoke. Her eyes went from me to Wren in the shadows behind me and back, and I watched her weigh something, the way she weighed everything, and I saw the scales tip.

"North road," she said quietly. Not a question. Almost a plea.

"North road," I agreed, and lied to her with my whole face, because we both knew there had never been a north road. There was only deeper into the mountains, toward whatever was waking, and the mark on my wrist pulsed twice as I said it, warm as a second pulse, like something on the far end of a very long silence had just turned its head.

Marisol watched me go. And behind me, as the door swung shut, I heard her break the wax seal on that courier's satchel — and begin, softly, to write.
