## 3. What the Mountain Remembers

The child slept like a stone that had been dropped from a great height, which was the only reason I could think about what I'd done without her watching my face do whatever it was doing.

Three days out of Greyhollow, two days into passes no pilgrim had walked in a generation and no smuggler admitted to knowing unless they were drunk enough to be deniable. Wren had the shrine's mark on her collarbone — a spiral of pale scarring like frost ferns — and every hour she slept with it uncovered, I expected it to start glowing like a beacon for the Ashen Court. It didn't. It just sat there, quiet as my own oath-mark, and that was somehow worse.

Because mine wasn't answering anymore. Hadn't answered in weeks. Ten years of a cold silver knot on my wrist that warmed faintly when I paid my respects at the forgotten shrines — ten years of *he's there, he's listening, he just doesn't speak* — and now nothing. Dead weight on my pulse. I'd told myself it was the mountain shifting, the season, my imagination. I'd gotten very good at telling myself things.

"You're making that face again," Wren said, without opening her eyes.

"I don't have faces. I have one face and it's professionally neutral."

"It goes all pinched. Like you bit something."

"Eat your oats."

She ate her oats. Nine years old, orphaned by a miracle, and already better company than most mercenaries I'd worked with. The Court had burned her whole village for the crime of having one prayer answered too loudly, and she hadn't cried once in front of me, which I understood completely and forgave entirely.

We broke camp before dawn and climbed. The deep passes were old roads wearing new disguises — smuggler switchbacks over pilgrim stairs over something older still, stones cut in patterns nobody living had an explanation for. My feet knew this country even when my head didn't. That's the thing about ten years in one mountain range: eventually the mountain gets into your blood and starts giving orders.

By midmorning we reached the high col where the trail split, and the cairn was waiting for us.

I say *waiting*. Cairns don't wait. This one was a heap of weathered stones taller than me, stacked at the col's apex the way travelers stack them — a marker, a wish, a habit. Except there were no travelers up here. Except the stones were arranged in a spiral, and I had just spent three days looking at a spiral scar on a child's collarbone.

"Oscar Reyes," said the cairn.

I had my knife out before my brain caught up, which is the correct order of operations. "Wren. Behind me."

She went behind me. Good child. Trained fast.

"Oscar Reyes," the cairn said again, and the voice came from everywhere — from the stones, from the scree slope, from under my boots — a voice like gravel poured slowly into a wooden bucket, patient and endless. "Vael's key. Mark-bearer. Oath-sworn, oath-bound, oath-*forgotten*." A sound that might have been laughter if rockslides laughed. "You walk past my door for ten years and never once say hello."

"Talking piles of rocks aren't usually chatty," I said. My mouth does that when my spine is cold. "Who are you?"

"A doorkeeper. A boundary-thing. Once I had a name with nine syllables and a festival day and maidens who danced around me in spring." The stones shifted, settled, shifted again — not falling, *breathing*. "Now I am mostly weather and memory, waking up out of order. Forgive the mess. It has been—" a long grinding pause, like the mountain doing arithmetic— "four hundred years? Six? Time pools strangely when no one pours it."

Wren peered around my arm. "Is it a god?"

"It's a problem," I said.

"I'm right here," the cairn said, mildly wounded. "And yes, little marked one. Minor. Very minor. The kind of god that gets demoted to superstition and then to scenery." The topmost stone tilted toward me, which I realized with a lurch was its version of looking at me. "But I remember what the major ones would rather stayed buried. And you, Oscar Reyes, are carrying his signature on your wrist like a brand on livestock."

My wrist, sensibly, chose that moment to burn.

Not warm. *Burn.* The dead silver knot flared white-hot under my sleeve and I hissed through my teeth and shoved my cuff back and there it was — the oath-mark, awake for the first time in months, glowing faintly gold, and the spiral on Wren's collarbone glowed back at it like two lanterns signaling across a dark valley.

"No," I said.

"Yes," said the cairn.

"No. He's gone. He's been gone. The Silent God died in the old war or faded or — that's the whole point of him. That's why the oaths are cheap and the shrines are cold. Everyone knows Vael is dead."

"Everyone knows," the cairn agreed, gravel-slow, almost gentle. "That was the design. You do not tell the flock the shepherd is chained in the barn. You tell them he wandered off, and you let grief do the rest."

The wind came down the col and it was very cold and I stood there holding my own wrist like I'd sprained it, and somewhere underneath the fear something older and uglier started to climb.

"Explain," I said. "Slowly. As if I'm stupid. Because right now I need to be sure."

The stones rearranged themselves — a slow tectonic shrug. "Before your grandmothers' grandmothers, the gods warred. You know this. Every hearth-tale knows this. What the tales do not know is how it ended. Not with victory. With a *truce*. The pantheon could not kill their brother Vael — death was never on offer for the likes of us — so they did worse. They bound him into the bones of this range. Made the mountain his cell and his silence the lock. Every prayer whispered to him afterward fell into that binding and fed it. His faithfulness became his chains' maintenance fund." The topstone rocked, a slow grim nod. "Ten years ago you swore to a god you believed alive and silent. Your oath went where all his worship goes. Into the walls of his prison. You have been paying his rent, mercenary, and calling it faith."

I want to tell you that I said something cutting and clever. What I actually said was nothing, because my throat had closed, and inside my skull a decade of small devotions was being refiled, one by one, into evidence.

Ten years of leaving the first bite of every meal on a flat stone. Ten years of walking the far edge of camps because he liked the horizon, didn't he, always had. Ten years of talking to the mark on bad nights — *I know you're busy, I know you don't answer, I'll keep the road anyway* — and believing, needing to believe, that the silence on the other end was choice. Character. A god of few words.

Not a gag.

"He didn't abandon us," Wren said quietly. She wasn't asking me. She was working it out aloud, the way children do, merciless. "He couldn't answer. He was *locked up*."

"He let us think he left," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and strange. "That's different."

"Did he?" the cairn asked. "Or did the victors write the story? Bound gods make poor editors, Oscar Reyes."

The fury arrived then, all at once, the way it does — not hot but *load-bearing*, rising up through my boots from the mountain itself. Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape: I had spent ten years grieving faithfully. Quietly. Like a professional. And the whole time the thing I mourned was not dead but shackled under my own feet, hearing every word, unable to say *here, here, I'm here, I never left you.* Every cold shrine I'd respected. Every silence I'd defended to younger mercenaries who asked why bother with a dead god. *Why bother,* they'd ask, and I'd say *because he earned it*, and he had, he'd earned it bleeding in a cage while his brothers and sisters spent his worship like stolen coin.

"You're saying he can wake," I said.

The cairn went very still. Stillness, from a pile of rocks, is a loud thing.

"I'm saying the lock is old," it said at last, "and the key is standing in front of me wearing it on her wrist. I'm saying the shrines have started catching fire, girl, and prayers are getting answered by things that have been starving in the dark since before your people had a word for mercy. The binding is thinning. Whether anyone turns the key or not." A pause; gravel settling. "You are not being asked, mercenary. You are being *noticed*."

"By whom?"

"By everything that's waking up. By everything that remembers what the truce cost and who paid it." The topstone tipped toward Wren, and the child's scar flickered. "And by some, toward whom the question of the key is more urgent than the question of the lock. There are old appetites coming down the passes, Oscar Reyes. The Ashen Court smells it. That is why they burn the touched. They are trying to blow out sparks before they learn what's smoldering."

"The Court hunts the marked," I said slowly.

"The Court hunts *evidence*," the cairn corrected. "And you are carrying the largest piece of it in the mountains."

Wren's small hand found mine. I didn't remember telling her to take it. I looked down at her — orphaned, marked, asleep on her feet with terror and still asking questions — and then at my own wrist, gold-lit and burning like a lit fuse.

Here is what ten years in the Thornfell teaches you: the mountain does not care what you planned. It only cares whether you can keep walking.

"Which way," I said to the cairn, "is the prison?"

The stones ground against each other. If rock could hesitate, it hesitated.

"West," it said finally. "Up. Past the Weeping Stair, past where the maps go honest about lying." The topstone dipped, almost a bow. "But understand, key-bearer — the mountain will not show that road to someone undecided. It will test you. It tested me, once, and look at me now." Gravel laughter, hollow and vast. "Go home. Bury the mark. Live."

"I've tried that," I said. "For ten years. Turns out I was guarding a cell door for free and calling it devotion."

I turned west. Wren turned with me.

"One more thing, Oscar Reyes," the cairn called after us, and the voice dropped low, low enough that I felt it in my teeth. "When you reach him — and you will, the mark sees to that — do not assume the god in the chains is the god from the stories. Six hundred years is long enough to change what anyone loves. Or what they'd burn to be free."

Behind us, the stones settled into ordinary rubble, a plain marker at a empty col, scenery again.

West, the trail climbed into cloud. My wrist pulsed like a second heartbeat, keeping time with something far above the snowline, and for the first time in ten years, the silence ahead of us didn't feel empty.

It felt like a held breath.

* * *

We made the Weeping Stair by dusk — a cliff-face of seeps frozen into a hundred gray icicle columns, steps cut so anciently the mountain had half-healed over them. Wren climbed well. Better than well. Where her bare hand touched the stone, the frost melted, just slightly, just enough for grip.

She noticed me noticing.

"It's been doing that," she said, defensive. "Since the shrine."

"I know," I said, and took her hand anyway, and we kept climbing — up toward the peak, the prison, and whatever six hundred years had made of the god I'd sworn to.
