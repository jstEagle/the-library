## 4. The Price of the Mark

The thing about oaths is that nobody tells you the interest rate.

I was twenty-two when I signed mine, and I signed it the way you sign anything at twenty-two: fast, bleeding, and certain the bill would never come due. The Saltmarsh Rebellion had been eating soldiers for eight months by then, and my squad — nine of us, all that remained of a company of sixty — was cornered in the drainage tunnels under Fort Marrow with the loyalists burning pitch above the grates and no way out but down, into the flooded dark where even the rebels wouldn't go.

That's where I found the shrine. Or maybe it found me. It doesn't matter now which way it happened; the result is the same either way, and the result has been sitting on my wrist for ten years like a debt I stopped reading the terms of.

* * *

Ten years ago. Water to my thighs, black and cold as a liar's promise. Torches drowned. Nine of us breathing in the dark, listening to the fire crackle overhead through the grates, and Corporal Hesk crying without any sound, which was somehow worse than if he'd been loud about it.

"Reyes," Sergeant Ovid said. "You're the one who knows the old stories. Your grandmother's people were marsh-folk."

"My grandmother sold fish," I said.

"Your grandmother put out milk for something on the solstice nights. Don't tell me you didn't see her do it."

I had seen her do it. I'd also seen her spit over her shoulder every time she passed the standing stone outside our village, which should have told me everything I needed to know about how marsh-folk felt about the old gods: you fed them, and you didn't trust them, and you certainly didn't *pray* to them, because prayer was an invitation and invitations get accepted.

But there was a niche in the tunnel wall. That's what I keep coming back to, ten years later, lying awake in whatever barn or ditch the road gave me that night. There was a niche in the wall, and in the niche there was a figure carved from black stone, worn smooth, featureless except for the suggestion of a mouth — closed. Not a speaking god. A silent one. Vael, though I didn't know his name then; I knew him only as the Quiet One, the god my grandmother fed and feared, the one the other gods supposedly killed before the world got organized.

"There's nothing down here but rats," said Tam, the youngest of us. Seventeen. He still had a sweetheart's ribbon tied around his wrist. "Rats don't help."

"Something helped build this fort," Ovid said. "Somebody worshipped something once. Reyes. Try it."

And here is the part I have spent a decade not thinking about, the part that surfaces now like a drowned body when the thaw comes: I didn't try it because I believed. I tried it because I was scared, and scared people make bargains, and I wanted someone else to hold the fear. That's all a god is, maybe. A place to put what you can't carry.

I knelt in the floodwater. I pressed my palm against the black stone. And I said — I remember the exact words, I will always remember the exact words —

*"If you can hear me, take the price I owe instead of theirs. Let them walk out. Take it from me."*

Clever, right? Noble-sounding. What I meant, what I truly meant down in the marrow of me, was: *take anything but them.* I thought I was being generous. I thought I was offering myself up like a saint in a ballad.

What I actually offered was open-ended. No terms. No ceiling. A blank contract with my name on it, and something in the dark read every word.

The water went still. Not calm — *still*, the way a room goes still when someone important walks in. The torches that weren't lit flickered anyway. And cold spread up my arm from the stone, cold like a signature being written, and when I pulled my hand back there was a mark on my wrist: a closed mouth, a line beneath it like a sealed ledger.

Nobody spoke. Then the far end of the tunnel — the end that had been collapsed since before the rebellion started, the end we'd been told was solid rock — groaned, and cracked, and opened onto starlight and the salt flats beyond the fort.

Nine of us walked toward it.

Three walked out.

* * *

I've told myself the story a thousand ways. Ambush at the tunnel mouth. Loyalist skirmishers on the flats. Bad luck, bad ground, the ordinary arithmetic of war. Three survivors out of nine was better odds than most of the rebellion offered, and I took my three — me, Ovid, and Tam with his ribbon — and I marched them to the coast and mustered out and never looked back, because looking back is how you drown in a place like the Saltmarsh.

But I never asked why. That's the wound under the wound. In ten years I never once asked why the god who took my bargain took so much more than he needed. If the deal was *their lives for my price*, why did six of them die between the tunnel and the tide line? Why did Hesk — who wasn't even with us anymore, who'd been left behind at the fort — die screaming two days later when the walls fell? Why did the mark stay quiet and cold and useless, a dead man's seal on a living wrist?

Because I already knew the answer. I just kept it locked in the same box as everything else I couldn't afford.

The oath wasn't *spare them*. The oath was *take the price I owe instead of theirs* — and the law of the old gods, the law my grandmother spat over her shoulder about, is that a price is a price. It doesn't care whose name is attached. Vael hadn't failed me. Vael had taken exactly what I offered: an unpriced debt, collectible whenever and however the holder pleased. Six lives at the tunnel mouth might have been the first installment. Ten years of silence might have been the interest accruing.

Or — and this thought arrives some nights like a knife slipped under a door — maybe he'd taken nothing yet. Maybe the whole debt was still outstanding, waiting, growing, and the silence wasn't abandonment at all.

Maybe it was patience.

* * *

Present day. Wren sleeps curled against the cairn-god's warm stone like a kitten against a hearth, and I sit up watching the pass, because sleeping is for people whose pasts stay buried.

The mark wakes at midnight.

Not burns — not at first. First it *itches*, deep under the skin, the way a healed break aches before weather. Then the itch becomes heat, and the heat becomes the closed-mouth sigil glowing faintly silver-white against my wrist, and I know — the way you know a storm's coming by the smell of the air — that something enormous has turned over in its sleep beneath the Thornfells.

"Stop," I whisper to it. To him. To whatever's listening after ten years of nothing. "Whatever you're doing, stop. We had a deal. You stayed quiet, I stayed ignorant, everybody lived."

The mark pulses. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat finding its rhythm.

And then the memories start going soft at the edges.

It's small things first. My grandmother's face — I reach for it and find the shape of her, the shawl, the spitting at the standing stone, but the eyes are smudged, like a thumb dragged across wet ink. Tam's laugh. Gone, or going — I know he laughed, I know it the way you know a fact from a book, but the sound itself is draining away like water through marsh mud. Ovid teaching me to read a trail. Fading. Fading.

"No." I'm on my feet. "No, no, no — those aren't yours. Those are *mine*. You don't get — "

The mark flares white-hot and I go to one knee in the snow, teeth gritted, and in the flare I feel him for the first time in ten years. Not words. Not a voice. Just *attention*, vast and weary and terribly awake, turning toward me the way a prisoner turns toward the one crack of light in the wall. And underneath the attention, threaded through it like wire through wax:

Hunger. Patient, ancient, and owed.

He's feeding. On the past. On the only currency I ever actually put on the table — because that's what I offered in the dark under Fort Marrow, isn't it? Not my blood, not my years. *Everything I am made of.* The blank contract. The unpriced debt. And now the holder has woken up, and he's come to collect, one memory at a time, starting with the ones I love best, because the old law takes the truest coin first.

Wren stirs against the cairn. "Oscar?" Small voice, sleep-thick. "You're glowing."

"I'm fine." I wrap my hand around my wrist, as if pressure could hold a ledger shut. "Go back to sleep."

She does, trusting, and I hate how easily she trusts me, and I hold on to that feeling too — the hating — because it's mine, and I am going to memorize what's left while it's still mine to memorize.

Marisol's face. Still there. For now. The squad. Slipping. My grandmother's eyes: gone entirely, a smooth blank where a life used to be.

Somewhere below us in the passes, I swear I can feel it — a tremor too deep for the ear, a mountain shifting its weight. And with it, faint as a rumor, the sense of something else moving too. Boots. Many. Coming up the roads behind us with purpose and silver badges.

The Ashen Court always did follow the burn marks.

I look at my wrist one last time — the sealed mouth, glowing now steady as a lamp — and I understand, finally, with perfect clarity, what I understood at twenty-two and refused to learn since:

The bill has come due. And something is coming to collect it in person.
