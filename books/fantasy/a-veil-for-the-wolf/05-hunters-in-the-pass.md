## 5. Hunters in the Pass

The waystation at Cold Sarrow had a roof, a hearth, and a door that locked from the inside, which put it two rungs above anywhere else I'd slept that month. The stove drew well. The timber walls had been cut from old-growth pine and still bled resin when the fire touched them, so the whole place smelled like a chapel having a fever dream.

Wren was asleep in the loft, curled under both my blankets because children radiate heat like small dishonest stoves and will claim they need all of them anyway. I sat by the window with my boots off — a luxury I'd regret in about four hours, I figured, but you take these things on credit — and watched the snow come down over the pass like the sky had finally decided to settle its accounts.

Three days out of the cairn's valley. Two more to the high country, if the weather held, which it wouldn't. Wren hadn't spoken much since the talking pile of stones called her *little anchor* and called me something worse. Kids are resilient the way green wood is resilient — they bend, and bend, and then one day you hear the crack and realize it was bending wrong the whole time. I'd been keeping her fed, kept her moving, kept my answers short. It was working about as well as anything works.

My wrist itched.

I'd been telling myself for a decade that the mark was just a scar with pretensions — Vael's sigil, a closed eye inside a broken circle, inked into me the night I swore my oath and three of my squad walked out of the Saltmarsh alive. It had never answered. Not once. Prayers slid off it like rain off wax. That was the deal. That was *fine*. A god who doesn't answer is a god who doesn't ask, and I'd built ten quiet years on that foundation.

Now it itched like a healing wound, and the cairn's voice was still rattling around my skull: *Vael's key. He was not dead, girl. He was bound.*

I was still turning that over, looking for the angle where it wasn't a lie I'd been living inside, when I heard the dogs.

Not dogs, exactly. Something that had agreed to sound like dogs. A low, harmonic baying from the treeline, too regular, like music played by something that had read about music. I had my boots on before I finished deciding to put them on.

Through the frost-furred glass: lantern light in the trees. Six points of it, moving in formation, which no pilgrim band or smuggler crew ever does. Smugglers move like spilled water. Soldiers move like a fist. These moved like a *sentence being diagrammed*.

Ashen Court.

I climbed the loft ladder and shook Wren's shoulder. Her eyes opened all the way, instantly, no child's slow swim to the surface, and I hated that. Ten years old and she woke like a soldier.

"Back wall," I whispered. "Behind the salt barrels. Don't come out unless it's my voice saying 'the fish are early.' Say it back."

"The fish are early," she said, and went.

I got the stove lid off and raked most of the coals into the ash box, then thought better of it and dumped the whole thing across the floorboards by the door. Fire buys time. Time buys exits. I pulled my coat on, checked my knives — hip, boot, the one sewn into the collar that Marisol's people swore I'd never need, and look at me now, Marisol — and sat down at the table like a woman waiting out weather.

Because here's the arithmetic I'd done while lacing my boots: six hunters, snow to the horses' hocks, one door, one shuttered window, and a child behind the salt barrels. Running wasn't off the table, but running through waist-deep snow at night with hunters behind me meant dying tired. Better to let them spend their courtesy first. The Court loved courtesy. It's cheaper than fighting and looks better in reports.

The knocking came at three minutes to midnight. Three measured blows, evenly spaced, like the door owed them money.

"It's open," I said, "if you're lost."

The door opened anyway. Of course it did. Courtesy is for people who believe other people keep their word.

She came in last, which told me she was in charge. Tall, gray-cloaked, ash-pale hair cut short as a convict's, with a face like a ledger — everything recorded, nothing forgiven. She took off her gloves finger by finger, crossed the room, and sat down across from me without being asked, setting a thin iron rod on the table between us. It was humming. Very quietly. Pointed at my wrist like a compass needle.

"Oscar Reyes," she said. "Sworn of Vael. Marked at the Saltmarsh Rebellion, year of the Ash Fall. Nine oaths given, three survived." She smiled without warmth, the way a knife smiles. "You've been difficult to find. Your god makes you hard to see."

"My god doesn't do anything," I said. "That's sort of his whole personality."

"Was." She folded her hands. "I'm Serah Vane. Chief hunter of the Ashen Court, First Chair. And I'm going to offer you the only clean deal you'll get this winter, so I'd listen."

Outside, her people were spreading around the building. I could hear them — boots in snow, unhurried, positioned. She wanted me to know the exits were already priced in. Fine. I poured myself the waystation's terrible wine and waited. If she'd wanted me dead, we'd have skipped the theater.

"The silence is ending," Vane said. "You know it. You've felt the mark wake up. The shrine at Greyhollow road, the cairn in the deep pass — every week another minor thing rolls over in its sleep and some farmer's prayer gets *answered*, and answered prayers kill people, Reyes. You saw it. You carried the survivor out."

I didn't confirm anything. My face has been interrogated by better than her, though not many better.

"The Court exists to keep the old gods down," she went on. "Not out of cruelty. Out of arithmetic. Every god that wakes needs fuel. Worship. Blood. Anchors." Her eyes flicked upward, toward the loft, and my hand moved to my knife before I could decide whether that was a mistake. "Yes. We know about the girl. We've known since the shrine."

"She's a child."

"She's a *fuse*," Vane said, flatly. "And so are you. That mark on your wrist isn't decoration. It's a lock mechanism, and your god is turning it from the inside. When Vael wakes fully, everyone sworn to him becomes his hands. His soldiers. His *price*. The Court can erase your mark — unswear you, burn the oath out clean, before he finishes waking. You walk away human, unmortgaged, free. In exchange—"

"Wren. And the cairn's location."

"And whatever you know about what's waking you. Yes."

The wine tasted like vinegar that had been disappointed in itself. I set the cup down.

"Let me tell you what I know," I said. "Ten years ago I swore an oath because I thought it would save people, and it didn't save enough of them, and nobody ever told me why. I spent a decade believing my god abandoned me, and it turns out he was chained up and my faith was just — background noise to him. Or a leash on *me*. Either way, I've been lied to by a god, and now you're sitting there offering to lie to me too, just with better paperwork."

Vane's expression didn't change. "Meaning?"

"Meaning 'erase your mark' is a lovely phrase for someone who's never had a piece of their soul carved on." I stood, slowly, and pushed back the chair with my legs. "And meaning I don't hand children to people who execute miracle survivors in town squares. I saw Greyhollow's answer to answered prayer. You hung a cooper from his own rafters because his wife's fever broke."

"The fever broke because something *fed* on the street to break it," Vane said, and for one moment her ledger-face cracked and showed me something underneath that looked almost like grief. "Everything the old gods touch, they touch at interest, Reyes. We're not the monsters. We're the collection agency standing between you and the invoice."

"Funny," I said. "The invoice always seems to be somebody else."

She sighed, and reached for the iron rod, and that was the signal — outside, the boots started moving toward the door.

So I threw the ash box.

Not at her. At the hearth. Coals I'd saved went up in a spray of sparks and half-burnt pine needles, and the room filled with smoke and the sweet reek of catching resin, and Vane — professional that she was — didn't even flinch, just came up out of her chair with the rod blazing white in her fist, and I was already moving, because the whole point of lighting my own shelter on fire is that it makes people prioritize badly.

Her first swing caught my shoulder instead of my chest, and the rod bit like frost and burning together — the mark on my wrist *screamed*, actually screamed, a sensation I don't have good vocabulary for and hope never to improve — and I went through the shuttered window instead of arguing about it.

Snow. Waist-deep, glorious, freezing snow. Behind me the waystation burned orange against the dark, and I could hear Vane shouting orders — *alive, I want them alive* — and two of her people crashing through the drifts toward me, and I did the only thing the mountain had left to teach me: I went uphill, into the rocks, where the snow lies deep but the stone runs shallow, and I ran the way smugglers run, ground none of her flatland hunters could follow in the dark with the wind rising.

I heard one of them go down hard somewhere behind me — snow hides holes the way kindness hides debts — and after that the pursuit got careful, and careful pursuers lose runners.

I circled wide, came back along the streambed, and dug Wren out through the back wall gap where the salt barrels had rotted through, and she came to me without a word, eyes huge, and said only, "The fish are early," which nearly broke me, right there, in the smoking snow.

We went up. Higher than I wanted, higher than was sane, because the only thing the Court fears more than waking gods is altitude and bad weather, and I could give them both.

Somewhere behind us the waystation fell in, throwing sparks at the sky like the mountain applauding.

* * *

Serah Vane stood in the falling snow and let it land on her bare hands until they ached.

The waystation was a black skeleton now. Two of her hunters were limping; one had a broken ankle and a broken pride; and the marked woman and the anchor-child had vanished into the high passes as completely as if the mountain had picked them up and swallowed them.

Which, Vane thought, it may have done. That was rather the problem.

"First Chair?" The young hunter, Corrin, breathless, holding out the recovered iron rod. Its needle swung and swung, unable to settle, pointing now northeast, now straight up. "The resonance is worsening. Whatever's bound under Thornfell — it's turning faster."

"I know," Vane said.

She looked at her hands. Ash-pale, like her hair, like her name. Thirty years in the Court, and she had burned the oath-marks off forty-one sworn servants of the old gods, and each one had thanked her afterward, weeping, relieved, freed. She had believed, once, entirely, that hers was a mercy's work.

But Reyes had looked at her across that table the way people look at tax collectors and plague doctors, and Vane had heard herself making the old arguments and recognized, with cold clarity, that she no longer believed a single word of them. The Court didn't stand between mortals and the invoice. The Court *was* the invoice. They executed fever survivors and called it surgery, and somewhere in thirty years of surgery she had stopped checking whether the patient had needed cutting at all.

It changed nothing, of course. She had her orders, and orders were the only prayer the Court permitted.

"She knows the prison," Corrin said. "The cairn spoke to her. If she reaches the peak—"

"Then we reach it first." Vane pulled her gloves back on, finger by finger. "Send riders to Greyhollow. Double bounty, name public: Oscar Reyes, harboring an anchor. And send word to the border networks." She paused, and something almost like distaste crossed her face. "Especially the Marisol lines. Smugglers sell anything, given a price and a grudge. I want that woman's every road watched, every safehouse turned, every old friend counting coins."

"And if the smugglers find her first?"

"Then we buy." Vane turned north, toward the peak, where the storm was gathering like something clearing its throat. "The Court pays its debts, Corrin. That's the one thing we've never lied about."

Above them, far up in the dark, thunder rolled with no cloud to carry it — deep, rhythmic, patient. Like something enormous, turning over in a sleep that was almost done.
