## 6. The Smuggler's Ledger

There are three rules to surviving the Thornfell, and Marisol taught me all of them: never sleep in a shrine, never owe a god, and never trust a smuggler further than you can throw their ledger.

I'd broken the first two already this week. I was on my way to breaking the third.

Greyhollow at dusk looked the same as it always did — a town built like an apology, hunched between two ridgelines as if it hoped neither mountain would notice it. Smoke from the tanneries. Dogs in the alleys with opinions about everything. And over the door of the Salt Cellar, Marisol's mark: a fish hook crossed with a knife, freshly painted, which meant she was home and open for business and lying about both.

I left Wren tucked in the coal cellar behind the tanner's row — the one place in Greyhollow where even the Court's hunters didn't like to look, because the tanner paid everyone generously to forget it existed. The girl hadn't argued. She'd just looked at me with those too-old eyes and said, "You're going to see her, aren't you? The queen."

"Marisol's not a queen."

"That's what queens say."

Ten years old and already smarter than half my employers. I gave her my spare knife and told her not to use it unless she had to, which is the kind of advice that sounds wise until you're the one bleeding.

The Salt Cellar smelled of brine, lamp oil, and twenty years of bad decisions aging in barrels. Marisol held court at her usual table in the back, doing what she always did when I walked in: not looking up from her ledger until she'd finished the line she was writing, so I'd know exactly how important she was.

"Oscar Reyes," she said, dipping her pen. "Heard you were dead. Heard it twice, actually. Once from a pilgrim, once from a man who owed me money. Only believed the second one."

"I need passage. Up-mountain. Quiet. No names on the manifest."

Marisol finally looked up. She was sixty if she was a day, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had priced every soul in three valleys, including mine. "Up-mountain." She said it the way other people said *leper colony*. "To the peak."

"You've heard something."

"I hear everything. That's the job." She closed the ledger slowly. "What I want to know is why a mercenary who spent ten years being nobody suddenly wants to stand on top of the world where everybody can see her."

"Family business," I said, and we both let that lie sit there, because Marisol knew I had no family and I knew she knew, and that mutual understanding was the closest thing to love either of us could afford.

She studied me for a long moment. Long enough that I noticed things I should have noticed sooner. Two of her boys by the door wearing mail under their coats. The back exit chained. A lamp trimmed low enough to hide faces, high enough to show hands — hers steady on the table, mine apparently expected to be otherwise.

"Passage will cost you," she said.

"Name it."

"The mark on your wrist."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere behind me, a chair scraped as one of the mail-wearing boys shifted his weight.

"Marisol," I said carefully, "that's not yours to take."

"No," she agreed. "But the Ashen Court thinks it is. They offered a great deal of gold for exactly that wrist, attached or detached, and I have thirty mouths fed off this network and a winter coming." She folded her hands. "You were worth more to me alive, Oscar. Right up until you decided to become interesting."

The cold that went through me wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. "How long have they known?"

"A hunter came through nine days ago. Vane. Sharp woman. Paid sharp prices." Marisol's voice didn't change at all, which was how I knew this cost her something, somewhere, in some room of herself she kept locked. "You've been sold since before you knocked on my door. This meeting is a courtesy. Mine, not theirs."

Nine days. Nine days of walking toward Greyhollow thinking *safe harbor* while she counted my price into her ledger. And the worst part — the part I'd be chewing on for miles — was that I understood. That was the bitter clarity of it, clean as cut glass: Marisol had lost people to the old gods' games. Everyone in the Saltmarsh had. The rebellion, the reprisals, the shrines running red while the pantheon played out its quarrels through mortal blood. Of course she wanted them buried forever. Of course she'd sell the one person who could dig them up.

I'd have done the same. Probably. If I were someone who could still do math without flinching.

"There's a child," I said. "Marked. The Court wants her dead."

"I know."

"They'll burn her."

"I know that too." For one flickering moment something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or its fossil record. Then it was gone, priced and shelved. "The world burns children every day, Oscar. I just decide whether mine get warm."

I stood up slowly, keeping my hands visible. Ten years of neutrality, and here was the sum total of what it bought me: a fair warning before the knife.

"Last chance," Marisol said quietly. "Walk out the front. Give Vane your wrist. Live fat on Court gold and forget you ever heard a god snore. It's not too late to be nobody again."

"That offer expired ten years ago," I said. "In the Saltmarsh."

Her jaw tightened. First honest thing on the table all night.

"So it did," she murmured, and snapped her fingers.

* * *

The fight lasted eleven seconds because I cheated immediately, which is the only way to survive fights with people who outnumber you.

Lamp first — kicked into the nearest barrel of lamp oil, which I'm fairly sure Marisol stocked specifically for betraying guests. While her boys were busy not burning to death, I went through the window instead of the door, because doors are where knives live. Glass, gutter, cobbles, run.

They came after me. Of course they came after me. But Greyhollow was my town before it was ever hers — ten years of running cargo through it buys you a map in your feet. Down Tanner's Row, through the gap between the smokehouse and the wall that only existed because someone's foundation settled crooked in the year I was learning to walk, into the coal cellar dark.

Wren was awake. Of course she was awake. She had the knife out and pointed at the darkness, and when she saw it was me she lowered it maybe four inches.

"You're bleeding."

"It's mostly theirs."

"Is she coming?" Wren asked. Not Marisol. She meant the hunter. Word travels fast among the marked; they can smell the hunt coming the way horses smell weather.

"Everyone's coming," I said, shouldering my pack. "We're leaving through the mines."

Wren's face did something complicated. "The flooded roads?"

"Flooded roads."

"They're underwater."

"Ankle-deep underwater," I corrected. "Mostly. There's a section past the third junction that's waist-deep, but it's short, and the water's snowmelt, so think of it as bracing."

"You're terrible at making things sound better."

"It's a skill I never needed. Come on."

* * *

The old mine-roads ran under the western ridge like veins under skin — dug in the silver boom, abandoned when the silver ran out, repurposed by smugglers when taxes went up, and flooded ever since the spring the mountain shrugged and rearranged its plumbing. Officially, nothing lived down there. Unofficially, everything lived down there, just none of it friendly.

We went in through the ventilation shaft behind the tannery, which meant a rope descent in the dark with a ten-year-old breathing hard beside me and torchlight — real torchlight, hunting torchlight — already flickering at the mouth of Tanner's Row above us. Vane moved fast. I'd known that since the waystation. Knowing it didn't make it better.

"Oscar." Wren's voice, small in the black. "Can I ask you something?"

"Now's fine."

"When we get to the peak. To him. What happens to me?"

I kept my voice level, which took work, because I'd been asking myself the same question since the cairn-god called her *anchor* and I'd been pretending I hadn't. "You stay behind me. That's what happens."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that's true."

She was quiet for a few steps. Water sloshed around our boots, ankle-deep, cold enough to make itself known. Then: "Marisol sold you. Are you going to sell me?"

The question hit cleaner than any of Vane's blades had. I stopped, turned, and crouched so we were eye to eye in the dark, which is a stupid thing to do tactically and the right thing to do anyway.

"Wren. Listen to me. Everything I've lost in the last week, I lost because I chose sides. You're my side now. That's the whole ledger."

She studied me with those old eyes, weighing it. Ten years old and pricing souls already — the Saltmarsh teaches its survivors young. "Okay," she said finally. "But if you ever put me in your ledger, tell me first."

"Deal."

We shook on it, grimy hand to grimy hand, and somewhere far above us the hunting torches found the ventilation shaft and the shouting started, and I figured we had maybe six minutes before the Court's climbers came down the rope after us.

Six minutes through the third junction. Where the water got deep.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about flooded mines: the water isn't the problem. The water is honest — it's cold, it's wet, it wants to kill you, and it doesn't pretend otherwise. The problem is the ceiling. Because when you wade into a stretch where the roof drops to within a foot of the surface, you learn very quickly that drowning in the dark, pressed flat against wet stone with a pack dragging you down, is a specific and personal nightmare.

I sent the pack ahead, floated it like a corpse. Sent Wren next, made her breathe deep and pull herself along the guide-rope with her face in the air pocket where the ceiling arched up. She went without crying, which broke my heart a little and made me prouder than anything has in years.

Then I went last, because I'm the adult, allegedly, and adults go last.

Halfway through, the rope went slack.

Someone above had cut it — or untied it, which was worse, because cutting is panic and untying is patience, and patience meant Vane. The air pocket shrank around my face. My boots lost the bottom. For one long, glittering moment I hung in the black water, hearing my own pulse and nothing else, and I thought, with strange calm: *so this is how the key gets thrown away.*

Then something took hold of my wrist.

Not a hand. Something colder and older, something that gripped like bedrock, and through the touch poured a single sensation — vast, patient, irritated, the way a mountain might feel about a landslide it intended to survive anyway. My boot found stone that hadn't been there a moment ago. My head broke the surface in the arch of the ceiling, gasping, and I hauled myself the rest of the way on rock that rose to meet my hands like a floor deciding to be helpful.

On the far side, Wren pulled me out onto dry gravel, soaked, shaking, alive.

"What happened?" she whispered.

I looked back at the black water. Somewhere in it, my supplies were settling into the silt — food, rope, the spare blade, ten years of careful mercenary logistics, gone. All I had left was what I carried and what I was.

"I think," I said slowly, flexing my wrist where the oath-mark sat warm as a banked coal, "the prisoner just paid his first bribe."

Wren stared at me. "Gods don't bribe."

"This one owes me," I said, and started climbing. "And he knows it. Come on — the peak's another two days, and now we're doing it hungry, cold, and hunted."

Above us, faint through the mountain's throat, came the sound of Vane's torches finding the shaft entrance.

Below us, deep beneath the stone, something old turned over in its sleep — and, for the first time in centuries, dreamed of getting out.
