## 7. Prisoner of the Peak

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain about two-thirds of the way up.

I'd climbed Thornfell's spine since I was nineteen, and I'd never gone this high, because the smuggler roads all bent away from the summit like water around a hot stone. Marisol's maps — the ones I'd memorized before she burned them, the ones I still carried behind my eyes — showed the peak as a blank space with a single notation in her cramped hand: *Don't.* That was the whole instruction. Marisol, who annotated passes with toll schedules and garrison rotations, had looked at the tallest thing in the world and written one word.

Wren climbed behind me, small boots finding holds I pointed out, breath steaming in little ragged flags. She hadn't spoken in two days. Not since the mine-roads, not since I'd dragged her out of the flooded dark with Marisol's name in my mouth like a stone I couldn't spit out. She watched me the way you watch a fire that's already burned down one house.

"Almost there," I said, which was a lie, because I had no idea where *there* was. The mark on my wrist had gone from burning to something worse: a steady, patient heat, like a hand resting on me. It pulled. Not hard. The way a tide pulls — so gently you only notice when you're already past the point of swimming back.

The snow gave way to bare stone. Not bare, exactly. *Clean.* No lichen, no ice, no bird droppings, no ten thousand years of weather. The rock was grey and smooth and warm under my palms, and when I pressed my bare fingers to it, I felt — I want to say a heartbeat, but that's too small a word. It was more like standing on the chest of something enormous that was breathing very, very slowly, and had been for a very long time.

"Oscar." Wren's voice, small and cracked. "The rocks are singing."

They were. A hum, subsonic, felt in the teeth more than the ears. And under it, faintly, something that was almost a voice — not words, not yet, but the shape of words, the way you hear a conversation through a wall and know it's about you.

I should have turned around. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does: I knew. I'd known since the cairn-god called me *Vael's key*, since the mark started eating my memories of the Saltmarsh, since Marisol's knife came out of the dark wearing a smile. Every step up this mountain was a step I chose with my eyes open.

I just didn't know what the view from the top would cost.

* * *

The summit wasn't a summit. It was a room.

The peak opened like a throat — stone folding back and down into a hollow, and the hollow was circular, deliberate, carved by nothing human. In its center stood a tree. That was the wrong word too, but language fails you up here. It was black wood, leafless, and it grew *out of* the mountain the way a nail grows out of flesh, and its roots — I could see them, because the stone around them was translucent, like ice, like skin — its roots went down and down and *down*, branching through the whole mass of Thornfell, through every pass I'd ever walked, every road I'd ever kept open for coin.

Chains hung from it. Not metal. The chains were made of oath-script, glowing faint gold, thousands of links, each link a word in a language I didn't know and understood perfectly. And wrapped in the chains, fused into the trunk, was a man.

Or the shape of one. He was huge — not giant-huge, but wrong-huge, the way a wolf is huge when you meet one in a hallway. His skin was the grey of the mountain. His eyes were closed. His hair had gone white as the snowline, and it moved in a wind that touched nothing else.

And the mark on my wrist flared so bright I could see the bones of my hand through it.

"Hello, Oscar."

The voice came from everywhere. From the stone. From the tree. From inside my own skull, wearing my own timbre like a stolen coat. Ten years I'd prayed into that silence. Ten years of keeping my head down and my god's silence unexamined, and now the silence opened its mouth and it knew my name.

"You're smaller than I expected," I said. My voice came out steady. Ten years of mercenary work teaches you that much — the voice holds, even when everything behind it is on fire. "The cairn said you were bound. It didn't say you were *decorative*."

One eye opened. Grey, like the stone. Old, older than old. Tired in a way that made my decade of exhaustion look like a child's tantrum.

"The cairn," he said, "was a pebble that learned to talk. Forgive it its poetry. It called this a prison because pebbles understand stones. It is not a prison." His gaze moved to the roots, the chains, the translucent rock. "It is a marriage."

"I've had marriages. They came with chains too."

Something moved across his face. It might have been a smile. It might have been grief. With gods, I was learning, the two share a border.

"When the first war ended," Vael said, "the pantheon and the mortal oaths were bound into a single law. Peace is not the absence of appetite, Oscar. It is appetite, chained and fed on schedule. They fed it me." He flexed, and the mountain groaned, and I felt the groan in my back teeth. "I am the truce. My silence is its mortar. Every answered prayer since — every shrine that burned, every miracle that killed — is mortar cracking. I am waking, and I did not choose to wake. Your mark did that. The oath you swore at Saltmarsh. You have been pulling on the chain for ten years without knowing it was a chain."

The Saltmarsh. The smoke. Nine of us kneeling in the mud, swearing to the silent god because the loud ones were killing everything we loved, and me with my wrist out and my blood on the stone, buying survival at a price I never itemized.

"Three of us walked out," I said. "Nine swore. Three walked."

"Yes."

"You could have said more."

"I could say a great many things." His eye closed again. Opened. "That is the problem with gods, and the reason they bound me deepest of all. I do not lie, Oscar. I have never once lied. And the truth is that I do not know what happens if I stand. The truce unmakes, and the old gods wake hungry, and the mortals who swore to them — all of them, every oath-mark in the world — become either soldiers or fuel. I would like to tell you I am worth it. I am tired enough to believe it. Tired things always believe they are worth the trouble."

Wren made a small sound behind me. Vael's eye shifted, and the weight of his attention settled on her like snowfall.

"The child," he said. "The anchor. They made her to hold me down if I rise. You know this."

"I know they'll have to go through me."

"Yes," he said softly. "I know. That is what the mark is *for*, Oscar. That is why they let oaths be sworn at all. Not for love. For leverage. You were never my faithful one. You were my lock, and someone has been picking you from the inside for a year."

The mark burned, and with the burn went something — I reached for my mother's face and found a kitchen with no one in it. I reached for Marisol's laugh and found a woman-shaped absence. The god was eating me by inches, and he was right: I'd been feeding him since Saltmarsh, one swallowed memory at a time, and I'd told myself the gaps were just age, just drink, just the way hard years sand a person down.

"You could go back to sleep," I said. My voice cracked on the last word. So much for the steady mercenary voice. "Let them reseal it. Let the girl — " I couldn't finish it. *Let them kill Wren* sat in my throat like a bone.

"I could," Vael said. "And the peace would hold another thousand years, built on a child's blood and a lie about my death, and every prayer in the world would keep falling into the same silence that swallowed your ten years. Or I could rise, and the war comes, and I cannot promise you the mountains survive it. I cannot promise *you* survive it. I can only tell you the truth, which is the one currency I have never been permitted to spend."

"And if I say no? If I walk down the mountain and take my chances with the hunters?"

"Then I sleep, and they take the child, and the world gets its peace." He said it without flinching. That was the worst part. A savior would have begged. A monster would have lied. He just laid it out like a contract, because that's what he was — the oldest contract in the world, and I was the signature.

The wind came up the hollow, and it smelled like snow and iron and, underneath, like the Saltmarsh mud I could no longer quite remember. I thought of Wren's hand in mine in the flooded dark. I thought of Marisol's face when she sold me, and realized with a lurch that I could no longer see her face at all — only the fact of the betrayal, the shape of it, the wound without the woman.

The price was already being paid. The question was only whether I'd get anything for it.

"They're coming," Wren said suddenly, at the rim of the hollow. Her voice was flat and certain, the certainty of a marked child who could feel what I could only guess at. "A lot of them. From both sides. They're coming *now*."

Vael's eye opened fully for the first time, and the mountain shuddered, and far below — down in the passes, down where the snow still lay honest and white — I heard the first horns of the Ashen Court, and beneath them, older and stranger, the drums of the waking gods.

"Choose quickly, Oscar," my god said, tired and angry and honest, chained in the heart of his marriage. "They are coming to decide for you."

I rolled up my sleeve and looked at the mark — the key, the lock, the leash, the price tag — and put my hand on the black wood of the tree.

"Then let's give them something to argue with."
