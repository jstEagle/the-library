## 8. Court of Knives and Prayers

The gods arrived the way weather arrives — a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the light, and then everything at once.

I was halfway up the final switchback, Wren's small hand welded to mine, when the mountain started talking. Not the cairn-god's gravelly mutter, which I'd grown almost fond of, but a chorus: voices in the rock, in the wind, in the snow itself. Old voices. The kind that predate language and resent having to use it.

"They're coming," Wren said. She didn't sound scared. She sounded like me, ten years ago, right before a job went sideways. "All of them."

"How many is all?"

"More than they let the priests count."

Wonderful. I turned back down the trail and saw them coming, both armies of them, converging on Vael's prison like wolves on a wound.

From the south, along the pilgrim road, came the Ashen Court — grey cloaks, white banners, and Serah Vane riding point on a horse that had no business being calm this high up. Behind her, two hundred hunters with silvered blades and the particular serenity of people who believe their butchery is bookkeeping.

From the north, out of the smuggler roads *my* smuggler roads, the ones I'd run for a decade — came Marisol's people. Torches in daylight, which told me they weren't for seeing by. They were for burning something. Her face, when I picked it out among the lead riders, had the flat stillness of a woman who had already finished grieving and moved on to arithmetic.

And from everywhere else — from the stone and the sky and the deep places under the snowpack — the old gods came down to defend their truce.

The first one passed so close I felt it in my teeth. It wore a body the way a drunk wears someone else's coat: a stag's rack of antlers on a shape that might once have been a woman, frost blooming and dying across its skin with every heartbeat. Its eyes were river water. When it spoke, the words arrived a half-second after its mouth moved.

"The anchor," it said, looking not at me but at Wren. "It comes to the peak on its own feet. The truce provides."

"The truce," I said, "provides a child to slaughter. Yes. I've heard."

"The law does not flinch." A pause, and something almost like regret moved through the frost on its skin. "Neither should you, oath-bearer. You swore to the silence. The silence kept you."

"It didn't keep anyone. It was *kept*." I put myself between it and Wren, which any sane tactician would call suicide, and which felt, in the moment, like the only honest posture available. "Move."

It moved. That frightened me more than anything else that morning. Gods do not step aside for mercenaries unless the outcome is already decided somewhere they can see and we cannot.

* * *

Serah Vane had commanded executions in eleven border towns, suppressed three shrine-rebellions, and once burned a cathedral with the congregation locked inside because two of them had been touched by an answered prayer. She did not believe in hesitation. Hesitation was how gods got footholds.

So she did not hesitate now. She rode to the base of the peak, dismounted into knee-deep snow, and studied the mountain the way she studied warrants: for the flaw.

Her lieutenant, a young man named Aldric whose piety she had never quite managed to cure, shaded his eyes against the glare. "The smugglers are forming up below the western col. Reyes is above them, near the summit approach. And the—" he swallowed— "the others are already on the mountain."

"Others" was the Court's word for gods, spoken the way sailors say *storm*. Vane allowed herself three seconds of pure hatred. Not for the gods — hatred for them was a luxury, like grief. For the *arrangement*. Two thousand years of truce, bought with a god in a mountain and a knife forever poised over some poor child's throat, and everyone called it peace.

"The anchor child is with Reyes," she said.

"Yes, Commander. If the old ones reach the summit first, they'll perform the rite themselves. Reseal him. Another century, another generation of children—"

"I know what another century means." She had read the founding texts of the Court. Buried beneath the hymns to divine order was the plain accounting: the truce required a vessel, born marked, raised unwary, and bled into the stone whenever the prisoner stirred. The Court had been founded not to kill gods but to manage the invoice. Every hunter she had ever lost had died keeping books balanced for powers that never once said thank you.

She had stopped believing in the truce around the same year she stopped believing her father was coming home. What she believed in was simpler: debts must be paid by those who incurred them, or by no one. Let the gods pay their own bill.

"Aldric. Signal the advance. We go through the smugglers, through the old ones, through Reyes if we must. We take the peak before the rite begins."

"And the child?"

Vane watched the frost-god climbing the switchbacks far above, patient as winter.

"If the old law demands a life," she said, "it can have mine or none. That's the only warrant I'll honor today. Move."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting uphill through a battle between gods and men: the gods are worse shots.

The waking ones didn't fight like soldiers; they fought like arguments. One of them made the snowfall itself take sides, and suddenly half the Ashen lines couldn't see and half of Marisol's people were blind in the other direction, and the mountain's gravity developed opinions about who deserved to stand upright. I dragged Wren through gaps in the chaos, using ten years of smuggler knowledge — the handholds, the blind gullies, the places where the scree would carry you forty feet down if you trusted it — and all the while the mark on my wrist burned hotter, pulling toward the summit like a fish on a line.

"Oscar." Wren tugged my sleeve. "They're saying things about me."

"Who?"

"The gods. They keep calling me *anchor*. Like it's my name." Her chin came up, stubborn as her mother's must have been. "What's an anchor do?"

I should have lied to her. I'd been lying to myself for a decade; one more lie would've been effortless. But the mark was burning and the truth was already in my mouth, tasting of iron.

"It holds something in place," I said. "By sinking."

Wren considered this with the terrible economy of children. "Then somebody has to cut the rope."

Before I could answer, the western slope erupted. Marisol's line hit the Ashen vanguard, torches first — and I understood, with a lurch, what the fire was for. Not the hunters. The *mountain*. They meant to burn the shrine-paths, sever the god-roads, cut the peak off from every power that could reach it. Marisol wasn't here for me. She was here to cauterize the whole infected limb, gods and oaths and marked children included.

"Stay behind me," I told Wren, and went down the slope to meet the woman who raised me.

We met between the lines, in a bowl of churned snow lit orange by spreading fire. Marisol looked older than she had three days ago. Selling your soul apparently costs sleep.

"Mijo," she said quietly. "Walk away. Last time I'll ask."

"You sold me."

"I sold a problem." No apology in it. Just the ledger. "You know what the old gods took from me in the Saltmarsh. You know what your precious silent god cost. Now the Court offers a world where prayers stay dead and children stop burning shrines. I'm buying it. You're standing in the doorway."

"And Wren?"

Marisol's jaw tightened. For one heartbeat, ten years of stolen meals and forged papers and gruff, unspoken love crossed her face like weather. Then it cleared.

"The anchor pays the debt," she said. "That's not cruelty, Oscar. That's just the price, finally landing on the right head instead of mine."

"The price lands where I say it lands."

I don't remember deciding to move. I remember the moment Marisol's knife came out — slower than she could have drawn it, and I will hold onto that slowness until whatever's left of me dissolves — and I remember going past the blade, taking the cut along my forearm as tuition, and putting her down hard in the snow with my knee on her chest and my oath-mark blazing between us.

She could have fought. She didn't, quite. Which in Marisol's language was a sentence with all the punctuation removed.

"Go, then," she rasped. "Break the mountain open. But when the war starts, remember: I chose the peace that existed. You're choosing the one that doesn't."

Above us, the summit lit white.

* * *

Serah Vane reached the high col as the light broke over the peak, and for the first time in her career, she stopped giving orders and simply watched.

The old gods had formed a ring below the summit, and inside the ring stood the mercenary Oscar Reyes, bleeding from a dozen cuts, holding off an entire pantheon with nothing but a burning forearm and the absolute refusal to move. Beyond her, at the crown of the peak, the prison-light was failing. Something vast was leaning against the inside of the mountain, and the mountain, after two thousand years, was beginning to lean back.

"Commander," Aldric panted, arriving with the survivors of the vanguard. "The smugglers are broken. Do we press? The rite — if the gods reseal him now, with the anchor child—"

Vane raised a hand.

On the summit, the child had stepped out from behind the mercenary. Small, marked, unafraid in the way of things that have already understood their purpose and rejected it. The frost-god extended a hand of river-water and antler. The mercenary raised her blade against a being older than the word for blade.

Every faction on this mountain had justice on its side. That was the horror of it, Vane thought. The Court's order, the smugglers' grief, the gods' law — all of them, every single one, had concluded that the correct answer to two thousand years of war was one small girl's blood.

Only one person on the peak appeared to find this unacceptable.

"Commander?" Aldric prompted.

Serah Vane unslung her sword, turned to face her own god-hunters, and discovered she had absolutely no idea whose side she was about to be on.

Below them all, in the roots of the mountain, something ancient drew breath — and the first thing it said, in a voice like a door finally opening, was Oscar Reyes's name.
