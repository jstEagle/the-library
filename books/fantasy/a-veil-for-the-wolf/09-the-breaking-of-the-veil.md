## 9. The Breaking of the Veil

The mountain was screaming, and so was everyone on it.

I'd like to tell you I had a plan. Mercenaries always have plans — that's the whole sales pitch. *Give me your coin and I'll bring back a strategy, a body count, or at minimum an apology.* But standing on the summit of Thornfell's highest peak, with snow whipping sideways and two armies and a handful of waking gods tearing the sky open above me, my plan had narrowed to exactly one step: get to Wren before anyone else did.

Everyone else's plan was simpler. Kill the child, reseal the god. Or kill the child, keep the god buried forever. Or — this one came from Vane, who never met a cruelty she couldn't dress as policy — take the child alive, let the Court decide which gods got fed.

Wren stood at the center of it all, small and dark-haired and marked with the truce-glyph that had bloomed across her skin like frost ferns, and she wasn't crying. That was the worst part. She'd looked at me when the armies closed in, and she'd said, in the flat voice children use when they've already done their arithmetic: "I'm the price, aren't I."

"You're the person," I said. "Prices are for things."

Then Serah Vane's hunters hit the first line of smugglers, and there was no more time for philosophy.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching gods fight: it doesn't look like war. It looks like weather with opinions.

The cairn-god from the deep passes — Halfmad, half-stone, still muttering my name like a prayer it had gotten stuck on — rose out of the mountainside to meet the river-god coming down from the north, and where they touched, the snow didn't melt, it *remembered being ocean*. The Ashen Court's hunters fired bolts of consecrated ash that unmade whatever they struck — not killed, *unmade*, leaving holes in the world that healed slowly and wrong. Marisol's smugglers fought like people who had nothing left to lose, which, given what she'd learned about her family and the Saltmarsh, was close to literal.

And through all of it, under my feet, Vael pulled at his chains.

I could feel him the way you feel a headache behind your eyes. Not words. Pressure. Ten years of silence, and now the silence was full — packed edge to edge with everything he'd been swallowing since the first divine war ended and the other gods hammered him into the bedrock and called it peace.

*I'm sorry,* the pressure said. It was not an apology. It was a warning label.

"Yeah," I told the mountain. "Me too. Hold on."

I went through the battle the way water goes downhill — not bravely, not cleverly, just following the only path available. A hunter's bolt took the man beside me and left a hole shaped like a man in the air behind him; I stepped around the hole and kept moving. Someone swung at me with a blessed axe and I broke his wrist with the pommel of my knife and felt nothing about it, which later would be its own kind of grief, but grief is a luxury for people with time.

Marisol found me halfway up the last slope. Of course she did. She had a cut across her cheek and her coat was smoking and she pointed a pistol at my chest with the weary competence of a woman doing paperwork.

"Walk away, Oscar." Her voice cracked on my name. "Let them seal it. Let the child — "

"No."

"The old law needs a payment either way. You know that now. The cairn told you. If Vael wakes free, the law takes its price from whoever breaks the binding." Her hand shook, just barely. "It'll take everything you are."

"It'll take my memories," I said. "That's the rate. Memories of everyone I love. I did the reading."

"And you'd pay that? For him? For a god who left you praying into a dead line for ten years?"

"He didn't leave." And here was the thing I'd been carrying since the cairn spoke, heavy as ballast, and I finally set it down between us. "He was screaming the whole time, Mari. Every prayer I ever sent up into that silence — he heard every one and couldn't answer, because answering would've cracked the truce and started a war that would've burned the lowlands flat. He stayed quiet for us. All of us. Even the ones who chained him."

Marisol's jaw worked. Behind her, the sky split along a seam of gold where the storm-god arrived, fashionably late, to defend a peace built on a prisoner.

"I lost everyone in the Saltmarsh," she whispered.

"I know. I know you did. And I can't fix that, and neither can he, and neither can sealing him back in the dark." I reached out and pushed the pistol barrel gently down. "But I can make sure no one else gets sold to keep the lie comfortable. Starting with a nine-year-old girl."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she holstered the gun, turned, and put two shots into the nearest hunter's knee without even glancing at it.

"I never sold you out," she said, not looking at me. "I want that on the record. I sold your *location*. There's a difference, and if you survive this, you're allowed to be angry about exactly one of them."

"Noted," I said, and ran.

* * *

Wren was at the summit ring, where the truce-stones stood in their ancient circle, and Vane got there first.

Of course she did. Serah Vane had been three steps ahead of me since Greyhollow, and she moved through the chaos of the battle like it was furniture — stepping over dying men, around a god's falling finger, unhurried, certain, her ash-bolt rifle leveled at a child.

"Step back, Reyes." She didn't turn her head. "You've been a complication all season. Don't be a tragedy."

"She's a child."

"She's an *anchor*. Born marked, bred by the truce itself — do you think that glyph appeared by accident? The old law grew her the way it grows everything it needs. Seal Vael with her blood and we buy another thousand years of quiet. Quiet where prayers stay dead and shrines don't eat pilgrims and nobody's daughter gets chosen by frost-ferns." Now she glanced at me, and there was something almost human in her face, which frightened me more than the rifle did. "I don't enjoy this. I want you to know that. Enjoyment is how gods think."

"Vane." I raised my hands, empty, knife still sheathed. "Ask yourself one question. The truce needed an anchor child. Where do you think the first anchor came from? Before Wren. Before her mother. How many children has your precious quiet eaten, one per century, quietly, while everyone slept easy?"

Her finger tightened on the trigger. "That's not — "

"That's exactly what it is. You're not ending the sacrifice. You're *administering* it."

And that — that was the moment. Not the armies, not the gods, not the mountain groaning under a prisoner's weight. Just a woman who had spent her whole life believing she was the cure hearing, for one bare second, that she might be the disease.

She hesitated.

I dove.

We went down together behind the truce-stones, and her rifle discharged into the sky and opened a hole in a cloud that is, I am told, still there. We fought — she was better trained, I was angrier, and anger covers maybe a third of the gap but it covers it long enough. I got her wrist pinned. I got my knife at her throat. And then the mountain spoke through both of us.

Not Vael. The law itself. The old, old law underneath the gods, the one that prices everything. It rose up through the truce-stones like water finding a crack, and it showed me — all at once, no mercy in it — the shape of the transaction.

Break the binding. Free the prisoner. Pay the rate.

Memories of everyone I love. Taken whole. Forever.

And beneath that, cold as fine print: *the law accepts only what is offered freely.*

Nobody could make me pay. Nobody could trick me into paying. The one thing the old law had never accepted in all its centuries was a volunteer.

Which meant the entire war, the whole rotten architecture of anchors and sacrifices and sealed gods and murdered children, rested on the assumption that no one would ever *choose* it.

"Oscar." Wren was looking up at me, her marked hand pressed to the truce-stone, frost-ferns crawling past her elbow now. "What's happening?"

I knelt down in front of her. Around us the battle raged; above us the gods circled each other like wolves deciding whether the meat was worth the teeth; below us something vast held its breath.

"Listen carefully," I said. "In about a minute, I'm going to forget you. Completely. Your face, your name, the way you hum when you're scared — all of it goes into the lock, and the lock opens, and Vael walks free, and nobody ever has to bleed for this again."

Her eyes went wide. "You can't — "

"I can. That's the joke, kid. Ten years I kept my head down, kept my oaths clean, kept myself *uninvolved*. Turns out the whole system runs on people staying uninvolved. One volunteer breaks it." I smiled, and I'm fairly sure it was mostly a real one. "So I need you to do something for me. When it's done — when I look at you like a stranger — you remember for both of us. Can you carry that?"

Wren cried, then. Finally, blessedly, like a nine-year-old should. And then she wiped her face with her marked hand and nodded, once, the way soldiers nod.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm good at remembering things."

"I know you are."

I stood. I walked to the center of the truce-ring, where the binding ran thickest, where ten years of silence had pooled like sediment. I drew my knife and opened the oath-mark on my wrist — the mark that hadn't answered in a decade, the mark that had been a key the whole time — and I pressed my bleeding arm to the stone.

"Old law," I said, loud enough for gods. "I'm offering. Freely. Take the payment."

The mountain exhaled.

* * *

They say you should count backward from ten. I counted forward instead, through names.

"Marisol Vega," I said, and felt the warmth of her go — the smoky room, the ledger, the laugh she only used when she'd won. Gone. I said the name anyway, holding it up like a lantern with the candle burning out. "Thank you for raising me mean enough to survive."

"Tomas Reyes." My father's hands, flour-dusted. Gone.

"Captain Ilse Marchetti. Dario. Fen. Okonkwo. Petra. Sable." My squad. Nine sworn, three walked away, and now I finally understood why I'd never asked — some questions are load-bearing, and I was pulling them out one by one to feed the fire. Their faces went soft and bright and then they were photographs of faces, and then they were nothing, and I kept saying the names because the names were the last place they lived.

"Wren." Last. I saved her for last because I wanted one clean memory walking into the dark with me. The shrine-fire. Her hand in mine on the frozen road. *You're the person.* Gone — and I felt it leave like a tooth coming out, and I understood, in the final second I had a self to understand with, why the law priced it this high.

Love isn't the cost. Love is the currency. That's the whole secret of the old law, and I paid it gladly.

The binding shattered. Not loudly — that was the terrible part. Ten thousand years of prison ended with a sound like ice letting go of a pond, and Vael rose out of Thornfell wearing the mountain's shape and then, folding down and down, a man's shape: grey-eyed, tired, scarred like a quarry.

The other gods stopped fighting. They looked at him — free, awake, *furious* — and then at each other, doing arithmetic. A war against a freed god and every mortal who'd ever sworn to him, against a law that had just proven it accepted volunteers, against a world that had watched a woman pay everything and walk away standing.

The storm-god withdrew first. Then the river. Then the rest, folding back into their hills and waters like cards into a deck, and the Ashen Court's hunters lowered their weapons and discovered, collectively, that they had no doctrine for mercy.

Serah Vane stood in the ruin of the truce-ring, staring at me — at the stranger kneeling in the snow with a bleeding wrist and no idea why everyone was looking at her — and I am told her face did something complicated.

A man with grey eyes knelt in front of me. He looked tired and angry and honest, and he said, gently:

"Do you know your name?"

I searched. Skills, roads, knots, knife-work, a hundred passes mapped in muscle memory. And one word, sitting alone in all that cleared ground like a house after a flood.

"Oscar," I said. "Oscar Reyes. Who are you?"

He smiled, and it hurt him to do it, and he did it anyway.

"A friend who owes you everything," said Vael. "Walk with me, and I'll spend the rest of forever repaying it."

Behind him, a small dark-haired girl was crying and smiling at the same time, clutching something — someone — like she'd never let go.

I didn't know her. But I offered her my hand anyway.
