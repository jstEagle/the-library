## 10. A Veil for the Wolf

The trouble with forgetting your whole life is that nobody warns you about the small things. The big things, sure. You wake up one morning and the mountain is quiet and your head is quiet and there's a man sitting across the fire who says he's been waiting for you to remember his name, and that's a big thing. That's a thing with edges.

It's the small things that get you. The way my hands knew how to strip a crossbow bolt before I knew what a crossbow was for. The way I flinched at the smell of pitch smoke without knowing why. The way I reached for a name — *Marisol* — the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't there anymore, and go down hard into nothing.

I don't know who Marisol was. My hands know her anyway. Some mornings I catch myself braiding my hair the way someone else taught me, some dead woman's habit living on in my fingers like a tenant who won't leave.

"You're doing it again," Vael said from across the fire.

"Doing what."

"Reaching." He turned the spit with the patience of someone who had once waited three thousand years inside a mountain and found it only mildly inconvenient. "You do it when you think I'm not watching. You reach for something behind your eyes, and then you get that look."

"What look?"

"The one you're making right now."

I made it harder, out of spite. He smiled. He was good at smiling now; he'd had weeks of practice being mortal, and mortals smile because otherwise the weather wins. He looked like a road-worn pilgrim, tall and grey-eyed, with a scar along his jaw that I'd watched him give himself one night just so people would stop asking why he didn't have any scars. A god learning to lie convincingly. It was going well. I was almost proud.

Below us, Greyhollow smoked gently in the evening light — hearth-smoke, cook-smoke, the ordinary smoke of a town that had decided not to burn this season. The passes were quiet. Quiet in the way a room is quiet after an argument everyone lost.

* * *

We came down into Greyhollow on market day, which was either brave or stupid, and I've learned enough about myself in these weeks to know I don't learn. The town remembered me even if I didn't remember it. People looked. Not the way they look at a stranger — the way they look at a story they heard secondhand and are now checking against the original.

A woman selling apples pressed two of them into my hand and wouldn't take coin. "For the wolf," she said, and touched her brow, and walked away before I could ask what that meant.

"I hate that," I told Vael.

"The apples?"

"The nickname. The reverence. The way they look at me like I'm a shrine." I bit the apple. It was good. Everything here was annoyingly good lately. "I'm a mercenary. I escort caravans. I have a rate card."

"You gave them back their gods," Vael said mildly. "People are bad at thanking that at scale. They'll settle down. Give it ten years."

"Ten years," I said, and something in my chest went sideways, and I ate the rest of the apple to cover it.

Wren found us before we found Wren, which was becoming traditional. She came out of the crowd like water finding a crack — twelve years old, unmarked now, scrubbed clean of the truce-anchor sigils that had once been carved into her fate by people who called it justice. She fell into step beside me without a word, the way she always did, and after a hundred paces she said, "You did the apple thing again."

"What apple thing?"

"You eat apples left-handed. You used to eat them right-handed. Before."

I stopped walking. "Before what, exactly? You keep saying *before* like it's a place I can visit."

Wren looked up at me with those too-old eyes, and for a second I saw her decide, again, not to tell me. She carried everything — that was the arrangement, though nobody had asked me to agree to it, since agreeing requires remembering there was ever a question. She carried Marisol. She carried nine names from a rebellion I'd apparently fought in. She carried the sound my voice made when I recited the last of them while the old law took its price out of me like a butcher taking payment in installments.

She carried it all, and she was twelve, and she never once made me feel the weight of it. Which was its own kind of unbearable.

"There's a contract," she said instead, and handed me a folded paper sealed with grey wax. "Came through the caravan office. They said ask for the wolf."

* * *

The contract was simple, which meant it wasn't. A village up past the high cairns — the ones that talked, or one of them did, half-mad and grateful — reported that prayers were going unanswered again. Not the old silence. Something new: prayers answered wrong, or answered by the wrong god, or answered with a bill attached. The old law, loose in the world since the truce shattered, was finding its feet. Every oath priced. Every prayer weighed. And somewhere in the arithmetic, ordinary people were getting shortchanged.

"They want escorts," Wren said. "Road wardens. Someone to walk between."

"Between what?"

"Between them and the gods." She shrugged, elaborately casual, which on Wren meant she'd rehearsed it. "That's sort of your whole thing now. Nobody's said it out loud yet, but everybody knows. You're the veil. Prayers pass through you fair, oaths pass through you honest. The old law recognizes you. It has to — you paid more than anyone."

I read the contract twice. Fair rates, honest terms, a route I knew without knowing how I knew it — my boots already planning the switchbacks, my mouth already tasting the weather three passes north.

Vael leaned over my shoulder. "There's a clause here about hazard pay."

"There would be."

"And a clause about dispute resolution."

"That one's new. Read it."

He read it aloud: *"Disputes between mortal and divine parties shall be arbitrated by the contractor, whose judgment shall be binding upon all parties, including—" *he paused— "*including the undersigned gods."*

Silence around the market stall. Somewhere a hammer rang on an anvil, unhurried, like the world had all the time in it.

"They're binding themselves to my arbitration," I said.

"You broke the truce that kept them caged and then paid the law's full price voluntarily. In the old accounting, that makes you the only party every faction trusts. The gods can't cheat you. The hunters can't touch you. The law itself treats you as..." He searched for the word, and a god searching for a word is a humbling thing to watch. "...a fixture. Like a border stone. Border stones don't lie, and everyone fights *around* them, never *over* them."

"A border stone that eats apples left-handed."

"A very respected border stone."

I should have laughed. Instead I stood there in the market noise with a contract in my hands and a hole where my life used to be, and I understood — finally, cleanly — what I'd bought with all those names. Not peace. Not freedom, exactly. A job. An eternal, thankless, essential job, standing in the gap between things too large to be reasoned with, forever, because I was the only one both sides trusted and the only one who couldn't remember why that should frighten me.

It should have felt like a cage. The mountain had been Vael's cage, and I'd broken him out of it at a cost I couldn't even grieve properly.

But standing there, with Wren's shoulder against my arm and Vael's shadow across the paper and a road in my blood pointing north — it didn't feel like a cage.

It felt like a road. And roads, whatever else you say about them, go somewhere.

"We'll take it," I said.

* * *

We signed at dusk in the caravan office, by candlelight, with a clerk whose hands shook until Vael signed under a false name and the wax took the seal without complaint. Gods, it turns out, can hold a pen. Three thousand years in a rock gives you excellent fine motor control.

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting, fat and slow, the kind that means the passes will close soon and open meaner. Wren pulled her hood up. Vael breathed out a long plume of steam and looked, for a moment, like something older than the mountains wearing a man's coat, which is what he was.

"One condition," I said to him.

"You have conditions now. How mortal of you."

"When we're on the road. When there's a prayer to carry or an oath to weigh or a god to argue with." I met his eyes. "Tell me the truth. All of it. Who I was. What I paid. Don't decide for me what I can't handle. I've had enough people deciding things for me — I can't remember who they were, but I'm fairly sure there were several."

He was quiet long enough that the snow filled the space between us.

"She said something like that once," he said finally. "The woman you were. Right before—" He stopped. Started again, gentler. "Yes. All of it. Every road, every truth. That's the debt I owe, and I pay my debts. Ask anyone I've ruined."

"Comforting."

"It's meant to be."

Wren tugged my sleeve and pointed north, where the peaks had gone white-to-the-waist and the wind was carrying something down out of the high country — faint, rhythmic, almost like singing, almost like bells, almost like a prayer being answered *correctly* for the first time in weeks.

"First job starts tomorrow," she said. "Long walk. Lots of passes."

"Good," I said, and found that I meant it. "I know the way."

I didn't, of course. Not really. I knew it the way my hands knew crossbows and my hair knew braids — some ghost of me had walked it and left the map behind in muscle and bone. Whoever she was, whatever she'd given up, she'd left me good roads and sharp instincts and two people who loved her enough to walk beside the empty space she'd left.

It wasn't nothing. Standing there in the falling snow with a contract in my pocket and a god at my shoulder, I decided it might even be enough.

Then the bells in the valley below began to ring — all of them at once, every shrine in Greyhollow, wild and urgent — and Wren went pale, and Vael lifted his head like a wolf scenting weather, and I understood that the quiet passes had only been quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

"Oscar," Vael said quietly. "Something's waking in the Saltmarsh. And it remembers your name — the one you sold."

Of course it did. I'd paid with my memories. Apparently the world intended to keep the change.

I pulled my hood up, checked the crossbow my hands already knew, and started walking north into the snow.

"Well," I said. "Let's go introduce ourselves."
