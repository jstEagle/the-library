## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The broth was trying to kill me again.

I could tell by the way it smiled. Cooks have a particular smile when they've done something to a dish — a little pride at the corner of the mouth, the spoon held just a shade too reverently. Master Ellery had that smile this morning as he set the tureen before me, and so I took my time with the first sip, because a taster who rushes is a taster who dies surprised, and I had made a private vow at fourteen never to be surprised again.

Hemlock, I thought. No — not quite. Hemlock is sweet at the start and then it turns on you like a debt collector. This was bitter up front, green and climbing, with something oily underneath that coated the back of my teeth. Wolfsbane, then, cut with hemlock to blur its signature. Clever. Someone in the kitchens below the keep had been reading the old texts, or someone had paid them to.

I swallowed.

My tongue catalogued it the way a priest counts beads: wolfsbane, second pressing, the kind that numbs the lips before it stops the heart; hemlock, older, musty, stored too near the smoked fish; a whisper of arsenic in the base stock, because apparently we were being thorough today. The broth itself was good. Barley, marrow bone, a bay leaf that had given up all pretense of being anything but decoration. Somewhere under the murder, someone had cooked with love.

"Your Grace's morning draught," Master Ellery said, meaning the duke's, meaning the cup I'd already certified by not dying. "Shall I send it up?"

"Send it," I said. "Tell them to let it cool first. The wolfsbane turns sweeter warm, and His Grace drinks fast when he's cold."

Ellery wrote that down. He was a careful man, Ellery, with ink-stained fingers and a face like unsalted bread, and he was the closest thing I had to a colleague in this valley, which tells you most of what you need to know about my social calendar.

For those who don't know — and no one knows, that being rather the point of me — a food-taster's work is not eating. Anyone can eat. What I do is closer to translation. Every poison has a grammar, every antidote a dialect, and my body has spent twenty-six years learning to read them all aloud without flinching. It started when I was a child, small doses, measured to the grain, building immunity the way you build a wall: one stone, one stone, one stone. By now there are perhaps nine substances in the world that would kill me outright, and I keep the list in my head the way other women keep prayers.

It is not bravery. I want to be clear about that. Bravery implies a choice made once, at a crisis, with your heart hammering. This was a choice made every single day for decades, so many times it stopped being a choice and became a texture. Like the valley itself.

Because here is the thing about Greymarrow Vale, where I have lived my whole life and expected to die: it is quiet. Not peaceful-quiet. Quiet the way a room is quiet after an argument. Magic does not live here. The old wards see to that — great buried seams of silence running under the soil, laid down so long ago that even the histories argue about who poured them and why. Nothing gifted can cross into the vale. Nothing enchanted survives a night within it. And nothing in me — whatever might be in me, which is nothing, which has always been nothing — can reach me here.

I grew up thinking everyone felt that hush. That the world was simply a muffled place, and other valleys were louder versions of the same. It wasn't until I was grown and read the border ledgers that I understood: outside these hills, the world sings. Stones remember names. Rivers carry gossip. The gifted walk around lit from inside like paper lanterns.

In here, none of it. In here, a candle is just a candle, and I am just a woman whose job is to die slowly on behalf of a duke who has never learned my name.

* * *

Mornings in the poison kitchens follow a liturgy older than the usurper's crown, older than the true king's crown, probably older than crowns generally, since poisoning predates monarchy by a comfortable margin and will outlast it too.

First bell: bread duty. Three loaves, three corners bitten, count to two hundred while the kitchen watches me with the particular boredom of people watching a ritual they've seen ten thousand times. The baker, Marta, always slips me the corner with the most crust, because crust is honest and she thinks I deserve honesty. She may be the only person in the vale who likes me without needing me alive for professional reasons.

Second bell: the cups. Wine, watered beer, the duke's morning tisane. I hold each in my mouth for ten heartbeats — long enough for the quick poisons to introduce themselves — then swallow or spit according to what my tongue reports. My tongue has a vocabulary of maybe four hundred entries. Bitter almond says cyanide. Sweet-metallic says antimony. A taste like old coins and regret says someone in the household is angry at the duke, because half of poisoning is opportunity and the other half is spite.

Third bell: the broth, the stew, whatever the great hall means to serve. Today, the murder soup.

Fourth bell is mine. Fourth bell I sit in the little stone alcove off the kitchens with a pot of plain tea — nothing in it, ever, I check twice — and I let my hands shake for exactly as long as they want to, and then I make them stop.

You build up a tolerance to poison. Nobody tells you that you also build up a tolerance to everything else. Fear, mostly. Hope, a little. There's a numbness that comes with the trade, the way calluses come with an axe. I tasted death so often, in doses so small, that death became a flavor, and flavors become familiar, and familiar becomes boring, and boring becomes — ash. That's the word. Everything tastes like ash if you taste it enough times. Even safety.

Especially safety.

Because here is my confession, in the privacy of my own skull where confessions go to die: I am very good at my work, and I am safe, and I hate it. Not loudly. I'm not the sort who hates loudly; loud hatred gets you conscripted or dead. I hate it the way the vale hates magic — quietly, thoroughly, with the whole weight of the ground pressing down on it.

Safe is a flavor. I know it intimately. It tastes like barley broth with nothing wrong in it. It tastes like Marta's crust. It tastes like fourth-bell tea. And underneath every one of those tastes, faint as arsenic in a wedding feast, is the knowledge that I am a woman kept in a jar. Fed, watered, protected, useful. The vale keeps me the way the duke keeps his seals — somewhere dark, somewhere dry, brought out when needed.

I used to think that was bitterness talking. Then I realized bitterness is also a flavor, and I could name it precisely, and naming it didn't help, and that's when I understood I wasn't going to think my way out of this valley. You don't think your way out of a wardline. Ask any ghost.

* * *

Afternoon brought the meat course, and with it, an education.

The venison came up from the duke's own forest, which meant it should have been clean — his huntsmen are honest men, his smokehouse is locked, and nobody in the vale has motive to kill him that isn't already on record in the tax rolls. But rule one of tasting is that clean is a claim, not a fact, and claims get verified.

I took my portion to the tasting bench, carved a sliver, and put it on my tongue.

And stopped.

There was something in the fat. Not a poison — my tongue knew all its poisons the way a mother knows her children's footsteps, and this wasn't one. This was stranger. A taste like standing in a doorway between rooms where different music is playing. A taste like a word in a language I almost remembered.

I chewed. I swallowed. I sat very still and waited for my heart to do something dramatic.

It didn't. My heart, bless its disciplined muscle, did nothing at all, because whatever was in the venison wasn't chemistry, and chemistry is the only language my body speaks.

"Hen," Ellery said — he calls me Hen, short for nothing, a habit from when I was small and new — "you've gone gray. Was it the meat?"

"No," I said. "Yes. I don't know." I looked down at the venison, ordinary and brown and glistening, and felt the strangest sensation, like a bell ringing somewhere far away in a valley where bells don't ring. "Where did this come from?"

"His Grace's forest. Why?"

"Did anyone dress it differently? New salt? New spice?"

Ellery frowned and fetched the smokehouse ledger, and we went through it line by line, and everything was as it should be, and that was somehow worse. Because the taste was still on my tongue, fading now, that almost-music, that doorway-between-rooms, and I knew — the way you know a smell from childhood, without knowing how you know — that the venison hadn't been poisoned.

It had been *touched*. Out past the wardline somewhere, by something gifted, before it ever came into the vale. And some residue of that touch had survived the crossing, the way perfume survives on a letter, and my tongue — my stupid, faithful, twenty-six-years-trained tongue — had tasted it anyway.

"Nothing," I told Ellery. "Fat gone slightly off. Clear it, but mark the batch."

He marked the batch. He always does what I say in the kitchens; it's the only place in the world where my word carries weight, and I try to spend it responsibly.

But I carried the taste with me through the rest of the day, through supper certification and the evening cups, through the long walk back to my rooms in the taster's wing where the candles burn plain and the locks are for my protection, they say, though jars are also protected by lids. I lay awake turning the flavor over like a coin of unknown minting.

Here is what frightens me, and I'll say it plainly because no one is listening: the taste didn't frighten me. What frightened me was that some part of me recognized it. Deep down, under the calluses and the catalogues and the twenty-six years of practiced nothing, something in my chest leaned toward that doorway-between-rooms flavor like a plant toward a window.

Like it had been waiting a long time to hear that music again.

Which is nonsense. I am Casper Villalobos, orphan of the vale, taster of the duke's table, a woman made entirely of scar tissue and routine. There is nothing in me but immunity and inventory. Whatever the world outside is singing, it isn't singing to me.

I repeated this to myself until it sounded like a prayer, and prayers, as every poisoner knows, are just doses you give yourself.

* * *

Fifth bell, the strange one, the hour when the kitchen staff go to their suppers and the great ovens tick as they cool. That's when the courier came.

I heard the hooves before I saw him — wrong rhythm for the vale roads, too urgent, too much metal. Through the kitchen's high window I watched a rider in royal colors come down the pass road at a pace that said the message mattered more than the horse, and I felt something tighten behind my ribs that had nothing to do with wolfsbane.

Royal couriers don't come to Greymarrow Vale. The vale pays its taxes in wool and silence and is left alone; that is the ancient arrangement, the whole point of the buried wards. Kings forget places like this on purpose. A royal courier riding hard is a change in the weather, and in my experience — which is considerable, and chemical — sudden changes in weather mean something has gotten into the air supply.

He clattered into the yard and handed his satchel not to the duke's steward but to the guard captain, and the guard captain's face went through several interesting expressions in sequence, ending somewhere near grief, and then the man looked across the courtyard, directly at the kitchen window, directly at me.

People look at me all day. They watch me eat. It's my entire profession. But they watch me the way you watch a tool — no more personal than watching a knife cut. This was different. This was a man looking at a person, and finding her already condemned, and pitying her for not knowing yet.

I turned back to my bench. There was a cup in front of me, the duke's evening wine, and I went through the motions — the sip, the ten heartbeats, the swallow — and tasted nothing at all, which meant it was clean, which meant I had done my job, which meant the day was over and successful and exactly like every other day of my life.

Safe. Certified. Sealed in a valley where nothing magical can reach me, doing work that matters to exactly one old man who doesn't know my name, surrounded by people who need me alive and none who need me otherwise.

The courier was still in the yard when I finished. The guard captain was walking toward the kitchens with a sealed writ in his hands, red wax catching the last of the light, and his boots on the flagstones sounded like a countdown.

I picked up my tasting spoon and held it, because a taster's hands want something to hold when the world changes flavor, and mine had changed. I couldn't name the note yet. But twenty-six years of training had taught me one thing above all others:

When you taste something new, you don't spit.

You wait, and you find out what it does to you.
