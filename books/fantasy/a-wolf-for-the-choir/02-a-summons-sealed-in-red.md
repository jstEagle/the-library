## 2. A Summons Sealed in Red

The courier came on a Tuesday, which I resented, because Tuesdays were broth days and I had been looking forward to the broth.

Not that the broth was good. It was never good. It was mutton and barley and whatever the cook had decided to lace it with that week — usually hemlock extract, sometimes foxglove, once, memorably, distilled oleander that made my tongue go numb for two days and cost me a favorite spoon. But it was *my* bad broth. It arrived at the same hour, in the same dented pewter bowl, carried up the same narrow stair by the same sour-faced undercook, and I drank it at the same window overlooking the same gray courtyard of Grayhollow Keep while the same six ravens argued over the same dead thing in the midden.

In a valley where nothing changes — where magic itself gave up trying to change anything and left, like a guest who realized the host wasn't going to offer more tea — routine is not a cage. Routine is a religion. And I was devout.

So when hoofbeats broke the Tuesday liturgy, I took it personally.

I heard them before anyone else did, because I hear everything first; poison sharpens the ears along with everything else, or maybe it just makes you paranoid enough to listen. Four horses, moving fast, coming down the mountain road from the pass — the wrong direction for merchants, too few for soldiers, too disciplined for bandits. I set down my bowl mid-sip and watched through the frost-furred window as four riders in royal livery clattered into the courtyard below, their colors the deep wine-red of the usurper's house, their boots leaving mud on stones that hadn't seen mud in years.

Grayhollow's garrison captain got there first, as captains do, all chest and bluster, demanding names and business. The lead courier didn't dismount. He simply held up a scroll case, black lacquer, capped with wax the color of old blood, and said something I couldn't hear but could read plainly off the captain's face: *this is above you.*

I have spent my whole life reading faces the way other people read books. It's a taster's skill. You learn to watch the duke's mouth before he drinks, the lady's eyes after she swallows, the kitchen boy who suddenly won't look at the soup. Faces tell you what tongues are afraid to say. And every face in that courtyard, in the space of about four seconds, told me the same story:

*Someone is here for Casper Villalobos.*

I finished the broth anyway. Habit. Also spite. If the world intended to ruin my Tuesday, it could at least wait until I'd gotten the barley down.

* * *

Master Aldous Vane found me twenty minutes later in the tasting chamber, which he must have known he would, because there is nowhere else I go. The tasting chamber is a long stone room with one high window, three tables, a cabinet of glass vials organized by lethality, and a chair bolted to the floor — mine, though no one has ever explained why a food-taster needs bolting. Perhaps they feared I'd run. The joke, of course, being that running requires somewhere to run *to*, and the valley does not permit exits so much as it permits delays.

Aldous Vane has kept me for eleven years. That is the word we use — *kept* — and neither of us has ever pretended it meant anything warmer. He is a thin man gone grayer than his years, with ink-stained fingers and the permanent stoop of someone who has spent decades leaning over ledgers, and he administers my life the way he administers everything: precisely, quietly, without affection and without cruelty. He signs for my food. He logs my doses. He watches me drink things that would kill a cathedral congregation and writes down how long my hands shake.

"Riders," I said, before he could speak. "Four. Red seal. They're waiting in your office."

His pen stopped moving.

"You knew," I said. "You've known since yesterday. You reorganized the vial cabinet. You only reorganize when you're frightened."

"I am not frightened," Aldous said, in the voice of a man who was frightened. "I am *procedural*. There is a difference."

"There really isn't."

He set down the pen and looked at me for a long moment, and what was in his face was worse than fear. It was guilt. Eleven years of it, arriving all at once, like a debt collector.

"Casper," he said. "There is a writ."

"I gathered. Red seal. Black case. The Queen's own colors." I turned my bowl idly on the table. "What does Her Usurped Majesty want with a poison kitchen in the middle of nowhere? We're hardly strategic. We're barely *geographic*."

"The writ concerns tasters." His voice had gone flat, careful, the way it went flat whenever the subject brushed against the edges of what I was allowed to know. "Specifically. Tasters of proven immunity are to be conscripted into royal service for the duration of—" he paused, and I watched him decide whether to say the next word honestly or not, and watched him fail— "the duration of the Crown's current security operations."

"Security operations," I repeated.

"The Great Hunt."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Even in the valley, even in our magic-dead quiet where news arrived late and half-rotted, everyone had heard of the Great Hunt. The usurper Queen's grand project. Every gifted soul in the realm to be found, catalogued, collared, and put to use — or put down, depending on the mood of the local officer and the depth of the local grave-diggers. The free cities had closed their borders. Refugees were flooding west. And somewhere far away, in a palace I'd never seen, a woman wearing a stolen crown was signing orders in red wax.

"And they want *me*," I said. "For a hunt against the gifted." I laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Aldous. I'm not gifted. I'm the opposite of gifted. I'm a woman whose entire talent is *not dying*. What possible use—"

"They want your immunity," he said. "Poisoned blades. Tainted wells. A taster can verify what's safe to use and what will kill your own troops." He hesitated. "And there are... other applications. For bodies that don't reject what should kill them."

I let that sit. Other applications. My imagination, which had been fed eleven years of poisons and therefore had an extremely vivid diet, supplied several, and none of them involved me growing old in Grayhollow.

"No," I said.

"Casper—"

"*No.*" I stood, and the chair scraped, and my voice cracked out of me louder than I'd meant. "This valley is neutral ground. This valley is *warded*. Nothing magical crosses the wardline — nothing gets *in*, nothing gets *out*, and that includes her writs and her riders and her Hunt. That's the whole point of this place. That's the whole point of *me*. I am safe here. I have been safe here for eleven years, and I am not walking past that border for a queen who stole her throne and wants to steal everything else—"

"The writ doesn't require your consent."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the ravens had stopped arguing. Even the keep seemed to hold its breath.

"It requires your compliance," Aldous said softly. "Or it requires someone to answer for your refusal. And the writ names the responsible party." He turned the ledger toward me, and there it was, in the courier's precise hand: *Keeper of record: A. Vane.* "If you refuse, I answer. And if I answer incorrectly, they send more riders, and those riders do not leave without someone."

I sat back down. Not gracefully.

"That's extortion," I said.

"That's *government*," Aldous said. "I'm sorry. I've been drafting a petition. An appeal on grounds of—"

"Aldous." I rubbed my eyes. "You've met the Queen's administration. Do you genuinely believe a petition—"

"I believe in procedure," he said, with the desperate faith of a man clutching a ledger in a flood. "Procedure has held this valley together for two hundred years. Procedure says the writ must be answered within three days. Procedure says the answer may be an appeal. So I am going to appeal, and you are going to stay in the valley, and in three days this will be resolved *correctly*."

I looked at him — this dry, gray, procedural man who had fed me poison for a decade and never once asked if I was happy, and who was now, in his terrible bloodless way, trying to save me — and I felt something I hadn't felt in years, which was almost fondness.

"You're going to get yourself killed," I said.

"Don't be dramatic. Drink your water."

* * *

He tried to burn the writ on the second night.

I want to be fair to him here. I want to record, for posterity or whoever ends up reading this, that Aldous Vane did not burn the writ out of stupidity. He burned it out of arithmetic. Three days, one appeal, zero chance of success — he'd done the sums, and somewhere between the second column and the third, he'd concluded that the only procedure left was arson. Destroy the writ, claim it never arrived, buy the valley time until the war moved elsewhere. It was, by his standards, a wild act of passion. By anyone else's, it was a doomed old man holding a candle to a forest fire.

I only know because I caught him at it.

I couldn't sleep — the valley's silence presses differently some nights, thick as wet wool, and that night it was pressing on my chest specifically — and I went down to his office to steal his good brandy, which is the closest thing we have to a tradition. The door was ajar. Candlelight. And there was Aldous, standing over the brazier, the black scroll case open, the writ half-unrolled, feeding it to the flames one corner at a time with the same meticulous care he brought to everything, as if even treason should be done neatly.

"You're supposed to appeal," I said from the doorway.

He flinched so hard he nearly dropped it. "Casper. You should be asleep."

"You're supposed to *appeal*," I said again, stepping inside, closing the door. "Three days. Procedure. You said—"

"Procedure delivers you to the Queen," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle, and for the first time in eleven years I saw the actual man underneath the keeper — old, tired, terrified, and stubborn as a root. "I've read the supplementary orders. I know what 'other applications' means. I know what happens to vessels of proven immunity in the Hunt camps. I signed for you, Casper. Eleven years ago I signed papers making myself responsible for you, and I told myself it was employment, and it was never employment, it was *custody*, and I have kept you like a — like an asset in a vault, and I thought that was safety, and now the vault has been sold." He turned back to the fire. "So no. No procedure. Some things you burn."

I should have helped him. That's the part that will live in my chest forever, the part I'll carry past the wardline and beyond: I should have grabbed the other end of the writ and fed it to the flames myself. Instead I stood there like a fool, watching the red seal curl and blacken, thinking the most useless thought of my life, which was: *so that's what he looks like when he cares.*

We never heard the horses. The valley eats sound the way it eats everything else — magic-dead, memory-poor, quiet as a held breath — but somehow, impossibly, they were already in the courtyard. Later I would learn the couriers had never fully left; they'd camped at the treeline, waiting to see whether the writ was honored, because the Queen's men trust procedure exactly as much as Aldous did, and they plan for arson the way farmers plan for rain.

The door burst open.

There were four of them. The lead courier — I never learned his name, only his face, which was the face of a man doing paperwork with a sword — crossed the room in three strides. Aldous turned, and I will give him this: he didn't beg. He held up the burning scrap of the writ like a torch, like a flag, like the last page of his own ledger, and he said, in a voice that shook but did not retreat:

"The valley is warded. Your Queen's power stops at the border. Whatever you think you are—"

"Sir," the courier said, almost gently, "we're not magic. We're just men."

And then it was very fast and very simple and very much like everything else in my life: quick, clinical, over before I understood it had begun. Steel does not care about wards. Wards keep out spells, not soldiers. I learned that lesson standing in a pool of lamplight with my keeper's blood cooling around my slippers, learning it the hard way, the way I learn everything.

The courier wiped his blade and turned to me, and his face did the thing faces do when they shift from violence back to business, the shutter clicking closed.

"Casper Villalobos," he said. "Conscripted. By order of Queen Isolde Vane-Marrec, First of Her Name, Protector of the Realm. You'll ride at dawn."

"My name," I heard myself say, from very far away, "is not a summons."

"No," he agreed. "It's an asset tag. Pack light."

They let me bury him. I don't know why. Perhaps even the Queen's men have a quota of decency per week. I dug the grave myself, behind the keep, in soil that has never grown anything magical and never will, and I marked it with a stone and no name, because Aldous would have appreciated the procedural anonymity, and because I couldn't carve letters with my hands shaking.

Before I left, I went back to the tasting chamber one last time. The vials stood in their cabinet, ordered by lethality, patient as a choir. I took nothing. There was nothing there worth taking that I didn't already carry in my own body — eleven years of hemlock and foxglove and oleander, a pharmacopoeia sleeping in my blood, an arsenal nobody can confiscate.

At the gate, the courier offered me a horse. I looked at the mountain road winding down toward the pass, toward the wardline I had never once crossed in thirty-one years, the invisible border where the valley's great silence ended and the loud, dangerous, magical world began.

Somewhere past that line, colors sang and stones remembered names — I'd heard travelers say such mad things, and always pitied them their addled heads. Somewhere past that line, the Queen's Hunt was gathering, and my name was already written in somebody's ledger, filed under assets.

"Last chance to refuse," the courier said, not meaning it.

I mounted the horse. It seemed important, somehow, that I do it myself — that no one lift me into the saddle, that I choose the motion, even if I didn't choose the direction. The valley's protection ended at its border. Everything after that line, I would have to survive the way I survived everything else:

One taste at a time.

The gates opened. I rode out, and behind me Grayhollow Keep shrank to a smudge of gray stone, and ahead the pass swallowed the road, and I did not look back again, because looking back is a flavor I already know —

and it tastes like ash.
