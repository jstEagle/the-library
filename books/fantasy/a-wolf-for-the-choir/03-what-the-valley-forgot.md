## 3. What the Valley Forgot

The valley taught me three lessons before I could write my own name: everything has a flavor, most flavors are lies, and the safest place in the world is the one where nothing answers when you call.

I had lived by those lessons for twenty-three years. I recited them to myself like a catechism as the Queen's men marched me up the switchback road toward the wardline, my wrists bound in silk — silk, because the courier's writ had specified that the asset be unbruised, and someone in the royal bureaucracy had actually underlined the word *asset* twice.

"You'll want to breathe deep when we cross," said the sergeant walking beside me. He was a broad, pink man named Hask, with the cheerful cruelty of someone who had never once been hit back. "First time out of the valley, taster? You're in for a treat."

"I've been out," I said. "There's a village four miles east."

"The village is inside the line, girl. Everything you know is inside the line." He smiled at me the way you smile at livestock. "Past it, the world's awake."

I did not tell him that I knew exactly where the wardline ran, because knowing things was a habit I'd spent a lifetime pretending not to have. I did not tell him that my keeper, Magra, had drawn me a map when I was nine years old, in broth-thick ink, and made me memorize it and then burned it, and said: *If they ever take you past this line, Casper Villalobos, you run until your lungs quit, and then you crawl.*

Magra was dead now, along with her attempt to burn a royal writ. The Queen's men had made her kneel in her own poison kitchen and had done it there, among my racks of tinctures, so that for days afterward the air had tasted faintly of hellebore and iron. Grief, I discovered, also has a flavor. It tastes like standing in a pantry where everything has been salted by someone else's hand.

So no, I did not run. There was nowhere to run *to*. The valley had been my whole world — its quiet, its gray-green light, its stones that sat dumb as bread and asked nothing of me. Beyond the line was the world that had killed my mother, sold my mother, whatever the verb was; I still didn't know the verb. Beyond the line was everything Magra had spent thirty years keeping on the other side of a hill.

And beyond the line, though I didn't have words for it yet, was the thing in my chest. The thing that had never once spoken inside the valley, and which I had therefore spent my whole life pretending wasn't there.

You can pretend a room is empty if it stays quiet enough. I had made a profession of it.

* * *

The wardline was not marked. That was the first betrayal of my expectations. I had imagined a wall, a gate, a carved stone, some ceremonial threshold a person could brace against. Instead there was just a saddle of moor between two shoulders of hill, heather going rust-brown at the tips, a crow somewhere complaining about the weather, and Hask saying, "Three steps, taster," in the tone of a man announcing soup.

The soldiers crossed without ceremony. They didn't even slow down. Of course they didn't; magic meant nothing to men who had never been without it.

I stopped at the invisible seam and stood there like a fool with my mouth open, because the world changed between one heartbeat and the next, and nobody had warned me it would be *loud*.

Here is what no one tells you about being born inside a silence: you don't know it's a silence until it ends. I had thought the valley was simply the world, plain and gray-flavored, the way I'd thought everyone's tongue burned the way mine did when they swallowed nightshade. I had thought I was a woman of ordinary senses living an ordinary muted life.

Then I stepped over a line of nothing — no stone, no arch, only heather and cold air — and the world *sang*.

Not metaphor. Not the pretty way poets mean it. The heather sang, a low rusted chord, every bloom a small throat, and the wind through it carried harmonies I could feel in my teeth. The color of the sky — I want to say the color itself, the blue, was a note held so long and so high it had become a kind of weather. Somewhere under my feet a stone remembered the name of a river that hadn't run here in a thousand years, and it said the name over and over to itself, patient as a prayer, and I heard it. Through my boots. Through the soles Magra had nailed on herself.

Colors sang. Distances had opinions. A hawk turned a circle overhead and left a bright scratch across the sky's music like a fingernail through wax. And every blade of grass I passed leaned very slightly toward my ankles, the way flowers lean at the sun, and whispered something I couldn't quite translate but which felt, horribly, like *greeting*.

I must have made a sound. Hask laughed. "There she goes," he said. "Every valley-born makes that face. Like a babe opening its eyes."

It was worse than a babe opening its eyes. A babe has no idea what it's looking at. I knew. Some cold, buried part of me — the same part that catalogued poison notes in a spoon, the part that had always known more than I let myself know — understood immediately what was happening, and understood what it implied, and began, calmly, professionally, to scream.

Because the world was singing *at me*. Not around me. At me. The grass leaned. The stones raised their voices. And underneath all of it, rising like sediment in disturbed water, something in my chest was answering.

I had a heart; I knew its rhythm the way a taster knows her own pulse, because reading your pulse is how you learn you've been poisoned before you learn anything else. This was not my heart. It sat behind my sternum, lower and deeper, a second pulse with a different meter, and as the world's song poured into me it began to *resonate*, the way a string on one instrument will hum when its twin sounds across a room.

Sympathy. That's the word musicians use. Sympathetic vibration.

Something in me was sympathizing with the world, and the world was sympathizing back, and the exchange was getting louder with every step I took away from the wardline, and I was tied with silk, and Magra was dead, and I finally understood — with the terrible clarity of a flavor identified too late — why an entire kingdom's bureaucracy had underlined the word *asset* twice.

"Stop," I said. My voice came out thin. "Hask. Stop walking."

"Can't stop, taster. Schedule."

"Something's wrong with me."

"Nothing's wrong with you." He glanced back, and his cheer curdled slightly, because I suppose I looked wrong — I had gone down on one knee in the heather without deciding to, and the heather had *surged* toward my knee like tide toward a drowning man. "Oi. Oi, get up. Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything."

Which was true, and was the whole problem, and neither of us had language for it yet. I knelt in the singing grass with a second heartbeat swelling behind my ribs like a bruise coming up in reverse, and I thought, distantly, with my taster's detachment, the voice I used for my own death: *so this is what was kept from me. This is what the quiet was for.*

They hadn't walled me into the valley to keep magic out. They had walled me in to keep *me* quiet. I was not a person who happened to live in a silence. I was a bell that had been muffled, and the muffling had been called a home for twenty-three years.

I am going to be sick, I thought, and then I was, generously, into the heather, while the heather forgave me instantly and in harmony.

* * *

They gave me an hour to recover, which was decent of them, and then the camp came into view below the saddle — the usurper's field army strung along a river valley like a knife laid on a table. Tents in ranks. Smoke in columns. Pens, I noticed, near the center, tall-fenced and draped with canvas, and my new senses, treacherous, over-lit, told me what was in the pens before my eyes confirmed it: grief. Fear. Dozens of small frightened signatures ringing the canvas like hands slapped against walls. The gifted, gathered for the Great Hunt. Bait pens. My destination, since bait presumably bunked with bait.

They put me in a tent alone, which I understood as both courtesy and quarantine, and brought me broth, which I understood as a joke, or possibly a test. I tasted it anyway, out of habit. Beef, thyme, no poisons, and — this was new, and I nearly wept at it — the thyme was *audible*. A green whisper. Twenty-three years of tasting the world with half my tongue, and now suddenly the whole instrument, and I couldn't decide if I'd been robbed or freed, and the second heartbeat pulsed on, indifferent to my opinion of it.

Sleep didn't come. Around midnight I went out to walk, because the guards had apparently decided the muffled bell should be permitted to ring gently within the fence line, and because sitting still meant listening to my own chest, and I wasn't ready for that conversation.

That was when I saw the wolf.

She was chained to a supply wagon at the camp's edge, and I want to be precise: she was not a wolf. She was a woman, tall, gray at the temples, built like something designed to hold a door, with fresh scars on her wrists that said the chains were newer than her defiance. But the first word my over-singing senses handed me was *wolf*, because her gift came off her in a low rolling note like a growl too deep to hear, and because her eyes caught the torchlight the way a predator's do — flat gold, unimpressed, taking inventory.

She was watching me before I saw her. Possibly before I left my tent. Wolves are like that.

"You're the taster," she said. Her voice matched the rest of her: low, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had decided years ago which things deserved urgency and found none of them sufficient.

"They're saying that like it's a title," I said.

"It is. It's a rare one." Her gaze moved down me and back up, not rude — diagnostic. "Valley-born?"

"Is it obvious?"

"Your colors are wrong." She said it flatly, factually, and my stomach dropped through the frozen ground, because I had spent my life making sure my colors were aggressively, boringly right. "Not wrong like a lie. Wrong like a door painted over." She tilted her head. "Something in you's knocking."

"There's nothing in me."

"Sera Aldane," she said, ignoring that entirely, offering her chained hands slightly as if to shake. "Formerly Knight-Captain of the Second Hunt. Currently furniture."

"Casper Villalobos." I didn't take the hands. I didn't go closer either, which I noted about myself.

"Villalobos." Something shifted behind the gold eyes — not surprise exactly. Confirmation. The way a tracker looks at a print she's been following for a long distance. "Wolf-name. Old one. Who was your mother?"

The question went through me like a blade through broth. "What makes you think I had one?"

"Everyone had one." Calm. Terribly calm. "Most people's don't matter. Yours does, or you wouldn't be here in silk bindings with a double guard and a sorcerer's writ." She let that sit. She was good at letting things sit; I would learn that about her, and hate it, and eventually be grateful for it. "I knew a Luz Villalobos once. Gifted so quietly the neighbors took years to notice. Sang old songs to stones to keep a bridge standing. Sold — or taken, versions differ — to the court beneath the mountain, twenty-odd years back."

The second heartbeat behind my sternum, which had been humming all evening like a struck bowl settling, went very still. So did I.

"You're mistaken," I said.

"Probably," Sera Aldane agreed, and closed her eyes as though the matter were settled, as though she hadn't just reached through twenty-three years of silence and plucked the one string I kept wrapped in wool. "Get some sleep, taster. Tomorrow the sorcerers start their tests."

"What tests?"

Her eyes opened again, and for a moment the flat gold was almost gentle, which was far worse than the alternative.

"The kind that find out what you're carrying," she said, "and whether it fits through a keyhole. Sleep well."

I walked back to my tent under a sky full of singing stars I no longer wanted to hear, and behind my ribs the thing that was not my heart knocked once, softly, like a visitor who knows she is expected — and waits.
