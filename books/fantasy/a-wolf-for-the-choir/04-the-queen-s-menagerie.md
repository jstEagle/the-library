## 4. The Queen's Menagerie

They put me in a cage with a view of the officers' mess, which I took as a compliment. Someone in the quartermaster's office had read my file.

I'd been in cages before — every wolf-knight has, it's practically a pension benefit — but this one was new timber, freshly planed, and stank of pine sap and somebody's ambition. It sat at the edge of the royal camp like a stage set waiting for its play: bars on three sides, open front facing a long trestle table where the Queen's officers ate roast fowl and planned the deaths of several hundred thousand people who had never done anything worse than be born wrong.

Wrong, in this case, meaning gifted. Meaning me, once. Meaning, apparently, still.

"You'll want to sit," said the sergeant who'd locked me in. He was young enough that his beard was more apology than statement. "The display starts at dusk."

"Display," I repeated.

He had the decency to look at the ground. "It's not my idea, Dame Aldane."

"Nothing ever is," I said. "That's how atrocities get built. One man at a time, all of them just carrying someone else's idea up the stairs."

He left quickly after that. Good. The young ones still bruised.

* * *

Dusk came the way it does in the lowlands — slow, purple, indifferent. Torches were lit along the camp's central avenue, and wagons rolled in from the day's collection, and the menagerie opened for business.

I use the word deliberately. I've seen menageries. I grew up two days' ride from the Duke of Harrowmere's, where he kept snow-lynxes and a fire-eating heron and once, briefly, a wyvern he'd bought as a hatchling and starved because it wouldn't perform. The Duke's menagerie had better accommodations than what the Queen's army provided its captives.

There were nine wagons. In them: an old man whose skin glowed faintly in the dark, like banked coals; twin girls who spoke to each other without moving their lips; a hulking farm-brother with stone under his fingernails who wept steadily and quietly, the way big men do when they think no one is watching; a woman with river in her hair, actual river, moving water that had decided to live there. And others. A boy who made small things float when he was frightened, which meant he made everything float, because he was frightened of everything now.

The officers came out with their wine and walked the line of wagons like buyers at a horse fair. One of them — a colonel with a face like a closed ledger — tapped the bars of the twins' cage and asked if they could do it on command. They did it on command. Of course they did. You learn fast in a cage which performances buy you another day of rations.

I stood at my own bars and watched, and something in my chest went very cold and very quiet, the way it does before a fight. Not rage. Rage is hot and spends itself. This was the other thing. The thing you build walls out of.

I knew these people. Not by name — by kind. For eleven years I wore the grey cloak of the Queen's Own Wolves, and for eleven years I told myself the orders were regrettable but necessary, that the purges were surgical, that the ones taken were treated gently. Then last spring they handed me the order for Cinderhollow. Four hundred souls, most of them children, and the order said *no survivors, no records, no witnesses*, and I looked at Captain Vess across the map table and said no.

One word. Eleven years of service traded for one word. Worth it. I'd say it again with my last breath, though saying it again would require breath, and the way things were trending, breath was a limited resource.

So now I was Dame Sera Aldane, stripped of title, sentenced to "service in the Great Hunt until such time as her treason is expunged" — which is court-speak for *we will work you to death and call it penance*. They'd chained my hands in iron-cold cuffs laced with nullshard, the black glass that eats magic, and set me to guard duty over the very people I'd refused to help destroy. There was a joke in there somewhere. I hadn't found the punchline yet. I suspected the punchline was me.

* * *

She arrived on the third night, and she arrived wrong.

Not wrong like the others. The old coal-skinned man, the floating boy, the river-woman — they came in glowing, humming, leaking their gifts into the air like open taps. The whole camp could feel them. Soldiers made signs against ill luck when they passed the wagons, and the horses hated the place, and even the torches burned strange colors near the cages.

But the girl who stepped down from the prison cart that third night cast nothing. Felt like nothing. Walked through the camp like a hole in the world, and the world didn't notice her, and that — that was the wrongness. Because I'd spent eleven years learning to sense the gifted the way a sailor senses weather, and here was a weatherless person, a gap in the pattern, and gaps are louder than noise if you know how to listen.

They put her in the wagon next to mine. She was small, brown-haired, maybe twenty, with the kind of face you forget on purpose — and I only noticed that because I realized I was trying to forget it and failing. Her wrists were chapped from irons. She didn't cry, didn't pray, didn't do any of the things new captives do. She sat down on the wagon floor, leaned against the bars, and began cataloguing the smells of the camp under her breath like a woman pricing goods at market.

"...tallow, wet canvas, horse liniment, something burnt — rosemary? No. Hemlock, probably. And under everything, iron." She paused. "Iron and ash. Every army smells like iron and ash."

"You're a taster," I said.

Her head turned. Up close her eyes were the flat amber of old honey, and they looked at me the way I imagine a jeweler looks at paste — politely certain it isn't real. "You're the traitor knight," she said. "They talk about you. Sera Aldane. Refused the Cinderhollow order."

"They do love their gossip."

"They also say you killed six handlers escaping a convoy." Her gaze dropped to my cuffed hands, then back up. "Those handlers look alive to me."

"The story grows in the telling. It was four, and they're alive, and I'm sorry about the noses." I shifted closer to the bars between us. "Taster. Which valley?"

Something flickered across her face — quick, gone. "Does it matter?"

"It matters because the valleys are dead ground. Warded. Magic can't follow anyone in, and nothing gifted comes out. So either you're lying about being a taster, or—" I stopped. Looked at her properly. At the nothing radiating off her, the perfect silence, the way the torchlight seemed to bend slightly around her rather than touch her. "Or you're not lying, and you're not empty, and I have no idea what you are."

"Most people don't," she said, and almost smiled. It wasn't a friendly smile. It was the smile of someone who had learned that being underestimated was the only currency that couldn't be confiscated. "Casper Villalobos. Food-taster to the valley keep, twelve years' service. Proven immunity. That's why they took me — the writ said proven immunity, conscripted for the Hunt."

"For the Hunt," I repeated. "They conscripted a *taster* for a purge of the gifted."

"That's what the writ said."

"And what do you taste for them out here? The officers' soup?"

"I don't know yet." She said it simply, without self-pity, and that simplicity frightened me more than any scream would have. "Nobody's told me anything since the courier came. My keeper burned the writ and they killed him for it, and then they put me in a cart, and now I'm here, smelling hemlock and listening to a disgraced knight ask questions."

I should have let it go. That's what survival demanded — keep your head down, serve your sentence, wait for a war to lose so the winners might pardon you. I'd been telling myself some version of that since my sentencing. But eleven years of pretending hadn't taken the wolf out of me, and wolves are constitutionally incapable of leaving a strange scent alone.

"Casper Villalobos," I said slowly. "Villalobos. That's a southern name. Lowland name."

"My mother's."

"And your father?"

"Never mentioned. Never mentioned my mother much either, come to that." Her voice stayed level, but her hand had drifted to her sternum, resting there lightly, the way people touch an old scar or a locket. "Why does a wolf-knight care about my family tree?"

Because I was starting to care, and caring was dangerous, and I wanted to know exactly how dangerous before it bit me. "Humor me. Your mother's name."

A long pause. The camp noises filled it — dice games, a farrier's hammer, the floating boy whimpering two wagons down.

"Luz," Casper said finally. "Luz Villalobos. Dead thirty years, near enough. Why?"

Thirty years. Thirty years ago the court beneath the mountain had been at the height of its trade — everyone knew the rumors, though no one said them above a whisper. Souls bought and sold, bound into vessels, stored like wine in the dark vaults. The crown had needed storage for things too dangerous to destroy and too valuable to free. And where do you store a soul where no magic can reach it, where no thief can dig, where the wardlines themselves are the lock?

In a valley. In a body. In a child raised to swallow poison daily, dosed and disciplined and kept numb, kept *quiet* —

Kept quiet. Gods. Kept quiet, because a vessel holding a bound soul must never wake it, and what keeps a soul quieter than a life spent tasting death in small sips, a life with no room left for anything else?

I looked at the flat amber eyes looking back at me, and the cold thing in my chest went from cold to arctic.

"When did you first feel it?" I asked. "The thing in your chest. The weight."

Her composure cracked — just a hairline, just for a second. "What did you say?"

"The night I passed the valley border, I felt every gift within ten miles like a shout. Except you. You weren't a shout. You weren't a whisper either. You were a *held breath*." I gripped the bar between us. "Someone hid something inside you, Casper. Something big enough that they built your entire life around keeping it asleep. The poison training. The valley. The isolation. None of it was protection. It was *storage conditions*."

"Don't." The word came out sharp, and beneath it, for half a heartbeat, I felt it — a pressure, vast and slow, like something enormous turning over in its sleep at the bottom of a lake. The torches guttered. The floating boy two wagons down woke screaming. Then it was gone, folded away, and Casper Villalobos was a small tired woman in a cage again, except her knuckles were white on the bars and her breath was ragged.

"What was that?" I whispered.

"Nothing." She swallowed. "That was nothing. That happens sometimes. When I'm angry. It's nothing."

"That was not nothing. That was the loudest nothing I have ever heard in my life."

"Then you heard wrong." But she didn't let go of the bars, and she didn't move away, and after a moment she said, quieter: "Storage conditions. Say that again. Slowly."

I said it again. Slowly. And I watched her take the words in the way she must have taken every poison of her working life — carefully, testing, refusing to flinch — and I watched the moment they stopped being words and became a history. Twelve years of broth and bitterness. A keeper who loved her, maybe, and died for a piece of paper. A mother sold before she was born. A childhood that was never a childhood but a warehouse with a girl in it.

"They used me," she said at last. Not a question. She didn't need it confirmed; she needed to hear it aloud, the way you need to hear a bone set.

"Yes."

"And whatever's inside me—"

"Is why they marched you past the wardline. Whatever the Hunt is, whatever the Queen thinks she's doing with you — you're not a soldier in it, Casper. You're cargo." I hesitated, then gave her the word, because she deserved the true shape of it, ugly as it was. "You're a reliquary. A living one. And somewhere under that mountain is the vault they intend to shelve you in."

For a long moment she said nothing. The camp went on around us — dice, hammer, distant laughter from the officers' mess where the colonel with the ledger-face was pouring more wine. Two wagons down, the floating boy had stopped screaming and started crying instead, softly, and nobody went to him.

Then Casper Villalobos laughed. One short, terrible note, with no humor in it anywhere.

"A reliquary," she said. "All those years I thought I was safe. I thought the valley was keeping me from the world." Her thumb traced her sternum, over the place where the pressure lived. "It was keeping the world from getting to the merchandise."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She turned those flat amber eyes on me, and they weren't flat anymore; they were lit from underneath, banked coals of a different color entirely. "You refused to burn Cinderhollow. You killed four men with your bare hands rather than stay leashed. Everyone in this camp thinks you're broken." A pause. "I don't. So here's my question, Dame Aldane, and think carefully, because I've tasted enough poisons to know that the dangerous ones are always the ones that smell like nothing: when the chance comes — and it will come, because armies are sloppy and cages are temporary — which side of the bars will you be standing on?"

Outside, the colonel with the closed-ledger face rose from the officers' mess, wine-flushed, and called for lanterns, and announced to his staff in a carrying voice that tomorrow the sorcerer would begin testing the new arrivals — beginning, he said, consulting a list, "with the taster. The quiet one. Her Majesty has particular interest in the quiet one."

Casper heard it. I watched her hear it. And I watched her decide — right there, in the space of one breath, the way soldiers decide and the doomed decide — that she was going to be neither tested nor shelved nor spent.

I wrapped my cuffed hands around the bars between us, iron-cold and null-dark, and gave her the only honest answer I had left.

"The same side as yours," I said. "Whatever side that turns out to be."
