## 5. Bait Is Still Meat

They came for my blood on the third morning, and they came with paperwork.

I want you to appreciate that. Somewhere in the usurper's war machine there was a clerk who decided that the extraction of a prisoner's blood required a signed authorization in triplicate, and that clerk went home that night believing they had served justice. That is the thing about tyrannies no ballad ever mentions. They are not run by monsters. They are run by monsters and their accountants.

The tent was the Queen's own — white canvas, gold fringe, the smell of cedar oil and something underneath it, older, like a cellar where the wine had died. Two guards flanked the entrance. A woman in gray waited at a folding table with the patience of someone who had never once in her life been kept waiting.

She was small. That was my first mistake, thinking it mattered. Her hair was iron-colored and cut close to the skull, and her eyes were the color of the sky in the hour before snow. When she looked at me, she did not look at a person. She looked at a ledger line.

"Casper Villalobos," she said. Not a question. She had my name the way a butcher has a cut of meat: already portioned.

"Depends who's asking."

"Magistra Corvane. Royal Sorceress, Third Circle." She gestured to a stool across the table. "Sit. This will take less time than your resistance."

There was a wit in there somewhere, dry as old bread, and I hated that I noticed it. You spend enough years alone with poison and you start cataloguing people the way you catalogue flavors. Corvane tasted of vinegar and iron. Vinegar for the tongue. Iron for the hands.

I sat. Not because she told me to. Because the stool was there, and my legs were tired, and a taster learns early that the smartest thing you can do with a dose you can't refuse is take it sitting down.

* * *

The kit she unrolled was beautiful, which should have been my second warning. Poison kitchens are full of beautiful things — glass vials like frozen tears, silver scales fine enough to weigh a spider's regret — and every one of them exists to hurt someone. Corvane's kit was the same, only the hurt was dressed in ceremony.

A basin. A lancet of black glass. A bowl of water so still it looked like a hole cut in the world. And a small book, bound in something that was not leather, with pages that whispered when they turned, though there was no wind in the tent.

"Your keeper in the valley," Corvane said, tying off my arm with a cord. Her fingers were cold and impersonal, a midwife's fingers, or a mortician's. "Master Aldous. He burned the writ."

"I know. I watched."

"Did you know he was paid to keep you? Not employed. Paid. There is a difference, in the ledgers that matter." She set the lancet against the inside of my elbow. "Hold still. This will feel like nothing. That is the point of it."

"You're very reassuring."

"I am very accurate. Reassurance is a different trade." She opened the vein with a flick so small I felt it as a rumor of pain. Blood welled, dark and ordinary, and dripped into the basin, and I watched it because watching was better than thinking about what Aldous had been paid.

Aldous, who had fed me my first poison at six years old with a spoon and a story about strengthening my blood. Aldous, who had taught me to catalogue flavors like prayers — *bitterness first, then heat, then the sweet lie at the end that means the poison is winning* — Aldous, who had held my hair back when the doses came back up and never once, in twenty years, told me why my blood was worth keeping alive.

"Your valley," Corvane said, watching the basin instead of me, "is a dead zone. Old wardline, pre-Imperial, designed to suppress resonance. Nothing gifted survives inside it. Nothing gifted can be *found* inside it." A pause. "Which raises the question of why a woman with your lineage was raised there at all, rather than somewhere useful."

"I'm not gifted."

"No," she agreed. "You're something else. Hold still."

She lifted the basin and poured my blood in a thin stream into the bowl of still water, and I will tell you honestly what I expected: nothing. Smoke, maybe. A parlor trick to frighten prisoners. I had spent my whole life being the proof that magic didn't want me. The valley had seen to that. I had made a philosophy of it. *The world is quiet, and I am quietest in it, and quiet is a kind of safety.*

The water turned gold.

Not the gold of coins or crowns. The gold of the inside of a bell, the gold you see when a sound is so large you stop hearing it and start being it. Threads of it spread from the center of the bowl like roots, like veins, like handwriting, and the whispering book on the table began to turn its own pages, faster, faster, and Corvane leaned in with the expression of a woman reading a letter she'd waited her whole career to receive.

"Well," she said softly. "Hello, little vault."

The gold light reached the rim of the bowl and stopped, as if it had hit glass. As if it had been *kept*. And in the middle of the light, where my blood thinned to nothing, I saw — or thought I saw, or was made to see — a shape. Small. Curled. Like a fist. Like a sleeping child. Like something that had been folded up very carefully by someone very good at folding, and stored.

The bowl went dark. The book stopped whispering. My heart was doing something I refused to call panic.

"What was that," I said. It came out flat. Tasters learn to keep their voices flat; it's the first thing the tongue teaches you, that surprise is a flavor you can't spit out.

Corvane was already writing. Her pen scratched with the same dry patience as her voice. "A soul-scar. Dormant. Sealed — and sealed *well*, I'll grant, the binding work is decades old, pre-Imperial craft, the kind they do beneath the mountain." She looked up, and for the first time she looked at *me*, and I wished she hadn't. There was something almost like respect in it. Almost like pity. "You are a vessel, Casper Villalobos. A reliquary with a heartbeat. You are carrying stolen property of the court beneath the mountain, and you have been carrying it your entire life."

"That's not possible."

"It's not *possible*," she agreed, "that a woman of no particular talent, raised in a magic-dead valley by a paid keeper, dosed with immunogens since childhood—" she ticked each item off with the pen, "—would survive to adulthood carrying an unsealed soul. Which is why yours is sealed. Which is why you were *dosed*. The valley wasn't your home, girl. It was your packaging."

I have taken poisons that stopped my heart for eleven seconds. I have taken things that peeled the inside of my mouth like fruit. I have sat very still through all of it with my hands flat on the table because a taster who flinches dies flinching.

I did not flinch now. I want that on the record. My hands stayed flat on that beautiful table and my voice stayed flat in my flat mouth, and inside, something that I had spent twenty years building — the competent loneliness, the ash-flavored safety, the whole small fortress of *I am safe and I earned it* — went down like a wall in an earthquake, quietly, floor by floor, with dust coming out of the windows.

"Say the rest," I said.

Corvane's pen paused. "You've guessed it."

"Say it anyway. I catalogue flavors. I want this one named."

She set the pen down. Folded her hands. Gave me, for one unguarded moment, the courtesy of the truth delivered straight, no vinegar in it.

"Your mother sold her soul to the court beneath the mountain. Or had it taken — the records differ, and the records are the court's, so believe neither. The court bound it, sealed it, and needed somewhere the Queen's predecessors would never think to look. A dead zone. A vessel with no gift of her own, no resonance, no signature. A child who would be dosed with immunogens until her blood was too hostile for any binding to unravel and any thief to touch." She picked the pen back up. "You were not hidden, Casper Villalobos. You were *stored*. Every meal Aldous ever fed you was a lock. Every poison you survived was a padlock. You are the most secure object in the Queen's dominion, and you walked out of your packaging on your own feet, and here you are."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, somewhere, a horse stamped, and an officer shouted a distance that might as well have been in another language, and the war went on without me, the way everything had always gone on without me.

I thought about the broth in the poison kitchen. Chapter one of my life, if you like. The way I had sipped it and survived and called it safety. The way I had catalogued the flavors like prayers — *bitterness first, then heat, then the sweet lie at the end* — and never once asked who was saying the prayer, or to whom, or what it was for.

I had thought I was the priest. I had been the offering.

"The sweet lie at the end," I said aloud, because my mouth had gone ahead of me. "That's what the last flavor always is. The poison's winning note. I used to think it was the poison lying to you." I looked at my own elbow, at the small dark bead of my blood, already clotting, already ordinary. "It's not the poison lying. It's the keeper. Aldous. *Safe*, he'd say, after every dose. *You're safe now.*"

"Master Aldous was compensated generously for twenty years of—"

"Don't." My voice did something I didn't authorize. It cracked, just at the edge, one hairline fracture, and I hated it more than I had ever hated a flavor. "Don't tell me the price. I don't want to know what I cost per year. I've been an asset my whole life; I'd like to keep the invoice unread."

For a moment — I want to believe this, and I am a woman who has learned to believe nothing that tastes sweet — for a moment Corvane's snow-colored eyes did something almost human. "You want to know the worst of it?" she said. "The court didn't sell you to us. We didn't buy you. You are not on *our* ledger at all. You are on theirs. And the court beneath the mountain does not lose property, girl. It *reclaims* it. Whatever you think you're running from, whatever you think you're running *toward* — there is no version of the next year where you are not an asset. The only question on the table is whose."

"Mine," I said.

"Excuse me?"

"My ledger. My line item." I stood. The guards at the entrance shifted, and Corvane raised one finger without looking, and they stilled. "You said it yourself. Sealed. Decades old. Pre-Imperial craft. You've been testing my blood for how long — an hour? And you needed a basin and a book and a bowl of hole-in-the-world water to find what's in me. So here's my counter-offer, Magistra: I am not going to be reclaimed by a court I've never seen, or spent by a queen I've never met, or filed by anyone. I am going to be *difficult*. You should note that in triplicate."

Corvane studied me for a long moment. Then, to my lasting horror, she smiled. It was a small smile, dry as her voice, and it was the first thing she'd shown me all morning that I believed was real.

"There she is," she said, almost to herself. "Twenty years of immunogens and she still has a spine. The binding is better than we thought." She began packing her kit, vial by beautiful vial. "You'll be moved to the vanguard camp. There are people who will want to look at you, and people who will want to *use* you, and the difference between those two groups is smaller than you'd hope. I'd advise you to be interesting rather than useful. Interesting things get studied. Useful things get spent."

"And which am I to you?"

She snapped the kit closed and looked at me one last time, and this is the truth of Magistra Corvane, Third Circle, and I have had a long time to decide whether to tell it: she looked *tired*. Old-tired, in a way that had nothing to do with her face.

"To me," she said, "you are a sealed door in a house that's burning. And I have been wondering, for a very long time, what's behind it." She nodded at the guards. "Take her back."

* * *

They walked me through the camp in the gray light before noon, past rows of tents and cages, and I kept my eyes forward because I had learned one new thing about myself in that tent and I needed to walk on it a while before it bore my weight.

*Stored.* Not hidden. Not protected. Not loved, not even in the crooked way keepers sometimes love the things they keep. *Stored.* Twenty years of surviving, and every survival had been someone else's plan. The valley hadn't been my fortress. It had been my *crate*.

And yet.

Here is the thing they don't tell you about being an asset, about being packaging, about being a vault with a heartbeat: the vault doesn't know what it's holding. The vault only knows that it held. Every dose I survived, I survived *myself*. Every morning I got up and catalogued my flavors and kept my hands flat on the table — that was mine. They built the crate, but I was the one who lived in it, and living in it, day after day, alone, was a thing no ledger ever paid for.

They could have the plan. They couldn't have that.

So I walked, and I did what I do. I catalogued. Woodsmoke and wet canvas. Horse and oiled steel. Under it all, faint as a memory of a memory, something I had never smelled before and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd: something golden. Something bell-like. Something that had been riding in my blood my whole life, and was, this morning, for the first time, awake.

The guards turned me past the cage row, and that was when I saw her — the wolf-knight from the border column, the one in chains, Sera Aldane, watching me pass with eyes like a banked fire. She had heard, of course. Camps are small. *They found a soul-scar in the taster.* She looked at me the way Corvane had looked at the bowl of gold light, and I thought: *there it is. Another person reading my ledger over my shoulder.*

But she didn't look away when I caught her at it. That was the difference. Corvane had looked at what was in me. The wolf-knight looked at *me* — at the woman walking, flat-voiced and flat-handed, carrying something stolen that she had never agreed to carry.

She mouthed something through the bars. Two words. I read them off her lips the way I read poison in a cup, and they were not *hello* or *poor thing* or any of the soft useless things people say to property.

They were: *They'll use you.*

I looked at her, this stranger in chains, and I did the bravest thing I had done in twenty years of being brave in small, survivable doses.

I nodded.

That night, in the vanguard camp, I lay awake under guard and listened to the mountain wind, and somewhere beneath the wind — beneath the camp, beneath the country, beneath the whole loud surface of the world — I felt it for the first time. Faint. Patient. Folded up small in the dark of me like a sleeping child with a fist.

Something in me was waiting to be found.

And for the first time in my life, I found myself hoping that whoever came looking would not be the court, would not be the Queen, would not be anyone with a ledger at all.

I was still hoping it when the alarms started.
