## 6. Teeth in the Dark

I have been in chains before. I want that on the record, because there is a difference between a woman who has worn iron and a woman who was born to it, and the Queen's officers never learned to tell us apart.

The first time was at seventeen, for brawling outside a shrine I hadn't wanted to enter anyway. The second time was at twenty-six, for refusing to burn a village of weavers who had done nothing worse than sing in a language the crown didn't speak. That second time cost me my knighthood, my name on the rolls, and — as of four days ago — my freedom entirely, because it turns out the usurper has no use for disgraced wolf-knights except as exhibits.

So: chains. I know their weight, their music, their particular way of teaching you patience. What I did not know, until this camp, was how much I would hate watching someone else wear them.

The girl — Casper Villalobos, food-taster, last vessel of somebody's stolen soul, though I didn't have all those words yet — wore hers like she'd been fitted for them at birth. Wrists held out. No flinching when the smith riveted the links. She even thanked him, in a flat little voice, and I thought: *there is a person so used to being handled that she's started doing the handling herself.* It made something in my chest go cold and quiet and very old.

I'd seen her eat, too. That was the part that kept me up at night, more than the chains. Every meal, the camp quartermaster would hand her a portion from the officers' pots — standard practice, apparently; the Queen travels with her own poison-proof taster now, like a lucky coin or a favorite dog — and Casper would take one bite, wait, take another. Her face never changed. Not once. A woman eating her own execution, slowly, three times a day, and bored by it.

I told myself it wasn't my problem. I told myself I had exactly one problem left in the world, and it was getting out of these chains before the Queen's sorcerers got around to cataloguing me like they were cataloguing everyone else in the menagerie.

I am, historically, a bad liar. Especially to myself.

* * *

The raid came on the sixth night, which surprised me only because I'd given it five.

You can smell a free-city partisan band coming if you've spent any time around them. Not literally — although Odile Ferrant's people do douse themselves in river-musk to kill their scent, and that you *can* smell, and it is not an improvement. No, what I mean is the camp changes first. The sentries get chatty. The horses stamp. The officers drink one cup more than usual, because soldiers always drink more when they're afraid and less when they're confident, and fear has a lag time of about a day.

Six days out from the valley border, six days of marching captured gifted through country that hated us, and the wine rations went up. I lay in the mud between the wagons with my wrists cuffed behind me and counted the cups.

Three. Maybe four. Enough.

The moon was a thin nail-paring, no help to anyone, which meant it was help to someone, and that someone was about to be me.

The first arrow took the watch-fire guard through the throat at roughly the hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest ones are pretending. I heard it the way you hear rain starting — not the sound itself but the change in everything else. Dogs going silent. A horse screaming two tents over. Then the fire-arrow arced down into the supply line and the whole eastern edge of the camp turned orange, and the shouting began, and I stopped counting anything except heartbeats.

Here is what nobody tells you about breaking your own chains: it is not dramatic. There is no heaving, no sweat-slicked triumph. There is a dislocated thumb — mine, third time, it barely hurts anymore, which is its own kind of horrifying — and then there is a lockpick, and then there is the long boring work of a manacle's mechanism while the world burns around you and nobody looks at the mud where the dangerous prisoner is lying very still.

Where did I get the pick? I'll tell you, since we're being honest, and honesty is the one luxury I refuse to give up along with everything else. Day two in the camp, a boy brought me water. Fifteen, maybe. Partisan look, wrong uniform, eyes that flicked to my ankle-cuffs and back up with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself at his age, standing in front of a burning shrine deciding whether to be obedient or to be *me*.

He'd slipped the pick into the water skin's seam. He'd said nothing. He'd walked away whistling.

Somebody in the free cities had looked at a chained wolf-knight and decided she was worth a gamble. I intended to make that gamble pay out so hard it embarrassed them.

The right cuff gave. The left followed. The ankle irons took longer, because the boy had only sent one pick and I had to improvise the second from a nail I'd spent four days working loose from the wagon bed with my teeth, which is a sentence I hope never to say aloud in polite company.

By the time I stood up out of the mud, the camp was properly on fire. Partisans in the lanes, cutting tent ropes, loosing arrows into the dark at shapes that screamed. The Queen's officers blundering around with swords and no targets. And somewhere in the middle of it, unguarded — because the guards assigned to the prisoners' wagon had run toward the fighting, because guards are people and people are stupid under pressure —

— was Casper Villalobos, sitting bolt upright in the wagon bed, chained at the wrist to a ring-bolt, watching the flames with an expression I will remember until I die.

Not terror. Not hope. *Assessment.* She was tasting the chaos the way she tasted broth. Deciding what was in it. Whether it would kill her.

Gods, I thought. Gods, they really did make you into a tool, didn't they. And the worst part, the part that made my hands shake on the wagon rail, was that I couldn't tell yet whether the tool resented it.

* * *

"Down," I said, and put my hand over her mouth before she could decide whether to scream.

She went rigid. Then — and this is the detail that matters — she went *still*. Not frozen-still. Calculating-still. Her eyes moved from my face to my broken chains to the burning camp and back, running the arithmetic, and whatever sum she reached, she nodded against my palm.

I picked the wagon-lock off her wrist-ring in nine seconds. My hands were steadier than they'd been all week.

"Listen fast," I said, low, close to her ear, because the noise covered us but wouldn't cover conversation. "Partisans. Free-city irregulars, Ferrant's lot by the musk-smell. They're here for supplies and prisoners, and in about ninety seconds they're going to reach this wagon, and if they find you they'll take you, and if the Queen's people find you gone they'll assume the same thing. Either way, tonight you stop being the crown's property."

She pulled back and looked at me. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadows, younger than I'd guessed and older than she should have been, and her voice, when it came, was the flattest thing I'd ever heard come out of a living mouth.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm leaving," I said. "And I don't leave people in cages."

"You don't know me."

"No," I agreed. "But I know what the Queen does with vessels, and I know what the court beneath the mountain does with them after, and neither of those things ends with you alive in any way that counts." I grabbed her shoulder, not gently — gentleness was a luxury and we were spending seconds. "So here's your choice, taster, and I'll only offer it once. Stay. Sit in this wagon, wait for whichever army finds you first, keep being a spoon the powerful dip into whatever they want. Or run. With me, without me, I genuinely don't care which — but *run*, and for the first time in your life let the decision be yours."

Somewhere behind us a tent collapsed in a gout of sparks. Screaming, hooves, the wet percussion of close fighting. Ninety seconds, probably less.

Casper looked at her freed wrist. Turned it over. Looked at the empty shackle like it was a dish she'd been served and couldn't identify.

"I don't know how to run," she said. "Nobody ever asked me to go anywhere. They just *move* me."

"I know."

"It's all I've ever been good for. Tasting. Surviving things that should kill me." Her voice cracked on the last word, one hairline fracture in all that porcelain, and I understood suddenly that the numbness was load-bearing — that underneath it was something so vast and so angry it would level the country if she ever let it stand up.

"Then survive this," I said. "Choose to. That's all running is — surviving *on purpose*."

And she ran.

Not with me, mind. Don't let anyone tell you this story has a rescue in it, because it doesn't. She went over the wagon's tailgate, hit the ground, and bolted — west, away from the fighting, away from the partisans, away from me, into the dark with nothing but a prisoner's smock and whatever she carried in that ash-flavored body of hers. She didn't look back. She didn't hesitate. She ran like someone who had just discovered the muscle existed and found it shockingly strong.

I stood there maybe three heartbeats, watching her go, feeling something in my chest that I refused to name because naming it would have required admitting I cared whether a stranger lived.

Then a partisan crossbow-bolt buried itself in the wagon post an inch from my ear, and I remembered I was also, technically, escaping.

* * *

I killed my handlers that night. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the last courtesy I owe anyone, including them.

They weren't at the wagon. They'd done their duty badly, as I predicted, and run toward the fires, and I found them afterward — both of them, Lieutenant Harrow and the big one whose name I never learned because he only spoke to give orders — at the camp's north edge, trying to rally a skirmish line out of drunkards. Harrow had my file in his satchel. I know because I took it. My sentence, my crimes, the order he'd been carrying for weeks: *on arrival at the capital, subject Aldane to be transferred to the College of Binding for assessment.*

Assessment. That's what they call it when they weigh your soul to see how much of it fits in a jar.

Harrow saw me coming and had time to draw half a breath. "Aldane — stand down, you're making it worse—"

"For who?" I said, and put my knife through the space where his orders lived.

The big one fought better and died slower, and I won't dress it up. War isn't dressing-up. He swung, I wasn't there, I was inside his reach, and after it was done I stood in the smoke with blood on my hands and my old life officially, irreversibly finished, and I felt — do you know what I felt?

Relief. Clean, cold, shameful relief. Like a tooth finally coming out.

The partisans were pulling back by then, torches winking out along the tree line, cargo wagons already rolling. Somewhere in that retreating column was a fifteen-year-old boy who'd gambled on me, and I owed him a debt I intended to pay in the only currency I had left, which was trouble for the Queen.

And somewhere ahead of me, alone, in the dark, in a country full of checkpoints and hunters and things worse than either — was a girl who had chosen, for the first time in her life, and chosen to run.

I looked west. The mountains were black against the slightly-less-black sky. Somewhere past them, the river towns. Somewhere past those, the free cities. Somewhere in between, one food-taster with a soul-scar she didn't know she carried, running blind through wolf country with every hound of the usurper about to be loosed on her scent.

The Queen would notice her missing by dawn. The sorcerers would scry. The Hunt would turn, and it would turn toward her, because bait that runs is still bait, and the Queen had paid too much for that girl's blood to let it wander off unclaimed.

Which meant that if I wanted Casper Villalobos to live — and I discovered, standing there in the ashes of my own chains, that I did, with a ferocity that alarmed me — then I had exactly one job.

Find her first.

I checked the knife, took Harrow's map and his purse, and went west into the dark after the only person in this whole cursed war who'd run *toward* freedom instead of away from it.

I just hoped I could catch her before the dark caught her first.
