## 7. The Smuggler Who Remembers

The river-town was called Brackish Hollow, which Sera said was a step up from its old name, Brackish Hole, and which Odile Ferrant would later insist had always been a joke that no one had gotten in two hundred years. I didn't care what it was called. It had a roof, and the roof had no Queen's seal on it, and after nine days of sleeping in ditches with Sera's knife between us and the world, I would have wept over a roof with a tax collector's seal on it.

We came in at dusk, when the lamps along the water made the river look like it was carrying its own small cargo of fallen stars. Sera walked with her hood up and her chains — the ones she'd broken, the ones she refused to throw away, coiled around her wrist like a bracelet with a grudge — tucked under her sleeve. I walked like a person who had never tasted poison for a living, which is to say I walked like a liar, because apparently that is the only way I know how to walk.

"Rules," Sera said, low, as we passed a fishmonger hauling in his stalls. "One: you don't talk about the valley. Two: you don't talk about the scar. Three: if a woman with grey in her braid and too many rings asks if we're the ones she's waiting for, the answer is no, we're pilgrims, and I'm your mute aunt."

"You're not old enough to be my aunt."

"I'm old enough to be your mute aunt. Muteness is a state of mind."

"Is that why you never say what you're thinking?"

She gave me the look. The one that said *I have killed men for less* and also, buried somewhere underneath, *and I would kill several more for you specifically, which is a problem I am choosing not to examine.* We'd been having that look at each other for nine days. It was, I think, the closest either of us had come to a conversation about feelings.

The woman with grey in her braid and too many rings was waiting for us at the door of a tavern called the Drowned Bell. She was leaning against the frame like she owned the building, the street, the river, and possibly the concept of dusk.

"Pilgrims," she said.

"No," said Sera.

"Shame." The woman pushed off the doorframe. "Because I've got a room upstairs with a bath in it, and a bath is wasted on pilgrims, who are famously filthy on principle. I've also got stew. Also wasted on the principled."

Sera's hand drifted toward her knife. The woman watched it happen with the mild interest of someone watching weather.

"Casper Villalobos," she said, and my whole body went cold, "taster of the valley keep, immune to eleven of the twelve classical venoms, and — if my information is current — recently escaped from the camp of Queen Maren the Usurper, may her reign be short and her tea be bitter. And Sera Aldane, wolf-knight, disgraced, chained, and — this is the part I like — *free*, which must be the strangest thing you've ever worn." She smiled. It was a good smile. It was also, I would learn, a weapon she kept oiled. "I'm Odile Ferrant. I smuggle things. Come inside before someone who matters sees you."

Sera didn't move. "How do you know her name?"

"Because I remember everything, wolf-knight. That's not a figure of speech. It's my gift, it's my curse, and it's my entire business model. Now, are you coming in, or are you going to stand there being suspicious at a woman who has already fed two of your partisans this month and buried a third?"

That got Sera's attention the way nothing else had. She looked at Odile for a long moment, and Odile looked back with the patience of a woman who had been looked at suspiciously by professionals and had outlasted all of them.

"Stew," Sera said finally, as if conceding a fortress. "Then we talk."

* * *

The stew was good. I want that on the record, because everything else that happened that night was terrible, and the stew deserves not to be remembered as an accessory.

Odile's back room smelled of tallow and river-damp and, faintly, of something I couldn't name — something that made the back of my teeth itch, like the memory of a taste rather than a taste itself. She sat across the table from us, rings clacking against her cup, and looked at me the way I had seen the Queen's sorcerer look at me: like a ledger with legs.

Except Odile's looking was different. The sorcerer had looked at me like property being appraised. Odile looked at me like a debt being counted. I didn't know yet which was worse.

"You want to know what the court beneath the mountain did with your mother," she said.

I put down my spoon. Sera's hand found the table near mine — not touching, just near, a border garrison of a hand.

"Say it plainer than that," Sera said.

"There's no plainer. There's only sooner or later, and I've decided on sooner, because later is when people run." Odile turned one of her rings — a heavy band of black iron I would come to know very well. "You've felt it, haven't you. The scar. The thing in your chest that isn't yours."

It wasn't a question. I answered it anyway, because my mouth has always been braver than the rest of me. "It hums. When magic gets close. It hums like —" I stopped. I had been going to say *like a kettle*, which was true and stupid. What it actually hummed like, since the night we'd crossed the wardline and the world had come back singing, was like a second heartbeat that wasn't keeping my time.

"Like a person," Odile said. "Yes. Because it is one."

The room got very quiet. The river kept going outside, indifferent.

"Thirty-one years ago," Odile said, and her voice changed — flattened, the way voices do when someone is reciting rather than speaking, "a woman came down the mountain road from the valley country with a child on her hip and a debt on her back. Her name was Luz Villalobos. She was gifted — a singer, a maker of wards, the kind of talent that doesn't just glow, it *holds*. The court beneath the mountain was buying talents in those years. Quietly. Through brokers. Through people like —" she touched the black iron ring, just briefly — "like me."

Sera had gone very still. Stillness, I had learned, was Sera's version of screaming.

"They didn't buy her gift," Odile went on. "Gifts can't be bought, not really — they live in the blood, and the blood dies with you. What the court buys is the *soul*. The soul carries the gift's seed. Put it in a vessel — a living body, one that can be kept quiet, kept dull, kept *somewhere magic can't reach it* — and the seed sleeps. Waits. For decades, if the vessel is well-made." Her eyes came up to mine, and there was something in them that I hated, because it wasn't pity. It was accuracy. "You were four years old, Casper Villalobos. They paid your mother's debts, gave her a choice of poisons or a choice of paperwork, and she chose the paperwork. She sold her soul to the court beneath the mountain, and they filed it, and they needed somewhere to keep it where no one would ever look. So they made a taster of her daughter. They built you a valley with no magic in it, and a job that would keep you dosed and docile and *dull*, and they called it mercy. They called it a career."

I don't remember standing up. I remember the table edge in my grip, and I remember Odile not flinching, and I remember Sera's hand finally landing on my arm, heavy as a gate-bar.

"Every sip," I said. My voice came out strange. Thin and even, like broth that had been strained too many times. "Every poison. Every year. That wasn't — I built that. That was *mine*. My discipline, my — I survived things that kill dukes. That was the one thing that was mine."

"It was," Odile said, without softness, which was the only mercy available. "And they used it. That's how it works. That's how it always works. They don't take your life, girl. They *invest* in it. And you've been paying interest your whole life without knowing you held the loan."

* * *

I walked out after that. Not far — to the water, where the lamps couldn't follow, and the river made its one patient argument against everything. Sera let me go. I heard her tell Odile, in a voice like a drawn blade, "If you ever handle her like that again —" and Odile's answer, calm as tide: "If I handle her gently, the Queen's men handle her worse. Grief with receipts, wolf-knight. It's the only kind that pays."

Grief with receipts. I stood at the rail over the black water and turned the phrase over, because it was exactly right and I hated it.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about learning your whole life was someone else's plan: it doesn't feel like a door closing. It feels like a door you'd been leaning against your entire life, suddenly opening, and you falling through into a room where all your furniture has been repossessed. My discipline — mine. My numbness — mine, hard-won, worn like armor. My safe little valley where nothing sang because nothing was allowed to. I had catalogued my poisons like prayers, I had built a whole self out of *enduring*, and it turned out the endurance had been the point. Not my point. Theirs. I was not a woman who had survived. I was a *warehouse* that had survived, and the inventory had a name, and the name was my mother's.

Luz Villalobos. I said it to the river. It didn't sound like a stranger's name, which was somehow worse. It sounded like a word I'd known all my life in a language I'd been forbidden to speak.

I had grieved her before, of course — the ordinary grief, the one with a body in it, a fever when I was four and a small coffin and a father who went quiet for a year and then all the years after. I had done that grief. I was *done* with it. And now here was Odile Ferrant handing me a second grief, one with paperwork attached, one that said: the woman you mourned chose to leave you. Chose it in a room with brokers, with a price list, with her four-year-old daughter as the collateral. Chose the paperwork.

And underneath the anger — under it, where I kept the things I didn't taste on purpose — a question I couldn't make go away:

*What would I have chosen, in that room?*

I was still standing there when the second heartbeat under my ribs hummed. Just once. Soft. The way you'd clear your throat in a quiet room.

* * *

Sera found me eventually. She did it the way she did everything — no announcement, just suddenly there, leaning on the rail beside me, watching the water like it owed her money.

"You're not going to ask if I'm all right," I said.

"No."

"Good. Don't."

We stood there. The river argued on.

"My mother sold herself," I said, when I couldn't stand the silence. "To keep me out of the poisons-or-paperwork column, she put herself *in* it. And everyone — the court, Odile, probably the Queen's whole cursed menagerie — everyone's been treating me like a *safe-deposit box* ever since. The valley. The taster's post. Even the Queen dragging me out as bait. I'm not a person in any of their ledgers, Sera. I'm a *location*."

"Yes," Sera said.

"You're supposed to argue."

"I'm not going to lie to you to make you comfortable. That's what the court did for thirty years." She turned her head, and in the dark I could just see the edge of her profile, the old scar through her brow that she'd never explained and I'd never asked about. "But here's what's also true, and Odile didn't say it because it's not in her ledgers. Ledgers say what you're worth to other people. They don't say what you are. You spent your life building something they thought was a cage. It isn't. It's *yours*. Nobody — not the court, not the Queen, not the Steward under that mountain — nobody can take the fact that you survived what you survived. They can only wish they'd thought of it first."

I laughed. It came out cracked and wet and surprised us both. "That's the worst comfort I've ever heard."

"I'm a wolf-knight, not a priest."

"No," I said. "You're really not. Priests tell you suffering has a purpose." I looked out at the water. "Mine had a *buyer*."

Sera was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "Then we go take it back."

And there it was — the thing I'd been circling for nine days, since the night she broke her chains and asked me to run and I ran. Not *you should*. Not *it's your duty*. *We.* I had spent my whole life being a function — the taster, the vessel, the asset, the bait. And this scarred, disgraced, chain-wearing woman kept saying *we* like it was a small word, like it didn't cost her anything, like she hadn't already lost everything once for refusing an order and wasn't lining up to lose it again for a girl who could barely sleep.

"Odile knows the way in," I said. "The tunnels. The ledgers. She said the court keeps records — everything, every sale, every vessel. If my mother's soul is in a vault under that mountain, there's a paper that says where."

"There is," Odile's voice said from behind us, and I didn't jump, because by then I was learning that Odile Ferrant simply *was*, wherever she decided to be, like weather or taxes. She came to the rail, rings clacking, and set something down on the wood between us.

A ledger page. Old, soft as cloth at the edges, written in a clerk's hand so neat it was obscene.

*Lot 114. One (1) soul, female, gifted, ward-singer class. Seller: L. Villalobos. Consideration: debts discharged, one (1) minor dependent placed, valley keep. Storage: Vault of Voices, tier three. Status: dormant. Condition: excellent.*

*Condition: excellent.* Like a horse. Like a cask of wine.

I read it three times. My hands didn't shake. Thirty years of poison training will teach your hands not to shake, and for the first time in my life I was grateful for it, because if my hands had shaken I think I'd have come apart entirely.

"She's not gone," I said. It came out as a whisper. "Odile. She's not — she's *in there*. Whole. Dormant. Waiting in a drawer like —"

"Like a person in a drawer," Odile said. "Yes. I told you I remember everything. I remember her too. The day they brought her down the mountain road. She held your hand the whole way to the gate, and at the gate she knelt down and told you —" Odile paused, and for the first time since I'd met her, the weapon-smile was gone, and what was underneath was older and sadder and much more dangerous. "She told you to count the flavors. She said, *whatever they give you, count the flavors, and none of it can own you.* I've remembered that for thirty years and never known what it meant. Until tonight, when I watched you read that page without your hands shaking."

The river went on. The second heartbeat under my ribs hummed again — longer this time, and I would swear, I would *swear*, that it hummed in a key. Like the first note of a song someone used to sing.

I folded the page. Put it inside my shirt, against my skin, where I used to keep the taster's counter-agent and where I now apparently kept my entire family history.

"Vault of Voices," I said. "Tier three."

"Casper," Sera said, in the voice she used for the beginnings of arguments she intended to win.

"Odile." I turned to the smuggler. "How fast can you get us to the mountain?"

Odile Ferrant smiled her oiled, weapon smile, and it was, for once, entirely genuine.

"Girl," she said, "I've been waiting thirty-one years for someone to ask me that. We leave with the morning tide."
