## 8. Roads Full of Hunters

I have been shot at, stabbed, court-martialed, and once formally cursed by a bishop with excellent penmanship, but nothing prepared me for traveling with Casper Villalobos and Odile Ferrant for six days.

The trouble is not that they argue. Everyone argues in wartime; arguing is how the body stays warm when the fires are forbidden. The trouble is that they argue *wrong* — sideways, at cross-purposes, like two people fencing in different disciplines who keep scoring points off each other's confusion. Odile speaks in bargains. Casper speaks in silences. I speak, apparently, in orders nobody wants to follow, which is what happens when you spend eleven years as a knight and then get stripped of it in front of your own regiment.

"Left fork," I said at the crossroads past Harrowmere.

"The right fork's faster," Odile said.

"The right fork has a checkpoint on it."

"The left fork has a burned village on it."

"Burned villages don't ask questions."

Casper said nothing, which was her way of agreeing with me, and we took the left fork, and the burned village was exactly as advertised: black rafters against a grey sky, a well with a child's shoe beside it, the smell of ash gone cold and sour. Three days old, maybe four. Not the Queen's regulars — they'd have raised their banner over the ruin so everyone knew whom to thank. This was done by men who didn't want credit, which meant men doing work they were ashamed of, which meant the purge had reached the countryside ahead of the army proper. Hunters, not soldiers. The distinction mattered. Soldiers could be beaten. Hunters had to be survived.

Casper stood in the middle of the lane and looked at the houses the way she looked at poisoned broth — cataloguing, filing, refusing to flinch because flinching was a luxury her whole life had taught her she couldn't afford.

"They were gifted," she said quietly. "Weren't they."

"Some of them." I kicked a fallen door aside. "Or sheltered someone who was. Or owed money to someone who was. The criteria have gotten creative."

"And we're walking toward that."

"We're walking toward the free cities, which are the only places in this country where that doesn't happen yet. *Yet* is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Odile crouched by the well, dipped two fingers, tasted the water, and made a face. "Still sweet. Whoever did this wanted them gone, not the land dead." She straightened, and something moved behind her eyes — that memory-magic of hers, turning over, checking its shelves. "There was a cooper here. Maro. He ran grain downriver for me two seasons back. His wife could light candles with a word." She said it flatly, but her hand stayed on the well's rim a moment longer than it needed to.

"You knew him," Casper said.

"I know everyone. It's my curse. Well. One of them." Odile walked on. "Grieve later. We're burning daylight, and daylight's the only thing out here that isn't already burned."

That was Odile's entire philosophy in one sentence, and I hated how often it turned out to be correct.

* * *

We made camp that night in a quarry half-full of rainwater, off the road, no fire. I took first watch because I always take first watch, because sleep and I have an arrangement that mostly involves me lying awake listening for hoofbeats.

Around midnight, Casper couldn't sleep either. She came and sat on the rock beside me, knees drawn up, and for a while neither of us said anything, which was the most comfortable conversation we'd had yet.

"Teach me," she said finally.

"Teach you what?"

"To shield it. Whatever it is I'm carrying. You've been watching me do it wrong all week. Don't pretend you haven't."

"I don't pretend," I said. "It's one of my many unattractive qualities."

"So teach me."

I studied her. In the moonlight she looked exactly like what she was: a weapon someone had spent twenty-odd years keeping in a scabbard, unaware it was a weapon, unaware there was a war. The soul-scar sat under her sternum like a banked coal. I'd felt it the first night in the Queen's camp, when our chains had clinked together and her gift had brushed mine through the iron — a recognition, deep and wrong, like hearing your own name in a stranger's mouth.

"All right," I said. "First lesson. What does grief feel like?"

She gave me a look. "Is this a lesson or an interrogation?"

"It's a lesson. Answer the question."

"Cold," she said slowly. "Heavy. Like swallowing river water."

"Good. Now, what does fear feel like?"

"Tighter. Fast. Here—" she touched her throat, her wrists.

"Better than most knights manage. Now the hard part: what does *your gift* feel like? When it stirs. When the valley's silence lets go of you and something answers from inside your chest. What is that?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Warm. And loud. But not sound-loud. Like — standing next to a crowd behind a wall. You can't hear words but you know there are people there."

"That's the truest description anyone's given me, and I once interrogated a poet." I shifted to face her. "Here's what nobody tells the gifted, because the gifted are usually found as children and trained before they can be told anything useful: your gift isn't separate from you. It runs on the same blood as your feelings. Fear feeds it. Grief feeds it. Rage especially. Every time you feel something hard, the scar opens a little wider, and everything with ears within ten miles can hear the crowd behind your wall."

"So I should stop feeling things."

"No. You should stop *leaking* them. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole discipline." I held up my hand, palm toward her. "When I was a girl, before the Order got me, my grandmother kept bees. When she went into the hives she didn't hold her breath and she didn't panic. She moved slow and even, and the bees read her calm and matched it. Your gift is a hive. Right now every feeling you have kicks the wall and swarms."

"What do I do?"

"You build a door. Not a wall — walls crack, and cracks leak worse than open doors. A door you can close. Feel whatever you feel, then close it behind you." I put her hand flat against her own sternum, over the scar. "Breathe in, and imagine pulling everything warm and loud inward, folding it small, like folding a letter. Breathe out, and shut it."

"That's it? Folding a letter?"

"That's it. All shielding is metaphor. The magic doesn't understand instructions, it understands pictures. Give it a picture strong enough and it obeys."

She closed her eyes. I watched her breathe — in, out — and watched her face do the thing it always did when she tried to feel something on purpose instead of surviving something felt *at* her: a small frown, like the concept itself was mildly poisoned and she was waiting to see if she'd die of it.

The coal under her sternum flickered. Dimmed. Flickered again.

"Again," I said. "You opened it halfway and then got embarrassed about it. Embarrassment leaks too."

"This is absurd."

"Welcome to magic. Again."

On the fourth try, the flicker steadied and sank, and the pressure I hadn't realized I'd been carrying since the wardline — that faint background hum of her, like a struck bell still ringing somewhere below hearing — went quiet. Not gone. Quieted. A door, closed.

Her eyes opened. For one second she looked almost young. Almost pleased.

"It worked."

"Don't celebrate. Celebration leaks." I stood and rolled my shoulder. "Do it again every hour until it's boring. Boring is the goal. A shield you find interesting is a shield that fails."

"You're a terrible teacher."

"I'm an excellent teacher. That's why you hate it."

* * *

The near-capture happened at the toll bridge over the Serpent's Tooth, three days later, and I want it noted for the record that I said the words "we go around" and was overruled by a smuggler with a map and a taster with a schedule.

"There's no around," Odile said, tapping the map with a finger whose nails were painted, gods help me, in peeling gold leaf. "The gorge runs forty miles. The bridge is the crossing. We pay the toll, we keep our heads down, we walk across like honest travelers."

"We are not honest travelers. One of us is a deserter with a bounty, one of us is a fugitive vessel the Queen's sorcerer has personally bled, and one of us is *you*."

"Which is why I do the talking." Odile grinned. "I've crossed that bridge under four different governments. The toll-keeper owes me his second marriage."

The toll-keeper did not owe her his second marriage anymore. The toll-keeper had been replaced by a Queen's man with a ledger, a crossbow, and the particular boredom of someone paid to be suspicious, and he checked papers now, and he had a sniffer-hound — a lean, mean-eyed thing bred to catch the scent of active giftwork, which is a real profession and a moral abomination.

We joined the queue of carters and pilgrims and tried to be furniture. Odile produced forged papers from somewhere in her coat with the ease of a woman producing a coin behind a child's ear. Casper folded her letter, door shut, hour after hour, boring as bread. I kept my hood up and my hands visible, because a knight's calluses read as a laborer's if you don't give them time to think about it.

We were four bodies from the bridge when the hound started toward Casper.

Not running. Interested. The way a dog gets interested in a kitchen. Its handler looked up from his ledger with the slow attention of a man whose animal has just improved his afternoon.

"Move along," I murmured, without moving my lips much. "Both of you. Now. I'll draw it."

"Sera—"

"*Now.*"

And here is the thing I will remember about Casper Villalobos until I die, whichever death I get: she didn't run. Running is what prey does, and prey gets chased. Instead she turned toward the hound, met its eyes, and did the single bravest stupid thing available to her — she *opened the door*. Just a crack. Just enough to let the thing smell not a stolen soul, not a vessel, not treasure, but something older and colder and hungrier underneath: twenty years of poison, metabolized, layered in her blood like sediment. The hound caught that scent, whined as if it had bitten a lemon, and sat down hard.

The handler yanked its lead, apologized to the queue, and marked us as cleared. "Dog's off today," he muttered. "Probably ate bad meat."

"Everyone's eating bad meat these days," Odile said sympathetically, and paid the toll, and we crossed the bridge with our hearts hammering so loud I was sure the planks would complain.

Only on the far side, in the shadow of the toll-house, did Casper's composure finally crack — not into fear, but into fury, which I was learning was her native weather.

"You told me to close the door," she hissed at me, low. "Then you told me to *open* it. Which is it?"

"Both. That's the whole discipline. A door you can only close is a coffin. A door you can only open is a wound. You need both directions, and you needed the second one faster than I could explain it, so I gambled on you figuring it out in the moment."

"You *gambled*. On me."

"Yes."

"With my life."

"With all our lives, yes, and I'd do it again, because the alternative was the sniffer-hound and the crossbow and a cage." I stepped closer, because some arguments deserve proximity. "Listen to me, taster. You have spent your whole life being handled — dosed, stored, marched, managed. I know. I've seen what it did to you. But out here I cannot handle you, because handling takes time and time is the thing hunting us. So either you trust me to make fast calls with your life in my hands, or you tell me now and I'll walk slower and we'll all probably die. Choose."

The silence stretched. Behind her, Odile had very carefully become interested in the river.

"You could have asked," Casper said at last. Quieter. Not backing down — never that — but setting the blade down on the table between us rather than pointing it.

"Yes," I admitted. "And if I'd asked, we'd be in a cage. Sometimes asking costs more than apologizing. I'm sorry. Both things are true."

She held my eyes a long moment. Then something in her jaw eased, a fraction, the way a lock eases when the right key turns partway.

"Next time," she said, "ask *fast*."

"Deal."

From the riverbank, Odile: "Oh, thank the saints, I thought I was going to have to watch you two fight and then I'd have missed the ferry schedule." She shouldered her pack. "Come on. Two days to Vellamare, if the roads stay ugly in the usual ways."

* * *

They stayed ugly in the usual ways. We passed another burn, then a hanging tree with too few leaves for its fruit, then a column of refugees walking the opposite direction with that terrible patient shuffle of people who have stopped expecting anywhere to take them in. Casper watched them pass. She had food in her pack and I saw her hands twitch toward it twice, and both times she gave it away — bread to a child, dried plums to an old man with a soldier's cough — and both times she came back to my side looking surprised at herself.

"You didn't have to do that," I said the second time.

"Yes I did." She said it simply, like reporting a flavor. "It tastes better than not doing it. That's new information. I'm collecting it."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, first time since the court-martial — and she looked at me like I'd done something suspicious, and Odile grinned like a cat, and we walked on.

Near dusk on the sixth day, we crested a ridge above the river plain, and there it was: Vellamare, spread along the water with its hundred towers and its thousand bells, catching the last red light like a city made of held breath. Even at this distance I could hear them — faint, braided, countless — the bells that rang for no decree and no god, just because the city liked the sound of itself alive.

Casper stopped walking. Stood very still, door wide open, leaking warmth like a cracked stove, and didn't fix it, and I didn't tell her to.

"They're singing," she said.

"They're bells."

"They're *singing*, Sera."

And then Odile, who had gone ahead three steps, stopped dead, and all the gold-leaf flippancy drained out of her face at once, and she said, in a voice I'd only heard her use once before, over a burned village:

"That's wrong. The evening bell's missing. Vellamare rings the evening bell at sundown, every day, through plague and siege and my three arrests. It's not ringing." She turned to us, and the light caught the sweat on her lip. "Something's happened in the city. Something bad enough to stop the bells."

Behind us, far down the darkening road, a horn sounded. Once. Then again — and a third time, closer, answered by another.

Riders. Moving fast. Between us and the gates.
