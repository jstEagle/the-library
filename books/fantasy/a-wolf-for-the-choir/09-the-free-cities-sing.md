## 9. The Free Cities Sing

I heard Vellamare before I saw it, which is apparently the correct order for a city that thinks in bells.

We came over the last ridge at dusk, Sera limping on her healed-slowly leg and pretending she wasn't, Odile ahead of us with the particular walk of a woman who has smuggled things through worse places than a mountain pass and considers this one beneath comment. The road was full of other people — refugees, carters, a family of fire-gifted children making their own lanterns out of cupped flames because they could, because here nobody would arrest them for it — and every one of them was walking toward the sound.

The bells rolled up out of the valley like weather. Not one bell, not even one tower's worth. Dozens. Hundreds, maybe, all ringing at once and none of them agreeing, and instead of noise it was — I don't have a better word. Music made by committee. Terrible committee. Wonderful committee.

"That's the Vespers Chord," Odile said. "They ring it every dusk. Every tower, every quarter, no coordination whatsoever. There's a guild that tried to standardize it forty years ago."

"What happened to the guild?"

"The city laughed them into the sea." She shrugged. "Vellamare's whole theory of government is that if everyone makes noise together without permission, no one can ever be silenced alone. It's sentimental. It's also why the usurper wants it burned."

And then we crested the hill and there it was, spread below us in the last copper light: white walls, terraced streets going down to a harbor full of ships, and magic everywhere, *everywhere*, hanging off the eaves like laundry. A woman on a balcony watering flowers that turned to follow the sun after it had already set. A lamplighter who lit his lamps by exhaling. A child skipping rope with a shadow that jumped independently and badly.

Here is what I had trained my whole life never to do: stare. In the valley, wonder was a liability. Wonder got people burned on the far side of the wardline, and inside the wardline wonder didn't exist, so either way you learned to keep your face like a closed ledger.

My face fell open. I stared like an idiot. Nobody flinched.

That was the part that undid me, if I'm honest. Not the singing stones or the harbor lights or the fishmonger whose hands frosted the ice around her mackerel. The part where a thousand strangers looked at me — a travel-worn woman with a wolf-knight on one side and a smuggler on the other, carrying whatever it is I carry under my ribs — and saw nothing to fear, nothing to catalog, nothing to hunt.

One old man selling chestnuts glanced at me as I passed and nodded, the way you nod at a neighbor.

"You're doing the staring thing," Sera murmured.

"I'm allowed."

"You are," she said, and something in her voice suggested she still found it remarkable, that I was allowed, that any of us were.

* * *

The first night we slept in an actual bed, three beds, in an actual inn called the Brine & Bell that smelled of salt and yeast and had no locks on the doors worth mentioning because, the innkeeper explained, "what would you steal that the whole street wouldn't give you back?" I lay there listening to the bells not stop — they thin out after midnight but never quit entirely — and I did not sleep so much as hover above sleep, afraid that if I went all the way down I'd wake up in the poison kitchens and the valley's silence would be back in my ears like cotton wool.

The second morning I woke up anyway, and the world was still there.

Two days. I want to record them properly, because later — after the council chamber, after everything — I will need proof that I had them.

Day one, Odile took us to the fish market, ostensibly to arrange passage for "cargo" that was very obviously three armed women, actually to see an old contact, and really, I think, because she wanted to watch me see it. The market ran the length of the harbor wall. Gulls screamed. And in the middle of it all stood the Weighhouse, where the free cities keep their records, and outside the Weighhouse was a public ledger stand — a great slanted board under an awning where anyone could write anything, in front of witnesses, and have it copied into the civic archive by scribes paid from the common purse.

"It's a memory tax," Odile said. "Every citizen owes the city one true thing per season. Names of the dead. Debts forgiven. Crimes witnessed. The court beneath the mountain keeps its ledgers locked and its memory-mages collared." She tapped the board with two fingers. "These lunatics nail theirs to a wall in the rain."

There was room left at the bottom of the slate. I stood in front of it for a long time with the chalk in my hand.

In the end I wrote: *Luz Villalobos. Sold, not lost. Her daughter is looking for her.* My handwriting shook on the last word. A scribe copied it into the archive without comment, which is its own kind of mercy, and when I turned around a stranger — a broad woman gutting mackerel ten feet away — said, "The soul-trade ledgers? Third tunnel past the Salt Gate, there's a man who's been collecting testimony for years," and went back to her fish, and that was that. No one demanded my story. Someone just handed me a piece of it, unsolicited, the way you'd hand someone an umbrella.

I cried into a cone of fried clams about it. Sera ate most of the clams while I did. That's love, in her language.

Day two, Sera took me to the training yards — "You've survived poison your whole life; it's time you learned to survive the opposite of poison, which is everything else" — and something happened there that I keep turning over.

A swordmaster named Ivet, gray-braided, missing two fingers, put a practice blade in my hand and watched me do everything wrong. Then she stopped, tilted her head, and said, "You're shielding. All the time. Like holding your breath."

"I don't know how to do anything else."

"No," she said. "But you know *that* you do it, which is more than most vessels manage. Show me the shape of it. Don't open anything. Just show me where you hold the door."

So I showed her — the place behind my sternum where I keep the clamp, the habit of decades, the way I press the scar flat whenever anything inside me stirs. She studied me the way I study a suspect broth: nose, eyes, the long patient attention of someone whose survival depends on noticing.

"There's a lot held in there," she said quietly. "You know that."

"I know that."

"And you've been holding it since you were a child, which means your body doesn't distinguish anymore between *holding* and simply *being*. That's dangerous in a different way than letting go. When you finally open — and you will, girl, doors like yours don't stay shut — you won't open a crack. You'll fail all at once, unless you learn where the hinges are."

Nobody in the yard moved away from me while she said it. That's the detail. In the Queen's camp, the word *vessel* would have cleared a circle around me in four seconds. Here, a dozen strangers kept sparring, kept laughing, one man paused to adjust his grip on a spear and went back to it. I was standing in a public square being told I was full of something terrible, and the city's response was the bells: everyone making noise together so no one is silenced alone.

After, Sera and I sat on the harbor wall sharing a jug of cheap white wine, our legs dangling over water full of light.

"They're going to ask you," she said.

"Who is?"

"The council. Tomorrow or the day after. They didn't bring us across half the country out of hospitality." She turned the jug in her hands. "Odile heard it from her contact at the Weighhouse. The council knows what you are. They've known since Odile sent word ahead."

"You could have told me yesterday."

"Yes." She didn't dress it up. Sera Aldane has many virtues and dissembling is not one of them. "I wanted you to have one full day of this city before it started asking things of you. Belonging shouldn't arrive pre-invoiced."

"And now?"

"Now you know, and you still have tonight, and tomorrow's wine, and whatever comes after. That's the trick they don't teach tasters." She knocked her cup against mine. "Take the good thing *while* you know the bill is coming. Otherwise you spend every good day waiting for it to turn, and that's just the kitchens with better scenery."

So I took it. I took the whole evening — dinner at a table where a stranger's grandmother blessed the food in three languages and none of the blessings required me to believe anything; fireworks over the harbor for a saint's day nobody could quite explain; Sera teaching Odile a wolf-knight drinking song with lyrics that would get them both excommunicated from several religions at once; my own reflection in the inn window, flushed and laughing, wearing a face I didn't recognize as mine because it wasn't braced.

I went to sleep unbraced, for the first time in twenty-six years.

* * *

The council summoned us at the ninth bell of morning, which in Vellamare is a courtesy; they could have sent soldiers, but they sent a clerk with an apology for the hour.

The chamber was nothing like the Queen's campaign tent. No throne, no dais. Just a round table, worn smooth, with nineteen chairs and nineteen people who looked tired rather than grand — a harbormaster with ink to her elbows, a fire-warden, two men who were clearly fishermen arguing in whispers, an elderly woman I recognized distantly as the chestnut seller from the market. Democracy looks unimpressive at close range. It also, I noticed, has no single throat to cut.

The Speaker was a heavyset man named Corvin Hale, and he did not waste the city's time.

"Three days ago the vanguard of the usurper's army crossed the Serra ford. Eleven days, perhaps nine, until they reach the outer walls. You've seen what precedes them." He didn't say *burned villages*; he let the words he chose do that work. "This council has three options and no good ones, and we have spent six days failing to choose. Two nights ago the choice changed shape." He looked at me, and his gaze was not hungry, which somehow made it worse. Hungry I knew how to refuse. "Madame Ferrant's letters reached us some weeks ago. We know what you carry, Casper Villalobos. Or rather, we know what the ledgers say you carry, and the ledgers of the court beneath the mountain are the only documents in this war that have never once lied, because lying to yourself in your own asset book is a poor investment."

"The soul-vaults," Sera said flatly, from beside me.

"Are the Queen's engine. Bound souls feed her binding-craft, her wards, her hunters' tracking chains. Break the source and her army is a mob with expensive boots. We believe it can be done. The vaults sit beneath wards that kill any gifted person who approaches — the court built them precisely so that power cannot touch power." He spread his hands. "Only something the magic passes *through* — only a vessel, only dead-scarred, only you — could walk in alive."

The chamber was quiet. Nineteen tired faces. Somewhere beyond the windows the bells rang the hour's remainder, indifferent and gorgeous.

"You should understand what we are not asking," Corvin Hale went on, and I'll grant him this: he understood the difference between a request and a requisition, which the Queen never had. "We are not the crown. We cannot conscript you. If you say no, you will have sanctuary here regardless — that is what Vellamare *is*, it does not stop being itself for anyone. But if you say yes—" he hesitated, the first honest crack in his composure, "—we don't know what yes costs. That's the truth of it. We'd be sending you through a door no one has opened, on the word of ledgers written by the people who profit from the door staying shut."

Silence. Everyone looked at me. Sera had gone very still, the stillness of a drawn bow that hasn't been given a target yet, and I could feel her wanting to speak and I loved her for holding it, and I was already — I realized, with the strange floating clarity I used to feel in the poison kitchens when the broth was bad and my body was quietly winning the argument — already going to answer before anyone could argue.

Because here is what those two days bought, and here is the bill arriving, and here is Sera's own trick turned back on both of us:

For twenty-six years, everything I survived, I survived *for* somebody else's plan. The crown dosed me to use my tongue. The valley kept me to prove a point about safety. Even my immunity — my one pride, my one competence — was just maintenance on a storage jar. I was a pantry. I was a strongbox that walked and swallowed and catalogued flavors like prayers and called it a life.

Then I wrote my mother's name on a wall in the rain, and a fish-gutter handed me an umbrella, and a city of strangers sang so I couldn't be silenced alone, and for two days I knew what it was to belong somewhere without condition.

And the condition arrived, and it turns out I would pay it. Not because the council asked. Because the ledgers downstairs in the dark filed my mother under *assets*, and there is a door beneath a mountain with her behind it, and I have spent my entire life learning exactly one heroic skill: how to swallow something deadly and survive it.

"I'll need to learn how to open it," I said. "The scar. Properly, not just — not the way I've been clamping it shut. Is there anyone here who teaches that?"

Corvin Hale exhaled, and nineteen tired faces did something collectively that I can only call hope, and Sera finally spoke, low and furious and right beside my ear:

"We are going to *talk* about this."

"I know," I said. "After the talk about this, there's going to be another talk about this."

"There are *several* talks—"

But the bells were ringing again — midmorning, all of them, the terrible wonderful committee — and beneath the bells, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the harbor, a new sound: horns. Not the city's. The kind that come ahead of riders with hard news.

A messenger burst through the chamber doors, salt-soaked, horse-wrung, and didn't bother with ceremony.

"Serra ford's fallen. The vanguard's not eleven days out. They're four."
