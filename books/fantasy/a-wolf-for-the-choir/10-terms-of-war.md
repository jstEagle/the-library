## 10. Terms of War

I have sat through a great many war councils in my life. I once endured four hours of debate over the correct height for siege ladders. I survived a general who insisted on opening every strategy session with a prayer to a god he privately didn't believe in, which he'd confessed to me while drunk and weeping outside Vellamare's harbor walls. So believe me when I tell you that the free cities' council was, by the standards of its kind, remarkably honest: they were going to argue until someone shouted, someone cried, and someone threatened to leave, and then they would do the thing they'd decided in the first ten minutes.

The council chamber sat at the top of the Tidehall, a round room with a ceiling shaped like the inside of a shell and windows open to the harbor on every side. Sea wind came through and put out half the candles, and nobody relit them, because Vellamare did not believe in darkness being an obstacle so much as an inconvenience. Nine delegates sat at a ring table old enough that its surface had been carved, burned, stained, and repaired by generations of people who could not agree on anything except that the table should keep existing.

Odile Ferrant had gotten us seats near the wall, which is smuggler-speak for *near the exits*. She leaned against the stone with her arms crossed, watching the delegates like a woman counting purses she intended eventually to lift. Casper sat beside her, very still, hands folded in her lap like a girl at a funeral. Which, given what the girl had learned about her own life in the past weeks, was fair.

And I stood, because I had been offered a chair and declined it. Old habit. Knights stand. Even disgraced ones. Especially disgraced ones — standing is how you prove you're still worth the trouble of looking at.

"Three options," said Delegate Maro of Brassport, a broad man with a merchant's voice and a soldier's hands, "and I'll thank this council not to pretend there are more."

He ticked them off on thick fingers. "One: flee. Evacuate the cities east along the coast road, scatter into the hill clans, and let the Queen's armies take empty streets. Two: fight. Muster everything we have — and I will remind the chamber that 'everything we have' is eleven thousand effectives, two hundred gifted volunteers, and a fleet of fishing boats — and hold Vellamare until the winter storms break the Queen's supply lines, if they break them." He paused, because honesty has a rhythm. "Three: strike. Go under the mountain and cut her throat where she drinks."

Silence, except the harbor. Gulls screaming over something dead, probably. Gulls are honest too.

"The court beneath the mountain," said Delegate Ishven, ancient and dry as kindling, keeper of whatever records the cities pretended to keep. "You propose to assault a court that has existed longer than any crown, any city in this room, possibly any language we speak."

"I propose," Maro said, "to cut off the Queen's supply of soldiers who don't die when you kill them. Every report from the front says the same thing. Her vanguard doesn't rout. Wounds don't stop them. You put a sword through one and it keeps walking, because there's a soul stitched into it that isn't its own, and the stitching doesn't tear. The court beneath the mountain does that stitching. Take away the tailor, and her army becomes ordinary frightened people in armor. Ordinary frightened people in armor, we can beat with our eyes shut."

"With whose eyes?" someone asked from the far side. "No one who goes into those vaults comes back. The court buys souls, delegate. It has bought better thieves, braver knights, and cleverer sorcerers than anyone at this table. It bought them and filed them."

That was true. I knew it was true the way you know a scar's history — by touch.

Here is what the free cities knew, and what I knew, and what the council spent the next hour pretending it was discovering: decades ago, before the usurper took her throne, the crown began selling souls to the court beneath the mountain. Not metaphorically. Actual souls, taken from prisoners and debtors and inconvenient relatives, bound and shipped underground like casks of salt pork. And the court paid in power. Power the usurper inherited with her stolen throne, power she now fed on, power that made her soldiers unkillable and her sorcerers bright as bonfires.

Break the court's hold on that ledger, and you broke her.

"There is another matter," Ishven said, and his voice went careful in a way that made the hair on my neck rise, "which the council has discussed quietly and would now discuss aloud, since the persons most affected are present whether we like it or not." His eyes found our corner. Found Casper. "A vessel can enter the vaults alive. A soul-vessel. Someone already carrying bound goods — someone whose blood carries a scar the court recognizes as its own handiwork. To such a one, the vaults do not close. The wards read the vessel as inventory coming home."

Every head turned. I felt the turn like weather.

"And the council knows of a vessel," Maro said, not quite asking.

"The Queen's own sorcerers confirmed it," Odile said from beside me, mild as milk. She hadn't moved her arms. "Tested her blood themselves, back in their camp, before we liberated her. Very thorough people, the Queen's sorcerers. Sloppy guards, though."

"That is not the council's information to share," Ishven said stiffly.

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "It's hers. But she's shy, so I'm sharing it."

Casper didn't look up. That was the part that scared me. In the weeks since the night raid — since she'd chosen to run, chosen it with a swiftness that had surprised even her, I think — the girl had developed a way of going quiet that wasn't numbness. Numbness I knew; I'd worn it myself for years after Aldane fell. This was different. This was a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

She'd known, of course. Odile had told her what the ledgers said: *Luz Villalobos, sold to the court beneath the mountain, year of the bad harvest.* Told her that the taster's valley wasn't sanctuary but storage — a magic-dead cellar where the court kept its finest bottle until it was wanted. Casper had received all of this with her jaw set and her hands steady, and then she'd walked out to the harbor wall and stood there until midnight, and none of us had been fool enough to join her.

Now the council debated her like cargo.

"She is one person," a delegate from the northern cities objected. "One vessel. If she enters the vaults and the bindings take her—"

"Then the court gains its asset back and the Queen keeps her source," Ishven said. "Which is why I counsel against option three. We risk everything on a girl who was, six weeks ago, tasting soup in a valley."

"Careful," I said.

It came out low, and the room heard it anyway. Shells carry sound. Ishven looked at me with the weary patience of a man who had watched wolves-knights interrupt councils before and expected nothing better.

"Knight Aldane," he said. "Or do we still call you that? Your order struck your name, I understand."

"They did. I kept it anyway. It fit better than the alternatives." I stepped forward, out of the wall's shadow. "You want to talk about her like freight. Fine. Freight doesn't have ears. She does, and so do I, so let me say plainly what your quiet discussions apparently missed: nobody is asking her to do this. Not while I'm standing."

"Nobody's asking," Maro said. "We're weighing."

"Weighing is asking with worse manners."

"Sera." Casper's voice. Quiet. My name in her mouth had a specific gravity I hadn't learned to resist yet and did not intend to learn.

I looked at her. She was watching me with those flat gray eyes, the ones that catalogued poison flavors like prayers, and there was something in her face that made my stomach drop before she'd said a word — the particular calm of a decision already made.

*Don't,* I thought, uselessly, at her, at the room, at whatever gods ran the arithmetic of girls like her.

"Delegate Maro is right about the army," Casper said.

Her voice didn't shake. Of course it didn't. She'd drunk things that would kill a duke without blinking; a room full of politicians was nothing.

"The Queen's soldiers walk because other souls carry them. I've seen it — we all saw it at the toll bridge, the one that got up again after Odile put a crossbow bolt through its chest. You can't win by fleeing. You might win by holding, but you'll bury this city doing it, and half of you are already deciding it won't be you doing the burying." A few delegates had the grace to look uncomfortable. Most didn't bother. "So it's the mountain. And the mountain only opens one way."

"Cas—" I started.

"You said nobody's asking me." She turned to the council, and something in her posture changed — the food-taster's stillness becoming something older and harder, a stillness with weight behind it, like deep water. "Then I'll ruin everyone's evening and volunteer. I'm the vessel. The vaults know my blood. My mother is down there."

The words landed in the room and sat there.

"My mother," she said again, softer, and this time there was a hairline crack running through the calm, thin and bright as the edge of a blade, "was sold. Filed. Kept in a jar under a mountain for twenty years while her daughter grew up drinking poison in the dark, being told the valley was safe. The court owes me a woman. I am going down to collect. I would appreciate the council's assistance with logistics, and I would appreciate the knight—"

She looked at me.

"—stop deciding what I can survive. I've survived everything so far. It's the one skill I have."

The council, being a council, immediately began talking at once, which meant the vote was as good as cast. They'd decided in the first ten minutes. They always do. What they needed was permission to feel resolved about it, and Casper had just handed it to them gift-wrapped.

I grabbed her elbow and steered her out onto the sea-balcony, and Odile followed with the unhurried air of a woman who had seen this exact argument before in several ports and always stayed for the ending.

* * *

The balcony wrapped the shell of the Tidehall like a hand. Below us the harbor lay gold-and-purple in the failing light, fishing boats coming home in strings, bells ringing somewhere in the lower city for no reason Vellamare ever needed. Open-magic city. Bells that sang warnings, lamps that dimmed politely when you wanted dark, doors that recognized their owners. A city where nobody flinched at Casper Villalobos.

Two days she'd had here. Two days of belonging without condition. I'd watched it happen — watched her start walking differently, shoulders down, like a creature unbracing. And now the city had asked her to be a weapon, and she'd said yes before the asking was done, and I was furious in a way I couldn't honestly aim anywhere useful.

"You want to explain to me," I said, "what exactly you think you're volunteering for?"

"Yes," she said. "Thoroughly. Would you like the list?"

"Don't be clever at me."

"It's either clever or crying, and I've budgeted the crying for later." She turned to the rail, to the water. Wind pulled strands of hair loose across her face and she didn't brush them away. "Sera. Tell me the plan that isn't this. Tell me what happens in spring when the Queen's whole army gets here carrying souls that don't die. Tell me how many Vellamares you're willing to spend instead of one trip underground."

"That's not—"

"Or tell me it's my choice and you hate it. Those are the only sentences I'll accept. Pick one."

Gods, I hated her a little, right then. The good hatred. The kind you feel toward people for being right in your own voice.

Because here is the truth I'd been carrying since the camp, since the first moment I understood what she was: I had already done this math myself. Alone, at night, while she slept. A living reliquary walks into the vaults and the wards read her as homecoming. It was the only door into the mountain that opened from the outside. I had turned the thought over and over like a coin I couldn't spend, and buried it each dawn, and told myself I'd find another way, and known I wouldn't.

And she'd picked it up out of my head and volunteered it to a council of strangers before I could object.

"You don't go alone," I said.

"Sera—"

"You don't go alone, or you don't go. Those are the only sentences I'll accept. Pick one." Her own trick, thrown back. It landed harder than I expected; her mouth twitched, almost a smile, quickly put away.

"Odile knows the trade tunnels," Odile offered from the doorway, raising both hands when we glared at her. "What? I do. Twenty years running cargo under that mountain, some of it cargo I'm not proud of. If anyone can get you to the vault door, it's me, and unlike you two I have no noble objection to running away the moment things get interesting."

"And I hold the tunnel mouth," I said. "Whatever comes down after you — the Queen's hunters, the court's wardens — they go through me first."

Casper looked at me for a long moment. The wind gusted; below, the harbor bells rang the evening hour, seven of them, sweet and cracked and stubborn.

"If you die holding a tunnel for me," she said, "I will be so angry with you."

"Noted."

"I mean it. Don't you dare give me something else to grieve. There's a queue."

"I've survived everything so far," I said. "It's the one skill I have."

This time she did smile — small, crooked, gone in a breath, but real. Then it faded into something steadier, and she turned back to the water, and I saw her look at the horizon northward, toward the valley, toward the mountain, and I understood that inside her the arithmetic had finished and the answer had been sitting there waiting for the council to catch up.

"One condition," she said. "Before the vote closes. Nobody down there uses my mother as leverage. Whatever the Steward offers me — her voice, her face, whatever they've kept of her — I need to hear beforehand what it might be, so it can't be a surprise. Odile. You ran souls for them once. Tell me what's in those vaults. All of it."

Odile's face did something complicated. For a heartbeat she looked every year of her age and several of her debts.

"Tomorrow," she said. "There's tea involved. And you'll want to sit down. And, wolf—" she flicked a glance at me, "—you'll want to be elsewhere, because some of it involves your order, and I'd rather you hear it angry in private than furious in public."

* * *

The vote, when it finally came, was eight to one, which in council terms counts as unanimity. Ishven cast the lone dissent and looked pleased about it; someone has to lose gracefully or the winning side forgets there was ever a question. Maro began assigning ships and sappers and a diversionary push along the coast road to pull the Queen's vanguard west, and the Tidehall filled with the particular industry of people relieved to finally be doing the terrible thing they'd agreed to.

I signed the muster rolls with my old name, the one my order struck. It seemed only fair. If I was going to die in a tunnel under a mountain for a girl who tasted poison for a living, the ledger ought to at least record who I'd claimed to be.

Late that night I found Casper on the balcony again, alone, watching the harbor lights. I stood beside her. Neither of us said anything for a long time, because we were both fluent in silence and it's the only language we've never fought in.

"You know what I was going to do," I said finally, "if you hadn't volunteered?"

"Stand up in front of nine delegates and offer yourself instead," she said. "Which would have been stupid, because you're not a vessel, and the wards would have closed on you like a trap, and then I'd have had to come rescue you."

"Would have been very heroic, though."

"Heroic," Casper said, "is what people call it when the plan is bad and the person is dear." She glanced up at me. "I learned that word down in the camp. Watching you refuse a purge order. That's what they sentenced you for, isn't it? Being dear to strangers."

Something in my chest turned over. Twenty years a knight, and this poison-taster with gray eyes had my file read cover to cover.

"Go to sleep, Villalobos," I said. "Tomorrow Odile teaches you what's in the vaults. After that, we've maybe two weeks before the Queen's scouts sight these walls, and every one of them matters."

She pushed off the rail and headed inside, then stopped at the doorway.

"Sera. Thank you for hating it properly. Everyone else just nodded."

Then she was gone, and I stood alone above the singing harbor, and far to the north — so far it was more memory than sight — I could have sworn the mountain had a faint glow around its roots, like a lamp left burning under a bed.

Waiting to be found.
