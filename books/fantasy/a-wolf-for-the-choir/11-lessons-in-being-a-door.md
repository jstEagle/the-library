## 11. Lessons in Being a Door

Odile Ferrant taught me to open my chest the way other people taught knife-fighting: with a great deal of swearing and an insistence that I was doing it wrong.

"Again," she said. "You're treating it like a door you have to batter down. It's not. You *are* the door. Stop fighting the frame."

We were in the cellar of a Vellamare safehouse that smelled of salt, lamp oil, and the particular damp of a city built half on pilings over its own harbor. A single candle burned between us because, according to Odile, light gave the scar something to push against. I didn't fully believe her. But Odile had run souls for the court beneath the mountain for eleven years before she ran from it, and she had a scar of her own — faded to a hairline, closed so well even she couldn't find it anymore — so when she said *push here*, I pushed.

The scar sat under my sternum, roughly where a second heart would go if someone had designed me carelessly. Which, as it turned out, was exactly what had happened. For twenty-six years I'd thought it was a childhood burn. Then a sorcerer's needle had found it in the Queen's camp, and everything I understood about my own survival had curdled into someone else's logistics. My whole life in the valley — the dosing, the immunity, the careful numbness — hadn't been a career. It had been storage conditions.

So: lessons.

I pressed two fingers below my breastbone and did the thing Odile kept describing and I kept failing at. Not forcing. Not flinching. Something in between, like unclenching a jaw you hadn't known was set.

The candle flame leaned toward me.

"There," Odile said softly. "There it is."

It felt — I don't know how else to say this — it felt like hearing a room through a wall. Not sound. More like pressure, or attention. Something vast on the other side of me, patient, aware. The vaults of the court beneath the mountain held thousands of stolen souls, and mine had been bought decades ago, filed away, and forgotten by everyone except the ledger. But it knew I existed now. I could feel it noticing me the way you notice weather.

I slammed shut. Reflexively. Like touching a stove.

Odile sighed and pinched out the candle with her fingers, which told you most of what you needed to know about her pain tolerance. "You lasted four seconds. That's up from two."

"I'm a fast learner."

"You're a fast *closer*. That's the easy half." She sat back against the wine racks, arms crossed, and looked at me with the flat appraisal of a woman who had spent her life pricing things that shouldn't have prices. "Anyone can slam a door when something growls behind it. The work is holding it open when you choose to, and shutting it when *you* decide, not when your terror decides for you. Because in the vault, girl, there will be a moment where you have to keep it open past every instinct you own. If your fear makes that call, you die with the door half-open, and then we've lost both Villaloboses."

"Lovely pep talk. Do you do weddings?"

"Do you want the honest version?"

"No," I said. "Give me the one with numbers."

She almost smiled. It was a rare currency with Odile; I'd learned to bank them. "Opening fully takes you about ninety seconds before bleed starts. Bleed is when whatever's on the other side starts writing itself onto you — memories that aren't yours, grief that isn't yours, names you never learned. Past three minutes, you stop being able to tell which thoughts are yours. Past ten—" She shrugged. "Past ten, the vessel doesn't come back. The soul does. Someone walks out of that body wearing your face, and they'll be very confused about why their hands smell of poison."

"And the elder wants to show me this personally."

"The elder insists," Odile said, and for once her voice went careful, "which is why you should go. Maren doesn't insist on anything anymore."

* * *

Maren Oduya lived in the top room of the Bellkeepers' Hospice, in a bed angled toward the window so she could watch the bells of Vellamare swing in their towers like slow bronze birds. She was ninety-one years old and had been a vessel for seventy of those years — carried her brother's soul from the burning of Harrow Fen when she was twenty-one, and had never given it back, because there had been nowhere to give it to.

She was dying the way vessels die: gently, from the inside out, like a house settling. Her body wasn't failing. Her *edges* were.

"Casper Villalobos," she said, when I came in. Her voice had the texture of paper that's been read too many times. "Sit. No — closer. I want to see if you flinch. Odile says you flinch beautifully."

"Odile talks too much."

"Mm. And yet you're here." She patted the blanket beside her hip, and I sat, and up close I could see what the war council's briefing had not mentioned: a faint luminescence under her skin, moving, like fish under ice. Seventy years of her brother, glowing through her. "Do you know what I remember that isn't mine?"

"No."

"Everything." She laughed, a small dry sound. "That's the joke of it. Everyone thinks carrying a soul means losing yourself. It doesn't. It means gaining someone who won't leave. Tomas is still furious about the winter of '43. He thought we should have gone south. He tells me every day. Sixty-eight years of being told I chose wrong, from inside my own ribs." Her eyes found mine, and they were sharp, amused, terribly tired. "You're going to carry Luz Villalobos."

Not a question. Word travels fast in a city of bells.

"If the plan works," I said.

"The plan will work. Plans always work. It's people who complicate them." She took my hand. Her skin was cool and slightly luminous, and beneath it I felt the faint double-pulse that Odile had told me to expect and hadn't believed until now: Maren's heartbeat, and underneath it, offset by half a beat, another. Two heartbeats. One body. "Now. You asked Odile what happens when a vessel holds too long, and Odile, bless her mercenary heart, gave you arithmetic. Ninety seconds. Ten minutes. Numbers are how smugglers love. I'm going to show you what it actually is, because you deserve to walk into that vault knowing, not guessing."

"Maren—"

"Hold still." She pressed her palm flat over my sternum, over the scar, and opened.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the only prayer I'd ever had, and old habits survive everything.

What came through wasn't power. It was *company*. Maren's edges thinned and for a moment I stood where she stood: seventy years long, a young woman running through burning reeds with her brother's soul already inside her and the Fen going up orange behind her, and Tomas saying *south, we should have gone south*, and her answering him aloud for the first fifty years and silently after that, and the terrible discovery — the one no one warns you about — that a soul you carry becomes more familiar than your own reflection. That somewhere around year thirty you stop being able to locate the seam.

And underneath all of it, threaded through it, the thing she actually wanted me to feel: that none of it had been a cage. She had chosen to hold him. Every morning for seventy years, she had woken and chosen again. The dying wasn't the cost of failure. The dying was just the cost of loving something that much, that long, in a body built for one.

Then she closed, gently, like a hand smoothing a page flat, and I was back in the hospice room with salt air coming through the window and my own single heartbeat loud as a drum in my ears.

"That's what reunion means," she said quietly. "Not getting her back. Getting to *keep* her. Are you ready to keep someone?"

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was no, and lying to a dying woman seemed like bad manners even by my standards.

Maren nodded anyway, as if I had. "Good. Fear means you understand the terms. Now listen, because I only have energy for one more thing today." She pulled me down until our foreheads nearly touched, and her breath smelled of chamomile and something older, mineral, like the inside of a bell. "When you open for her — fully, in the dark, with the court's bindings snapping tight around you both — she is going to be angry. She has been in a box for twenty-six years while her daughter grew up alone in a valley that smells of nothing. Let her be angry. Don't you dare close the door on her anger. Anger is just grief that still believes in justice."

"How do you know she'll be angry?"

"Because I raised a daughter," Maren said, "and I know exactly what I'd be."

* * *

I walked back through Vellamare at dusk, when the bells rang the hour in overlapping rounds and the whole city sounded like it was arguing joyfully with itself. Free cities. Open magic. A child on a stoop made the lamplights dance without anyone scolding her, and a fishmonger haggled in two languages and a third that might have been weather. Nobody flinched at me. I was still not used to that.

At the safehouse, Sera was waiting on the steps, arm still splinted from the gate she'd held during the First Fall, looking like a wolf-knight pretending she hadn't spent all evening worrying. Odile was inside sharpening knives that didn't need it, which was her version of praying.

"Well?" Sera asked.

"I know what I am now," I said. "A door. And I know what's on the other side of me, and she's going to be furious, and I'm going to let her in anyway." I turned my hand over and looked at my palm — poison-calloused, steady, trained since childhood to hold a cup without spilling. "Twenty-six years I survived by keeping everything out. Turns out the whole time I was also keeping her locked in. So."

"So?"

"So I'm done being storage." The words came out quieter than I meant them to, and steadier than I felt, which I've since learned is what purpose sounds like from the inside. "Teach me the rest tomorrow. All of it. I want to open on command, close on command, and hold past ten minutes if I have to. Whatever it costs."

Sera studied me for a long moment — the look she gave walls before deciding whether to scale them. "You understand that if you hold too long, we lose you."

"I understand that if I *don't*, we lose her twice."

Before either of them could answer, the bells changed. All of them, every tower, ringing a pattern I'd learned in two days of war councils and hoped never to hear at night: the vanguard alarm, fast and ragged, rolling in from the eastern wall. Not a probing attack. Not a skirmish.

The mountain had noticed its property walking around loose — and it had sent the bill.

Odile appeared in the doorway with a knife in each hand and her old smuggler's calm stretched thin over her face. "Change of schedule, children," she said. "We go underground *tonight*."
