## 12. The First Fall of Vellamare

I have held gates before. I want that on the record, somewhere other than a court-martial transcript: Sera Aldane has held gates before, and she was good at it, and the only reason she is not still doing it for the crown is that she declined to burn a village full of children, which the crown apparently considered a personality flaw.

So when the horns started at dawn and the war council looked at me like I was a tool they'd forgotten they owned, I didn't argue. Arguing takes time. Time was the one thing Vellamare didn't have.

"Saltgate," I said. "Give me Saltgate and forty men."

The council gave me eleven.

"They're holding the harbor wall," Odile said later, when I told her. She was re-inking her smuggler's tattoos in a doorway, because apparently that's what you do when the end of the world schedules itself for morning — you finish your errands. "Eleven men and a disgraced wolf. That's not a defense, that's an apology with a delay on it."

"It's an hour," I said. "It's supposed to be an hour."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something worse than pity. She handed me her knife. The good one, the one with the river-pearl in the grip that she'd once told me she'd traded a man's memory to get.

"You'll want steel that doesn't rattle," she said. "And I want it back. Write that down somewhere. That's a contract."

Odile Ferrant makes contracts the way other people make jokes. I took the knife because refusing would have meant admitting what we both already knew about how the day was going to go.

* * *

Casper found me at the gate an hour before the vanguard did.

She came down the harbor road with that walk of hers — the food-taster's walk, measured, hands loose, like every step might be the step where somebody slips something into your cup. Eleven years of sipping poison will teach you a way of moving through the world. It's not grace. It's inventory.

"You're supposed to be underground," I said. "With Odile. Learning how to be a door."

"I was." She stopped at the base of the wall, looking up at me on the rampart. "I got as far as the second lesson and then the bells started."

"The bells are going to crack," I said. "You should be under them or away from them. Not here."

"Where's your forty men?"

"Reassigned. Reallocated. Rendered elsewhere." I shrugged. "Armies are weather, Casper. You don't argue with weather, you just stand in it."

She climbed the ladder. Slowly, deliberately, the way she did everything — like she was tasting each rung first. When she reached the top she stood beside me at the parapet and looked out at the gray water and the gray line of ships beyond it, and she said nothing for a while, which is one of the things I like best about her. Most people fill silence like it's a wound they're afraid of. Casper treats silence like a broth she's waiting to cool.

"You're going to die here," she said finally.

"That's a grim thing to say to a woman before breakfast."

"I've watched people die for eleven years. I know the flavor of the ones who've decided." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were doing that thing they do — flat and clear, cataloguing, a poison-taster's eyes reading me like a cup of wine. "You've decided."

"I've decided to hold a gate for an hour," I said. "What happens after the hour is above my pay grade, which is currently nothing, because I'm technically a criminal."

"Sera."

"Casper." I matched her tone exactly, and something almost like a smile crossed her face, there and gone, a fish under ice. "Go back down. Find Odile. Learn to open whatever it is you carry so we can go take it back from the people who sold it. That's the mission. I'm just the part where you get to the mission alive."

"And if the gate falls?"

"Then the city falls, and the mission gets harder, and you get angrier, and honestly? Angry suits you."

It didn't work. Nothing works on Casper Villalobos; she has been lied to by professionals her whole life and she can taste a falsehood through three layers of gravy. She stood there on the wall with the wind pulling at her hair and said, quietly:

"When I was six, my keeper told me the valley keep was the whole world. That beyond the wardline there was only fog and monsters. I believed him for years, because believing was easier than climbing the wall to check." She looked out at the ships. "I'm not six anymore. And I'm not leaving you alone at this gate to be a story someone tells later. So either give me a weapon or give me a job, wolf, because I'm staying."

There are moments in a soldier's life when you understand that command is a fiction — that you never actually controlled anyone, that obedience was always a gift being lent to you, and that sometimes the person lending it decides to spend it somewhere you didn't authorize. I could have ordered her down. She'd have ignored me. I could have had her dragged off. She'd have come back.

So I gave her a job.

"Arrows," I said. "There's a store room under the east stair. Bring everything sharp and everything that burns. And then get behind the inner door, and when I tell you to run, you run, and you don't look back, and you hate me forever if you want, but you run."

"Deal," she said, and went, and I knew even then that at most half of that deal would survive contact with the morning.

* * *

They came out of the water like the sea had decided to apologize for something.

Not ships — the ships stayed far out, black silhouettes against a bruised horizon, launching platforms. What came ashore were the Queen's bound ones. Soldiers with soul-chains sunk into their spines, marching through surf that should have killed the formation and didn't, because dead men don't break ranks. I'd seen them at the purge camps. I'd refused to burn a village rather than march alongside them. Up close, across two hundred yards of harbor mud, they were worse than I remembered: not mindless, that was the horror of it. Their faces were still theirs. You could see the men inside, watching themselves walk, screaming without sound.

Vellamare's outer walls had never fallen. Three hundred years, four sieges, and the harbor wall had laughed at all of them. But walls are stone, and stone is honest, and the Queen's sorcerers had spent the night teaching the stone new words. Where the bound ones touched the masonry, it softened — not crumbled, *softened*, like wax remembering it used to be warm — and hands sank in and pulled, and a section of wall the size of a chapel folded inward with a sound I felt in my teeth.

"Breach!" somebody shouted, uselessly, because everyone could see it, and then the shouting stopped mattering entirely.

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a gate. It isn't glory. It's arithmetic done with bodies. It's plugging holes in a line that keeps developing new holes, and it's knowing — knowing in your gut, the way Casper knows poison — that you cannot win, that winning left the building hours ago, and that the only question left is the exchange rate. How many seconds per soldier. How many soldiers per minute bought. An hour is not a number you reach. It's a number you bleed toward and usually fall short of.

My eleven became nine in the first ten minutes. A crossbowman named Pell — I never learned his whole name, and now I never will — took a bolt through the throat and kept working his windlass for another three shots before he sat down against the merlon and stopped. Nine became seven. Seven became five.

And the bells.

Gods, the bells. Vellamare is a city of bells; they ring for births and bargains and funerals and tides, and the great bell in the Harbor Tower — Old Meridian, they called her, cast from shipwreck bronze — had begun tolling the alarm, and the sound rolled over us like a hand pressing down on the whole world. Then a sorcerer's fire took the tower's upper third, and Old Meridian's voice changed mid-swing, went from bronze to something cracked and wrong, a note split down the middle, grief with a frequency. Every fighter on that wall heard it and understood it in their bones: the city's oldest voice had broken, and the city kept fighting anyway.

That's the thing about Vellamare they never put in the ballads. The free cities aren't free because they're strong. They're free because they refuse, specifically and repeatedly, to be anything else.

* * *

The inner gatehouse was where I made my last good decision of the day, and it was also where Casper broke half our deal.

The breach had become a flood. Bound soldiers poured through the folded wall in ranks, and behind them came the regulars — living men, conscripts mostly, boys from conquered provinces with the Queen's red stitched on sleeves that shook. Behind *them*, the fire. The streets below the wall were burning, and the smoke was the color of old bruises, and through it I could see civilians running for the inner quarter, and I could do the arithmetic faster than any of them: the inner gate was the next hole, and nobody was standing in it.

So I stood in it.

I'm not going to dress it up. There was no plan past *stand here*. There was me, and Odile's knife, and a pike I took off a dead man who no longer needed the irony, and a gatehouse choke-point maybe eight feet wide, which is the width at which one angry wolf-knight becomes a math problem instead of a person. They came in files because the architecture made them, and I made the files stop, and the files piled up, and the pile grew, and the minutes went by.

I counted the minutes. That's the discipline. You don't count kills — kills are meaningless, kills are just the tax — you count minutes. Ten. Twenty. Somewhere in the twenties, a spear found the gap under my shield arm and opened my side, and I killed the man who'd done it and the arithmetic continued. Thirty. My left arm stopped answering around minute thirty-five, and I switched the shield, and the arithmetic continued. Forty.

At some point during the forties, I realized someone was fighting beside me.

She'd come back, of course. Casper. Armed with a kitchen knife and a boat-hook and absolutely no training, planted in the choke-point at my shoulder like she'd been born to it, using the boat-hook the way she probably used tasting spoons — precisely, economically, going for hands and knees and eyes, never the killing stroke, always the disabling one. A poison-taster's philosophy applied to close combat: don't destroy the thing, just make it unable to hurt you.

"I told you to run," I said, between parries.

"You told me to run *when you say*," she said. "You haven't said. Technically I'm following orders." A bound soldier grabbed the boat-hook; she let him have it and put the kitchen knife through his wrist instead. "Also, these ones scream. The chained ones don't scream. Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"Why don't they scream?"

"Because the part of them that would scream is filed away somewhere under a mountain," I said, and saw her go still for half a heartbeat — the word *mountain* landing in her like a hook in a fish — and then she set her jaw and went back to work, and I understood that I had just shown her the mission's face, and that she would follow it now into any dark place it led.

Fifty minutes. Sixty. We passed the hour — the actual hour, the promised one, the whole contractual obligation of my existence — and the gate still held, because the gate was me, and I was still, improbably, upright.

That's when the sorcerer came down off the wall.

He walked through his own fire like a man strolling a garden, robes smoking, face serene, and he looked at the pile of bodies in the gatehouse and then at me, and he did the courteous thing, which was the worst thing: he bowed. Court manners. The court beneath the mountain trains its people well.

"Wolf-knight Aldane," he said. "The Queen remembers you. Refused orders at Thornmere. She said you'd die well." He raised a hand, and the air around it went thick and wrong, magic gathering like a storm deciding. "She asked me to make it slow enough to learn from."

"Tell her," I said, leaning on the pike, blood going through my boot, "that I've stopped learning things from queens."

He struck, and I caught it — I don't know how, I caught it, shield up, both arms, everything I had left — and the blow went through the shield and through me and threw me the length of the gatehouse into the inner door, and the world went white and then very, very quiet.

From very far away, I heard Casper scream my name. And then I heard something else, something I had no framework for: her scream *answered*. From inside her. A second voice, older, furious, rising out of her chest like a bell cracking — and the sorcerer staggered, his fire guttering, his composure shattering into something I'd never seen on the court's people before.

Fear.

The last thing I saw clearly was Casper standing over me with a kitchen knife and a boat-hook and two heartbeats visible in the sweat on her throat, facing down a sorcerer of the court beneath the mountain, and the last thing I thought was not a prayer, because I've never been a praying woman.

It was a bet. I bet everything I had left — which wasn't much, a few minutes of blood and breath — on the girl who'd learned to survive poison by drinking it. Hold on, I told her, or told myself, or told whatever gods run the arithmetic of gates. Hold the gate. Buy the hour. Get to the mountain.

Get her mother back.

Then the dark came up over the wall of me like tide, and I let it, because I'd earned the rest, and because — for the first time since Thornmere — I trusted someone else to hold the line.
