## 13. Underground, Under Contract

The tunnels smelled like the inside of a ledger: dust, tallow, old ink, and something faintly sweet that I chose not to identify. Three hundred years of commerce had soaked into the stone, and commerce, it turns out, has a smell — like a merchant's coat left on a hook too long.

"Cheerful place," Odile said behind me, lifting her lantern so its light crawled up walls carved with tally-marks. Not decoration. Accounting. Someone had counted their way down this passage, stroke by stroke, and never bothered to make the counting beautiful.

I touched one mark as we passed. The stone was warm.

Everything down here was warm. That was the worst part. The mountain was supposed to be cold and dead and indifferent, but it had the temperature of a sleeping animal, and every few steps I felt the scar under my sternum lean toward the dark like a dog toward an open door.

*Not yet,* I told it. *We haven't even gotten to the part where they ruin my life retroactively.*

"You're doing the thing again," Sera said. She walked at my shoulder because her ribs wouldn't let her walk anywhere else — three cracked, one broken, courtesy of holding a gate alone against the Queen's vanguard while Vellamare burned around her ears. The free cities' healers had wanted to keep her abed for a month. Sera Aldane had informed them, with the flat courtesy of a woman reading out execution orders, that she would be descending into the earth after the only person alive who could open the vaults, and they could either splint her or get out of her way. They'd splinted her. She hadn't thanked them. Sera didn't thank people; she remembered them, which she considered a higher honor, and she was right.

"What thing?" I said.

"The talking-to-your-own-chest thing."

"It's called internal dialogue. Very fashionable among the deeply traumatized."

"Mm." Her eyes did the wolf-thing in the lantern light, catching gold and giving nothing back. "Do it quieter."

Odile snorted. "Let her talk to her chest. It's the most honest conversation anyone's having on this expedition."

We went down.

* * *

The trade tunnels had been built by smugglers, back when smuggling was a respectable family business instead of a hanging offense, and then they'd been *bought*. That was the word Odile kept using, in the tone other people used for plague ships. Bought. The court beneath the mountain didn't conquer; it acquired. It had acquired the tunnels, the tolls, the towns above them, the debts of everyone who'd ever traded through them, and — according to the ledger pages Odile had memorized and burned, twice, once for safety and once for spite — several thousand human souls.

Including mine. Well. My mother's. Filed under my name, because I was the warehouse.

I tried not to think about it as a warehouse. I failed. I'd been thinking about it for six days straight, ever since Odile had recited the entry from memory in a safehouse that smelled of river mold:

*Villalobos, Luz. Gifted, class indeterminate. Sold by next of kin, price settled in grain futures and one pardon. Transferred to Court custody, year 41 of the old reign. Vessel assigned: Villalobos, Casper. Status: dormant. Location: valley keep, food-taster's wing.*

*Food-taster's wing.* They'd known. They'd placed me myself, personally, in the one valley in the world where magic couldn't reach, and given me a job that required me to poison myself daily, and I had thought — all those years, sipping broth while dukes watched me live — that I was clever. That I'd built my immunity brick by brick, alone, out of stubbornness. And some clerk beneath a mountain had written *vessel maintenance* in a margin somewhere and gone to lunch.

"They'll have records down here," Odile said now, as the tunnel widened into what had once been a counting-house cut into the rock — rotted shelves, a counter worn smooth by centuries of elbows, and, miracle of miracles, a strongbox whose lock surrendered to her picks like it had been waiting years for someone competent. "Originals. The copies upstairs are for lawyers. These are for *them*."

"For them meaning?"

"The ones who think paper is permanent." She got the lid open and lifted out bundles wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string gone soft as hair. "Paper isn't permanent. Paper is a hostage situation. But it remembers everything you made it say."

Sera posted herself at the tunnel mouth with her sword across her knees — sitting down like a normal injured person for possibly the first time in her life, and hating it — while Odile unwrapped the first bundle on the counting-house counter and I held the lantern.

Ledgers. Dozens of them. Leather covers stamped with a seal I recognized without wanting to: a closed hand. The court's mark. A hand that gives nothing and takes everything, and keeps excellent records of both.

"Start anywhere," Odile said quietly. "It doesn't matter where. It's all the same book."

She was right.

* * *

I read in the dark because the lantern only reached so far and because I couldn't stop, and the dark seemed appropriate. Names. So many names. Not numbers — numbers would have been easier, numbers would have let me keep the distance I'd spent thirty years building like callus. Names, ages, gifts, prices. Some entries were a line. Some were paragraphs, because someone's gift had been interesting enough to describe.

*Aldous Merrin, weather-sense. Sold by his guild after bankruptcy. Price: forgiveness of debt, two casks of wine. Note: subject wept. Note: stopped.*

*Maren Ostreva, dream-walker. Purchased for the Queen Mother's insomnia. Price: a title, unused. Note: effective. Note: depleted. Note: discarded.*

Discarded. There was a whole column code for it. *DPL*, Odile said, when she saw me stop on it. Discarded, depleted. You don't want to know what the third letter stood for in the worst cases. I wanted to know. I looked. I wished I were the kind of person who could unlook.

My hands were steady. That was the horror of it — thirty years of tasting poison had trained my body to stay calm while it absorbed terrible things, and here was the training's finest hour, my pulse barely elevated, my breathing even, while I read the inventory of thousands of stolen people. Somewhere a version of me was screaming. She was muffled under layers of professional composure, and I hated her a little, and I needed her, because if my hands shook I'd drop the lantern, and the lantern was the only light between us and a very long way down.

"Casper." Sera's voice from the tunnel mouth, low. "Your heart rate."

"I don't have a heart rate. I have a serviceable rhythm."

"That's not — " She exhaled through her nose. "Breathe slower anyway."

"She's right," Odile said, not looking up from the page she was copying. "Anger is fuel, but it burns fast. You want coals for what's coming. Bank it."

"I'm not angry."

Three people in a hole in the ground listened to me lie.

I turned pages. The ledgers went back further than the old reign, further than the dynasty before it, back to entries written in a script so old Odile had to squint at it and mutter translations. The court beneath the mountain had been buying souls since before there was a crown to usurp. The usurper Queen wasn't the disease. She was a customer.

That was the thought that finally found the target. Not rage — rage had been with me since the safehouse, hot and useless, filling my mouth like blood. This was colder. This was arithmetic.

Every name in these books had been sold by someone. A guild, a parent, a creditor, a king. The court never stole. Stealing leaves witnesses with grievances. The court *bought*, and the whole country had lined up to sell, and then the country had the gall to hunt the gifted like vermin when the gifted were the only stock it had ever traded in. The Queen's Great Hunt wasn't a purge. It was a restocking.

And I had been the reserve supply. Kept fresh. Kept *dormant*. Kept in the one place magic couldn't check the merchandise.

I set down the ledger, carefully, the way you set down a weapon you're not done with.

"Find mine," I said.

Odile looked up. "Casper — "

"Find my mother's entry. All of it. Every note, every transfer, every margin comment some clerk made about her while she was — whatever they did to her. If I'm going to burn this place's claim off my chest, I want to know exactly what I'm burning." I heard my own voice go flat and precise, the taster's voice, the one that described arsenic to a duke without letting him see his death coming. "And I want the original. Copies can be forged. I want the page with her name in their own hand."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Odile nodded slowly, and started sorting bundles by date, and Sera — who had been watching me from the tunnel mouth with that stillness she wore like armor — said nothing at all, which from Sera was a benediction.

It took two hours. The lantern burned low. We fed it oil from Odile's pack and kept working, and the mountain breathed its warm animal breath around us, and somewhere far below, things with excellent manners waited for their property to come home.

Then Odile made a sound — small, bitten-off — and turned a ledger toward me, and pointed.

*Villalobos, Luz.*

Her entry ran four pages. Four pages, when Maren Ostreva the dream-walker had gotten eight lines. Someone had found Luz Villalobos *interesting*.

I read it standing up, because I knew if I sat I might not stand again for a long time.

Sold in the last year of the old reign, by her husband — my father, though the ledger didn't know him as anything but *next of kin, cooperative* — for debts I'd never known he had. Price: settlement of accounts, plus a consideration. *Consideration* was their word for a bonus. My father had gotten a bonus for selling my mother, and then he'd taken me to the valley and left me at the keep's gate with a note pinned to my coat, and I had grown up thinking I'd been abandoned out of weakness. He'd been paid twice. Once in coin, once in not having to look at what came next.

The notes began clinical. *Subject's gift resists classification. Soul unusually dense. Recommend long-term storage over use.* Then less clinical. *Subject requests information regarding offspring. Request denied. Subject repeats request. Denied. Subject ceases eating. Force-fed. Subject resumes eating upon learning vessel remains healthy.* 

My vision blurred. I blinked it clear and read on, because she'd fought, she'd fought for *years*, in whatever room they kept her, and the least I owed her was to read every line.

*Note: subject refers to vessel as "my girl" despite having never met her. Recommend no further contact; attachment destabilizes storage quality.*

*Recommend.* They'd weighed whether letting a mother know her daughter lived might damage the merchandise. And decided against it. And I had spent my whole life believing no one had ever come looking.

The final entry was recent. Recent enough that the ink still looked wet in the lantern light, recent enough to have been written after the Queen announced her Hunt, after the sorcerer tested my blood in that camp and found the scar and smiled like a man finding money in an old coat.

*Vessel status: active, en route. Prepare reception. Steward advises: do not threaten, do not coerce. Property returns willingly when it believes it is choosing.*

I read that line three times.

*Property returns willingly when it believes it is choosing.*

Every choice I'd made since the camp — running with Sera, trusting Odile, volunteering at the war council, walking into this mountain — all of it mapped onto their plan like a key into a lock they'd cast decades ago. Even my rebellion had a file number.

Except.

I put my hand flat on the page, over the wet ink, and felt the scar under my sternum turn over like something waking, and I understood something the Steward's clerks had failed to record, because it wasn't the kind of thing that goes in ledgers.

They thought the scar was a door they owned. But a door doesn't care who built it. A door opens both ways. And I had spent thirty years learning, sip by sip, dose by dose, how to take a lethal thing into my body and survive it — how to hold poison without dying, how to be the vessel that outlasts what's poured into it. They'd trained me for this without knowing it. The valley had trained me. Every bowl of laced broth had been a rehearsal.

They expected property. They were going to get a taster.

"Odile," I said. My voice came out level. Coals, banked. "Copy everything. Especially the last page."

"And then?"

"And then we go down and introduce ourselves." I closed the ledger. Somewhere below us, deep in the warm dark, something vast and courteous felt the small concussion of a claim being tested, and shifted in its sleep. "They're expecting a delivery."

Sera rose from the tunnel mouth, sword in hand, ribs and all, and grinned at me with too many teeth.

"Good," she said. "I've always wanted to rob a bank."
