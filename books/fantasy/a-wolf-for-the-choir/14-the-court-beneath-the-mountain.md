## 14. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not want us inside it. I want to make that clear from the start, because everything that happened afterward — every soft word, every offered chair — should be understood against the fact that the stone itself resisted us the whole way down, like a throat trying to swallow in the wrong direction.

The trade tunnels Odile had promised turned out to be real, which surprised me more than they should have. She had a way of describing her past that made you assume ninety percent of it was embellishment and then feel foolish when the remaining ten percent turned out to be load-bearing. The tunnel mouth hid behind a dead watchtower three miles south of where the Queen's hunters were still picking through Sera's blood on the rocks, and it went down at a slope so gentle and so long that my legs ached worse than they had crossing the war roads.

We walked for the better part of a day underground. Odile carried a lamp with oil that burned green and gave off almost no heat, and she refused to say what it burned. Sera, bandaged and grey-faced and leaning more on me than either of us acknowledged, kept one hand on the wall the entire time.

"She's listening," Sera said once, when I asked why.

"The mountain?"

"The court. It's not a place, it's an *attention*. Every footstep we take down here is being filed."

I put my own hand on the wall after that. The stone was cool and faintly wrong, the way a room is wrong when someone has just left it. And under my palm — I can't describe this any better now than I could then — under my palm the rock had a pulse. Slow. Patient. The heartbeat of something that had never once needed to hurry, because everything it wanted was already owed to it.

The scar under my sternum answered that pulse like a struck bell answering its twin. I'd gotten better at closing it — Odile's lessons had been brutal, repetitive, effective — but down there, in the dark, in the deep register of that mountain-heartbeat, keeping it closed felt less like discipline and more like holding a door shut against a polite, enormous tide.

"Talk to me," I said, because silence made the pull worse. "Tell me something useless."

Odile, ahead in the green light, obliged without being asked twice. She told us about a bishop she'd smuggled across a border in a wine cask who got out at the wrong end and thanked her for the voyage. She told us about a canal town where the toll was paid in secrets and she'd gone broke twice. She told us about her name, briefly, and then stopped herself, and none of us pressed, because we were two days from the vault and some things you don't spend early.

It was Sera who finally said what all of us were circling.

"When we get down there," she said, "they're going to be courteous. That's the part people don't survive. A monster that snarls, you can fight. This one will offer you a seat."

"I've sat in fine chairs before," I said. "I tasted for the crown. I know how courtesy works. Courtesy is what power wears when it wants you to forget the order things happen in."

"And what order is that?"

"Its comfort first," I said. "Your consent second. Your body third, if there's time."

Sera looked at me for a long moment in the green light. Then she nodded, which from Sera was roughly equivalent to applause.

* * *

The tunnels ended at a door that had no business existing underground.

It was tall — taller than any door I'd seen outside a cathedral — and made of pale wood that glowed very faintly, like something remembered rather than seen. There was no frame around it; the wood simply grew out of the rock as if the mountain had been waiting for it. Carved into its surface, in lines so shallow you had to be close to read them, were names. Thousands of names. I recognized the style before I recognized a single word, because I'd spent eleven days reading ledgers in the dark by green lamplight, and this was ledger-hand. Neat. Alphabetical. Filed.

My mother's name was somewhere on that door. I didn't look for it. Some part of me understood that if I found it carved there — *Luz Villalobos*, entered like an invoice — I would do something irreversible with the knife I'd stopped carrying openly but hadn't stopped carrying.

"Don't knock," Odile said. "They know."

The door opened on its own, inward, with the hush of a well-run household.

And the court beneath the mountain received us.

I had prepared myself for monsters. I want credit for that. I had spent the whole walk down imagining things with too many joints and voices like grinding stone, because that's what you imagine when someone says *court beneath the mountain* in the tone Odile used. What waited instead was a foyer.

That's genuinely the best word. A foyer, vast enough that the ceiling vanished into darkness above lamplight range, floored in dark polished stone that reflected us upside-down like black water. Warm air moved through it gently, carrying the smell of bread and woodsmoke and, absurdly, orange peel. Somewhere, unseen, a string quartet was playing — badly, or rather humanly, with small imperfections no enchantment would allow. There were chairs. There were side tables. There was a coat stand.

There were people. Or figures shaped like people, moving through the space with trays and ledgers and unhurried purpose, and every one of them was beautiful in the specific way of things that have been maintained rather than lived in. None of them looked at us directly. That was the first mercy, and I understood it as a mercy, and therefore as a message: *you are not guests. Guests get looked at.*

A woman came toward us across the black floor. She was older than the others — or dressed older, in grey wool and sensible boots, hair pinned with what looked like an ordinary iron pin. Her face was kind the way a hospital corridor is kind. She stopped a respectful distance away and inclined her head.

"Taster Villalobos," she said. "Welcome home."

Every answer I'd rehearsed died at once. In their place my mouth produced, with the flat precision of eleven years of professional habit: "I'm not poisoned, if that's a concern."

One corner of her mouth moved. It might have been amusement. It might have been a hinge working.

"We know precisely what you are," she said. "Asset 4471, vessel-grade, dormant. You've been kept beautifully." Her eyes went, without hurry, to my chest — to the scar she couldn't see and saw anyway. "Eleven years past your sister assets' decay. Whatever they fed you in that valley, we should thank them."

"Sister assets," Sera repeated, in the voice she used just before killing someone.

"The soul does not sit still in a living body," the woman said, mildly, as if explaining weather. "It erodes the vessel. Most don't hold. Yours held. Come. You've walked a long way and the Steward dislikes receiving tired property; it makes the negotiations sentimental."

She turned and walked, and we followed, because the alternative was standing forever in a foyer designed to make standing still feel like surrender.

"That was the Steward?" I whispered to Odile.

"No. That was a receptionist." Odile's face, in the shifting green of her own lamp, was carefully blank. "The Steward is worse."

* * *

The court unfolded around us as we walked, and I'll admit — because this chapter of my life deserves honest bookkeeping — that parts of it were lovely.

There were galleries hung with paintings that moved at the speed of seasons. There was a garden under a vaulted cavern where the trees grew silver-leafed and the light came from nowhere and fell like late afternoon forever. There were children's voices somewhere, laughing, and I hated myself for the half-second of warmth that gave me before Odile caught my elbow and murmured, "Don't count those voices. Count the ledgers. Everything happy here has a filing number."

We passed a hall of doors, each one carved with names like the entrance door, each one humming faintly. We passed a chamber where a choir of perhaps thirty figures stood in perfect rows, mouths open, producing no sound at all — and every open mouth pulled, subtly, toward the same distant point, like iron filings finding north. Sera made a sound low in her throat and put her hand on her sword hilt and kept it there for a hundred paces.

Then the corridors widened one last time, and we were admitted to the Steward.

I don't know what I expected. Something grander, certainly. Something with more teeth visible.

What sat behind a desk of black stone in a room the size of a generous library was — a clerk. Grey-robed, ageless in the way of things that have decided their age and been believed, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles it did not need, perched on its nose purely, I think, to give visitors something to focus on besides its eyes, which were the color of the black floor and reflected you upside-down in exactly the same way.

"Sit," it said warmly, indicating three excellent chairs. "Please. You especially, Taster. You've earned a seat more than most."

I sat. So help me, I sat, and the chair fit me like it had taken measurements.

"Tea?" said the Steward. "No — forgive me — you're a taster, tea is a professional insult. Water, then. From our own spring. Nothing in it. Nothing has needed to be in anything here for six hundred years, because nothing gets in or out without our say."

Water arrived. It was, I noted with professional despair, the finest thing I had ever drunk.

"You came for the vault," the Steward said. "Of course you did. You crossed a war to do it. I admire that enormously — I keep records of admiration, it's a much-neglected emotion in the literature. And I want you to understand, before we speak of terms, that you are welcome here. Truly. Not as prisoners — please, dismiss your knight's hand from her sword, Ser Aldane, it tires both of you — but as what you are. Returning goods. And returning goods are treated better than guests in this house. Guests can leave. Goods must be maintained."

"There it is," Sera said softly.

"There it always is," the Steward agreed pleasantly. "Now. Let me tell you what you actually walked into, because your smuggler has told you history and history is a rough draft of accounts."

It opened a ledger on the desk. I knew the handwriting on its pages the way you know your own face in a bad mirror.

"In the year your valley was warded," the Steward said, "a woman named Luz Villalobos sold us something she could not otherwise have afforded to protect. Do you know what a gifted woman with an infant daughter could buy, in those years, with one soul's worth of collateral? Passage. Papers. A wardline drawn around an entire valley, paid in advance, permanent. The crown was hunting then too, Taster. Different queen, same hunt. Your mother could not shield you both. So she pawned herself."

The words went in like a blade going into water — no resistance, all depth.

"She bought you twenty-two years of silence," the Steward continued, "with the only currency the court accepts. And the interest on that loan has been accruing ever since. Which brings us," and here it closed the ledger and folded its ink-stained hands, "to my offer. Not demand. Offer. I deal entirely in offers. Demands create martyrs and martyrs are terrible collateral."

"Say it," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Stay," said the Steward. "Freely. That is the entire shape of it. Stay of your own will, sign yourself into the ledger with your own hand, and everything else follows. Your mother's soul comes out of the vault — today, tonight, whenever you wish. She is housed in a vessel upstairs within the week; we grow them well here, better than the free cities ever managed. You will have her *whole*, Taster. Not a whisper, not a haunting. A woman who can hold your hand. Who can tell you why she really named you Casper. Who can apologize properly, over years, the way these things deserve."

The scar under my sternum was wide open. I hadn't felt it open. Somewhere in the middle of that speech, my discipline had simply set down its tools and walked out.

"And in exchange," I managed.

"You live here," the Steward said. "In comfort. With your family complete. You maintain the asset — her, and eventually the arrangement itself, because vessels need vessels, and who better than the daughter of the house? You will want for nothing. You will grieve for nothing. Everything you crossed a war to steal, we will simply... hand you. All we ask is that the door stay shut behind you while you enjoy it."

Silence. The quartet, wherever it was, had arrived at something slow and sweet.

"And if I refuse?" I asked. "If I take her the hard way? Break your vault, burn your ledgers, walk out?"

The Steward removed its unnecessary spectacles and polished them, unhurried.

"Then you may certainly try," it said, "and some of you would likely even reach the surface. But consider the arithmetic first, Taster, because arithmetic has always been your gift — you counted poisons in broth for eleven years and survived. If you force it, your mother spends decades relearning how to be a person inside a grown body in a city under siege, and you spend those same decades watching her flinch at your face because you wear the body she died escaping. If you accept, she wakes whole tomorrow, and the war passes over this house like weather. I am not threatening you. I am showing you the two futures and letting you pick, which is more consideration than any throne has ever shown anyone."

And that was the trap, and it was a good one, and I want it recorded that I saw it clearly even as it closed: the offer was cruel precisely because it was kind. Every word of it true. She would wake whole. I would have her. The cost was only the thing I'd crossed a country to learn — that belonging given as terms of contract is just ownership wearing its good coat.

I thought of Sera breaking her chains in the dark and asking me a question with no comfortable answer. I thought of Odile burning her name out of a ledger page by page. I thought of Vellamare's bells, and how nobody in that city had flinched at me, and how nobody there had asked me to be anything in exchange for it.

"Here is my counteroffer," I said.

The Steward's upside-down eyes brightened with what looked like genuine pleasure. Clerks love negotiation the way wolves love running.

"Show me my mother," I said. "Right now. Not the vault. Her — whatever form they keep her in. You want my signature? I don't sign things I haven't inspected. Ask your ledgers. They'll confirm I'm the type."

For a moment — one full breath — the court's warm air went cold as the tunnel, and every beautiful figure in the doorway behind us stood perfectly, perfectly still.

Then the Steward smiled, replaced its spectacles, and rang a small bell on the desk.

"Clerk's instinct," it said approvingly. "Inspect before purchase. Very well. Asset 4471, condition of goods verified prior to transfer — I'll note the diligence in your favor."

It gestured, and far across the black floor, a door I had not noticed opened onto a stairway going down, down, down, lit with the same green fire as Odile's lamp.

"Come along, then," said the Steward, rising, ink-stained fingers smoothing its robes. "Let's go meet your mother. And Taster —"

It paused at the head of the stairs, and its reflection in the black floor looked up at me while it looked down.

"— do try not to recognize her too quickly. The last vessel who saw her whole wept for three days, and it plays havoc with the paperwork."
