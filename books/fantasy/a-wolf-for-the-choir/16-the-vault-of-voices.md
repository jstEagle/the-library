## 16. The Vault of Voices

The last door did not look like a door. It looked like a ledger.

That was the joke the court beneath the mountain never tired of telling, I think — that everything down here was filed, shelved, catalogued, and the people most of all. The tunnel behind me had been lined with them: bound volumes in iron cages, each spine stamped with a year and a region and a number where a name should have been. I had walked a mile of names. I had found my own twice.

Now the corridor ended in a slab of black stone with no handle, no hinge, only a recess shaped like an open palm at the height of my chest.

"You are expected," said the Steward's voice from everywhere and nowhere, the way it had been saying it for three days, patient as dripping water. "Place your hand."

I placed my hand. Of course I did. I had come four hundred miles to do exactly this, and if the stone wanted my palm first, it could have it. My body was a professional courtesy by now. I'd spent twenty years feeding poisons to it on someone else's schedule; one more transaction barely registered.

The stone drank warmth out of my skin and then, with a sound like a very large book closing very politely, opened inward.

Cold came out. Not temperature-cold. Archive-cold. The air of a place that has been holding its breath for decades because breathing would disturb the collection.

And then the voices.

I want to be precise about this, because later — after everything — when people asked what the vault was like, I never had a clean answer. It wasn't screaming. Screaming would have been almost kind, because screaming means the screamer still believes someone might listen. This was quieter than that. Hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, all speaking at once but not *at* anything — reciting. Names. Their own names, over and over, the way you'd repeat a word until it lost meaning, except they were doing it to keep the opposite true. To keep the meaning from wearing off.

*I am Marta of the reed-flats. I am Marta of the reed-flats. I am—*

*Ilo. Ilo. I was called Ilo.*

*Sixth daughter. Sixth daughter. Sixth—*

They were keeping themselves alive by remembering themselves aloud. That was the vault. A library where every book was a person, and every person was reading herself under her breath so she wouldn't be forgotten by accident.

My legs nearly went out from under me, which would have been embarrassing, so I converted the stumble into a long stride, which fooled absolutely no one, least of all the Steward, whose presence I felt settle behind me like a coat being held out for my arms.

"Steady," he said gently. "Most vessels weep here."

"Most vessels," I said, "were raised somewhere with better manners." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. Twenty years of tasting poison teaches you one transferable skill above all others: keep your face pleasant while your insides revolt.

He laughed. It was a warm sound. That was the worst thing about the Steward — everything about him was warm. He had greeted me at the mountain gate with tea and genuine-seeming concern about my travel blisters. He had walked beside me through the tunnels pointing out architectural details like a proud grandfather. He wore a face like a beloved schoolmaster and hands I hadn't once seen do anything, which meant they did everything.

"The scar is responding already," he said. "You feel it."

I did. Under my sternum, the soul-scar — Odile's word for it, the seam where I'd been stitched around something that wasn't mine — had begun to pull. Not pain exactly. *Attention.* The sensation of a room full of people turning to look at you at once, except the room was inside my own ribs.

"Walk," said the Steward. "She has waited a long time. It would be cruel to make her wait longer."

*She.* He didn't say her name. He never said her name. Luz Villalobos, sold in the spring market year of my birth, price entered in a column, buyer: the court beneath the mountain. He said *she* the way you'd say *the item*, and I hated how much tenderness he packed into it.

I walked.

* * *

The shelves went up farther than the lamplight reached. Souls were stored in glass — I had expected that much, from Odile's stories — but not bottles, nothing so honest as bottles. They were kept in lamps. Beautiful ones. Bronze and blown glass, each one glowing faintly from within, hung in rows on iron branches like fruit trees in an orchard that had never heard of seasons. Each lamp hummed its occupant's name in a voice too small to parse unless you stood close, and I made the tactical error of standing close to one.

A child's voice. *Pell. Pell. My name was Pell and I liked eels.*

I stepped back. My hand had risen toward the glass without asking permission, the way you reach toward a candle flame, and I put it down and kept walking and told myself there would be time. There would be time to open every lamp in this place. There had to be. That was the deal I'd struck with myself somewhere past the toll bridge, in the dark, while Sera slept sitting upright against a wall with her wounds weeping through the bandage: get in, get Luz, get out, and then come back for all of them. Every lamp. Every name.

Odile had burned her own name out of the ledger two nights ago rather than let the Steward hold it over us. I intended to burn the whole library next.

But first.

At the center of the vault stood a tree — the largest of the lamp-trees, old iron gone black, its branches heavy — and on the branch nearest my eye level, alone among hundreds of identical lamps, hung one that glowed gold instead of white.

It wasn't humming a name.

It was humming a lullaby.

Four notes, over and over, slightly out of tune, the way someone sings who learned the song from their own mother and never cared about pitch. I knew it. I knew it before I knew I knew it — my body knew it, my mouth moved around the shape of it without sound, because apparently some things survive being buried in a person for twenty-six years.

"That's—" My voice cracked and I let it. There was no one left to perform composure for. "That's the song from the kitchens. The valley kitchens. The head cook used to—"

"She didn't," the Steward said softly. "The head cook learned it from the taster's quarters, where it was sung in sleep for years. Sound travels in old keeps. You thought you remembered a cook. You remembered a lamp."

I crossed the floor to the gold lamp. I don't remember deciding to. My feet handled it.

Up close, the glass was warm. Everything else in the vault was archive-cold, and this one lamp was warm as a cheek, and inside the glow there was a shape — not a ghost-shape, nothing wispy, nothing theatrical. Just light gathered the way water gathers, denser at the center, moving. Restless. The other lamps' lights sat still and recited. This one paced.

It had been pacing for twenty-six years.

"Hello," I said. Which was absurd. I was addressing a lamp. I was a grown woman, a professional poison-swallower, a fugitive, a weapon the free cities had sharpened and aimed, and I was standing in enemy territory saying hello to a piece of glassware.

The light stopped pacing.

Turned.

And the lullaby cut off mid-note, and what replaced it was not a whisper. What replaced it was a *voice* — full-throated, furious, alive, a voice with gravel and music in it that filled the entire vault and made three hundred other lamps flicker:

**"Who is standing there wearing my chin?"**

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Terror and laughter use the same muscles, and mine have always been confused.

"It's complicated," I managed.

The light pressed itself against the glass like a palm against a window. "Complicated. *Complicated.* Girl-child, I have been in a jar for twenty-six years. Do you know what I have had time to think about? I have composed speeches. I have composed *letters*. I have rewritten my death eleven ways and improved it each draft. Do not tell me it's complicated. Tell me who you are."

"Casper," I said. "Casper Villalobos."

Silence. Even the reciting lamps seemed to lean in.

Then, quieter, with something underneath the fury that shook worse than the fury had: "Villalobos. That was his surname. The man at the border house. The one who was supposed to run me south and instead ran me to a market stall and a scale." A pause. "He had a son. A baby. He showed me the baby to prove he was trustworthy. That was the trick, showing me the baby. Trustworthy men don't need proof."

"You're her," I said. "You're Luz."

"I'm *furious*," said my mother's soul, "is what I am, currently. Who are you to me? Say it plainly. I have done twenty-six years of guessing games in the dark and I am *done* with them."

"My mother," I said. It came out small. I tried again. "They took your soul when I was an infant. They put it — they hid it in me. I'm the vessel. I've been carrying you my whole life. I didn't know. I didn't know until a month ago, and then it took me a month to get here, and I know that's not — I know a month is obscene, I know you've waited—"

"Stop." The voice gentled by degrees, the way weather changes. "Breathe, girl. Look at me. No — look at the lamp, you're looking at the floor like it owes you money." I looked up. The light had arranged itself, deliberately, unmistakably, into the posture of a woman leaning close. "You came. Four hundred miles, through a war, past my daughter's grave of a childhood, and you *came*. Nobody came for any of us. In twenty-six years, nobody came." The light trembled. "You came."

"Somebody came for you," I said. "You came for you. You just needed to borrow my legs."

That startled a laugh out of her — a real one, rough and sudden, and I heard myself in it, or she heard herself in me, and either way something in my chest that had been clenched since the wardline cracked open half an inch.

Then the Steward cleared his throat, and the whole vault seemed to flinch.

* * *

"A touching reunion," he said. He had drifted closer without footsteps, which should not have been possible for a man with a body, and yet. "And I confess, vessel, I find myself moved. But we should discuss terms."

"There are no terms," I said. "I take her and go."

"You take her," the Steward agreed pleasantly, "the moment you can lift the lamp. Go on. Try."

I lifted the lamp.

It weighed nothing. That was the trap, of course; I knew it even as my fingers closed around the bronze and found the glass light as a bird. Nothing happened. No resistance, no alarm, no weight. The lamp simply declined to leave its branch, the way a word declines to leave a sentence.

"Bindings," the Steward said. "Each soul in this vault is knotted to its anchor-point by contracts older than your queen, older than the free cities, older, frankly, than good taste. They cannot be carried out. They can only be *transferred* — from storage into a living vessel. And the only living vessel with a scar wide enough to receive this particular asset is standing in front of me, holding her mother in both hands, and asking me for permission."

"And if I don't ask permission?" Sera's knife was somewhere in my boot. It would have accomplished nothing. I reached for the thought anyway, for comfort.

"Then you leave with empty hands, the armies at your back arrive in six days, and Vellamare burns with its bells ringing. Or—" and here his voice did the terrible warm thing, the grandfather thing, "—or we discuss what the court actually wants, which was never cruelty, child. We are archivists. We *preserve*. Your mother has been safe for twenty-six years. Not one hour of decay, not one dream of dissolution. Stay. Both of you. Be kept. There is a wing of this court where vessels live comfortably for centuries, visited, honored, *listened to*. You would never hunger, never fear, never age past your prime. And she would be near you. Isn't that what family is? Proximity?"

And the awful thing — the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn — is that for one full second I considered it. Because he was right about the shape of the offer. Family *is* proximity. I had spent my life in a valley learning that safety tastes like ash, and here was ash with a roof on it.

"No," I said. My mother said it too, at the same instant, louder — "*No*" — and our voices overlapped for the first time, and I felt the scar under my sternum pull wide like a keyhole recognizing its key.

"Ah," said the Steward, and his warmth acquired a hairline crack. "You're going to try to take her the other way. Into yourself. Fully."

"Open the bindings," I said. "Or I'll open them myself. Odile says the knots are ledger-work. Ledgers burn."

For the first time since the mountain gate, the Steward was quiet for longer than a sentence.

Then: "Do you know what dissolution feels like, vessel? When a soul enters a body that cannot hold it? I ask professionally. We have records. The vessel does not die quickly. The vessel comes *apart* — memory by memory, boundary by boundary — until there is no Casper left to carry anyone, and the court simply files what remains. Two souls in one jar. Efficient. Tragic. Filed under *consolidation*."

"Then file this under consolidation," I said, "because I'm not leaving without her."

"*Girl,*" my mother said sharply, and the sharpness was love, I understood that now, sharpness had always been love in this family, "listen to me. If it tears you apart—"

"Then you'll be inside to catch the pieces," I said. "You've been catching me my whole life from inside a lamp. Figured you'd want the promotion."

Another laugh, wetter this time. "Gods. You argue like me."

"I'm told it's genetic."

* * *

The Steward watched me set the lamp down on the vault's central table — an iron slab, ink-stained, a working surface, because of course even miracles down here got processed as paperwork — and he did not stop me, and that frightened me more than any alarm would have. He wanted to see. Archivists always want to see.

"How," I asked him, "do I open the scar?"

"You already know," he said. "You've known since the wardline. The valley taught you to keep it shut — twenty years of practice, clamped down like a fist. Opening it is only unclenching. The difficulty is not the opening, child. The difficulty is that you must not merely open it. You must *unmake the fist entirely*. No clamping back. No retreat. Half-measures produce exactly the tearing I described. To hold her, you must stop being a container." His voice dropped to something almost like respect. "You must become a doorway that has forgotten it ever had hinges."

Terror, meet tenderness. Tenderness, terror. You'll be sharing a ribcage.

I put my hand flat on the lamp glass one more time. Inside, the light pressed against my palm, warm, impatient, afraid, brave — all of it at once, because she was a whole person, not a whisper, and whole persons get to be contradictory.

"Last chance to talk me out of it," I said.

"Twenty-six years," said my mother, "and you think I'd waste my one opening on talking you *out* of anything? Open the door, Casper. I've been knocking since before you could walk."

So I stopped clenching.

It began as heat under the sternum, then as light behind my eyes, then as every memory I owned peeling loose from its moorings and drifting — the poison kitchens, Sera's crooked grin, Odile's smoke-and-rum smell, the valley's terrible safe silence — all of it loosening, floating, becoming negotiable, while the scar tore open down the center of me like a seam giving way, and through it poured not wind but *her*: twenty-six years of stored fury and grief and pride and lullabies, a whole woman arriving in the house of my body all at once, too big, too bright, too *much*, my boundaries dissolving like sugar in—

Somewhere far away, iron alarms began to ring, shaking dust from the vault's high dark, and the Steward said, in a voice stripped of all its warmth at last:

"They've breached the third gate. She's out of time — and so are you."

Two heartbeats started in my chest, and neither of them was going to stop for anyone.
