## 17. Everything Owed

The mountain had been screaming for an hour before I understood it wasn't screaming at us.

That's the thing about alarms — they don't discriminate. The great bell in the Queen's forward camp tolled the same note for *enemy at the walls* as it did for *prisoner escaped* and *the sky is falling, act accordingly*. I'd heard it twice in my career. Once when a supply train burned. Once when I refused an order and became the reason it rang.

This time it rang because of a food-taster with ash-colored eyes and a spine I'd have sworn she didn't own three months ago.

"Left side," Odile said, from somewhere below me in the dark. "Your left side is bleeding again."

"My left side is always bleeding again."

"Then stop being stabbed on that particular side."

I pressed my palm back against the wound under my ribs — Sera Aldane, knight of a kingdom that had revoked the word *knight* along with everything else I'd ever owned, holding her own guts in with the same hand she'd once used to salute. The bandage Odile had wrapped me in was river-linen, smuggler-grade, which meant it smelled of tar and had probably carried contraband pepper in a former life. It was doing its best. We were all doing our best. Our best was currently crouched at the mouth of a trade tunnel on the mountainside with three swords between four people, one of whom was underground committing what the court beneath the mountain would certainly call theft and what I preferred to call *taking back your mother.*

Above us, the alarm kept tolling.

Below us, Casper Villalobos was walking into a vault full of stolen souls to open a scar in her own chest.

And me? I was standing guard. Which was, if I was honest with myself — and I try to be honest with myself exactly once per campaign — the first assignment in years I hadn't wanted to trade away.

"They're coming up the switchbacks," Odile said. She'd gone still in the way she did when her memory-magic brushed against something fresh, something happening now instead of forty years ago. Her eyes had that faraway shine, like a woman reading tomorrow's ledger. "Two patrols. Maybe six men each. They'll reach this ledge in a quarter hour, maybe less if their sergeant has a spine."

"Do they?"

"The last three sergeants the Queen sent into these hills didn't." She smiled without any warmth in it whatsoever. "But hope springs."

I looked down the tunnel behind us. It went down into the dark the way all the old trade tunnels did — steep, dry, lined with the ghost-smell of salt and silk and things better not itemized. Somewhere down there, past the ledgers and the courteous monsters, Casper was finding out whether her whole life had been a cage or a key.

"She'll make it," I said.

Odile glanced at me. "I didn't say she wouldn't."

"You were about to do arithmetic. I could hear you counting odds. You get a little line between your eyebrows, like a ledger closing."

"I am a smuggler. Arithmetic is how I pray." She settled beside me against the rock, pulling her coat tight. "Fine. No arithmetic. Faith, then. That's new for both of us."

"It's not faith," I said. "It's a decision."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't take anything away. It just moves everything you have into a smaller room.

When the Queen's council stripped my rank — after Vharen's Ford, after I put my sword down in front of the purge column and said *no, find another wolf* — they took the title, the banner, the pension, the name carved into the rolls. They did not take the part of me that stood in front of people. That part had nowhere else to go. It just kept reporting for duty, unemployed, waiting for someone worth standing in front of.

For a while I thought that someone would be the free cities. Then I thought it might be Odile, who deserved a wall between herself and her past more than anyone alive. Then a chained girl with poison-calloused hands had looked at me across a campfire and asked, in the flat voice of someone cataloguing flavors, *"Why are you kind to me? What's it cost?"*

And I thought: *her. Fine. Her.*

Now she was underground, and I was topside with a hole in me, and the Queen's hunters were coming up the switchbacks, and the only thing left of my knighthood was the standing.

"Sera." Odile's voice had changed. Not faraway anymore. Close. Urgent. "Lantern light. Switchback three."

I rolled to the lip of the ledge and looked down.

They came up the mountain path in a loose double file, torches hooded but leaking, boots finding the old smugglers' steps with more care than soldiers usually bothered with. Six in the lead file. Another six strung out behind. And at the front, walking point with his hood back despite the cold, a man I knew.

Corven Asche. Captain of the Queen's Hounds. The man who'd read my sentence aloud. He had a face like a closed ledger and a voice like a key turning, and he had personally dragged eleven gifted children onto the Hunt's wagons, and he was climbing toward me right now with thirty years of professional competence in every step.

"He brought hounds-hounds too," Odile murmured. "Look at the rear. Those aren't soldiers."

She was right. Behind the second file moved shapes that didn't quite walk right — bound things, leashed things, the Queen's answer to gifted resistance. Sniffers. Trackers made from people who'd once had names.

"Well," I said. "Good news and bad news."

"Give me the bad news."

"That's Corven Asche, and he doesn't leave jobs unfinished."

"And the good news?"

"He also doesn't hurry. Pride. He'll want to take us alive so he can read the sentence himself. He likes reading sentences." I drew my sword. The sound of it leaving the scabbard was very loud on the quiet ledge, and somewhere below us, I hoped, Casper heard nothing at all. "Odile. You have the back tunnel mapped?"

"Mapped, memorized, and bribed. There's a goat track off the lower gallery that comes out by the shrine ruins. If she runs, she can be in the pines before moonset."

"If she runs."

"Yes," Odile said quietly. "About that. What's the plan, wolf? Truly. Because I've run the numbers you forbade me to run, and six-and-six plus two sniffers against a bleeding knight and a retired smuggler is not a battle. It's a delay. A good delay buys minutes. Minutes are only useful if there's something to spend them on."

The wind came up the mountain and pushed my hair back and I let it, because it was honest wind, valley-adjacent, magic-dead, carrying nothing but cold. Below us the torches climbed. Above us the alarm tolled its idiot single note. And inside my chest, in the small room where disgrace had packed everything I owned, something sat down and got comfortable and said: *here. This is the post. Hold it.*

"The plan," I said, "is that she comes up this tunnel, and when she does, there is a mouth open for her. Whatever's chasing her down there — wards collapsing, Steward's creatures, the whole rotten court waking up — none of it will matter if she surfaces into a ring of spears. So the spears go through me first."

"That's not a plan, that's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's a decision," I said. "Faith is just a decision you keep making after the arithmetic stops helping."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. In the hooded lantern light her face was all angles and old regrets, and then, slowly, she laughed — a real laugh, low and rough, the first one I'd heard from her since the river-town.

"Gods. You actually believe she'll come back up."

"I believe she's her mother's daughter, and I met Luz Villalobos's reputation in a ledger written by her enemies, and even her enemies wrote her name like they were afraid of it." I set my back to the rock and tested my weight on my legs. They held. Mostly. "Casper spent twenty years drinking poison so other people wouldn't die. You think she walks into a vault full of stolen souls and leaves them there? She'll come up carrying more than she went down with. They always do, the ones like her. It's the whole disease."

"And if she doesn't come up?"

"Then I'll have held the door for no one, and it will have been the best posting of my career anyway."

* * *

They reached the ledge in the way professionals reach places: spread out, quiet, blades already drawn because Corven Asche never announced himself to prey.

I stepped out of the shadow of the tunnel mouth and let him see me.

For a moment — a genuine, unguarded moment — he simply stopped. Torchlight found my face and I watched him match it to the woman whose sentence he'd read, and I watched him decide, visibly, that dead women didn't climb mountains. His men fanned out along the ledge with the smooth economy of a unit that had done this a hundred times, cutting off the path down, sealing the cliff edge, and — I noted with grim approval — leaving the tunnel mouth itself alone, because Asche was smart enough to know that tunnels are doors and doors are held, not approached.

"Sera Aldane." He said it the way he'd said it at my sentencing: correctly, completely, like a man filing paperwork. "You're supposed to be dead at Vellamare."

"I'm told that often. It's become a compliment."

"You're bleeding."

"You're observant. They must miss you at dinner parties."

Behind him, one of the sniffers made a sound. Not a word. A sound — wet, eager, wrong — and its leash-hand had to lean back against the pull. It had caught the scent of the tunnel. Of what had gone down it. Of soul-scar and poison-blood and a girl who was, right now, somewhere beneath our feet, deciding the shape of the war.

Asche didn't look at it. He kept looking at me, which I appreciated. If you're going to die in front of a man, it's polite of him to watch.

"Step aside, Aldane. The vessel comes with us. The Queen's terms are generous — the girl lives, comfortable, honored. You know how the court beneath the mountain treats its property."

"I've read the ledgers," I said. "Comfortable isn't the word I'd choose."

"The ledgers lie."

"No," I said. "That's the terrible part. They don't. Every name, every price, every year of every stolen life, written down neat as scripture. Your Queen built her throne on a filing system, Corven. And tonight a girl with poison in her veins is down there reading it aloud."

Something moved behind his eyes. Doubt, maybe. Or the memory of doubt, filed away long ago. He raised his sword anyway, because that was the job, and I understood the job better than any living person, and I pitied him for exactly as long as it took him to attack.

"After her," he said to his men, nodding at the tunnel. "Two in. The rest—"

"The rest," I agreed, and hit the first one before he finished the sentence.

* * *

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left of that night, and because someday — if there is a someday — I want to remember it without embellishment.

There were twelve of them and parts of two worse things. There was me, with a reopened wound and a sword arm that had thirty seconds of glory left in it before the blood loss started collecting rent. There was Odile at my shoulder with a smuggler's knife and a smuggler's ethics, which is to say: no rules except winning, and a truly vicious talent for going for knees.

We did not win. Let's be clear. Winning was never on the table. What was on the table was *time*, and we spent it like drunk lords.

I broke a man's guard on the third exchange and put him down hard enough to stay down. I took a cut across the forearm returning the favor to his friend. The ledge was narrow, which helped — they couldn't bring numbers to bear, had to come at me one and two, and I had learned long ago that narrow ground is the poor knight's castle. I gave ground by inches, always keeping my body between their blades and the black mouth of the tunnel, and every inch I gave, I made them pay a toll for.

A sniffer got loose at some point. I remember the sound of it — that eager wet sound — and Odile's knife taking its handler's hamstring, and the two of them going over the ledge edge together in a tangle of chain and screaming. I remember thinking, distantly, *that's going to be in her memoirs, and she'll make it funnier than it was.*

Corven Asche fought the way he did everything: correctly. No flourish, no rage, just angles and pressure, herding me back step by step toward the cliff edge where my footing would fail before his patience did. He was better than me. I want that recorded. On a good day, with two working arms and no hole under my ribs, he was better than me, and this was not a good day.

So I cheated.

"Your Queen's source is dying," I told him, mid-exchange, conversational, while our blades were locked and our faces were close enough to share breath. "Right now. Tonight. There's a girl in the vault doing it, and she's not doing it for the free cities or for me or even for her dead mother. She's doing it because it's hers. Her mother. Her choice. Do you understand what's coming up that tunnel, Corven? It's not property. Property doesn't choose."

"Steady talk from a traitor."

"I prefer 'wolf.' It's accurate."

He shoved; I let him think the shove worked; and when he committed to the follow-through I dropped my weight and took him over the hip, down onto the stone, and put my knee on his sword arm and my blade at his throat, and for one heartbeat the ledge was silent except for breathing — mine ragged, his controlled, Odile's somewhere behind me with the wet sound of her knife doing its work.

"Yield the ledge," I said.

"You'll kill me anyway."

"I've killed eleven men tonight and regretted nine. Don't be the tenth." My vision was going gray at the edges, and I knew — the way you know weather — that I had perhaps a minute of standing left in me. "Your men are running, Corven. Look."

He looked. Because he was a professional, and professionals assess. Down the switchbacks, torches scattering, the second file breaking apart — because the mountain itself had begun to shake.

Not the alarm anymore. Something older. Something underneath. The rock under our feet hummed like a struck bell, and far below us, deep in the dark, I felt — everyone on that mountain felt — a silence opening up. A magic-dead quiet spreading outward from the valley's old wardline like ink through water, eating the court's power as it came.

The Queen's source, severed. By hand. By a food-taster.

By Casper.

Asche stared at me, and I watched the sentence-writer understand that the ledger had just been balanced by someone with no authority to hold a pen, and that it was the most lawful thing he'd ever witnessed.

"She'll come up," I said. My legs were shaking now. I stayed on my knee above him because standing was suddenly theoretical. "Whatever happens next — whatever the cities do, whatever your Queen does — she'll come up that tunnel carrying her mother, and I am going to be sitting right here when she does. That's not faith, Captain. That's a decision. I made it in a campfire's light, and I keep making it, and I will keep making it until she walks out or I stop being able to sit."

He lay there under my blade and looked up at me, and did the last thing I expected.

He laughed. Short, exhausted, almost human. "The Hounds had a wager," he said. "On what the vessel really was. I said bait. Orlan said a reliquary. Toma said—" He shook his head. "Nobody bet on this."

"Nobody ever bets on the taster," I said. "That's the whole trick of her."

And then the tunnel exhaled.

Warm air, smelling of stone dust and — impossibly, absurdly — of bread. Of a kitchen. Of something baking, somewhere far below, in a place that had never known a kitchen in its long cold life. The rock hummed a lower note, gentler. The shaking stopped. And from the darkness of the tunnel mouth, footsteps began to climb.

Two sets of them. Unhurried. One light and familiar, with the careful tread of a girl who'd spent her life testing floors for poison. And one slower, older, steadier — a rhythm I had never heard and recognized instantly anyway, because I'd read its owner's name in a ledger written by her enemies, and they had written it afraid.

My arms finally quit. I sat down hard on the stone beside Corven Asche, sword across my lap, and turned my face toward the dark.

"Casper?" I called. My voice cracked on it, damn it, cracked like a green recruit's. "Is that you?"

The footsteps paused. And then her voice came up out of the earth, hoarse and stunned and utterly, unmistakably alive:

"Sera? Why are you sitting on the floor?"

And behind her, warm as bread, amused as morning, another voice answered before I could:

"Because she waited for us, little wolf. Get used to it. I'm told it's a habit of hers."
