## 18. Two Heartbeats, One Body

My mother's soul did not look like a soul. It looked like a storm that had been folded, badly, by someone in a hurry.

The vault held it in a lattice of gold light — no, not gold. I'd been trained to name what I tasted, and this was the color of old coin handled by too many hands, the green-brown of money passed under tables. The court beneath the mountain had wrapped her in ledgers and called it preservation. Somewhere behind me, Odile was doing something loud and flammable to the door wards, and somewhere above me Sera was bleeding on a tunnel mouth so that I could stand here, in a room where the walls knew my name, and do the bravest stupidest thing of my life.

"Hello, Mamá," I said, to a folded storm.

The lattice hummed. And inside it, something *turned* — the way a sleeper turns toward a lamp left burning in the hall.

*You have his jaw,* said a voice like a bell rung underwater. *And none of his sense, I hope.*

"I never met him," I said. "The jaw came free."

*Good.* A pause, the sound of twenty years of grief rearranging itself into something with edges. *Then we owe him nothing. Not even resemblance.*

Odile had told me what taking her in would feel like. She'd used the word "opening." She'd made it sound like a door, like hinges, like something architectural and polite.

It was not a door. It was a scar I had carried since birth — a soul-scar, the sorcerers called it, the place where a taster's body had been hollowed into a strongbox — and when I put my hand to it from the inside, for the first time in thirty-one years, it opened the way a wound opens. The way a mouth opens to scream.

I took my mother in.

* * *

Here is what nobody warns you about reunion: it is crowded.

She poured into me like water finding an old channel, and every channel was a memory I hadn't known I kept — a kitchen I'd never stood in, a song in a language the valley's silence had starved out of me before I could learn it, the smell of iron and river-water that was apparently *my own newborn smell*, which she remembered better than I remembered anything about myself. Two heartbeats. One body. Hers didn't beat; hers *burned*, and my ribs were suddenly a lantern with a fire inside it that had opinions.

*Stop bracing,* she said, from inside my own skull, in a tone I recognized immediately because it was mine. *You'll crack a rib. I need those.*

"You don't get to have opinions about my ribs," I said aloud, "you've been a ledger entry for twenty years."

*I have been a ledger entry, a filing error, and a very patient prisoner. I can have opinions about anything I like. Now move.*

I moved. Because the vault had noticed us.

The bindings snapped taut around us both — I felt them come alive like fishing lines going rigid, golden-green threads running out from my chest in all directions, up through the vault's ceiling of packed earth and old contracts, down into the roots of the mountain. The court beneath the mountain had bought my mother decades ago, filed her under assets, and built its whole rotten fortune on the interest. And now the collateral was walking out the door wearing her daughter as a coat.

*They'll pull,* Luz said. Her voice had settled somewhere behind my breastbone, warm and furious and terribly familiar. *When they realize what you are, they will pull, and they will not be gentle.*

"I've survived everything they've ever served me," I said. "Broth laced with widow's-tears. Wine with grave-rose. A duke's entire dinner, once, because he was paranoid and I was hungry."

*You taste poison for a living?*

"It pays poorly and the retirement plan is death, but I'm very good at it."

A laugh moved through my chest — *her* laugh, using *my* throat, and I nearly staggered at the strangeness of it, the rightness of it. Twenty years I'd catalogued flavors like prayers, alone in a valley where nothing sang, telling myself I was safe. Safe had tasted like ash. This — being argued with by my own dead mother from inside my own body — tasted like salt meat and woodsmoke and the first hot meal after a long road.

The first thread pulled.

* * *

The wards began collapsing around us in sequence, which Odile had promised would happen, though she'd described it as "a controlled demolition" and this felt closer to a cathedral falling over slowly, with commentary.

Each ward was a contract written in light, and each one, dying, tried to collect its debt. The first caught me across the shoulders and I learned what twenty years of compound interest felt like: cold, administrative, absolutely certain of its own arithmetic. My knees hit the vault floor. The threads hauled. Somewhere in my chest, my mother's fire guttered sideways—

*Bite it,* she said sharply.

"What?"

*You taste things. You take them into your body and your body learns them and refuses them. You told me. So bite this. Taste the binding. Learn its flavor. Then tell me, daughter of mine, what it lacks.*

That was the maddest instruction I had ever received, and I once worked for a queen who seasoned soup with paranoia. But there was no time to be sane. I turned inward, toward the thread hauling at my soul, and I did the thing I had done ten thousand times in the poison kitchens: I opened my senses and I *tasted*.

The binding tasted like copper and ink and the specific bitterness of a promise made by someone who intended to break it. It tasted like a signature. And underneath all of it, holding it together, was—

"Fear," I said aloud. "It's made of fear. Their fear. They were terrified someone would come back for her."

*Everything they built is made of fear. Fear is cheap. Fear has no spine.* My mother's fire gathered along the thread like flame up a fuse. *Now, we refuse the meal.*

She burned the thread. Not gently. The recoil threw the nearest ward-contract into visible panic, its letters scrambling like ants whose stone had been lifted, and I got my feet under me just as the second and third bindings pulled together, opposite directions, trying to tear open the scar they themselves had made.

*Left,* Luz said. *The floor remembers a door.*

"The floor doesn't remember anything, it's a floor—"

But the stones here were old, older than the court's ledgers, and magic flooded everything below the wardline — and I was learning, at speed, what my mother had always known: that everything remembers if you're rude enough to ask. I put my palm flat against the vault floor and the stone gave up its secret grudgingly, a seam in the shape of a trade-tunnel, and beyond it dark, and beyond the dark, faintly, impossibly, the sound of bells. Vellamare's bells, cracked but still ringing, still fighting.

*Sera is holding the tunnel mouth,* my mother said quietly. She felt it too now; we shared blood and a body and apparently a sense of far-off people. *She has taken a wound. She is refusing to lie down about it.*

"Of course she is. She's the most stubborn wolf the crown ever failed to housebreak."

*You love her.*

"I've known her six weeks."

*I knew your father three days before I married him, and I knew your grandmother not at all until the day she died saving me. Love does not keep ledgers, Casper. That's rather the whole point of it. That's why they hate us.*

The third binding found us then, and it did not pull. It *spoke*.

*ASSET LUZ VILLALOBOS,* it said, in the Steward's courteous dust-dry voice, everywhere at once. *YOU ARE OUTSIDE YOUR CONTRACTED STORAGE. RETURN FOR RECALIBRATION.*

"Recalibration," I said. "You mean the thing you do when a tool stops working."

*THE DAUGHTER IS NOT PARTY TO THIS AGREEMENT.*

"She is now," my mother said, and pushed forward into my mouth, and for one impossible moment we spoke with one voice, two timbres braided: "*She inherited the debt.*"

I bit down on the binding the way I'd bitten down on every poison ever meant for a duke. Tasted it. Learned it. Found the fear at its center — the Steward's fear, old and calcified, the terror of a court that had sold souls and spent souls and told itself every night that nothing stolen could ever want to leave — and I refused the meal.

The thread snapped. The vault screamed in ink.

* * *

We ran. Or rather, I ran and my mother navigated, calling turns from inside my skull like the world's most invested passenger.

*Right. The ward ahead is a census-ward, it counts souls — hold your breath when you cross, both of us, count backwards from seven—*

"Why seven?"

*Because the court fears sevens; odd numbers resist pairing, and pairing is how they bind—*

"That's superstition."

*Superstition is what the powerful call the rules they don't understand. Breathe.*

I held two breaths — hers didn't need air, needed only *room* — and crossed the census-ward, and felt it grope after us and find nothing to count, because there was no longer a clean line between her soul and mine for its arithmetic to grab. We were, briefly, unfiled. Unledgered. Free in the only way this court understood freedom: illegible to its books.

In the gaps between wards, while the mountain shook dust down on us and Odile's distant arson made the tunnels cough smoke, we talked. There was nothing else to do with a mouth and a lifetime.

*Tell me the valley,* she said. *I chose it for you. Tell me if I chose well.*

"You chose a prison," I said, before I could make it kind. "A quiet one. Nothing magical could follow me there, including my own gift, including answers. I grew up tasting poison for a queen's cousin and calling it safety. I thought the numbness was my personality."

Silence, inside my own chest. The worst kind of silence, the kind with guilt in it.

*Twenty years ago a woman came to me in the free cities with a prophecy and a price,* Luz said finally. *She said the court had marked my unborn child as a vessel. She said the only place their contracts couldn't reach was the valley's dead air — that if I bore you inside the wardline, the scar would sleep, and sleeping, might pass unnoticed. She said you would live. She did not say you would live* well*. I have been dead for twenty years, Casper, and even dead I have been counting that debt.*

"The woman with the prophecy. Gray coat, smelled of river-mud, talked like she was quoting something?"

*Yes.*

"Odile says that was her. Before she defected. She ran souls for the court — deliveries, hiding places. You were her last job." I picked my way over a collapsed beam, my mother's fire steadying my balance from within, an absurd and tender sensation. "She's upstairs holding our exit open. She burned her own name out of their ledger tonight to do it."

*Then she has paid more than I have,* my mother said, and there was steel in it. *Remember that, when the war is done and the forgiving starts. Debt-forgiveness is easy. Choosing people afterward, at breakfast, on ordinary Tuesdays — that is the hard arithmetic. Do that one properly.*

"I'm thirty-one. I don't know how to have a mother. I know how to detect henbane in wine."

*And I died at twenty-six. I don't know how to be one. I know how to outrun a wardline while pregnant.* A pause. The fire in my ribs flickered — laughter or grief, indistinguishable. *We will be terrible at this together. It will be the best work of my death.*

Something in me cracked — not the scar; the other thing, the wall I'd built in a silent valley out of routine and immunity and competent loneliness. I kept running because if I stopped I would cry and crying uses breath and breath was currently needed for stairs. Steep ones. Upward. Toward Sera and smoke and bells.

*One more thing,* Luz said, softer. *When we reach the surface — what I asked of you in the vault. What I begged. Take me up the mountain. Into the old wardline. Break their claim with the valley's silence.* She hesitated, and hesitation sounded strange in a voice that had commanded me through half a collapsing mountain. *Casper. Doing that may unmake us both. I asked because I am angry and I have been angry for twenty years. But you are the one with the living body. So I am asking again, properly, the way I should have: not as a soul demanding its vessel. As a mother asking her daughter. Will you carry me?*

I reached the top of the stairs. Beyond the last door: Sera's silhouette braced in the tunnel mouth, sword low, blood on her teeth, grinning at me like the end of a bad bet paying out. Above the mountain, the Queen's hunters were coming down in numbers, and the alarms had stopped shrieking because the alarms, like everything else down here, had realized their contracts were void.

Two heartbeats. One body. One answer, and I gave it with both voices, because by now I'd earned the braid:

"Mamá. I've been carrying you since before I was born. Let's finish the delivery."

I pushed open the door. Sera saw us — saw *me*, and whatever was shining through my skin — and her grin went wide and wolfish, and she raised her sword toward the descending horde, and shouted the only order that had ever mattered:

"*Run, Casper!*"
