## 20. What the Hunt Becomes

They tell me I died for six minutes on the floor of the gatehouse at Vellamare.

I would like to state, for the record, that I was not consulted about this, and that whoever made the decision did so without my authorization. Six minutes is a long time to be dead. It is also, apparently, exactly long enough for a field surgeon with blood on her elbows and a prayer she didn't believe in to decide that a disgraced wolf-knight was worth the trouble of dragging back across the line.

I woke up three days later under a canvas roof that smelled of smoke and boiled linen, with a pain in my side like someone had installed a second spine sideways, and a young corporal sitting beside my cot who jumped up so fast he knocked over the water basin.

"Ser Aldane," he said, in the reverent tone people use for shrines and for things they're afraid might still be dangerous. "You're awake."

"No," I said. "I'm a very articulate corpse. Fetch me something to drink that isn't water, and then tell me what happened while I was busy dying."

He told me. He talked too fast and got half of it wrong, but I pieced together the shape of it before he finished, and the shape of it was this: the mountain had exhaled.

That's how the soldiers were saying it, anyway — the ones who'd been near the valley when Casper dragged the court's power up out of the dark and into the old wardline. The mountain had exhaled, and every bound soul from beneath it had come loose at once, and the Queen's armies — an army held together by fear on top and stolen souls underneath — had come apart like wet rope.

The vanguard that had breached our outer walls simply stopped. Not routed. Stopped. Stood in the burning streets holding their weapons as if they'd forgotten what the weapons were for, because half of them had been fighting on borrowed courage, on bound will, on souls that weren't theirs and suddenly, violently, were. Some of them sat down in the road and wept. Some of them looked at their own hands like they'd never seen them before. A few — the officers, the ones whose cruelty had been entirely their own — tried to keep the machine running and were killed by their own men, quickly, almost politely, the way you put down a horse with a broken leg.

And above it all, the bells of Vellamare started ringing again. The cracked ones too. Especially the cracked ones.

* * *

By the time I could stand without the wall's help, the war had turned inside out.

I watched it happen from a balcony over the harbor square, wrapped in a blanket like an invalid elder, which is what I was, technically, though I resented the blanket. The free cities had spent two years losing gracefully — retreating, trading ground for time, singing defiant songs in cellars. Now they advanced, and it wasn't a battle so much as an unbinding. Every town the Queen's army had garrisoned opened its gates before the free-city banners arrived. Every checkpoint emptied itself. Soldiers deserted by the hundred, then the thousand, walking home along roads they'd marched down in chains of one kind or another, some of them carrying their weapons slung backward like men ashamed of them.

Because here is what nobody in the throne's councils had understood, and what Odile Ferrant had known all along, and what Casper Villalobos had paid for with her own body: an army built on stolen souls is only ever one honest moment away from collapse. You can bind a man's will. You cannot bind his grief. And when the bindings snapped — when ten thousand conscripted soldiers woke up mid-siege and remembered their mothers, their farms, the names of the villages they'd burned — there was no order in the world heavy enough to hold them.

The Hunt turned inward. That was the phrase going around the camps, passed mouth to mouth like contraband: *the Hunt has turned inward.* The gifted who'd been hunted were now the ones doing the hunting, and what they hunted was the throne itself. Partisan companies that had spent years hiding in marshes came out into the open. The border lords who'd sworn to the usurper discovered, with impressive speed, that they had always secretly loved freedom. There was a great deal of that going around. History is mostly composed of men remembering their principles at convenient moments.

And at the center of it, the story — the true one, spreading faster than any army could march: a girl had gone down beneath the mountain carrying her mother's soul, and had come back up having broken the oldest contract in the world. They were already getting it wrong in the telling. By the third week she was seven feet tall in the songs. By the fifth, she breathed fire. I heard a version in a riverside tavern where she fought the Steward single-handedly with a poisoned spoon, which, I'll admit, had a certain accuracy to it that the rest lacked.

Casper hated all of it. I know because I watched her hear the songs herself, once, standing in the back of that same tavern with her hood up, listening to a blind fiddler sing about her own death and resurrection, and her face went through expressions I catalogued carefully for future use in arguments: horror, disbelief, and finally a kind of resigned nausea, like a woman tasting broth she knows has been laced.

"He got the spoon wrong," she said to me afterward. "It was a ladle."

"I'll write to him."

"Don't you dare."

* * *

The council summoned me eleven days after I woke up.

Not the war council — the new one, the provisional assembly of the free cities, the one meeting in the bell-tower hall where the great cracked bell hung overhead like a scarred chaperone. I went in uniform, or what remained of it; my old knight's coat had been cut off me in the gatehouse and someone had returned it laundered and mended, which felt like being handed back my own ghost.

There were nine of them around the table. I recognized four: Marshal Okonwe of Vellamare, silver-haired and sharp as a fishhook; the twins from Carreth who ran the river fleets; old Mother Anselm, who had been hiding gifted children in her laundry for six years and now found herself a stateswoman, to her visible disgust. The other five were newer faces, faces from the liberated towns, still wearing the look of people who couldn't quite believe the door was open.

"Sera Aldane," Okonwe said. "Sit down, please."

I stayed standing. Old habit. Standing makes refusal easier.

"You held the east gate alone," he said. "For nearly an hour, against a vanguard company, after the wall behind you had already fallen. The surgeons say you should be dead. The men say you refused retreat four times." He spread his hands. "The cities want to thank you. Properly. With more than thanks."

Here it comes, I thought.

"The armies are converging on the capital," said one of the new faces, a lean woman with a burn scar down her jaw. "The Queen's guard is deserting faster than she can execute the deserters. Within a month, maybe less, we take the palace. We need a commander the soldiers will follow. Someone with a name. Someone they already—" she hesitated, choosing the word, "—already trust with their lives."

"A general," I said.

"A Marshal of the Free Host," Okonwe corrected, gently, the way you correct a child who has said something true in the wrong words. "Commission signed by all nine cities. Rank, command, a seat at this table. Sera Aldane, restored. Your sentence struck from the record. Your name cleared."

My name. Cleared. As if it were a stain on a shirt instead of a thing I had worn deliberately, these five years, like armor worn inside out.

I looked at the cracked bell hanging above them. It had rung through the fall of the outer wall and through the counterattack and through every hour I'd lain bleeding, and it was cracked, and it rang anyway, and there was a lesson in that which no commission could improve upon.

"May I ask a question first?" I said.

Okonwe inclined his head.

"When you offer me this," I said, "are you offering it because I'm the right soldier for the job? Or because I'm the right story?"

Silence. To their credit, none of them looked away.

"Both," said Mother Anselm, bluntly. "Mostly the story, if we're honest. The men need a name to march behind. Yours happens to be attached to a gate and an hour and a lot of blood. That's not an insult, girl. That's how armies work."

"It's not an insult," I agreed. "But it's the reason I'm going to say no."

The lean woman with the burn scar leaned forward. "You'd refuse? After everything?"

"After everything," I said, "is precisely why." I put my hand flat on the table, over the map that lay there — the capital, the palace, the roads converging on it like veins toward a heart. "Five years ago I was given an order to purge a village of the gifted. I refused it, and everything I've lost since then came from that refusal. My rank, my order, my name. I don't regret a day of it. But I learned something in those five years that I don't intend to unlearn now that refusing has become fashionable." I tapped the map. "The Queen built an army on the idea that power flows downward — from the crown, through commanders, onto the backs of the commanded. If we win this war and hand all that flow to one person again, even a good person, even *me*, especially me — then we haven't won. We've just changed whose name is on the leash."

"So what do you propose?" Okonwe asked. "We fight the last battle by committee?"

"We fight it the way the cities have always survived. Nine voices. Shared command. Rotating authority if you must. Make it slow and make it loud and make it impossible for any one person to become the next thing the country needs to be freed from." I straightened. "And give the actual fighting to officers who've earned it in the field, not to a woman whose main qualification is having bled impressively in front of witnesses."

"That's a speech," said Mother Anselm, "that will be quoted against us for a hundred years."

"Good. Let it be quoted accurately."

Okonwe studied me for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — a small, dry smile, the smile of a man watching a trap close on himself. "You realize," he said, "that refusing a marshal's commission in front of this assembly is itself going to become part of your legend. 'She wouldn't take the crown's gold, wouldn't take our banner.' They'll love you more for it."

"I know," I said. "It's infuriating. Virtue is wasted on the modest."

* * *

I walked out of the bell tower into a city rebuilding itself loudly.

Vellamare in those weeks was the loudest place I have ever stood. Hammers everywhere — on roofs, on walls, on the frames of new bells being cast to replace the cracked ones, though the city had voted, formally, in one of its rowdier assemblies, to keep the cracked ones ringing regardless. *Let them ring crooked,* the resolution read. *Crooked is how we got here.* Bakers argued politics in doorways. Children played Hunt-and-Huntress in the alleys, and always, always, the children insisted on being the hunt turning inward. A mural was going up on the harbor wall: a mountain with its peak open like a mouth, and light coming out of it, and small figures climbing. One of the figures had a wolf at her side. The painter had given the wolf rather more muscle than the original possesses, and I confess I did not correct him.

I found Casper on the seawall, as I usually did in those days. She liked the water. She said it tasted of nothing, which for her was restful — a taster's tongue, given a sea instead of a cup. Her mother was with her, as she almost always was now, the two of them sitting shoulder to shoulder in the way of people relearning each other's weight. Luz Villalobos was thin and gray-eyed and moved like someone negotiating freshly with her own limbs, and she laughed easily, and when she laughed, Casper looked at her sidelong with an expression I can only describe as a man watching a locked door swing open on a room he'd stopped believing existed.

"—and then he says to me," Luz was saying, "says, *madam, the contract is quite clear*, and I said, sir, I have been dead for twenty years, I promise you the contract is the least clear thing in this room—"

"Luz," Casper said, with the weary dignity of a daughter, "you threatened a notary."

"I *negotiated* with a notary. Aggressively."

I sat down on the seawall's other side, and Luz shifted to make room without pausing her story, folding me into it the way she'd begun, in those weeks, to fold me into everything — meals, arguments, the small domestic parliament of their rented rooms above the chandler's shop. It still caught me off guard sometimes, that folding. I had spent five years being a thing the world set down carefully and stepped away from.

"How did it go?" Casper asked. "The council. Did they give you the shiny hat?"

"They offered me the whole army."

"And?"

"I declined."

Luz stopped mid-story. Casper turned and looked at me fully, and I braced for the argument — I had rehearsed several — but what crossed her face wasn't disappointment. It was relief, quick and naked, gone almost before it arrived, and I understood that she had spent the morning dreading this exact conversation, dreading the day the cities would finish converting her friend into an instrument and send the instrument marching east.

"Good," she said simply.

"That's it? No lecture about duty?"

"You lectured the entire ruling council of the free cities. I heard about it within the hour. Three separate people retold it, and in two versions you made Okonwe cry." She shrugged. "What would I add?"

Below us, the harbor glittered. Out past the breakwater, the first grain ships from the river towns were coming in — the roads open now, really open, for the first time in years — and on the quay a crowd had gathered around a crier reading the latest dispatches from the east. Even at this distance I could pick out the rhythm of it: the capital surrounded, the Queen's inner guard shrinking around her like a fist closing on nothing, the end coming not with a battle but with an emptying. Somewhere in the palace a woman who had bought souls by the ledger-full was learning what her purchases had actually cost, which was everything, which was always everything.

"They'll offer you something too, eventually," I said to Casper. "A pardon. A title. They're drafting it already, I'd bet my other kidney."

"Let them draft it." Casper picked up a pebble and weighed it in her palm, the way she used to weigh the taste of a poison, cataloguing. "I've decided something, actually. All three of us have. Luz and Odile and me." She glanced at her mother, who nodded, and went on with the particular steadiness she got when she'd stopped asking permission. "When it's over — when the capital falls and they've written whatever histories they're going to write — we're going home. To the valley. And we're going to open it."

"Open it," I repeated.

"It kept me safe by keeping everyone out. That was the bargain, and it was a bad one, and I lived it, so I get to say so." She tossed the pebble into the water. "There are people like me everywhere, Sera. People who buried their gifts to survive, people still running, people the Hunt never got around to un-hunting. The valley's quiet saved my life. It can save more than mine. So we're opening the borders, and anyone who wants quiet can come in, and anyone who wants to leave can leave, and there won't be a keeper deciding who counts." She looked at me then, direct, the way she'd looked at me the night in the camp when I broke my chains and offered her a choice. "That's the announcement. You're hearing it before the councils do, because you'd have argued them into it anyway and this saves time."

I laughed — really laughed, the first time since the gatehouse, and it hurt my ribs, and I didn't care.

"You're building a country out of a valley," I said, "with a smuggler, a resurrected soul, and a wolf with a limp."

"And you," Casper said. "If you want in. No commission. No banner. Just a chair at a kitchen table that's never going to be poisoned again."

Out over the water, the cracked bells began to ring the evening hour — all of them, cracked and whole together, slightly out of step with each other, making a sound that was not harmony exactly, and not silence either, but something better than both: a city full of different voices refusing to be tuned.

"Yes," I said.
