## 21. Luz, Awake

The body took eleven days to grow.

I know because I counted them the way I used to count sips — one for the broth, two for the wine, three for the duke's favorite stew — except this time I was counting toward something instead of away from it. Odile said that was unhealthy. Sera said nothing, because Sera was still mostly bandages and opinions, in that order. And my mother's soul sat inside my chest like a lodger who'd been promised the house was almost ready, humming old lullabies against my ribs whenever I got too quiet.

"You could hum back," she said on day four, while I sat in the valley kitchen watching a clay basin of dark soil do absolutely nothing, which is to say everything.

"I don't know your songs."

"You will."

That was the thing about sharing a skull with Luz Villalobos. She had no doubt left in her. Thirty years in a vault, filed under assets, and she came out of it certain as sunrise. Meanwhile I had spent thirty years being certain of exactly one thing — that nothing in this world belonged to me — and now there was a woman growing in the garden who had every legal, magical, and biological right to call me daughter, and I didn't know how to answer when she did.

* * *

Let me explain the soil, because everyone asks, and because I like having an answer that isn't just *magic, shut up.*

The valley is dead to magic. Has been since before I was born — some old working, some wound in the land, nobody agrees which. That's why they put the poison kitchens here. A gifted taster can't cheat; a gifted assassin can't follow; a stolen soul can't be called home across the wardline. For thirty years that silence was my prison. It turned out to also be the only place in the world where my mother's soul could rest without the court's bindings finding her again.

And then, on the mountain, I dragged the whole rotten claim up out of the dark and snapped it over my knee, and the bindings went with it. Which meant the silence wasn't a cage anymore. It was just quiet.

Quiet, it turns out, is what healing sounds like.

Odile found the method in a ledger — because of course she did, the woman can find anything in a ledger including, once, her own name, which she burned — an old rite from before the soul-trade went industrial. You grow the body from the ground where the soul feels safe. The valley soil had never been touched by magic, never bought or sold or bound, and so it was the only honest dirt left. We planted her the way you'd plant a cutting: a basin of soil, a few drops of my blood — "family rootstock," Odile called it, and I chose not to think too hard about being a cutting myself — and my mother's soul poured out of me slow, like decanting, into the dark.

It hurt less than I expected. It also left me emptier than I expected. Two heartbeats for a month, and then one, and I lay awake that first night listening to the single drum of myself and grieving it, which felt ungrateful, and which Luz — from somewhere in the soil, from everywhere — told me not to be stupid about.

"Grief isn't a debt," she said. "You don't owe it to anyone. It's just weather."

"Easy for you to say. You're compost."

"I am," she agreed, serene as a saint, "a very well-read compost."

* * *

On the eleventh morning, the basin bloomed.

Not flowers. Her. Hands first — I saw them before anything, long fingers pushing up through the dark soil like someone surfacing from deep water, patient, unhurried, as if she'd done this before and knew better than to panic. Then arms, shoulders, the line of a throat. Hair last, black going silver at the temples, exactly as I remembered it from the one portrait Odile had recovered, except the portrait hadn't shown how she'd look when she opened her eyes.

She opened her eyes. They were mine. Or I should say mine were hers — I've been borrowing them my whole life without knowing the lender.

"Casper," she said. Her voice was rust and honey. First word out of thirty years in the ground and it was my name, and I would like the record to show that I, Casper Villalobos, veteran of poisons that drop dukes, did not cry.

I cried for approximately forty minutes.

Sera held me up by one arm like a coat rack with feelings. Odile pretended to be very interested in the ledger. My mother stood in the basin, naked as the day the earth made her, dripping soil, and waited with the patience of someone who had already waited thirty years and could clearly afford more.

Then she stepped out of the dirt, wrapped herself in the blanket Odile thrust at her, looked around the kitchen — the copper pots, the herb bundles, the long scarred table where I'd learned every flavor of dying — and said:

"This is where you lived?"

"This is where I worked."

"It smells like thyme and loneliness."

"That's accurate."

She nodded slowly, cataloguing, the way I catalogue flavors. I recognized the habit and it undid me a little. All those years I thought my careful tongue came from nowhere, from the crown, from necessity. It came from her. Even my survival had her fingerprints on it, and this time that thought didn't taste like betrayal. It tasted like inheritance.

* * *

Here is what nobody warns you about resurrection: it's awkward.

You spend your whole life building a person out of absence, and then absence walks into your kitchen and asks where the cups are kept, and every answer you give is a small archaeology of everything she missed. The cups are third shelf. I learned to cook from a keeper who died for burning a writ. I learned to sleep light because the Queen's men came at night. I have scars in the shape of every poison ever survived, a map of the crown's menu written on my own liver.

Luz wanted all of it. Every story. She asked questions the way other people breathe.

"The night they took you from me—" she started, our second evening, and stopped herself. Started again. "No. Tell me something good first. I'm allowed one good thing before the bad ones. Vault rules."

"Vault rules?"

"Thirty years in a box, you make rules. Rule one: one good thing before any bad thing. Otherwise the bad things eat the day."

So I told her about the broth. The first thing I ever tasted that I chose — after the war, after the mountain, after we walked back through the wardline and the silence closed over us like a hand closing over a candle flame, gentle, protective. I'd gone to the old keep kitchen, empty now, its ovens cold, and made broth from memory. Just broth. Nothing in it but itself.

"And?" she prompted.

"And it was the best thing I've ever tasted. Because it wasn't trying to kill me."

She laughed. It was a real laugh, unpracticed, with a crack in it, and I understood that she was doing the same arithmetic I was — thirty years of missing, weighed against one laugh at a table — and that the math came out unbearable no matter which way you ran it.

Then she paid her one bad thing. She told me about the night of the sale. Not the theft — the sale. How the court beneath the mountain hadn't even come for her in the dark. They'd sent paperwork. A man with a ledger and a polite voice, and neighbors who looked away, and a price that she made me repeat twice so I would never forget it, so that anger would have a number to stand on.

"They sold me for less than a horse," she said. "Remember that. When the histories get written — and Odile will write them, don't think she won't — remember that the price of a soul, itemized, certified, witnessed, was forty silver less than a good cart-horse."

"We'll remember," Sera said from the doorway. She'd taken to standing in doorways since the gate, since holding one alone cost her two ribs and most of the use of her left arm. She stood like doors owed her money. "I'm good at remembering things I hate."

"You're the wolf-knight," Luz said, studying her. "Casper talks about you when she thinks I'm asleep in the soil. She thinks soil people can't hear. Soil people hear everything."

"Luz—"

"She says you argue like it's a love language."

Sera's ears went red. Sera, who held a gate alone against the Queen's hunters, who broke her own chains in the dark, went red to the ears over a dead woman's gossip, resurrected specifically to embarrass her. I decided in that moment that growing my mother back from the dirt had been worth every hour, purely for this.

"It is a love language," Sera said finally, with dignity. "In the north we don't waste words on people we've given up on."

"There," Luz said to me, satisfied. "One good thing. Now the bad ones can wait till tomorrow."

* * *

They didn't entirely wait. Grief keeps vault hours; it comes when it comes.

Some nights I woke with two-heartbeat phantom ache, reaching for the weight in my chest that wasn't there anymore, and found the bed across the room empty too, and panicked — properly, animal-panicked — until I found Luz sitting outside under the stars, wrapped in her blanket, learning the valley sky. She always heard me coming. She always moved over.

"I used to hold you like this," she said one such night, gesturing at the dark. "Under stars. You were two. You pointed at the moon and said a word I've never found in any language since. What was it?"

"I don't remember."

"No," she agreed softly. "But your mouth remembers. Sometimes when you're falling asleep you say it. I heard it, that first week, through the soil." She pulled the blanket wider, an invitation I pretended not to need and needed completely. "We lost the word. We'll make a new one. That's how language works. That's how families work, if they're lucky. Not recovered. Remade."

I sat under the remade sky with my remade mother and let the valley's silence do what it does, which is hold everything without judging any of it.

"I grieved you already," I told her. "Years ago. I didn't know it was you I was grieving. I just knew something was missing, and I built my whole life around the shape of the hole."

"I know. I watched from the vault. They let us watch, sometimes, the ones they liked least to talk about." Her jaw tightened — the fury was still in her, banked, permanent, a hearth-fire rather than a wildfire now. "I watched you sip death for strangers and survive it, and I was proud, and I was furious, and I couldn't do either one out loud. So. I'm doing them both now. Out loud. Get used to it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Don't 'ma'am' me. I was a horse's worth of silver."

I laughed until my ribs hurt, and she laughed with me, and above us the moon hung there, unnamed, waiting for whatever we decided to call it.

* * *

By the second month, Luz had opinions about everything, which Sera claimed proved the bloodline beyond any ledger. She reorganized the kitchen. She declared Odile's ledgers "morally necessary and physically obscene" and built her a proper desk. She interrogated Sera about the north, about wolves, about the order that had cast her out for refusing a purge — and when Sera finished, carefully neutral, expecting pity, Luz said only: "Good. You kept your teeth. Teeth matter more than titles," and Sera looked at her the way soldiers look at a general they'd follow into a second hell.

Me, she taught. That was the part I hadn't planned for — that resurrection goes both directions. I thought I'd be showing her the world. Instead she showed me mine.

How to make bread without measuring, because measuring is fear and dough can tell. How to sing — badly, on purpose, because "your gift answers joy faster than grief, and you've spent enough time feeding it grief." Because yes: past the wardline's edge, walking the border roads, my gift woke like a plant toward light, and Luz — a soul who had been nothing *but* gift, once, before they filed her — taught me to hold it the way she wished someone had taught her. Open. Close. Open. Like a door, like a throat, like a hand deciding whether to trust.

And how to grieve on schedule, sort of. One bad thing per day, vault rules, and then the day belongs to the living.

Today's bad thing arrived at dusk, riding up the valley road with dust on its boots: a courier, crown seal, the third this month. Pardons. The new council — half free-cities, half defectors, all very sorry — offering full restoration of name and station to "the heroes of the mountain," lands, titles, a general's commission for Dame Aldane, a seat for the vessel who broke the court.

Sera read hers standing in the doorway. Odile read hers sitting on her new desk, feet up. Luz read hers by holding it close to the lamp and squinting, because resurrected eyes are still broken in.

"What's yours say?" I asked.

"'To Luz Villalobos, wrongfully entered into record,'" she recited, "'the crown offers formal apology, restitution, and restoration of citizenship.'" She set it down. "Restoration. As if I was a building."

"What do we do with them?"

Sera answered by feeding hers to the stove. Odile answered by folding hers into a paper boat and floating it in the wash-basin, where it sank, which she declared symbolic. Luz answered by using hers to prop the wobbly table leg.

I read mine twice. Lands. Title. *Lady Villalobos of the Valley.* The crown, offering to give me the only thing I'd ever owned outright, as if it needed their signature.

I fed it to the stove too. The fire took it gratefully. Everything in this kitchen burns clean now.

"Tomorrow," Luz said, watching the paper curl, "you'll tell me one good thing."

"I have one already."

"Save it. Vault rules. One good thing *before* the bad thing, and the bad thing hasn't happened yet." She smiled, rust and honey, and stirred the pot. "Tomorrow's bad thing is that the choir arrives. Forty-seven formerly-stolen souls, Odile wrote to them, and they're coming here, to a valley with one road and four beds, and you, my practical daughter, are going to have to figure out where they all sleep."

I looked at the kitchen — the pots, the herbs, the long scarred table, the stove eating a king's pardon — and started counting beds the way I used to count sips.

One for the broth. Two for the wine. Three for everyone the crown had counted as assets, coming home up the valley road, singing.
