## 22. A Wolf for the Choir

The pardon arrived in spring, carried by a courier who looked like he'd rather have been carrying anything else — a writ, a sack of coin, and the Queen's new seal, which was the old seal with the stag's antlers filed down to nubs. Even emblems were being reformed these days.

"Casper Villalobos," the courier read, in the flat voice of a man reciting someone else's grocery list. "In recognition of services rendered to the realm, the Crown offers full pardon for all crimes of sedition, theft, trespass, and—" he squinted "—*destruction of a sovereign court.*"

"That last one's my favorite," I said. "I worked very hard on it."

Sera, leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed and her scar pulling at the corner of her mouth the way it did when she was amused and pretending not to be, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a wolf clearing its throat. Hard to say. It was usually both.

The courier held out the writ. I looked at it. It was very fine vellum. Someone had spent real money on it, which told me exactly how nervous the Crown still was about the mountain, about the silence where its soul-source used to be, about the woman who had walked into a vault of stolen voices and walked out with her mother and a grudge.

"Does it come with an apology?" I asked.

The courier blinked. "I— no one mentioned an apology."

"No. They never do." I took the writ anyway, because I wanted to burn it properly, with witnesses, and you can't burn what a courier keeps. "Tell whoever sent you that the valley thanks the Crown for its interest, declines the pardon, and would appreciate it if the Crown stopped sending people up the wardline. The road is open. The border is not negotiable."

"The road is *open?*" He said it like I'd offered him a bowl of live eels.

"It's a long story," Sera said. "It involves a war."

Everything involved a war, that year. You couldn't buy bread without the baker telling you which front had moved overnight. But this chapter of mine isn't about the fronts. This is about the kitchen.

*

Let me back up, because pardons deserve context, and context deserves honesty, and I've spent enough of my life being dishonest by omission to last several lifetimes.

After the mountain exhaled — after the Steward came apart like wet paper and the court beneath the stone went dark and quiet in the way only our valley had ever been quiet — we came home the slow way. Luz rode in a cart for the first hundred miles because her body was three weeks old and still learning how knees worked. She complained about it constantly. It was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard.

You should understand something about my mother. For thirty years she had been a voice in a vault, filed under assets, and when I finally opened the scar and let her in, I expected a ghost. What I got was a woman with opinions. About my posture. About my cooking. About Sera Aldane, whom she assessed in approximately four seconds and pronounced "*that one stays,*" with the authority of someone who had been sold once and intended to do the choosing from now on.

She was right, incidentally. That one stayed.

Odile Ferrant also stayed, though she'd have denied it at knifepoint. Odile did not stay anywhere; Odile *passed through*, she said, with cargo and contacts and a past she'd burned out of a ledger with her own hands. But she kept finding reasons to pass through the valley. Then reasons became seasons. Then one morning I found her re-shelving the poison kitchens' old ledgers — not the soul-trade ones; those we burned, all of them, page by page, until the smoke smelled like nothing at all — and she said, "Someone has to keep the records of a place that's going to matter," and that was that. Smugglers are terrible liars about everything except themselves.

And the valley. The valley changed, or maybe it just finally said out loud what it had always been.

Here is what nobody understood about the magic-dead quiet, including me for twenty-nine years: it was never a wall built to keep the world out. It was a wound. My grandmother's generation had cut the valley off from magic deliberately — not out of fear, but out of grief, after the first soul-hunts, after they watched their gifted neighbors dragged beneath the mountain and decided that if magic could be stolen through them, they would rather go silent. A whole valley holding its breath for sixty years.

When Casper Villalobos dragged a dead court's power up the mountainside and snapped it against that old wardline, the breath went out. And what rushed in wasn't chaos. It was choice. Magic could enter the valley now — but only if it was invited. Only if it knocked. The hunted learned about it the way the hunted learn everything: in whispers, on roads, in the space between one checkpoint and the next. *There's a place where the wards ask permission.*

They started coming in autumn. By winter there were tents by the river, then cabins, then a second well. People with gifts that flickered and sparked and occasionally set things on fire, and people with no gifts at all who simply hadn't felt safe anywhere since the Hunt began, and every single one of them stood at the wardline and waited to be asked, and every single time, the valley asked.

Luz presided over arrivals like a general reviewing troops, except instead of inspecting weapons she inspected whether people had eaten.

"You're too thin," she told a fire-handed boy of eleven, and he burst into tears, and she fed him until he was a different shape entirely.

"You're too thin," she told me, weekly, and I pointed out that I had survived on poison rations for a decade and thin was structural now, and she said, "Then we'll build you new structure," and handed me bread.

That's my mother. Thirty years in a vault and her first act of freedom was feeding strangers. I think the court thought they'd broken her, keeping her as a voice. They'd actually just been marinating her. Never underestimate what patience does to a woman with opinions.

*

But I promised you the pardon, and the burning of it, and I keep my promises now. It's a recent habit. I recommend it.

We burned the writ in the kitchen hearth on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were baking days and there was already a fire going and I am, at heart, an efficient person. Sera threw it in. Luz salted it first — "so the Crown's bad faith doesn't season anything else" — and Odile narrated the combustion like an auctioneer: "One royal pardon, gently used, never honored, going once—"

The vellum caught and curled and the stag-without-antlers blackened and I felt — I want to describe this accurately — I felt *nothing dramatic*. No thunder. No sense of destiny fulfilled. Just paper becoming ash becoming warmth, same as any other fire, and maybe that was the point. Maybe that's what choosing your own life feels like from the inside: not a coronation. A Tuesday.

"What did it even pardon us for?" Odile mused, poking the ash. "Trespass? We trespassed *beneath a mountain.* Whose jurisdiction is that?"

"Ours now," Sera said. "We killed the landlord."

"And what does a wolf-knight do when the war ends?" I asked her, because I'd been wondering, because I'd watched her turn down a general's commission in Vellamare with a courtesy so glacial it qualified as weather.

Sera looked into the fire. Her scar moved. "Wolves don't do well in packs that appoint them," she said finally. "I spent ten years taking orders that turned my stomach. I'm done being useful to thrones." A pause. "I'm not done being useful."

"To whom?" Luz asked, though her tone suggested she already knew, because mothers always know, even mothers who missed thirty years. Especially them.

Sera shrugged, which for Sera was a confession. "Whoever needs teeth," she said. "This valley's going to need teeth. Word travels. Kings notice places where the hunted gather." She looked at me. "Somebody should stand at the gate who knows what kings send."

So that's what she does now. There's no title. We offered titles; she threatened us. She walks the wardline at dawn and dusk, and travelers see a grey-cloaked figure with a soldier's stride and a wolf's patience, and they feel safe before they know why. Once-stolen people recognizing a guard who guards instead of collects. It works better than any charter.

*

Winter deepened. The valley filled.

I want to tell you about the winter honestly, without the varnish that endings like to wear, because the truth is that belonging — chosen belonging, kept belonging — is not a state you achieve. It's a chore. It's dishes. It's arguments about whose turn it is to mend the roof, and Odile vanishing for three weeks and coming back with frostbite and saffron and no explanation, and Luz teaching the fire-boy letters while he accidentally singed the alphabet cards, and me lying awake some nights in a house full of people who chose me, waiting for the old arithmetic to return — *you are storage, you are bait, you are what you're worth to someone else* — and having to get up in the dark and put my hand on the kitchen table, the actual wood of it, and remember: *no one here bought me. No one here owns me. They kept the seat warm because they wanted me in it.*

Grief doesn't finish just because joy arrived. They share a house, grief and joy. Ours shared a kitchen.

My mother and I fought that winter, too. About small things, mostly — she wanted to plant the south field; I said the soil was still sour from sixty years of silence; she planted it anyway and it came up green out of pure spite, which she considered vindication and I consider the most maternal thing that has ever happened. But sometimes we fought about the big thing, the missing decades, the birthdays she'd filed away in a vault while I grew up tasting death for a living. She'd go quiet mid-sentence, staring at her own hands like they were borrowed. I'd find her at the window at odd hours.

"I heard you," she told me once, apropos of nothing, in the dark. "All those years. Not clearly. Like hearing rain through a roof. But when you dosed yourself, when you swallowed what would kill a duke — I knew. I screamed, somewhere in there. You probably tasted it."

I had. There had always been one flavor I could never catalogue, a bitterness under everything, like a prayer said wrong. I'd assumed it was fear. It had been my mother, screaming through thirty feet of stone and sixty years of silence, and I had swallowed her voice along with the poisons and called it survival.

"We're even now," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it, and she let me have it, which was mercy, which was also mothering.

Nothing is even. Nothing gets even. You just decide, daily, to build forward from the uneven ground. That's the whole secret. Nobody writes it down because it makes a terrible ballad.

*

Spring came the way springs come to valleys that have remembered how to breathe: rudely, gloriously, all at once. The south field — spite-green, I concede the point, Mother — went to seed. The river-town downstream sent word that the toll bridge was rebuilt and, more remarkably, unguarded; the Crown had pulled its checkpoints back after the Hunt collapsed in on itself, after the armies found their bound-soldier regiments waking up free and unwilling, after the usurper discovered that a throne built on rented souls pays its debts the moment the lender dies. I did that. We did that. Some mornings I believed it. Most mornings I just swept the floor.

And on a particular evening in that particular spring, the kitchen filled up.

It started, as our best things did, with food. Luz had declared a feast for no occasion except survival itself, which she held quarterly. Odile produced wine of frankly suspicious provenance. Sera carved the roast with a knife she'd once held at a queen's throat, which she claimed improved the flavor. The fire-boy — Tam, eleven-going-on-twelve, hands steady now unless startled — brought his adopted family. The widow Maren who could mend bone with a touch sat next to the twins who spoke to crows. A dozen more, two dozen, faces I'd fed through the winter, faces that had arrived hollow at the wardline and been asked, politely, by a valley, *do you wish to come in?*

Luz stood, raised her cup, and said grace.

Not a prayer. Our family didn't pray to anything above; we'd seen what lived in the ceilings of power. Luz's grace was a list. She named the dead — the ones from the vaults whose names Odile had memorized and read aloud every month so the ledger of small betrayals would end in remembrance instead of accounting. She named the living. She named the valley. And then she said, "Sing, somebody, before I cry into the gravy," which was her way of saying *I love you* to a room.

Tam started it. Of course Tam started it; children have no shame, which is the last magic left in the world. A harvest song, old and off-key, the kind with a chorus designed for many voices and no talent. The widow Maren joined. Then the twins. Then Odile, who sang like a smuggler — quietly, beautifully, and only when certain no one was watching, except everyone was watching and she pretended not to know.

Sera sang one line. One. Low, rough, barely above the fire's crackle. But she sang it looking at me, and it was the line about the wolf who lay down among the sheep not to eat them but because the flock sang better than the hills, and I understood that I had been given something no crown could mint and no ledger could hold.

I sang too. Badly. Loudly. With both heartbeats — mine, and the echo of hers that lives in my chest now, the way a scar lives in skin: proof of damage, proof of repair, proof that a body can be opened all the way down and still choose to hold someone.

The kitchen was full. Nothing in it was poisoned. I checked — old habit, tongue cataloguing the air like prayers — and found only bread and wine and woodsmoke and forty voices, and underneath it all, faint as rain through a roof, my mother's laughter, which needed no vault, no vessel, no contract. Just a chair. Just a seat kept warm.

Outside, the valley breathed. Somewhere far off, kings were still counting what they'd lost, and couriers were still carrying writs to people smarter than their senders. Let them. The road was open. The border was ours. The choir was singing.

And I, Casper Villalobos — taster, taster's daughter, thief of souls, keeper of none — passed the bread.
