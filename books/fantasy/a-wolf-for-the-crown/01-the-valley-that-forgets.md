## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bone was warm before Petra ever touched it.

That was the first sign, always. A dead woman's rib, dug from the churchyard loam three days past, should have been cold as river stone. But when Petra lifted it from the box of birch bark where it had been resting — resting, she thought, the way a sleeper rests before waking — it carried a heat like a bird's breast against her palm. The elders called it residue. Petra had long ago stopped calling it anything at all. Naming it felt like pressing a thumb into wet clay; the shape stayed whether she wanted it to or not.

She sat in the back room of the carver's shop with the shutters drawn against the afternoon, though there was nothing in Hollowmere's sunlight that could hurt her. Habit, more than fear. Her lamp burned low and steady, and around her the room smelled of linseed oil and chalk dust and the faint sweet rot of old antler. Racks of finished work lined the walls: buttons and combs, needle cases and flute stops, the small beautiful useless things that Hollowmere carved from what its dead left behind. To any visitor the room would look like a workshop. Only Petra knew it was also a listening place.

She turned the rib in her fingers. It was a fine specimen — dense, uncracked, the marrow long dried to a honey-colored ghost. Somewhere beneath her sternum, her gift stirred like a cat hearing its name.

"Right," she said softly, to no one. "Let's hear you, then."

She took up her finest graver — a sliver of moon-steel no wider than a grass stem — and set the edge against the bone's pale curve.

In Hollowmere, magic could not follow. Everyone said so, everyone believed it, and it was true in the way that most comfortable things are true: because no one had ever tested it hard enough to be disappointed. The valley sat cupped between two shoulders of mountain, ringed by grey peaks that shrugged off storms and travelers alike. Witches' fires guttered at the borders. Curses curdled and died in the high passes. Once, so the story went, a sorcerer-king had sent his shadow-hounds over the ridge to drag back a runaway bride, and the hounds had stood at the tree line all night, howling at something they could not cross, until dawn came and unmade them. Hollowmere slept safe inside that old refusal. Magic could not follow you here.

But magic did not need to follow anyone into the valley, because some of it had never left.

Petra drew the graver down in a single clean stroke, and the bone spoke.

Not aloud. Never aloud. The voice came up through her fingertips, through the bones of her own wrist, and settled behind her eyes like light seen through closed lids. It was a woman's voice, worn smooth as a river pebble, and it said:

*—the blue dress, tell her I kept the blue dress, she'll know which one, she'll know—*

Petra breathed out slowly and let her hands do their work. This was the part no one taught, because no one else could do it. The words lived in the bone the way water lived in a spring, and carving was not digging but *inviting* — each stroke of the blade opening another inch of channel for the voice to rise through. Too fast and the words came out tangled, a dozen years of a life compressed into gibberish. Too slow and they sank back down, sometimes forever. You had to listen with your hands. You had to be patient as geology.

*—tell Marta the blue dress, under the floor by the hearthstone, I meant to mend the hem, I never mended the hem—*

"Who is Marta?" Petra murmured. Speaking helped, oddly. Gave the dead something to push against, a wall to lean their voices on. "Who am I telling?"

*—my girl. My girl who went east. She thinks I was angry. I wasn't angry, I was frightened, there's a difference, nobody told me there was a difference until it was too late to say it—*

The strokes came easier now. Petra worked the graver along the rib's outer face, laying down a spiral pattern, the old carver's path that followed the grain of memory instead of fighting it. Shavings curled away pale as fingernails. In the lamplight the bone seemed almost translucent, and deep inside it something flickered, the way a fish flickers far down in clear water.

This was Tam Ellery's mother. Old Widow Ellery, who had died in her sleep a fortnight ago with her hands folded and her face composed, and whose son had brought Petra the rib wrapped in linen with an expression of careful, terrible hope. *You'll make something nice of it,* he'd said. Not a question. In Hollowmere, the bereaved brought their dead to the carvers the way other folk brought them to priests, and if the carvers were better listeners than priests, well — that was a thing people said quietly, or didn't say at all.

*—and the loom weights, tell her to take the loom weights, they're good ash wood, my father cut them, don't let them sell them to the Tanners, the Tanners will melt them down for the iron—*

"I'll tell him," Petra said. "Tam. His name is Tam."

*—Tam.* The voice turned the name over like a coin bitten for gold. *Yes. Tam. He used to sleep in the crook of my arm until he was four years old, did you know that? He'd fit right there, under my chin, like a loaf of bread. Nobody tells you they stop fitting. Nobody warns you about that either.*

Petra's throat tightened. She kept carving. Outside, beyond the shuttered window, she could hear the ordinary music of the valley: cart wheels on the mill road, someone calling a dog home, the smith's hammer keeping its patient time. Hollowmere going about its evening. Forty hearth-smokes rising straight up into windless air. A place so small you could shout across half of it, and so stubborn it had outlived three kingdoms without ever once appearing on a map worth drawing.

She loved it the way you love your own heartbeat — without thinking, without choosing, without any sense that it might one day stop.

And she did not entirely belong to it. That was the other truth, the one that sat in her chest on evenings like this, heavy and quiet as a stone at the bottom of a well.

*—the hem, don't forget the hem, she'll want to know I thought of her every time I looked at that dress—*

"I won't forget," Petra whispered.

The door opened behind her without a knock. Only two people in Hollowmere opened doors that way, and one of them was dead.

"You're still at it." Maren Cooper filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley — broad, grey-bearded, smelling of pipe smoke and tallow. He was the master carver, which was to say he was the best hand with chisel and lathe in three villages, and he had been Petra's teacher since she was seven years old and caught talking to a goose skeleton in the midden. He crossed the room, glanced at the rib, and read the spiral of her cuts the way a literate man reads a page. His eyebrows climbed. "Widow Ellery?"

"She has a lot to say."

"They usually do." He lowered himself onto the stool by the door with a groan that was half performance, half honest complaint, and watched her work for a while in silence. Then, in a different voice, quieter: "How much longer?"

"Another hour. Maybe two. She keeps circling back to a dress." Petra paused, graver hovering. "Master Cooper — why do we do it this way? The spirals. The listening. No other valley does this. I've asked. Travelers say carvers elsewhere just — carve. Buttons. Combs. Nothing that talks."

Maren's silence stretched long enough that she looked up.

He was looking at her with an expression she had learned to dread, the one that meant he was deciding how much truth she could carry today. He had been making that face for eleven years, and every year the truth he gave her came in slightly larger pieces, like bread rationed through a famine.

"You know why," he said finally.

"Because of the gift."

"Because of the *gift*," he agreed, "which is not a gift, whatever our grandmothers liked to call it, and which you will speak of to no one outside this room. Say it back."

"Never speak of the gift," Petra recited, in the flat tone of a child saying grace. "Not to travelers. Not to the priest. Not to Tam Ellery, even if he asks, even if he cries. Especially not if he cries."

"Especially then," Maren said. "Crying people remember things. Remembering people talk."

"And talking people get visited." She set the graver down and flexed her stiff fingers. "I know the liturgy, Master. I've known it since I was seven. But knowing a rule isn't the same as understanding it, and you've never once explained it. Why hollow? Why us? If the gift runs in families — and you've admitted it runs in mine, you've all but drawn me the family tree — then it ran somewhere *before* it got here. Gifts don't grow in valleys. They walk into them."

For a moment she thought he would give her the whole truth, whatever the whole truth was. Something moved behind his eyes — an old tired thing, surfacing and sinking again like the fish-flicker in the bone.

"Ask the dead," he said. "They're chattier than I am."

"That's cruel."

"It's practical." He levered himself up, joints crackling, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame. The lamplight caught the deep lines of his face, and for a heartbeat he looked less like her teacher than like a man standing watch over something buried. "Finish the widow. Be kind to Tam. And Petra—" He didn't turn around. "If riders ever come up the mill road wearing colors that aren't ours, you run for the pass before you ask a single question. Promise me."

"Riders," Petra said. "In Hollowmere. Master, no one comes to Hollowmere. That's rather the point of it."

"The point of it," Maren said, "is that it stays that way." And he was gone, his footsteps fading down the stairs into the shop below, leaving Petra alone with the lamp and the warm bone and the slow spiral of a dead woman's unfinished sentences.

* * *

She finished near midnight.

The last stroke of the graver completed the spiral at the rib's crown, and the voice rose one final time — not words now, but a feeling, the way the dead ended everything: a warmth spreading through Petra's chest that was not her own. Love, maybe. Or the shape love leaves behind when the loving is done. Then it faded, and the bone went cool in her hands for the first time, and it was only bone again. Finished work. A comb-blank, eventually, or the handle of a knife that would outlive everyone who remembered the woman who owned it.

Petra sat for a while in the dark with her eyes closed, letting the widow drain out of her the way you let water drain from your ears after swimming. This was the cost no one warned you about, either: you carried them home with you, a little. Every voice left sediment. Some nights she went to bed with three strangers' griefs layered under her own like silt in a riverbed, and woke not sure which worries were hers.

Tonight the sediment was a blue dress and an unmended hem and a boy who used to fit in the crook of his mother's arm.

She found Tam Ellery the next morning at the ford, mending a net, his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his forearms goosefleshed against the cold. He saw her coming and stood too fast, dropping the net, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Up close she could see he hadn't slept. Grief had carved him already, quick and rough, the way winter carves a riverbank.

"Well?" he said. His voice cracked on the single word. "Did she — is there anything —"

"There's a lot," Petra said gently. "Sit down, Tam. It takes a while to tell."

He sat. She crouched beside him at the water's edge, where the ford ran shallow and bright over its brown stones, and began with the blue dress — under the floorboards by the hearthstone, she said, and watched Tam's face break open like a door — and the loom weights, good ash wood, don't let the Tanners have them, and the hem she never mended, and the anger that had been fear all along, and the boy-shaped warmth under her chin that no one had warned her she would lose.

By the end Tam was crying, which was allowed, and holding the rib, which was also allowed, and Petra was watching the water slide past the ford stones and thinking, with a strange detached clarity, *this is the whole world, right here. This ford. This grief. This valley that keeps what it loves.*

"Hollowmere's lucky," Tam said thickly, when he could speak. "To have you. To have — this. Anywhere else, she'd just be gone."

"Anywhere else," Petra agreed, and heard the hairline fracture in her own voice, and hoped he hadn't.

Because that was the thing she could never say, not to Tam, not to anyone: that the gift was not of Hollowmere. That it had walked into this valley from somewhere else, long ago, wearing someone else's face — hers, or her mother's, or a grandmother's whose name no living person would give her. That the elders guarded its secret with a ferocity that made no sense unless the secret was dangerous. That Maren had made her promise to run from soldiers who, in all the history anyone would admit to, had never once come.

Magic could not follow you into Hollowmere. But it had never needed to. It was already here. It was her.

She was still crouched at the ford, turning these thoughts over like the rib in her hands, when the dog started barking.

It was the Tanner's dog, a big ugly mastiff-cross that feared nothing on four legs, and it was standing at the mouth of the mill road with its hackles up, barking at the tree line — barking the way animals bark at things that should not be there. Petra rose slowly. Down the valley, other dogs took it up, one after another, a chain of sound rolling from hearth to hearth, and the ordinary morning music of Hollowmere stuttered and went quiet.

At the far end of the mill road, where the pines crowded down to meet the last farmstead, something glinted. Metal. More than one piece of it.

Moving.

Coming down.
