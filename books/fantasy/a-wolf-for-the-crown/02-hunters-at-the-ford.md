## 2. Hunters at the Ford

The river told her first.

Petra had gone down to the Brightwater at dawn to wash the dust of last night's carving from her hands — old habit, though no one in Hollowmere would have called it a ritual. The valley's water ran cold and clear and utterly silent. That was its gift, or rather the valley's: nothing here spoke. Not the dead, not the stones, not the wind that came down off the mountains carrying whatever it carried from the wider world. Hollowmere swallowed magic the way sand swallowed rain. It was why her grandmother's grandmother had come here, why the carvers had hidden their craft in a place where the craft itself could not be heard.

But that morning the river was muttering.

Not words. Not yet. Just a tremor under the surface, like a sleeper about to wake, like bone held close to a dying mouth. Petra crouched at the ford with her sleeves pushed up and her hands half-submerged, and she felt it through her fingertips the way she felt everything — faintly, wrongly, as if her skin were only a thin wall between her and a room full of voices.

*Something is coming,* said the river, in the way rivers say things.

She stood so fast she splashed herself to the waist. The cold didn't touch her. Her heart was already climbing into her throat, because she knew what it meant when the dead grew restless near the water, and she knew what it meant when they were restless *here*, where nothing should ever reach them.

Upstream, where the river narrowed between grey shoulders of rock, the mist was moving against the current.

Petra watched it for a long moment. Then she turned and ran for Old Wren's house, barefoot, her boots dangling from one hand, wet skirts slapping her legs, past the sleeping crofts and the smoke still unrisen from the morning fires, past the shrine-stone where the elders left offerings to keep the valley quiet. She did not knock at Wren's door. She never knocked. She let herself into the smell of tallow and dried yarrow and found the old woman already awake, already dressed, already standing at the window with her hands folded like a woman who has been waiting all night for bad news.

"You felt it," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"The river's whispering." Petra's breath came ragged. "Wren, nothing whispers here. Nothing *can*—"

"Riders." Wren said the word flatly, the way you'd name a disease. "At the ford by midmorning, if the mist doesn't slow them. Crown riders."

Petra laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin. "That's impossible. The whole reason — the covenant of the founders, the valley's silence, that's the entire *point* of Hollowmere. Magic can't find us. The crown's hunters can't scry us, can't track us, can't even remember we exist half the time. You've said so yourself a hundred times. You've *taught* me so."

"I have." Wren turned from the window. In the grey light her face looked carved from the same pale wood as her walking staff, deep-grained and weathered and very tired. "And I have also taught you that every sanctuary is a promise made by someone who is not the one seeking shelter. Promises break, child. Valleys forget. And sometimes the thing hunting you doesn't need magic at all. Sometimes it needs only a name written on paper, and a man willing to ride toward it."

"A name," Petra repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. "Whose name?"

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the first cock crowed, absurd and ordinary, and somewhere down the lane a neighbor's dog barked twice and fell silent, and the world went on being the world, and Petra understood, in the way one understands a bone breaking before the pain arrives, that none of it would be ordinary again.

"Go home," Wren said gently. "Wake your mother. Tell her nothing except that I need her at my hearth within the hour. Then come back to me. There are things we must do while there is still time to do them."

* * *

They burned the records in Wren's back garden, in the iron cauldron usually reserved for rendering fat, while the sun climbed toward midmorning and the mist on the river thinned and the sound of hooves did not yet come but was already, somehow, audible in the silence where it wasn't.

There were more records than Petra had ever imagined. She had known Wren kept accounts — every carver did, tallying commissions and payments in the plain script of any village tradeswoman, because bones were sold openly here as buttons and knife-handles and flute-pipes, and no one thought anything of it. But beneath the ledgers, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a chest that smelled of forty years of winters, lay the true archive. Names. Lineages. The genealogy of the gift, traced back through eleven generations of Hollowmere carvers to a woman named Sera Brightwater who had come over the mountain with a bundle of tools and a secret folded into her shawl.

Eleven generations. And on the last page, in Wren's careful hand, freshly inked:

*Petra Brightwater, daughter of Ilse. The gift is strong in her. Stronger than mine ever was. May the valley hold her all her days.*

"Wren." Petra's voice cracked. "You wrote this. When?"

"Last autumn." Wren fed another page to the flames without looking at it. "After the harvest market, when you carved the miller's mother's fingerbone and the whole crowd heard her singing. You thought no one noticed. Everyone noticed, child. They noticed because they love you. But love travels, and so does talk, and talk reaches ears that love does not."

"But the valley — the silence—"

"Keeps magic out." Wren shrugged, an old woman's shrug, heavy with everything she'd never said. "It does not keep men out. Men walk. Men ask questions at the crossroads inn two valleys over, where our wool is sold and our cider drunk, and men hear a story about a girl who makes dead things speak. And there are men now, girl, whose whole trade is listening for exactly that story. The crown has grown afraid. Afraid people make dangerous students."

The fire ate the names. Petra watched her great-great-grandmothers curl and blacken and go up in sparks, each one a woman who had lived and carved and died inside the valley's blessed silence, and she felt the sanctuary failing around her not as walls falling but as something quieter and worse: a warmth withdrawing, a hand opening, the long held breath of Hollowmere finally, finally letting go.

"Why didn't you warn me?" she whispered. "Last autumn. Why didn't you tell me then?"

"Because telling you would have changed nothing except how many nights you slept." Wren's eyes, when they lifted, were fierce and wet. "Because I hoped I was an old fool. Because hope is the last tool in the kit and you use it until the handle cracks." She seized Petra's wrists, grip surprisingly strong. "Listen to me. The riders will have a list. Lists are how the crown hunts now — names gathered by informers, verified by priests, signed by the Office of Inquiry. If your name is on that list, no valley in the world will hide you, because they won't need magic to find you. They'll need only a road."

"Then where do I go?"

"The pass." Wren jerked her chin toward the northern wall of the valley, where the mountains shouldered together and a thread of white marked the trail over the saddle — the trail no one used, the trail the elders called the Back Door, kept open by law older than the crown for reasons older than the law. "Over the saddle, down into the wolf-wood beyond. The kindred's country. The crown's writ runs thin there, thinner than anywhere, and the wolves—" She stopped. Something moved behind her face, some memory she chose not to share. "The wolves owe the carvers. Remember that, if you meet one. The wolves *owe* us."

"The wolves are stories, Wren."

"So were crown riders in Hollowmere, this time yesterday." Wren pressed a bundle into her arms: the oilcloth, empty now; a pouch of coins; dried meat; a knife with a bone handle that Petra recognized with a lurch as her own first carving, aged twenty years. "Take the north trail. Don't stop at the saddle, don't stop at darkfall, don't stop until the trees close over your head. And Petra—" The old woman's voice dropped to almost nothing. "If they take you, give them nothing. Not your name, not your fear, and above all not your gift. A carver's voice can be bent to wicked uses in the wrong hands. Promise me."

"I promise." The words tasted like ash from the fire. "Come with me. Wren, please. You're on the list too, surely. You taught me everything—"

"I am seventy-one years old and my knees are shot and someone must stay to look the riders in the eye and convince them the mad old carver woman knows nothing worth burning her for." Wren smiled, and it was the worst thing Petra had ever seen, that smile, brave and cracked as old glaze. "Someone must stay to buy you the hours. That is what mentors are for, child. We are the ones who stand in doors."

Down in the valley, a dog began to bark and did not stop.

* * *

They came across the ford at noon, and Petra watched them from the high pasture through Wren's spyglass with her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

Eight riders. Not the gaudy cavalry of festival-day rumors but lean, hard-marched men on lean, hard-marched horses, in dun cloaks and dull steel, moving with the unhurried economy of professionals. At their center rode a single figure in black — not the black of mourning but the black of office, cut close and severe, and even at this distance Petra could see the silver chain of his rank catching the light.

She passed the glass to her mother, whose face went white and stayed white, and then to the three elders who had climbed up to look, and none of them said anything, because there was nothing to say. The impossible had crossed the river. The silence of Hollowmere had been forded like any shallow stream, by men who hadn't needed to hear it at all.

"They're stopping at Wren's," murmured Elder Cassock, who had buried two wives and a son and therefore recognized catastrophe by its gait. "Good. Good. That buys time. Girl — north trail, now, while they're asking their questions."

Her mother grabbed her then, and held her hard enough to hurt, and Petra pressed her face into her mother's neck and breathed in woodsmoke and bread and home, and neither of them cried, because crying took time and time had just become a currency they couldn't afford.

"Over the mountain," her mother whispered into her hair. "Find the wolves. Find whoever your grandmother's grandmother trusted. And live, Petra. Whatever else happens, whatever you have to become — *live.*"

Then hands were pulling them apart, gently, the way you peel a bandage, and Petra was running.

She took the shepherd's path first, then cut east where the heather gave way to scree, following the thread of white she'd known since childhood, the Back Door, the crack in the valley's shell. Behind her, small with distance, she could see the cluster of figures at Wren's gate, and she made herself not look again, because looking was a kind of staying and staying was death.

The climb took everything. Twice she slipped on wet stone and caught herself on hands already raw. Once she stopped, lungs tearing, certain she'd heard hoofbeats on the trail below — but it was only her own blood, loud in her ears, keeping its own frightened time.

At the saddle, where the wind came through the notch in long cold blades, she allowed herself one glance back.

Hollowmere lay below in its bowl of mountains, green and gold and utterly peaceful, smoke rising straight from thirty chimneys, the river glinting like a vein of glass. It looked exactly as it had looked every day of her life. It looked like a place where nothing terrible could happen, which was the whole trick of it, the whole beautiful lie of it. Somewhere down there, Wren was standing in her doorway. Somewhere down there, men in black were reading a list.

Petra stood a moment longer than she should have. Long enough to see, far below on the valley road, a knot of villagers gathered — and at its center, a small commotion, figures moving quickly, and one figure being walked, not dragged, walked with terrible dignity, toward the riders' horses.

She couldn't tell if it was Wren. She told herself it wasn't. She knew, in the place where the dead spoke to her and she could not unhear them, that it was.

Petra Brightwater turned north and went down into the wolf-wood, and the trees closed over her head like a door shutting, and somewhere behind her the valley that forgot went on forgetting, and ahead of her, in the blue dusk under the pines, something four-legged and vast was watching her come — and had been, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the altitude, since the moment she'd crossed the tree line.

Its eyes caught the last light. They were the color of old bone.
