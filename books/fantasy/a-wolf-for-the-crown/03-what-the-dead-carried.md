## 3. What the Dead Carried

The pass above Hollowmere had no name, because no one had ever needed to name it. Nothing came down it, and nothing was meant to go up. It was a seam in the world, a place where the mountains leaned together like conspirators, and for nineteen years Petra had looked at it from her carving bench and thought of it as scenery.

Now it was the only door left.

She climbed through the night, and the valley fell away beneath her in stages — first the roofs of Hollowmere, small and golden-lit, then the river, a gray ribbon stitched through dark fields, then nothing but the dark itself, and the cold coming down off the peaks like a hand pressing her flat against the stone. Twice she stopped and looked back. The second time, she saw the glow of the carver's hall burning, and she did not look again.

Master Aldren had burned the records himself. She could still see his hands, steady and terrible, feeding forty years of her apprenticeship into the fire — the pattern-books, the lineage rolls, the delicate rubbings of every carving she had ever studied. *They will read our dead like ledgers,* he had said. *Burn the ledger.* And then he had shoved her toward the mountain with both hands, an old man's hands, surprisingly strong, and said the words she would carry up the pass like a stone in her chest:

*You are not running from them, girl. You are running toward whatever it is they're afraid of. Find it first.*

The snow began an hour past midnight. It came fine and dry, hissing against the rock, and it erased her trail behind her as if the mountain itself were complicit. Petra was grateful and afraid in equal measure — grateful because Serel Vane's riders could not follow what was not there, afraid because the same mercy extended to her. She could vanish up here, easily, permanently. The dead she had spoken to all her life had never once mentioned what waited above the tree line, and their silence felt like a kind of answer.

She climbed until her legs stopped negotiating and simply obeyed, and then she climbed past that, into the numbness beyond obedience, and when the sky went from black to the color of a drowned man's lips, she saw the notch in the ridge ahead and knew she had crossed out of Hollowmere.

She knew it the way you know a room has gone quiet.

All her life, the valley had pressed against her gift like wool packed in her ears. In Hollowmere the dead were muffled, distant, speaking through bone as if through walls. She had thought that was the nature of death — that the living world was thick and the dead were far away, and carving was the long labor of reaching them.

The moment she crossed the ridge, the silence *tore*.

It came in like a flood through a broken dam. Not one voice — hundreds. Thousands. The air itself was crowded, the snow crowded, the stone under her boots crowded, every dead thing that had ever fallen on this mountain still murmuring where it lay. A shepherd three centuries gone, complaining eternally about the weather. An eagle, puzzled by its own ending. A soldier in armor no kingdom she knew had ever forged, counting his wounds over and over like coins. They pressed against her, curious, hungry, and Petra dropped to her knees in the snow and pressed her palms over her ears, which helped not at all, because they were not speaking to her ears.

"Stop," she whispered. "Stop, stop —"

They did not stop. But they thinned, the way a crowd thins when someone important enters it. The voices drew back and back, and the mountain's dead went quiet in a great ring around her, the way water goes still around a dropped stone, and Petra knelt in that ring of silence, shaking, and understood that she had spent her whole life in a whisper and the world outside the valley *shouted*.

*This is what they're afraid of,* she thought. Not me. This. What I'd be, out here.

She got up. There was nothing to do but get up. Behind her lay the hunters and the burning hall and Master Aldren's last shove between the shoulder blades; ahead lay a world that was too loud to survive in unless she learned to close her ears. So she walked, and by slow degrees, by gritting her teeth and imagining the wool of the valley packed back around her skull — a trick, a pretense, a lie she told her own gift — the shouting dimmed to a murmur, and the murmur to a sea-sound, and she went on.

* * *

She found the wolf at midmorning, in a hollow below the ridge where the wind had scoured the snow down to bare stone.

She saw the bones first and thought, absurdly, *someone's dog wandered up here and died.* Then she got closer and stopped thinking anything at all.

The skeleton was enormous. It lay on its side in the wind-scoured hollow, articulated still, as though it had lain down and simply stayed — ribs like the frame of a boat, a skull longer than her forearm, and legs that were wrong for any wolf she had ever seen in the valley's winter drawings. Too long. Too fine. Built for running in a way that made her legs ache just to look at them, the way you ache watching a dancer.

And every bone was carved.

She knelt without deciding to kneel. The script ran along the femurs and up the spine, fine as spider silk, dense as the oldest pattern-books Aldren had shown her only once and then locked away — no, older than those. Older than the lineage rolls he'd burned. The cuts were not decoration. She had spent nineteen years learning the grammar of bone, and this was grammar, this was *language*, laid over the skeleton like a second skeleton, and it predated every kingdom whose dead she had ever questioned. She knew that the way she knew the weight of a carving knife: by feel, by certainty, by the way the script hummed against her fingertips before she even touched it.

The dead around her had gone silent again. The whole ring of the mountain's murmuring dead had drawn back from this hollow, leaving a clean circle of quiet, and Petra realized with a slow cold slide of understanding that the silence was not reverence.

It was *avoidance*.

"Old one," she said softly, in the way she'd been taught to open — courtesy first, always, because the dead remember being people even when they were never people. "I see you. I mean no theft."

Nothing. Not the flat nothing of an empty grave — she knew that nothing, it was like a room with the furniture gone. This was a *held* nothing. A breath drawn and kept.

Her hand hovered over the great skull. The script there was different — denser, cut deeper, and at the brow it gathered into a single mark she had seen exactly once before: pressed into wax on the flyleaf of Aldren's oldest book, which he had shown her at fourteen and said, *this is the seal your line served, and pray you never have to.* She had thought he meant the valley's founding families. She had thought a great many wrong things.

She touched the mark.

The world *rang*.

Not sound — she had no word for it, and she suspected there was no word in any living language, because the thing that happened next had not happened in the living world for a very long time. It rang through her bones, through the bones of the mountain, through the bones of everything, and the script on the skeleton lit — not with light, exactly, but with *attention*, every cut line waking like a vein remembering blood — and the hollow filled with the smell of pine and far snow and something wilder, musk and cold air and running, the smell of a body she did not have.

Then the wolf stood up out of its own skeleton.

It did not rise the way the dead rose for her — gathering, reluctant, like smoke deciding to be a shape again. It rose the way a living thing rises: all at once, with intent, forelegs first, head swinging toward her, and its eyes were not the pale lamp-flame she knew from the dead but a burning gold that had *opinions*.

Petra fell backward into the snow. Her knife was in her hand before she knew she'd drawn it, which would have been funny if she could breathe, a bone-carver threatening a ghost with the tool of her trade.

The ghost looked at the knife. Something moved across its face that she would spend years learning to read and that, in that first moment, she understood perfectly: *amusement.*

"You cut that mark," it said. Its voice was not a voice. It arrived inside her the way the dead's voices did, but lower, layered, a chord instead of a note. "Nineteen years, and the first thing you do with the blood-right is threaten me with a paring knife."

"I—" Her breath came back wrong. "You know how long—"

"I know what the seal told me." The wolf paced a slow half-circle around her, and where its paws touched the snow, the snow did not print. "I felt you cross the ridge. I felt the valley's hush fall off you like a coat. And then I felt you kneel at my bones and *reach*, the way they reach, the way the founding line reached. So." It sat, abruptly, tail curling around its paws, an oddly domestic gesture from a creature the size of a pony. "Say your name, carver. Properly. The old way."

"I don't know the old way."

Something flickered in the gold eyes — not anger. A kind of tired recognition, as if it had expected exactly this answer and had hoped, against its own judgment, to be wrong.

"Then say it the new way," the wolf said. "It will hold. Barely. Everything holds barely, now."

"Petra." Her voice came out small in the vast ringing silence of the hollow. "Petra Brightwater. Of Hollowmere. Apprentice carver." A breath. "Last apprentice carver, as of last night."

"Petra Brightwater." The wolf said it slowly, tasting it, and where its voice touched the carved script on its own bones, the lines brightened faintly, like coals breathed on. "I am called Hallow. I was scout of the eastern verges, of the kindred of the Wolf Gate, and I have been dead on this mountain for two hundred and eleven years, waiting for a hand of the founding line to touch my brow." He inclined his great head, and the gesture was formal, archaic, and utterly sincere. "You are bound to me by blood-right, Petra Brightwater. And I to you."

"I didn't bind anything."

"No. You *inherited* it." He rose and paced again, restless, and Petra had the strange sense that the pacing was for his own benefit, a habit worn into the ghost the way paths wear into hillsides. "Your grandmothers' grandmothers' grandmother stood where you stand and spoke the words over my body when I fell holding the pass against the first breach. The covenant-script on my bones is the record of that oath. It does not fade. It *waits*. And now it has stopped waiting, and I confess I would like to know what took the founding line so long."

"I don't—" Petra's hands were shaking; she sheathed the knife so she wouldn't have to look at them. "I don't know what you're talking about. I carve the dead's last words for their families. That's all. That's the whole of it. Bones that whisper, that's what we call it, bones that whisper. Nobody ever said anything about a covenant, or a founding line, or—"

"Wolves," Hallow said.

"Wolves," Petra agreed, and then the word turned over in her mouth and showed its other side, and she looked at the too-long legs, the too-fine skull, the script older than any kingdom, and said, very quietly: "You're not a wolf."

"I am wolf-*kindred*," Hallow said, "which is a different thing, and the difference is the entire reason your valley exists." He stopped pacing. The gold eyes fixed on her, and the amusement was gone out of them, and what remained was old and cold and heavy as the glacier she could see, far to the north, from the mouth of the hollow. "And the difference is dying, carver. Has been dying for two hundred years. The winter you people keep blaming on witches — it is not weather. It is a wound. And the last hand of the founding line just knelt in the snow two hundred and eleven years late, holding a paring knife."

* * *

They argued for most of the afternoon. Petra would not, later, have called it arguing; Hallow, when she told the story in after years, always corrected her. *You argued,* he said. *I explained. Repeatedly. To someone who kept asking the same question in different words, which is a thing the living do when the truth is too large to swallow whole.*

The truth, as he laid it out in the failing light with the glacier glowing pink and gold to the north, was this: there had been two realms once, side by side, folded together like hands. The world of the living, and the world of the kindred — and between them, a gate, and over the gate, a covenant, and under the covenant, a line of carvers who kept the words of binding cut into living bone. The covenant was not a wall. It was a promise, renewed at each midwinter by blood and voice: the kindred would hold the wild places and the long dark at bay; humanity would hold the hearth-fires and the memory. Each realm the other's guarantee. Each realm the other's *witness*.

"And the winter?" Petra asked. She had built a small fire against the hollow's wall, more for her hands than for warmth; Hallow sat in it without effect, which she was trying not to find unnerving.

"The winter is the promise failing." He said it flatly, without drama, which made it worse. "A covenant is a living thing. It must be fed. Voice, and blood, and keeping. When the last of your line went silent — when the valley sealed itself and the carvings stopped and the words were burned or buried or forgotten — the covenant began to starve. It does not die quickly. It dies the way a great tree dies, from the farthest twigs inward. The gate is nearly shut. The kindred's realm grows dark and lean and *hungry*, and the hunger bleeds through into yours as cold. Your queen's hunters burn gifted children in cages because they cannot bear to look north and admit the truth: that their grandfathers' grandfathers broke a promise, and the world is presenting the bill."

Petra stared into the fire. The dead murmured far off beyond the hollow's ring of silence, and for once their murmur was a comfort, proof that the world still had witnesses in it.

"My mentor burned our records last night," she said. "The crown's riders came into a valley where magic can't follow. They shouldn't have found us. Nothing finds us."

"Nothing *magical*," Hallow said. "Men on horses are not magic. They are worse. They are *persistent*." He tilted his head. "They carried a list. I felt them pass below the ridge two days ago — dead men on their conscience, rattling like coins in a purse. One of them is wrong, Petra Brightwater. Not evil. Wrong. There is a dead thing walking beside him, wearing him like a coat, and it is older than him and colder than him and it *knows what you are*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, snow slithered off a ledge.

"It's hunting me."

"It is hunting the last hand of the founding line before it can reach the gate," Hallow said. "Yes. Which brings us to the question I have been circling for three hours, carver, and I will ask it plainly, because the sun is going and I have waited two hundred and eleven years and I find my patience is not what it was." He rose, and even as a ghost he seemed to fill the hollow, and his voice came into her like a hand closing around her wrist — not cruel, but unbreakable. "The blood-right binds you to me, and me to you. It is not a leash. It is not a summoning. It is a *partnership*, sworn in the old grammar, and it can be refused — by either of us. Refusal is clean. Refusal costs nothing but everything it costs." The gold eyes held hers. "So. The crown is behind you, the winter is before you, and the gate is three hundred miles north under a glacier. Will you walk with me, Petra Brightwater? Will you keep the old promise, that neither of us ever had the chance to keep?"

Petra looked at him for a long time. At the script glowing faintly along his ghostly flanks, the oath of some grandmother she'd never known, waiting in the snow for two centuries like a letter unopened. She thought of Master Aldren's hands shoving her toward the mountain. *Find it first.*

"I'm not a hero," she said. "I'm a girl who talks to bones."

"Good," said Hallow. "Heroes die of their own importance. Carvers get things done." He turned his head north, toward the darkening glacier, and the last light caught his eyes and set them burning. "Now sleep, blood-of-my-blood. Tomorrow we go down out of the mountains — and I must warn you, the first thing you will see is ash."

Below them, far to the north, in the last light, a village was burning.
