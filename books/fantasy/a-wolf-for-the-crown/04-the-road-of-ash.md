## 4. The Road of Ash

The pass spat them out onto the world like a stone from a sling.

Petra had imagined the lowlands all her life — green and soft and full of people, the way travelers described them in Hollowmere's tavern when they still came. What she found instead was smoke.

It rose in columns ahead of them, six or seven of them, leaning east on the wind, and beneath the columns the land lay brown and stubbled where it should have been gold with harvest. The road below the pass was a scar of packed earth running north to south, and along it moved people. Not travelers. Refugees. Carts piled with children and chickens and grandmothers, families walking with their beds strapped to their backs, a man carrying a door. Carrying a *door*, as if a house were something you could take with you when everything else burned.

Hallow's ghost paced beside her, insubstantial as heat-shimmer, his grey fur stirring in winds that did not touch him. To anyone else he would have been nothing at all. To Petra he was a weight at her shoulder, warm in a way that made no sense for something dead.

"Small villages," he said. His voice was the voice of the bone she'd carried out of the mountain, dry and resonant, a sound felt in the teeth more than heard. "That is how they do it. Burn the edges first. The center watches and tells itself the fire will stop."

"You've seen this before."

"I have seen three hundred years of this before." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Covenants fail slowly, carver. Then all at once."

She walked. There was nothing else to do but walk, down the switchbacks, past a shrine to some river god whose face had been chiseled off — not weathered, *chiseled*, recently, the marks still pale in the old stone. Hallow paused at it and growled, low, a sound she felt through the soles of her boots.

"They're afraid of anything that listens," he said. "Fear makes poor masons."

By late afternoon they reached the first village, or what remained of one. The writ had come through here — she could see it nailed to the well post even at a distance, a sheet of vellum gone soft with rain, the crown's seal dripping wax-red like a wound. She should have kept to the trees. Hallow told her to keep to the trees. But the smell reached her on the wind — burnt thatch and burnt grain and something underneath both that she refused to name — and her feet took her down the slope before her mind agreed to go.

The village had perhaps forty homes once. Now it had chimneys. They stood in rows like grave markers, black against the sky, and between them the ash lay ankle-deep and fine as flour. A dog followed her at a distance, ribs showing, too wary to come close. Somewhere a goat complained about the state of the world.

"Petra." Hallow had stopped at the edge of the square. "There are dead here. Many dead. If you listen—"

"I know." Her gift didn't need magic to work; that was the horror of it. Hollowmere's silence had never muted the bones, only everything else. Here, outside the valley, the dead pressed against her hearing like faces against glass. The charred beams of the houses still held echoes of the people who'd died under them. Not words, not yet — but a pressure, a crowd-silence, the way a room feels when everyone in it has stopped breathing to listen.

She knelt in the ash of what had been a bakery — she could tell by the iron oven door lying open like a jaw — and pressed her palm flat to the ground.

The dead spoke all at once, and she wished they hadn't.

*—the riders came at dawn, they came at dawn, we thought the river would hold them, the ford has held everyone—*

*—my daughter reads. That's all. She reads the way the old women read, she knows things off the page, and they put her in the wagon—*

*—run, Marta, run into the barley, don't look back, don't—*

Petra snatched her hand back as if the ground had bitten her. Her breath came ragged. Ash clung to the wet tracks on her cheeks.

"The children," she said. "They took the children."

"They always take the children," Hallow said quietly. "A gifted child is worth ten grown witches to the crown. Grown witches have opinions. Children can be shaped." He circled her, restless, his ghost-paws making no prints in the ash. "This is not your war, carver. This happened before you crossed the pass and it would have happened if you had died in your cradle. Your road runs north. The Gate is north. Every day you spend here is a day the winter grows teeth."

"And every day I walk north is a day I spend stepping over bodies." She stood. Her knees were shaking and she locked them. "You said I'm bound to you by blood-right. You said my line forged the covenant. Then tell me — what was the covenant *for*? Was it a fence? A trade agreement?" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Because the elders in Hollowmere taught us it was a promise. That the wolf-kindred would guard the borders of human grief, and humans would guard the wild places from greed, and neither side would let the other fall alone. If that's true, then this—" she gestured at the chimneys, the ash, the chiseled god, "—this isn't just politics going wrong. It's the promise dying. And I'm supposed to be the last person alive who can mend it."

Hallow was silent a long moment. Wind moved through him without resistance, bending the grey shapes of trees behind his shape.

"You argue like a wolf," he said finally. "It is unbecoming. Continue."

* * *

They found the second village still burning.

It stood in a river bend, bigger than the first, and the hunt had not bothered to be tidy here. The writ was nailed to the church door alongside four others, layered like feathers, each newer than the last: *by order of the Queen Regent, all practitioners of unlicensed arts to surrender themselves for assessment; harboring is treason; the assessment wagons depart weekly for Corvane.* Someone had tried to scratch out the word *surrender* and write *run* instead. The crown's men had burned the hand that wrote it, judging by the size of the pyre in the square.

Petra watched the flames eat a granary and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the fire was not chaos. It was arithmetic. Burn the stores, and the survivors must follow the roads to the relief towns. Burn the edges, and the center walks itself into the pens. The inquisitors weren't hunting witches the way hawks hunted hares. They were herding.

"There." Hallow's muzzle swung toward the far side of the square, where the smoke thinned. "Do you see?"

She saw.

The wagons stood in the shade of the church wall, three of them, heavy-wheeled, their beds caged with iron bar. And in the cages—

Children.

Six of them, maybe seven, small shapes huddled together in the largest cage for warmth, because no one had given them blankets. Two smaller cages held one child each: a boy who sat perfectly still with his hands over his ears, and a girl who stared at nothing while frost formed on the bars around her, blooming and melting, blooming and melting, in time with her breathing. The guards around the wagons stamped their feet against the cold and laughed at something, passing a flask, ordinary men doing ordinary work.

One of the children in the big cage was singing. Very softly. A lullaby, half-remembered, the kind sung to children so they'll sleep through storms. The others had joined in by the end of it, barely audible, a thread of sound under the crackle of the burning granary. Petra stood in the shadow of a collapsed stable and listened until the song ran out, and then the silence after it was the worst thing she had ever heard.

"Petra." Hallow's voice was very gentle now, which meant he already knew. "There are eight men. Perhaps more in the church. You have a knife you do not know how to use and a gift that frightens sheep. The Gate is five days north. Midwinter does not wait."

"I know."

"The covenant cannot be reforged by a corpse."

"I *know*." She pressed her fist against her mouth. Below her fear, below the guilt, something else was setting like concrete. She thought of her mentor, Maera, shoving her toward the pass with the smoke of the burning records rising behind them. *Run, girl. Run and don't look back. That's the whole of the craft — survive long enough to matter.* Maera had believed it. Petra had believed it, all the way down the mountain.

But Maera wasn't here. Maera hadn't seen the girl with frost on her bars, wearing a dress sewn by someone who loved her, staring at nothing because whatever lived inside her frightened even herself. Nobody had carved that child's bone yet. Nobody had asked her name. To the crown she was cargo, assessed and listed, bound for Corvane in a cage marked with the regent's seal.

And Petra Brightwater was going to be the last heir of the founding line, the woman who mended the covenant between two worlds, and she was going to get there by walking past a cage of children and telling herself the fire would stop.

Survival alone, she thought, is just complicity with better posture.

"Hallow," she said. "How much of you is left? Could you — I don't know. Frighten them. A dead wolf in the smoke. Men shoot at wolves, but they *flee* from ghosts."

He turned his head and looked at her for a long moment, and in his eyes there was something ancient and tired and, unexpectedly, pleased.

"You are asking me to haunt crown soldiers on behalf of stolen witch-children," he said. "Three hundred years ago I swore an oath to guard the borders of human grief. I had begun to think no one remembered the wording." His form gathered itself, denser, darker, the ghost-light around him sharpening into something that hurt to look at directly. "I can be terrible for perhaps a hundred heartbeats. Use them well."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, lasted ninety seconds and went wrong in eighty of them.

Hallow went first, pouring himself up the street like fog given intent, and the moment the nearest guard saw him — a dead wolf the size of a pony, eyes like banked coals, walking out of a burning village — the man's flask hit the cobbles and his scream brought the other seven running, which was the point. Petra ran the other way, low along the church wall, knife in her teeth because her hands were shaking too badly to trust, and got the lock of the big cage under her fingers.

Iron. Cold iron, and her gift sputtered against it like a wet wick. She couldn't whisper the lock open; there was nothing in metal to hear. So she did it the honest way, jamming the knife's tip into the mechanism, swearing under her breath in the bone-carver's cadence, hands slipping on frost-slick iron while behind her men screamed about hellhounds and one of them fired a crossbow bolt that passed through Hallow entirely and buried itself in a doorpost.

The lock broke. The door swung. Small hands grabbed hers.

"Up," she whispered. "Out, quiet, follow the wall, there's a gap in the hedge — *go*." Five of them went, stumbling, silent as mice, the oldest no more than twelve and already steering the youngest by the shoulders. Petra turned to the small cages.

The frost-girl looked at her then, straight through her, eyes the grey of deep ice, and the frost on the bars bloomed so hard it cracked.

And that was when the shouting changed. The panic drained out of the guards' voices and something colder replaced it, and a voice Petra had never heard but knew instantly — a voice like a file drawn slow across silver — cut across the square:

"*Enough. Stand down. It's a haunting, not a siege — and hauntings have authors.*"

Lantern light swung. Found her.

Petra froze with her hands on the frost-girl's cage, kneeling in the open, lit like an actor on a stage. The guards parted, and through them came a tall man in the grey-and-silver of the crown's inquisition, unhurried, dusting ash from his sleeve. He was younger than she expected. Tired-looking. His eyes, when they found hers, did not gleam with zealotry or cruelty.

They looked, unbearably, *understanding*.

"Well," said Serel Vane, inquisitor of the crown, and glanced at the empty big cage, the fleeing children, the crossbow bolt standing in a doorpost where no wolf had been. "The valley sent me a bone-carver, and the bone-carver sends me a ghost story." He drew a slender blade, not threateningly — almost wearily. "You're on my list, Petra Brightwater. You've been on my list since before you were born."

Behind her, in the dark of the hedge-gap, the smallest child began, very softly, to sing.
