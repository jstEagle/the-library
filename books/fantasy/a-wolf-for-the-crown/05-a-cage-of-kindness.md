## 5. A Cage of Kindness

The village was called Brackenholt, though by the time Petra reached it, the name had become a cruelty — a promise of green things in a place where nothing green remained. She came down from the ridge at dusk with ash on her lips and Hallow's voice a low growl at the back of her skull, and she saw the cages before she saw the houses.

There were six of them. Iron-barred wagons, the kind used for hauling hogs to market, parked in a ring around the village well like a corral. Torches staked at the corners threw orange light across the bars, and behind the bars, small shapes huddled in the straw.

Children.

Petra had seen the burnings from the ridge — the black rectangles where houses had stood, the fields gone to stubble and cinder under the hunt's writ. She had told herself, *keep walking, keep walking, the dead will guide you, you have a Gate to find.* Hallow had told her the same, in his way. *You are one carver. They are an army. The covenant does not need your corpse.*

But the covenant had not put a child's face in her mind. The dead had.

The bone at her throat — a finger-joint of the woman from the pass, worn smooth now against her skin — had been whispering all day. *Small ones. Small ones behind iron.* The dead of Brackenholt, the ones whose houses still smoked, had been pressing against her hearing like beggars at a door, and every one of them said the same thing: *the children, the children, the children.*

"You are going to do something stupid," Hallow said. He was beside her in the way he was always beside her — not flesh, not quite, but a grey presence at the edge of vision, a cold weight against her left side where the blood-bond sat. To anyone else there would have been only a girl standing alone on a ridge, talking to the dark.

"Yes," Petra said.

"At least be stupid *well*. There are four soldiers on the wagons and six more sleeping in the tithe-barn. The one with the red plume is drunk. The one by the well is not, and he checks the locks every hour on the hour. It has been fifty minutes."

Petra almost smiled. A dead wolf-kin scout, three hundred years in the ground, giving her the guard rotations of a crown foraging party. The world had become a strange place since the ford.

"Then I have ten minutes," she said.

"You have less. The drunk one is about to knock over a torch, and when he does, the sober one will start his rounds early."

She was already moving.

* * *

She went down through the village the way water goes — along the low paths, behind the burned granary, through the gap where a garden fence had been pulled up for firewood. The dead of Brackenholt walked with her, or their voices did, murmuring directions from the bones in the ash. *Left here. The dog is dead, do not fear the yard. The man sleeping in the tithe-barn snores; you will hear him before he sees you.* She had never been guided by so many voices at once. It was like walking through a crowd of whispers, all of them leaning toward her, all of them wanting something done that they could no longer do themselves.

The nearest wagon stood in torch-shadow, its iron bars black with old rust and new soot. Inside, three children — she could count them now, three small shapes pressed together against the cold, a girl perhaps ten and two boys younger, all of them with the grey-lipped stillness of children who had learned that crying brought soldiers.

The lock was a crown-issue padlock, iron, heavy. Hallow had told her that. What Hallow had not told her — what she discovered only when she knelt and put her fingers to the cold metal — was that the lock had been forged in a fire where something had died. A horse, she thought. A carthorse, old and overworked, burned in a kiln or a barn or a pyre. Its echo was still in the metal, faint and confused, going in circles.

"I'm sorry," Petra breathed to it. "I'm sorry, old mother. But I need your strength, not your shape."

She had no chisel. She had her thumbnail, and the trick her mentor had shown her only once, in the years when the valley still pretended her gift was a rumor: that bone and iron were both once alive, that everything forged remembers its making, and that a carver's task was never to *force* the memory but to *ask* it to move.

She asked. The lock's memory of being solid softened like wax in a warm hand, and the shackle fell open with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a bell.

The girl's eyes came open in the dark of the wagon. They were huge, and they were not afraid, and that frightened Petra more than screaming would have. Children who had stopped being afraid of soldiers were children who had already decided what the world was.

"Are you the bone witch?" the girl whispered.

"I'm nobody's witch," Petra said. "I'm getting you out. Can you walk?"

The girl nodded. The two boys stirred, blinking. One of them had a burn scab on his cheek shaped like a moth. Petra lifted the smallest over the bars — he weighed nothing, nothing at all, and that weightlessness went through her like a blade — and set him down in the straw.

Then the torches went out.

All of them. Not blown by wind — the night was still — but snuffed, in sequence, around the ring of wagons, as if a hand were moving through the camp pinching each flame between finger and thumb. And in the sudden dark, a voice spoke from the direction of the well. Calm. Unhurried. A voice that had been waiting.

"Ten minutes, forty seconds," it said. "You were slower than I expected, carver."

* * *

Petra knew the voice. She had heard it across the shallows of the ford, reading names from a list while her mentor's records burned behind her. Serel Vane. The inquisitor with the grey eyes and the patient hands.

She put herself between the soldiers' firelight and the children, and she reached for the only weapon she had ever owned: the dead. But Brackenholt's dead were ash and silence around her, and the bone at her throat gave her nothing she could use, and she understood, with a cold drop of her stomach, that she had walked into this ring of wagons exactly as a lamb walks into a pen.

Torches relit — his soldiers, moving at his signal, unhurried. And there he stood by the well, no longer in the grey riding cloak of the ford but in a hunt-officer's black, the crown's silver sun pinned at his throat. He was younger than she had thought. That was the worst of it. He had a face that might have been kind, in another life, on another road.

"Step away from the wagon," Vane said.

"They haven't done anything." Her voice cracked on it. She hated that. "They're *children*. What could they possibly have done?"

"Exist," Vane said, simply. "It is enough. It has always been enough." He came closer, boots quiet on the frozen mud, and his soldiers closed the ring behind him with the ease of men who had done this a hundred times. "Petra Brightwater. Of Hollowmere. Carver's line. Do you know how long your name has been on my list?"

"Long enough that you burned a valley to find me."

"I burned nothing." Something flickered across his face — too fast to name, gone before she could read it. "The writ burns. I only carry it. There is a difference, and I tell myself it is a difference every morning, and every morning I believe it a little less." He stopped an arm's length from her, and his grey eyes went to the children in the wagon, and stayed there a moment too long. "You freed the lock without touching it. I watched. Do you know how rare that is? To work iron? Iron resists the gift. It is the one thing the old texts agree on."

"Then I'm a marvel," Petra said. "Write it on your list."

Something that was almost a smile moved at the corner of his mouth and died there. "You are not what I expected."

"What did you expect?"

"A crone. A fanatic. Someone who *believes*." He said the last word the way a man spits out a stone. "Not a girl who talks to her necklace and cries over children she's never met." He glanced at the soldiers. "Back to the fire. All of you. This is inquisitor's business."

They went. The ring of wagons stood in torchlight and silence, and Petra stood in the middle of it with her heart hammering and three children behind her watching everything.

"You're going to take me to the capital," she said.

"I am going to offer you a bargain," Vane said. "And you are going to hate it, and then you are going to take it, because you are the kind of person who takes bargains like it." He clasped his hands behind his back — she noticed, absurdly, that his knuckles were scarred, old and white, the marks of a life of labor, not letters. "But first, a demonstration. Since you have shown me yours."

He raised one hand, palm up, toward the dead fire pit beside the well. And the ashes *stirred*.

Not wind. Not trickery. The grey ash lifted, swirled, and settled into a shape — a bird, a small wren of ash, standing on the cold stones, its head cocked. It looked at Petra with two hollows where eyes should be, and it shivered, and it came apart, falling back into grey.

Petra's mouth went dry. "You—"

"I was six years old," Vane said quietly. "A miller's son, in a village much like this one. I moved flour across a room to keep it from the rats. My mother's brother saw. My mother's brother reported me, because reporting was the only way to prove the family clean." His voice did not shake. It had been worn smooth, like a stone in a river, by years of not shaking. "They took me to the capital. They tested me. And an old inquisitor looked at me across a table and said: *you are useful, boy, or you are ash. Choose.*"

The wren of ash had been him. The dead fire, the small gray bird. A man who could raise only cinders and dust, in a world where the crown burned everything, and so he had become the crown's hand to keep from becoming its fuel.

"So you hunt your own kind," Petra said. The words came out flat, and she meant them to wound.

"I *serve*," Vane said, and now something did shake, deep underneath, like ice over moving water. "I serve, and because I serve, there are children in that wagon who are alive, who were not culled on the road like the others, who will reach the capital's schools instead of a pyre. Do you understand what I am telling you? Every name I take alive is a name I keep from the fire. It is a filthy arithmetic. I do it every day. I have done it for twenty years." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and for one moment the inquisitor was gone and there was only a tired man with scarred knuckles. "And now the arithmetic has put you in front of me, Petra Brightwater, and I am going to make you an offer that I have never made anyone on my list."

The bone at her throat was cold. Hallow's presence pressed against her side like a warning — *do not trust, do not trust* — but Hallow had never stood in a burned village. Hallow had never been six years old and ratted out by his own blood.

"Say it," Petra said.

"Come with me. Willingly. Tonight. You will be treated as a guest, not a prisoner — as much as that word means anything coming from me. In return, these three go free. Not to the schools. Not to the wagons. Free — I will write them into my ledger as dead, culled on the road, and I will pay a widow in the next village to take them in. They will vanish. No one hunts the dead." He paused. "You have my oath on it. I know what my oath is worth to you. It is all I have, so I am spending it."

Petra looked at the wagon. The girl was watching her with those unafraid eyes, and the small boy with the moth-shaped scab had crept forward and taken hold of the older girl's sleeve, and none of them understood what was being weighed on their behalf.

"If I refuse," Petra said slowly, "you take all four of us."

"I take all four of you," Vane agreed. "And the children go on the list, and the list goes to the capital, and I will spend twenty years of filthy arithmetic trying to keep them breathing, and I may fail, because I fail, Petra Brightwater. I fail more often than I succeed. That is what serving costs." He held out his hand. Not to shake — palm up, open, the same gesture he had used to raise the ash-wren. An offering. "Or you come with me tonight, and they live. That is the whole of the bargain. There is no third door."

There was always a third door. Her mentor had died saying so, shoving her toward the pass with the carver records burning behind her. *Run, girl, run, the Gate is waiting, you are the last of the line.* The covenant. The winter. The wolf-kindred starving behind their sealed gate. The dead king she had not yet heard of, the midwinter night she did not yet understand. All of it waiting for her at the end of a road that ran through the capital, and here was the crown's own hand offering to carry her there — at the price of three small lives she could save right now, tonight, with a single word.

*Survival alone is complicity.* She had learned that on the Road of Ash, watching the cages roll past. But this — this was the other edge of the same blade. To save these three, she had to put herself in the crown's grip, and everything the dead had shown her, everything Hallow had bled into her mind across the bond, said the crown's grip was where the covenant went to die.

"Petra." Hallow's voice, low, at the edge of hearing. "The wolf in me says: never barter with the hunter. But—" a pause, the first she had ever heard from him, "—the scout in me says: he left the lock unguarded for ten minutes. He *wanted* you to try. He has been herding you toward this offer since the ford."

She had known that. She had known it and walked in anyway, because the children were behind iron and the dead had asked.

Petra looked at Serel Vane's open hand. Then past it, at the children.

"Swear it to me properly," she said. "Not to me. To them." She turned and lifted the smallest boy up so he could see over the wagon bars, his moth-scarred cheek pale in the torchlight. "Swear where the dead can hear you. Because I promise you, inquisitor — the dead of this village are standing all around your fire, and they remember every oath ever sworn in this ring of wagons, and they *collect*."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. Then he turned, slowly, to face the dark beyond the torches — the burned houses, the ash fields, the well where so many of Brackenholt's dead had gone down screaming — and he knelt. The crown's silver sun at his throat caught the light as he bowed his head to a village he had been sent to empty.

"By the ash I raise and the ash I am," he said, "these three are dead to the crown. Let no writ find them. Let no list hold them. So I swear, and if I break it, let my gift turn on me and my own fire take me."

The words hung in the frozen air. And all around the ring of wagons, so faint that Petra alone could have sworn she heard it, the dead of Brackenholt sighed — a long, cold exhale, like wind through winter wheat. Acceptance. Witness.

Vane rose. There was ash dust on the knee of his black coat, and he did not brush it off.

"Your answer, carver."

Petra set the boy down gently inside the wagon. She looked at the girl one last time — those unafraid eyes, memorizing her — and mouthed, *live*.

Then she turned to the man who had hunted her across half a kingdom, and held out her own wrist.

"Bind me," she said. "And know this, Serel Vane. I am going to your capital because it serves *my* purpose, not yours. And when I find out what your crown has done to the covenant — and I will — every filthy sum in your arithmetic will come due. For both of us."

Vane took her wrist. His grip was careful, almost gentle, the grip of a man handling something he was afraid of breaking — or afraid of being broken by. He did not bind it. He simply held it a moment, and something passed across his grey eyes that might have been recognition: one caged thing looking at another.

"Welcome to the gilded road, Petra Brightwater," he said. "I only pray you never learn what I learned the day I took the crown's silver — that the worst cage is the one you build yourself, one compromise at a time, and call it kindness."

Behind them, the ash-wren stirred once more on the cold stones of the fire pit, lifted its small grey head, and sang — a sound with no throat in it, no breath, a song made purely of memory and grief — before crumbling back into the dark.

And far to the north, beneath a glacier that no living map named, something ancient turned in its sleep, and began, very slowly, to listen.
