## 6. The Gilded Road

The cage was gilded.

That was the first thing Petra understood about the crown's mercy, three days out from the burning villages: they had not chained her. They had *dressed* her. A traveling coach with velvet seats and a lacquered roof, four gray horses, an honor guard of six riders in the queen regent's silver-and-black. Serel Vane sat across from her with a ledger on his knees and his hands folded atop it like a man at prayer, and outside the window the world went white.

Snow had begun to fall in earnest now — too early, too thick. The fields they passed lay under it like corpses under shrouds. Shepherds stood in doorways watching the coach go by, and Petra watched them back through the glass, and wondered what they saw. A lady, perhaps. A prisoner. A witch.

She touched the window. The glass was cold enough to bite.

"You could have put me in a wagon," she said. "With bars."

"I could have put you in irons." Vane did not look up from his ledger. "The regent's roads are bad enough without making them worse. A dead captive is a wasted journey. A resentful one is worse."

"And what am I? A grateful one?"

That earned her a glance — brief, measuring, gone again. "You are a guest of the crown until Corvane decides otherwise. I would advise you to remember which word carries the weight."

*Guest.* Petra leaned back against the velvet and let the road jolt through her spine. Three children rode somewhere behind them in a proper wagon, warm and fed and alive because she had traded herself for them, and every mile north that truth sat heavier in her chest. Not regret. She checked for regret each morning the way other people checked their bootlaces, and each morning it was not there. But its absence was not the same as peace. It was more like a bone set badly — healed, but never quite right again, aching when the weather turned.

The weather was turning everywhere.

* * *

**From the journal of Serel Vane, Inquisitor of the Second Chair:**

*Day nine out of the Ashlands. The Brightwater girl sleeps like a soldier — light, facing the door. She has asked me eleven questions since we took the road. I have answered two.*

*The children are being sent to the foundling house at Marrow's Cross, as promised. I keep my word. It is the only currency I have ever been permitted to hold.*

*The snow is wrong. I know snow; I grew up north of here, before the capital taught me to be ashamed of it. This falls too straight, too silent, like something holding its breath. The old men in the last village said the river ice sang at night. They were afraid to say more with my badge on my chest.*

*They are right to be afraid. Just not of me. Or not only of me.*

*I wonder if she can hear the dead in this coach. I wonder what they would say about me, if she asked them. I wonder why I hope she never does.*

* * *

Petra learned the shape of the court the way she'd once learned the grain of bone: slowly, by touch, by the resistance it offered.

Vane gave her pieces. Never whole ones — he spoke the way a miser spent, one coin at a time, and always as if the coin cost him. But the road was long, the coach was small, and a man who kept a journal wrote his thoughts even when his mouth stayed shut. She caught him at it twice, hunched over the little book by lamplight while she pretended to sleep, and after the second time she began to watch his face when certain subjects came up, reading him the way she read carvings: pressure here, hesitation there, the places where the surface dipped over something hollow.

"The regent," she said on the eleventh day, when the snow had buried the fences along the road to the height of a man's shoulder. "Queen Regent Isolde. She hunts the gifted. Why?"

"Because they exist," Vane said.

"That's not a reason. That's a fear wearing a reason's clothes."

His pen stopped moving. For a long moment the only sound was the horses and the hush of snow, and then he closed the ledger and looked at her properly, and Petra saw again what she'd seen in the burning village — that tired, buried flicker behind his eyes, the thing he starved and could not kill.

"Three years ago," he said, "the winters began to fail. Not cold — *wrong*. Rivers freezing to their beds in a night. Orchards dying root-deep. Calves born white-eyed. The priests called it a judgment. The scholars called it a cycle. Neither explanation fed anyone." He turned his hands over, palms up, as though weighing something invisible. "Then someone at court remembered the old stories. That magic walks hand in hand with winter. That the last long frost, two hundred years ago, ended when the gifted were driven into the sea."

"And people believed that?"

"People believe whatever lets them do what they already wanted to do." He said it flatly, without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "The harvests were failing. The granaries were thin because the regent's council had been skimming them for a decade. And then a reason arrived, gift-wrapped, that made the hunger someone else's fault. You tell me whether people believed it."

Petra thought of the cages in the village square. Of the children's faces pressed between the slats. Of the woman in the Ashlands who had spat at the wagon as it passed — not at Vane, not at the guards, but at the small dark-haired girl inside it, a girl of perhaps eight who had done nothing in her life worse than make rain come when the wells ran dry.

"They needed witches," Petra said quietly. "So they grew some."

Vane's jaw tightened. He picked the ledger back up. "Careful," he said. "That kind of clarity doesn't survive at court. They'll teach you better or break you faster."

"Which will they try first?"

"Neither." He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile — like sunlight on a locked door. "They'll try to make you *useful*. Clarity is only dangerous when no one profits from it."

* * *

**From the journal of Serel Vane:**

*She is quick. Too quick. Today she unpicked the whole architecture of the hunt in four sentences while I pretended to study supply requisitions, and I sat there like a novice letting her do it because hearing it said aloud — by someone else, for once — was like scratching an itch I've carried for nine years.*

*The hunt is a lie. I have known it since my second year in the chair, when I processed a storm-speaker boy of twelve and watched the winter deepen anyway, and understood that we were not curing the disease. We were bleeding the patient so the court wouldn't have to look at the wound.*

*But knowing a lie and surviving it are different arts. I chose survival. I told myself it was strategy. Some nights I still believe that.*

*If she says these things where the wrong ears can hear them, she will die in Corvane before midwinter, and the children's freedom will have bought nothing but a shorter road to the same fire. I should warn her. I should frighten her into silence.*

*I find I cannot decide whether that would be to protect her or to protect myself. With me, the two have always worn the same face.*

* * *

The world worsened as they climbed toward the capital's plateau.

Refugees came down the north road in strings, pulling carts heaped with everything they owned, their breath hanging in clouds. Vane's escort cleared the way with flat authority, and Petra watched faces turn toward the coach — hollowed, furious, hopeless faces — and saw the moment in most of them where recognition arrived. The silver-and-black livery. The Second Chair's badge. Mothers pulled their children close. Men looked away first, which was its own kind of message.

"They hate you," she said, after a column of them had shuffled past, close enough that she could hear a baby crying somewhere in the middle of it.

"Yes."

"Do you hate them back?"

He was quiet for a long time. Through the window she watched a man carrying a grandmother on his back stumble in the snow, right himself, keep walking.

"When I was young," Vane said at last, "my sister could call birds out of the air. Nothing grand. She'd hold out seed and sing a note, and they'd land on her arms like she was a tree. Our village loved her for it." His voice stayed level, recitation-flat, but his knuckles had gone pale around the ledger. "Then the winters came, and the same village burned our barn with her inside it. My father shook the hands of the men who did it. He said it was grief. He said they hadn't meant it."

"What happened to them?"

"Nothing. That's what happened to them. Nothing ever happens to the ones who burn." He turned a page that did not need turning. "I joined the Inquisition two years later. Everyone assumed it was zeal. It was arithmetic. Inside the machine, I could lose names off lists. Outside it, I could do nothing at all."

Petra stared at him. "You've been saving them. All this time. Off your own lists."

"I've been *delaying* them." The correction came sharp, automatic, the reflex of a man who had rehearsed humility like a drill. "There's a difference, and the difference is measured in graves. Don't mistake me for anything good, Brightwater. I'm a man who found the least bad place to stand in a burning house, and I've been standing in it so long I've forgotten how doors work."

"No, you haven't." She said it simply, the way she'd say *this bone wants to be a bird*. "You opened one for those children."

He didn't answer. But that night, when he thought she slept, she heard him writing for a long, long time — and the next morning there was honey in her tea, and he never mentioned it, and neither did she.

* * *

**From the journal of Serel Vane:**

*I have made an error. I let her see the mechanism. Nine years of careful opacity, undone by a girl of twenty-odd with a carver's hands and a refugee's eyes, because she asked questions the way surgeons ask them — cleanly, at the joint, where the cutting hurts least and does most.*

*Worse: I gave her honey in her tea like some lovesick clerk. What is happening to me?*

*No. I know what is happening. It happened nine years ago too, the day I signed my first execution order and discovered that shame, repeated daily, becomes furniture. She has moved the furniture. Everything is where it shouldn't be and I keep bruising myself on truths I had arranged to stop seeing.*

*Corvane will test her within a week of arrival. The regent collects the gifted the way other collectors collect porcelain — displayed, admired, and never, ever trusted near an open window. If the girl bends, she lives as a tool. If she breaks, she dies as an example. If she stands—*

*If she stands, God help us both, because I am no longer certain whose side of the wall I'll be standing on when it happens.*

* * *

On the seventeenth day, the road crested a ridge, and Petra saw the capital for the first time.

Corvane filled the valley below like spilled mercury — a hundred thousand roofs under snow, spires rising out of the white haze, walls wide enough that roads ran along the top of them. Even from this distance she could see the smoke of it, a thousand chimneys breathing into the frozen air, and beneath the smoke, faint as a scar, the pale gleam of the palace on its hill: white stone, white towers, white banners hanging stiff as carved things.

It should have been beautiful. She supposed, distantly, that it was.

But the dead had begun whispering to her again two days ago — the first time since Hollowmere, since the valley's silence had fallen away behind her — and they whispered loudest here, a low tide-murmur rising off the city like heat off summer stone. Thousands of voices, layered and blurred, the accumulated weight of everyone Corvane had ever buried pressing up through the frozen ground. She gripped the edge of the seat. Somewhere in that murmur she could feel *shapes*: grievances, unfinished sentences, old angers banked like coals. A city built on its dead, and the dead very much awake.

"You hear it," Vane said. It wasn't a question. He was watching her face instead of the city. "What is it like?"

"Like standing in a house where everyone is talking in another room," Petra managed. "Forever. And every so often, one of them says your name."

Something crossed his expression — envy, or terror, or both wearing the same coat. "And what do they say about cities?"

She listened. Really listened, letting the murmur resolve the way she'd let a bone resolve into its shape. And what came through, beneath the blur, beneath the centuries, was not random at all. It was a chorus with a single refrain, worn smooth by repetition:

*"Cold. Cold. Cold. Colder than when we died. Colder every year. Ask the wolves. Ask the wolves. Ask the wolves—"*

Petra went still.

"Ask the wolves," she repeated aloud, not meaning to.

Vane's head came up sharply. "What did you say?"

"The dead." Her heart had begun to hammer, slow and heavy, like something turning over in deep water. "They're saying it about the cold. All of them, every generation — the cold comes, and the cold gets worse, and they say *ask the wolves*. Like it's an instruction. Like it's a debt." She looked at him, and the words came out of her before she could weigh them, before caution could catch them. "Serel. Your regent isn't fighting the winter. She's hunting the only people who might know how to end it. The stories — the old stories — they're not superstition. Something was *made* once, between humans and wolves, something that held the cold back. And it's breaking."

For a moment he said nothing at all. The coach rattled down toward the gates, toward the smoke and the murmur and the white palace on its hill, and Serel Vane sat with his ledger forgotten in his lap, looking at her the way a man looks at a map when the territory suddenly rearranges itself.

"The archives," he said finally, very quietly. "The restricted vaults under the palace. There are documents older than the dynasty. I've seen the seals. No one living is permitted to read them." His eyes narrowed. "No one *dead*, either, I imagine."

"The dead are permitted," Petra said. "The dead are the only ones who remember."

Below them, the great gates of Corvane swung open to receive them — black iron faced with silver, tall enough for a giant, and worked across every span of it, so worn by time that only the outline remained, was a single repeating figure.

A wolf, running beside a human figure. Over and over, hundreds of times, all along the wall.

Neither of them spoke. But Petra saw Vane see it — saw his gaze snag on the worn carving, saw his lips move slightly, counting, remembering — and when he looked away his hand had gone, very deliberately, to rest flat against the journal on his knee.

As if to hold something in.

As if to keep something from getting out.
