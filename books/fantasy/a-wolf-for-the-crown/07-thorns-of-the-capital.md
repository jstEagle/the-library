## 7. Thorns of the Capital

Corvane announced itself before Petra ever saw it.

She felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, a dull ache like the onset of a storm — but the sky was clear, hard blue, and the frost on the wagon's iron bars glittered in morning light. Then she understood: it was the dead. So many dead. The city ahead sat atop its own history the way a stone sits atop a beetle, and beneath it, layer upon layer, the bones were *loud*.

"You feel it too." Vane rode beside the cage-wagon, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak that did nothing to soften what he was. He didn't look at her when he spoke. He rarely did. "First time I came here as a boy, I was sick for three days. Thought it was the smell."

"It isn't the smell," Petra said.

"No." A pause. "It isn't."

The road crested a hill and the capital opened below them like a wound. Corvane sprawled across three hills and the river valley between them, a hundred thousand rooftops smoking with ten thousand fires, walls so old they had been built, fallen, and rebuilt until no one could say which stones were which age. Banners hung from every tower — black and silver, the crowned stag of the regency — and above it all, cutting the winter light into shards, rose the Ivory Court, the palace whose spires were said to be carved from the femurs of giants.

Petra gripped the bars. The children huddled behind her — Tam, who was nine and hadn't spoken since the cages; the twins, Sef and Ilse, who clung to each other and to her skirt-hem as if she were the only fixed point in a spinning world. Three days on the road since Vane's bargain, and she still woke each dawn expecting to find them gone, sold off to some other wagon while she slept. They were not gone. Vane kept his word the way a man keeps a knife he doesn't yet know how to use — carefully, and pointed away from himself.

"What happens when we get there?" she asked.

Vane was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. "There's a ceremony. The regent likes the city to see what the crown catches. It's theater. You'll be displayed, then taken inside. The children go to the custody houses." His jaw tightened around the words. "They're fed. They're warm. No one is hurt."

"And then?"

"Then nothing. That's all there is. Being kept is not being hurt, Brightwater. Learn the difference and you'll live longer."

"That's what someone told you once, isn't it?"

His horse shifted. He gathered the reins and rode forward without answering, and Petra watched his back grow small against the city gates, and hated him a little less than she wanted to.

* * *

They brought her through the Gate of Chains at midday, and the crowd was waiting.

She had imagined jeering, rot thrown from windows, the animal hatred of a mob. What she got instead was worse in a quieter way: curiosity. Thousands of faces lining the processional route, craning to see the witch. Mothers lifted children onto shoulders. Shopkeepers abandoned their stalls. A woman near the gate crossed herself with the old sign, thumb to sternum, two fingers to brow — a warding gesture older than any church — and beside her a girl of perhaps twelve stared at Petra with naked, hungry wonder, the way one stares at a story made flesh.

Petra walked between four guards with her hands bound in iron filigree that hummed faintly against her skin. Dampening-iron. She'd learned its taste on the road; it sat on her gift like wet wool over a mouth. Even muffled, though, the dead pressed close. The processional route itself was paved over a graveyard — of course it was; cities are always built on their dead — and as she passed, voices brushed the edges of her mind like moths against glass.

*—the witch, look, look at her, she's just a girl—*

*—my mother said her grandmother saw one burned, on this very street—*

*—she's looking at me. Saints, she's looking at me—*

*I'm cold. Why am I so cold. Someone tell me why I'm so cold.*

That last voice stopped her mid-step. Not a spectator's voice. It came from below, from under the cobbles, thin and frayed and endless. A guard's hand closed on her arm and shoved her forward, but she turned her head, found the spot — a seam between two paving stones, dark with old rain — and held the guard's eyes.

"There's a child buried under this street," she said. Her voice carried further than it should have. The dampening-iron should have prevented that, and yet the crowd went silent anyway, the way water goes still. "Under this exact stone. She's been cold for sixty years because no one marked her grave."

The silence stretched. Somewhere a pigeon burst upward in a clap of wings.

"Move," the guard said hoarsely, and shoved her again, harder, and the crowd's murmur broke over them like a wave — but it was a different murmur now. Not *witch*. Something more uncertain. More afraid, and afraid in a new direction.

From the corner of her eye she caught Vane watching her from his place in the procession, and his expression was unreadable, but he did not order her silenced.

* * *

The display yard stood in the shadow of the Ivory Court itself: a walled enclosure where the crown kept its prizes. There was a scaffold built for show, draped in black-and-silver bunting, and a line of iron cages along the east wall, and it was into one of those cages — clean, at least, and large enough to stand in — that they put her, while heralds read her name and crimes to a packed square.

*"Petra Brightwater, of Hollowmere. Taken in the act of necromantic practice. Condemned by writ of the Queen Regent—"*

The rest washed over her. She had learned on the road how to make herself small inside her own skull, how to fold the gift down tight and wait. But the cage was iron, and the yard was full of the court's cruelty in smaller forms — gawkers throwing things, a clerk sketching her portrait for a broadsheet already being printed, SOLDIERS OF THE CROWN TRIUMPH OVER THE WITCH OF HOLLOWMERE — and by dusk, when the crowd thinned and the cold deepened, Petra had retreated to the far corner of her cage and pressed her forehead to the bars and let herself shake where no one could see.

The dead of Corvane did not let her rest. Without the discipline of carving, without bone to give them shape, they were formless — weather rather than voices, a low ceaseless pressure of grief and hunger and confusion. She breathed through it. In Hollowmere she had learned that the dead were not monsters; they were people who had forgotten how to finish. She repeated it like a prayer. *Not monsters. People who forgot how to finish.*

"You're the Hollowmere one."

The voice came from the cage to her left. Petra started — she hadn't heard anyone approach, and the neighboring cage had seemed empty all day, its occupant a shadow-shape in the far dark. Now the shadow moved, and firelight from the yard's torches caught a face: young, sharp-boned, brown-skinned, with hair cropped short and eyes the grey-green of a sky deciding whether to storm. One eye was ringed by an old burn scar, a pale crescent like a moon caught mid-eclipse.

"I'm Maren," the stranger said. "Storm-speaker. Or I was, before they stuffed me in a box that eats weather." A crooked smile. "You're staring."

"You're real," Petra said. "I thought your cage was empty."

"Everyone thinks that. It's useful." Maren came closer, and Petra felt it then — a pressure in the air, faint but unmistakable, like the moment before lightning. The dampening-iron suppressed it, but couldn't kill it entirely. It leaked out of the other woman the way heat leaks from a hearthstone. "Sixty years," Maren said. "That thing you said about the child under the street. How'd you know?"

"The dead told me."

"They *talk* to you? Just — talk?"

"When I carve their bones. Otherwise it's more like... weather. Pressure." Petra hesitated. "You really speak to storms?"

"Storms aren't picky about company." Maren settled cross-legged against the bars between their cages, close enough to talk low. "My grandmother's people called it the sky-cousin gift. We lived up on the Stormreach coast. When the hunt came, we ran. Didn't run fast enough." She touched the burn scar beneath her eye, lightly, a gesture worn smooth by repetition. "Inquisitor's brand. Marked me at fourteen so they could find me anywhere. Took them six years to catch me. Six years of sleeping in ditches." She looked at Petra with those storm-colored eyes. "So. Witch of Hollowmere. What did you do to end up here?"

"I ran," Petra said. "And then I stopped running."

Something moved across Maren's face — recognition, maybe, or respect. "Dangerous thing, stopping."

"Yes."

* * *

The days in the yard took on a rhythm, and rhythms are dangerous, because they teach you to survive anything.

Morning: bread and water pushed through the bars by a turnkey who never met anyone's eyes. Midday: the display hours, when crowds came to stare at the crown's captured witches — for Petra was not alone. Besides Maren there was a boy called Wren who wept in his sleep and left frost on the bars when he did; an old man, Daun, whose gift no one had ever determined and who claimed, cheerfully, that neither had he; and a hollow-eyed woman named Sable who had been in the yard longest and spoke to no one, except once, at night, when she told Petra: "The ones they take inside don't come back out. Remember that."

Evening: darkness, torchlight, the palace looming above them with its bone-white spires.

And in the dark, Maren talked.

She talked the way starved people eat — urgently, hoarding nothing. She talked about the Stormreach coast, about her grandmother's stories, about the old days when the gifted had been *counseled*, not hunted. And slowly, in fragments, she gave Petra pieces of something larger.

"My gran used to say the winter wasn't natural," Maren said one night, five days after Petra's arrival. Snow fell past the torchlight in slow spirals, and their breath fogged the space between cages. "She said there was a bargain, way back. Before the kingdoms. Men and the wolf-kindred, and a door between the worlds, and the door kept the worst of the cold out. She said it in the old rhyme — you know any of the old rhymes?"

"Hollowmere sang some," Petra said carefully. "Harvest songs. Most of the words were worn off."

"*When the door stands true and the carver's hand is sure, the wolf keeps faith and the winter keeps the door.*" Maren recited it softly, half-chanting, the cadence of something learned in childhood. "Gran said the carvers made the bargain. Bone-carvers. Said they could talk to the dead because the dead remembered the making." She glanced sideways. "Your valley. Hollowmere. Where magic can't follow. Gran mentioned a valley like that once. Said the carver-folk hid there after the last breaking."

Petra's heart was beating very slowly and very loudly. "After the last *what*?"

"The last time the covenant cracked." Maren shrugged, as if she were discussing crop rotation. "It's cracked before, according to gran. Little cracks. Cold years, bad harvests, wolves seen where wolves shouldn't be. Each time, someone went and patched it. Carver blood, she said. Only carver blood holds the stitches." She frowned suddenly. "Why are you looking at me like that?"

Because my mentor burned our records. Because an inquisitor with a list came for my name. Because a dead wolf in the mountains called me blood-right and bound. Because everything I've been running from has been walking toward me the whole time.

"Tell me the rest," Petra said. "All of it. Everything your grandmother ever said."

* * *

They talked for three nights, hoarding whispers like coins. Maren knew fragments — a rhyme here, a half-remembered place-name there. The Wolf Gate. The founding line. A king who turned away from the door. Petra fed the fragments into the only loom she had: the dead. At night, pressing her hands to the dampening-iron until her palms ached, she reached down through the yard's foundations, through the layered graves of the city, asking the same question in a dozen shapes: *What do you remember of the wolves? Of the door? Of the carvers?*

Most of the dead were too old, too broken, too buried to answer. But memory sinks, and settles, and pools in the low places of a city, and Corvane was very old and very low.

On the fourth night, something answered.

It came not as a voice but as a vision, sudden and total, the way ice gives way: a hall of stone lit by green fire, and wolves the size of horses standing on their hind legs like men, and human hands — carver's hands, scarred and sure — binding a covenant in script that crawled like living things across a door of bone. And a voice, vast and tired, speaking words that Petra felt rather than heard:

*The hinge is failing. Find the hand that made it. Find the last daughter of the founding line.*

Then the vision tore away and Petra was on the floor of her cage, gasping, frost blooming across the iron around her in feather-patterns despite the dampening bands, and Maren was gripping her fingers through the bars saying her name, saying it over and over.

"Maren." Petra's voice came out scraped raw. "The regent. Why does she hunt us? Really. Not the official reason."

Maren's laugh had no humor in it. "Official reason is that the gifted curse the land, bring the winter, anger the old powers. Every bad harvest gets blamed on somebody's neighbor's daughter." She paused. "But gran said something else, once. Drunk on plum wine, winter before she died. She said the crown knows the truth. Knows about the door, the bargain, all of it. And she said—" Maren lowered her voice to almost nothing, "—she said the royal line broke the bargain themselves, ages back, and have been spending generations making sure no one carver ever lives long enough to fix it. Because a fixed door means old debts come due."

The vision's words rang in Petra's skull. *Find the last daughter of the founding line.*

The regent wasn't hunting witches. The regent was hunting *her*. Had been hunting her line for longer than anyone alive knew — and Petra had walked into the capital wearing iron and a broadsheet with her name on it.

"Petra?" Maren's grip tightened. "You've gone white as the snow. What is it?"

Before Petra could answer, torchlight flared at the yard gate. Boots — many boots — and the rattle of keys, and Serel Vane striding through the falling snow with six guards behind him and a document in his hand sealed with the black stag.

He stopped before her cage. For the first time since the ford, he looked directly at her, and there was something in his face she had never seen there before — something that might, in a better man, have been apology.

"Petra Brightwater," he read aloud, formal as a herald. "By command of Her Grace the Queen Regent, you are summoned to the Ivory Court. Tonight."

Behind her, in the dark of the next cage, Maren whispered, so softly only Petra could hear it:

"The ones they take inside don't come back out."
