## 8. The Queen Regent's Bargain

They bathed her before the audience.

Petra had not understood, at first, what the women with their buckets and scented oils were doing — she had thought, absurdly, that they were preparing her for execution, and that Corvane liked its deaths clean. Then they brought the dress. Deep blue wool, silver stitching at the collar, cut for a woman of means rather than a caged witch from a valley that didn't exist on any map worth owning.

"They want you to look like something they caught on purpose," Maren murmured as a guard walked Petra past the communal cell. The storm-speaker's wrists were raw beneath her chains; frost still rimed her lashes from whatever they did to suppress her between interrogations. "Remember that, bone-carver. Whatever they offer you in that room, remember they dressed you first."

"Will you be here when I come back?"

Maren smiled with cracked lips. "I'm not going anywhere. That's the trouble with cages."

The guard's hand closed on Petra's shoulder, and then the door was closing behind her, and the corridor swallowed her whole.

* * *

The regent received her in a room with no windows.

Petra noticed that first, before the guards, before the throne — or the chair that wanted to be a throne but was only a chair, because Queen Regent Ysolde Vayre did not yet permit herself the real thing, not while the true heir drew breath somewhere beyond her reach. The chamber was deep in the palace's heart, walled in grey stone veined with white, and it was cold. Not winter-cold. Deliberate cold, the kind that came from keeping fires small and servants few, a cold worn like a hair shirt. The regent sat wrapped in grey fur and let her courtiers shiver around her like moths pretending not to mind the lamp.

She was smaller than Petra had imagined. Smaller, and older, and tireder — a woman of sixty with a face like a folded letter, all creases and sealed edges. Only her eyes were alive, and they were very much alive, pale grey as a frozen river, and they went through Petra the way an appraiser's thumb goes through a coin.

"So," the regent said. Her voice was quiet enough that Petra had to lean to catch it, and understood immediately that this was a technique. "You are the bone-carver."

"I'm a carver of keepsakes," Petra said. "Combs. Buttons. Whistles for children."

One of the courtiers made a sound that might have been a laugh and strangled it quickly.

"You carved the name of a dead man into a rib and he told you where his murderer buried the deed to three farms." The regent unfolded a paper from her lap without looking at it. "Vane's report is thorough. He is a thorough man. It is one of the few things I trust about him." She set the paper aside. "You will not waste my time with denials, girl. Denials are for people whose gifts can be hidden. Yours announced itself across half a kingdom."

Petra kept her hands still at her sides. In Hollowmere, when the elders wanted to unnerve you, they asked questions they already knew the answers to, and waited to see if you lied anyway. She would not lie. But she would not kneel either, not to this cold woman in this cold room, and something in her spine must have said so, because the regent's mouth moved by a fraction. It might have been approval.

"Do you know why I hunt your kind?" the regent asked.

"The winter," Petra said. "You blame us for it."

"The winter," the regent agreed, "which came eleven years ago and has not loosened since. Which killed the harvest in the western marches three years running. Which froze the River Andel solid last midwinter for the first time in living memory, so that the northern lords walked their armies across the ice to threaten my capital." She leaned forward slightly, fur shifting around her shoulders. "The common folk blame the gifted. My predecessors blamed them too, in smaller ways, in pogroms and village hangings that never reached the histories. I have simply done it with better organization. But I will tell you something I do not say in public, Petra Brightwater, because you are going to be useful to me and useful people deserve honesty."

Petra waited.

"I do not know what causes the winter," the regent said. "Eleven years of hunting, and I have burned more witches than my grandsire burned heretics, and the winters grow worse, not better. Either your kind is not the cause — in which case I have committed atrocities for nothing, and a crown cannot survive that admission — or your kind is hiding the cause from me, in which case I have not burned nearly enough. You see my difficulty."

It was, Petra thought, the most honest thing anyone in power had ever said to her, and it was also the most terrifying. A ruler who knew she might be wrong and could not afford to stop.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"Answers." The regent rose. She was shorter than Petra by half a head, and she crossed the cold stone slowly, one hand trailing along the wall as though drawing strength from it. "Dead men keep secrets, and living men keep more. There is a tomb beneath this palace. Kings in it. Queens. A chancellor who died the same fortnight the winter began, very suddenly, very conveniently. I have spent eleven years trying to question the living about that fortnight and gotten nothing but corpses who used to be witnesses." She stopped, close enough that Petra could smell the fur, and under it something medicinal. "But you speak to the dead themselves. Don't you."

It wasn't quite a question. Petra thought of the bone in her satchel — the satchel was gone, confiscated, everything gone — and of the dead woman's voice rising through the grain of it like warmth through floorboards. *Tell my daughter the loom key is under the third hearthstone.* Even now, even here, the memory hurt in the old familiar way. The dead deserved better than this room. They always did.

"And if I refuse?" Petra asked.

The regent did not perform surprise. She turned and looked at her courtiers, and one by one they found reasons to study the walls, and Petra understood that she had been given this moment alone deliberately. What happened next was not for witnesses.

"If you refuse," the regent said, "nothing happens to you today. I am not a stupid tyrant; martyrs make poor instruments. You will be returned to your cell, comfortably, and fed, and asked again in a month. And meanwhile—" she paused at the chair and rested her hand on its back— "there are three children in the cells below. The fire-speaker from Drenn's Ford, age six. The twins who sing the rain out of clouds, age nine. And the storm-speaker, the woman, who has been protecting those children in there with what strength remains to her, which is not much. The law says the gifted are transported to the capital for assessment. Assessment takes time. Some assessments take years. Some, when the winter presses hard and the granaries run low and the northern lords need a spectacle..." She shrugged, a small economical motion, and finished: "...are concluded early. Do you understand me?"

"I understand," Petra said, and her voice came out flat and strange, "that you are offering to buy me with children."

"I am offering to buy you with truth," the regent said. "The children are merely the currency you happen to value. Every person has one. Yours is not bones, Brightwater. Bones are just your trade." She sat again, folding her hands, and for a moment the folded-letter face opened, and Petra saw underneath it something exhausted and human and afraid — a woman who had held a failing kingdom together for eleven years of ice with her bare hands and knew the hands were freezing too. "Ask the dead what broke the winter. That is all I ask, at first. Ask them, and tell me truly, and the children live warm, and you live free within walls I will show you. Refuse, and I go back to burning my way toward answers, starting with the loud ones in the cellar." She tilted her head. "What is your answer?"

* * *

They gave her until evening. This, too, was technique — Petra recognized it now, saw the shape of the machine she stood inside. Time to think was time to imagine. Imagination did the regent's work for her.

They put her back in the cell with Maren and the children, which was also deliberate. Of course it was. Let the bone-carver watch the six-year-old sleep. Let her count ribs.

The fire-child, Tansy, was asleep against Maren's side, breathing little smoke-sighs that warmed the air an arm's length around her. The twins, Bram and Wren, had fallen asleep holding hands across the gap between their pallets, the way twins did, the way Petra had once watched her own sisters do through a wall she shared with them in Hollowmere, in another life, three kingdoms ago. Maren sat awake against the wall, frost melting off her in slow beads because suppressing her gift took everything and the cells were cold and the cruelty of that arithmetic was surely not accidental either.

"Well?" Maren asked.

"She wants me to interrogate royal corpses. Find out what started the winter." Petra lowered herself to the floor, knees up, arms around them. "And she's right that I don't have a better idea who did."

"Mm." Maren studied her. "And the price?"

"My freedom. The children's lives." Petra pressed her forehead against her knees. "Yours too, probably. She named you specifically, Maren. She knows exactly what I care about. Vane wrote it down, all of it, every step of the road. He's thorough."

"He is," Maren said, and there was something complicated in her voice, some history Petra didn't have yet. The storm-speaker was quiet for a long moment, and then she said: "In the fishing villages where I grew up, when the catch failed, the elders would pick a family to go hungry first. Quietly. So the rest could eat. And every family swore, every year, that they'd rather starve together than let it start — and every year, when it started, the chosen family went hungry, and the village survived, and nobody spoke of it." She looked at the sleeping children. "I swore I'd never be the elder. Then I got gifted, and got chained, and now I'm the catch, and some clever woman upstairs is deciding which family eats." Her voice roughened. "Don't you dare carry this alone, carver. If you say yes, we all say yes. That's how it works. That's the only way any of it works."

"The dead aren't yours to bargain with," Petra whispered.

"No," Maren agreed. "But the living are, and here we all are."

Petra closed her eyes and listened — not to the cell, but below it, below the stone, into the deep places where the palace's dead lay stacked like ledgers. She had felt them since they brought her under Corvane: a pressure in her skull, a murmur at the edge of hearing, thousands of voices braided into a low drone, the ossuary humming like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing. Royal dead. Dead who had died with crowns on their minds and secrets in their teeth. Somewhere down there, in that dark archive, was the answer to eleven years of winter — she could feel it the way you feel weather coming in an old scar.

All she had to do was open the ledger. All she had to do was become, for a queen, what she had been for Hollowmere: a door. And the dead, who trusted carvers, who came to carvers willingly with their last words and their loom keys and their unforgiven murders — the dead would never know that this time the door opened into a throne room. That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Not the danger. The *use*. Serel Vane served the crown to stay alive, and she had despised him for it, and here she was, dressing herself in his exact compromise, finding that it fit.

*Terror and betrayal as sanctuary fails,* she thought bitterly. *Next lesson: sanctuary was never yours to begin with.*

"Tansy's mother hanged herself in Drenn's Ford rather than be taken," Maren said quietly, into the silence. "The child doesn't know yet. She thinks her mother is coming." A pause. "Somebody has to be alive to decide whether she ever learns the truth, and how. Somebody has to be alive to teach her that the fire isn't shameful. Those are not small things, Brightwater. They're just the things that don't look like battles."

Petra opened her eyes.

The torchlight through the barred door made stripes on the far wall, and Tansy stirred in her sleep and reached for warmth that wasn't there, and little flames curled off the child's fingertips to fill the space — automatic, innocent, the gift keeping her comforted even in dreams. Six years old. Six years old and already the crown called her an abomination responsible for the ice.

"Yes," Petra said. It came out barely louder than breath. She made herself say it properly, made it real, made it cost. "Yes. Tell the regent yes."

Maren nodded once, slowly, and there was no triumph in it, only a grim settling, like a body accepting weight. "Then we start tonight," she said, "because I don't trust her to honor it past dawn."

* * *

They came for Petra at moonrise.

Not soldiers this time — servants, silent, carrying lamps, walking her down through levels of the palace that grew older as they descended, newer stone giving way to old, old to ancient, until the corridors were cyclopean blocks fitted without mortar and the air smelled of deep earth and tallow and time. Two guards flanked her, and ahead of them walked a clerk with a ledger under his arm and a face like a man attending his own funeral, and Petra realized with a strange distant clarity that everyone who worked in these depths had that same face. Everyone down here was complicit in something. Everyone down here had made their own bargain.

At the bottom, a door of black oak banded in iron, and beyond the door—

She had prepared herself for a crypt. It was not a crypt. It was a *library*, and the books were bones.

Shelves of them rose into lamplit shadow — skulls sorted by reign, femurs bundled like scrolls, ribs fanned in rows, each shelf labeled in a spidery clerk-hand. Thousands of the dead. Tens of thousands. And over it all lay that pressure she had felt from her cell, the drone of the long-ringing bell, except now, standing inside it, the drone resolved. Voices. Hundreds of voices, murmuring, muttering, some singing, some weeping, some simply repeating their final thought over and over the way the drowned repeat the name of whoever they were reaching for. The dead of Corvane, filed and shelved and waiting eleven years and more for someone who could hear them.

The clerk set down his lamp. His hands shook.

"Which one do you want?" he asked, and his voice suggested that in fifteen years of service, no one had ever answered him.

Petra stepped forward into the hum, and the hum *noticed* her. The murmur shifted, the way a crowd shifts when someone important enters — a ripple of attention, of recognition, running shelf to shelf into the dark. *A carver.* She felt their hope like heat from a forge, and it nearly buckled her. Eleven years of winter, and all this time, the dead of the palace had been waiting for a listener.

*Carver.* The word rose from everywhere at once, thin as smoke, overlapping, urgent. *Carver, carver — listen — listen —*

"I hear you," Petra said aloud, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated the regent, hated the crown, hated the necessity that had brought her here to use the dead like tools — and loved them, helplessly, in the same breath, because they were only people, in the end, and people waited so long to be heard.

She turned to the clerk. "Show me the shelf for the king who died the winter the cold began."

The clerk's shaking hands consulted his ledger. "Edric the Fourth," he said. "Reigned thirty-one years. Died in the ninth month." He led her down an aisle, past reigns and reigns, and stopped at a shelf near shoulder height, and lifted down a single skull, and set it in her hands.

It was lighter than she expected. They always were.

Petra carried it to the table the clerk indicated, sat, laid her palms against the cold bone, and closed her eyes. Behind her, the clerk retreated to the door, and the guards took their posts, and the regent's orders hung over the room like frost: *ask the dead what broke the winter.*

But Petra Brightwater had grown up in Hollowmere, where the elders taught that a carver owes the dead courtesy before commerce. So first, softly, she gave the old greeting of her line, the one her mentor had taught her, the one carved into covenant-script older than any kingdom:

"Edric the Fourth. I am the door. Speak, if you will."

The skull warmed beneath her hands.

And King Edric began, at last, after eleven years of silence, to talk.
