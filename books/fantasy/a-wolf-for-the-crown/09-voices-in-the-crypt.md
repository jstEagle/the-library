## 9. Voices in the Crypt

The crypt beneath the Ivory Palace smelled of cold stone and older lies.

Petra had expected grandeur. She had imagined the resting place of kings as a cathedral of marble and gold leaf, candles burning in ranks, incense thick enough to choke a saint. What she found instead was a corridor of plain grey vaults descending into the mountain's root, each sarcophagus shut beneath a slab carved with a face that time was patiently erasing. The candlelight did not so much illuminate the passage as negotiate with it, throwing long shadows that moved when nothing else did.

"Mind where you step," Serel Vane said behind her. His voice was low, stripped of everything but instruction. He carried no torch himself; he walked half a pace back and to her left, the position of a man guarding something he could not afford to lose and could not afford to be seen cherishing.

"The dead don't care where I step," Petra said. "They care whether I listen."

"They should have made you an inquisitor."

"They tried. I declined."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped him, quickly smothered. Three days she had been down here now, three nights since the regent's bargain, and already the rhythm of it had worn grooves into her: descend at dusk, work until her hands shook, sleep in the narrow cell they called quarters, wake with bone dust under her fingernails and other people's last words caught in her throat like fishbones.

She knelt before the next sarcophagus in the row. King Aldric the Second, said the inscription. *He Gave the Realm Peace.* The slab was heavy as a millstone; she had learned to shift them with wedges and patience rather than strength. Beneath lay a skeleton dressed in rotted velvet, one hand still clasping a scepter whose gilt had flaked away like old skin.

Petra drew out her carving knife and touched its edge to the king's radius, just below the elbow.

The valley had taught her that every bone remembers. In Hollowmere she had carved sheep bones and grandmother bones and once, secretly, the finger-bone of a drowned child, and always the same truth held: the dead do not leave when they die. They settle into what carried them, the way silt settles into a riverbed, and if you cut a channel they will flow into your hands and speak.

King Aldric spoke like a landslide.

*—the gate, the gate, you fool, you short-sighted, grasping fool—*

Petra braced herself. This was the part no one warned you about: the dead were not polite. They were not stories. They were people with all the softness boiled away, leaving only what they had refused to say while living.

"I'm listening," she whispered, which was the carver's courtesy, the first thing her mentor had ever taught her. *Never command the dead. Invite them.*

The voice gathered itself, circling. She felt it the way you feel weather change — pressure behind the eyes, a taste like iron at the back of the tongue. Then it settled into her, and for a little while she was standing in a room four generations gone, wearing a king's grief like a coat two sizes too small.

*They came to me in autumn,* Aldric said. *The wolf-kin envoys. On the night my father died, with the crown not yet cold on my brow.*

Petra saw it through his remembered eyes: a chamber hung with green banners, three figures that were almost human and were not, tall and grey-eyed, their shadows moving wrong against the walls. The eldest envoy laid something on the table between them — a length of antler, carved over every surface with script that made young Aldric's eyes water to look upon.

*They said the covenant was straining. They said the Gate needed tending, and the tending required a carver of the founding line, and the founding line had thinned to a single family in some forgotten valley. They asked for the carver to be brought to the Wolf Gate. They asked for honor to be paid, the old honors, the ones my grandfather's grandfather had let lapse because paying them was inconvenient.*

*And I said no.*

Petra's breath fogged in the crypt air. "Why?" she murmured, knowing the dead could not hear questions but asking anyway, the way mourners talk at gravesides.

But Aldric answered. The dead often did, in their own order, on their own terms.

*Because they would not tell me how to find the valley. Because the carver they wanted was a woman, and I had just buried a father who told me women's gifts were for hearths, not councils. Because the wolf-kindred stood in the north where I wanted to build, and their protection came with conditions, and conditions are chains to a young king.* A pause, and then, quieter, the voice of an old man remembering a young man's arithmetic: *And because a merchant prince named Corvane offered me a fleet, and gold enough to raise an army, if the wolf-kindred's claim on the northern roads was set aside. He said the winter was a fable. He said the covenant was a leash. He said, Your Majesty, that a king who fears old stories will never make new ones.*

*I believed him. Gods forgive me, I believed him because believing him was profitable.*

The vision shifted. Petra saw the king older now, heavier, alone in that same green-hung chamber at night, prying up a floor stone with a crowbar. Beneath it lay a hollow, and in the hollow a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it with hands that shook.

A horn. Carved end to end with covenant-script, humming faintly even in memory, even dead.

*This was the token,* Aldric said. *The pledge. As long as it rested in the keeping of the crown, the Gate stayed open, the kin kept their watch, and the winter stayed beyond the mountains. It was given to the first king as a trust. Not a gift. A trust.* His voice curdled. *I sold it. Corvane melted it down within the year — said the metal in the fittings was worth more than the superstition. And the first snows came early that year, and everyone said, how charming, an early winter, and no one thought to ask why.*

Petra sat back on her heels. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, something vast and quiet was rearranging itself, the way a landscape rearranges after an avalanche.

The winter was not a punishment from heaven. It was not the work of witches. It was not the failing of the gifted, the scapegoats rotting in the palace cages above her head.

It was a debt. A debt incurred by a dead man's vanity, compounding for four generations, and the interest was being collected in frozen children and starved wolves and burned villages.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the bones, to the dark. "You knew what you'd done, and you let them blame everyone but yourself."

*What would you have had me confess?* Aldric's voice had gone thin, defensive, the voice of a man cornered by his own reflection. *Kings do not confess. Kings are confessed about.* And then, after a silence in which Petra could feel him turning the memory over like a stone looking for something alive underneath: *There is more. If you are truly a carver of the founding line — if the blood runs true in you — then you can mend it. But you must know where the fault lies before you can know where the mending goes. Ask me. Ask me properly, carver, and I will show you the whole of it.*

Petra looked up. Vane stood at the mouth of the stair, watching her with an expression she had learned to read the way sailors read clouds — carefully, and expecting it to change.

"Well?" he said.

"He broke it." Her voice came out flat and strange. "The covenant. Deliberately. Sold the pledge-token to pay for a fleet."

Vane was silent for a long moment. Torchlight moved across his face and made him look briefly, horribly young. "That," he said finally, "is the single most dangerous sentence spoken in this palace in twenty years."

"I know."

"Do you? Do you actually understand what happens if the regent hears it? If her chancellors hear it? The entire hunt — the writs, the cages, the burnings — it all rests on the story that the gifted broke the world. You are holding a knife to the throat of that story, Brightwater. Stories defended with knives tend to bite."

"Then I'll carve faster than they bite."

He stared at her. Whatever he saw in her face made him look away first, down the corridor of sleeping kings, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "The regent expects a report by morning. Give her corpses' gossip. Lovers' quarrels. Poisonings. Give her anything except this."

"And if she asks?"

"She won't. She never asks what the dead say about the crown. She thinks the dead are hers." A muscle worked in his jaw. "Everyone who serves power eventually believes power owns things it doesn't. That's how it keeps us."

* * *

She went deeper that night than she had ever gone.

Below the row of kings, below the queens and the infant princes in their small sad coffins, the stairs narrowed and the air changed. Older stone. Older silence. Vane had told her the lowest vaults predated the palace itself — that the mountain had been a holy place before Corvane the merchant-prince put his name on it, and that the founders of the dynasty had built their tombs on top of something they preferred not to name.

Her candle found the door before she understood what she was seeing.

It was not a grand door. It was a slab of black stone set into the living rock, and across its face ran carving — not decoration, not heraldry, but script. Covenant-script. The same flowing interlocking lines she had seen on Hallow's ancient bones in the pass, the same hand, or hands trained in the same school. Her fingers rose to it without her permission and traced the grooves, and the stone was warm.

Not warm like sun-warmed rock. Warm like skin.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you're not a wall. You're a *seal*."

Behind the seal, faint as a heartbeat heard through a winter window, something stirred. Not dead. Nothing down here was quite dead — she understood that now, understood it in her marrow. The crypt was not a graveyard. It was a hinge, and someone had built a palace on top of it either in reverence or in the oldest human instinct of all: the desire to sit on a thing so it cannot move.

"Carver."

The word came from everywhere and nowhere, layered voices braided into one, and Petra's candle flame bent sideways though there was no draft. The dead of the deep vault were waking around her, drawn up through the stone like moisture through cloth. She felt them settle at the edges of her senses — dozens, hundreds, a congregation.

"We heard the king speak," the voices said. "We heard him confess. Now hear us."

The cold that pressed against the seal was not the winter outside. It was older, patient, and it was *waiting*, and it had been waiting so long that waiting had become its whole nature.

"The Gate starves," the voices said. "The kin starve. The snow does not fall — it *creeps*. It is not weather, carver. It is hunger, given four generations to grow teeth. And when it has grown strong enough, it will not stop at the mountains. It will not stop at anything."

"What do you want from me?" Petra whispered.

"To remember," said the voices, and the word cracked like ice on a pond. "To carry it out of this dark. The crown buried the truth with the man who made it. Truths buried by kings do not die, carver. They wait for hands like yours."

Then the pressure withdrew, all at once, like a tide going out, and Petra was alone in the dark with a warm stone door and a candle burning straight again.

She stood there a long time, one hand flat against the seal, feeling the slow pulse beneath it. Somewhere far to the north, under a glacier, a gate of bone and stone was dying of neglect. Somewhere closer, in a cage in the palace yards, Maren was probably awake, watching the frost climb the bars and muttering storm-lore to keep herself sane. And somewhere inside Petra's own chest, in the place where Hallow's bond lived like a second pulse, something turned over in its sleep and *listened*.

The bond had been quiet for days — stretched thin by distance, by stone, by whatever law separated the realms. But now, standing at the hinge of the deep vault with a dead king's confession sitting in her like swallowed coals, she felt it wake.

Not words. Wolves did not send words down the thread. They sent *need*, and images, and a direction.

North. Cold. A running shape, gaunt as famine, moving through snow that fell upward somehow, wrong, unnatural. And threaded through the image, unmistakable even across the impossible distance: fear. Not Hallow's fear for himself. His fear for her, urgent and immediate, arriving with the force of a hand gripping her shoulder.

*Come north. Come now. Something is coming to meet you, and it is not me, and you will not survive it unprepared.*

Petra took her hand off the seal. The candle steadied. Above her, through forty feet of stone, she could just barely hear the palace waking — bells, boots, the ordinary machinery of a court built on a lie.

She picked up her knife, closed the king's sarcophagus, and started up the stairs, taking them two at a time, already composing the harmless report she would feed the regent at dawn and the true report she would feed to the only people who might live long enough to act on it.

The winter had an author. Authors could be answered.

But first, she thought, climbing into the torchlight with her heart hammering a war-drum rhythm, she had to find out what Hallow had seen coming through the snow.
