## 10. Hallow's Reckoning

Hallow had not slept in three days, and the wolf-kindred did not dream the way humans did — but that night, in the den-hollow beneath the frost-bitten pines of the far realm, he dreamed anyway. He dreamed of a girl with bone-dust on her fingers and a voice like a river under ice, and when he woke, she was already there.

Not beside him. She could not be beside him; a mountain range and a sealed gate stood between them. But she was *there*, in the way the bond had taught them both to recognize: a warmth behind his ribs that was not his own, a second heartbeat layered over the first, faint as a moth against glass.

*Petra.*

*I'm here.* Her thought arrived the way her carvings must have looked — careful, deliberate, shaped by hands that knew how much pressure a thing could bear before it broke. *You called me. I felt it. What's wrong?*

*Nothing is wrong.* The lie sat in him like a swallowed stone. *Everything is wrong. I want to show you something, and I don't know how to do it gently.*

*Don't be gentle,* she said. *I carve dead women's bones for a living, Hallow. Gentleness isn't what I'm for.*

So he opened the bond wide, wider than he had ever dared, and let her fall into his world.

* * *

She fell through winter first.

That was how the wolf-kindred realm began, as far as any of the living remembered: with winter, and beneath winter, and inside winter, until winter was less a season than a country, and they were all its citizens whether they wished it or not. Hallow showed her the high valleys where the snow never melted, the forests where the trees had stopped growing rings because growing required a spring, the rivers frozen so deep that the ice had its own weather — storms that lived and died within the frozen water, silver lightning crawling under the surface like veins.

*It's beautiful,* Petra said, and meant it, and Hallow loved her a little for that.

*It's dying,* he answered. *Both things are true. That's the cruelty of it.*

He took her — or her awareness, her ghost-self riding the bond — down into the heart of the kindred lands, to the den-city of Vhal Marenna, carved into the roots of a mountain that had once been warm. He showed her the great hearth-caves where the elders kept the last of the old fires burning, fires fed by wood hoarded for generations, rationed now to embers no larger than a fist. He showed her the hunting grounds, stripped bare. He showed her the larders, and the way the guards stood outside them not to keep thieves out but to keep the hungry from counting what remained.

*How many are you?* she asked.

*When the covenant was forged, we were forty thousand souls across nine packs.* He paused at the edge of the whelping dens, gathering himself. *Now we are perhaps eleven thousand. And the young—*

He stepped aside and let her see.

The whelping dens had been the pride of Vhal Marenna once. Hallow's mother had been born there; her mother before her; the walls still bore the tooth-marks of cubs a hundred years dead, little crescents gnawed into the stone by children teething on the world. Now the dens were half-empty, and the emptiness was worse than death, because death at least left bones, and bones could be honored, and this was only absence — small beds of moss and fur with no one in them, cold as unlit lamps.

The cubs who remained were thin in a way that hurt to look at. Not starving — the pack would have torn itself apart before letting the young starve — but *thinned*, drawn down to essential lines, their coats dull, their growth stunted. They played, because cubs always played, but their games had changed. They no longer hunted each other through the tunnels shrieking with joy. They played a quieter game now, one they had invented themselves without any adult teaching it: they lay very still in the dark and pretended to sleep through the winter, and the one who stayed still longest won.

*Oh,* Petra said. It was not a word so much as a wound. *Oh, Hallow.*

*They've forgotten what spring looks like,* he said. *The oldest among us remember it. The middle-aged have heard it described. The young think it's a story we tell to make the hunger bearable. My sister's cub asked me last moon whether the green place was real or whether we made it up the way we made up the gods.* His voice, even in thought, even across realms, roughened. *I didn't know what to tell her. I couldn't swear to her that I hadn't made it up myself.*

Petra was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her thought was very quiet and very steady, the way she got when she was most afraid.

*Show me the gate.*

* * *

They traveled light and fast, Hallow's body running the frozen passes while her mind rode him like wind rides a banner, and all day he ran and all day she watched his world unspool beneath his paws, and by the time they reached the glacier she understood things that no human court understood and no human chronicle recorded.

The Wolf Gate was not a door. That was the first thing she saw, and Hallow felt her revise everything she'd imagined. It was a *seam* — a place where two worlds had been sewn together by the founding carvers, human hands and wolf-kindred magic interlaced like fingers, and the covenant was the thread. For a thousand years the seam had held two climates apart, two peoples apart, two hungers apart, while letting passage flow between those sworn to it: kin and carver, scout and speaker, the old traffic of a shared borderland.

Now the thread was rotting.

Hallow stood before it in the blue shadow of the glacier, and Petra saw it through his eyes: a wall of interlocking bone and stone rising out of the ice, vast as a cliff face, and across its whole breadth the script of the founding carvers — the same script she had read on a dead wolf's skeleton in a mountain pass, the script that lived in her own blood — flickering like a fire running out of fuel. Some glyphs had gone dark entirely. Others burned wrong, guttering, stuttering, and where they failed, the cold came through. Not the honest cold of the kindred winter, but a thin, greedy, seeping cold, the cold of a wall with a crack in it, leaking one world into another.

*That's why your winter crosses into ours,* Petra breathed. *It's not the covenant failing everywhere at once. It's failing* here *first, and spreading.*

*The carvers built it from your side,* Hallow said. *We maintained it from ours. There were once a hundred wardens of the gate, drawn from every pack. Now there are four, and I am the youngest, and I am sixty years old by your reckoning.* He let her feel it then, the thing he had been circling: *Petra, the gate isn't just weakening. It's closing. Sealing itself, glyph by glyph, the way a wound closes — except a wound closes to heal, and this—*

*This closes to die,* she finished.

*Yes. And when it seals completely, two things happen. Your side loses whatever warmth bleeds through from ours — you'll get your long winter in full, permanent, the one your queen regent is blaming on gifted children in cages. And we lose—*

He stopped. Across the bond, he felt her arrive at it herself, because she was Petra, and she always arrived at the worst truth on her own, ahead of anyone telling her.

*You lose the last of the old magic,* she said. *The covenant magic. The magic that keeps you... you. The wolf-kindred aren't just wolves who talk. You're wolves* bound *to something. Shaped by the covenant. Sustained by it. Without it—*

*Without it, our children are born smaller,* Hallow said. *And their children smaller again, and dimmer, until what's left is an animal that remembers nothing and a people that no longer exists. We will not die, Petra. We will* dissolve*. Slowly, over centuries, in the dark, forgetting ourselves one generation at a time.* A pause. *Some of the elders say that would be a gentle end. I have watched cubs play at sleeping through winter. I do not think anything about it is gentle.*

* * *

That night — if the endless twilight of the kindred realm could be called night — Hallow brought her to the council-fire, because she had asked, and because hiding the truth from her had become impossible, and because some part of him wanted her to see this too: not just the suffering, but the anger that suffering had curdled into.

The council met in the Hearth-Cave of Nine Voices, a vaulted hollow where the founders had once spoken and nine packs had listened. Now eight voices were silent — packs gone, merged, dissolved — and the cave echoed strangely, too big for the few who huddled near the coals.

Skarn was speaking when they arrived.

Hallow's brother stood at the center of the firelight, and even starved down to sinew, Skarn had a presence that filled space the way a storm fills a valley. He was the elder by two years, broader, darker-pelted, scarred across the muzzle from a duel Hallow had witnessed and never asked about. Where Hallow had spent his life as a warden and a scout — watching borders, carrying messages, learning patience — Skarn had spent his as a war-leader of the border raids back when raids were still possible, and everything in him was aimed like a weapon.

"The humans burn their own gifted," Skarn was saying, and his voice carried to the back of the cave without effort. "Did you know this? Their queen puts children in cages and calls it justice, and their scholars write books about why the winter is the witches' fault, and their farmers pray to a god who has never once sent them spring." He turned slowly, letting the whole council see his eyes. "These are not a people worth saving. These are a people worth *ending*."

Murmurs. Not universal — Hallow marked old Wrenna shaking her head, and the healer Doss staring into the coals — but murmurs, and more of them than there had been a season ago. Hunger made converts faster than any speech.

"Brother." Hallow stepped into the light. "You've counted the packs that tried war. Tell them what you told me."

Skarn's gaze found him, and there was something in it that was almost fond, which was somehow worse than hatred. "Tell them what? That the Frostfangs charged the gate-seam two winters ago and broke against it like water? That the seal doesn't care whose side it kills?" He smiled, and the smile had no warmth in it at all. "No, little brother. I'm not talking about charging the gate. I'm talking about *opening* it. All the way. Tearing the seam wide and pouring our winter through onto their heads until their rivers freeze and their crops blacken and their cities go dark, and then we walk through over the ice and take what we need. Let *them* learn what a dying world feels like. Let them starve symmetrically."

"And then what?" Hallow asked quietly. "You break the gate, the covenant dies with it, and in three generations your grandchildren are beasts in the snow who've forgotten your name. You'd trade a slow dissolution for a fast one and call it victory."

"I'd trade it for *revenge*," Skarn snarled, and the cave went very still, "which is at least a thing a wolf can hold in his teeth. What are you offering, Hallow? More waiting? More wardens watching a wall rot? A *human girl*?"

The silence that followed had teeth. Every eye in the cave turned toward Hallow, and Hallow felt Petra's presence inside the bond like a hand gripping his — she had heard all of it, seen all of it, and she did not look away, and he loved her for that too.

"Yes," Hallow said. "A human girl. The last heir of the line that forged this covenant, walking toward your gate right now with hunters on her trail and the crown's chains waiting at the end of the road. She is the only living soul who can reforge what's breaking. And she owes us *nothing*, Skarn. Nothing. Our kings broke faith with hers a thousand years ago — yes, I've read the deep records, I know what the old royals traded away and lied about — and still she comes. Still she's coming. And you want to stand in front of the only door out of this winter and growl about revenge."

"She's one human. One. Against a thousand years of rot."

"One," Hallow agreed. "The founding carver was also one. So was the first wolf-kin who laid his throat on the altar-stone and gave his life-thread to bind the two worlds together. The covenant has never been made by armies, brother. It's always been made by *one*, willing, on both sides of the seam." He looked around the cave, at the hollow dens and the dull-coated cubs and the elders counting embers. "I'm not asking you to hope. I'm asking you to wait one season. Give me until midwinter. If she fails, do what you like. I'll stand aside."

Skarn held his gaze for a long, long moment. Then he laughed — short, hard, without humor — and turned back to the fire.

"One season," he said. "Pray your human walks faster than the winter does, little brother."

* * *

Later, alone on the glacier shelf above the gate, Hallow let the bond open one last time, and found Petra waiting in it, quiet as snowfall.

*You heard all of it.*

*All of it.* A pause. *Your brother isn't wrong to be angry. If I'd watched my people thin the way yours have, I'd want someone to blame too. The crown blames us. He blames everyone. It's the same fire, just pointed different directions.*

*What are you saying, Petra?*

*That I'm coming,* she said simply. *Midwinter. The gate. I made that promise before I ever saw your whelping dens, and now I've seen them, and the promise hasn't gotten smaller. It's gotten heavier. That's all.* Another pause, longer, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into something raw and human and afraid. *But Hallow — the reforge needs a life freely given from each side. I read it in the crypt-script. I haven't told you that yet, because I was a coward about it. Whose life, Hallow? On your side of the seam. Who gives?*

The wind came down off the glacier, moving through the dead gate's darkened glyphs, and Hallow looked at the wall that held two worlds together and thought about the first wolf-kin who had lain his throat on the altar-stone, and about his brother's smile, and about cubs playing at sleeping through winter.

*I'll show you tomorrow,* he said. *Tonight, rest. You're being hunted, and I need you alive.*

*Hallow. Who gives?*

He closed the bond gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom, and stood alone under the stars with the answer in his mouth like a stone:

*Me.*
