## 12. Betrayal in the Ivory Court

The dead did not sleep in Corvane, and neither, lately, did Petra.

She worked by candlestub in the narrow room the regent had given her — a gilded cage three floors above the crypts, its windows too high to see anything but sky and the tips of the palace spires. On the table before her lay a rib bone wrapped in linen, one of dozens she had smuggled up from the royal ossuary over the past month, each one a voice she was not supposed to be hearing.

This one belonged to a woman who had died forty years ago, a duchess whose name had been struck from every record in the palace. When Petra carved the old script along its curve — carefully, shallowly, the way her mentor had taught her in Hollowmere's long evenings — the bone warmed against her palm like a bird's breast.

*He knew,* the duchess whispered through her. *The old king knew what he traded. He wrote it down. Three copies. One in his tomb, one in the archive of thorns, and one—*

The door below slammed.

Petra's carving knife stilled. Footsteps on the stairs — not the soft shuffle of servants, but boots, several pairs, moving with the particular rhythm of people who had been given permission to do something terrible.

She had perhaps thirty heartbeats.

She swept the bones into the fire bucket — no time for the hearth — and pressed the carved rib into the lining of her boot, where Maren's smuggled notes already rode against her ankle. The knife she slid beneath the mattress. The candle she pinched out, plunging the room into grey winter light, and sat down at the table with her hands folded like a good prisoner awaiting her keeper.

The door opened without a knock.

Lord Cassian Thorne filled it the way frost fills a window — slowly, beautifully, and with the certain promise that everything behind him would die. He was the regent's master of whispers, a man of silver rings and softer words, and he had smiled at Petra exactly twice since her arrival in the capital. Both smiles had been the kind that made her think of wolves circling something already wounded.

Behind him stood four guards in ivory livery, and behind them, at the edge of the corridor as if he had been made to wait there, Serel Vane.

Her heart did something complicated and painful. She kept her face still. She had learned stillness from the dead; they were very patient teachers.

"Lady Brightwater." Thorne crossed the room without invitation, his rings clicking against the table's edge as he leaned down to study her — the way she'd seen men study horses they intended to work until they dropped. "You've been busy."

"I've done everything the regent asked of me."

"You have. Diligently. Almost *lovingly*." He plucked something from inside his coat — a scrap of parchment, and her stomach turned cold before she even saw what was written on it, because she recognized the hand. Her own. A fragment of covenant-script, copied from the crypt walls, annotated in her careful carver's shorthand: *the gate requires two threads, freely given.*

One of her practice sheets. She burned her practice sheets. She *always* burned them—

Except three nights ago, when exhaustion had taken her mid-stroke and she had fallen asleep at the table, and in the morning the ash pan had been empty because the servants emptied it, and one scrap must have survived half-burned, curled at the edge, legible.

Thorne watched her realize it. He enjoyed watching. That was the worst thing about him — worse than the whisper network, worse than the rumors of what happened to people who displeased him. He *enjoyed* it.

"Curious writing," he said. "Not any script taught in the capital. Not any script at all, according to my archivists — which means it is either nonsense, or it is older than the kingdom." His smile widened by a degree. "And you have been asking the archivists questions, Lady Brightwater. About the Wolf Gate. About midwinter. About the founding line of bone-carvers." He straightened, turning the scrap between two fingers. "A captured witch, interrogating the dead on the regent's behalf, who spends her free hours researching how to *unmake* the very winter the crown blames her kind for. Do you know what that looks like?"

"It looks like me doing my work thoroughly."

"It looks like treason dressed in scholarship." He let the scrap fall to the table. "The regent will hear it now. All of it. The night visits to the ossuary beyond your sanctioned hours. The servant girl you paid to look away. The storm-speaker in the cells below who receives your letters."

Ice went through Petra's chest. *Maren.* "There are no letters."

"There were. My people read everything that moves through this palace, Lady Brightwater. It is what I am *for*." He said it pleasantly, the way one mentions a hobby. "You've been careless. Or perhaps you never understood that mercy is a leash, and leashes are meant to be felt."

* * *

They marched her down through the palace's bones — past the banquet halls where courtiers feasted while the provinces starved, past the tapestries of kings who had all, she now knew, inherited a broken covenant and called the breaking peace. Down, down, to the antechamber outside the regent's audience hall, where the candles burned white and the floor was marble cold enough to bite through boot leather.

Vane walked beside her. The guards had allowed it — or Thorne had arranged it, which amounted to the same cruelty: making the traitor watch, making the collaborator be seen.

"Tell me it isn't true," Vane said quietly. His eyes stayed forward. "The letters. The research. Tell me Thorne is lying."

"Why?" Petra kept her voice as flat as his. "So you can decide whether I'm worth saving? I'm tired of being worth saving, Serel. I'm tired of everyone deciding things *for* me."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it? You put me in a cage to save three children. The regent put a knife at those children's throats to save herself. Everyone in this palace calls their chains kindness." She finally looked at him, and the look cost her more than she wanted it to. "I found something in the crypts. Something real. The winter isn't a curse on the gifted — it's a debt, and there's a way to pay it, and I was going to tell the regent the truth because I am apparently the only person in this entire ivory rot-hole who thinks the truth might be *useful*."

"The truth about the gate." His jaw tightened. "Petra. If the regent believes you can end the winter, she doesn't need you as an instrument anymore. She needs you as a *threat*. People who might fix what the crown has blamed on others — do you understand what happens to them?"

"I understand what happens to people who stand in this palace's way. I've read enough dead kings to fill a graveyard."

Doors opened. The herald's voice rang out, thin and ceremonial: *"The witch Petra Brightwater, brought before Her Grace the Queen Regent."*

* * *

The regent received her standing, which was itself a message. Queens sat for supplicants; they stood for sentences.

Isolde Vane-Marren — no relation to Maren, though Petra had once wondered — wore winter even in summer, the court joked. Today she wore it literally: grey fur at her shoulders, grey silk beneath, her face pale and precise as a blade left out overnight. Behind her stood the full weight of the Ivory Court, ranks of nobles drawn by the scent of blood like fish to chum.

Thorne presented his evidence with the relish of a man unveiling a masterpiece: the half-burned scrap. The servant girl, weeping, coached into confession. A guard who swore he had seen Petra speaking to the sealed door of the old king's tomb. And last, laid gently upon the regent's table like a wounded bird, one of Maren's letters — Petra's own handwriting, describing the founding line of bone-carvers, describing *herself*.

"Your Grace," Thorne concluded, "the witch has been using the gift you granted her use of to build a case against the crown itself. She intends, at midwinter, to go to the Wolf Gate. What she intends to do there, only she can say. But I ask the court to consider: why does a prisoner need to know how the cage was built?"

Silence, the expensive kind, the kind nobles wore like jewelry.

The regent descended one step. Then another. She stopped close enough that Petra could smell the rosewater over the cold.

"I gave you the dead," Isolde said softly. "I gave you run of my crypts. I spared your little criminals in exchange for your obedience. Was that so heavy a price?"

"Your Grace, everything I found confirms what I told you from the first day. The winter comes from a broken covenant, not from the gifted. Your hunt is—"

"My hunt," the regent said, "is the only thing holding this kingdom together. Do you know what happens if I tell three million starving people that the winter is their own king's doing? That their grandparents' king sold their protection for power?" Her voice didn't rise; it *narrowed*, like ice forming. "They will burn the capital. They will burn each other. Order is a candle in a windstorm, Lady Brightwater, and I do not cup my hands around it to admire the flame. I *guard* it."

"Then let me mend it," Petra said. "Let me go to the Gate. Let me end the winter honestly, and you won't need scapegoats at all."

Something flickered across the regent's face — want, quick and naked, there and gone. For one heartbeat Petra thought: *she's tempted. She's actually tempted.*

Then Lord Thorne murmured something to the chamberlain, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"There is a simpler reading," the regent said, turning away. "That everything you've discovered serves you alone. That a bone-carver of the founding line, heir to the Gate, would say *anything* to reach it." She reseated herself, and the ceremony of it was terrible in its casualness. "Serel Vane."

Petra's whole body went still.

Vane stepped forward from the ranks of courtiers. Up close, in this light, she could see what a month in the palace had done to him — the hollows under his eyes, the way his hunter's lean frame had gone rigid, a man holding himself together with discipline and nothing else.

"Your Grace."

"You have served the crown since you were nine years old. You have hunted the gifted, guarded the gifted, and lied to both on my behalf. Your loyalty has never been in question." The regent folded her hands. "Until now. There are those at this court who note that the witch flourished under your supervision. That she reached the crypts. That she corresponded with prisoners. That you, alone of all my servants, argued for her usefulness *after* evidence of her treachery was in my hands."

"Which I reported," Vane said. "Every word of it. I brought Thorne the first suspicion myself—"

"You brought Thorne what you chose to bring Thorne." The regent's gaze was glacial. "Loyalty that requires interpretation is not loyalty. So you will resolve the question tonight, publicly, beyond any interpreting."

She gestured, and a guard stepped forward bearing a sword on a cushion of black velvet. Not an executioner's blade — a court sword, slender and bright, the kind duels were fought with.

"Kill her," the regent said. "Here. Now. Before the court. Do this, and every doubt dies with her, and you remain what you have always been: mine."

The word fell into the silence like a stone into a well. Petra heard it land at the bottom of everything.

* * *

Later — years later, in the half-remembered country where she would keep her memories — Petra would try to reconstruct those next moments and find them strangely slow, strangely clear, the way the dead saw things. As if some part of her had already stepped halfway out of the living world and was watching her own heartbeat from the far shore.

Vane took the sword.

Of course he took the sword. He was Serel Vane; he had spent twenty-five years learning that survival meant picking up whatever the crown put in front of him. His fingers closed around the grip the way they must have closed around a hundred tools of other people's wills, and the court exhaled with pleasure, and Petra thought with a strange, distant grief: *there it is, then. The truth of him. I always knew it was there, under the kindness. Everything in this palace is a leash, and he just showed me which end he holds.*

He walked toward her. The courtiers parted for him like wheat.

"Look at me," she said. Quietly. Only for him.

He didn't. He stopped an arm's length away, and raised the sword, and the steel caught the candlelight and threw it across her face, and somewhere in the ranks of nobles someone tittered and was hushed.

"Serel." Still quiet. "The children. The ones you bought with my freedom. If you do this, who keeps the regent's leash off them? Who walks me to the block in a month when she finds a new reason? You're not proving you're loyal. You're proving you'll always pick up the sword."

"Petra—" His voice cracked, barely, a fault-line in stone. "Don't make this harder."

"You make it hard. Every single day, you make it hard." Her eyes burned and she refused to blink. "I trusted you. In the Bone Garden, in the snow, in every dark hour since the ford — I decided you were worth the risk. So look at me and tell me I was wrong. Look at me and mean it."

For one suspended instant, something moved behind his eyes — the whole buried architecture of a lifetime of compromise, the boy who had chosen servitude over death and called the choosing wisdom ever since. She saw him teeter on the edge of it, sword raised, the court holding its breath, the regent watching with her winter-grey patience.

Then he moved.

The stroke came fast — faster than she could follow — and the world did not end. There was no pain. There was a sound instead, a ringing snap of steel on stone, and cold air on her throat, and when Petra's senses returned to her the court was screaming and the chain at her wrists lay severed on the marble, cut clean through, and Vane's sword-point was leveled — not at her.

At the throne.

"Enough," Serel Vane said, and his voice carried to the rafters, twenty-five years of swallowed words breaking loose all at once. "Your Grace, I have been loyal to this crown since I was a child, and loyalty has required me to burn villages, cage children, and call it order. I draw the line at murder on a throne's convenience." The sword did not waver. "She goes to the Wolf Gate. And God forgive me — *we* go to the Wolf Gate — because everything she found in your crypts is true, and you know it, and you would rather freeze a kingdom than lose control of the story."

The regent rose. The court rose with her, a field of grey and silver bristling.

"Guards," she said.

And every door in the audience hall opened at once.

Petra's hand found Vane's sleeve in the chaos — his choice, hers, theirs, tangled past undoing now — and above the rising roar of the Ivory Court, from the direction of the deep crypts far below, she felt it through the soles of her feet: the dead of Corvane, dozens of them, hundreds, the angry ones, the murdered ones, the ones she had spent a month listening to and who had listened back —

— beginning, all together, to scream.
