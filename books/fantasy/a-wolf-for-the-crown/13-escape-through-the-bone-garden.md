## 13. Escape Through the Bone Garden

The key turned in the dark before I heard the footsteps.

That was how I knew it wasn't a trick — or rather, it was how I knew it was a different kind of trick than the ones the Ivory Court played. The lock on my cell had rust in its throat. It complained when it opened, a thin iron whine like something being skinned, and the sound woke me from a dream of Hollowmere where my mother's hands were steady and the river ran backward and none of it hurt.

I sat up on the cot. The everburning lamp outside the bars had guttered to its lowest flame, the way it did in the hour before the wardens changed watch. And there in that failing light stood Serel Vane with a ring of keys still in his fist and his face arranged into nothing at all.

"You have until the bell," he said. "Get up."

I did not get up. I had learned something in three weeks of the court's hospitality, and it was this: every gift in Corvane had teeth somewhere. "Why?"

"Because in six days they'll stop asking you to read corpses and start asking you to die for what you've found." He spoke fast and low, the words worn smooth like river stones, as if he'd rehearsed them against the inside of his own skull for years. "The courtier Aldery has already told the regent about your research. About the crypt-voices. About the dead king." His jaw worked. "They can't let the winter be a king's doing, Petra. It has to be the gifted's doing. It *has* to. Everything hangs on that."

"And so you'll hang me instead of them."

"I'll open your door instead of sealing it." He held up the keys, and the lamplight caught the fine bones of his wrist, and I thought — not for the first time — that he had the hands of someone who should have been a carver. "That is all I am offering tonight. Not forgiveness. Not an explanation you'd believe. A door."

Through the bond, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Hallow stirred. *He is afraid,* the wolf said from wherever his body paced the frozen hills north of the city. *Good. Afraid men run faster.*

"He's afraid," I said aloud, watching Vane flinch at nothing — at everything. "Hallow says fear makes honest men of everyone eventually."

"Hallow," Vane repeated, tasting the name. "The kin-bond. Yes. I know what it is." And then he did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any argument could have: he pulled his collar aside, down past his collarbone, and showed me the mark.

It was old. Faded almost white, the way covenant-script goes when the blood that carries it thins through generations. But I knew the shape. I had traced its ancestor in the pass above Hollowmere, carved into a wolf's ribs by hands ten centuries dead. The founding line's knotwork. The carver's mark.

"You're heir to one of the lines," I whispered.

"My grandmother's line. One of the lesser ones — smiths, not carvers. They made the hinges of things, the fittings, the parts nobody sings about." He let the collar fall, and his voice went flat and terrible with old shame. "When the hunts began under the old regent, my grandmother burned our records and told us to forget. She said forgetting was survival. I have been surviving for thirty years, Petra Brightwater. It looks exactly like cowardice from the inside. You would know."

The bell in the tower above began to toll the change of watch. Vane's hand tightened on the keys.

"Now," he said. "*Now.*"

---

We went the way the dead go.

Not out through the gates or down the great stair — Vane led me deeper, past the punishment cells where the silence itself seemed to bruise, to a door of black oak banded in silver that no warden troubled to guard. Why guard a door the living never open?

"The ossuary," Vane said. "The palace was built over its own ancestors. Six hundred years of Corvane's dead, packed into the foundations like ballast. The regent's grandmother thought it fitting. Her grandson's court walks on bones every day and calls itself eternal."

"And you think we can cross it alive?"

"I think the dead owe me nothing but their anger, and their anger points the same direction mine does." He lifted the silver bar. "Besides. You're a bone-carver, aren't you? This will be the friendliest room in the palace."

He was wrong, and he knew he was wrong, and he took us in anyway.

The ossuary breathed cold. Not winter-cold — older, patient, the cold of earth that has been asked to hold too much. Our lamps found walls built entirely of the dead: femurs stacked like cordwood, skulls mortared in spirals, pelvises fanned into roses. Somewhere water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental. The air tasted of chalk and old incense and, beneath both, the sweetish rot of secrets.

And the dead were *loud.*

In Hollowmere I had to carve a bone to hear its voice. Here I needed nothing. Six hundred years of unfinished business pressed against my skin like a tide against a door, and I stood in the middle of it shaking, because every skull on that wall was speaking at once.

*—they took my name when they took my land—*

*—the child, tell me if the child—*

*—I signed it, gods forgive me, I signed the writ myself—*

"They know what you are," Vane said quietly. He had gone pale; whatever gift slept in his blood, it didn't wake for voices. "Can you — quiet them?"

"I'm not going to quiet them." My voice came out strange, doubled, as if the ossuary had begun borrowing my throat. "They've been quieted long enough."

I put my palm against the nearest wall of bone.

I did not carve. There was no time, and no need. I simply *listened*, the way my mentor had taught me in the valley — listening as a courtesy first, power second — and the tide of voices broke apart into individuals, and the individuals began to speak to me.

A woman in a plague-nurse's shroud. A soldier with a coin in his teeth. A girl no older than Maren whose skull bore the small neat hole of a courtier's knife. They crowded close, these dead, hungry not for freedom — most of them had made peace with staying — but for *witness*. Someone to carry the memory outward. Someone to say: you were here, and it mattered.

"The regent's men passed through here two nights ago," I told them, because they asked, because asking was all they had left. "They carried writs. They're hunting the gifted north of the city, along the ice road."

The wall *shivered.* Bone against bone, a sound like a forest in wind, and out of it rose one voice louder than the rest — a deep, cracked voice that came from everywhere in that corridor at once.

*THE WARDENS CHANGE WATCH AT THE THIRD BELL. THE POSTERN STAIR IS BARRED BUT THE OLD SERVANTS' WAY BESIDE IT IS NOT. THEY FORGOT IT WHEN THEY SEALED THE KITCHEN PASSAGE IN MY GRANDFATHER'S TIME.*

"Who are you?" Vane breathed.

*A MASTER OF KEYS. DEAD NINETY YEARS. FORGOTTEN BY EVERYONE EXCEPT THE DOORS HE LOVED.* A pause, and then, with something dangerously close to humor: *AND YOU ARE THE COWARD'S GRANDSON. YOUR GRANDMOTHER MADE MY LAST SET OF HINGES. FINE WORK. STILL SWINGS TRUE.*

Vane closed his eyes. For a moment he looked like a man being flayed very slowly by kindness. "Tell her," he said to the dark, "that I'm trying to do better than she did."

*TRYING IS A WORD FOR PEOPLE STILL ALIVE. GO BE ALIVE, THEN.*

---

We collected Maren first.

Her cell was in the storm-wardens' wing, which should have been impossible to reach in twelve minutes and took us nine, because the Master of Keys guided us through the walls themselves — service passages, dead ends that weren't, a coal chute that had been a priest-hole before it was a chute. The dead of Corvane knew every seam in their own tomb, and they opened the seams for me.

*Left,* the cracked voice said, each time. *NOW RIGHT. MIND THE STEP; THREE MEN HAVE DIED ON THAT STEP AND THEY WILL TELL YOU ABOUT IT IF YOU LINGER.*

Maren fought like a wildcat when the door opened — I still had the bruise on my wrist from teaching her that I wasn't a warden — but when she saw my face she stopped mid-swing, and when she saw Vane she did not stop at all.

"You," she snarled. Thunder muttered somewhere far above us, indoors, impossibly, gathering around her shoulders like a shawl. "You're the queen's hound. You brought her in chains. You stood there while they—"

"I did." Vane didn't flinch. That was the thing about him I could never decide whether to hate or admire: he refused the comfort of excuses. "Every word true. And I am opening doors for you now, which does not erase it, and I would thank you to remember both facts at once, because if you forget either one you'll get us killed."

Maren looked at me. I looked back, and I tried to put into my face what I actually felt, which was: *trust him the way you trust ice in early winter. Carefully. Knowing it may crack. Crossing anyway.*

"We need her," I said. "She knows covenant lore. And the children won't leave without her."

"The children," Vane said, "are the reason we take the ossuary instead of the postern gate. The warden rosters list three gifted children in the holding cells below the treasury. Two boys and a girl. The girl is six." His voice stayed level; his knuckles did not. "Their transport order is dated tomorrow. I signed it."

The corridor went very quiet except for the dripping.

"You signed it," Maren repeated, soft and lethal.

"Because if I hadn't, another name would have signed it, and that man would not be standing here unlocking cells." Vane met her eyes and then, deliberately, mine. "I have spent thirty years telling myself that compromise keeps people alive. Sometimes it even has. Tonight I intend to find out what happens when I stop. Judge me afterward, if we live to have an afterward."

*WELL SAID,* rumbled the Master of Keys. *FOR A LIVING MAN. NOW MOVE.*

---

The children were asleep in a heap, the way puppies sleep, for warmth and comfort and because no one had taught them yet that comfort was dangerous. Tam and Osric, eight and eleven, storm-touched like Maren. And the little girl, Wren, who was not a storm-speaker at all but something the wardens couldn't classify — she made plants grow crooked, they said, and sang to locks until they opened.

Wren woke when my lamp touched her face, and did not cry, and did not scream. She looked at me with eyes too old for six and said, "You smell like the bones talk to you."

"They do," I whispered.

"Grandmother said someone would come. Before they took her." Wren stood up, small and certain in her prison-gray shift. "She said the carver girl would come and the bones would show her the way out because bones remember every road."

Behind me, I heard Vane exhale slowly through his nose — the sound of a man discovering that his sins had witnesses younger than his guilt.

"Then let's not disappoint her," I said, and gave Wren my hand.

---

The ossuary again, but lower now, and angrier.

The upper galleries held the honored dead, the named dead, the dead whose grievances had at least been recorded. Down here, beneath the treasury vaults, the bones were anonymous — heaped, not stacked, poured into the walls like grain into a silo. Slaves and debtors and plague-pits and the unclaimed. Their voices weren't words anymore. They were weather.

Maren's storms answered the pressure; sparks crawled along her arms. Tam started to whimper and Osric clapped a hand over his little brother's mouth, eleven years old and already fluent in the grammar of hiding. Vane drew his sword — useless, and he must have known it was useless, and he held it anyway, because a blade is a kind of prayer when you have nothing else.

"Petra." Hallow's presence flooded the bond, closer now, running — I could feel the ground-eating lope of him, miles shrinking. *The angry dead. Do not fight them. Do not fear them. GRIEVE WITH THEM. That is the key to every locked thing.*

So I stopped walking. In the middle of that heaving dark corridor, with the hunt's bells beginning above us and four frightened lives depending on my spine staying straight, I knelt down and laid both palms flat against the heaped bones, and I grieved.

I grieved for the nameless dead who built a palace they were never allowed to enter above ground. I grieved for the gifted children in cages across a burning country. I grieved for Serel Vane's grandmother, who loved her grandchildren enough to teach them erasure. I grieved for myself, for the girl from Hollowmere who had carved a dead woman's last words by firelight and believed the world could be kept out by a valley's worth of forgetting.

The weather of voices stilled. Not silenced — *attended.*

*THE CARVER LISTENS,* said a thousand throats at once, soft as snowfall. *THE CARVER LISTENS. THEN WALK, LITTLE HEIR. WE WILL HOLD THE ROADS REMEMBERED.*

And the wall ahead of us — a wall of packed bone that no map could have shown, because it wasn't a passage at all — sighed, shifted, and opened.

Beyond it lay the servants' way the Master of Keys had promised, running cold and clean toward the north postern, and beyond that, somewhere in the frozen dark, a wolf running to meet us with the whole starving patience of his kindred at his back.

We went through. All six of us. Vane last, and as he crossed the threshold he paused, laid one gloved hand briefly on the bone wall, and said, so quietly I almost lost it: "I'll come back. When it's safe to be remembered, I'll come back and learn all your names."

The bones said nothing. But the passage behind us sealed itself gently, like a door closed by a forgiving hand, and I chose to take that as an answer.

---

We came up out of the earth into falling snow, in the shadow of the north wall, and Hallow was there — gray ghost-shape materializing out of the drifts, gaunt from the hard winter road, golden eyes finding me first and everyone else second. He pressed his broad head against my chest hard enough to stagger me, breathing me in like something stolen and recovered.

*You smell of the dead city,* he said. *And of fear. And of purpose. It suits you.*

"Flatterer."

Then his head came up, nostrils flaring, fixed on Vane — and a growl started low in him, subterranean, ancient, the sound of a verdict being reached. *This one hunted you.*

"This one unlocked me," I said. "Both things are true. We're carrying both things."

Hallow's growl went on a moment longer, testing my words the way ice is tested — weight applied, cracks listened for. Then he stepped back, though his eyes never left Vane. *Very well. Both things. But know this, hunter-of-my-bonded:* the wolf's voice rang clear across the snow, audible to every one of us, human ears included, *my brother Skarn has smelled the blood in your line. The radicals ride south behind me, and they do not share my patience with cowards, however reformed. If we mean to reach the Wolf Gate by midwinter, we have four days and a country full of hunters between us and it.*

Above us, the palace bells began to toll alarm — one, two, three, a rhythm that meant *escape, loose, hunt.*

Maren pulled Wren up onto her hip. Osric took his brother's hand. Snow came down thicker, erasing our tracks as fast as we made them, and I looked north toward the glacier-hidden gate where everything I was would soon be spent.

"Four days," I said. "Then we run."
