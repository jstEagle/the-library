## 14. Winter Closes

The snow came down like a judgment.

Petra had known winter her whole life — Hollowmere winters that whitened the valley and silenced the roads and sent the elders grumbling about salt and firewood. She had thought she understood cold. She had not. Hollowmere's winters were a season. This was a siege.

Three days out of Corvane, the rain had turned to sleet. By the fourth day, the sleet had turned to something finer and crueller, a dry hissing snow that fell without wind and never seemed to land, hanging in the air like ash from a fire too far away to see. The sky was the color of dirty wool, pressed down low, and the sun was a pale smear behind it, a coin rubbed nearly blank.

They traveled north in a ragged line: Maren at the front with the children strung out behind her like beads on a fraying cord — Tam, who was nine and stubborn and had already learned to pick any lock smaller than his fist; Wren, seven, who spoke to birds and had not spoken to people since her village burned; little Sefa, five, who rode in a sling against Maren's chest and cried only when she was too tired not to. Behind them came Petra, and behind Petra, at a distance he seemed to think she didn't notice, came Serel Vane.

He had given them his horse. He walked. Petra suspected the penance was deliberate. She had not yet decided whether it moved her or infuriated her, and the not-deciding was beginning to wear a groove in her.

* * *

They crossed the river Liss on the sixth morning, or rather, they crossed where the river Liss had been.

It was frozen solid. Not crusted at the edges, not skimmed with ice along the banks — frozen through, top to bottom, a hundred feet of running water stopped like a held breath. The surface was smooth as a blade and white as bone, and when Petra knelt and laid her palm flat against it, she felt nothing underneath. No current. No life. Rivers, she knew from the dead, were the loudest things in the world if you knew how to listen — a thousand years of drowned voices, of fish and flood and ferry. This one was silent as a sealed tomb.

"It shouldn't be able to do this," Maren said. She stood with her boots planted wide on the ice, a storm-speaker testing the weather's temper and finding it beyond her. Her breath came out in ragged plumes. "Rivers don't freeze like this. Not through. Not in six days. The water keeps moving underneath, it keeps its own heat, it—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "The covenant's dying faster than we thought."

"Then we go faster too," Tam said. He had appointed himself the company's conscience of pace, because it made him feel less like a child fleeing and more like a soldier marching. Nobody had corrected him.

They crossed the dead river in single file, and Petra, last across, paused at the far bank and looked back the way they'd come. South, toward the capital, the sky had a different quality — not lighter, exactly, but busier, smudged with the smoke of a hundred thousand hearths burning against a winter that would not end. North, where they were going, the sky was emptier and somehow more terrible, as if the land had already given up pretending.

Hallow was a warmth against her hip where his skull rode in her pack, wrapped in wool. He did not speak much these days. The bond between them had thinned with distance from the wolf realm, and each day she felt him fading a little, like a voice calling from farther and farther away. When he did speak, it was in fragments.

*The young die first,* he had said the night before, in the dark, in that voice like wind through a hollow reed. *The old hold on out of spite. It was ever so. Hurry, carver.*

* * *

They passed the first dead orchard on the eighth day, and the first refugee column on the ninth.

The orchard was worse, somehow. Trees that had stood for a hundred years, black and split down their trunks, their frozen fruit still clinging to the branches like a crop of stone. A farmstead nearby stood with its door open to the snow. No bodies. The living had walked out; the dead, if there were dead, had been taken by neighbors or by nothing. On the lintel someone had scratched a mark Petra recognized from Vane's descriptions of court propaganda — the crown's sigil, and beneath it, in a crude hand: *THE GIFTED DID THIS.*

She stood looking at it for a long time.

"That's not true," Wren said suddenly. Petra startled; the girl almost never spoke. She was staring at the scratched words with an expression far too old for her face. "The birds told me. The birds say the cold comes from under the ground. From under the *ice*. It's old, and it's angry, and it isn't people."

"No," Petra agreed quietly. "It isn't people."

But it would be people who paid for it. It was already people paying. The refugee column they overtook that afternoon stretched along the north road for half a mile — carts piled with children and grandmothers and feather mattresses and cages of chickens, all of it moving at the speed of the slowest ox. They had the gray, patient faces of people who had left too late and knew it. A woman walking beside a handcart nodded to Petra as she passed, and her nod was the nod of one drowning person to another.

"We can't feed them," Maren said, low, when the children had been herded out of earshot. "We can barely feed ourselves. And every hour we spend in a crowd is an hour someone might see Vane's face."

"Then we go around."

"Petra." Maren caught her sleeve. Her eyes were red-rimmed; she had not slept a whole night since Corvane. "Look at them. Half of them won't see spring. You know that. I know that. And we're going to walk past them on our way to save the world."

Petra looked at the column. At a boy about Tam's age sharing a crust with a dog. At an old man carrying his wife on his back because the cart had broken and the ox had been eaten.

"I know," she said.

It was all she could say. It was becoming, she was learning, the most honest sentence in the world.

* * *

It happened at a place called Harrow's Rest, on the eleventh day.

It was a crossroads village, or had been — a coaching inn, a smithy, a shrine to some road-god whose statue had lost its head to weather or to mobs. Now it was a refugee camp, forty or fifty souls huddled around fires fed with fence rails and furniture, and the company had no choice but to stop, because Sefa was feverish and Tam's boots had split and pride was a luxury none of them could afford.

Vane went in first. That was the argument — he insisted, saying his face would draw trouble away from the children, and Maren had snarled that his face would *be* the trouble, and in the end he simply walked toward the inn before anyone could stop him, because that was what Vane did, decided things alone and called it protection.

Petra found him twenty minutes later, and she knew before she reached him. She knew by the way the crowd around the inn door had gone still, the way a field goes still when a hawk's shadow crosses it.

A man stood in front of Vane. He was missing two fingers on his left hand and most of his right ear, and his face was the face of a man who had spent eleven days looking at everyone who passed him. He was looking at Vane now the way you look at a ghost.

"Inquisitor," the man said. Not a question.

The word went through the camp like a spark through dry grass. Petra heard it repeated — *inquisitor, inquisitor, the inquisitor* — in voices that ranged from whisper to shout. A woman near the well began to cry. A man picked up a length of fence rail.

"You burned Fenwick," the man with the missing fingers said. His voice was steady, which was worse than screaming. "You came to our village in the autumn. You had a list. My daughter was on it. She was *nine*." He held up his maimed hand. "I asked you to take me instead. Do you remember what you said?"

Vane did not answer. He stood very straight, the way he always did, but Petra, who had spent weeks learning to read him, saw the thing underneath the straightness — the way a tree is straight right up until the axe bites.

"I remember," the man said, answering himself. "You said the crown takes what the crown requires. You said it gently. That was the worst part. You were *kind* about it."

"We should go," Maren breathed at Petra's shoulder. "Petra. We should *go*."

But Petra was looking at Vane's face, and Vane was looking at the man's ruined hand, and something in her — the part of her that had carved the dead and heard their last words, the part that knew every soul ends its story with a sentence it never chose — would not let her walk away from this one mid-telling.

"Say what you came to say," she said to the man. Loudly. The camp had gone quiet enough that it carried.

He turned to her, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Who is she? His witch? His—" He stopped. Squinted. "You're her. The Brightwater girl. The one they say broke out of the Ivory Court." A ripple went through the crowd — wonder this time, and hope, dangerous hope. "They say the dead king spoke through you. They say the winter is the old king's sin and not ours."

"The dead king spoke," Petra said. "The rest is true enough."

"Then why," the man said, and his voice cracked at last, "is he *walking free*?"

The fence rail was in the crowd's hands now, passed forward like a collection plate. Vane had not moved. He had not reached for a weapon. He stood in the falling snow with his arms at his sides, and he said, in a voice pitched to carry the way a confession carries:

"Because she hasn't killed me yet. And because everything that man said is true. I burned Fenwick. I burned eleven villages before it. I took children from their mothers and called it mercy, because the alternative was to refuse and be taken in their place, and I was a coward, and I told myself the cowardice was wisdom." He paused. "I have nothing to say in my defense. Only that I am trying, now, to spend what's left of my life paying down the debt. And that I will kneel, if it helps. Or I will leave. Or I will give this crowd my throat. Whatever the lady—" he inclined his head to Petra, and there was nothing performative in it, nothing she could dismiss, "—decides. It was her mercy that freed the children. It should be her mercy that judges me."

Every face turned to her.

This was the moment Petra understood, with a sick lurch, what it cost to be the one the dead king had spoken through. People did not want her to be a person. They wanted her to be a verdict.

She looked at the man with the missing fingers. "Your daughter's name," she said.

His fury faltered. "Liss. Her name was Liss."

"May I?"

He stared at her. Then, slowly, he drew a cord from under his shirt — a small carved charm, crude work, a child's attempt at a bird. "They found this in the ashes. It's all I have."

Petra took it in both hands. She closed her eyes. She was tired, so tired, down in the marrow, and the carving was crude and the girl had been nine and had never been taught properly, but the dead did not need polish. They needed only a listener.

*Liss* came through like a candle flame in a draft: *Liss, who was not afraid until the very end, and who at the very end was not afraid of the men but of her father's face when he saw her go. Papa, don't look like that. Papa, it isn't your fault. Papa, I hid the bird where you'd find it.*

Petra opened her eyes. They were streaming, and she did not try to hide it.

"She wasn't afraid of you," she said. "She was afraid for you. She says it isn't your fault. She says—" her voice caught, steadied, "—she says she hid the bird where you'd find it. She wanted you to have it."

The man made a sound that was not a word. He folded over the charm like a man folding over a wound. And the fence rail, somewhere in the crowd, quietly went down.

Petra turned to Vane. "You will carry water for this camp until we leave. You will split wood until your hands blister. And you will look at every person here, and you will not look away." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped so only he could hear. "I don't forgive you. I don't know if I ever will. But the dead taught me that a soul is the sum of what it does *next*, not what it did before. So do next, Serel Vane. Do next, every day, until the ledger balances or you die trying."

He held her gaze. "Yes," he said simply. And went for the water.

* * *

They stayed two days at Harrow's Rest, because Sefa's fever would not break and because the camp's well had frozen and Vane spent those two days with a pickaxe and a rope, hauling ice, hauling water, splitting wood, saying nothing, looking at everyone. On the second evening, the man with the missing fingers — his name was Aldous — brought Vane a bowl of stew. He did not speak. Vane did not speak. They sat together by the fire like two men shoveling out a collapsed tunnel, and Petra, watching from the doorway of the inn, thought: *this is what atonement looks like. Not the grand gesture. The bowl of stew.*

Sefa's fever broke at dawn on the third day, and they went north again.

* * *

The land worsened.

They left the north road entirely on the sixteenth day, because the road was a river of refugees now, a slow-motion flood of carts and livestock and frostbitten despair, and Maren said they'd make better time over the high ground even if the high ground might kill them. The farms they passed stood empty or burned. The forests were dying — not browning, but *blackening*, the trees freezing from the roots up, so that a whole hillside of oaks could stand green and glossy and dead as a waxwork. The birds had gone. Wren said the birds had all flown south past the edge of the world, and that the ones who stayed were sorry.

At night, Petra carved.

She had almost no material left — a few slivers of bone, a knuckle of antler Hallow had guided her to beneath the snow. She carved the dead of the villages they passed, when she could find their remains, and she sent their last words home with refugees or scratched them onto waystones for whoever came after. *Tell my sister the deed is in the bible box. Tell my son I was proud of him every day. The cold came from the north and it sang.* It was not enough. It was a cup bailing the sea. But the dead deserved a listener, and she was the only one for a thousand miles, and so she listened until her fingers cracked and bled, and Maren bandaged them without comment, and the company moved north.

Hallow spoke to her less and less. On the eighteenth night, she took his skull from her pack and held it in her lap by the fire, and his voice came so faint she had to still her breath to catch it.

*Carver. I am sorry. The distance—*

"I know."

*The gate. You must reach the gate. I will hold what I can hold, but I am becoming an echo, Petra. Even echoes have a limit.* A pause, and then, with the ghost of his old dry humor: *Though I was always told I was too loud to fade entirely.*

"Hold on," she said. "Hold on for me. Midwinter is nine days away. We're close. I can feel it — the cold comes *from* somewhere, Hallow. It has a source. It's like a wound in the world, and we're walking toward it."

*Then walk faster,* he said, and was gone, and she sat holding a skull that was beginning to feel, horribly, like any other skull — like bone, and nothing more.

* * *

On the twentieth day, they saw the mountains.

They had been a rumor on the horizon for a week, a gray suggestion behind the gray sky. Now, as the company crested a long frozen ridge, they stood up out of the world's edge like the spine of some buried god: the Teeth, the range the old maps called the Wall of the North, white and vast and utterly silent. And in their midst, half-hidden in cloud, a single peak that was not a peak at all — a wall of ice so high and so sheer that it looked less like geography than like architecture. Like a door.

Maren stopped dead. The wind pulled at her gray-streaked hair. "The Wolf Gate," she said. Not a question. She was a storm-speaker; she could feel it the way Petra could feel the dead, and what she felt had drained her face of color. "It's *open*, Petra. Cracked. The seal is failing, and everything on the other side is pouring through — not wolves, not kin, just *cold*, the raw cold of their dying realm, leaking into ours through the wound." She wrapped her arms around herself. "That's the winter. It isn't weather. It's a *haemorrhage*."

"Then we seal it," Petra said.

"With what? The lore says a life from each side. A life freely given. You read the same crypt-voices I did." Maren's voice was gentle and terrible. "Petra, we're nine days out, and midwinter is—"

"Nine days away. I can count." Petra looked at the ice-wall in the distance, at the wound in the world that she was the last living soul able to close, and she felt something settle in her that was not peace but was adjacent to it — the stillness of a decision already made, waiting only for its moment. "One thing at a time. First we cross the high pass before the next storm. Then we find the gate. Then—" she hesitated, and did not say the rest, because the rest was a bridge she would cross when she came to it, alone, in the dark, where no one who loved her could argue.

Behind her, she heard Vane shift the pack on his shoulders. He had been quiet since Harrow's Rest — not sullen, just *present*, a man doing next, doing next, doing next. He came up beside her and followed her gaze to the distant wall of ice.

"That's where it ends," he said. "One way or another."

"That's where it begins," Petra said. "The end already happened. A long time ago. In a palace, to a king who wanted more than he was given."

She turned north, and the company turned with her, six small figures on a white immensity, walking toward the wound in the world.

They made camp that night in the shadow of the first foothills, and Petra dreamed of the gate. She dreamed it as the dead had described it in the crypts: a door of interlocking bone and stone older than any kingdom, breathing slow and labored, its seal cracked like an eggshell. And in her dream, something on the other side was pressing against the crack — not cold, not wolves, but *hunger*, a hunger old and vast and patient, and behind the hunger, faint as a voice through a wall, she heard something else.

Not the wolves. Not the kin.

A voice, thin and furious and unmistakably alive, crying out in a tongue she almost knew:

*They're coming. The carver is coming. Brother — brother, we have to stop her before she reaches the gate.*

Petra woke with the name already forming in her mouth, a name Hallow had spoken only once, in bitterness, in the dark of a cell in Corvane.

Skarn.
