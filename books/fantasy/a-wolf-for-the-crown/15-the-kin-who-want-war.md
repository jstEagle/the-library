## 15. The Kin Who Want War

The snow told Hallow they were coming three days before they arrived.

Not the ordinary speech of weather — the small complaints of wind, the mutter of ice settling on stone — but a deliberate message, pressed into the drifts by paws that knew the old signs. *We see you. We come. Stand and be counted.* The script was his mother's script, the one she had used to mark the borders of the den when he and Skarn were cubs, meaning *this is ours, stay inside it.*

Someone wanted him to know they were kin before they showed their teeth.

He stood at the ridge above the refugee road, breath steaming, and did not tell Petra what he had read. Below, the human column wound through the frozen pass like a thread of embers: Maren at the front with frost crowning her dark hair, the children roped together in the middle where the wind couldn't strip the heat from them too fast, Vane limping on his healed crooked leg and pretending he wasn't. And Petra in the rear, as always, walking with her head half-turned toward the north as if she could hear the glacier calling.

She could. That was the problem. He could feel it through the bond, that thin silver thread the covenant had spun between them back in the pass what felt like another lifetime — her attention pulling northward like water finding its level, and his own answering whether he willed it or not.

And now someone else had felt it too.

*Brother,* the snow said, in a hand he would have known blindfolded, in the dark, dead. *You travel with the carver. So do we. Let us meet you at the Split Stone before midwinter eve, blood of my blood, or let us meet you however it must be.*

Skarn.

Hallow closed his eyes and let the wind carry his scent backward down the valley, deliberately, so there could be no mistake. *I will be there,* he wrote into the gust with the muscles of his throat, the old way. *Come in peace and I will greet you as my heart greets you. Come otherwise, and I will still greet you — but I will not step aside.*

The wind took it south. There was no answer. There never was, with Skarn; his brother had always believed that silence was a form of strength, that a wolf who explained himself had already lost. Their mother had died trying to teach him otherwise. Their father had died proving her right.

Hallow turned and loped down toward the road, toward the humans, toward the bond that pulled at him like a second heartbeat, and hated — quietly, in the place where he kept all his oldest hates — how much easier it would be to simply run the other way.

* * *

"We should go around," Hallow said, crouched beside the fire that night in the lee of the Split Stone itself, a granite fang split down the middle by some ancient violence, its two halves leaning together like conspirators. "South fork of the valley. It adds two days but takes us off the meeting ground."

"Two days." Maren fed a twist of scrub-pine to the flames. "In two days the cold reaches the lowlands and every river from here to Corvane turns to stone. We don't have two days, wolf."

"You don't understand what's coming."

"Then make me understand."

He looked at Petra instead. She sat wrapped in the grey wool cloak Vane had stolen for her from a garrison storehouse, hands stretched to the fire, and the light moved across her face the way the light of the living world always moved across it now — touching her, and yet not quite settling, as though her skin had forgotten how to hold warmth all the way through. Since the crypts of Corvane, since the gate's power had begun waking in her blood, she cast two shadows in torchlight: one hers, one longer and older, shaped faintly wrong.

She was becoming the bridge. Every day a little less woman, a little more door.

"The ones following us are mine," Hallow said. "Wolf-kindred. From the northern packs — the starving ones, the ones whose realm is dying fastest. They call themselves the Red Snow. They believe the covenant can't be reforged. They believe it shouldn't be."

Vane looked up sharply. Inquisitor's instincts; even redeemed, even limping, even half-dead of cold and shame, the man heard a threat being named and reached for it. "Shouldn't?"

"They want the gate sealed." Hallow held his brother's gaze in memory — amber gone flat as old honey, the scar through his brow from the duel Hallow had refused to finish. "Sealed forever. Both sides locked apart. Let the humans keep their realm and freeze in it, and let the kindred keep theirs and starve in it, clean and separate, no more bargains, no more half-blood covenants, no more—" he paused, and the pause said everything — "*betrayal*. They think the long winter is already the answer. They just want to make it final."

Silence around the fire. One of the children, a boy called Tam with a burn-scarred chin, stopped chewing.

"And your brother leads them," Petra said quietly.

"My brother leads them." He had known this for months, in the way you know a wound has gone bad — by the smell. Skarn had been at the gate when the last emissaries were turned away. Skarn had buried more cubs than any wolf should count, and counting them had broken something in him that grief alone had only bent. "When I left the northern packs to find the carver line, I thought I was carrying hope. He thought I was carrying a lie. To him the founding covenant was the day our kind knelt. Reforging it is kneeling again — worse, because this time we'd kneel to a girl who smells of grave-dirt and speaks to dead men's bones."

"That girl," Maren said mildly, "is sitting right here."

"I know," Hallow said. "That's why I'm telling you instead of lying to you."

Petra had not moved. She was watching him with those pale eyes that saw too much, that had seen the dead king's confession out of his own skull's dust, and through the bond he felt her take the shape of what he hadn't said and turn it over, gentle as a paw turning a beetle.

"There's more," she said. Not a question.

"He doesn't want the gate sealed with stone and ritual." Hallow's voice came out lower than he intended. "A seal needs blood to bind it. Old law, same as the reforge. Kindred blood on one side." He made himself say the rest. "Carver blood on the other. The last heir of the founding line, freely given — spilled, not spent. Her death would close the gate for ten thousand years."

The fire cracked. Somewhere up-valley, ice fell from a branch with a sound like a snapped bone.

"So I'm not just an obstacle to him," Petra said. "I'm an ingredient."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, and Hallow marveled — as he had marveled every day since the pass, since the skeleton he had haunted woke to her carving-knife — at how this small, quiet creature who had grown up in a valley where magic could not follow kept absorbing enormities the way stone absorbs rain. No screaming. No bargaining with heaven. Just the nod, and then the next question.

"How many are with him?"

"Thirty spears. Maybe forty." Kin, not wolves — the Red Snow fought in their second shape, upright and armed, which told him everything about how far they had fallen from the old ways. Wolves hunted. Men made war. "And Skarn has killed better-protected travelers than us in worse ground than this."

"Then we don't run," Petra said. She looked around the fire, at each of them, and Hallow felt through the bond the thing he was learning about her: that her courage was not the absence of fear but a decision made fresh each hour, renewed like bread. "Running north with forty killers behind us means dying tired. If they want a meeting at the Split Stone—" she touched the granite beside her, almost fondly, "—we'll give them one. On our feet."

* * *

They came at dusk on the second day, out of the treeline in perfect silence, which was itself a message — wolf-kindred who moved without song had abandoned more than peace.

Hallow counted thirty-six. Tall in their man-shapes, gaunt with the northern hunger, cloaked in white-dyed hide so they melted into the snowfield like gaps in the world. Bone talismans at their throats — real bone, carved, and the sight of it made something twist in Hallow's chest, because those were the death-marks of their own dead, worn as war-tokens. His people had started eating their grief. That was what starvation meant.

Skarn walked at their head.

He had been the beautiful one, once. Even now, lean and silver-grey with winter, there was a terrible rightness to him, a whetted elegance. But the scar Hallow's refusal had given him — the dueling scar, the mercy-scar — had been joined by others, and his eyes had gone the color of frozen amber, light passing through them without warming.

"Hallow." His brother's voice hadn't changed. It never needed to; Skarn had been born knowing exactly how loud to be. "You look well. Starvation suits some of us better than others."

"You look like our father," Hallow answered. "Right down to the mistakes."

A ripple went through the Red Snow lines. Behind Hallow, he heard Vane's hand move to his sword, and Maren's breath gather like a storm deciding, and the children being drawn backward by the eldest girl, Asha, who had learned in a cage how to put her body between smaller bodies and danger.

But Petra stepped forward.

Past Hallow's shoulder, past the fire-ring, out onto the trampled snow between the two companies, and Hallow's heart seized in his chest like a fist closing on a bird. Through the bond poured her fear — real, cold, human — and beneath it, steady as bedrock under topsoil, the thing she had become. The gate hummed in her blood. Even Skarn's veterans shifted at whatever it was they scented off her now: winter and hearth-smoke both, the living and the leavings, a threshold wearing boots.

"You are Skarn Wolfson," she said. Not loudly. But the valley carried it, the way Hollowmere valleys carry everything. "Brother to Hallow. Son of Mera of the Northreach, who signed the third renewal of the covenant with her own thumbprint in ash, which I have seen, because I have spoken with her."

Dead silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Hallow watched his brother's composure crack — not much, a hairline, the width of a claw-tip — at the name of their mother. He watched Skarn's jaw work once.

"You speak to ashes," Skarn said at last.

"I listen to them. There's a difference." Petra clasped her hands before her, and Hallow recognized the stance: it was how she'd stood among the bones in her workshop, patient, certain, terribly awake. "Your mother does not want the gate sealed, Skarn. I've asked her. Would you like to know what she said?"

"You'll say whatever keeps you breathing—"

"She said: *my sons fight over my grave, and neither of them will dig it.*" Petra's voice didn't rise. It dropped, into the register Hallow had heard her use only twice — the voice that was partly hers and partly the long corridor of the dead speaking through her. "She said: *tell Skarn the seal he wants is a tomb, and he knows whose body goes in it first.*"

"*Enough.*" Skarn's snarl broke across the snowfield, and forty throats answered, and for one suspended heartbeat Hallow stood at the exact center of his whole life: his brother's rage on one side, his bond's gravity on the other, loyalty and truth made flesh and set against each other in his own chest like two hands crushing the same heart.

Because that was the truth of it. He loved Skarn. He had carried Skarn through a blizzard when they were yearlings, had given him the warm side of his body, had bitten the ear of any wolf who mocked his stutter. And he loved — *loved*, in the strange new grammar the covenant had taught him — the woman standing in front of his brother's war-band with the dead whispering in her sleeves. There was no version of the next hour in which he did not betray one of them. There was only the question of which betrayal he could live inside afterward.

So he chose neither.

He walked forward and stood beside Petra. Then, slowly, deliberately, in full view of forty witnesses, he bared his throat to her — the kindred gesture of absolute submission, the one no free wolf gave to any human, the one that had not been performed since the founding.

Behind him he heard Maren's sharp intake of breath. Heard Vane swear softly in court-tongue.

"Skarn," Hallow said. "I won't fight you. I won't let her be taken. And I won't run, because I'm done running from family." He held his brother's frozen stare. "Kill her and you kill the last carver and the gate dies unsealed and our young keep freezing in the dark forever. Take her blood and you feed your pride with our children's graves. Or stand down for six days — *six days*, brother — and watch her open the gate at midwinter and give the lie to every word the Red Snow ever sang."

"And if she fails?" Skarn's voice was silk over gravel. "If your little bridge can't bear the crossing? Will you bare your throat then, Hallow? Will you kneel while the last of our line freezes beside the last of hers?"

"If she fails," Hallow said quietly, "then I'll stand beside you in the dark, and we'll freeze together as brothers, and it will have cost us nothing but hope — which we never had anyway, you and I. Not really."

For a moment — one long, glacial moment — he believed it had worked. He saw it in his brother's face: the exhaustion underneath the fury, the cub who had once slept curled against his flank looking out through the war-leader's eyes, wanting so badly to put the weight down.

Then Skarn smiled, and Hallow's stomach turned over, because he knew that smile. It was the smile Skarn wore when he had already moved the board.

"Six days," Skarn said pleasantly. "Granted. Blood of my blood — granted. Meet us at the glacier mouth on midwinter eve, and may the best faith win." He turned, cloak sweeping snow, and his band melted after him into the treeline the way they had come, silent as falling white.

Only when they were gone did Hallow realize his claws had sunk fully through his own palms, and that Petra had taken his arm, and that through the bond she was sending him steadiness she did not herself possess.

"It was too easy," she said.

"Yes," Hallow said, watching the treeline where his brother had vanished. "It was too easy."

Far to the north, faint as a hairline crack across the sky, the horizon flickered — not lightning, not dawn. Something vast shifting its weight beneath the glacier.

Maren shaded her eyes. "What in the storm's name was that?"

Hallow didn't answer. He was reading the tremor through his paws, through his teeth, through the old blood in him that remembered what the gate sounded like when it was being *woken* — and Skarn had gone north, not south.

His brother wasn't waiting for midwinter to test the gate.

He was going to break it open early — and whatever came through first, he meant to blame on them.
