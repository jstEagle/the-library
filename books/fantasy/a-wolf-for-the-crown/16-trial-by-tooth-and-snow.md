## 16. Trial by Tooth and Snow

The wolves came at dawn, when the snow had stopped and the world lay blue and silent as a held breath.

Petra heard them before she saw them — not with her ears, which the dead had long since taught her to distrust, but through the bond, where Hallow's heart suddenly went taut as a bowstring. She was kneeling by the fire pit, coaxing warmth back into her fingers, when every hair on her arms rose.

*Skarn,* Hallow said inside her skull, and there was something in his voice she had never heard before. Not fear. Something older than fear. *He found us.*

They came down the ridge in a line, eight of them in their half-shapes — not wolves, not quite people, but the terrible country between: tall as men, furred in white and grey, moving on two legs with a predator's rolling gait. Frost smoked from their jaws. Their eyes caught the early light like chips of flint.

The children woke screaming. Maren was up first, storm-light crackling along her knuckles, and Vane — who had died a dozen small deaths since the Ivory Court and somehow kept walking — put himself between the children and the treeline with nothing in his hands but a hunting knife and a lifetime of bad decisions.

"Behind me," he said. "All of you. Now."

The lead figure stopped twenty paces out, where the snow lay unbroken, and let the others fan wide around the camp in a slow, patient crescent. Up close, Petra could see the family resemblance that Hallow carried like a scar: the same broad cheekbones, the same silver-grey pelt, the same gold-flecked eyes. But where Hallow's face was weathered into something almost gentle, this wolf's face had been carved by colder hands.

Skarn looked at her the way the inquisitors used to look at the caged children — cataloguing, weighing, deciding what she was worth.

"So," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over ice. "The little carver. The one my brother died for."

"He didn't die for me," Petra said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "And he's not dead."

"No." Skarn's lip curled back from teeth too long for any human mouth. "Worse. He's *bound*. Tied by the throat to a soft-skinned thing that smells of grave-dirt and human blood." He circled left; one of his kin drifted right, cutting off the path toward the pass. "I smelled you three days ago, carver. I've been letting you walk. I wanted to see if you'd turn north on your own. Toward the Gate."

"I'm going to the Gate," Petra said.

"You're going to *open* it." Spittle froze in the air between them. "That is the whole of it, isn't it? The last of the founding line crawls out of her hiding valley at the end of the world, and everyone tells her she must reforge the covenant, renew the old bargain, save both realms." He spat. "My pack's young are dying in the dark, carver. My mother starved beneath the glacier rather than eat the frozen dead. And your kind broke the word that doomed us. Your king traded our protection for his throne and thought we'd never know."

"We know now," Petra said quietly. "I heard him confess it. In his own voice, from his own bones."

Something flickered across Skarn's face — pain, quickly buried. "Then you know why I can't let you reach it. A reforged covenant means the gate opens. It means humans crossing again, trading again, *taking* again. It means another king, a hundred years from now, selling us out a second time while we starve politely at the table we built for them." He raised one clawed hand, and his kin tightened their circle. "There is another way. Your blood, spilled at the threshold, seals the gate forever. The realms part clean. Humanity freezes in its own winter. And the kindred keep what's ours, alone, whole, free."

"That's murder dressed as mercy," Maren snapped. Lightning crawled up her arm; the nearest wolf-kin flinched.

"It's *justice*," Skarn snarled, "and you—"

"Skarn." Hallow stepped forward, placing himself at his brother's shoulder, close enough that their fur nearly touched. His voice was low and terribly calm. "Brother. Look at me. I stood where you stand. I wanted the same. And then I walked beside her through the burning villages, and I watched her free human children from cages meant for her, and I listened to the dead king weep through her hands." His gold eyes did not waver. "The gate does not need her death. It needs her *choice*. There is a difference wider than the sea between them, and you are standing on the wrong shore."

"The choice of one soft-skinned girl against the survival of the kindred?" Skarn's laugh was a horrible sound. "You always were our mother's fool, Hallow. Sentiment. The pack has voted with its hunger. Stand aside, or stand with them, and be counted."

Hallow didn't move. "Then count me with them."

For one endless moment, the whole world was the space between those two brothers, and the snow falling into it.

Then Skarn turned back to Petra, and all the grief in his face hardened into something with edges. "Last chance, carver. Kneel at the threshold and open your throat, and I'll let the children and the storm-witch walk away. That is more mercy than your kind ever showed mine."

Petra looked at the knife in Vane's hand. At Maren's shaking, glowing fists. At the children huddled behind the sledge, small faces white above their scarves — Jory, who still sucked his thumb at night; little Ash, who hadn't spoken since the cages. At Hallow, standing in the snow between two worlds, ready to be torn apart by his own blood for her sake.

And something in her went very quiet, and very cold, and very clear.

She was tired. She was so tired of running, of being bargained for, of being the price other people paid. She had been shoved through a mountain pass, sold to a queen regent, locked in an ivory cell, hunted across half a frozen kingdom. Every step of her life, someone else had decided what she was worth — the elders who hid her gift, the hunters who listed her name, the regent who priced her freedom in children's lives, and now this grieving, starving wolf who wanted to spend her blood like coin.

No more.

"No," Petra said.

Skarn's ears flattened. "Then die standing."

"*No*," she said again, louder, and reached into the bundle at her belt, and drew out the thing she had carried since the pass — the wolf skeleton's skull, Hallow's mortal remains, carved over every surface with covenant-script older than any kingdom. She had polished it each night by the fire without fully knowing why. Now she knew.

She held it high, and she spoke in the Old Tongue, the words rising out of her like water finding its level — words she had never learned, that lived in her blood the way the covenant-script lived in bone:

*"By tooth and by snow, by the law that was cut before crowns were cut, I claim what is mine. I am Petra Brightwater, daughter of Hollowmere, last carver of the founding line. The road to the Wolf Gate is mine by right. Let any who bar it answer to the law."*

The words did not echo. They *sank* — into the snow, into the trees, into the bones of the mountain itself. Petra felt them go, felt the land take hold of them the way her hands took hold of fresh-carved bone. The air changed pressure, the way it did before a storm or a verdict.

The wolf-kin froze. All eight of them, mid-stride, as if the mountain had put its hand on their shoulders.

Skarn stared at her. Then at the carved skull in her hands. Then at his brother, whose expression had gone strange — awe and grief tangled together.

"She invoked passage-right," Hallow said softly. "The old law. The one even the kings were bound by, back when the covenant was strong." He looked at his brother, and there was no triumph in it at all. "You know it, Skarn. Mother taught us both. *A carver of the founding line may claim the road to the Gate, and none may bar her, on pain of breaking the last law that holds the wild and the woven worlds apart.*"

"The covenant is broken," Skarn growled. "Its laws died with it."

"No." Hallow shook his head slowly. "Broken things still have shape, brother. That's how we know they were once whole. The law stands because the *Gate* stands. Refuse her passage, and you don't defy a girl — you defy the door itself. And the door remembers."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked down through the branches. One of the younger wolf-kin — a female with frost-scars across her muzzle — glanced at Skarn, then at the sky, then deliberately took one step backward out of the crescent. Then another. Whatever the old law weighed in their hearts, it was heavier than hunger.

Skarn's claws flexed. Opened. Flexed again. Petra watched him stand at the exact center of himself — the brother who had starved beside dying pups, the son of a mother who ate frost rather than shame, the radical who had chosen war because hope hurt too much — and she saw the moment the law landed in him like a stone in deep water.

"You would go anyway," he said at last, quietly. "Even without your magic words. Even knowing what waits for you under the glacier."

"Yes," Petra said.

"And if the Gate demands a life? If the old bargain wants blood freely given, as the oldest bargains always do?" His gold eyes bored into hers, hunting for fear, for hesitation, for the softness he could use. "Will you pay it, carver? Or will you stand at the threshold with your hand out, asking the kindred to bleed so the soft world can keep its summer?"

"I'll pay what it costs," Petra said. "Whatever it costs. But it will be *my* choice, made at *my* pace, on the road that is *mine* by right. You don't get to hurry me into the grave to spare yourself the waiting."

Something moved behind Skarn's eyes — not surrender, never that, but a grudging, furious recognition. The look of a wolf meeting something with teeth of its own.

He stepped back. One pace. Two. His kin melted away from the camp's edges, folding back into the treeline like smoke returning to a fire.

"Passage is yours," Skarn said. "By the old law, I cannot bar the road, and I will not break the last law we have left — the gods know we've broken everything else." His lip lifted, showing one long white fang. "But hear me, Petra Brightwater. Conceding is not trusting. I will follow you to the Gate. I will watch every mile, every meal, every hour you sleep. And the moment you falter — the moment you hesitate at the threshold, or flinch from the price, or show me one heartbeat of weakness — the law ends where your courage ends. Remember that."

"I remember everything," Petra said. "It's my curse and my craft."

For just a moment — a single heartbeat — Skarn's gaze dropped to the carved skull in her hands, and his expression cracked open like river ice, and she saw underneath it not hatred but a grief so vast it had nowhere left to go except outward, into cruelty. Then it sealed over again.

"Move at dusk," he said. "The drifts past the ridge will swallow anything that travels by day." And he turned and was gone into the trees, white on white, as if the snow had simply agreed to pretend he had never existed.

* * *

They walked at dusk, as he had told them to, because Petra was pragmatic enough to take tactical advice even from a wolf who wanted her dead.

The company strung out along the old road beneath the ridge: Vane in front with the knife and the sledge rope, the children bundled between them, Maren watching the flanks with storm-light banked low in her palms, Hallow ranging ahead in his full wolf-shape, grey ghost between the trees. And somewhere behind them, always exactly at the edge of sight, Skarn and his seven — following, watching, patient as winter itself.

"They're not trying to hide," Maren muttered, glancing back for the tenth time. "That's the part I hate. He *wants* us to see him."

"He does," Petra said. "It's not a threat. It's a test. He's measuring us with every mile. How fast we tire. How the children slow us. Whether I limp." She adjusted the strap of her pack, where the carved skull rode against her spine like a second heartbeat. "He conceded the law, not the fight. He's waiting for me to give him a reason."

"And when you don't?"

"Then he waits until the Gate," Petra said. "And watches what it asks of me."

Vane fell into step beside her, breath smoking. Up close, in the failing light, he looked ten years older than he had in Corvane — grey coming into his beard, hollows under his eyes, the brand of the queen's service still faintly visible on his wrist where his glove had slipped. "That thing you did back there," he said. "The claiming. Where did the words come from?"

"I don't know." Petra flexed her fingers inside her mittens. They still tingled, faintly, as if the Old Tongue had passed through them like current. "The same place the carving comes from. Somewhere under thinking. My mentor used to say the founding line doesn't learn the craft — it *remembers* it." She paused. "He also used to say never to speak of the gift. Look how that turned out."

Vane huffed something that might have been a laugh. Then his face sobered. "I've served the crown fifteen years, Brightwater. I've watched nobles invoke laws, rights, precedents, bloodlines — words as weapons, all of them hollow at the center. What you did wasn't that." He shook his head slowly. "The mountain *answered* you. I felt it in my teeth."

"The law isn't a weapon," Petra said. "That's what Skarn doesn't understand yet, maybe what none of them understand. It's a promise. The oldest one. It only works if you mean it." She looked down the darkening road, north, toward the glacier she could already see gleaming on the horizon like a wall at the end of the world. "I meant it."

They made camp at midnight in the lee of a stone waymarker — an ancient boundary post, half-buried, its surfaces crawling with the same covenant-script that covered Hallow's bones. Petra knelt beside it while the others slept and traced the lines with numb fingertips, reading by touch the way she read all bones. Most of it was record-keeping: names, tolls, oaths sworn and kept. But near the base, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather and pilgrim hands, was a single line repeated over and over, in a script older than the rest:

*The road belongs to the one who pays for it.*

She sat back on her heels and stared at it for a long time.

*Pays for it.* Not *wins* it. Not *claims* it. Pays.

Under the glacier, something vast and patient waited to name its price. Skarn thought she would flinch from it. Half of her own flesh flinched from it already, in the small hours, in dreams of cold and closing doors.

But she had told the truth on the road, and the mountain had believed her, and that belief was hers now — the first thing in her life she had not been given, or sold, or stolen. She had claimed it with her own voice, and the world had listened.

Petra pulled her blanket around her shoulders and looked back down the trail, to where two gold eyes floated in the darkness between the trees, watching, always watching.

"Keep following, Skarn," she murmured to the night. "You'll want to see this."

Far off, beyond the ridge, beneath a hundred fathoms of ice, the Wolf Gate dreamed — and for the first time in a generation, something at the heart of that dream stirred, and turned over, and began, very slowly, to wake.
