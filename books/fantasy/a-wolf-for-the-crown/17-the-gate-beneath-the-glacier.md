## 17. The Gate Beneath the Glacier

The glacier spoke before they saw it.

Petra heard it through her boots first — a low groan rolling up through stone and ice, the sound of something vast shifting in its sleep. Then through the air, a crack like a snapped bone, echoing off the mountainsides, and the snow ahead of them shivered loose in a white curtain.

"It's moving," Maren said. She stood at the head of the column, breath steaming, one hand raised as though she could feel the glacier's mood the way she felt storms. "Not sliding. *Breathing.*"

"That's the gate," Hallow said.

He had changed in the last days of the northward march. The wolf-kindred who had woken for her in the pass — gaunt, sardonic, half-ghost — had filled out as they climbed into the old country, his pelt thickening, his eyes brightening from ember to flame. The covenant-script on the skeleton she carried in her pack, the bones of his first death, had begun to warm against her back. He was coming home, and home was waking to meet him.

"Two miles," he said. "Under the ice. We'll go down through the Weeping Stair."

"The Weeping Stair," Skarn repeated from the rear, where he had walked for six days, bound by the old law of passage, his yellow eyes never leaving Petra's spine. His voice was a rasp of contempt. "You lead her to the door of the world and she doesn't even know what it weeps for."

"I know what it weeps for," Petra said, without turning. "It weeps for what's coming. I've heard it in the bones for three hundred miles."

She had, too. Every night by the fire, when she drew the dead close — the scout's bones, the crypt-kings' whispers she still carried folded in memory, Maren's fragmentary lore — the same sound threaded beneath all their voices. Not words. A *strain*, like a rope bearing too much weight, like a hinge that had not been oiled in a thousand years.

The covenant was not failing, she understood now. It was *holding*. Barely. With its last strength, the way a drowning man holds a plank.

They climbed.

The Weeping Stair was a crevasse in the glacier's flank, its walls streaked with meltwater that froze as it fell, so that the passage glittered with a thousand hanging blades. Petra walked with her hand on the ice and felt the dead of the glacier — not people, but older things, the slow memories of snow that had fallen before kingdoms, compressed into blue silence. They did not whisper. They *hymned*, low and vast, and the hymn was about a door.

Twice the passage narrowed so far that they went through sideways, packs scraping, breath held. Vane's ghost walked beside her — not Vane himself, Vane was a month dead under the pass at Skarn's Hour, but the shape of him she still carried, the memory of his hand unlocking her cell, his voice saying *I was a coward my whole life; don't you be one in the hour that matters.* She had stopped talking to him out loud weeks ago. She had not stopped listening.

At the bottom of the stair, the ice opened.

* * *

Petra had imagined a door. What she found was a cathedral.

The cavern beneath the glacier was vast beyond sense, its ceiling lost in blue-black dark where the ice thickened into something like sky. And set into the far wall — no, *growing* from it, the way a tree grows from the earth, root and trunk indivisible — stood the Wolf Gate.

It was a door the height of a hill. Bone and stone interlocked, rib through granite through rib, an architecture of vertebrae and arches of living rock, and every joint of it was carved with script so old that the letters had worn into the material itself, become texture rather than writing. Half of it gleamed white and warm in the light of their torches, alive, humming faintly like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

The other half was grey. Dead. Crumbled at the edges like old cheese, its script eaten away, its stone pitted and hollow. Where the living half met the dead half, a seam ran down the gate's face like a scar, and through the seam the wind of the other realm came sighing — cold, but a different cold, an old cold, a cold that smelled of pine and blood and distant wolvesong.

"That's the border," Hallow said quietly. He had stopped at the cavern's edge, and his voice had gone strange — reverent and grieving at once. "Living side, dead side. When the covenant was whole, there was no seam. The door breathed both ways."

Petra walked toward it.

She could not help herself. Her feet moved the way they had moved toward the ford the day the hunters came, the way they had moved into the crypt beneath Corvane — drawn by the dead, and the gate was *full* of dead. Not the recent dead. The founders. The first carvers and the first wolves who had stood in this cavern a thousand years ago and given their lives freely, thread by thread, to weave two worlds together.

She could hear them. All of them. A thousand voices braided into the gate's living half, singing the covenant the way a heart sings blood — without thought, without pause, because to stop was to die.

And in the dead half, silence. A thousand empty places where voices used to be.

She laid her palm against the living bone.

The gate *noticed* her.

It was not like touching a carved bone in Hollowmere, where a dead woman's last words came thin and far away. This was a hand closing around hers from inside a mountain. The founders' song surged up her arm, and within it she felt the gate's attention settle on her — old, vast, and *hungry* in a way that had nothing to do with malice. The way winter is hungry. The way a wound is hungry.

*Carver,* the gate said, in a voice that was a thousand voices. *Daughter of the founding line. You are late.*

"I know," Petra whispered. "I'm sorry. I came as fast as I could."

*The seam widens. Midwinter is four nights gone from perfect. After that, the door sleeps for another age, and the age will be ice.*

"Then tell me what to do." Her voice cracked. "I've crossed a kingdom for this. I've — I've given up —" She thought of Vane, of Maren's frostbitten fingers, of the children she'd freed from cages who now huddled near the cavern mouth with Skarn's kin watching them like hawks. "Tell me the reforge. Tell me the price."

The gate was silent for a long moment. The wind through the seam sighed.

*You know the price,* it said. *You have always known. It is written in the first law your line carved, and your line carved it in their own bones so no one could forget: a covenant is a bridge, and every bridge is built from what it spans. A life freely given, from each side. Human and kindred. Given, not taken. Taken blood seals nothing — it only feeds the seam.*

Petra's hand slid from the bone.

She turned. The others had gathered at the cavern's edge — Maren with her storm-grey eyes wide, the children pressed behind her skirts, Skarn leaning against an ice pillar with an expression that was almost, horribly, *satisfied*.

"You heard," Petra said.

"I heard," Maren said softly. "Petra — there are other ways. There must be. Lore fragments, half-translations—"

"There aren't." Petra was surprised at how steady her voice was. "The dead don't lie to me, Maren. It's the one thing they can't do. A life freely given, from each side. That's the price."

"Then it's mine," Hallow said.

Everyone turned. He stood apart from the others, and the light of the torches moved on his pelt like light on water.

"I have died once already," he said. "My thread is frayed, my first bones are in her pack, and my brother—" a glance at Skarn, cold and level, "—has spent a century telling me the kindred need no bridge to the world of men. If the gate must have a wolf, it will have one who *chose* the bridge. That is the whole point of freely given. Skarn would give nothing freely. He'd give only to shut the door."

"You presume," Skarn said softly, "that you'll be the one asked."

"The gate doesn't ask twice," Hallow said. "It asked a thousand years ago, and the founders answered. It's asking now."

*It is not asking you, kindred,* the gate said, and its voice filled the cavern, and the glacier overhead groaned like a ship in a storm. *It is asking her.*

Every eye came back to Petra.

"The human half," Maren whispered. "The founding line. Petra, no. No. There has to be another of your blood, someone, somewhere—"

"There isn't." Petra heard the words come out of her own mouth and was startled by their calm. "The crypt-kings made sure of that. They hunted my line for three hundred years, Maren. Killed the carvers, burned the records, caged the children. I'm not the last heir because of fate. I'm the last heir because a dead king was afraid of exactly this — that someone like me would come here and *fix* what he broke."

She looked at the gate. At the seam where the living half met the dead, where the wind of two worlds mingled. At the grey crumbled bone that had once sung.

She thought of Hollowmere. Of the valley that forgot, where magic could not follow, where she had sat by the fire carving a dead woman's last words into bone and felt, for the first time in her life, that the silence in her chest was not loneliness but *purpose*. She thought of her mentor shoving her toward the mountain pass, of the burned records, of the children in cages, of Vane dying in the snow so she could stand here. She thought of the dead king's confession in the crypt: *I traded their protection for my crown, and the winter is my legacy.*

*And therefore fixable,* she had thought then.

The price came into focus the way a landscape comes into focus through clearing fog — not all at once, but ridge by ridge, each detail more terrible and more beautiful than the last.

"Freely given," she said to the gate. "What does that mean? Truly. If I climb onto your altar with a knife, is that free?"

*No,* the gate said. *The knife is the old way. The old way is how the seam was born — a giving made under compulsion rots into a taking. You must stand at the hinge and offer your thread as a bridge offers itself: not dying, but* becoming. *You will not pass through the door, carver. You will* be *the door.*

The words settled into the cavern like snowfall.

"You'll live," Maren breathed, and there were tears freezing on her cheeks. "The gate said *become*. You'd live, Petra—"

*She will live,* the gate agreed. *And she will not. She will stand between, half in each world, whole in neither. She will hear the dead of both realms forever, and the living will half-forget her face. She will keep the door through every midwinter until the door no longer needs her — and the door will always need her. That is the price. That is what the founders paid, and why no one remembers their faces either.*

Petra closed her eyes.

Half-heard. Half-remembered. A keeper, not a woman. A bridge, not a girl from a valley who liked the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of her mentor's terrible jokes.

*And you would pay it again,* whispered a voice she carried — Vane's voice, or her own memory of what he would say. *You'd pay it twice, and you know it.*

She opened her eyes.

"Four nights," she said. "You said midwinter is four nights from perfect. Then I have four nights to decide?"

*You have four nights to stop deciding,* the gate said. *You decided in the crypt, child, when you heard a dead king's sin and thought: that can be mended. You decided on the Road of Ash, when you opened the cages. You decided the day you carved your first bone and did not run from the voice inside it. I do not need your courage. I need your* yes. *Spoken here, at the hinge, on midwinter night, with a kindred thread beside yours.*

"And if I say no?" Petra asked. "If I walk away?"

The gate did not answer with words. It answered with the seam — a slow, grinding shudder ran through the dead half of the door, and a crack spiderwebbed across the living half, and from high above, the glacier calved an iceberg the size of a village into the dark sea beyond, and the boom of it rolled through the cavern like the world clearing its throat.

The gate did not need to say *the ice age comes, and everyone you love freezes.*

"Right," Petra said. Her voice was very small in the enormous place. "Four nights."

* * *

They made camp in the cavern's mouth, where the glacier's melt had carved a shelf of bare stone sheltered from the worst of the wind. Maren built a fire with the last dry wood, and the children slept in a heap like puppies, and Skarn's kin melted back into the storm outside — all but Skarn himself, who sat apart, sharpening a knife on a whetstone, the sound carrying in the still air like a small, patient threat.

Petra sat with Hallow at the edge of the shelf, looking back down the way they had come. Far below, the world was already white to the horizon. Somewhere under all that white were the roads, the villages, the valley of Hollowmere, the capital with its new regent and its dissolving hunt. Somewhere under the white was everything the gate had asked her to become a door for.

"You don't have to do this," Hallow said.

"Yes, I do."

"That's not the law." His voice was gentle in a way she had rarely heard from him. "Freely given, Petra. If you go to that hinge because you believe you *must*, the giving rots. The gate told you so itself. It has to be a *choice*. Chosen with open eyes, on the night, in the moment. Not now. Not out of duty. If you stand at that hinge carrying only duty, you'll kill us both and seal nothing."

Petra looked at him. The firelight moved in his eyes, and beneath the wolf-shape she saw the scout who had woken in a pass a lifetime ago and claimed her by blood-right, and who had walked every mile beside her since — arguing, protecting, grieving, *staying*.

"What are you afraid of?" she asked him. "Truly."

He was silent a long moment. When he answered, he looked at the gate, not at her.

"That you'll give your thread the way my brother wants to take yours," he said. "As a weapon. As an ending. The kindred who want war think a sacrifice is a way to close a door. The founders knew it was a way to *open* one." His jaw tightened. "I'm afraid you'll stand at the hinge and think of everything you're losing, and the gate will taste it, and it will be true, and it will not be enough."

"And what should I think of instead?"

"That," Hallow said, "is the question you have four nights to answer."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, the glacier sang its vast slow hymn. Petra pulled the scout's bones from her pack — Hallow's first bones, warm against her palms — and held them, and listened, and beneath the founders' song and the wind and the whetstone's patient rasp, she heard something new.

A thread of the dead-half silence, moving.

Something on the other side of the seam had noticed them. Something old, and hungry, and *waiting* — and it was not the gate.

It was coming up the far side of the door, and it was singing Skarn's name.
