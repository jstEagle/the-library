## 18. Skarn's Hour

I smelled the treachery before the moon cleared the peaks.

It came on the wind out of the north, thin and bitter beneath the pine: old blood, old hate, and the particular musk of my brother's rage, which I had known since we were cubs wrestling in the den-moss beneath the Mother Tree. Skarn had learned to mask his scent from the pack-hunt, but he had never learned to mask it from me. Blood remembers blood. That is the first law of the kindred, older than the covenant, older than the Gate — and it is also the cruelest.

"Hallow." Petra's voice, small and human in the dark of the shrine-cave, where she knelt among the scattered bones of the last gate-wardens. She had been reading the script on them for hours, her fingers black with ash-ink, her lips moving over words no living throat had spoken in three hundred years. "You've gone rigid. What is it?"

"Skarn," I said. "He's found us. He's brought the Redmuzzle band — twelve, perhaps fourteen. They're circling above the pass."

Maren swore in her storm-tongue, and somewhere above us the clouds answered with a low grumble of thunder, though the sky was too cold now for rain. The children huddled together by the fire — the three Petra had freed from the cages on the Road of Ash, and two more we'd gathered since, hollow-eyed little survivors who flinched at every sound. Tam, the eldest, put his arm around the smallest girl without being asked. He had learned that in the weeks since the cages. Children learn quickly which way the world is breaking.

Vane rose from the cave mouth where he had been sitting watch. He had not slept in two days. None of us had, truly, but it showed on him worst — the hollows under his eyes, the way his hand kept drifting to the sword hilt he no longer trusted himself to draw. "How long?" he asked.

"Until moonrise. He'll want the light. Skarn believes the moon is a witness, and he wants witnesses for what he intends." I turned to Petra. "He intends to kill you, carver. He intends to take your blood and seal the Gate shut forever, and let the human realms freeze while the kindred retreat into the deep wilds and endure as we endured before the covenant. He has perhaps a third of the western packs behind him. More wavering."

"And you?" Petra asked quietly. "What do you intend?"

What did I intend. I looked at her — this small, stubborn, bone-carved woman who had crossed a dying world because she could not bear to watch children in cages, who had offered her own life-thread to a door of stone because the dead told her it was the only way. My brother called her an abomination. The old law called her the last heir of the founding line. My heart, which I had kept shut for a hundred years, called her by a name I had not yet dared to speak aloud.

"I intend," I said, "to stand between."

* * *

We made our stand at the throat of the pass, where the mountain narrowed to a corridor of ice-slick stone barely wide enough for three wolves abreast. It was the only approach to the shrine-cave, and Skarn knew it as well as I did — he had hunted this range with me when we were young, before the Gate began to die, before the winter came down on our realm like a lid closing on a coffin.

The moon rose. Of course it rose. Skarn had always had a talent for making the world arrange itself around his dramas.

They came out of the treeline in a crescent formation, low and silent, and even now, even knowing what they intended, some old part of me lifted at the sight of them — the kindred in their war-shapes, silver-eyed, frost steaming from their flanks. Beautiful. We were always beautiful when we did terrible things. Perhaps that is why we do them.

Skarn came last, as befitted a war-leader, and he had taken his greatest shape for the occasion: the dire-form, half again my size, his coat the color of old ash, his eyes burning the deep amber that only the oldest blood carried. He stopped ten paces from me, out of lunge-reach, and the Redmuzzles fanned out behind him along the rocks.

"Brother," he said. His voice in the great shape was like a landslide heard from far away. "Step aside."

"You know I won't."

"She is *human*, Hallow. She is the reason the Gate is dying — her ancestors built the lock, and her king's ancestors broke it, and now the whole rotten covenant comes down on *our* heads while their realm burns and starves and does nothing. You've seen the den-hollows. You've counted the dead cubs. Don't tell me you haven't." His voice cracked on the last word, and I saw, beneath the rage, the thing that had made him what he was: he had carried more of our dead than any wolf alive. He had dug the graves. He had named the names. I had run south and found a human to love, and he had stayed north and buried everyone.

"I've counted them," I said. "I count them every time I close my eyes. And I have seen what she carries in her bones, brother. She can mend it. Not avenge it. *Mend it.* There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

"She can mend nothing." Spittle flew from his jaws. "The Gate takes a life from each side — her own dead-voices told her so, and she's too much a coward to pay it. So she'll stand at the door and chant her little carver-songs and the winter will keep falling and our young will keep dying, and in a hundred years there will be no kindred left to remember there was ever a covenant at all. Blood closes the Gate forever, Hallow. Blood ends it *now*, and we endure the way we always endured before we knelt to soft-skinned kings. Alone. Free."

"And frozen," I said. "And dwindling. And hating. You call that freedom, Skarn. I call it a slower grave with a better view."

He lunged.

* * *

We met in the center of the pass with a crack that shook ice from the walls, and the Redmuzzles surged forward, and then everything was noise and blood and breath.

I will not pretend I fought well at first. Skarn is stronger than I am — he always was, even as cubs — and grief had made him faster, the way it does, the way it did to me for a hundred years until Petra put her hand on my skull and told me the dead were not a weight but a *voice*. He drove me back along the pass, jaws snapping at my throat, and I gave ground, and gave, and the shrine-cave mouth came nearer and nearer behind me, and I understood with cold clarity that he had planned this too. He would break me at the door of the very hope I'd chosen.

Then a wall of fire and thunder came down the pass.

Maren. She had climbed the rocks above with the last of her strength, and she called the storm down into the corridor — not to kill, she hadn't that in her, but to blind and deafen, a howling column of snow and lightning that scattered the Redmuzzles and tore the crescent formation apart. Through the white chaos I heard a human voice shouting, cracking, *absurdly* brave.

"NOT THIS DOOR. NOT TONIGHT."

Vane.

He had taken the narrowest point of the pass — the place where three wolves abreast became one — with a sword in one hand and a hunting knife in the other, and he planted himself there the way a man plants himself when he has finally, after a lifetime of stepping aside, decided where he stands. The Redmuzzles came at him in ones and twos, hampered by the storm and the narrow ground, and he met them. He was not a great swordsman; he had spent his life in corridors and courtrooms, not battlefields. But he was desperate, and desperate men in narrow places are terrible mathematics.

I tried to reach him. Skarn's shoulder slammed me into the ice wall hard enough to crack a rib, and we went down together, rolling, his teeth closing on the meat of my shoulder while I raked his belly with my hind claws. We were beyond words now, back in the old language of blood, the one we had spoken since the den. He was trying to kill me. I was trying not to kill him, and that difference was killing me.

Over his shoulder, through the storm, I saw Vane fall.

I saw it the way you see things in the worst hour of your life, in fragments that will not stop replaying: a Redmuzzle's jaws closing on his sword-arm, the blade spinning away ringing off the stone. Vane going down to one knee. And Vane — Serel Vane, who had hunted gifted children for the crown, who had carried a list with Petra's name on it, who had spent thirty years being exactly what his masters needed him to be — Vane driving his hunting knife up into the wolf's chest with his remaining hand, and then turning, deliberately, his back to the second wolf coming at him, so that his body filled the gap in the pass and no one could pass him.

"No," I heard him say. Not a scream. Almost conversational. "No, I don't think so."

They took him down, and he held the gap anyway. Dead men cannot hold gaps, but he held it, because the wolves had to climb his body and the storm was in their eyes and Maren's lightning split the rock above and buried two more of them in a fall of stone, and by the time they cleared the tangle of him the moment had passed, the crescent was broken, and Petra's voice was rising from the shrine-cave in words of covenant-script that made the very ice hum.

Skarn heard it too. He went still beneath me, my teeth at his throat, his at mine, and for one long moment the whole pass hung on that balance — the Redmuzzles milling in the storm, the dead lying still, the script-light pulsing gold from the cave mouth like a heartbeat.

"Kill me, then," Skarn said, low, only for me. "Take the pack. Take the law. Take everything. It's what you want, isn't it? To be right. To be the one who *mended* things."

And I will be honest, because the dead deserve honesty and so does the reader: I wanted it. In that moonlit moment, with my brother's blood on my teeth and Vane's body cooling ten paces away, I wanted to close my jaws and end him and be done with the whole long war of us. The cycle offered itself to me, smooth and easy as a worn path: he killed, so I kill, so his followers kill, so on, forever, the old arithmetic that had dug every den-grave in the north.

Petra's voice rose to its highest note, and the ice of the pass glowed, and somewhere in the glow I heard Vane's voice too — memory, or the wind, or the man himself already halfway to whatever waits on the other side: *Someone has to stop being what the war needs.*

I opened my jaws and stepped back.

"Yield," I said, in the formal tongue, the law-tongue, the tongue of moots and judgments. "Yield, brother, and live under the new covenant, or die here and be the last wolf who ever fought for the old one. Those are your choices. I will not give you a third."

The Redmuzzles had gone still. Wolves understand law even when they hate it; it is stitched into us deeper than rage. Skarn lay in the snow, bleeding from a dozen wounds, my mercy lying on him like a weight heavier than my jaws had ever been. I watched him understand, slowly, what I was doing to him — that I was not sparing him out of softness but out of *judgment*, that his death would have been a mercy and his life would now be a sentence he would have to serve in a world remade without his permission.

"You'll regret this," he whispered.

"Almost certainly," I said. "But I will regret it as a free wolf, and you will live as one. That is more than either of us gave anyone else today." I looked at the Redmuzzles, at their lowered heads and uncertain eyes. "The moot will judge us all when the Gate stands open. Until then — sheathe your teeth."

And because they were wolves, and because the storm was dying and the gold light was growing, and because somewhere in their blood the old covenant stirred at the sound of carver-script and remembered what it had been made for — they sheathed them.

* * *

Vane was still breathing when we reached him.

Not for long. The wounds were past any craft I knew and past any craft Petra knew, and she knew the ones that mattered. We dragged him inside the shrine-cave, out of the wind, and Maren held snow to his brow and the children — the children, all five of them, who had no reason in the world to love this man — knelt around him, because children know a shield when they have been standing behind one.

Petra took his hand. "You could have run," she said. "You've run your whole life. Why now?"

Vane smiled. It was a terrible, beautiful smile, the smile of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it was there. "Because you looked at me in the Bone Garden," he said, "and you didn't look away. No one's ever... I spent thirty years making sure no one would look at me at all." His breath rattled. "Tell the moot. Tell them Vane of Corvane died facing north. Facing the right way, at the end. That's all. That's all anyone gets."

"More than most," Petra said, and her voice broke, and I put my head against her shoulder because it was the only thing I knew how to give.

He died as the moon reached its height, with the covenant-script glowing on the walls around him and five children and a storm-speaker and a wolf keeping watch, which is more than kings get, and better.

Petra closed his eyes with two fingers, gently, the way she closed the eyes of bones before she carved them. Then she stood, and wiped her face, and turned to the deep of the cave where the Gate's first threshold waited, and I saw her gather herself the way a wave gathers before it commits to the shore.

"It's midwinter tomorrow," she said. "And the Gate is taking a life from each side." She looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us said the other thing we were both thinking — that Vane had paid one side's price already, freely given, at the throat of the pass. "Come," she said. "We have one night to learn how a life is given."

Above us, through the cave mouth, the moon slid behind cloud, and the winter held its breath — and far to the north, beneath a hundred fathoms of glacier, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to wake.
