## 19. Midwinter Night

The glacier sang.

Petra had heard it all the way up the last mile of ice — a low, resonant hum that rose through her boots and settled in her teeth, like a struck bell too vast to finish ringing. Now, standing at the mouth of the Wolf Gate, she understood why. The gate was not silent. It was dying, and dying things made noise when they could no longer help it.

It filled the cavern beneath the glacier like the ribs of some god-sized beast: two great doors of interlocking bone and stone, each slab taller than the watchtowers of Corvane, carved so long ago that the script covering them had worn to shallow ghosts of itself. The bone had gone gray and brittle as old candle wax. Through the seam between the doors, a thread of pale light bled out into the dark, thin as a wire, shivering with every pulse of the hum. Cold poured through that seam. Not the ordinary cold of winter — this was older, patient, the cold of a realm slowly starving behind a door its keepers could no longer open or close.

Maren stood at Petra's shoulder, breath steaming, hands jammed into her armpits against the chill. Behind them, huddled in furs among the ice-slicked stones, the children waited with Skarn's wolves watching them from the shadows with yellow, unblinking eyes. Even the kin were quiet. Even Skarn.

*He'll come at moonrise,* Hallow had said. *But he'll wait until after. He wants to see it fail.*

Petra touched the seam of light with one gloved finger. It was warm. That was the cruelest thing about it — everything here was death and frost except this one thin line, which burned like a held breath.

"It's still alive," she said.

"Half-alive." Maren's voice was rough. She had not slept since Vane died; none of them had, really, but grief sat differently on Maren — it made her loud where others went quiet, and tonight she was very quiet indeed. "The lore says the covenant isn't a wall, Petra. It's a heartbeat. Two hearts, actually. One on each side of the door. When they stopped beating together—" She made a small, terrible gesture, fingers closing. "The door started forgetting how to be a door."

"Then we remind it."

"You say that like it's simple."

Petra almost laughed. Almost. "I carve bones for a living, Maren. Nothing is simple. You just keep cutting anyway."

She unwound her scarf, then her gloves, then the heavy coat Maren had insisted on, standing in the blue-black cold in only her shirt and the carver's satchel slung across her chest. Inside the satchel: her knives. Her father's knife. A curl of shavings from the wolf skeleton in the pass, the first covenant-script she'd ever read. And the fragment of royal bone she'd taken from the crypt beneath Corvane — the dead king's knuckle, which had confessed his betrayal to her in a voice like rotting silk, and which she had carried north ever since, half evidence, half penance.

The dead king's voice had been the last piece. *I traded their protection for my crown's weight in gold and years,* he'd said. *I gave them nothing back. A bargain needs two sides, girl. Always did.* He had sounded, in the end, almost sorry. The dead usually were. It was the living who doubled down.

Hallow came to her then, out of the dark at the edge of the torchlight, and the wolves parted for him without looking at him, the way water parts around a stone it has grown used to.

He wore his human shape tonight. He almost never did anymore — said it felt like wearing a coat tailored for someone else — but he stood before her now as a tall man with frost-gray eyes and a scar bisecting his jaw, and Petra thought, not for the first time, that the shape suited him less than the wolf did. In the wolf there was no pretense. Here, in the man, she could see him deciding things. Weighing. Preparing to argue.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to tell me there's another way."

"There is always another way," Hallow said quietly. "That's what I told my pack when the young began dying. There is another way, there is another way. And then there wasn't." His jaw worked. "Skarn never forgave me for being wrong slowly."

"Hallow—"

"The lore is clear, Petra. I've read it a hundred times since the pass. A life freely given, from each side. Freely given means freely given. Not coerced, not tricked, not offered by someone standing behind you with a knife. If you give yourself to that door, you don't come back. You become the hinge. Half-heard. Half-remembered. A voice in the woodwork of the world." His voice cracked, just barely, on the last word. "I have watched you refuse to disappear since the day I woke bound to you. I will not stand here and hand you to a door."

"And I watched three hundred gifted children in cages while you told me to run," Petra said. "We've both been wrong slowly, Hallow. This is us stopping."

She reached into the satchel and drew out the dead king's knuckle. It was small in her palm, yellowed, absurdly ordinary — a piece of a man who had broken the world for power and died comfortable. She set it on the ice before the doors.

"This is what one side looks like when it takes and takes and gives nothing," she said. "I'm done being on that side. Both of me. The carver and the woman."

Maren made a sound — half sob, half laugh. "You absolute mule. You know I can't talk you out of it."

"No," Petra agreed. "You never could."

"So I'll say the words with you instead. All of them. Every fragment I ever pried out of a storm or a drunk old lore-keeper. And if the door takes you, I'm going to spend the rest of my life making sure they remember your name in every village that ever burned." Maren grabbed her by the shoulders, hard enough to bruise. "Promise me something. Promise me you'll fight it. Whatever it feels like in there — promise me you won't just let go because letting go is easy."

Petra looked past her, at the children. Little Wren, who had not spoken since the cage, was watching her with wide, steady eyes. Beside her, Tam had positioned himself in front of the younger ones, arms crossed, the way he'd seen soldiers do. They had followed her across half a kingdom because she was the first adult who hadn't lied to them.

"I promise," Petra said, and found she meant it. Surrender wasn't the same as giving up. She was beginning to understand the difference, and the difference was everything.

* * *

They prepared the way the old lore described, as best as fragments allowed.

Maren chalked the circle from memory — a double ring, the inner one inscribed with the founding script Maren had copied from the pass skeleton, the outer one left deliberately blank. "For whatever comes," she said. "Doors need room to swing."

Hallow shed his human shape and took his true one, huge and silver-gray, frost glittering along his spine. At the circle's edge he paused, and looked back at her with eyes that held ten thousand nights of running beside her, and said, in the bond-voice that needed no air:

*Whatever happens on the other side of this, Brightwater — I was glad. Every mile. Even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones.*

"Even the Road of Ash?" Petra asked aloud, and her voice broke on it.

*Especially that. Because that was the night you became someone worth following.*

Then he stepped into the circle, and lay down before the doors like a scout taking his post, and the hum of the glacier deepened to meet him.

Petra knelt opposite him, the seam of light between them, thin and burning. She drew her father's knife. The blade had carved a thousand whispers free of the dead; it had never once been turned on living flesh, and her hand shook as she pressed it to the meat of her palm.

"Don't hesitate," Maren said softly, from outside the ring. "A gift given halfway is no gift at all."

"I know." Petra breathed out, slow, the way she did before the first cut of a difficult carving. "I know."

She cut.

The pain was bright and clean and shockingly small — a red line across her palm, blood welling black in the torchlight. She turned her hand over and let it fall, drop by drop, onto the ice inside the circle. Each drop struck with a sound far larger than it should have made: a bell-note, a door-knock, a name being called.

"A life freely given," Petra said. Her voice rang off the ice. "From the side of humankind. From the line of the founders. From Petra Brightwater, daughter of Hollowmere, carver of bones, keeper of voices. I give it whole. I give it gladly. I ask nothing back but the door."

The blood finished falling. The silence afterward was enormous.

Then the glacier answered.

Light flooded the seam between the doors — not the thin dying wire anymore but a flood, gold-white, blinding, and the hum rose into an actual chord, harmonics stacking on harmonics, and Petra felt the circle take hold of her offering and *pull*, gentle and irresistible, like a river accepting rain. Something left her. She felt it go — not blood, not breath, something deeper, the thread at the center of her that was most essentially *Petra*, unraveling out of her chest and streaming toward the doors—

—and meeting, halfway, another thread.

Silver-gray, moonlit, unmistakable. Hallow's life-thread, given from the other side, freely, gladly, winding around hers the way two rivers wind before they become one river. Where they touched, the chord changed key. The doors groaned — a sound like continents settling — and began, at last, after centuries of forgetting, to remember how to open.

Not all the way. A crack. A hand's width of gold light spilling into the dark, and through it, faintly, Petra saw snow that fell upward, and forests the color of summer, and shapes moving — wolves, hundreds of wolves, gaunt and hopeful, pressing toward the light —

and the threads pulled taut, and did not carry her through the door.

They carried her *into* it.

The pain, when it came, was not pain. It was subtraction. Piece by piece, the door took the parts of her that belonged only to herself — her privacy, her separateness, the walls between her mind and the world — and replaced them with connection. She felt the wolf-kindred realm breathing on the far side, vast and starving and suddenly, impossibly, fed. She felt the human world's ten thousand hearthfires, its griefs and grudges, its stubborn little lights. She felt both at once, permanently, and understood that this was the price and the gift in a single motion: she would never again be only in one place. She would never again be wholly anywhere.

Somewhere outside the circle, distantly, Maren was screaming her name. Somewhere closer, in the bond, Hallow was *with* her — his thread braided through hers, his presence steadying hers the way a scout steadies a rope bridge — and through him she felt his own subtraction happening, mirrored, his wildness woven into the world's fabric rather than his own legs alone.

And then, at the center of it, the door spoke.

Not in words. In recognition. It knew her — knew the founder's blood in her, knew the king's betrayal, knew every mile of ash and ice that had brought her here — and it accepted her the way stone accepts a keystone: completely, structurally, forever.

The light peaked. Held. Began, gently, to fade.

When Petra opened her eyes, she was still kneeling in the circle. The doors stood closed again — but changed. The gray brittleness was gone from the bone; it gleamed ivory-pale, warm to the touch, and along the seam ran a new line of light, steady and strong, beating slow as a sleeping heart. The script on the doors had renewed itself, sharp-edged, legible. She could read it without touching it now. She could read it from across the cavern. She suspected she could read it from Hollowmere.

*You are the hinge,* the door said, in a voice that was also her voice, and also Hallow's, and also the glacier's. *Two realms, one keeper. Half-heard, half-remembered. This is the bargain, and it is kept.*

Beside her, Hallow rose to his feet — the wolf, shaking frost from his fur, and even as she watched, the change rippled through him and he stood as the man again, though his eyes stayed the wolf's eyes, gold-ringed and ancient. He looked down at his own hands, flexing them, and laughed — a startled, wondering sound.

*I can feel both sides,* he said. *All of it. The herds coming back. The den-sites under the southern ice, thawing.* His voice caught. *The young. Petra. The young are going to live.*

"We did it." It came out of her small and stunned. "We actually—"

The rest of the sentence died, because the moonrise chose that moment to arrive.

Moonlight poured down the ice-shaft above the cavern, silver and merciless, and in it, at the mouth of the cavern, stood Skarn.

He was not alone. A dozen kin flanked him, lean and hard-eyed, and their gazes went from the renewed doors, to the bright seam, to Petra kneeling in the chalk circle with blood still wet on her hand — and Petra saw the exact moment Skarn understood. Saw the war he had wanted die in his brother's renewed body before his eyes. Saw the future he had staked everything on — a frozen humanity, a sealed gate, wolf-kindred ascendant in a world of ice — close forever, quietly, in the space of a single heartbeat.

His lips peeled back from his teeth.

"You gave it *away*," he snarled, and the sound echoed off the renewed doors like a challenge thrown down in front of God. "You gave our birthright to the *meat-things*. To the species that starved us. Brother — you didn't save the kindred. You leashed us to them. Forever."

Hallow stepped forward, placing himself between his brother and Petra, and his voice, when it came, was the voice of the new covenant itself — calm, vast, immovable.

"No, Skarn," he said. "I saved it. Come and see."

Behind them, unnoticed in the chaos, the dead king's knuckle lay on the ice where Petra had placed it — and it had begun, very softly, to whisper.
