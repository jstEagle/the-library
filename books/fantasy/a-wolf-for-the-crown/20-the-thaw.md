## 20. The Thaw

The first sound of the thaw was water.

Petra heard it before she saw it — a thin, insistent dripping from the eaves of the gate-shrine where she had slept, or half-slept, in the days after midwinter. She lay still beneath Hallow's cloak and listened to it the way other people listened to music. Drip. Drip. A pause. Then another, faster, as if the ice itself had remembered how to move.

She rose and pushed open the shrine door, and the light that came through nearly put her on her knees.

It was not the hard white glare of winter. It was gold — low, slanting, morning-gold — pouring across the glacier's shoulder and down into the valley below, and everywhere it touched, the world was weeping. Snow slid from branches in soft collapses. The river at the valley's mouth, frozen to its bed for three months, groaned like something waking from a long illness, and then cracked, and then *ran*.

Petra stood in the doorway of the Wolf Gate's shrine with her hands pressed flat against the doorframe and felt the covenant hum through the stone, through her bones, through the place where her heartbeat used to be the only thing she could hear. Now there were two rhythms in her chest, or one rhythm with two voices, and the second voice was the gate itself, steady as a drumbeat under the world.

*You did this,* it seemed to say. Or perhaps she only wanted it to say that. The dead were often easier to understand than the living, and she was no longer entirely either.

Behind her, inside the shrine, Maren stirred among the blankets. "Is it—"

"It's melting," Petra said. Her voice came out rough. She hadn't spoken aloud in two days; there had been no need, and speaking cost her something now, a small toll paid out of a purse that never quite refilled. "Maren. Listen."

Maren came to stand beside her, storm-grey eyes wide, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all. They just watched the water come alive down the mountainside, threading silver through the white, gathering, quickening, becoming a stream, becoming a river, becoming the first honest sound either of them had heard since autumn.

Then Maren laughed — a cracked, disbelieving laugh — and caught Petra around the shoulders, and they held each other up in the doorway like two drunks, shaking, while somewhere below them the children whooped and shouted and went sliding down the melt-slick slope on their backsides, all dignity abandoned, all of them alive.

All of them alive. Not all of them whole.

Petra's laughter died quietly, the way it did now, without drama. She thought of Vane. She would think of Vane every morning for the rest of her life, she suspected, in exactly this way: suddenly, between one breath and the next, the way grief arrives when it has been waiting politely at the edge of joy for its turn.

He had died holding the pass so that she could work. He had died with his sword out and his back against the ice and his lifetime of compromises finally, at the last, resolved into a single clean act. He had died without ever hearing her say — what? That she understood him? That she forgave him? That forgiveness wasn't hers to give, not really; it belonged to the villages on the Road of Ash, to the children in the cages, to everyone whose names he'd carried on the crown's lists?

She had carved his name into the glacier's face with her knife, in carver-script, the old script, the script that made bone remember. The ice would keep it until spring. After that, the mountain would hold it, and mountains are patient.

"Dawn's coming," Maren said softly. "The others will want you at the fire."

"I know." Petra did not move yet. Below, the valley was waking — cook-smoke rising straight in the still air, the wolf-kindred moving among the humans with the careful courtesy of people learning a new language together. Skarn's pack kept to the northern ridge, apart, watching. Always watching.

"Petra." Maren hesitated. "There's a rider coming up the road. Human. Crown colors, but — wrong. Half the livery's torn off. He's carrying a banner he keeps having to stop and refold, like he doesn't know which side is supposed to show."

Petra turned. Far below, on the thawing road, a single horseman picked his way north through the mud, and even at this distance she could see the truth of it: a man dressed for one war and riding to surrender to another.

"The regent," she said.

"You can't know that."

"I can know it," Petra said, "because I sent the testimony ahead of me." She touched the hollow at her throat, where the dead king's confession slept in a locket of carved whalebone — his voice, his crime, his confession that he had broken the covenant himself, sold the kindred's protection for power, and let the winter fall on his own kingdom rather than admit it. Three copies she had smuggled south: one to the Lord Marshal, one to the Archivist of Corvane, one to the queen dowager, who had reasons of her own to hate the regent. The dead king's voice, speaking from her hand, in the halls where he had once ruled.

She had wondered, on the long ride north, whether anyone would listen to a dead man. She should have known better. Dead men, in the capital, were the only ones who told the truth.

* * *

They received the regent's envoy in the shrine at noon, because Petra refused to travel down to meet him. If the crown wanted to treat with the keeper of the Wolf Gate, the crown could climb.

He was a tired man, the envoy — a marshal of the second rank with frostbite scars on his cheeks and the look of someone who had ridden through a country coming apart. Behind him, visible through the shrine door, his escort waited at a respectful distance, and behind *them*, filling the road below, came the thing Petra had not expected: refugees. Hundreds of them, walking north out of habit, following the rumor that the winter had broken at its source, carrying their children and their pots and their dead wrapped in sledge-cloths, coming to see the place where the world had turned over.

"They call it the Thaw Road already," the envoy said. He knelt, which surprised her, and laid a sealed scroll at her feet. "The regent sends terms. What remains of the regent sends terms." His mouth twisted. "Madam Keeper, I will be plain with you, because the plainness will reach your ears eventually regardless. The testimony you sent south broke the court in half. The dead king's voice, played before the full Ivory Court — the Archivist has a device, some relic, it carries a voice in a shell of glass — half the court heard their own history confessed by the man who ruined them. The regent's faction called it witchcraft. The other half called it justice. By evening they were calling each other traitors, and by the next morning the City Watch had chosen sides, and after that it stopped being politics."

"And the regent?" Maren asked from the shadows.

"In custody. Her own guard handed her over when the granary ledgers came out — the ones showing she'd hoarded grain through the worst of the winter while the hunt burned the farms that grew it. There will be a trial. There may be worse than a trial." The envoy looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed with exhaustion. "The hunt is dissolved. Every writ burned, every bounty struck. The gifted prisoners are being released — those that lived. There are lists, madam Keeper. Long lists of the ones who didn't." He bowed his head. "I'm sorry. I know sorry buys nothing. But the new council asked me to carry it anyway, and I'll carry it honestly: the kingdom is sorry, and the kingdom is ashamed, and the kingdom does not expect forgiveness. It asks only what terms you set."

Petra looked at the scroll at her feet and did not pick it up.

Terms. As if the winter had been a negotiation. As if the cages had been a bargaining position. She thought of the list of names Serel Vane had carried across the ford at Hollowmere, her own name written on it in a clerk's neat hand, and how much of the world had burned because of ink on paper.

But she also thought of the children behind her — the three she'd freed from the cage on the Road of Ash, and the dozen more gathered to them since, and Maren already teaching them to shape their gifts instead of hiding them, her voice rising over the meltwater in something that was almost a song. And she thought of the wolf-kindred on the northern ridge, starving no longer, their young running fat and quick through a realm that had food in it again for the first time in a generation.

Two peoples, one hinge. That was her now. That was what she had bought with her voice among the living.

"Read me the regent's terms," she said, "and then I'll tell you mine, and mine are the ones that matter."

* * *

Her terms were simple, and the envoy wrote them down with a shaking hand.

First: the gifted would be registered, not hunted — registered by their own school, run by their own teachers, answerable to no lord. Maren would build it in Hollowmere's foothills, where magic could not follow and therefore could not be weaponized, and the crown would fund it and never once set foot inside it uninvited.

Second: the wolf-kindred would have passage rights through the human realms, and humans would have the same through the kindred's lands, and both passages would pass under the Wolf Gate, where the keeper could see them.

Third: the regent would stand trial before a court that included, among its judges, one voice of the dead — summoned by the keeper, questioned openly, so that no ruler of either realm would ever again believe that the grave kept secrets for the powerful.

And fourth, last, the term she made the envoy write twice because he flinched the first time: every name on every list the hunt had ever kept would be read aloud, publicly, in the capital, on the anniversary of the thaw — and after each name, the reader would say the words *we remember*, and would keep saying them until the list was done, however many days it took.

"Some of those lists have thousands of names," the envoy said quietly.

"Then the reading will take a long time," Petra said. "Grief should be inconvenient. That's how you know it's real."

When he had gone, Maren looked at her sideways. "'One voice of the dead.' You're going to go sit in judgment in the capital."

"I'm going to send my voice," Petra corrected. "I don't leave the gate often anymore. You know that." She smiled, and it cost her, and Maren saw it cost her, and said nothing, which was its own kindness. "Besides. The dead make better judges than the living. They've had time to think."

* * *

Skarn came down from the ridge at dusk.

Petra saw him coming from the shrine door — a big shape against the last red light, moving alone, unhurried, and she felt Hallow wake inside the bond like a banked fire stirred. *He's mine to meet,* Hallow said through her, through them, the way they spoke now, wordless and warm and strange. *But he kneels to you.*

*Or he doesn't,* Petra answered. *That has to be allowed too.*

Skarn stopped ten paces from the shrine door. Up close, in the dying light, he looked older than he had at the trial by tooth and snow — thinner, as if the breaking of the winter had taken something out of him along with his grievance. Perhaps it had. A war is an easy thing to carry; it fits the shoulders. Peace sits heavier, because peace asks questions.

"Keeper," he said. His voice was gravel over deep water.

"Skarn of the northern ridge."

"My brother says you hear the dead." His eyes flicked to the glacier, to the carved name half-lost in new meltwater. "I have dead I need heard."

Petra waited.

"Our mother," Skarn said, and the word cost him visibly, as if he had not said it aloud in years. "She died in the famine winters, before the gate failed — before we knew why it failed. She starved giving her ration to the pups that weren't even hers. And I spent every winter since hating humans for it. Every single one." His jaw worked. "Hallow told me what your dead king confessed. That the famine was a human's doing. A human's *choice*. Do you know what I did with that, Keeper? Nothing. I couldn't use it. Hate needs a whole people to hate, and you took that away from me, and I have been standing on that ridge for six days trying to learn how to live without it."

The wind moved through the shrine door, smelling of wet stone and green things not yet grown.

"So," Skarn said, and lowered himself — slowly, joints cracking, an old warrior's body remembering forms his pride had forgotten — until one knee pressed the thawing ground. "I kneel to the covenant. Not because I love you. Because my brother loves you, and because my mother deserved better than to die for a lie, and because someone has to be the first of us to stop fighting, and it might as well be the one who fought hardest." He lifted his head, and his eyes were bright and furious and free. "But hear me, Keeper. If this peace fails — if your humans cage our young again, or your kings sell us again — I will be the first through the gate, and I will not kneel twice."

"That's fair," Petra said. "If we fail you, you shouldn't."

Something shifted in his face — surprise, maybe, at being met instead of managed. He rose, nodded once to Hallow's presence inside her, and walked back toward the ridge without another word. But at the edge of the firelight he paused, and said, without turning: "My mother's name was Vessa. If you ever hear the dead of our side — put her name on your lists too. *We remember.* Both directions, Keeper. That's the only way it holds."

"Both directions," Petra agreed.

And that night, at the fire, she carved a small round of antler-bone with a wolf's head and the name *Vessa* in covenant-script, and set it beside the shrine's altar next to a chip of wood bearing Vane's name, and the two tokens leaned against each other in the candlelight like travelers sharing a wall.

* * *

Spring came up the valley like a tide.

By the third week the lower slopes were green, actual green, a color Petra had begun to fear was a story. The refugees on the Thaw Road turned around and went home, or stayed and built, and a village sprang up at the glacier's foot that the people named Brightwater without asking her permission, and she found, to her own surprise, that she didn't mind. Let them have the name. Names should belong to the living.

Maren left for Hollowmere's foothills with the children at the end of the month, and the parting was harder than any battle. Maren held her a long time at the gate.

"You'll come down sometimes?" Maren said into her hair. "For the school. For us. Even if they forget your face between visits?"

"I'll always speak," Petra said. "That's the one thing I have left that's fully mine."

"And the rest of you?"

Petra stepped back and looked at her own hands — carver's hands, scarred, steady — and felt the gate humming in her chest, and the wolf-presence curled warm at her center like a second heart, and the vast slow attention of the dead on the far side of everything, waiting for their keeper.

"The rest of me," she said, "belongs to the door."

Maren laughed and cried at once, kissed her forehead, and led the children south, and Petra stood at the threshold of the Wolf Gate and watched them go until the road bent and took them, and then she went inside, where Hallow waited in the bond like sunlight through a window, and the candles burned, and the names leaned together on the altar.

Outside, the meltwater ran silver toward the sea. Inside, the dead murmured their long patience. And far to the south, in a city she could feel through the covenant like a splinter in the world's thumb, a trial was beginning — and on its first day, in a hall packed past breathing, an Archivist raised a shell of glass to the light, and a dead king began to speak.

Petra felt it happen. Felt the words move through the bond like weather.

"Hold fast," she whispered to the gate, to herself, to the year to come. "We're not finished yet."

The gate hummed agreement, and the thaw ran on.
