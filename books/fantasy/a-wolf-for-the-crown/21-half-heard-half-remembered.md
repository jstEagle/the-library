## 21. Half-Heard, Half-Remembered

The road into Hollowmere had not changed, and that was the first cruelty of it.

Petra walked it in the last week of thaw, when the snowmelt ran loud in the ditches and the birches stood dripping like candles. Behind her the world was mud and banners and the slow machinery of a kingdom learning to live without its hunt; ahead of her the valley opened the way it always had, green and folded and deliberately blind to everything beyond its ridgeline. Magic could not follow her here. It never could. That had been Hollowmere's whole gift — a pocket of forgetting stitched into the mountains, a place where the dead kept quiet and the living kept their heads down.

She had thought, walking north from Corvane through a spring that smelled of rot and renewal both, that coming home would feel like setting down a weight she'd carried for two years.

It did not. It felt like carrying the weight *into* the valley, and discovering there was nowhere left to set it down.

The first farmstead sat where it had always sat, low stone walls, a crooked barn, geese screaming at nothing. A woman was in the yard beating a rug against a fence rail — Marta Ellensdottir, who had once traded Petra a basket of plums for a carved whistle that whistled back in her dead husband's voice. Petra knew her instantly. The line of her shoulders, the way she paused between blows to press a hand to her lower back.

Marta looked up as Petra came through the gate. Her eyes passed over her face the way they might pass over a fencepost, or a shadow thrown by the passing cloud, and went back to the rug.

Petra stopped. "Marta."

The rug-beating stopped. Marta turned, frowning slightly, searching — and then her whole face changed, warmed, broke open. "*Petra Brightwater.* Sweet saints, girl, we thought you dead. We thought—" She dropped the rug in the mud and crossed the yard with her arms out, and held her, hard enough to hurt, smelling of soap and wool and lye.

"You didn't know me," Petra said into her shoulder. "Just now. You looked straight through me."

Marta pulled back, and Petra watched the confusion come and go across her face like weather. "I — did I? I'm sorry, love, my eyes aren't what they were." But her eyes were fine; Petra could see they were fine. "You've been away. People change." A pause. A small, honest frown, the kind people wear when they are trying to remember a dream. "Though I'll say you look... well. Rested. Like someone's been minding you."

*Someone has,* Petra almost said. *Something has.*

"Where is everyone?" she asked instead. "The elders. Master Odell."

The warmth drained out of Marta's face by degrees. "Odell died the winter after you ran. His heart. He was old, and then he was older, if you take my meaning — after the riders came and went, after we learned what they'd wanted you for, he just... stopped tending himself." She hesitated. "There's those in the valley who still say we should have fought them for you. And those who say fighting would have burned us all. They argue about it in the tavern like it happened yesterday. Like you're a story." She touched Petra's arm, gently, the way one touches something to confirm it is solid. "But you're here. You're actually here. Come inside, I'll feed you, you're thin as a winter hare—"

"I have to see the elders first," Petra said. "All of them. There are things they need to hear, and things I need to ask."

"They meet at the moot-hall on rest days. Tomorrow." Marta studied her again, and again Petra felt that terrible soft sliding, that flicker behind the eyes — recognition guttering like a lamp in wind — before Marta blinked and found her again. "You'll stay with me tonight. Don't argue. You look like you haven't slept under a roof in a year."

She hadn't, quite. She let herself be fed and wrapped in blankets and fussed over, and it was good, it was almost unbearably good, and all through the evening she catalogued the small failures of it: the way Marta's daughter came in and had to be told twice who Petra was; the way the neighbor boy stared at her across the table with polite blankness until she spoke and his face cleared like sun through cloud; the way that even Marta, holding her hand across the table, sometimes had to glance down at the hand to remember whose it was.

Her voice anchored her. Only her voice.

When she said nothing — when she simply sat by the fire while the household talked around her — she felt herself going out of the room without moving. Not invisible. Worse than invisible. *Unremarked*, the way furniture is unremarked, the way the dead are unremarked once the mourning is done. She was half-heard and half-remembered now, a hinge between worlds, and hinges are noticed only when they creak.

That night she lay in the bed she'd slept in as a child guest of Marta's mother, and listened to the house settle, and reached — carefully, the way you reach toward a hot stove — for the other side of herself.

They were there, of course. They were always there now. The dead of Hollowmere murmured beneath the valley floor like water under ice, patient, unhurried, and for the first time in her life she could hear them clearly *inside* the valley, which should have been impossible, which proved how thoroughly the covenant's remaking had rewritten her. Old Tam the shepherd, dead forty years, wondering idly where his crook had gone. A child's voice, wordless, humming. And beneath them, fainter but constant as a pulse, the far voices of the wolf-kindred, carried down the bond like light carried down a fiber of glass: Hallow, somewhere north, running the high ridges; the Gate breathing its slow bone-deep breath beneath the glacier; the whole vast sleeping architecture of the peace she had bought.

*You're home,* Hallow's voice said, not words exactly, more a warmth along the thread. *Does it fit?*

"No," she said aloud, to the dark ceiling, because saying it aloud made it true and true things hurt less than uncertain ones. "It doesn't fit anymore."

*No,* he agreed, gentle. *But you knew it wouldn't.*

She had known. Knowing and feeling it were different countries, and she had just crossed the border into the second one.

* * *

The moot-hall smelled of smoke and tallow and fifty years of arguments, and it was full past standing room, because word had run through the valley overnight like fire through dry grass: *the Brightwater girl is back, the carver girl, the one the crown came for.* They crowded the benches and the doorways and the windows, farmers and fisherfolk and the tanner's family, faces she had grown up among, and every single one of them had to be reminded who she was — some twice, some three times — until she learned to open every conversation with her own name, spoken plainly, like a key turned in a lock.

"Petra Brightwater," she said, standing at the front, and watched the hall come into focus around her, faces sharpening, attention gathering. "Daughter of this valley. Carver of bones. And keeper of the Wolf Gate, though most of you won't know what that means yet, and by the end of today you will."

Elder Sef, oldest of them now that Odell was gone, sat at the center table with his hands folded. He had been kind to her once, and cautious, and those two things had always warred in him visibly, like weather fronts meeting. "We heard the hunt dissolved," he said. "We heard the regent fell. We heard—" he hesitated, choosing, "—strange stories. Winter breaking all at once, in a night, from the glaciers southward. Rivers rising with the thaw in midwinter. And a name in the stories that sounded like yours."

"The stories are true. Most of them." She took a breath. "I'm going to tell you what actually happened, all of it, because you hid me your whole childhood and paid for it, and you deserve the truth even if the truth costs. The valley's secrecy kept us safe for nine hundred years. It can't anymore. Not because the crown knows where we are — they don't, and the new king has sworn the old writs burned — but because the world outside changed. The covenant is reforged. Magic is waking everywhere, honestly, openly, and children born in valleys like this one are going to keep being born different, and hiding them is how we got the cages and the pyres in the first place."

She let that sit. Somewhere in the crowd a baby fussed and was hushed.

"There's a school," she said. "Being built, right now, four days' walk east of here, in the foothills below the pass I came through. Maren Storm-call runs it — you'd have called her a witch, two years ago; she can speak to thunder, and she taught three frightened children to do less frightening versions of the same. Gifted children from six kingdoms study there now. They learn to carry what they carry without it crushing them. And they learn about the covenant, and the kindred, and the Gate." She looked around the hall, at the faces she loved, faces that kept needing her voice to hold them. "I'm telling you because some of your children are going to be like me. Some already are. And when they are, you'll have a choice the elders of my childhood never gave my parents: send them somewhere they'll be taught, or hide them until the hiding breaks."

An uproar, of course. She had expected it. Fear dressed as prudence, prudence dressed as faith, an old woman weeping quietly in the corner, a young man shouting that no child of his would be taught devil-lore within a hundred miles. Sef let it run itself out, the way elders do, and then raised a hand.

"And you?" he asked. "Will you teach there? At this school?"

And here was the thing she had rehearsed on the road for sixty miles, the speech she'd built plank by plank in the dark of campfires, and found now, standing in front of them, that she could not deliver.

Because the truth was stranger. The truth was that she could go anywhere now — the valley's blindness no longer held her, the covenant's remaking had made her a citizen of both worlds, free to walk out and walk back as she pleased, the first soul in nine hundred years with that freedom. And the truth was that freedom had a shape, and the shape was this: she belonged fully nowhere. In the world of the living she was a story people forgot unless she kept retelling herself. Among the kindred she was honored and half-understood, a two-legs hinge, a curiosity of the Gate. Here in Hollowmere she was loved, and would be forgotten by tomorrow's supper unless she spoke, and speak, and speak.

"I'll come and teach," she said. "In winters, when the Gate needs me least. Midwinters I'll be north — that's not negotiable, that's the office I hold. But yes. I'll teach the carving. Someone has to teach the next generation to listen to the dead properly, and I am, as it turns out, extremely qualified."

A ragged laugh moved through the hall, and with the laugh some of the fear loosened its grip, and Sef almost smiled.

"And where do you *live*, Petra Brightwater?" asked a voice — the tanner's wife, blunt as ever, unimpressed by destiny. "Where does a person sleep, when she's half of somewhere else?"

Petra thought of the Gate chamber, the interlocking ribs of bone and stone, the cold blue light, Hallow's warmth beside her through the long midwinter nights. She thought of Marta's spare bed. She thought of a schoolhouse not yet roofed, in the foothills, where children were learning not to be afraid of themselves.

"Everywhere," she said. "And nowhere. And mostly, I suspect, wherever I'm needed." She smiled, and it cost her, and she let it cost her, because that was the price and she had agreed to it and agreeing was the whole point. "I know that's not an answer. It's the only honest one I have."

* * *

She stayed in the valley through the turning of the year's first month, and it was sweeter and sadder than she had any right to expect.

She relearned her mother's grave — found it weeded, tended, someone had kept it all these years, and she sat on the turf and talked to the stone for an afternoon, and for once the dead woman beneath it talked back easily, warmly, *there you are, little carver, I wondered when you'd get around to visiting*. She carved again in the old workshop, Odell's tools still hanging in their oiled leather rolls, and found that her hands remembered everything and her craft had deepened past anything Odell could have taught: she could now carve a name into bone and the dead would answer it from any distance, could shape a listening-stone that heard both sides of the veil at once. She made three of those and sent them to Maren's school by carrier, wrapped in waxcloth, labeled in her own hand: *For the children. Speak to them kindly. Both kinds of them.*

And every evening, without fail, she performed the small labor of her existence: she introduced herself. To Marta. To the neighbors. To the innkeeper's new dog, who alone seemed immune, and greeted her ecstatically each time as though she'd returned from years away rather than hours — animals remembered her, which was its own strange comfort. *It's Petra,* she'd say, and watch the face clear, and receive the joy of recognition like a gift she had to unwrap anew each day. Some evenings she was too tired, and sat silent in corners, and those nights she learned what she was to the living when she wasn't speaking: a presence, a draft, a chair that seemed warmer than chairs should be. Children, oddly, did best with her. Children kept catching her out of the corner of the eye and waving, delighted, as though she were a game only they could see.

On her last full evening, Marta sat with her by the fire and was quiet a long time, and then said, carefully, the way you handle a thing that might break:

"I forget you. Don't I. Every day I forget you, and every day you tell me your name and I get you back, and I've started writing it on the slate by the door so I don't lose the whole day waiting." Her eyes were wet. "Is that the price? Is that what it cost, up there under the glacier?"

"It's part of it," Petra said.

"And you'd pay it again?"

Petra looked into the fire. Through the bond, faint and constant, she felt Hallow running the northern ridgelines under a fat moon, joyous, tireless, alive in the way only the newly remade are alive. She felt the Gate breathe. She felt the long peace holding, seam by seam, the peace that had a face now and a name and a slate by a door with *PETRA BRIGHTWATER* chalked on it in a kind old woman's handwriting.

"Yes," she said. "Every time. Without hesitating."

Marta nodded slowly, wiped her eyes, and did the bravest thing Petra saw anyone do in that whole bittersweet homecoming: she picked up the slate, and instead of writing the name, she handed the chalk to Petra.

"Then write it yourself," she said. "From now on. So I know it's really you doing the reminding, and not just... habit."

Petra took the chalk. Her hand shook slightly. She wrote her name in big clear letters, and beneath it, after a moment, added: *Friend. Carver. Keeper. Come home whenever you like.*

"That last bit's for me, isn't it," Marta said.

"It's for both of us."

* * *

She left on a morning of high thin cloud, walking east toward the foothills and the sound, already audible on the wind, of hammers raising a schoolhouse roof. Maren had written — a letter full of exclamation marks and storm-metaphors — that enrollment had doubled, that a wolf-kindred envoy arrived monthly to teach the lore of the Gate, that one of the children, a small ferocious girl named Tally, had begun hearing stone the way Petra heard bone, and needed "someone who actually knows what she's doing, preferably immediately."

Four days' walk. She'd have liked company. Instead she had the better thing: the dead of three kingdoms murmuring along the road like travelers on the same track, and the warm thread of the bond running north, and — when she crested the pass on the third evening and looked down at the lamplit skeleton of the school, half-built, stubborn, alive — the sight of a figure waiting at the gate of the worksite. Tall, gray-cloaked, unmistakable even at distance, because no human stood quite like that, with that stillness, that patience, that particular tilt of the head.

Hallow had come south. Hallow, who never left the high country, who had sworn the ridges were in his blood — standing at the edge of the lamplight like a man waiting at a door.

Along the bond, warm as a coal cupped in both hands, came a single feeling, wordless, enormous, and she understood it before she understood why: not *welcome home*, because this wasn't home, not yet.

It was *look what we built.*

Down in the valley of her birth, a slate hung by a door with her name on it. Down in the lamplight below, a future was being framed in timber, and children were learning not to be afraid, and one small ferocious girl was hearing voices in the stones and needed someone, immediately, who understood.

Petra Brightwater shouldered her pack, spoke her own name aloud to the empty air — a habit now, a prayer, a key — and started down the hill toward the light.
