## 22. A Wolf for the Crown

The snow came down over the Wolf Gate like a benediction, slow and silver and endless.

Petra stood at the threshold as she had stood every midwinter night for nine years, her hands bare to the cold, the first star of the longest night catching in the script that ran down her forearms like rivers of pale ink. The script moved when the wind moved. It had learned to breathe. She had learned to let it.

Behind her, the gate rose out of the glacier's heart — that vast interlocking door of bone and stone, half-dead once, half-dead no longer. It glowed now with a light like the memory of firelight, and through its seams she could feel the other realm pressing close, warm as a sleeping animal: the wolf-kindred's world, its forests green even in this season, its rivers running, its young alive and loud and hungry. The covenant held. The covenant sang.

She no longer needed to carve bones to hear the dead. She was the carving now. The valley of Hollowmere had made her a tool for listening; the gate had made her the listening itself.

"You're standing in the cold again," said a voice at her shoulder, rough with affection, "as though you were still a woman who could catch a chill."

Hallow came up beside her, his great grey shape resolving out of the snowfall, frost glittering in the ruff of his neck like a king's collar. Nine years, and he had not aged a day. Neither, she suspected, had she — not truly. The covenant took its tithe of time from both of them and spent it elsewhere, on the gate, on the thaw, on the slow mending of two worlds.

"I like the cold," Petra said. "It reminds me I chose this."

"You chose it on a night much worse than this one."

"Every night was worse than this one, back then." She smiled. Her breath did not fog the air anymore. That had been the first thing to go, and she had mourned it longer than her voice — the small human proof of a body at work in the world. "Do you remember the pass?"

"I remember you arguing with a dead wolf about whether you were allowed to exist." Hallow's laugh was a low rumble she felt in her ribs more than heard. Half-heard, half-remembered — that was what she was to the living now, and sometimes she wondered if she had become the same to him, if the bond had softened him to her the way it had softened the world. But then he would say a thing like *I remember*, and she would know that some part of her had been carved permanent into him, the way she once carved names into bone so the dead would not be lost.

"I wasn't arguing," she said. "I was losing. There's a difference."

"You were winning. You just didn't know the rules yet."

* * *

They came up the glacier road at dusk, as they always did: the carvers.

Nine years ago there had been none. Nine years ago the founding line had been a dead woman's whisper in a burned archive, and the craft of bone-carving had been a crime in three kingdoms and a rumor in the rest. Now there were forty-one of them, and every midwinter they made the pilgrimage north through the thawing world — through villages that had learned to stop fearing their own gifted children, past the school at Hollowmere's edge where Maren taught storm-speech to a classroom of children who no longer cowered at the sound of their own voices.

Petra watched them come, as she always did, from the gate's threshold. They were so young. That was the thing that undid her every year, the thing no amount of eternity seemed to blunt: they were so young, and they carried their knives and their bones and their terrible listening ears like pilgrims carrying relics, and half of them were frightened, and all of them were hopeful, and every one of them had a name she could speak and a face the world would remember because she would remember it for them.

The first up the road, as always, was Wren.

She had been one of the three children in the cage on the Road of Ash — the smallest, the one who had held Petra's hand through the bars and asked, in the matter-of-fact way of children, whether the witch-hunters would hurt her the way they'd hurt her mother. Petra had lied to her then, because the truth was unbearable, and Wren had known she was lying, and had squeezed her hand anyway. Forgiveness before it was asked for. Petra had built nine years of keeping on that squeeze.

Wren was nineteen now. She walked the glacier road like she owned it, which in a sense she did — she had helped carve the new covenant-script into the gate's western door, and her hand was in every line of it. She carried her mentor's old knife. Petra's old knife. The one that had opened a dead woman's femur in a quiet valley where magic could not follow, back when Petra had believed herself small.

"Keeper." Wren bowed, breath fogging, snow in her dark hair. Behind her the other carvers bowed too — the old law, the old courtesy, the words that still made Petra's throat tighten no matter how many midwinters she heard them.

"Get up, all of you," Petra said, as she always did. "You'll catch your deaths, and I've spent enough of my eternity on funerals."

Laughter ran down the line of them, nervous and bright. They always laughed at that. They always looked at her a moment too long afterward, the way people look at a fire — grateful and a little afraid. She had stopped apologizing for it years ago, somewhere around the fifth midwinter, somewhere around the night a carver's apprentice named Tam had asked her, with a child's terrible directness, whether it hurt, being the gate.

*Every day,* she had told him. *And every day it's worth it. Both things are true. Hold them together. That's the whole craft.*

* * *

The rite began at moonrise, as it had on the night the world almost ended.

The carvers arranged themselves in the crescent before the gate, each with a bone in their hands — not trophies, never trophies; bones given freely by the wolf-kindred, shed antler and honored dead, each one carved with a name and a listening. Petra walked the crescent slowly, touching each carver's work, reading it the way her mentor had once taught her to read in a valley that forgot — not the script, but the voice beneath it. A dead scout's joke about a river crossing. A grandmother's lullaby in a language older than kingdoms. A young wolf's last thought of running, running, running, given to the gate so that the running would never stop.

"You've improved," she told Wren, at the crescent's heart. "The scout's voice. Last year you were smoothing out his accent."

"He sounded like a drunk riverboat captain."

"He *was* a drunk riverboat captain. That's the point of the work, Wren. We don't carve what the dead should have said. We carve what they did say. All of it. Even the ugly. Even the funny." She tapped the bone. "Especially the funny. The dead are so serious in the histories. Someone has to remember they were people."

Wren's eyes were wet, and she laughed, and Petra moved on.

At the gate's heart, the script waited for her. It always did — the founding lines, the covenant's spine, renewed each midwinter by the hand of the keeper. She knelt. She bared her arms, and the pale rivers of ink rose to meet her knife, and she began.

This was the part no one else saw. This was the part that was hers alone, as it had been hers alone on the worst night of the world: the moment of giving. Each stroke of the knife took something from her — a memory, a sensation, a small bright coin from the purse of her humanity. The taste of her mentor's bread. The sound of rain on the Hollowmere roof. Her mother's face, which she had given in the first year and never gotten back, and which she now knew only because Hallow remembered it and gave it back to her each spring, patiently, like a man returning a borrowed book.

The price of survival was everything she was. She had known that on the night she offered herself. She had paid it anyway. And every midwinter since, kneeling at the gate with the knife in her hand, she paid a little more — and every midwinter she would have paid it again, all of it, twice over, without hesitation, because the alternative was a world of frozen rivers and caged children and a winter that never broke.

The last stroke fell. The gate flared — that light like remembered firelight, that warmth like a sleeping animal — and the script on her arms settled, and the covenant sang through the glacier like a struck bell, and somewhere on the other side of the door a chorus of wolf-voices rose to meet it.

Petra stayed kneeling a long moment, in the silence after the song.

*You gave the taste of bread this year,* Hallow said, when he came to stand over her. Through the bond, his voice was not sound but weather, a pressure, a warmth. *I felt it leave you.*

"I did." She rose. Her knees did not ache. Nothing about her ached; that was one of the strangest mercies and one of the strangest griefs of what she was. "It was a good taste. It deserved to go to something important."

*Next spring I'll give you something back,* he said. *The smell of the river at Hollowmere. You've forgotten the smell. I haven't.*

"You keep my life for me, old friend."

*You keep mine.* He pressed his great head against her shoulder, careful as he always was, as though she were still breakable — and perhaps, in the ways that mattered, she was. *That's what the bond is. That's what the covenant is. Two halves, each holding what the other gave away.*

* * *

The feast came after, as it always did — carvers and wolf-kindred together in the long hall the kin had hollowed from the glacier's blue heart, firelight and lantern-light and the strange green glow of the gate itself, venison and bread and Maren's impossible spiced wine hauled all the way from Hollowmere by mule train every single year because, in Maren's words, a celebration without good wine is just a meeting.

Petra sat at the head of the table and did not eat. She never ate. But she drank the wine — taste was one of the few senses the gate had left her, perhaps because the covenant had been sealed with an oath, and oaths live in the mouth — and she listened, and being the listening, she listened better than anyone alive.

She listened to Wren argue covenant-law with a wolf-kin elder twice her size, and win, and know she'd won, and have the grace not to gloat. She listened to Tam, the blunt child of years ago, now a carver of real skill, telling the younger apprentices about the night of Skarn's attack with the usual exaggerations — Vane holding the pass alone against twenty, though it had been seven, though it had been minutes, though it had been enough. She listened to the wolf-kindred's young, the generation born after the thaw, tumbling over the hall's benches and begging the human carvers to carve their names, *please, please*, because a name in bone meant you would never be forgotten, and the young of both kinds feared the same dark.

And she listened, as she did every year, to the space where Skarn was not.

He came to the gate at midwinter. He always came. He stood at the edge of the firelight, at the edge of the crescent, at the edge of everything, and he watched the rite with his brother's eyes in a harder face, and he did not kneel — not quite, not fully, not in the way the chronicles would one day claim he had. But he came. Every year he came. And every year, as the carvers packed their sledges for the road south, Petra found some small token at the gate's threshold: a shed antler, a river stone, once — the year of the bad blizzard — a whole haunch of venison, left without a mark of who had carried it through the storm.

Hate, Hallow said once, is just love that lost its road. My brother is still walking. He'll find it, or he'll die walking, and either way he is my brother, and either way he is *ours*.

Petra looked across the hall at Hallow, at the firelight moving in his eyes, and thought: nine years, and I have never once regretted it. Not the knife. Not the winter. Not the giving. The price was everything she was, and what she was now — the keeper, the hinge, the half-heard and half-remembered guardian of two worlds — was worth every coin in the purse.

She would pay it again. She would pay it every midwinter until the mountains wore down to gravel and the glacier gave up its gate to the sea. Gladly.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when the last of the carvers sought their beds, and the hall's fires burned low, and Petra walked out one final time to stand before the gate.

The snow had stopped. The stars were ferocious. And in the east, just barely, just at the lip of the world, there was a light that was not a star — the year's first dawn, the dawn after the longest night, the dawn that meant the winter would break again, as it broke every year now, as it would break forever if she had anything to say about it, and she had everything to say about it, because she was the saying.

Hallow came to stand beside her. Together they watched the light come.

"Keeper," Wren said behind them — she must have followed, or never gone to bed at all; Petra suspected the latter, remembered being nineteen and unable to sleep on the holiest night of the year. The girl stood in the gate's glow with her mentor's knife at her belt and something carefully rehearsed in her face. "I've been meaning to ask. The southern carvers — the ones in the Corvane archives, the ones the old regent burned. We've found references. A second gate."

The words went through Petra like the knife's first stroke of the night.

"A second gate," she repeated.

"In the far south. Beneath the desert. The archives call it the *other door* — and they call its keeper the *second listening*." Wren's voice was steady, but her hands were not. "The records say the founding line made two hinges, so that neither realm would ever depend on a single point of failure. Petra — the southern line wasn't destroyed. The records say it was *lost*. There's someone out there who doesn't know what they are."

The gate hummed at her back, warm and strong and half a world away from whatever slept beneath the desert. Petra looked at the dawn coming up over the glacier, at the young carver waiting with her whole life in her face, at the wolf beside her whose ears had gone forward, whose eyes had gone bright with the old hunt-joy.

Nine years of peace. Nine years of the price paid and worth it.

Petra Brightwater, keeper of the Wolf Gate, last heir of the founding line, half-heard and half-remembered and wholly unafraid, began to laugh.

"Well," she said. "It seems the dead aren't finished with me after all."
