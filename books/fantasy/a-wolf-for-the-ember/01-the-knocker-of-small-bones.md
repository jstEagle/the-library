## 1. The Knocker of Small Bones

The bones woke before she did.

They always did. In the hour before dawn, when Grayhollow's chimneys had not yet begun their gray exhalations and the river fog pressed its wet palms against every shuttered window, the bones in Wendell Okafor's shop began to murmur. Not loudly—never loudly. A whisper like sand shifting in an hourglass, like pages turning in a book no one was reading. The rabbit femurs in the second drawer spoke of running, always of running, even now, even dead, even boiled clean and polished smooth beneath her thumb. The sparrow skulls murmured in tiny voices about the shape of sky. And the old ox knucklebone that sat on her workbench—the one she should have thrown away years ago, the one her grandmother had given her—hummed a low, patient note, like a man waiting at a door he knows will open eventually.

Wendell lay in the narrow bed above the shop and listened to them the way other people listened to rain.

*Fifteen years,* she thought. *Fifteen years of waking to voices.*

She rose without lighting a lamp. She knew the room by heart: four steps to the washstand, six down the stairs if she skipped the third from the top, which creaked in a note suspiciously close to a musical interval, and music was the sort of thing neighbors remembered. In Grayhollow, memory was currency, and Wendell Okafor paid nothing, spent nothing, kept her ledger blank. Fifteen years of blankness. It had cost her almost everything else, but the ledger stayed blank, and blank ledgers did not burn.

The shop below smelled of tallow and sawdust and the faint mineral sweetness of bone dust, which never quite left the air, which lived in the grain of the floorboards and the weave of her aprons. She lit the stove first—it was late autumn, and the fog off the Vell came into the city like something with intent—and then she stood in the middle of the dark shop and let herself hear them properly, just for a moment, before the day's silencing began.

*Good morning,* said the rabbit bones, who were simple souls and greeted her the way dogs greet their owners, with undiminished astonishment that she existed.

*Cold,* said the sparrow skulls, aggrieved. *Cold cold cold.*

*Soon,* hummed the ox knucklebone, in its low patient register, and Wendell did not ask it *soon what*, because it never answered, and because some questions you keep in your pocket like stones until you're strong enough to set them down.

"Morning," she said aloud, her voice rough with sleep, and the sound of it made her feel briefly, foolishly less alone—a woman saying good morning to a drawer of dead rabbits—and then she got to work.

* * *

The work was the point. The work was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing and unremarkable, and she had built it so carefully that even she sometimes forgot there was anything underneath.

To the customers of Copperlane Row, Wendell Okafor was a bone-carver, and a good one. She carved charms the way any licensed craftsman might: knucklebones drilled and strung on waxed cord for luck; small vertebrae polished into buttons; delicate fish-bone needles for lacemakers; whistles cut from goose wing-bones that played a thin, sweet note children loved. Her sign—a carved hare, leaping—had hung over her door for fifteen years, weathering from white to the color of weak tea. She paid her guild dues. She filed her quarterly inventories with the Office of Trade in her own careful hand, listing quantities and weights and nothing else, because nothing else was legal to list.

What the inventories did not say was that each charm whispered.

Not to the customer. Never to the customer—they heard only bone, only craft, only the pleasant rattle of a thing made well. But Wendell heard. When she strung a luck-charm for a fisherman, she could feel the particular animal it had been, could feel the wet bright panic of the net, and so she chose, always, bones from animals that had died quickly and cleanly, because she would not hang terror around anyone's neck. When she carved a mourning piece—a lock of hair-box lid pinned with dove bones, for the widows of Copperlane who wanted *something* of the sky to bury their grief under—she asked the dove bones to be gentle, and they were, they usually were, settling around the widow's wrist like a bird coming to rest.

It was a small magic. That was the lie she told the Wardens' census-takers, and the deeper truth she told herself: *it is a small magic, barely magic at all, a sensitivity, an ear, nothing the law means by the word.* The law meant fire from fingertips. The law meant raising the dead in shrieking ranks, or reading futures out of entrails, or the great resonant workings that had cracked the world along its seams during the Sundering and drowned half the Vell valley in shadow. The law did not mean a tired woman in an apron listening to sparrows complain about the cold.

But the law did not make fine distinctions. The law had one word for all of it—*resonance*—and one punishment, and the punishment was a pyre in Warden's Square with the whole city invited, and so Wendell Okafor had spent fifteen years being very, very good at being nobody.

There were costs to this that she had learned to carry the way mules carry loads: evenly, without complaint, and without ever setting them down.

She paid Master Corvane at the registry office a small envelope twice a year, wrapped in brown paper, tucked inside a purchase of carving blanks. She paid the night-watch sergeant on Copperlane a smaller envelope more often, because sergeants drank and talked. She paid the lamplighter to always light her corner last, so that her windows sat in shadow a few minutes longer than her neighbors'. None of these payments bought anything so grand as protection. They bought *invisibility*—the slow, grinding, daily labor of not being noticed—and invisibility, Wendell had learned, was not a state but a chore, like hauling water, like splitting wood. You did it every day or you lost everything.

"You spend your life paying people not to see you," her grandmother had said once, near the end, her hands like curled leaves around the ox knucklebone. "That's a hard way to live, little wren."

"It's a way to live," Wendell had answered. "The other way is shorter."

Her grandmother had laughed at that, a dry rustling laugh, and said something in the old language that Wendell had never translated, though she understood it perfectly, the way you understand a lullaby sung to you as a child—not the words, but the meaning, warm and sorrowful in equal measure. Then the old woman had closed her eyes, and the bones in the house had gone quiet all at once, every one of them, like birds falling silent before a storm, and Wendell had known.

That had been fifteen years ago. The Sundering was older than that—thirty years, nearly—but the purges came after, the way fever comes after a wound, and it was the purges that had taken her grandmother's generation, and nearly taken Wendell too, and had taught her the arithmetic she lived by now: *heard equals seen, seen equals named, named equals burned.*

So: silence. Every day, silence.

* * *

The evening ritual was the oldest part of it, older than the bribes, older even than the shop. Her grandmother had taught it to her as a child, back when it had been a comfort rather than a necessity—a way to settle the household's bones for sleep, the way you'd bank a fire.

When the last customer had gone and the shutters were barred, Wendell took down the long tray of finished charms and carried it to the workbench, and one by one she held each piece and quieted it.

It was harder to describe than to do. She would cradle a charm in both hands, thumbs resting on the bone, and breathe out slowly, and reach—not with her hands, with something behind her sternum, something she had no better name for than *the listening*—and she would find the residue of the animal still caught in the material, the ghost-shape of its living attention, and she would smooth it flat. Like smoothing a wrinkled cloth. Like pressing a palm over troubled water. The bone didn't stop being what it was; it simply stopped *reaching*. Stopped singing. Went to sleep.

*Rabbit,* she'd think, over the luck-strings. *You ran well. Rest now.*

*Sparrow. The sky keeps. Sleep.*

*Dove. You were gentle. Be gentle still. Sleep.*

One by one, forty pieces some nights, sixty on market weeks, until the shop sank into the deep ordinary silence of wood and stone and dead matter, the silence every other building in Grayhollow took for granted. Only the ox knucklebone she never silenced. She told herself it was because it was her grandmother's, because silencing it felt like closing the old woman's eyes a second time. But that wasn't entirely true, and she knew it wasn't, and on nights when the fog came up thick from the river she would catch herself holding the knucklebone to her ear like a shell, listening to its low patient hum, its *soon, soon*, and feeling—beneath the fear, beneath the fifteen years of practiced blankness—something she refused to call loneliness, because calling it loneliness would mean admitting what it was loneliness *for*.

She was not lonely, she told herself. She was safe. Safety was a room with one chair in it, and she had furnished hers beautifully.

* * *

On the evening the story truly begins, the fog came in early and mean.

Wendell had been working a commission since noon—a crib mobile for the tanner's wife, little carved birds and fish and a sleeping moon, all from shed antler, which was safe, antler being the one material that never spoke to her, having been given rather than taken—and her shoulders ached, and the stove had burned low, and through the gaps in the shutters she could hear the wind turning ugly in the chimney pots. Storm weather. Curfew weather. The watch bell would ring at dusk, and after that, anyone on the streets belonged to the Wardens, and the Wardens did not ask questions so much as they asked answers.

She was banking the stove when the bones woke.

All of them. At once.

Forty sleeping charms cried out in a single ragged chord—the rabbits, the sparrows, the doves, even the antler birds, which had *never* spoken, not once in fifteen years—and the sound went through Wendell like a blade of ice down the spine. She stood in the dark shop with her hand pressed flat against the stove's cooling iron and listened to her life's work scream like gulls before a wave.

And beneath them, beneath the chord, a single new note. Far away. Faint. High and thin and utterly unlike anything she had ever heard, because it was not the voice of any animal, and it was not quite the voice of any person either. It was a voice like the space between two words. It was a voice speaking—

Wendell's breath stopped.

She knew it. Gods help her, she knew it. Not the meaning—she couldn't have said what it *said*—but the sound of it, the cadence, the rise and fall like water finding its level. It was the language her grandmother had sung to her. The language under language. The one that had died with the old woman's generation, that had been burned out of the world pyre by pyre, that Wendell had not heard in fifteen years and had believed, had *prayed*, was gone from the earth entirely.

The voice sang three notes of it and stopped.

The charms fell silent too, all together, as if they had only been echoing something far away—as if they had heard it better than she had, through the ground, through the bone of the world itself.

Wendell stood a long time in the dark. Outside, the wind climbed, and somewhere down Copperlane Row the curfew bell began to toll, slow and iron-heavy, counting the city into its cage. Rain followed, hard as flung gravel.

She should go upstairs. She should bar the door, bank the coals, do the ritual early, sleep. Tomorrow was a market day; tomorrow she would be plain Wendell Okafor, carver, payer of envelopes, keeper of a blank ledger. Whatever had sung out there in the storm—whatever had made the dead things cry out in forty voices—it was none of her business, and making it her business was the one unforgivable sin, the sin her whole life was built to avoid.

She went to the door instead.

She told herself it was to check the bar. She put her eye to the gap in the shutters, where the lamplight of the corner post leaked through in a thin gold line, and she looked out at the drowning street—at the fog rolling black off the Vell, at the rain stitching silver down through it, at the empty road running with water like a gutter of stars.

And at the small figure standing in it.

A child. Barefoot, soaked through, arms wrapped around herself, standing in the flooded street directly before her door as though the door were the only fixed point in a spinning world. Too small to be out in this. Too calm, too still, for a child lost in a curfew storm. And as Wendell watched, the child turned her face up toward the shop—to the sign of the leaping hare, or to the window, or perhaps, Wendell thought with a cold sliding feeling, to *her*, to the warmth of a listening behind the wood—and spoke again.

Three notes. The language under language. Closer now, clear now, threading through the rain.

In the tray on the workbench behind her, forty sleeping charms woke and began, softly, tenderly, terribly, to sing along.

* * *

Wendell's hand was on the door bar before she had decided anything at all.

That was the thing about the listening, the thing she had spent fifteen years pretending was small: it did not ask her permission. It reached. It opened. Somewhere behind her sternum a door she had painted shut was already swinging wide, and through it poured the child's voice and the answering chorus of the dead-bones and, faint and patient beneath everything, the old ox knucklebone humming its one low note—*soon, soon*—as if it had known for fifteen years exactly what was coming up the street through the rain.

*Don't,* she told herself. *Every instinct you have. Don't.*

Her grandmother's laugh, dry as leaf-fall: *a hard way to live, little wren.*

The child swayed on her feet in the floodwater, and Wendell Okafor—who had survived fifteen years by never opening doors—lifted the bar.
