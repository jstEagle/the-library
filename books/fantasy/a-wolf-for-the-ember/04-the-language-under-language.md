## 4. The Language Under Language

*The gate was singing when I left it.*

That is the first thing. Before the running, before the cold, before the woman with the kind hands and the shop that smells of dust and animal fat — before all of that, there is the song, and the song is breaking.

I do not know how to say this in their words. Their words are like stones in my mouth, smooth and wrong-shaped. But in the old speech — the one that lives under language, under the floor of everything — I could say it easily. *The gate was singing when I left it, and the song had holes in it, and through the holes the dark was leaning.*

***

Here is what I remember, though remembering is like trying to hold water:

A road of white stones. My mother's hand around mine, her palm cracked from work, smelling of tallow and river-mud. She was walking too fast. Mothers walk too fast when they are afraid; they think you cannot tell because their faces are calm, but their hands always tell you. Her hand told me everything.

Behind us, the horizon was the wrong color. Not fire-color. Fire I know — fire is orange and hungry and it makes shadows dance. This was a color with no name in any tongue, a color like the sky forgetting itself, like the moment just before waking when dreams come apart. The Hollow Gate stood against it, far away now, small as a tooth, and its light pulsed the way a throat pulses when someone is crying without sound.

My mother said words to me in the old speech. I will keep them forever even if I lose everything else. She said: *You are the last door that opens from this side.* And then she said: *Run where the bones remember.*

I did not understand then. I am beginning to understand now, here, in this city of gray stone and grayer faces, sleeping on a pallet behind a counter full of little carved animals that whisper at night.

Because yes — the bones whisper. The woman thinks I do not notice. She thinks I am only a mute child who stares. But I hear them the way she hears them, or almost. They speak the old speech, badly, the way a bird speaks after living in a house too long. *Small sister,* they say. *Small sister, why does the air taste of ending?*

And I cannot answer them. That is the worst part. I carry the whole language inside me like a river carries a boat, and I cannot put it down, and I cannot use it, because using it would be like lighting a signal fire on a hilltop for everyone hunting me to see.

So I hold it. All of it. Every word my grandmother ever sang, every prayer the gatekeepers spoke at dawn, every name of every spirit between here and the Veil. It sits in my chest like a stone swallowed whole. Some nights it is so heavy I cannot breathe right, and I lie awake listening to the city breathe around me — thousands of people, and not one of them knows the words anymore. Not one.

The words are dying. I can feel them dying, the way you feel a tooth going bad: a slow sweet ache, a loosening. Each year fewer people speak them. Each year the spirits grow quieter, harder to call, thinner at the edges like old cloth. My grandmother said the covenant was a bridge built of voices — that as long as both sides spoke to each other, the world held its shape. But one side has stopped speaking. One side burned its own tongue out of its mouth and called the burning holy.

And now the bridge is failing, and I am what's left of it. A bridge of one. A girl of eight years carrying a door on her back.

***

Sometimes I dream of the night we ran.

In the dream I am smaller than I am now, which seems impossible, but dreams have their own arithmetic. There are four of us on the white road: me, my mother, old Uncle Tam who walked with a stick that was really a staff, and baby Rill strapped to Tam's back in a sling of woven grass. We were the last of the anchor-line. Everyone else had already gone ahead into the dark country beyond the Veil, or been taken by the gray-coats, or simply stopped, mid-step, mid-word, like candles pinched out.

The Wardens came at moonset. I did not see them first — I heard them, or rather I heard the absence of them, the way the night went silent around a moving hole. Uncle Tam turned and planted his staff and said a word I had never heard a living person say, a word so old it made the ground flinch, and the marsh-water rose up around him like a wall of black glass.

"Go," he said. In Common, so my mother would be sure to understand. "Go, and don't look—"

Then the light took him. Not fire-light. The other light. The color with no name. When it faded he was still standing there, holding his staff, smiling at us — but he wasn't in his eyes anymore. Something else was looking out of him, something patient and cold, wearing my uncle like a coat.

We ran. My mother ran with me and Rill wailed and the wall of water came down behind us and I heard — this is the part I try not to remember, but dreams are thieves, they take what you've hidden — I heard Tam's voice calling after us in my uncle's voice, saying *come back, little fish, come back,* in a tone that had never once been in his mouth while he was alive.

Three days later my mother hid me in a grain-barge heading for Grayhollow. She sewed a charm into my collar — bone, carved with a spiral, warm as a heartbeat — and she kissed my forehead and spoke the last words I would ever hear her say in the old speech:

*When the bones sing, follow them. They will lead you to the carver's daughter. Tell her the ember remembers.*

The carver's daughter. I did not know who that meant. I was eight and I was alone on a barge full of wheat, and the charm against my neck grew colder each day, and somewhere behind me the Hollow Gate's song developed another hole.

Now I know. Now I watch the woman with the tired eyes and the scarred fingers, the one who talks to her bones when she thinks no one is listening, and I think: *the carver's daughter.* Except she isn't a daughter anymore, she is grown, and there is fear in her the way there is water in a well — deep, and drawn up fresh every morning.

She is afraid of exactly what I am carrying.

***

The dead-bones in her shop sing to me at night. That is how I know the covenant is worse off than even my grandmother feared.

They sing fragments. Half-verses. A lullaby missing its middle. A naming-prayer with no names left in it, just the shape where names should go, like footprints in snow. The old speech survives in them the way a smell survives in a coat — present, but fading, belonging to someone gone.

I lie on my pallet and listen and my chest aches with all the words I cannot safely say. Because here is the secret, the terrible one, the one my mother died keeping: the words aren't just words. They are load-bearing. Say a true covenant-word in a thin place and the Veil thickens. Sing a gate-song and the dead rest easier. The whole world is a roof held up by spoken pillars, and the pillars are being pulled out one by one, and everyone in Grayhollow walks around beneath the sagging sky congratulating themselves on their silence.

Last night the bones sang something new. A warning-verse. I knew it instantly — my grandmother used it to call the household spirits home before storms. But the version the bones sang was wrong. Corrupted. As if the verse itself were drowning.

And underneath it, faint as breath on a winter window, I heard the other thing. The thing from beyond the Gate. The leaning dark. It was listening too. It has been listening ever since I arrived, I think. It knows what I am. It is very patient, and very hungry, and it is waiting for me to speak — because if I speak the old speech aloud, truly aloud, at full strength, every spirit within a hundred miles will turn toward my voice.

Including the ones wearing my uncle's smile.

So I stay silent. Mute, they call me. The dumb child. And I let them believe it, because my silence is the last wall between the living world and the color with no name.

But walls crack. And today, when the woman held up the little carved wolf and asked me — gently, in her rough Common — *do you like it?*, the word rose in me like floodwater, the true answer, the old answer, the whole sentence my grandmother would have sung: *I like it, sister-of-the-carving-hand, and the wolf-spirit in your knife remembers your grandmother's grandmother, and she says you carve truer than you know.*

I bit it back. I swallowed the river. I smiled the empty smile they expect from me.

But the bones heard anyway. All of them, at once, in the dark of the closed shop, began to sing — softly, softly, like rain starting — and the woman went still, and looked at the shelf, and then at me, and I saw in her face that she understood more than I wanted her to.

Tomorrow, maybe, I will give her one word. Just one. A small one. *Ember,* perhaps. Or *remember.*

The inheritance is heavy. But I am beginning to think I was never meant to carry it alone.
