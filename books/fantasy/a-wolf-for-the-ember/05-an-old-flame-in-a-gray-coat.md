## 5. An Old Flame in a Gray Coat

The knock came at midmorning, three measured raps, and every bone in the shop went silent at once.

Wendell had spent fifteen years learning the bones' moods—the way chicken-wing charms murmured in their sleep, the way knucklebones chattered when storms rolled off the Graywater, the way the old femur she kept for luck hummed a note so low it was less sound than memory. She knew their voices the way a mother knows her children's breathing through a wall.

She had never heard them go quiet like this.

Not hushed. *Listening.*

She set down the awl mid-stroke, leaving a charm of rabbit bone half-finished on the bench, and wiped her hands on her apron out of habit rather than need. Through the shop's front window, gray light fell on the cobbles of Tallow Row, and the morning market was doing its usual shuffling business—fishwives arguing over eels, a cooper hauling staves, two children chasing a cat between the stalls. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary.

But the Wardens' barge was moored at the canal gate, its gray sail furled, and two figures in gray coats stood at the mouth of the row, speaking to the fishmonger Brannit, who was pointing.

Brannit was pointing at her shop.

Wendell's heart did not race. That was the strange thing, the thing she would remember afterward: how still everything went inside her, as if some deep animal part of her had decided that panic was a luxury and had simply closed that account. She thought of Sefa upstairs, curled on the pallet beneath the eaves, still asleep after a night of murmuring in that language older than kingdoms. She thought of the bones, silent now, listening with her.

Three raps again. Patient. The knock of someone who expected doors to open because doors always opened for them.

Wendell crossed the shop floor, unlatched the door, and opened it.

Inquisitor Mara Voss stood on her threshold with rain still beaded on the shoulders of her coat, and the world tilted sideways without moving an inch.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years since the purges, since the night Wendell had watched from a doorway while torchlight turned Mara's face to gold and shadow, since the last time those gray eyes had looked at her with anything other than official distance. Mara had been twenty then, all sharp angles and sharper convictions, her dark hair cropped short for the Academy, her hands still soft from books instead of hard from work. The woman on the threshold was harder everywhere. Lines at the corners of her eyes. A scar bisecting one eyebrow, thin and white as a fishbone. The gray coat fit her like a second skin, buttoned to the throat, the Inquisitor's silver quill-and-flame pinned at the collar.

But she stood the way she had always stood, weight slightly on the left foot, chin level, as if the world were a debate she intended to win.

"Bone-carver Okafor." Her voice was even, courteous, pitched to carry no further than the doorway. "Inquisitor Voss. I'm conducting routine inspections of licensed crafters in the Tallow district. May I come in?"

*Licensed.* The word was a hook dressed as a courtesy. Wendell held her license—a scrap of vellum with the council seal, renewed each year by a clerk who took his bribe in carved saints—hanging by the till. She could have shown it. Instead she stepped back and said, "Mind the shavings. They get everywhere."

Something flickered behind Mara's eyes—amusement, maybe, or its ghost—and she stepped across the threshold into the shop where she had once stood a hundred times, before, when it had been Old Nana Okafor's shop and two girls had hidden among the hanging charms and kissed until the bones rattled on their strings.

The bones were silent now. All of them. Even the luck-femur. It was the loudest silence Wendell had ever heard.

Mara walked the aisle slowly, gloved fingers clasped behind her back, cataloguing. Wendell watched her do it and understood that this was not a browsing walk. This was a search conducted at conversational speed. Mara's gaze touched the workbench, the half-finished rabbit charm, the jars of tallow and sinew, the license hanging by the till—lingered there a half-second longer than anywhere else—and moved on.

"You've done well," Mara said. "The shop. Grandmother Okafor's place, isn't it? I remember it."

"Do you."

"I remember a great many things." Mara lifted a finished charm from its hook—a hare's foot bound in red thread, a traveler's luck-piece—and turned it over with a connoisseur's care. "Your work has improved. The old woman's carving was heavier-handed. Yours is..." She searched for the word, found it, and gave it like a gift wrapped around a blade: "*Listening.*"

The word dropped into the silent shop like a stone into a well.

"Carving wants a light touch," Wendell said. Her own voice sounded far away to her. "Bone remembers the knife. You work against it, you get splinters."

"Mm." Mara hung the charm back precisely where it had hung. "And how is trade? The district reports say carvers are struggling. Too much competition from the guild shops in Hallowmarket."

"Trade keeps me fed."

"And yet you pay your license fees promptly. Every year. On time, or early." Mara turned from the shelf at last and faced her fully, and the morning light through the window caught the scar above her brow. "That's unusual, for a struggling crafter. Unusual enough that I pulled the records. Fifteen years of prompt payment, Wendell. From a woman whose ledger, if she has one, must bleed."

There it was. Not proof. A thread, held up to the light, waiting to see if Wendell would pull it herself.

"The clerk takes his fee in goods," Wendell said. "Charms for his wife. His mother's grave-marker, carved free. People on Tallow Row look after each other. You'd know that if you'd stayed."

The words were out before she could weigh them, and she saw them land—saw the small contraction around Mara's eyes, the way her breath went shallow for exactly one heartbeat before discipline smoothed it away. Fifteen years ago, Wendell would have given anything to make Mara Voss's composure crack. Now she had done it with six words and felt nothing but ash.

"I stayed," Mara said quietly. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

They ended up at the workbench, because Mara asked to see her work and refusing would have been louder than agreeing. Wendell sat, took up the half-finished rabbit charm, and carved while the Inquisitor of Grayhollow stood over her shoulder watching her hands.

It should have been unbearable. It was worse than unbearable; it was almost sweet. Mara leaned close the way she used to lean close over shared books in the loft above this very room, her breath stirring the fine curls at Wendell's temple, and Wendell's body remembered all of it—the weight of her arm, the smell of her, ink and lavender soap then, ink and cold iron now—and grieved it afresh with a completeness that made carving difficult.

"You cut against the grain there," Mara said, nodding at the charm.

"That's where the strength is."

"Hm. The Academy teaches that magic runs along the grain of things. Along the natural order." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You disagree?"

"I don't know anything about magic. I carve bones for fishermen's wives."

"No," Mara agreed softly. "Of course you don't."

The awl slipped. A hairline scratch across the charm's face, nothing, invisible to any eye but hers—but Mara saw Wendell's hand flinch, and Wendell saw Mara see it, and the whole room rearranged itself around that small failure like water finding a new level.

Above them, faint through the ceiling, came a sound. A child's voice, murmuring in sleep. Words in no living tongue, rising and falling like a tide.

Neither woman moved.

The murmur faded. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice and went still.

"Curfew storm last night," Mara said, conversationally, as though continuing an entirely different conversation. "Bad one. And yet this morning, Brannit tells me, there's a new face on Tallow Row. A child. Dark-skinned, foreign, seen entering this shop at first light." She let the silence stretch. "The census for this district lists you as a single occupant, Wendell."

"She's a customer's daughter. Waiting on a commission. The mother's ill; couldn't keep the girl at home."

"What's her name?"

"Sefa."

The name left Wendell's mouth and she knew, instantly, that she had made a mistake—not in giving it, but in giving it too smoothly, too readily, the rehearsed answer arriving without the half-beat of innocent hesitation. Mara's eyes came up from the charm to Wendell's face, and for a moment the Inquisitor was gone entirely and something older looked out: the girl in the loft, the one who had memorized Wendell's tells because she loved them, because knowing where Wendell hid things had been a form of touching her.

"You haven't hesitated once," Mara said, barely above a whisper. "Not once in this whole inspection. Except just now."

"Because I have nothing to hide."

"Everyone hides something. I've built a career on it." Mara straightened, and the gray coat settled around her like armor settling onto a body that had grown into it. When she spoke again, the courtesy was back, polished and lethal. "I'll tell you what I told the hedge-witch on Candlemaker's Lane, before we took her. I told her: the law doesn't require you to be guilty. It only requires you to be *found*. Resonance reports in this district have tripled since the Sundering anniversary. Something is singing under Grayhollow, Wendell, and my Wardens can feel it in their teeth. When I find the source—and I will find the source—I would greatly prefer to find it *before* the Hall sends someone who isn't me."

The threat wore kindness's clothing, and that was the cruelest part. Wendell set down the awl.

"Is that what you told yourself about the three they burned this week?"

Mara didn't flinch. That was the worst of it—she'd learned not to. "The Hall burned them. I filed the report."

"You wrote the report."

"I write all the reports." For a moment—only a moment—the armor showed its seam. Mara's voice dropped, and underneath the Inquisitor was a tired woman counting bodies. "Do you imagine I don't know what I am, Wendell? Do you imagine I sleep?" She stopped herself. Drew breath. Became official again, the way another woman might smooth a skirt. "The inspection is concluded. Your license is in order. Your craft is... unremarkable."

They both heard the word mean the opposite of itself.

Mara walked to the door. Stopped with her hand on the latch, her back to Wendell, and the gray coat pulled straight across her shoulders. When she spoke, she did not turn around.

"There was a night, fifteen years ago, when you asked me to run with you. South, past the Graywater, anywhere the law got thin." Her thumb moved once over the latch, a tiny motion, unconscious. "I said the law was worth staying for. I believed it. I *built* myself out of believing it." A pause. "I still believe it. That's what frightens me—that after everything, I still believe it. Because if I stop, I don't know what's underneath."

"Mara—"

"Don't." Sharp, immediate, a blade flat against the name. Then, softer: "Don't use that voice. Not with me. Not today."

She opened the door. Morning noise poured in—gulls, eel-carts, Brannit's laugh—and Mara stepped halfway through it before pausing one final time, profile half-turned, the silver quill-and-flame catching the light.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the resonance reports put the strongest reading within thirty feet of this shop. I logged it as instrument error." Her eyes met Wendell's at last, gray and unreadable and full of fifteen buried years. "Instrument error, Wendell. See that it stays one."

Then she was gone, gray coat swallowed by the gray street, and the door clicked shut behind her with the gentleness of a drawn blade being sheathed.

* * *

Wendell stood in the empty shop for a long time. Outside, the market resumed its noise; inside, the silence held.

Then, slowly, the bones began to speak again—one voice, then ten, then the whole hanging chorus of them, whispering and clacking and humming, the shop filling back up with its secret life like blood returning to a slept-on limb. The luck-femur hummed its low note. The knucklebones chattered. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable now that she knew how to listen, ran a single warning repeated in a dozen voices:

*She knows. She knows. She knows.*

"I know," Wendell whispered back.

She climbed the stairs to the loft, where Sefa sat awake on the pallet, knees drawn up, watching the door with eyes too old for her face. The girl had heard everything. Of course she had. Children like Sefa heard everything.

"She'll come back," Wendell said, sitting on the edge of the pallet. "Sooner or later, she'll come back, and next time she won't be alone."

Sefa studied her for a long moment. Then the girl reached out and touched Wendell's hand—the hand that still ached faintly from gripping the awl too hard—and said a single word in the dying language. Wendell didn't know its meaning. But the bones downstairs sang it back to her, all of them at once, clear as a bell over water:

*Soon.*

That night, Wendell did not silence the bones. She sat among their whispers until dawn, and somewhere past midnight she understood what the stillness in the shop had really been—not fear, not warning, but recognition. The bones had gone quiet because they had known her. The dead remembered Mara Voss too.

And they were afraid of what she was becoming.
