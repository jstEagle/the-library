## 6. The First Ember Stone

The bones began singing on the third night after the storm.

Wendell heard it first as a wrongness in the shop's quiet—the particular silence of the bone-charms hanging from their pegs had changed texture, like a room where someone has stopped breathing. She set down her carving knife and listened with her whole body, the way her grandmother had taught her before she'd taught her to be afraid.

The knucklebones in the jar by the window were humming.

Not whispering—she was used to whispers, the faint residual voices that clung to worked bone like scent to cloth, the murmurs she smothered each night beneath wax and will and fifteen years of practiced deafness. This was different. This was *song*, low and directional, a thread of sound pulling westward through the walls of her shop toward the Weepers' Field, where Grayhollow buried the ones it didn't burn.

She stood very still in the middle of her darkened workshop, sawdust settling around her ankles like snow, and let herself feel the full terrible shape of what was happening. In the back room, Sefa slept—or didn't sleep; Wendell could hear the child's breath through the wall, quick and shallow as a bird's. The girl had been with her four days now, mute in Common, fluent in a language that made dead things remember they had been alive. And now the bones were singing because of her, reaching toward something buried in the city's western edge like roots reaching toward water.

*The covenant is failing,* Sefa's hands had said, drawing spirals in spilled flour. *The stones are waking. You must gather them or everything falls.*

Wendell had spent half a lifetime learning not to hear. Now the hearing would not stop.

She dressed in her oldest coat, the one without charms sewn into the seams, wrapped her scarf high over her jaw, and took down the jar of knucklebones. They were sheep bones, mundane, bought from a butcher on Tallow Row—but they had been handled by so many grieving hands, carried against so many hearts, that they had learned to listen. She poured them into her palm and held them out like a compass needle, and the song flowed through them, warm as blood, pointing west.

Behind her, small feet on the floorboards. Sefa stood in the doorway wrapped in Wendell's spare blanket, eyes enormous in the lamplight, and Wendell braced herself to send the child back to bed.

But Sefa only looked at the bones, then at Wendell, and pressed one small hand flat against her own sternum. *I go too,* the gesture said, in the grammar they had built between them out of flour-dust and necessity. Then, more urgently: *They are calling me. I have to answer.*

"You're eight years old," Wendell whispered, though she knew even as she said it that age meant nothing here, that this child had crossed distances no adult in Grayhollow could name. "If the Wardens catch us—"

Sefa shook her head once, slow and absolute, the way children shake their heads when adults say foolish things. Then she reached up and touched Wendell's wrist, right over the pulse, and spoke—not aloud, but into the skin, the way she did sometimes, words that arrived as warmth rather than sound:

*You heard your grandmother sing this song when you were smaller than me. Don't pretend you don't know the way.*

And that was the cruelest thing anyone had said to Wendell Okafor in fifteen years, because it was true.

* * *

They went out through the coal-cellar and up into streets the color of wet ash. The curfew had lifted an hour past midnight, but the storm-wash still lay everywhere, puddles holding broken reflections of gaslight, the whole city smelling of drowned cinders. Wendell kept to the service lanes behind the tanneries, Sefa's hand tucked in hers, small and cold and gripping hard enough to hurt.

The Weepers' Field lay beyond the western wall, past the leper gate that hadn't opened in thirty years. It was where Grayhollow buried its suicides, its unclaimed dead, its executed criminals whose families wouldn't pay for consecrated ground—and its witches, in the early years, before the Inquisition decided burning was cleaner than burying. The ground there was soft and black and nothing grew above two inches tall, and everyone in Grayhollow knew to walk past it quickly and not look at the low mounds.

The gate in the wall was rusted open. Of course it was. The bones' song had grown so loud in Wendell's palm that she could feel it in her teeth, and the knucklebones had begun to tremble like living things trying to leap free.

At the field's edge, Sefa stopped walking.

The child turned her face up to the sky—clouded, moonless, pressing down like a lid—and her mouth moved, shaping silent syllables, and every hair on Wendell's arms rose. Because for one moment, just one, Wendell heard it: the language under language, the covenant-speech, rising out of the girl like heat off a stone. It sounded like her grandmother's lullabies. It sounded like the voice that had sung to her at night about the Gate and the Ember and the keeping of the world's two halves, before her grandmother went quiet forever and the purges came and Wendell learned that songs like those got people hung from the Saltgate bridge with their tongues cut out first.

"Grandmother," Wendell breathed, before she could stop herself. "You sang like her."

Sefa looked at her with an expression far too old for her face—pity, maybe, or recognition—and took her hand again, and led her into the field.

* * *

The grave was the fourth mound from the leaning stone, and they found it because the ground itself was glowing.

Faintly, beneath the black soil, a light the color of banked coals pulsed in rhythm with the bones' song—slow, patient, like a heart that had been beating underground for decades and saw no reason to stop now. Wendell knelt in the wet grass with her sleeves pushed up and dug with her bare hands, and the earth came away easily, too easily, as if the soil wanted to give up what it held.

Three feet down, her fingers struck cloth.

She lifted it out into the open air: a burial shroud, heavy linen gone gray-brown with age, stitched closed with wire instead of thread. Someone had sewn this shut in a hurry, or in anger, or both. The fabric was stiff in places with old stains Wendell chose not to identify. And sewn into the shroud's center, wrapped in layer upon layer of linen like a wasp in a cocoon, was something that radiated warmth against her palms even through the cloth—a warmth with a pulse to it, a slow deep beat that matched the glow in the pit.

The moment her fingers closed around it, the ghost arrived.

It came up out of the grave like smoke finding a draft, and Wendell had communed with the dead since childhood, had heard their thin residual voices in every bone she carved, but she had never felt anything like *this*. The presence filled the field. The grass flattened in a circle around them. The temperature dropped so fast that Wendell's breath bloomed white, and the air itself seemed to lean in to listen.

*I KNOW THAT FACE.*

The voice was a woman's, cracked and furious, coming from everywhere—from the soil, from the shroud, from inside Wendell's own skull. It was the voice of someone who had been angry for a very long time and had gotten very good at it.

Wendell's hands shook. She forced herself to speak the old courtesy, the words her grandmother had drilled into her: "Dead one, I see you. Dead one, I hear you. Speak, and be witnessed."

A laugh rolled across the field, ugly and delighted. *Witnessed. Fifteen years I've lain under this dirt with my rage for company, and now an Okafors daughter comes digging and offers me witness.* The presence gathered itself, condensed, and suddenly Wendell could almost see her: a tall woman, hollow-cheeked, rope-scars livid on her throat, wearing the plain gray dress of the hanged. Her eyes were coals. Her eyes were the same color as the thing in the shroud. *Look at me, carver. Look at what your city does, and then tell me you don't know who I am.*

"I don't," Wendell said honestly. "I was a child when the burnings ended."

*Brannagh,* the ghost said, and the name hit the ground like a dropped anvil. *Maeve Brannagh. Keeper of the Western Veil. Hanged from the Saltgate on the ninth of Frostmonth, forty-one years ago, for the crime of keeping the dead where they belong.* She drifted closer, and the cold of her was the cold of deep wells. *Your grandmother wove this shroud with her own hands. Your grandmother kissed my forehead and told me my death would matter. Your grandmother swore the Okafor line would guard what I guarded, carry what I carried.* The coals of her eyes flared. *And then your grandmother died, and her granddaughter buried her gift under sawdust and lies, and the veil has been thinning ever since, and I have been lying here rotting with a piece of the world's salvation sewn into my winding-sheet, waiting, WAITING—*

"I didn't know," Wendell said. Her voice came out small. "I swear it, I didn't know what she was, what we were—"

*You knew enough.* The ghost's fury was a physical weight, pressing Wendell to her knees in the wet grass. Sefa cried out and stepped between them, arms spread wide, and the ghost paused—looked down at the child—and something in her burned expression flickered, guttered, like a candle in wind. *Ah,* she said, softer. *Ah. So it's come to this. A child anchor. They always send children, in the end. It was a child the last time, too.*

"What last time?" Wendell whispered. "What happened the last time?"

The ghost was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the wall, a Warden bell tolled the second hour, and the sound seemed to reach them from another world. When Brannagh spoke again, the fury had cooled into something worse: contempt, worn smooth by decades.

*Ask your bones. Ask the stones. Ask the founders who broke the covenant to steal its power and then wrote laws making sure no one could ever mend it.* She extended one translucent hand toward the shroud in Wendell's arms. *Take it. Take my ember. It's why I've been singing—it's all I could do, lying here, singing into the dark and hoping someone with ears would finally come. But understand what you carry, Okafor. That stone is not a treasure. It is a debt. My death is folded into it, and the deaths of every witch who died while your family hid. When you reforge the covenant—if you live long enough to try—you will pay for all of us. The dead do not forgive. We only wait.*

Wendell unwrapped the linen with numb fingers.

Inside lay a stone the size of a hen's egg, and it was wrong in the way that holy things are wrong—its surface shifting like embers seen through a grate, orange light moving beneath a crust of black, warm as a sleeping animal. When her skin touched it, the song that had led her here resolved at last into words, into a single sustained note of purpose, and she felt it sink into her chest and take up residence beside her heartbeat.

Beside her, Sefa made a sound—the first sound Wendell had ever heard her make aloud. Not speech. A hum, three notes, falling. The same three notes her grandmother used to hum at the end of every lullaby, the notes that meant *sleep safe, the gate is kept.*

The ghost watched them both, and her burning eyes were unreadable.

*Four stones,* Brannagh said. *One in my grave. One where the plague-dead lie heaped beneath the Hall of Inquisitors—they built their seat of power on a mass grave, because cruelty is never finished being cruel. One beyond the Veil, in the country of the dead themselves, and may whatever gods remain have mercy on whoever goes to fetch it. And the last in the vault of the man who ordered my hanging, passed down like a signet ring through every Inquisitor since.* Her form was thinning now, unraveling at the edges like smoke. *Go to the plague-pit next, carver. And when the thousands down there ask you for witness—and they will ask, oh, they will ask—you had better be prepared to give it. Witness is not a word. Witness is a wound that never closes.*

"Wait," Wendell said. "My grandmother—did she suffer? Did she die knowing—"

*Everyone suffers,* said Maeve Brannagh, and her voice was already fading, sinking back into the soil, into the long patience of the dead. *The question is whether it means anything. Make it mean something, Okafor. I am so tired of meaning nothing.*

Then the field was only a field, black grass and leaning stone and the ordinary dark, and Wendell knelt alone in it with a warm stone in her hands and a child's cold fingers laced through hers, and somewhere across the city a bell tolled the hour, and the note of the stone in her chest reached westward, and southward, and down—toward the plague-pit, toward the thousands, toward the next debt she would have to pay.

Sefa tugged her sleeve. Pointed home. Then pointed, after a moment, at the stone, and at Wendell's heart, and made a shape with her small hands that Wendell didn't yet know how to read—but would learn, soon enough, was the old sign for *price.*
