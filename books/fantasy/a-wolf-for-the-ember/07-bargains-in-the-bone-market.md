## 7. Bargains in the Bone Market

The way down to the Bone Market began behind a tannery that had not tanned anything in twenty years.

Wendell knew the door by its smell—old lime and older lies—and she stood before it for a long moment with Sefa's small hand wrapped in hers, listening to the rain gutter off the eaves of Tanner's Row. The child had been quiet all day, the particular quiet of someone conserving something precious. She had eaten the whole bowl of barley soup Wendell put before her, then held the empty spoon up to the light as though checking it for truth. Now she looked at the tannery door with an expression far too old for her face, and her lips moved around words that made the two Ember Stones in Wendell's satchel hum like struck bells.

"Hush," Wendell murmured, though no one could hear them but her. "Hush now. We're among friends."

That was a lie too. In Grayhollow there were no friends, only people who hadn't yet decided what you were worth.

She knocked the pattern: three slow, two fast, one that dragged like a dying man's breath. The door opened on darkness and the smell of river water and tallow, and a boy of perhaps twelve peered out at her with eyes that had seen everything worth seeing twice.

"Carver," he said. Not a question. Osric's people knew her work; half the smugglers in the undervaults wore her charms against drowning, against scrying, against the Warden hounds' noses. They paid in coin and never asked why the bones sometimes seemed to look back.

"I need to see him."

"He doesn't see anyone before the tide turns."

"The tide turned an hour ago." She let the satchel shift on her shoulder, just enough. The stones sang—one clear note, thin as wire, and the boy flinched as if he'd heard it too, or almost had. His face went carefully blank, which was its own kind of answer.

"Stairs are down and left," he said. "Don't touch anything that glows."

* * *

The Bone Market was not a market for bones, whatever the name suggested. It was a market for everything the city had outlawed and everything it had simply forgotten: charms and countercharms, unlicensed remedies, love philtres cut with river-herbs, prayer flags from the old faiths, a jar of preserved grace that was probably just honey and wishful thinking. It lived in a flooded cistern beneath Tanner's Row, a vaulted cavern where the water lay black and mirror-still between walkways of plank and stone, and the vendors hung their wares from hooks so that the whole ceiling glittered like a cave of frozen stars.

Wendell walked it with her hood up and Sefa pressed close against her hip. Around them the dead whispered—not loudly, not accusingly, just the ordinary murmur of any place where many people had died and been forgotten about it. Drowned laborers in the cistern walls. A plague-year's worth of beggars. The whispers rose when Sefa passed, the way candle flames lean toward an open window, and Wendell felt each one turn toward the child like faces turning toward warmth.

*She is the door,* one voice said, very old, very patient. *They will all know it soon.*

*I know,* Wendell thought back, and kept walking.

Osric held court at the far end of the cistern, on a platform built over the deepest pool, in a chair that had once belonged to some magistrate's house and still bore the faded crest of a family purged two generations past. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a smuggler's hands—scarred, careful, always moving—and a face that did its grieving privately, in the hours when no one watched. Wendell had known him eleven years. He had never once asked what she heard in the bones, and she had never once told him, and this mutual silence was the closest thing to trust the city allowed.

He saw her coming and waved his guards back with two fingers.

"Wendell Okafor." His voice was gravel rolled in honey. "You honor my humble sewer."

"Your humble sewer has better manners than most of the houses above it."

"That's because down here we admit what we are." He gestured her onto the platform, and his eyes—quick, merchant's eyes—went straight to the satchel, then to the child, then back to Wendell's face, reading all three in the order of their danger. Something shifted behind his expression. Not greed. Recognition. "Ah," he said softly. "So it's finally come for you too."

"What's come for me?"

"The thing you've been running from since I've known you." He poured three cups of plum brandy from a clay jug, and then, after a pause that cost him something, set the third cup down within Sefa's reach without comment. "Sit. Tell me what you need. And then tell me what it costs, because nothing under Grayhollow moves for free, and you've never yet asked me for anything small."

Wendell sat. The stones in her satchel had gone quiet, listening.

* * *

She told him plainly, because Osric dealt in plainness the way other men dealt in gold. The covenant was failing. The Hollow Gate was opening. There were four Ember Stones scattered through the city and beyond it, and she had two, and she needed passage—quiet passage, unwatched passage—to places the Wardens had already begun to search. The plague-pits beneath the Inquisitors' Hall. The marsh roads south to Ashfen. Perhaps, if the bones spoke true, farther than that.

Osric listened without interrupting, which was unlike him, and when she finished he was silent for a long moment, looking down into the black water below the platform where his own reflection floated, pale and upside down, like a drowned man waiting to be identified.

"You're asking me to move contraband that isn't goods," he said at last. "You're asking me to move *hope*. Hope is the one cargo that gets men hanged faster than ember-dust. Wardens can confiscate dust. They can't confiscate hope—they have to burn the man who carries it."

"I'm asking you to move a woman and a child."

"And the stones. Don't soften it, carver; I can hear them humming from here, and so can half the things in this water." He leaned forward. "I'll do it."

Wendell blinked. Of all the answers she had rehearsed against, easy agreement was not one of them. "Just like that?"

"No. Not just like that." He smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only weariness worn smooth as river glass. "Two prices. The first you've already guessed: your silence. What happens in my cistern, what routes my barges run, whose palms my coin greases—you carry it nowhere, you whisper it to no bone, no matter what the bones ask you. Swear it."

"I swear it." That price was cheap. Her silence had fifteen years of practice.

"The second—" He stopped. His hands, which never stopped moving, went still. Below them the black water held its breath. "The second is harder, and I'll not pretend otherwise, and you have every right to refuse and walk out of here and find another way, though there isn't one, and we'll both pretend there might be."

"Osric."

"My daughter." The word came out of him like a splinter drawn slow. "Maren. She was seven. The flood year, nine years back—the Undervault took three of my people and the river took her. We found her on the third day, down where the water pools under the old sluice gates." His voice stayed level, which was how Wendell knew how much it was costing him. "I buried her above the waterline, in the paupers' field off Gallows Hill, because even grief has to be licensed in this city, and I couldn't afford a plot anywhere the ground remembers."

The dead around them had gone very quiet. Even the market's murmur had dimmed, as though the whole cistern were leaning in.

"She was mute," Osric said. "From birth. Never said a word in seven years, and I used to tell myself it didn't matter, that I knew her heart without the words, and maybe I did, maybe I did—but she never got to say anything, Wendell. Not one word. Not *goodbye*, not *father*, not *it's dark down here and I'm frightened*. Nothing." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and ashamed of being either. "You hear the dead. Everyone knows it, whatever they say aloud—that's why your charms work, that's why smugglers pay triple for Okafor bone. So here is my price. Carve her. Carve my Maren from whatever bone will hold her best, and let me hear her voice *once*. One time. One word, even. And in exchange I'll move you through every dark vein under this rotten city until the gate itself stands open, and no Warden's hound will catch your scent, and my silence will be yours until they put me in the ground."

The brandy sat untouched in Wendell's cup. Somewhere across the cistern a vendor laughed at a customer's joke, bright and obscene against the hush.

*There it is,* whispered a voice near her ear, dry as old paper. *The oldest bargain in the world. Compassion holding out its hand with a hook inside the glove.*

Wendell looked at Sefa. The child had picked up the third cup and was cradling it in both hands without drinking, watching Osric with an expression that Wendell was beginning to recognize as the child's version of weeping—stillness, total and terrible, like the moment before snow breaks from a branch.

Because Wendell knew this temptation. She knew it the way a scar knows weather. Fifteen years she had refused to listen fully—to call back, to converse, to open herself to the dead who wanted so desperately to be heard—because she understood, in the marrow the bones came from, that hearing was a door and doors swing both ways. Every medium who ever claimed to speak with the beloved departed had ended either a fraud or a slave, chained to the grief of paying customers, mining her own gift for other people's dead until there was nothing of herself left unclaimed. The dead did not mean to devour you. They didn't have to mean anything. Grief was simply hungry, and hunger did not negotiate.

And here was Osric, offering her exactly that chain, polished and priced.

"I can't promise she'll speak," Wendell said quietly. "I don't command the dead, Osric. No one does. That's the first lie every charlatan sells and the reason the Wardens are right about half of what they burn us for. If I carve her—if I make a vessel true enough to hold what remains of her—all I can promise is that you'll be in the room when whatever happens, happens. It might be her voice. It might be her silence. It might be something wearing her shape that never was her at all, because grief calls things up out of the deep water, and not everything that answers loves you."

"I know the risks."

"You know the words for the risks. Knowing them is different." She set down the cup. "And I want you to understand what you're really buying. Because it isn't one word from your daughter. It's the rest of your life spent at that door, wanting another word, and another, and hating yourself for wanting, and hating me when I tell you the vessel is spent. That's the true price. You'll pay it whether I carve her or not—the carving only decides whether you pay it alone or with company."

Osric was silent a long time. The water lapped. Above them the hanging charms turned slowly on their hooks, a sky of small forbidden stars.

"Do it anyway," he said at last, hoarse. "A drowning man doesn't ask whether the rope will hold forever. He asks whether it holds tonight." He fumbled at his neck and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small lock of hair, fine and pale, kept nine years in a locket gone green with handling. "This is hers. You'll need it, won't you? For the... for the resonance."

"Yes." She took it. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "Whose bone?"

His jaw worked. "Whatever holds best. But—" and here his voice cracked, the first crack in all that careful masonry, "—not hers. Whatever you do, don't use hers. I couldn't bear—I couldn't—"

"Never," Wendell said. "I would never."

* * *

She worked that night in the back room of her shop, after walking Sefa home through streets gone strange with curfew-silence, after checking the wards on the doors and windows twice and a third time for luck. The child slept on the cot by the cold hearth, curled around the second Ember Stone like a kitten around a coal, and in her sleep she murmured covenant-speech, soft and ceaseless, a fountain running backward.

On the workbench, Wendell laid out her materials by lamplight. The lock of hair. A length of swan-bone, hollow and white, bought years ago from a fowler who owed her and never asked questions. Her finest knives, the ones she used for work that mattered, edges honed on leather and prayer.

Swan-bone for a drowned girl. Water birds carried souls between worlds; every tradition agreed on that much, even the traditions the Sundering had outlawed. It was good bone. It was right bone.

But her hands hesitated over the first cut, and in the hesitation she heard it—the whisper she had been refusing to hear since Osric's platform, the voice she had sworn her own private oath never to entertain.

*You could do it for yourself, too,* the whisper said. It sounded like her mother. It sounded like her grandmother. It sounded, worst of all, like Mara, fifteen years younger, laughing in a kitchen that no longer existed. *One carving. One night. Just to hear—*

"No," Wendell said aloud, to the empty room, to the bones, to herself. "Not that way. Never that way."

But her hands, when they finally began to cut, were shaking, and the swan-bone gave off a faint sweet smell like a lake at dawn, and somewhere in the walls of the old house the dead stirred and listened, patient as tide, certain as debt.

Outside, far off toward the Inquisitors' Hall, a bell rang once—the signal that a patrol had found something, or someone. Wendell carved, and did not let herself wonder who.

The stone in Sefa's arms pulsed gently in the dark, keeping time with the knife.
