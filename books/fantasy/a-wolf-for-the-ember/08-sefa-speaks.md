## 8. Sefa Speaks

The dream does not begin. It is simply there, the way rain is simply there when you wake and hear it on the roof—no door it came through, no hand that opened it.

Sefa stands in a field of white grass that hums.

She knows this field. She has known it since before she had a name for knowing, back when the world was still wide enough to hold both its halves. The grass is not grass, exactly; it is the pale hair of the land itself, and each blade carries a small voice, and all the voices together make a sound like breathing, like a city asleep, like her mother's chest rising and falling in the dark of a house that burned—

No. Not burned. She must not think of the burning yet. The dream will not let her skip ahead. Dreams are like covenant-speech: they go in order or they do not go at all.

*Sit,* says the grass, in the old tongue, in the tongue under language, the one that lives in the roots of words the way roots live under stones. *Sit, little anchor. There is a woman sleeping above you who needs to see what you saw.*

Above her. Sefa looks up and finds not sky but a ceiling—grayhollow plaster, water-stained, the underside of the bone-carver's floor. She understands then that this is not only her dream. The woman with kind hands and sad eyes, the woman whose bones sing even when she pretends they don't—the woman is here too, folded into the dream like a second heartbeat. Sefa can feel her at the edge of everything, large and wary, smelling of wood shavings and tallow and old fear worn smooth as a river stone.

*Okafor,* whispers the grass, and Sefa feels the woman flinch, though flinching in a dream is a strange thing—a whole body trying to become smaller inside itself.

Sefa sits down in the white grass. She takes a breath the way the gatekeepers taught her, before the teaching ended, before the running: in through the mouth of the world, out through the soles of the feet. And she begins to speak—not Common, which is a language made of doors and fences, but the older speech, the one that opens.

---

*In the beginning there were two rooms,* she says, because that is how Grandmother Ilya-of-the-Marsh taught her to say it, in words a child could carry. *One room was the living world, warm and loud and quick to forget. One room was the dead world, cool and quiet and slow to forgive. Between the rooms stood a door, and the door was not stone. The door was a promise.*

The grass parts around her words, and the dream builds what she describes, because dreams are obedient to covenant-speech the way rivers are obedient to valleys.

*A promise needs keepers. So the first peoples chose four families—four hands, four bloodlines—and gave them the Ember Stones, four pieces of the first fire, the fire that burned at the making of things. The Stones did not belong to the families. They belonged to the door. The families only held them, the way you hold a bird: gently, ready always to open your fingers.*

Four figures rise out of the grass, tall as trees, their faces smooth as eggs. Sefa does not know their names. Names were the first thing the Sundering ate. But she knows their shapes, because shapes are carved into every gatekeeper's bones before birth. She points.

*There. The Hand of the East, keeper of the Stone of Waking. The Hand of the South, keeper of the Stone of Rooting. The Hand of the West, keeper of the Stone of Naming. And the Hand of the North—*

She stops. Her throat closes. In the dream, the fourth figure turns its smooth face toward her, and behind the smoothness, faint as a watermark, there is a face Sefa has seen above a workbench lit by one candle: sharp cheekbones, tired eyes, hands that move over bone like they are apologizing to it.

*—keeper of the Stone of Opening,* Sefa finishes, very quietly. *The Okafor line.*

Beside her, the presence that is Wendell makes a sound like a struck bell that no one else can hear.

---

The dream turns, the way weather turns.

*For nine hundred years, the door held,* Sefa says. *The living buried their dead with songs, and the songs walked them through the door, and the dead rested, and sometimes—only sometimes—they came back to the edge of the door and left gifts: warnings, blessings, the memory of how to mend a broken thing. The world was two rooms and one promise, and the promise was kept.*

Then the grass darkens from white to gray to the color of ash, and the four figures shrink down into men and women, and men and women are exactly the size where wanting fits.

*But the founders' grandchildren grew rich, and richness is a hunger that eats its own tail. They looked at the door and did not see a promise anymore. They saw a tollgate. If the dead must pass through, why should the living not be paid? If the Stones open the way, why should the way not be owned?*

Sefa watches it happen, because the dream makes her watch, because witnessing is the whole of a gatekeeper's duty and there is no looking away, not ever, not even now, especially not now.

*They came to the Hollow Gate on the longest night of the year. Four families went in as keepers. Three came out as thieves.*

The grass is black now, and the figures stand around a door that Sefa can finally see whole: an archway of woven light, older than kingdoms, humming the same note as the white grass once hummed. And the three descendants of the East and South and West reach out—not together, never together, each hoping to be first—and they *pull.*

The sound the door makes is not a sound. It is the absence of every sound at once, a hole in hearing, and Sefa claps her hands over her ears even though she knows ears mean nothing here, and the archway cracks along lines no eye can follow, and the four Stones—

The four Stones tear loose.

They fly apart like sparks from a kicked fire, four embers scattering into the dark of the world, and where each one lands, the dream marks it with a burning point that Sefa's heart memorizes against her will: *one wrapped in a shroud, one sunk beneath the weight of uncounted dead, one carried beyond the Veil by a keeper who would not surrender it, one locked in iron by the thieves themselves.* She does not understand the images yet. She only holds them, the way a child holds hot coals, because someone must.

*And the North line—the Opening line, the Okafors—did not pull,* Sefa says. *They tried to hold the door. That was their crime, in the end. They were loyal.*

The last figure, the one with the watermark face, is seized by the other three. Sefa watches them speak a word that is not a word, a curse shaped like a knot, and she watches it sink into the figure's blood, into the blood of everyone who will ever come after, and the curse says:

*Your line shall hear the dead, and the dead shall bring you nothing but danger. Your gift shall be outlawed in the country your loyalty saved. You shall hide or burn, generation upon generation, until the door fails and the world remembers too late whose hands it needed.*

The figure screams without a mouth. The scream goes down through blood like dye through water.

*That is why the bone-carver hides,* Sefa tells the sleeping woman above her, telling her the way you'd tell someone something across a wide cold river, cupping the words in both hands. *Not because she did anything wrong. Because her great-great-grandmothers did something right, and the thieves wrote the story backward so no one would ever look for the true ones. Your exile was engineered, Okafor. Your hiding place was built for you, brick by brick, by people who were afraid of what your hands could do.*

---

* * *

The dream shifts again, gentler now, the way a storm gentles when it has spent its worst.

Sefa is standing at the Hollow Gate—or what is left of it. The archway leans like a drunk against the air, its woven light fraying into threads, and through the gaps she can see the other room pressing close: faces, thousands of faces, patient and terrible, the dead who have waited nine hundred years at a door that no longer opens all the way.

They are not angry, mostly. That is the worst part. They are *tired*. They have been standing in the doorway of the world like travelers at a closed inn, and their patience is wearing thin as the veil itself, and when it wears through—

Sefa has seen what happens when it wears through. She ran from it. She is the last anchor, the last child taught the covenant-speech while it still lived in enough throats to teach, and an anchor is only a rope, and a rope is only as strong as the hands that hold it, and hers are eight years old and shaking.

*Why show me this?* asks the presence of Wendell, and her voice in the dream is not angry, which surprises Sefa; it is hollowed out, like a bone after the marrow is gone. *Why show me my own curse?*

Sefa considers the question with the seriousness of a child, which is to say completely.

*Because you keep asking why the bones whisper to you and whether you are sick,* she says. *You are not sick. You are the last hand of the North. The bones whisper because they have been waiting nine hundred years for someone who could answer.*

She reaches up—she has to reach far; the dream makes Wendell tall as a tree—and touches the tall dark shape of the woman, right where a heart would be if hearts existed in dreams, and presses into her, deliberately, the first image she was ever given, the one the dying gatekeeper pressed into her on the road with blood on her lips:

*Four embers scattered. Gather them. Reforge what was broken—not as it was, for it was built on theft—but new. A new promise. The dead will help, if asked rightly. The Stones remember being whole.*

*And me?* Wendell's voice, very small now, tree-small voice gone child-small. *What will it cost?*

Sefa does not answer, because she does not know, because the dying gatekeeper died before that part, because some prices are only named at the counter.

Instead she shows her the last thing: the Hollow Gate, leaning, fraying, the faces in the gaps pressing closer than they were a moment ago. Time in the dream is honest. The veil is thinning. Not in years. In *months*. Perhaps in weeks. The shadows in Grayhollow's alleys already stand a half-inch wrong. The dogs already bark at empty corners. The dead are already leaning on the door.

*Wake up,* Sefa says, in the tongue under language, and then, because kindness matters even at the end of worlds, she says it again in Common, clumsy and careful: *"Wake up. We have work."*

---

* * *

Sefa wakes in the narrow bed above the shop with dawn graying the window and her heart going like a trapped bird.

Below her, through the floorboards, she hears it: the scrape of a stool, the clatter of carving tools set down too fast, and then silence—the particular, listening silence of a woman sitting among her own charms and hearing them, for the first time in fifteen years, answer back.

Sefa creeps to the top of the stairs and looks down through the gap.

Wendell Okafor sits at her workbench with a knocker-bone in her palm, one of the small finger-bones she uses for yes-and-no, and the bone is trembling, and Wendell's lips are moving around a word in a language she should not know, a word Sefa put there in the dream the way you slip a coin into a sleeping traveler's pocket.

The word is *gather.*

Wendell looks up—straight at the gap in the stairs, straight at Sefa's eye—and her face is the face from the fourth figure, the watermark face, the loyal cursed face, and she says, in Common, in a voice sanded raw:

"Tell me about the plague-pit."

Sefa's stomach drops through the floor, through the earth, all the way down to the second ember, sleeping in the dark beneath the Inquisitors' Hall with ten thousand dead for company.

Because that is where the next one lies. And Wendell cannot know that. No one alive can know that—except the bones, except the dead, except whatever the dream poured into her while she slept.

Sefa comes down the stairs slowly, one step at a time, an anchor being lowered.

And outside, in the street, boots pass the shuttered window in pairs—four pairs, eight pairs, the measured tread of Wardens walking their morning rounds past the shop of the last hand of the North, who has just, in her sleep, begun to remember what her hands are for.
