## 9. The Warden's Dilemma

The evidence fit inside a tin snuffbox.

Mara Voss turned it over in her gloved hands as the lamplight of her office guttered against the rain-streaked window. A curl of bone shavings, pale as milk teeth. A thread of gray wool snagged on a nail by the back door of Okafor's shop—wool that matched the coat Wendell had worn for fifteen years, the one with the frayed left cuff she worried between her fingers when she was thinking. And beneath it all, pressed into the wax seal of an unregistered charm some informant had sold to the Wardens for three coppers and a promise: a maker's mark. A small wolf's head, its jaw open around a single tooth.

She knew that mark. She had watched it being cut into soft bone on a winter night fifteen years ago, in a room that smelled of tallow and rosemary, when Wendell had laughed and said, *There. Now even my lies will carry my name.*

Mara set the snuffbox down on her desk as though it might bite.

Outside, Grayhollow was drowning in the season's first real storm. Rain hammered the leaded glass in fists; somewhere below, the night patrol was calling the hour—*third bell, all is quiet, all is lawful*—and the words came up through the floorboards like something rising from water. All is quiet. All is lawful. She had spoken those words herself for so long they had worn smooth as river stones, and she could no longer feel their edges.

The warrant lay beside the snuffbox, half-finished, her own handwriting marching across it in the neat, merciless script the Inquisitors' Hall demanded:

*Warrant of Inquiry issued to one WENDELL OKAFOR, bone-carver, of Chandler's Row, on suspicion of unlicensed resonance, possession of proscribed materials, and harboring—*

Her pen had stopped at *harboring*. The ink had dried there, a small black wound in the page.

Harboring what? The informant had said a child. He had described her badly—a small girl, dark-skinned, silent, seen at the shop's window during curfew on the night of the storm. Children were not named in warrants unless they were charged themselves, and no one had charged a child of eight with anything. But Mara had been an Inquisitor long enough to know what happened to unregistered children found in the houses of unregistered witches. They did not burn. They were simply taken, and the paperwork used words like *relocation* and *assessment*, and no one ever asked after them again because asking was itself a kind of suspicion.

She looked at the warrant until the letters blurred.

* * *

The Hall grew quieter as the night deepened, the way a body grows quiet after the heart stops. Mara kept her lamp lit past the hour permitted to junior clerks, but no one reprimanded her; she was Voss now, Inquisitor Second Rank, and Inquisitors kept whatever hours their consciences demanded. Hers demanded everything and paid out nothing.

She opened the drawer where she should not have kept what she kept.

A ribbon, faded from red to the color of old brick. A pressed sprig of ashfen heather gone brown and brittle. And a letter, never sent, written in a younger hand—the hand she'd had before the purges taught her to write like a blade.

*Mara,* the letter began. *If you are reading this, then either I am dead or you have finally come to your senses, and knowing you it will be the second, and knowing me I will have forgiven you for it before you finish apologizing.*

Wendell had written it the week before the Sundering's anniversary, the year the third purge swept through the low districts like fire through dry grass. Three hedge-witches hanged on Wailing Bridge. A bone-singer drowned in his own bath, officially an accident. And Wendell Okafor, who heard the dead in every charm she carved, who could not stop her hands from listening even when her life depended on deafness—that Wendell had sat across the table from Mara and said, very quietly, *Run with me. There are places east of the mountains where the old language is just a language, where nobody burns.*

And Mara had said no.

Not cruelly. That was the poison of it. She had said no gently, reasonably, in the voice of a woman who believed in law the way other people believed in weather—as vast, as indifferent, as impossible to argue with. *The covenant broke for a reason. The Sundering nearly ended the world. Someone has to hold the line, Wen. If not us, who?*

And Wendell had looked at her with those dark, patient eyes and said, *You're holding a line someone else drew. Find out who drew it before you die defending it.*

Then she had folded the letter into Mara's coat pocket, kissed her once—softly, at the corner of the mouth, almost chaste, which somehow hurt worse than anything passionate would have—and walked out into the snow. Fifteen years ago. Mara had kept the letter through four postings, two promotions, and one ceremony where she swore the Inquisitor's oath with her hand on the Ledger of Ashes and felt, for one vertiginous moment, absolutely nothing at all.

She touched the letter's edge now and thought: *Find out who drew it.*

* * *

At the second bell past midnight she gave up pretending to work and went walking.

It was against protocol to walk a district alone at night without a scribe or a second blade, but protocol bent for Inquisitors the way light bent around the edge of a door. She pulled her gray hood up and went out into the storm, down the Hall's great stairs, past the memorial wall where the names of the Sundering's dead were carved so densely the stone looked diseased, and out into streets running silver with rain.

Chandler's Row was ten minutes' walk if you took the arcades, and she took the arcades, telling herself it was procedure—Inquisitors verified their targets, confirmed patterns of behavior, established routine. Telling herself she was not going to stand outside the shop of the woman whose warrant sat unsigned on her desk. Telling herself, in short, the same lies she had been telling herself for fifteen years, each one wearing the uniform of duty.

The shop was dark. Of course it was dark; it was past curfew, and Wendell Okafor had survived fifteen years in Grayhollow by being invisible, by paying her bribes and keeping her shutters closed and carving only what the law allowed—animal bone, mundane charms, luck-knots for fishermen and teething rings for babies. Mara stood under the sagging awning across the lane, rain drumming on her hood, and let herself remember the shop as it had been before it was this shop.

It had been a bakery then. Wendell had lived above it with her grandmother, the last of the Okafors to practice openly, back in the brief thaw between purges when the council pretended tolerance the way a cat pretends to sleep—with one eye open. Mara had been nineteen, newly sworn, assigned to the district as a watcher of low-level resonance. Her job had been to report. Instead she had bought bread every morning from a girl with flour on her cheekbones and bone-dust under her nails, and one morning the girl had said, *Your grandmother's ring is singing to you. You keep touching it. What did she say to you before she died?*

No one had known about the ring. No one living.

Mara had stood in that warm bakery with rain still dripping off her gray coat and understood, with the terrible clarity of the young, that her whole life had just changed shape—and that she would spend the rest of it deciding whether that was ruin or rescue.

For six years it had been rescue. Six years of stolen evenings above the bakery, of Wendell teaching her the names of bones the way other lovers taught constellations, of falling asleep to the faint chorus of charms whispering on their hooks—*warm, warm, the harbor bell, tell her the harbor bell*—and waking to rosemary and woodsmoke and the sense, unprecedented in her life, of being entirely known and entirely safe.

Then the fourth purge. Then the bakery seized. Then the choice, and the snow, and the letter in her pocket like a coal she carried ever since, too hot to hold, too precious to drop.

The shop across the lane might have been sleeping. But Mara had been an Inquisitor for fifteen years, and she knew the difference between a dark house and an empty one. This house was occupied. Behind the shuttered window, faint as breath, a candle moved—or something moved, a shadow crossing light—and she caught, just for a heartbeat, a sound that had no business existing in lawful Grayhollow.

Singing. Very high, very thin, in a language older than kingdoms. A child's voice.

And beneath it, answering, the unmistakable harmonic hum of resonant bone—not one charm but dozens, perhaps hundreds, waking in the dark like a field of flowers turning toward the sun.

Mara's hand went to her baton before her mind caught up. Every trained instinct screamed *now*—kick the door, rouse the watch, take them all, the witch and the child and the contraband singing in the dark. It would be clean. It would be by the book. It would be the thing she had sworn to do, the line she had chosen to hold, and no one, least of all Wendell, would ever be able to say that Inquisitor Voss had let love rot her judgment.

She stood in the rain and did not move.

The singing stopped. The light behind the shutter died. And the shop settled back into its disguise of darkness, and Mara Voss, Inquisitor Second Rank of the city of Grayhollow, discovered that her feet had rooted themselves to the cobblestones like something grown rather than placed.

*Find out who drew it.*

* * *

She got back to her office soaked to the skin and sat down before the warrant, and the warrant sat before her, and between them the night stretched long and pitiless.

She tried the discipline they had taught her at the Academy: reduce the matter to its elements. Fact: Wendell Okafor possesses resonant materials. Fact: she has concealed unlicensed magic for a minimum of several years, likely fifteen. Fact: a child resides in the shop, unregistered, speaking—if the informant and her own ears agreed—an outlawed tongue. Conclusion: issue the warrant. Execute at dawn. File the report. Go home. Sleep.

Sleep. She almost laughed. She poured herself the dregs of cold coffee instead and drank it like medicine.

Because here was the element the Academy's discipline had no column for: the hedge-witches on Wailing Bridge had been guilty of less. Old Mother Cass, who hung first, had kept a single singing stone for pain-craft, easing the deaths of the poor who couldn't afford a licensed physician. Mara had signed the order confirming her sentence. She remembered the weight of the pen. She remembered thinking, *the law is the law*, and she remembered—this was the part she never told anyone, the part that woke her some nights with her heart slamming—remembered that Mother Cass had looked at her from the gallows cart, through the crowd, directly at her, and had not cursed her. Had only nodded, slowly, the way one nods at a child who has not yet learned what she is carrying.

*You're holding a line someone else drew.*

Who had drawn it? She had spent fifteen years not asking, and tonight, with rain still drying in the creases of her knuckles, she finally let the question unfold. The Sundering. Everyone knew the Sundering: the founders' great sacrifice, the sealing of the Hollow Gate, the banishment of spirit-craft that had nearly eaten the world. That was the story in the primers, in the oaths, carved into the memorial wall in letters deep enough to bleed.

But Mara had read the restricted archives. She had read the founders' private correspondence, sealed for a century, and she had told herself she'd misread the tone of those letters—the triumph in them, the *gloating*. She had told herself that gatekeepers' lines were culled for public safety, not for the inconvenient fact that gatekeeper blood could reopen what the founders had locked. She had told herself the Ember Stones were destroyed, all four, though no record of any destruction existed anywhere in any ledger she had ever been permitted to touch.

Four stones. Four records of seizure. Zero records of destruction.

She put her head in her hands.

The lamp burned lower. Somewhere in the Hall a door slammed, and boots passed her threshold and faded, and the rain kept up its patient assault on the windows, and Mara Voss sat with a warrant and a snuffbox and a letter fifteen years old, and understood at last that she was not choosing between duty and love. She had been told that was the choice, all these years—sentiment or law, weakness or strength—and she had believed it because believing it made her righteous instead of complicit.

But that wasn't the choice. The choice was this: she could file the truth, and the truth would burn a child. Or she could file a lie, and the lie would make her, for the first time in fifteen years, an honest woman.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin through the rain-washed glass.

Mara dipped her pen. Her hand did not shake; that surprised her. She wrote in the neat, merciless script the Hall demanded, and the script obeyed her, and the words came out clean:

*Report of preliminary inquiry, Chandler's Row district. Subject: W. Okafor, bone-carver. Finding: trace resonance consistent with legacy contamination of stock materials; no evidence of active practice. Recommend continued passive observation. Note: informant claims regarding a minor resident unsubstantiated; no child observed. No further action warranted at this time.*

*No child observed.* Five words, and beneath them, a whole hidden world: a girl singing in the dark, a field of bone-flowers turning toward her voice, a woman upstairs holding all of it together with flour-dusted hands and fifteen years of silence. Mara signed the report, blotted it, and slid it into the dispatch tray, and felt the paper leave her fingers the way one feels a bone leave its socket—small, precise, irrevocable.

She had bought time. Days, perhaps a week, before the district reports cross-checked and someone sharper than a clerk noticed the gaps. Perhaps less. The Hall had ears everywhere, and Osric's people moved goods beneath the city the way blood moves beneath skin, and sooner or later pressure found every vessel.

She cleaned the snuffbox, wrapped the bone shavings in oilcloth, and hid them—not destroyed, never destroyed—in the bottom drawer beneath the letter and the ribbon and the heather, the archive of a woman she had been trying to bury for fifteen years and evidently could not.

Then she sat back in her chair as the Hall woke around her, clerks clattering up the stairs, bells ringing the lawful hour, and she looked at her reflection in the darkened window: a gray coat, a composed face, eyes that belonged to someone standing on a bridge between two burning countries.

She had filed the report. She had saved the child, for now. And she had done it not out of mercy—she distrusted her own mercy—but out of something colder and more certain, something that had been growing in her all night like frost on glass:

If the covenant was a lie, if the purges were theft dressed as piety, if the line she held had been drawn by thieves—then Wendell Okafor was not a criminal. She was a witness. And witnesses, in the end, were the only things Mara had ever truly been sworn to protect.

The dispatch bell rang. Down the corridor, a runner was already hurrying toward her office with a sealed folder and a face like bad news, and Mara Voss rose to meet the day with her heart split cleanly down the middle, both halves still beating.

The folder, when it arrived, bore the seal of the Inquisitor-Principal himself.

Inside, a single line: *Voss — the Okafor woman. My office has questions your report does not answer. Attend me at ninth bell.*
