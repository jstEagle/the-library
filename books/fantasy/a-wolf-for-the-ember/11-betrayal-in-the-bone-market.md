## 11. Betrayal in the Bone Market

The lanterns in the Undervault burned green, the way they always did when the tide came up through the old cisterns and stirred whatever slept in the water below. Osric Vane had run his trade beneath Grayhollow for nineteen years, and he had learned to read those lights the way sailors read weather. Green meant patience. Green meant the night would be quiet, that the smugglers' boats could slip along the flooded galleries with their cargo of charms and relics and unlicensed hope, and no one above would ever know.

Tonight the lanterns burned green, and Osric did not believe them.

He sat at his counting table with a ledger open before him and a cup of plum brandy he had not touched, because the brandy was for steadying hands and his hands needed to shake. Shaking was honest. Steadiness, tonight, would be a lie he told himself with his own body, and he had already told himself enough lies for one evening.

They had Tam.

He turned the thought over the way he turned a coin suspected of being clipped, looking for the flaw in it, the trick of it. They had taken the boy three days ago—not arrested, nothing so clean as arrest. Warden-Sergeant Corvel had come to Osric's own front door, in daylight, hat in hand like a neighbor come to borrow a ladder, and had said, *Your son's been seen in the company of unlicensed practitioners. We've taken him in for questioning. He's a minor. He can't be held long. Of course, questions go faster when parents are cooperative.*

Tam was eleven years old. Tam had never been in the company of anyone; Tam mended nets on the Saltquay and collected river-glass and had a gap in his smile where a tooth had come out crooked. The charge was fiction, and both men at the door had known it was fiction, and that was the whole point of it. It was not an accusation. It was an invitation.

Osric had not invited them in. He had stood on his own threshold with the harbor wind pushing at his back and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the moment of ruin, that the door of his house and the door of his life were the same door, and it had been open to them all along.

*Cooperative,* Corvel had said, and smiled with the bottom half of his face.

Now Osric sat in the Undervault among his lanterns and his ledgers and his drowned-green light, and he made himself look at the thing squarely, because a man who could not look at what he was about to do had no business doing it.

Wendell Okafor would move tomorrow night. He knew her route because she had paid him to know it—paid him well, paid him in coin and in silence, the two currencies that mattered down here. She would take the child out through the Tanner's Cut, along the low gallery where the ceiling wept, past the shrine some forgotten stonemason had carved into the living rock two hundred years ago—a woman with her hands open—and up the stair into the coal yard behind the chandler's on Mire Street. From there, north, toward Ashfen, toward whatever mad errand had put that light in the bone-carver's eyes when she'd come to him asking after passage.

He had carved her daughter's face for her once, Osric remembered. No—that was wrong. She had carved *his* daughter's face for *him*. Little Mira, drowned nine years ago in the flooding of the Salt Row, and Wendell Okafor had sat across this very table with her scarred hands steady and given him back his daughter's face in bone, and when he'd held it, he had heard—God help him, he had *heard*—a small voice saying *Papa, the water wasn't cold, Papa, I wasn't scared.* Whether it was truly Mira or only his own grief speaking through good craft, he had never decided, and he had guarded the not-deciding like a candle flame in wind. Because if it was true, then everything the Wardens burned was true, and if it was false, then he had built nine years of surviving on a kindness, and either way he could not afford the answer.

That was the bargain he'd struck with himself, the night she gave him the carving: he would keep her secret, and she would keep his daughter's voice, and neither of them would ask the other to prove anything.

And now here was Corvel's invitation sitting on the table beside his ledger like a second ledger, the kind written in a column he couldn't refuse to total.

* * *

The Warden station on Candlerigge Row had been a granary once, and it still smelled faintly of grain gone sweet with rot, as though the building itself remembered better uses. They brought Osric through the side door, past the holding cells, and he made himself look through the bars because he owed Tam that much—to see him, to let the boy see him, so the boy would know his father had come.

Tam sat on a cot with his knees drawn up, and when he saw Osric his face did something terrible: it didn't light up. It went careful. Eleven years old, and his son had already learned the expression of a prisoner calculating what his father's visit might cost.

"Da." Just that. His voice hadn't broken yet; it cracked in the middle of the single syllable like thin ice.

"They're feeding you?" Osric asked, which was a stupid question, which was the question fathers asked in stories, and Tam nodded the way children nod when they know the answer matters more than its truth.

"He can go home tonight," said Corvel, who had appeared at Osric's shoulder without sound, a talent Osric supposed they taught alongside the gray coats and the burning. "Pending resolution of certain inquiries."

"What inquiries."

Corvel smiled his bottom-half smile and gestured down the corridor, and Osric followed because there was nowhere else in the world to follow.

The sergeant's office was small and tidy and had a window, which in a Warden station was either a courtesy or a message. On the desk lay a map of the undervaults—not a guess, not a sketch, but a survey, inked in a careful clerk's hand, marking galleries Osric had believed known only to him and the dead. Someone had talked, somewhere, sometime; the machine ground slowly but it ground everything. One gallery was circled in fresh red ink. The Tanner's Cut.

"We have reason to believe," Corvel said, "that a licensed artisan of our acquaintance has been moving contraband resonance through your—establishment. A woman. Bone-carver. Okafor." He said the name lightly, tasting it. "There's a child involved. Unregistered. Possibly foreign. Possibly worse than foreign." He tapped the red circle. "Tomorrow night, by our reckoning. We could take her on the street, of course. Messy. Public. The district's fond of her—shopkeepers, grandmothers, the sort of people who leave flowers where hedge-witches are burned, which is sedition of its own kind, really. A quiet seizure below the city would be cleaner. For everyone." He looked up. "But we don't know her route. You do."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." Corvel opened a drawer and took out a paper—not a warrant, Osric saw, just a plain sheet, unsigned, blank except for a heading. *Statement of Cooperation.* "Here's what happens next, Vane, and I'll say it once, so attend. If you say nothing, we take the woman on the street anyway, publicly, and we also take the child, and we also take you—we've wanted your operation for years, and martyrdom suits you poorly, you'd die badly and your people would scatter like pigeons. And the boy stays here while we sort out the charges against him. Boys age in places like this. You've seen it." He set the pen down on the blank paper, aligned it neatly with the edge, a tidy man finishing a tidy thought. "Or you tell us the route, we take her quietly below, no mob, no fire, no spectacle—and the boy walks out tonight, and your name never appears in any file, and maybe—" and here Corvel leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost sincere, which was the most frightening register he owned, "—maybe nobody burns at all. Maybe she comes in quiet, answers questions, takes whatever registration they offer her now that things are—changing. There's talk the Council's reconsidering the old edicts. Mercy, Vane. That's all this is. Mercy, moved along by practical men."

Osric looked at the blank paper. He thought: *there is no talk of reconsidering the edicts, or if there is, it is talk like mine will be—words said to make a man sign.* He thought: *they will not register her, they will burn her, and the child—what does a machine want with a child except to feed it in?*

He thought about Mira. Not the drowning—he never let himself think about the drowning—but the carving, upstairs in a box lined with wool, the small bone face that might speak or might not, the kindness he had never tested because testing it would cost him either his grief or his faith, and he could not survive losing either.

He thought: *I am telling myself a story right now. I am standing in the story of a merciful man, and the story is warm, and I should get out of it while I still can.*

Then he thought about Tam's careful face behind the bars, the face of a boy learning early what things cost, and every story in him collapsed into a single sentence: *not my son.*

"Low gallery," Osric heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's, someone older, someone who had already done this. "Tanner's Cut. Past the weeping ceiling. There's a stair up behind the chandler's on Mire Street. She'll have the child with her. She'll be armed with nothing but bone charms, and half of them are luck-charms that only work if you believe in them." He swallowed. "She'll go quietly if you let the child stay close. She won't fight you. She's not a fighter. She's a carver."

Corvel wrote it all down in his clerk's hand, neat as scripture, and pushed the statement across the desk, and Osric signed it, and his signature came out steady, which was the last lie his body told that day.

* * *

They released Tam at dusk. The boy came out of the station squinting against the ordinary evening, the lamplighters working Mire Street, the smell of frying fish from the corner cart, the whole unremarkable miracle of a city that did not know what had been traded beneath its feet. Tam took his father's hand without being asked, which he hadn't done in two years, being eleven and too old for it, and Osric nearly came apart right there on the cobbles.

"Did I do something wrong?" Tam asked. "In there. They kept asking me about bones. Did I touch somebody's bones?"

"No," Osric said. "You didn't do anything. You never did anything."

"Then why—"

"Because sometimes," Osric said, and found he had no ending for the sentence. Sometimes what? Sometimes the world runs on leverage. Sometimes good men are the load-bearing walls of wicked buildings. Sometimes mercy is a coat that cruelty wears when it wants to be welcomed indoors. "Sometimes grown men are cowards," he finished, "and their sons pay the toll."

Tam considered this with the grave economy of children. "Are you a coward, Da?"

Osric laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked sound like a boat hull taking on gravel. "Tonight," he said, "I'm the bravest coward in Grayhollow."

They walked home along the quay. The tide was coming in. Somewhere under their feet, in the green-lit dark, water moved through galleries that had carried worse cargoes than fear, and Osric walked above his own betrayal like a man walking over his own grave, listening for it to answer.

* * *

He did not sleep. Near midnight he went down to the Undervault alone, because he could not bear the sound of Tam breathing in the next room—breathing bought, breathing paid for—and because there was one thing left he could do that was not nothing.

At the shrine in the low gallery—the woman with her hands open, carved into the living rock by some mason who had believed in something—he knelt, which his knees protested, and he set the bone carving of Mira's face against the stone, and he spoke to the dark the way unbelievers pray: hedging, bargaining, ashamed.

"If she gets out," he said, "if she slips your net somehow, Corvel, if the route changes, if the child stumbles, if anything in this rotten arrangement goes sideways—I'll burn my own boats first. I'll flood the Cut myself before I let them use it twice." And then, quieter, to the older dark beneath the newer dark, the dark that had listened to nineteen years of whispered cargo: "And if there's anything down here that hears—if it's really her voice in this bit of bone, Mira, love, if you're anywhere—then forgive me. Or don't. But watch her. Watch the bone-carver and the little one. Do what you couldn't—"

He stopped. He could not finish that sentence. Some sentences, once spoken, become true, and he was not yet ready to be the man who had failed two daughters.

The green lanterns guttered, all together, though there was no draft in the low gallery. Osric told himself it was the tide.

It was easier to tell himself it was the tide.

He gathered his keys, his coat, his ledger of debts both kinds, and climbed the stair toward the coal yard and the ordinary lying city above, and behind him the shrine woman kept her hands open, offering nothing, receiving everything, the way shrines do, the way the dead do, the way the betrayed do.

Tomorrow night, Wendell Okafor would walk the Tanner's Cut with a child who spoke the language under language, and Osric Vane would be waiting at the top of the stair with a lantern and a lie on his face—or he wouldn't. He had until moonrise to decide which man he would be, and he already knew, in the place where he kept Mira's voice and his own shame in the same small box, that he had made his choice three days ago at his own front door, hat in hand, door open.

But the dead, he told himself, the dead keep their own ledgers.

And somewhere in the flooded dark, something turned a page.
