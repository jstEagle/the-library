## 12. Flight Through the Undervault

The knock came at the hour when Grayhollow's lamps burned lowest—three strikes, iron-shod, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned to count.

Wendell was awake. She was always awake now, it seemed, with Sefa asleep in the back room and the two Ember Stones wrapped in wool beneath the floorboards, singing their low twin note into her teeth. She had learned to sleep beside that song the way one sleeps beside a river: never quite, never wholly.

"Back door," she breathed, already moving.

Sefa sat up in the dark, eyes open, unafraid in the way of children who have already seen the thing adults fear. She did not ask questions. She had not spoken a word of Common since the night she arrived, but she understood Wendell's hands, her silences, the grammar of urgency. The girl slid from the cot barefoot, silent as spilled water.

The front door shuddered under the second round of knocking. Then came the sound Wendell had dreaded for fifteen years: splintering wood, the wet crack of a ram finding its rhythm.

She pressed her palm flat against the floorboard over the stones—one heartbeat of apology, one of promise—and took Sefa's hand instead. The back room's window opened onto the drainage channel behind Tallow Row, a brick throat barely wide enough for a grown woman, running down toward the old cisterns where the city kept its shame and its water in equal measure.

Cold water to the ankles. The stink of tallow and rot and something older underneath, something mineral and dead. Wendell lifted Sefa onto her hip—the child weighed almost nothing, all ribs and stubbornness—and waded into the dark.

Behind them, above them, the shop door gave way. Boots. Lantern-light spilling yellow through the window they'd left open, painting the water gold for one terrible moment.

"Hold there!" A voice, young, uncertain. "In the name of the Inquisition—"

Wendell ran.

* * *

Sefa counted the woman's heartbeats through her ribs. It was how she knew things now—had always known things, maybe, though before the Gate she hadn't known she knew. The woman's heart beat fast and hard, like a bird held too long in two cupped hands, but it did not stutter. That was how Sefa understood they would live. Hearts that stuttered were hearts that had already begun to stop.

The tunnel smelled of everything the city wanted to forget. Sefa liked it better than the city itself. Cities lied with their faces; tunnels told the truth with their smell.

Below them, far below, something answered her. Not words. Not yet. A pressure, like a hand laid flat against the sole of her foot through six layers of stone, patient as winter.

*They are coming,* said the pressure. *The ones who smell what you carry.*

Sefa tightened her arms around Wendell's neck and spoke into the dark—not aloud, because her mouth had forgotten how, but in the old way, the way that made the bones in Wendell's satchel hum faintly even through the wool.

*I hear you. Show us the way that isn't a way.*

The dark considered this. Somewhere ahead, water changed direction.

* * *

Wendell felt the change before she saw it—a current against her calves where no current should be, pulling left. She followed it on faith and found the seam: a junction where three brick throats met around a dry channel choked with a century of silt, and at its end, half-drowned in shadow, an iron hatch with Osric's mark stamped into it—a fish swallowing a key.

He'd shown her once, years ago, drunk on plum brandy and grief, pointing at the map he kept tattooed inside his own forearm. *"If they ever come for you, Wendell-girl, you go down past the third cistern and you knock twice and once. But pray you never need it."*

Prayers were for people the world hadn't noticed yet.

She set Sefa down, knocked twice and once. The silence afterward lasted long enough for her to hear the hounds.

Not dogs. Hounds. There was a difference, and every bone-carver in Grayhollow knew it: the Wardens' beasts had been bred in the years after the Sundering from stock that had once been fed on resonance, and they did not track scent so much as they tracked *song*. They could hear an Ember Stone the way a deaf man hears thunder—through the floor, through the blood.

The song beneath the wool grew louder. Wendell pressed her whole body over the satchel, absurdly, as if her spine could muffle stone.

The hatch opened six inches. One eye, gray-rimmed, red-shot. Osric.

"You absolute fool," he whispered. "You brought it *here.*"

"They were burning my door down."

"So you brought the fire to my house instead." But he was already hauling the hatch wide, dragging them both through into lamplight and the smell of tar and river-mud, and slamming it behind them. His hands shook. Wendell noticed his hands shaking and understood, with a cold drop in her stomach, that Osric's hands never shook.

* * *

The undervault was not what Sefa expected. She had imagined smugglers' dens as caves; this was a cathedral that had been buried alive. Vaulted ceilings sweated moisture. Racks of contraband rose into the dark—candles made of grave-wax, bottled breath, charms wrapped in oilcloth, and everywhere, everywhere, the murmur of small magics waiting to be sold to people desperate enough to break the law and poor enough to need to.

And people. Dozens of them, curled in alcoves among the goods. Families. Sefa saw a woman breastfeeding a baby by the light of a candle that burned without heat. She saw an old man holding a carved bird, his lips moving against its wing.

*They are hiding,* Sefa thought. *All of them. The whole city is full of people hiding.*

It made her sad in a place below sadness. Above her, through the stone, the hounds were singing.

* * *

Osric moved through his kingdom like a man counting losses. "You can't stay. You understand that. An hour, maybe two, while I decide—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "They have my son, Wendell."

The words fell into the room like stones into still water.

"I know," Wendell said quietly. "I've known since the market. Since you started flinching every time a lantern passed."

"Then why did you come?"

"Because your passage is the only one that goes under the Wall, and because—" she looked at him, really looked, at the man who had once wept over his drowned daughter's name until dawn, "—because I believe you haven't decided yet. Whatever they've made you promise. You haven't finished deciding."

Osric laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old bone. "Fifteen years and you still talk like your grandmother. Deciding. As if men like me get to decide anything." He turned away. "Rest. Feed the girl. I'll send Tamra with bread. And Wendell—" he didn't turn back, "—if Wardens come down that hatch, you run the east passage and you don't wait for me. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to keep to cowards."

"That's the only kind worth making."

* * *

Sefa slept in a nest of grave-wax candles and dreamed downward.

The dream was the Gate again, but closer now, closer than it had ever been, close enough that she could see the cracks running through it like veins through a leaf. Behind the cracks, faces. Thousands of them, pressed to the thin places, mouths open in a soundlessness that was louder than any screaming.

And threading through the crowd of the dead, moving against their press like a salmon against current, something else. Something hunting. It wore the shape of a hound the way a liar wears a smile—correctly, and only on the surface.

*It smells the stones,* the dead murmured around her. *It smells the anchor. Run, little mouth. Run, last word.*

Sefa woke with Wendell's hand already on her shoulder, shaking her, and the whole undervault ringing with a sound she realized had been building in her dream and in the world at once:

Baying. Below them. Around them. In the walls.

* * *

"They're in the drains," Tamra gasped, sliding down the ladder with a lantern swinging wild. "Three of them and a handler, Warden-gray, they—they took the north junction, they're coming—"

"How?" Osric's voice cracked like a whip. "That hatch is sealed, that route is known to four living people—"

He stopped. Everyone in the undervault stopped, the whole buried congregation going still, because everyone understood at the same moment what four people knew, and which of the four had a son in Warden custody.

Osric's face did something terrible and private. Then he straightened, and Wendell watched him become, in the space of one breath, the man he might have been if the world had been kinder—or crueler in different ways.

"East passage," he said. "Everyone out. Take the children, take the old ones, scatter to the river cells. Tamra, kill the lamps behind them." He turned to Wendell, and thrust something into her hands: a coil of oiled rope and a small carved fish, white bone, worn smooth. "Third cistern, the ladder behind the sluice-gate. It comes up in the tanner's yard outside the Wall. And Wendell—"

"Don't," she said.

"—I'm sorry," he said anyway. "For whatever I've done. For whatever I'm about to do. Go."

* * *

They ran.

The east passage was narrow enough that Wendell's shoulders brushed both walls, low enough that she ran bent double with Sefa's hand locked in hers and the satchel banging against her spine like a second heart. Behind them the baying swelled and split into harmonies—three voices, then four, then a fifth that was human and shouting orders—and the lamplight died section by section behind them as Tamra snuffed it, plunging them into a darkness so complete that Wendell ran by touch alone, by the pull of the stones in her satchel, which sang brighter in the dark, treacherous lighthouses.

Something splashed behind them. Closer than it should be.

Light flared—a handler's lamp, reflected off black water—and Wendell saw, for one frozen instant, the hound.

It was wrong. Dogs were made of flesh and appetite; this thing was made of *attention*. Its body was hound-shaped but its edges blurred, as if the world couldn't quite agree it existed, and where its eyes should have been there was only a pale luminance, empty and total, the light of a thing that had been fed on song until nothing else remained in it.

It saw the satchel. It *sang*—a high, glassy note that made Wendell's fillings ache and Sefa cry out and the Ember Stones answer, blazing through the wool, announcing them to every listening dark.

"Wendell." Sefa's voice. Her first word in weeks, hoarse, in Common, in *any* language. "Wendell, *down.*"

Wendell dropped. The hound leapt—and struck the low ceiling where her head had been, and for a moment it hung there, stunned, its borrowed shape flickering like a guttering flame, and in that flicker Wendell saw what was inside it: nothing. A hunger wearing a dog. A tool the Sundering had left lying around for the Inquisition to pick up.

She grabbed Sefa and ran.

* * *

The sluice-gate was rusted shut. Of course it was. Fifteen years of rust, and behind them the passage filling with light and song and the wet slap of paws, and Osric somewhere in the dark between them and the hunters, doing whatever a man does when he is trying to be two people at once.

Wendell put her shoulder to the wheel. It groaned. Moved an inch. Behind them, the first hound rounded the bend, lamp-light streaming off its wet wrongness.

Then Sefa stepped forward, laid both small palms flat against the gate, and *spoke*.

Not Common. Not the covenant-speech either, exactly—it was older, or deeper, the language under the language, the one the bones whispered when they thought no one listened. The sound of it went through the rust like water through sand. The wheel turned as if greased. The gate swung wide onto a shaft of night air and rain-smell and freedom, and Sefa staggered, nose bleeding, smiling.

Through the opening, Wendell looked back one last time.

Down the passage, twenty yards off, stood Osric, lantern raised, planted squarely in the middle of the corridor between the hounds and the open gate. He wasn't fighting them. He wasn't helping them. He simply *stood*, a fat old smuggler blocking a hallway, buying seconds with his body while pretending to fumble with his keys, shouting apologies to the handlers, *so sorry, sirs, the mechanism, it sticks—*

His eyes found Wendell's across the distance. He winked.

Then he turned his back on her, deliberately, theatrically, and began loudly directing the Wardens toward the west passages, and Wendell pulled Sefa through the gate and into the rain.

* * *

They climbed the ladder behind the tanner's yard with the stones singing against Wendell's back and the hounds' voices fading below, cheated, circling. Rain fell soft and gray on Ashfen Road. Ahead, the marsh country opened like a held breath.

Sefa leaned against her side, exhausted, bleeding gently from the nose, and pointed south with a small cold hand.

Wendell looked. Far off, past the last lamps of Grayhollow, past the Wall and the watchfires, lightning stood up over the Ashfen marshes—and in the lightning's white flash she saw, or thought she saw, a figure on the far road. A woman in a gray coat, riding alone, riding fast, and riding not toward the city.

Toward them.

The stones in the satchel sang a single clear note, like a bell struck once, like a name spoken kindly, like a beginning.

Wendell took Sefa's hand and walked into the dark.
