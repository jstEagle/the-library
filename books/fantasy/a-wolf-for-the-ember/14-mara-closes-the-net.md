## 14. Mara Closes the Net

The summons came at the hour when Grayhollow's lamps gutter low and even the Inquisitors' Hall seems to hold its breath.

Mara Voss read it twice, standing in the corridor outside her cell-quarters, the paper thin as a moth's wing between her fingers. *Warden-Captain Voss. Report to the Chamber of Inquiry, seventh bell. Bring your case files.* No signature beyond the seal—the inverted flame of the Order, pressed in wax the color of dried blood.

She had known this was coming for eleven days. She had counted them the way she once counted the hours until Wendell Okafor came home from the bone market, back when they were young enough to believe that love was a thing the world would permit.

*Bring your case files.*

Her case files were a lie with very good posture. Three years of reports on unlicensed resonance in the Tanners' Ward, meticulous, exhaustive, and hollow at the center like a carved gourd. She had written them herself, had built them the way a mason builds a wall—stone by stone, omission by omission—and every stone had been laid to hide one woman and, lately, one child.

She dressed in her gray coat. She checked her boots, her belt, the warrant-tube at her hip out of fifteen years of habit. In the polished steel of her breastplate she caught her own face and did not look away from it, because looking away was the first surrender, and she was not yet ready to surrender anything except, perhaps, everything.

The corridors of the Hall smelled of tallow and cold iron. Somewhere below, in the vaults, the fourth Ember Stone sat behind three locks and a warding sigil that hummed like a trapped wasp. Mara had stood before it once, early in her service, and felt it call to something in her blood she had never named. She understood now what that something was. She understood a great many things now, all of them too late or too soon.

At the door of the Chamber of Inquiry, two junior Wardens flanked her—not escorting, *flanking*, which was different—and did not meet her eyes.

Inside, the room was lit by a single brazier, because the Order believed shadow made men honest. High Inquisitor Corvane sat behind the black table with his hands folded, a man assembled entirely of angles and patience. Beside him, taking notes in a hand so small it might have been an apology, sat Under-Inquisitor Brell, who had once wept at his own mother's trial and been promoted for recovering so quickly.

"Warden-Captain." Corvane did not invite her to sit. "You've been thorough in the Tanners' Ward."

"Thank you, High Inquisitor."

"Thorough," he repeated, tasting the word. "Three years of patrols. Forty-one interviews. Six arrests—all minor offenders, all quickly processed." He turned a page. "And yet in those same three years, my diviners have recorded resonance events in your district that would fill a cathedral. Unlicensed craft of considerable power. A hedge-coven burned in Ash Row whose leader, before her death, spoke your name."

The brazier popped. Mara kept her spine straight and her face still, the way her father had taught her to stand in storms.

"I reported everything I found, High Inquisitor."

"You reported what you *chose* to find." Corvane closed the file. It was a small sound, final as a coffin lid. "Brell. Show her."

Under-Inquisitor Brell rose, apologetic as a wet dog, and spread documents across the table: surveillance transcripts, a charcoal rubbing of a sigil found on a smuggler's door, and—a knife between the ribs, this one—a sketch. An artist's rendering, drawn from a Warden's description, of a woman entering a bone-carver's shop on Vetch Lane during curfew. Gray coat. Warrant-tube. Her own jawline, her own eyes, rendered faithfully by someone paid to be accurate.

"The child," Corvane said, "was seen entering with you. Or rather, you were seen *not* seeing her. There is a difference, Warden-Captain, and my people are trained to notice it."

Mara looked at the sketch for a long moment. The girl in it had Sefa's slight frame, Sefa's way of walking close to walls. Something in her chest, which had been cracking quietly for weeks, shifted another degree, the way ice shifts on a river before it goes.

"I was conducting an inquiry," she said.

"You were conducting a *romance*." Corvane's voice did not rise; that was the terrible thing about him. He spoke of executions the way merchants spoke of grain. "Fifteen years ago you were given a choice after the Okafor matter, and you chose the Order. I signed that decision myself. I have never regretted it, because until this season you were the finest hunter I had." He unfolded his hands at last, and laid them flat. "So I will offer you the same arithmetic I offered you then. There are two ways this ends."

He slid a single sheet of paper across the black table.

"One: you are stripped of rank, tried for obstruction and complicity, and purged alongside the bone-carver and the child. Your name goes into the record beside hers. Your coat goes into the fire with the rest of the evidence." He let that sit in the smoky air. "Two: you take the warrant yourself. You find them. You bring me the woman and the girl alive—the Order wants the anchor intact—and in exchange, your omissions become clerical errors. Regrettable oversights. Diligence slightly misapplied."

Mara stared at the paper without touching it. The warrant bore her own name as hunter and Wendell's as quarry, and beneath both, in the old formal script reserved for covenant-crimes, the word *purge*.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I am sorry," said Corvane, and he almost sounded as though he meant it, which was somehow worse. "I have watched you for fifteen years, Voss. You are the best instrument this Hall has produced since the Sundering. Instruments that crack get melted down."

Outside, thunder moved over the city like something vast turning over in its sleep. Mara thought of Wendell's hands, ink-stained and bone-dusted, shaping small comforts out of death. She thought of Sefa's face the single time their eyes had met across a crowded refuge, the child's gaze ancient and frightened and *knowing*. She thought of Mother Ilya's name in the intercepted reports, of Ashfen burning on the horizon two nights past, of the pass held by an old woman against forty Wardens so that a fugitive could run.

She thought of the oath she had spoken at nineteen, kneeling on cold stone: *I will hold the line between the living world and its ruin.*

She had believed, for fifteen years, that the line ran between people like her and people like Wendell. Now she knew where it actually ran. It ran through the middle of her chest, and it was breaking, and whatever she chose, some part of her was going to be on the wrong side of it forever.

But there were sides, still. That was the mercy no one mentioned: there were always sides, and she could still choose which part of herself survived the choosing.

Corvane waited. Brell's pen scratched. The brazier breathed.

Mara picked up the warrant.

"If I take this hunt," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, cold, armored, "I do it alone. My squad reports to me and through no one else. And when I deliver them—" she met Corvane's eyes, and let him see nothing, absolutely nothing, which was its own kind of confession if he'd had the wit to read it— "the child comes to no harm. The Order wants the anchor intact. Intact means whole. Whole means unburned. I'll have that in writing."

"A condition," Corvane said, "noted and granted. The Order keeps its instruments clean."

Then let me be filthy, Mara thought, and signed her name beneath the word *purge*, and felt the ink go through her like a nail through a plank—fixing her, fastening her, making of her something that could be hung on a wall or driven into a door.

"High Inquisitor." She rolled the warrant, seated it in the tube at her hip, and saluted. "I'll need hounds. And access to the undervault maps. They went to ground through Osric's territory once; they'll trust it again."

"They fled east," Brell offered helpfully. "Toward Ashfen. Toward the marshes."

"Toward the Veil," Mara said quietly, and watched Corvane's eyes narrow, because she was not supposed to know that word, and knowing it told him exactly how deep her rot went—and exactly how useful she remained.

"Find them before the new moon," he said. "After that, the sky itself becomes evidence, and the Order stops being selective about what it burns."

* * *

She walked out of the Hall into rain.

Grayhollow in the wet season was a city of running shadows, gutters loud with water, lamplight smeared long across the cobbles. Mara pulled up her hood and walked without direction for six streets, seven, letting the rain do what rain does to a face that has been holding itself together.

At the corner of Vetch Lane she stopped.

The shop was dark. Of course it was dark; the shop had been dark for days, the shutters drawn, the little brass knocker shaped like a sleeping bird hanging crooked where someone had left in haste. But Mara stood in front of it anyway, rain sliding off her hood, and remembered it lit: warm gold through horn-paned windows, the smell of beeswax and sawdust, Wendell in the doorway with bone dust on her cheek saying *you can't come in, Mara, you know you can't,* and laughing, and meaning it, and meaning *come in anyway* underneath it the way she meant everything.

Fifteen years ago, Mara had chosen the Order over that doorway. She had told herself it was duty. She had told herself it so many times the word had worn smooth as a river stone, and only now, standing in the rain with a purge-warrant warm against her hip, did she feel the shape of the truth underneath: she had chosen survival, and called it duty ever after, the way cowards everywhere have always done.

Not tonight.

Tonight she had signed her name to a hunt, and the hunt was armor, and under the armor her heart was going on cracking, joint by joint, and she would wear the cracks like a fitted coat because that was the only way to keep walking.

Because here was the arithmetic Corvane didn't understand, the sum she'd carried since the night she'd filed her partial report and omitted a child: the Order would send someone regardless. If not her, then Halloway with his fire-pikes. If not Halloway, then the diviners, the hounds, the vault-breakers. The machine did not stop because an operator loved the cargo. The machine stopped only when someone inside it put their body against the gears.

So she would hunt them. She would hunt them better than anyone in Grayhollow could, because she was the best instrument the Hall had produced since the Sundering, and because she alone knew where they were truly going—not east to Ashfen, not to the marshes, but to the Hollow Gate itself, to the place where the world's oldest promise was waiting to be kept or broken.

And somewhere on that road, between here and the Gate, between duty and ruin, she would find the moment. The hinge. The instant when putting her body against the gears would matter more than any report, any warrant, any word she'd ever sworn on cold stone.

She turned up her collar and walked toward the kennels, where the hounds that smelled magic were already singing in their cages, low and eager, a sound like the dead-bones must sound, like the covenant itself fraying thread by thread.

Behind her, lightning stitched the sky above the Inquisitors' Hall, and for one white instant the whole city showed its bones: rooftops like ribs, spires like needles, the Hollow Gate far to the north a pale seam in the hills, older than kingdoms, waiting.

Mara did not look back at the seam. She had spent fifteen years learning to walk toward things that frightened her.

But her hand, without permission, rose to the tube at her hip—to the warrant, to the word *purge* in her own handwriting—and rested there the whole way, the way a hand rests on a wound, the way a hand rests, once, on a lover's door before knocking or turning away.

The hounds began to sing louder as she approached.

Good, she thought. Sing. Lead me true.

She had a hunt to lose.
