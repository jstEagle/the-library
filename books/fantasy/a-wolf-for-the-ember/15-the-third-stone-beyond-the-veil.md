## 15. The Third Stone Beyond the Veil

The marshes of Ashfen did not want them there.

Wendell felt it in the way the ground gave beneath her boots—sullen, waterlogged, reluctant to hold her weight. The reeds whispered against each other in a dialect of suspicion, and the fog that rose from the black water had a texture to it, almost like wool, almost like a shroud. Somewhere behind them, Grayhollow smoldered in memory: Ilya's pyre still burning on the ridge, the pass still smoking, Sefa's small hand torn from hers by Warden gauntlets.

She did not let herself think of that. Not yet. Thinking of it would unmake her, and she could not afford to be unmade. Not with two Ember Stones riding against her sternum in a leather pouch, singing their low, patient song. *Find the third. Find the third. Find the third.*

Mother Ilya's last instructions had been given in gasps, blood on her lips, her ancient hands pressed flat against the earth of the pass as though she could hold the whole world together by will alone. *"The Veil is thin at the Drowned Chapel,"* she had rasped. *"Where the fen swallowed the steeple. You'll know it by the bell that rings underwater. Cross at moonset, girl. And Wendell—"*

Wendell had leaned close.

*"Count what you pay. The dead over there will take anything you offer. Anything. And they never give change."*

Now, three days later, she stood at the edge of a black pool where the fog parted like a curtain drawn back by careful hands, and she heard it: a bell, tolling beneath the water. Slow. Deep. Each peal sent ripples across the pool that moved *against* the wind, spreading outward from some center that was not a center, that was everywhere at once.

The drowned chapel.

She could just make out the steeple's tip breaking the surface, black stone furred with moss, tilted at an angle that hurt the eye. The rest of the chapel lay below—pews, altar, the bones of whatever congregation had been caught inside when the fen took it, centuries ago. The dead of Ashfen did not rest. They waited.

Wendell sat on a hummock of peat and took stock of herself the way she took stock of a difficult carving: what material was here, what could be worked, what would break.

She had the two stones. She had Sefa's teachings, the covenant-speech syllables the child had pressed into her mind through shared dreams—*the language under language*, words that tasted of smoke and milk and old rain. She had her craft, her hands, her stubbornness. And she had her memories.

That was the currency. Ilya had been clear. The spirit realm did not trade in coin. It traded in *self*—in the weight of what a person had lived. One year of memory for one step across the Veil. The chapel's threshold lay perhaps thirty paces out, beneath the black water, at the base of the steeple where the door still hung on rusted hinges.

Thirty years.

Wendell was forty-one years old. Thirty years was nearly everything. Her hands began to shake, and she pressed them flat against her knees until the trembling stopped.

*You don't have to take every step yourself,* the bones had whispered to her that morning—all the small charms she still carried, the knuckle-bone and the sparrow's wishbone, her oldest companions. *The dead can carry you. For a price.*

But she had seen what the dead carried looked like. She had seen the hollow-eyed faithful in Grayhollow's hidden chapels, the ones who'd bartered scraps of themselves for small mercies until there was barely anyone home behind their faces. No. If she was going to pay, she would pay deliberately. She would choose what to give, or she would die trying.

Because that was the other thing Ilya had told her, the thing that had kept her walking through the fen with a cracked rib and a fever: *the stone beyond the Veil is guarded. Not by monster or ward, but by a shade who has reason to hate you. Your grandmother's shade, Wendell. The Okafor line broke the covenant's keeping, and the dead do not forget.*

* * *

Moonset came slow, the moon sinking through the fog like a coin dropped into milk.

Wendell stripped off her boots, her coat, her knife—everything but the pouch of stones and the small bones. She waded into the pool. The water was not cold. That was the first wrongness: it was the temperature of blood, of a bath someone had just left, and it clung to her skin with an intimacy that made her want to scream. The bell tolled again, and she felt it through the soles of her feet, through the water, through the cage of her ribs.

At the fifth step, the world changed.

There was no dramatic tearing. The Veil did not roar open. It was more like walking from one room into another where the light is slightly wrong—where shadows fall from a different sun, where the air tastes faintly of every fire that was ever lit. The drowned chapel was suddenly *around* her, whole and intact, water and not-water, the pews rising on either side of an aisle floored with silt and starlight. Candles burned underwater without flickering. And everywhere, everywhere, the dead.

They stood in the pews. They stood in the aisles. They hung like reflections in the black glass of the walls. Hundreds of them—fen-folk, plague-dead, the drowned of a hundred years of Ashfen's swallowing. They did not reach for her. They did not threaten. They simply *watched*, with the terrible patience of things that have all the time that was ever minted.

A voice like silt settling spoke from everywhere: *What do you bring?*

"Myself," Wendell said. Her voice came out strange, doubled, echoing a half-beat behind itself. "I've come for the third Ember Stone."

*Nothing is given here,* said the dead. *Everything is traded. What will you pay for passage?*

She had rehearsed this. She had stood on the peat hummock for hours, sorting through her life like a bone-carver sorting stock, deciding what she could bear to lose. And now, standing in the drowned chapel with the dead arrayed before her, she found the sorting was harder than she'd imagined. Every memory she reached for turned in her hands like a carved charm, whispering its own argument for staying.

"I'll pay a year for each step," she said. "But I choose which years."

*The dead accept,* said the congregation, in one voice, with one breath. *Begin.*

The aisle was thirty paces long. At the far end, past the altar, past the drowned candles, an archway of black stone led deeper—down, or inward, to some chamber where something sang. The third stone. And before it, she knew without knowing how, a figure waited. Small. Straight-backed. Wearing a shawl Wendell had last seen folded over a chair in a kitchen that smelled of anise and woodsmoke, thirty years gone.

*Grandmother.*

Wendell took her first step.

The memory came loose the way a tooth comes loose—horribly, easily, with a small wet sound only she could hear. Her twelfth year. The whole of it, lifted out: her mother's laughter in that year, the first knife her grandfather gave her, the summer the river flooded and she'd been sick for a month and her grandmother had sat by her bed carving a little wooden wolf, saying, *There. Now the fever has somewhere else to live.*

Gone. Not faded—*gone*, a clean absence shaped like a year, and she staggered, gasping, the chapel tilting around her. The dead murmured approval. Somewhere, the bell tolled, and the tolling was *inside* her now, ringing in the hollow where the year had been.

*Twenty-nine,* said the dead, counting with her.

She walked.

The second year was her thirteenth: her first successful carving, the first time a bone had whispered to her and she'd whispered back, the terror and the joy of it, her grandmother's hand on her shoulder saying *you are the keeper's blood, child, whatever they tell you in Grayhollow.* She gave it up and felt the shape of the gift remain without the story of it—she still knew she could carve, still knew the bones spoke, but the *beginning* of it was a smooth blank, a river stone where a room had been.

The third year took her first love—a boy named Tam with flour on his hands, a baker's son, a kiss behind the granary that tasted of yeast and daring. She wept as she walked, and the dead collected her tears in their cupped hands like offerings, and she hated them, and she kept walking.

*Twenty-six.*

By the tenth step she had learned the trick of it, if it could be called a trick: to hold a memory lightly as she offered it, to look at it once, fully, the way you look at a finished piece before it leaves the shop, and then to let it go *cleanly*. Clinging made it worse. Clinging left ragged edges. The dead took the ragged edges too, and charged extra.

She gave her fifteenth year and her sixteenth. She gave the year of the first purge, when the Wardens came to the Okafor door and her father stood in the entryway with his arms spread and his voice steady, lying beautifully, buying the family another decade of safety with words. She gave the year her mother taught her to make anise tea, and the year her mother died—no. She *tried* to give the year her mother died, and the dead took it, and she screamed, because she had not meant to offer that one, had meant to keep it, and the dead said, in their silt-voice, *you reached for it. In this place, reaching is offering.*

After that she walked with her hands clenched at her sides, her eyes fixed on the archway, giving years like a miser giving blood—counting each one, mourning each one, hoarding the ones that mattered most for the steps that remained.

*Fifteen.* The year she opened the shop. *Fourteen.* The year of the great storm, when half of Grayhollow drowned and she'd pulled a Warden from the canal without knowing his face, and he'd never known what she was. *Thirteen.* The year she carved the charm that saved the cooper's daughter from a fever. *Twelve.* The year she met—

No.

She stopped, one foot raised over the black water of the aisle, and the whole chapel held its breath.

The year she met Mara Voss. The year of the rain-slicked steps of the University library, of a gray coat and a laugh that Wendell had not known the law could hold, of a shared umbrella and a shared bottle of plum wine and the slow, astonished discovery that the person across from her was the person. Her twenty-ninth year. The first year of Mara.

*Twelve,* said the dead. *Twelve steps remain. What will you pay?*

"I'll pay another," Wendell said. Her voice cracked. "But not that one. Not yet."

*Then choose another.*

She chose her thirty-fourth year—the year the loneliness had grown teeth, the year after Mara left for the Inquisition, the year Wendell had sat alone in the shop every night silencing the bones and telling herself this was safety, this was enough, this was the life she'd chosen. It was, in its way, a mercy to lose. But when the dead took it, they took with it the *reason* she'd built the silence, the whole architecture of her exile, and she felt something in her understanding tilt, some load-bearing wall of herself groan.

She kept walking. *Eleven. Ten. Nine.*

She gave the year she'd watched the hedge-witch burn. She had meant to keep that one—had thought its pain was the engine of her resolve—but the dead reached for it hungrily, and she let it go, and found that without it the guilt remained but the *specificity* was gone: she knew she had failed someone, once, terribly, but the face was smoke now, the scream a bell-tone. She understood, suddenly and horribly, what the hollow-eyed faithful had purchased. Not peace. Erasure with a conscience attached.

*Eight. Seven. Six.*

The archway was close now. Through it she could see a small round chamber, and in the chamber a stone that glowed like a coal breathed on too long, and before the stone a figure in a shawl, hands folded, waiting with a patience older than patience.

*Five,* said the dead. *Five steps remain. What will you pay?*

And Wendell, forty-one years old and thirty-six years lighter, stood in the aisle of the drowned chapel and took inventory of what was left. Her childhood: mostly gone, a smooth blank from twelve to eighteen. Her mother: a warm shape without a face. Her craft: present but rootless, a river with its source erased. Mara: the first year, held like a coal in a closed fist. The last fifteen years of the shop, of the hiding, of the small daily courage of it—still hers, mostly, because she'd been saving them, rationing herself, and now the ration was nearly spent.

"I have five years left to give," she said, "and I mean to reach the stone."

*Then pay,* said the dead, *and be quick. The veil thins in the living world too, bone-carver. Your city's dead are pressing. They grow impatient.*

She paid her twenty-second year—the year of the fever that nearly killed her, the year Ilya's name first reached her in a smuggled letter. She paid her thirty-eighth, the year of the quiet bribes, the year of learning the Warden patrol schedules by heart. She paid her fortieth, the year before Sefa came, the year the bones had begun to sing in their sleep, the year the silence in the shop had grown so complete she'd talked to the furniture.

*Two,* said the dead. *Two steps remain.*

She stood before the archway. Behind it, her grandmother's shade had not moved, but the shawled shoulders had shifted, and Wendell would have sworn the dead woman was watching her with an expression she could not read—grief, or pride, or the terrible arithmetic of a keeper watching her line pay down its debt.

"One more step to the threshold," Wendell said. "And one more beyond it. I have two years. I pay them both."

*Which two?*

And here Wendell did the hardest thing she had done in the chapel, harder than the walking, harder than the letting-go. She opened her fist. She looked, one last time, at the year she'd guarded through twenty-eight steps—the rain-slicked steps of the library, the gray coat, the laugh, the umbrella, the plum wine, the beginning of the only person who had ever—

She looked at it. Fully. The way you look at a finished piece before it leaves the shop.

"I pay the year I met Mara Voss," she said, "and the year I lost her."

The dead were silent. The bell did not toll. Even the congregation in the pews seemed to lean forward, the way crowds lean at an execution, or a wedding.

*You understand,* said the silt-voice, almost gently. *When you leave this place, you will not know her. You will pass her in the world and feel nothing. Is this the price?*

"It's the price," Wendell said. "But hear me, dead of Ashfen, and mark it well." She lifted her chin, and her voice found the covenant-speech, Sefa's syllables, the language under language, and the whole chapel shivered at the sound of it. "I pay this willingly, and I pay it *forward*. Whatever the new covenant is, whatever we forge at the Hollow Gate, it will not run on stolen years or stolen children. It will run on what is *given*. So witness this: I give. First of the new covenant. Witness it and remember."

The bell tolled once, so deep and so near that her bones rang with it, and the dead said, in one voice, with something that might have been awe: *Witnessed.*

The memories came out of her like two long breaths. She felt them go—the library steps, the gray coat, the laugh, and then the other year, the bitter one, the gray coat again but different now, Mara's back receding down the Inquisition's marble hall, the door closing. She felt the shapes of the absences settle into her like stones into a riverbed, smooth and clean and wrong.

She did not know who Mara Voss was.

She knew she had loved someone, once—the way you know a room was painted a color you can no longer name. She knew it from the hollow. She walked through the archway.

* * *

The chamber was round and small, and the third Ember Stone sat on a pedestal of black stone, glowing like a heart, singing like a kettle. And before it, at last, her grandmother turned.

She was as Wendell had carved her a hundred times in memory: small, straight-backed, eyes like wet river stones, the shawl the color of rust. But her face was not angry. That was the cruelest surprise. It was *tired*—tired the way only the dead are tired, tired of waiting, tired of guarding, tired of a debt thirty years in the paying.

"You came the long way," her grandmother said. Her voice was anise and woodsmoke. "Most cross the Veil and arrive at my door in three steps. You walked the whole aisle."

"I chose what to pay."

"I know." The shade's eyes went over her, cataloguing, the way she'd catalogued Wendell's carvings a lifetime ago. "You gave cleanly. Ilya taught you after all, from a distance. Come here, child. Take the stone. I have guarded it long enough, and my hands are tired, and the dead of this fen have been better company than my own guilt."

"You hate the Okafor line," Wendell said. "The hanged witch told me. The fracture—"

"The fracture was not ours alone." The shade's voice sharpened, and for a moment Wendell saw the keeper she must have been, the woman who had held a gate with her bare hands. "But we carried it, and we hid, and we hid, and we let the hiding become a life. That is the debt. You are paying it now, step by step. Come. Take it."

Wendell crossed the chamber. Her grandmother's shade raised one translucent hand and touched Wendell's cheek, and the touch was cold as well-water and warm as a kitchen at once, and it passed through her and did not pass through her.

"You gave the years of the woman you loved," her grandmother said. "Both of them. The meeting and the losing."

"You were watching."

"We are always watching. It is the one thing the dead are good at." The shade smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and a kind one. "Then hear a keeper's counsel, since you are the last of us: what is given is not destroyed. It is *held*. The covenant holds everything, child. That is what a covenant is—a keeping. What you paid today is not gone. It sleeps. And sleeping things can wake."

Wendell's breath caught. "Wake?"

"If the reforging holds. If the new covenant is just. If the anchor—" the shade's eyes flickered, and Wendell's heart seized, thinking of Sefa in a Warden cell, "—lives to speak the words. Then what was given may be given back. Not by the dead. By the living who remember you. That is the secret at the bottom of the old language, the one the founders never understood: memory is not currency, child. It is a *seed*. Now go. Take the stone. The veil is thinning, and somewhere a gray coat is deciding what she is."

Wendell took the third Ember Stone from its pedestal, and it was warm as a sleeping bird, and it sang against the other two, and the song had a direction now, a hunger: *north, north, to the Hall, to the vault, to the fourth.*

Her grandmother's shade was already fading, the chamber dissolving around them like sugar in tea, the drowned chapel reassembling itself into water and ruin and fog. The last thing Wendell saw was the shawl, the rust-colored shawl, lifting like a wing.

"Child," the shade said, from very far away, from nowhere, from the bottom of everything. "When you stand at the Gate and they ask the price—remember that you already paid it once, walking. What remains will be only the interest."

* * *

She woke on the peat hummock in the grey before dawn, soaked to the skin, the three stones against her sternum singing their northward hunger. She did not know why her face was wet. She did not know why, when she thought the word *gray coat*, something in her chest rang like a struck bell in an empty room—a bell with no clapper, tolling over a hollow shaped exactly like a person.

She sat up. The fog was lifting. Somewhere north, past the fen, past the ridges, Grayhollow waited with its vault and its Wardens and its captured child, and the fourth stone sang to her across the miles like a lighthouse singing to a ship.

Wendell Okafor stood, swayed, and took inventory of herself one last time: her hands, her craft, her stubbornness, three stones, a hollow where a woman used to be.

"Hold on, Sefa," she said to the fog, to the north, to no one. "I'm coming. And this time I don't walk alone."

Behind her, in the black pool, the drowned bell tolled once—soft, and patient, and *counting*.
