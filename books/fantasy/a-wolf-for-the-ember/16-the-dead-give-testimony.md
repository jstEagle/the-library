## 16. The Dead Give Testimony

Sefa dreamed the marsh awake.

That was how Mother Ilya described it afterward, in her voice like dry reeds: *You didn't cross into their country, child. You opened the door and let their country pour through.* But in the moment there was no door, no crossing, no marsh at all—only the thin place under the ash trees opening like an eye, and Sefa standing at its lid while the veil came apart around her like wet paper.

She was eight years old and she had been carrying a language since before she could carry anything else. It lived behind her sternum. It had lived there since the Hollow Gate spat her out into the living world with her voice burned down to nothing, since she walked a hundred miles of winter road mouthing words that no one alive could hear. Here, now, in the place where the world wore thin, the words woke all at once—and they were not hers anymore. They were everyone's.

*Come,* said the covenant-speech, in the voices of every dead thing that had ever loved the living. *Come and be witnessed.*

* * *

The spirit realm did not look like anything Sefa had been taught to expect. She had imagined gray fields, drifting shapes, cold. What she found instead was memory given geography: hills made of harvests, rivers that ran with lullabies, a sky the color of lamplight seen from underneath a blanket. The dead were everywhere and nowhere—she felt them as warmth against her skin, as pressure in her ears, as the particular ache of being known completely by strangers.

They gathered because the stones sang them in.

Wendell stood beside her, three Ember Stones cradled in a sling of burial linen across her chest, and even here—even in the country of the dead—the bone-carver looked like a woman who mended things for a living. Her hands were stained. Her coat was patched at both elbows. She had crossed into the Veil carrying a knife, a spool of sinew thread, and an expression Sefa had learned to read over the long weeks of flight: the look of someone about to argue with something much larger than herself.

Behind them, at the ragged edge where the marsh showed through, Mother Ilya kept watch over the thin place, staff planted, mouth moving in the old syllables. Holding the door. And beyond Ilya, faint as a bruise under skin, the living world: Grayhollow's distant smudge, the Inquisitors' Hall with its lit windows, the smell of woodsmoke and fear.

The dead came closer. Sefa felt them the way you feel weather change.

An old man made mostly of plow-work and patience. A girl who had died of fever at eleven and still carried her doll's ribbon. Soldiers. Midwives. A whole congregation of plague-dead whose faces Sefa knew without knowing how—the ones beneath the Inquisitors' Hall, the thousands Wendell had promised witness to in the dark under the memorial candles. They pressed in like a tide deciding whether to come ashore.

And above them, assembled out of the lamplight sky itself, sat the Judges.

There were nine. Sefa understood this the way she understood everything in covenant-speech—not learned but remembered, the knowledge arriving already worn smooth, as if it had always been in her. Nine figures woven from the first deaths, from the first griefs, from the moment some grieving hand first set a stone on a grave and asked the dark for an answer. They had no faces. They had attention instead, which was worse.

*The anchor speaks,* said the centermost Judge, and its voice was the sound of a word being unlearned. *Or she will. Let the last speaker of the true tongue come forward.*

Sefa's legs shook. Wendell's hand settled on her shoulder—warm, calloused, real—and did not push. Just rested there. *When you're ready,* the hand said, in the grammar of touch. *Not before.*

So Sefa stepped forward alone, into the ring of the dead, and opened her mouth, and gave back the language.

It poured out of her like water finding a crack. She spoke the covenant-speech the way rain falls—without decision, without artistry, simply because gravity and the sky required it. She told them what she was: the last living anchor, the knot at the end of the rope, the small body chosen by a dying magic because dying magic cannot afford to be choosy. She told them the Gate was failing. She told them the veil was thinner than it had been in four hundred years, that shadows were already walking in Grayhollow's alleys, that the dead were pressing against the boundary like faces against glass.

And then she stopped, because the next part was not hers to say.

* * *

*The covenant was forged in theft,* said the Judge on the left, and its voice was the sound of a ledger closing. *We remember. We have always remembered. The founders came to this place with their stones and their ambition, and they bound us—not in partnership, but in service. They took the gatekeepers' line and made it a lock. They took our names and made them fuel. Every prayer spoken under their law was a tax collected from the dead.*

Murmur moved through the congregation like wind through wheat. Sefa felt it in her teeth.

*Four hundred years,* said another Judge, and this one sounded tired, which frightened Sefa more than anger would have. *Four hundred years we have held up our side of a bargain written by thieves. The veil holds because we hold it. And now the stones scatter, the anchors die out, the living forget us entirely—and when the veil tears, they will call it our rage. Our fault. As if a wall blames the bricks.*

Wendell stepped forward.

Sefa watched her do it and thought, with the strange clarity the Veil lent to everything: *she is not afraid, or she is afraid and walking anyway, and those are the same thing here.* The bone-carver stood in the ring of the dead with her patched coat and her stained hands and three singing stones against her heart, and she looked up at the faceless Judges the way she looked at a badly carved joint—as a problem that could be worked.

"I'm going to speak Common," Wendell said. "Because part of my argument is that your language shouldn't have to translate itself for anyone ever again."

A ripple. Somewhere in the congregation, someone laughed—a short, surprised, living-sounding laugh.

"The covenant deserves to fail," Wendell said.

The murmur cut off. Even the Judges went still. Beside the thin place, Mother Ilya's chanting faltered for half a breath.

"You heard me." Wendell's voice didn't rise; it sharpened. "I've spent fifteen years hiding what I am in a city that burns people like me. I've watched friends burn. I've paid bribes to stay invisible. I've silenced bones every night of my life so no Warden would hear them sing. And I could stand here and ask you to restore what was—to put the old covenant back, the founders' covenant, with the gatekeepers as locks and the dead as fuel and the whole rotten machine running smoothly again until the next ambitious fool breaks it." She lifted her chin. "I won't. I didn't walk into hell with stolen stones to rebuild a prison."

"Then why have you come?" The centermost Judge leaned—or seemed to lean; attention has weight here. *Why does the thief's descendant stand in the thief's courtroom?*

"Because," Wendell said, "the alternative to a bad wall isn't no wall. It's a door."

She knelt, right there in the ring, and set the three Ember Stones on the ground between herself and the dead. Their light was the color of hearthfire, of banked coals, of the exact shade of safety. Sefa felt the congregation lean toward it the way the starving lean toward bread.

"My grandmother was a gatekeeper. Your records will show her line was cursed, bound, broken on purpose—because the founders feared what her family could do. I grew up thinking my exile was punishment. It wasn't. It was design. Everything I lost was engineered by men who wanted magic without obligation." Wendell's voice caught, just once, and she let it. "So here is what I propose. Not restoration. Reforging. A new covenant, written new, witnessed by everyone—the living *and* the dead, neither holding the other's leash. The stones remade into one ember that belongs to no founder's blood. The Gate open not as a wound but as a door, with keepers who choose the work instead of inheriting it. And the dead—" she spread her stained hands toward the thousands gathered in the lamplight dark, "—the dead get what you should have had from the beginning. Voice. Witness. Standing. Not worship. Not servitude. *Citizenship.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then the plague-dead began to move. Slowly, like ice shifting on a river, they turned toward each other, and Sefa heard them speaking—not in covenant-speech but in the raw material beneath it, the groan and whisper of the unconsulted. She caught fragments: *witness*, someone said. *No one asked us. Four hundred years and no one asked us.* The girl with the doll's ribbon tugged her mother's shade closer. The old farmer made of plow-work nodded, slow as seasons.

"They're listening," Sefa whispered, in Common, forgetting herself, and Wendell's hand found hers and squeezed.

* * *

*It is well spoken,* said the leftmost Judge, and its tone made clear that well-spoken and accepted were different countries entirely. *But words are the cheapest currency in either realm. The founders also spoke beautifully.*

"We know," Wendell said. "I've read their founding charter. It's engraved on the Hall in gold leaf. Very moving. Complete garbage."

Another laugh from the congregation, quickly smothered.

*What would you bind yourself to?* the Judge asked. *Name the terms. Name them precisely, bone-carver, for we are old and we are literal and we have been lied to by lawyers.*

Wendell took a breath. Sefa watched her take it and understood, with the terrible precognition of children and anchors, that whatever came next had already been decided—that Wendell had done this arithmetic somewhere on the long road, alone, in the dark, and had not shared the answer with anyone.

"Three terms," Wendell said. "First: the ember belongs to the covenant, not to any person. No bloodline owns the Gate again. Keepers are chosen by the dead and the living together, renewed each generation, and can be unmade by either."

*Murmur of assent, cautious.*

"Second: passage through the Gate goes both ways, freely, for grief and for counsel—but never for conquest. The dead may visit the living; the living may visit the dead. What happened under the founders—summoning, binding, burning the dead into weapons—is forbidden to both realms equally. The law cuts both ways or it doesn't cut at all."

*Longer murmur. The Judges confer in a language older than sound.*

"And third?"

"Third," Wendell said, and here her voice finally, finally wavered, "the covenant must be anchored in willing flesh. That was always the flaw. The founders used conscription—a captive line, forced to serve. Magic knows the difference. Magic has been straining against that lie for four hundred years, and that strain is why the veil is tearing. The new anchor must be voluntary. Chosen. Paid for honestly."

*Paid for how?* asked the centermost Judge, very softly.

And Wendell Okafor, who had hidden her gift for fifteen years, who had crossed the Veil bartering memories a year at a time, who had already sold her grandmother's face and her mother's kitchen and the sound of her own childhood name to buy three steps into this place—Wendell smiled. It was not a happy smile. It was the smile of a craftswoman looking at the true cost of good work and refusing to flinch from it.

"A life's worth," she said. "All of it. Every memory I have left. My name. My loves. My griefs. The whole self I built out of the wreckage your old covenant left me—I give it to the ember, freely, and it becomes the mortar. Memory is what holds a covenant together. So I'll pay in the only coin that matters."

"No." 

The word came out of Sefa before she knew she owned it—in Common, cracked, eight years old, furious. "No. *Nei. Nei!* You don't get to—you promised—we're supposed to—" She was crying and she didn't care. The whole congregation of the dead turned to look at her, thousands of faces, and she stared past all of them at the kneeling woman with the patched coat. "You taught me the lullabies. You said memory endures. You *promised.*"

"Sefa." Wendell's voice went gentle, the voice she used for frightened animals and frightened children and, once, in a dream, for a ghost who only wanted witness. "Look around you. Look at them. Four hundred years of nobody asking. If I keep my memories and the covenant fails, everyone I love dies anyway—including you. If I give them up, you live. You learn. You keep the door." She reached out and touched Sefa's wet cheek with a thumb, wiping a tear the way she'd wipe dust from a finished carving. "That's not a sacrifice, little anchor. That's just the job."

*The terms are heard,* said the nine Judges together, and their unified voice made the lamplight sky shudder. *The terms are... just. More just than anything the living have brought before us since the first grave.*

*But justice demands a price from both sides,* said the leftmost Judge, and now its voice had an edge Sefa didn't like at all. *You ask the dead to become citizens. Citizens can be tried. Witnesses can be compelled. If we step through your door into the world of the living, we bring our grievances with us—and some of those grievances wear gray coats and sit in marble halls. There are living people who owe the dead debts that can only be paid in reckoning. Do you understand what you are inviting, bone-carver?*

"I do," Wendell said quietly. "I'm inviting justice. Justice doesn't always knock politely."

*Then hear the final condition.* All nine Judges rose, or seemed to rise, and the entire spirit realm tilted toward the three stones on the ground like a field tilting toward the sun. *The reforging cannot happen here. The stones must be made one at the Hollow Gate itself, in the living world, at the place of the original theft—on the night when the veil is thinnest. Three days hence. Bring the fourth stone. Bring the anchor. Bring your keeper, if she chooses to come.*

*And know this:* the centermost Judge's attention settled on Wendell like a hand on her head, gentle and absolute. *Once begun, the reforging cannot be paused. Once named, the price cannot be refunded. When you carve the ember, bone-carver, you will forget why you are carving it. You will forget the child who taught you the words. You will forget the woman in the gray coat. You will stand at the Gate a stranger doing a kindness for people you cannot remember loving—and you will do it anyway, because the oath will live deeper than the memory. That is what honest payment means. Do you accept?*

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Sefa counted them. She would count them for the rest of her life.

"I accept," said Wendell Okafor.

The three stones flared. The congregation of the dead bowed—all of them at once, thousands, like wheat before wind—and the lamplight sky opened, and the Judges' verdict rang through both worlds at once:

*Then let the living prepare. Let the guilty tremble. In three days, at the Hollow Gate, the dead will come to testify—and the world that outlawed the truth will hear it whether it wishes to or not.*

* * *

They came back through the thin place into the marsh's cold and the smell of ash trees and rot, and Mother Ilya caught Sefa before her knees gave out, and Wendell stood swaying with the stones against her chest and a look on her face that Sefa had never seen before: relief and terror wearing the same expression, like two people sharing one coat.

"It's done," Ilya breathed. "Nine Judges. A unanimous—" She stopped. Her ancient eyes went from Wendell's face to the eastern horizon, where the marsh met the dark, and everything in her posture changed. "Child. How long since we crossed?"

"Hours," Wendell said. "Why?"

Ilya pointed with her staff. Far off, past the black line of the fens, the sky over the direction of Grayhollow glowed—not with dawn, which was hours away, but with the orange flicker of many torches moving in formation. And beneath the torchlight, faint but unmistakable on the night wind, came the belling of hounds.

"They voted faster than I expected," Ilya said grimly. "So did the Inquisition. Ashfen is about to burn, gatekeeper—and they're not coming for the stones."

She looked down at Sefa, and her old face was full of a sorrow she didn't try to hide.

"They're coming for the anchor."
