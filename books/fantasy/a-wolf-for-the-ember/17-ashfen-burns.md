## 17. Ashfen Burns

The smoke came before the dawn did.

Wendell smelled it in her sleep—a thin, acrid thread winding through the peat-sweet air of Ashfen, wrong the way a false note is wrong, and she woke with her heart already hammering because she knew that smell. She had known it once before, fifteen years ago, from a street corner she still could not walk past without her hands going cold. Burning pitch. Burning wards. Burning people.

She rose from the pallet beside Sefa's, careful not to wake the girl, and went to the one window of Mother Ilya's crooked house. The marsh stretched eastward under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, mist lying on the black water like a shroud laid out and waiting. And there, at the far edge of the fen where the causeway climbed toward the high pass, a line of orange light moved. Torches. Many torches. Carried in formation.

They had found them. Of course they had found them. Osric's confession had bought his son's life and spent hers, and Mara Voss—Mara, who had opened a vault door for her, who had chosen ruin with her own two hands—was somewhere inside that column of gray coats and drawn steel, or perhaps ahead of it, or perhaps not coming at all. Wendell pressed her forehead to the cold glass and let herself feel, for exactly three breaths, the full weight of what was about to happen. Then she stopped feeling it, because feeling it would not save anyone.

"Sefa." She touched the child's shoulder. "Get up, little anchor. We have to move."

Sefa came awake the way she always did—not gradually but all at once, like a struck bell, her dark eyes finding Wendell's in the dimness and reading them faster than any adult could have. She looked toward the window. She looked at the stones.

The three Ember Stones sat in their nest of wool and waxed leather on Ilya's table, and even now, even quiet, they were singing. Wendell heard it the way she heard everything the dead said: not with her ears but somewhere behind her sternum, a vibration older than music. Three voices, three notes, braided together—and beneath the braid, a pull, a leaning, like compass needles straining toward north. Toward Grayhollow. Toward the Inquisitors' Hall, and the vault beneath it, where the fourth stone lay caged in lead and law.

*Soon,* the stones sang. *Soon, soon, soon.*

"Not yet," Wendell whispered to them, as she had whispered every night since Ashfen. "Not until we're ready."

Behind her, Ilya stirred in her chair by the dead hearth. The old woman slept less every night; Wendell had watched her sitting upright through the small hours, hands folded over her cane, eyes open in the dark as though listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear. Perhaps she was. Gatekeepers heard differently than bone-carvers. Or perhaps she had simply known, the way old soldiers know, that the war was coming up the road toward her door.

"They're at the causeway," Wendell said. "How long do we have?"

Ilya's eyes were pale as frost-rimed glass, and utterly calm. "An hour, if they're cautious. Less if they've brought the hounds." She pushed herself up, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Which means you take the marsh path now, girl, and you don't look back."

"No." Wendell heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "We go together."

"You go together." Ilya nodded toward Sefa. "I stay. Someone has to hold the pass, and my holding days are done everywhere else but here." She smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile, the smile of a woman who had been exiled forty years and had finally, at the end of it, been given something worth dying for. "I trained your grandmother, Wendell Okafor. I broke the first ward on the Hollow Gate when your mother was in swaddling clothes. You think I don't know how this ends? I've known since the night you knocked on my door with a mute child and three stolen suns in your bag."

"Ilya—"

"Take the west path. It floods at midmorning, so you'll be wet to the thigh by noon, and you'll thank me for it, because hounds won't follow water and Wardens hate wet boots." She was already moving, pulling bundles of dried herbs from the rafters, crumbled chalk from a jar, things Wendell recognized with a lurch: gate-wards, threshold-craft, the oldest magic in the world done with the humblest materials. "And take this." She pressed something into Wendell's hand—a small carved token, whalebone worn smooth as river stone, shaped like an eye. "Your grandmother's. She left it with me the last time she crossed the Veil. She said—" and here the old woman's voice caught, just barely, the only crack in all that granite composure, "—she said to give it to the Okafor who finally finishes it."

Wendell closed her fingers around the eye. It was warm. Bone was never warm unless something lived in it still.

Outside, the torchline had reached the foot of the pass.

* * *

They went down into the marsh in the last dark, Sefa's small hand locked in Wendell's, the stones wrapped tight against Wendell's chest beneath her coat where their song became a heartbeat, became *her* heartbeat, until she could no longer tell which rhythm was hers. The water of the fen came up black and mirror-still between tussocks of sedge, and the mist swallowed them to the shoulders. Somewhere a bittern boomed. Somewhere else, closer, a hound gave voice—one long belling note that rolled across the water and made Sefa flinch.

"It's all right," Wendell breathed, though it wasn't, though nothing had been all right since Grayhollow burned its first hedge-witch, and Sefa squeezed her hand anyway, forgiving the lie the way children forgive everything, which is to say completely and without ever forgetting.

They were halfway across the flooded meadow when the sky above the pass turned orange.

It began as a glow, diffuse, like sunrise arriving from the wrong direction. Then it deepened, and sharpened, and Wendell understood—Ilya had fired the peat banks, the old gatekeeper's trick, the fen itself called up as a wall of slow-burning smoke—and then she heard the first scream, and knew it wasn't only peat that burned.

She stopped. Sefa bumped against her hip. And Wendell stood in the freezing water of the Ashfen marsh with a dead woman's whalebone eye in her fist and three singing stones against her heart, and she *listened*, because listening was the only thing she had ever been good for, the only gift she'd ever been given, and if Ilya was dying on that pass then Ilya deserved to be heard doing it.

The dead of Ashfen spoke to her all at once—the generations of exiles buried in the peat, the hedge-witches, the unregistered, the drowned and the starved and the quietly forgotten—and through them, threaded like gold wire through gray cloth, she felt Ilya. Not words. Gatekeepers didn't speak the way the dead spoke. But a *shape*: a doorway held open by a body standing in it. A will like bedrock. And underneath, unmistakable, unbearably tender, aimed down through the miles of mist at the two small figures fleeing through the water:

*Go. Go. Go.*

Then fire, and then silence, and then the stones sang so loudly that Wendell cried out and doubled over, because grief had cracked something open in her and the covenant poured through the crack. For one moment she saw the whole web of it—the living world and the spirit world bound at ten thousand points, every grave a knot, every memory a thread—and she saw the threads fraying, and she saw, at the center of the web, the Hollow Gate standing dark and hungry, waiting for someone to give it back its heart.

"Wendell." Sefa's voice. Her real voice, the covenant-speech, the language under language, and it cut through the roar in Wendell's skull like a blade through fog. "*Wendell.* Walk. She bought the walking. Don't waste it."

Waste it. Yes. Wendell straightened. She wiped her face with a sleeve that was already soaked, and she walked, and she did not look back, because looking back was a luxury purchased with other people's lives and she had spent enough of those.

But rage—rage came with her. It settled into her bones like marrow. It kept her legs moving when the cold took them, kept her mind sharp when exhaustion blurred the reeds into ghosts. By the time the sun hauled itself up blood-red over the eastern fens, Wendell Okafor had stopped being afraid, and that frightened part of her, the part that had hidden for fifteen years behind charms and bribes and smallness, was simply gone, burned away on a distant pass along with the last gatekeeper of the old line.

What remained was a carver. And carvers, Wendell thought grimly, wading through water that tore at her thighs, carvers know that bone always breaks along the line you choose.

* * *

They made the far shore as the morning mist burned off, and that was nearly their undoing, because the mist had been hiding them and the sun had no loyalties. Wendell saw the riders at the same moment Sefa did—three of them, gray-coated, cresting the low ridge to the south where the marsh gave way to heath, and between the second and third rider loped a shape that made Wendell's stomach drop: a warden-hound, big as a calf, nose down, tracking.

"There," Sefa said, pointing north, where a stand of black alders crowded a stream channel. "Water. Trees. Go."

They ran. The ground sucked at their boots; the stones sang against Wendell's ribs like alarm bells; behind them the hound's voice rose again, closer now, and she heard the riders shouting, heard the crack of a ward-lance charging—that high insect whine she had heard once before, fifteen years ago, from a street corner, right before—

The lance hit the alder trunk a hand's width from her head and the tree exploded into burning splinters.

Wendell threw herself and Sefa both into the stream channel. Cold water closed over them; she surfaced gasping, hauling the girl up by the collar, and shoved her toward the alder roots on the far bank. "Under the roots—there's a hollow—stay down—" Another lance shrieked overhead and detonated the water where they'd been standing, steam boiling up white.

She got Sefa to the bank. She got the girl wedged into the root-hollow, dry-ish, hidden. And then she turned, dripping, furious, three stones blazing against her chest, and faced the riders coming down through the heath.

There were four now, not three. And leading them, riding a gray mare with her coat buttoned to the throat and her face pale as paper, was Inquisitor Mara Voss.

For a moment neither of them moved. Water dripped off Wendell's chin. The hound circled at the stream's edge, whining, unwilling to enter. The stones sang. Somewhere far behind them, smoke still rose off the pass where an old woman had made her last doorway.

"Mara." Wendell's voice came out low and steady, and she marveled at it. "You're a long way from your paperwork."

"Give me the child." Mara dismounted. Slowly. Deliberately. Her hand nowhere near her weapon, her eyes wet-bright and hard as river ice. "Wendell. Please. Command has taken the hunt from me twice and given it back once. If I come back without the anchor, they send Kessler's squad next, and Kessler doesn't negotiate, doesn't hesitate, and doesn't—" her voice frayed, just once, "—doesn't love anyone in this fen."

"And if you bring her in?" Wendell spread her arms, letting the coat fall open, letting Mara see the faint amber glow bleeding through her shirt where the stones lay. "You know what they'll do with her. You've read the doctrine. An anchor isn't a prisoner, Mara. She's a *component*. They'll work her until the covenant dies and call it containment."

"I know." Barely a whisper. "Gods help me, I know."

"Then stand aside."

"I can't." Mara stepped forward, boots sinking into the boggy margin, and now her hand did go to her side—not to the lance at her saddle but to the short truncheon every Warden carried, the humane tool, the arrest tool, and Wendell understood with a strange floating clarity that Mara Voss intended to take her alive or die trying, and that this was, in the ruined currency of their situation, a declaration of love. "I filed a false report for you. I opened the vault. I am a traitor twice over as of yesterday noon, and the only thing I have left, the only thing, is that no one touches you or the girl while I'm standing—"

The ward-lance bolt came out of the heather to the east.

Later, Wendell would piece it together: Kessler's squad, flanking wide, exactly as Mara had feared; a young Warden with a shaking hand and orders he was afraid not to follow. Later she would understand that the bolt had been aimed at the glowing thing at her chest, not her heart. But in the moment there was only the whine, and Mara moving—moving *fast*, faster than Wendell had ever seen her move, fifteen years falling away like a dropped cloak—and the impact, and the two of them thrown together into the freezing shallows with the smell of scorched wool and blood.

Mara's blood. A burn across her shoulder blade the size of a dinner plate, coat smoldering, breath punched out of her. She had taken it meant for Wendell. She had taken it without hesitation, and as Wendell dragged her up out of the water, as the riders crashed down through the heather shouting, as Sefa screamed from the root-hollow in the language under language and the whole marsh lit amber with answering light—through all of it, one thought rang in Wendell louder than the stones:

*She chose. Here, now, in front of God and the Inquisition, she chose.*

"Go," Mara rasped, shoving her, shoving her hard, toward the alders and the girl. "North—there's a boat—old ferry stage, half a mile—*go*, Wendell, I'll hold them, I'm still an Inquisitor, they won't fire on—"

Another bolt. This one from close range, deliberate, and it caught the gray mare and dropped the animal screaming, and in the chaos of it—in the screaming horse and the shouting men and the amber light pouring off three Ember Stones whose song had become a roar—Wendell made the choice that would carve the rest of her life along its grain.

She ran for Sefa. She got the girl out of the roots, got her hand, got three steps.

And the hound hit them like a landslide.

* * *

Pain arrived late, the way it does in the worst moments, politely waiting its turn. Wendell came back to herself face-down in the mud with the taste of iron in her mouth and a weight on her back that resolved, slowly, into a Warden's knee. Her left arm wouldn't answer. Through the ringing in her skull she heard Sefa—heard the child fighting, really fighting, small fists and covenant-words that made the nearest Warden stagger and cross himself—and then rough hands, and rope, and the terrible ordinary sound of a child being picked up and carried away.

"No—" Wendell got one arm under herself. Got her head up. Saw Sefa slung over a trooper's shoulder like a sack of meal, saw the girl's eyes find hers over the man's back, wide and furious and *unafraid*, and saw her mouth one word in the old tongue before they turned away with her.

*Remember.*

Then the marsh, the alders, the smoke over the pass, all of it swung away like a door closing, and Wendell was alone in the mud with three singing stones and a broken arm and a rage so vast it had passed through fury and come out the other side into something crystalline and cold and absolutely clear.

They let her live. That was the message; she understood it even through the pain. Kessler wanted her followed, not taken—an anchor-carver leads to other carvers, to sympathizers, to the whole hidden web of the faithful. Let the wounded wolf run; run her to ground later. So they left her there in the shallows with her blood diluting into the fen water, and they rode south with a child, and Mara—she found Mara after, or Mara found her, one shoulder blackened, one eye swelling shut, kneeling in the mud with her warrant-case open and empty beside her, having torn it to pieces, having fed it to the stream.

"I'll get her back," Mara said. It wasn't comfort. It was a sentence being handed down, judge and defendant both. "Whatever it costs. Whatever's left of me after. I'll get her back."

Wendell looked at her—at this woman who had been her whole heart once and her enemy twice and her shield today—and she unbuttoned her coat, and she took out the stones.

Three of them, in the wrecked nest of wool and leather, glowing like coals that refused to die. And even now, even broken, even robbed, they leaned. North and east. Toward Grayhollow. Toward the Hall, and the vault, and the fourth stone singing back to them across the miles like a fourth voice in a round, patient, imprisoned, *waiting*.

"They have Sefa," Wendell said. Each word cost her; she paid anyway. "They think that means they've won. They think a child is a component and a carver is a criminal and a gate is a door you can lock." She closed her good hand around the stones, and their song rose through her arm, through her ribs, through the marrow where the rage lived, and she let them see—all three of them, all three voices—exactly what she intended.

"But they're wrong," she said, "because I'm a bone-carver. And bone always breaks along the line you choose."

She stood. Mara stood with her. Behind them Ashfen smoked, and ahead of them the road ran north toward the city that had outlawed them both, and the stones sang *soon, soon, soon*—and this time, Wendell answered aloud:

"Yes. Soon."
