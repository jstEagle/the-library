## 18. Two Women at the Vault Door

### Mara

The Hall of Inquisition smelled of tallow and old smoke, the way it always did before a burning. Mara Voss stood in the antechamber beneath the memorial frieze—founders in gray marble, their faces worn smooth by a century of reverent thumbs—and listened to the bells toll the hour of compline. Nine strokes. Somewhere above her, the vault wardens were changing shift.

In her left hand she carried her warrant. In her right hand, folded small as a prayer, she carried the truth.

She had spent fifteen years learning to hold those two things apart, one in each hand, like scales that must never touch. Tonight they were touching. She could feel it in her palm: a heat where the paper met her skin, as if the warrant itself had begun to burn from the inside.

*Wendell Okafor is in the building.*

She had known it since sundown, when the fourth stone's resonance had shivered through the Hall's foundations like a struck bell heard through water. Every Warden with untrained ears had felt only a draft. Mara had felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue over her ribs where a bone charm had once rested against her heart—Wendell's carving, the wolf with the ember eye, confiscated and burned on the day Mara took her oath. Some things you burn and they stay burned. Some things you burn and they simply move into you and live there.

The corridor to the vault was empty. Of course it was empty. She had arranged for it to be empty—the shift change delayed by a forged order, the lanterns doused along the eastern passage, the warden-captain sent chasing a phantom report of smugglers at the river gate. Fifteen years of meticulous service, and this was what it came to: she was spending all of it, every coin, in a single night.

Her boots made no sound on the stone. She had learned silence the way other people learned scripture.

At the turning of the stair she stopped, because there—faint, unmistakable, threading up through the rock itself—came singing. Not the stone's song. Not the dead's. A woman's voice, low and cracked and stubborn, humming a lullaby in a language older than kingdoms, the language Sefa spoke, the language Wendell had sworn she'd forgotten.

Mara closed her eyes. *You never forgot anything,* she thought. *That was always your curse. You remember everything, and I remember nothing I want to.*

Then she went down to meet her ruin.

* * *

### Wendell

The plague-pit beneath the Hall breathed cold.

Wendell knelt at its edge with Sefa's small hand gripped in hers and let the dead speak, because the dead were the only ones down here who would not lie to her. Thousands of them. The pit was not a pit so much as a wound—a hollow where the founders had dumped the plague-dead of three generations, uncounted, unnamed, sealed under consecrated floor while the city above built monuments to its own mercy. Their bones had been whispering to her since she'd slipped through the memorial crypt an hour past, and now, with two Ember Stones warm against her sternum, the whispering had become a tide.

*Witness us,* they said, in the wordless way bones said things. *We were people. Write our names somewhere. We were people.*

"I will," Wendell whispered. "I swear it on my grandmother's grave. When the covenant holds again, I will carve every name."

Sefa tugged her sleeve and pointed.

Above them, dim through the grate in the crypt ceiling, lamplight moved. Boots. More than one pair. Wendell's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

They had been found. Of course they had been found. Osric's confession had bought them perhaps half a day, and half a day was what it was. She pressed the second stone into Sefa's hands—*hold this, whatever happens, hold this and run toward the river, don't wait for me*—and drew the bone knife from her belt, the little carving knife she'd used for fifteen years to make charms for frightened women who paid her in eggs and secrets.

The grate lifted.

Lamplight poured down like honey, and standing in it, gray coat immaculate, face pale as the founders' marble above, was Mara Voss.

Fifteen years, and Wendell still knew the exact weight of her name in her mouth. *Mara.* It had been the first word she'd carved into anything, once—a wolf's rib, a girl's initials, a promise neither of them had understood.

"Inquisitor," Wendell said, and put herself between the light and the child.

"Step away from the pit, Mistress Okafor." Mara's voice was level, official, dead. But her eyes went past Wendell's shoulder—to Sefa, to the stones, to the knife—and something flickered there, quick as a fish under ice. "You are under arrest for unlicensed resonance, theft of consecrated property, and conspiracy against the peace of Grayhollow. Kneel and place your hands flat on the stone."

"The last time someone knelt in front of you," Wendell said quietly, "did it help them?"

Mara flinched. It was small—it was nothing, really, a tightening around the mouth—but Wendell had spent one whole year learning that face, and fifteen years memorizing it from memory, and she saw it. She saw everything.

"Kneel," Mara said again, softer. "Please."

"Come down here and make me."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The dead held their breath, which is to say the cold deepened until Wendell's lungs ached. Then Mara did something Wendell would remember even after she'd forgotten everything else: she set her lamp on the crypt floor, stepped down into the dark of the plague-pit's threshold, and pulled the grate shut behind her.

* * *

### Mara

The dark swallowed them both, and the warrant in Mara's hand became just paper.

She could hear the child breathing behind Wendell—quick, frightened, trying to be quiet about it. She could hear the stones. Gods, she could hear the stones, a low harmonic like a choir warming up in a cathedral of ash, and she understood suddenly why the hedge-witches had gone to the pyres singing rather than recanting. There was worse music in the world than this. There was the silence the Hall called law.

"Wendell." Her own voice sounded strange down here, stripped of its office. "Three hedge-witches burned this week. You know that. I signed two of the warrants."

"I know."

"The third was a boy of nineteen. He begged." Her throat closed. She made herself go on, because if she stopped she would never start again. "I stood there and I watched, and I told myself it was justice. I have told myself that every morning for fifteen years, and this morning I woke up and could not find the sentence anymore. It's gone. The words won't fit together."

"Mara—"

"Let me finish. I have rehearsed this in the dark for hours and if I stop I'll lose it." She heard Wendell go still, the particular stillness of a listener, the gift she'd always had, hearing what lived underneath. "I filed a partial report. I omitted the child. They found the omission. This afternoon the High Inquisitor gave me a choice: bring you in tonight, or stand beside you on the pyre. And I sat with that choice through the memorial service upstairs, with four hundred mourners singing hymns over a pit full of people nobody ever mourned, and do you know what I decided?"

"No," Wendell said. "Tell me what you decided."

Mara laughed. It came out broken, half a sob, and she didn't try to fix it. "I decided I've been kneeling my whole life. To the Hall. To the oath. To the version of myself that survives by being useful to monsters." She took out the ring of keys—all of them, the full set, the ones only the Inquisitor-First and her second were meant to carry—and held them out into the darkness between them. "So. I'm done kneeling. Take me to the vault."

Silence. Then Wendell's voice, very careful, the voice of a woman handling something that might be a trap or might be a heart: "Why?"

"You know why."

"Say it anyway. I've waited fifteen years. Say it in the dark where it's safe."

Mara closed her eyes. Down here, in the cold, with the dead listening—which they were; she could feel them the way you feel weather coming—she found that the truth was easier than it had ever been above ground. Perhaps the dead made honest people of everyone. Perhaps it was only that she was so tired.

"Because I loved you," she said. "Before the purges. Before I chose the gray coat over you. Because I have loved you every day since, in the coward's way, from a distance, behind duty, and duty has eaten me alive and given nothing back. Because the covenant is failing, and the veil is thinning, and I have seen the shadows walking in the Ashfen reports, and I know—if I am honest for one moment—I know the Hall will not save this city. It will only burn whoever it can reach and call the ashes order." Her hand shook; she let it. "And because you are going to that Gate whether I help you or hang you, and I would rather be ruined at your side than honored at your back."

The dark was quiet a long time.

Then Wendell's hand found hers—not taking the keys, just finding it, wrapping around it, warm and callused and trembling exactly as much as her own. Fifteen years fell away like a coat dropped on a floor.

"You always did make long speeches," Wendell whispered, "when you were terrified."

"I'm not terrified."

"Liar. Your pulse is in your wrist. I can feel it. I used to fall asleep counting it."

Mara bowed her head until her forehead touched Wendell's. "Take the keys," she said, "before I come to my senses."

* * *

### Wendell

The vault door was iron faced with silver, worked with wards in the founders' script, and it sang when Mara's keys touched it—a high, offended note, like a struck animal.

Wendell felt every ward as it broke. That was the thing about her gift that no one understood, that the Wardens had never managed to torture out of the hedge-witches before the end: magic wasn't cast, it was *heard*. Each seal on the door was a voice, and each voice cried out as it died, and Wendell stood with her free hand pressed flat to the cold iron and wept without shame while Mara turned the locks one by one.

"I'm sorry," she murmured to the door. "I'm sorry. Sleep now. It's almost over."

"You apologize to doors," Mara said softly. Behind her, Sefa watched with enormous eyes, the second stone cradled against her chest like a bird.

"I apologize to everything. It's a flaw."

"It's the reason I—" Mara stopped. The last lock turned. "It's the reason I couldn't hate you. All these years. I tried."

The door swung open on darkness and gold.

Not gold the color of coin—gold the color of the inside of an ember, a slow pulsing warmth that filled the vault like honey filling a jar. Confiscated charms lined the shelves in lead boxes, thousands of them, muzzled and stacked: bone whistles, dream-knots, grandmother's charms, midwife's charms, the whole silenced choir of Grayhollow's hidden faithful, seized over thirty years of purges. And in the center, on a pedestal of black marble under a bell jar, the fourth Ember Stone burned like a captive sunrise.

Sefa made a sound Wendell had never heard her make—not speech, not quite song, something between. She walked forward as if sleepwalking, and the bell jar's ward parted for her like water, and when her small fingers closed around the stone the whole vault exhaled.

Every muzzle in every lead box broke at once. The shelves began to sing—faintly, then fully, hundreds of voices rising, charms remembering themselves, the dead-bones crying out their names and their griefs and their small human hopes. Wendell pressed both hands over her mouth. Beside her, Mara Voss, Inquisitor of Grayhollow, stood in the flood of everything she'd spent her life suppressing, and did not look away.

"This is what we burned," Mara said. Her voice was wrecked. "Gods forgive us. This is what we burned."

"Yes," Wendell said. "Now help me carry it out."

* * *

### Mara

They came up through the crypt with the stones wrapped in Wendell's shawl and the singing muffled but not silenced—you could not silence it anymore, it was in the walls now, in the marrow of the building—and Mara walked point with her sword drawn, not against pursuers but against her own reflection in every polished surface. Traitor. The word followed her up the stairs. She let it. It weighed less than the warrant had.

In the memorial hall, the compline congregation had dispersed, but the High Inquisitor's honor guard remained—six Wardens, lanterns lit, and at their center, waiting with his hands folded like a patient priest, the High Inquisitor himself.

"Mara." His voice was gentle. It was always gentle. That was how you knew. "Bring me the stones and the child, and your report may yet survive review. Mistakes happen. Even to my best."

Mara looked at him—at the gray coat she wore, at the gray coat he wore, at the marble founders watching from their frieze with their smooth thumb-worn faces—and she thought of a boy of nineteen begging at the stake, and a woman in a doorway fifteen years ago saying *come with me, Mara, come with me now*, and a night of sitting alone with a choice until the choice sat up and looked at her with her own eyes.

"No," she said.

The Wardens reached for their blades. The stones flared. And from the floor of the crypt below—from the plague-pit, from the sealed dark where three generations of the city's dead had waited without names—the cold rose up through the consecrated stone like a tide answering a moon, and every lantern in the hall guttered blue, and the shadows of the six Wardens stood up and turned around to face the men who cast them.

The High Inquisitor's composure cracked, just once, just enough.

"Run," Mara said to Wendell, and meant it with her whole ruined heart. "Run now. I'll hold the door."

"We hold it together," Wendell said, gripping her hand, "or not at all. That's the whole point of the new covenant, love. Nobody kneels alone."

Behind them, faint and growing, from the direction of the river and the low places and the Hollow Gate itself, came a sound like ten thousand voices drawing one breath—

—and the veil began to tear.
