## 19. March on the Hollow Gate

The Hall of Inquisition had never been quiet, but that night it was loud in a new way—a sound beneath the sound, like a bell rung under water. Wendell felt it through the soles of her boots as she crouched in the shadow of the buttress across the processional square: a low, grinding hum, the frequency of stone remembering it was once alive. The fourth Ember Stone was somewhere inside that building, and it was singing to her bones.

All three of her stones answered from the satchel against her chest. They had not stopped singing since Ashfen burned. Even wrapped in wool and ash-cloth, even pressed beneath her heartbeat, they sang—three thin silver threads of sound winding toward the fourth, pulling at her ribs like hooks set in living flesh.

Beside her, Mara Voss stood in full Warden gray, sword at her hip, warrant tube empty at her belt. She had walked out of the Hall an hour ago with Sefa's cell number written on her palm and had not washed it off.

"The east postern," Mara said. Her voice was flat, professional, the voice she used for reports. "Two guards. I relieved them myself an hour ago; their replacements won't come until the changing of the watch at midnight. The holding cells are below the memorial crypt—the same crypt you crawled through for the second stone." A pause, barely a breath. "You've been there before."

"I remember the way," Wendell said. "The dead gave me directions."

Something moved behind Mara's eyes—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "Of course they did."

Above them, the sky was wrong. Everyone in Grayhollow could see it now; no one pretended otherwise anymore. The clouds moved in slow spirals that no wind explained, and between them, where stars should have been, there were gaps—soft-edged absences, like holes burned in cloth. Through the largest gap, just above the Inquisitors' spire, something pale drifted. It might have been mist. It might have been a hand.

Three days since Ashfen. Three days since Mother Ilya died on the pass with her arms spread wide and her voice raised in covenant-speech so old the mountains themselves seemed to lean in to listen. Three days since Mara's squad took Sefa, and Wendell had lain bleeding in the bracken, clutching three stones that sang toward a fourth, too broken to follow.

She had spent those three days learning what rage could become if you fed it nothing but purpose.

* * *

The postern door opened to Mara's key without protest. Inside, the Hall smelled of tallow and cold incense, and beneath both, faintly, of ash—the smell that had been following Wendell since childhood, the smell of everything the city had tried to burn out of itself.

Mara led. Wendell followed two steps behind, hood up, hands buried in her sleeves around the warm weight of the stones. Every corridor was a corridor she'd mapped in terror once, crawling through plague-dark; now she walked it upright, escorted by its own law, and the absurdity of it made her want to laugh or weep or both.

"You understand what happens to me after tonight," Mara said quietly, not turning. "Whatever the outcome."

"I know."

"And you're asking me anyway."

"I'm not asking," Wendell said. "I'm walking beside you. There's a difference."

Mara stopped at a landing where a lamp guttered in an iron cage. For one moment—one unguarded moment that cost her something visible—she looked back over her shoulder. Fifteen years stood in that look, and a night on a riverbank when they were young and the world hadn't yet taught them what it cost to be seen.

"Below," she said. "Stay close."

The cells were cut into the rock beneath the crypt, and the dead up there pressed close to the floor above, thousands of them, plague-dead and purge-dead, all of them murmuring. Wendell heard them the whole way down: not words, exactly, but pressure, attention, the weight of being witnessed. They knew why she'd come. They had been waiting.

Sefa sat in the third cell, small and straight-backed on the stone bench, hands folded in her lap like a child waiting out a long sermon. When she saw Wendell through the bars she did not cry out. She simply began to speak—that deep, layered language, covenant-speech, each word ringing like a struck bowl—and every bone in Wendell's satchel sang back until the corridor itself seemed to hum.

"Open it," Wendell said.

Mara's key turned. The lock clicked like a knuckle cracking.

Sefa crossed the cell and took Wendell's hand, and through the touch came images, fast and bright: the Gate, the veil stretched thin as wet paper, shadows walking in the streets of Grayhollow already—people seeing their grandmothers at corners, their drowned children in rain barrels, and calling it fever-dreams because the alternative was unbearable. Then another image, sharp with intent: below the reliquary vault, in the deepest chamber, the fourth stone, and around it, wards carved by frightened men who thought power could be imprisoned.

"She says the stone is grieving," Wendell translated, though Sefa needed no translation. "It's been alone down there so long it's forgotten it's part of a song."

Mara stared at the child. "You understand her."

"I'm starting to," Wendell said. "She's teaching me. One lullaby at a time." She shouldered the satchel. "The vault, Mara. Take me to the vault."

* * *

The reliquary vault lay behind a door of black iron banded with silver script—wards, dozens of them, renewed generation after generation by men who feared what they had stolen. Mara stood before it a long moment, key ring in hand.

"When I open this," she said, "there is no report I can file that makes it right. No partial truth. No omission." She looked at Wendell. "I spent fifteen years learning how to survive inside this machine by bending it. Tonight I break it. Do you understand what you're asking?"

"I'm not asking anything," Wendell said again. "But I'll tell you what I know. That stone has been locked in the dark for two hundred years while the world above forgot the words that keep the dead sleeping gentle. Every day it stays there, the veil tears wider. Every day, more of them press through—confused, hurting, hungry for witness. Your institution didn't imprison magic, Mara. It imprisoned the thing keeping the roof up, and then wondered why the house was falling."

Mara closed her eyes. Opened them. Turned the key.

The wards shrieked—a sound like a thousand small bones breaking—and then the door swung wide, and the darkness inside was not empty. It was full of song.

Wendell walked in and lifted the fourth Ember Stone from its cradle of iron, and the moment her skin touched it, all four stones spoke at once. The sound was not sound. It was recognition. Four voices finding each other after two centuries of silence, weaving into a chord that made the vault walls sweat moisture and the silver script flake from the door like dead skin.

In her mind, calm and vast, the dead of Grayhollow turned their attention toward the Hall of Inquisition. And began, all together, to rise.

* * *

They came up out of the crypt into chaos.

The memorial hall was full—wardens, clerics, the night shift of the entire apparatus, drawn from their posts by the humming in the walls. Mara's squad commander stood at the head of the nave, warrant tube in hand, face white as candle-tallow.

"Voss." His voice cracked on the name. "Step away from the heretic."

"No," Mara said.

One word. Wendell watched it land on him like a blow. Fifteen years of the Inquisitor's finest, the woman who filed perfect reports, the woman whose arrests were clean and whose conscience was a locked drawer—and she said *no* in front of forty armed men.

"Voss—" he started again.

"The covenant is failing," Mara said, and her voice carried the way it did in courtrooms, level and terrible. "Look up. Look at the sky. Look at your own chapel windows—the glass has been singing for a week. Whatever you believe about magic, whatever you've been ordered to believe, the roof is coming down, and this woman is the only hand in Grayhollow that can hold it up. Arrest me if you must. But stand aside."

For a moment, no one moved. The lamps flickered. Somewhere above, glass chimed—actually chimed, a clear sweet note rolling through the Hall like a finger run around a wet bowl's rim.

And then the dead came through.

Not violently. That was the terrible part—they came gently, the way fog comes through a window seam. Pale shapes rising through the memorial hall's marble floor, drifting between the wardens, faces familiar and half-remembered: mothers, sons, plague-victims, witches burned in the purges, all of them looking at the living with expressions of such patient sorrow that men began to drop their weapons not from fear but from shame.

An old woman drifted to a stop before the squad commander. She reached out a translucent hand and touched his cheek, and he made a sound like a man gut-struck, and sank to his knees.

"That's my mother," Wendell heard him whisper. "That's—I buried her nine years ago—"

"Yes," Wendell said, stepping forward, hood back, letting them see her face, letting them see the stones blazing in her arms like coals. "And she's been waiting to be seen. All of them have. Two hundred years your law told you the dead were gone, that silence was safety, that forgetting was virtue. Look at them. *Look.* This is what your silence bought."

The hall was utterly still except for the singing of the stones and the soft, continuous murmur of the gathered dead.

A warden near the doors—young, freckled, hardly more than a boy—took off his helmet. Set it on the floor. Knelt.

Then another. And another.

Mara stood beside Wendell, sword still sheathed, watching her institution kneel around her, and Wendell saw her hands trembling at her sides—not from fear. From the vertigo of a life's architecture coming down.

"What now?" Mara asked quietly.

"Now," Wendell said, "we walk."

* * *

They walked out of the Hall of Inquisition at midnight, and the city rose to meet them.

Word had traveled the way word travels among the hidden—through bone-charms passed hand to hand, through hedge-whispers, through the smugglers' tunnels and the prayer-nets of the unregistered faithful. Osric was waiting at the Hall's steps with thirty of his people, lanterns unshaded, which in Grayhollow was a declaration of war or of faith, possibly both. He looked at Wendell, and at the stones, and inclined his head with the gravity of a man settling a debt.

"For my daughter," he said simply.

"For your daughter," Wendell agreed.

Behind them came others. A widow in a shawl carrying her husband's skull wrapped in linen. Twin sisters who had kept a forbidden shrine in a cellar for twenty years. Hedge-folk, charm-sellers, bone-readers, the old and the frightened and the furious, emerging from cellar doors and attic stairs as though a tide were going out and revealing all the things the sea had hidden. Some carried candles. Some carried bones. One ancient man carried nothing but a wooden spoon he swore his grandmother's ghost lived in, and Wendell, hearing the faint querulous voice within it, did not contradict him.

By the time they reached the Processional Way, they were hundreds. By the Rivergate, thousands.

And overhead, the veil tore.

It did not tear all at once—it frayed, like rope under strain, threads of pale light unraveling from the gaps in the sky. And through the fraying, the ancestors came. Not rushing, not raging. Walking. Shadows moving at street level with the unhurried gait of people going home after a long absence, pausing at doorways they remembered, touching walls they had built. A fisherman's shade stood on the quay staring at boats two centuries newer than his own. Children's laughter echoed from a well that had been sealed for a hundred years.

The living lined the streets and did not flee. They came out of their houses instead, holding lamps high, and some wept, and some knelt, and one old woman at a second-floor window called down, in a shaking voice, *"Papa? Papa, is that you?"*—and the shade below raised its face to her, and the whole street went silent as a held breath.

"They're not invading," Sefa said.

Wendell startled. Not because the child spoke—but because she spoke in Common. Clear, accented, careful, the first Common Wendell had ever heard from her mouth.

"Sefa—"

"The gate is opening whether we reforge it or not." The girl's eyes reflected the stones' ember-light, steady and old. "Open wide, it swallows us. Reforged, it becomes a door with a latch. Grandmother-mother"—her word for Wendell, the one that undid her every time—*"walk faster."*

So they walked faster.

Through the Warrens, where the poor hung charms from their windowsills like festival garlands. Across Sorrow Bridge, where the river ran silver and slow and full of faces looking up. Up the long spiral road to the Hollow Hill, the procession stretching back down into the city like a luminous serpent, thousands strong, the living carrying lamps and the dead walking among them without distinction now, so that a stranger watching would not have known which were which—and perhaps that was the point. Perhaps that had always been the point.

At the crest of the hill, the Hollow Gate stood open.

It had always looked like ruins—a broken arch, weathered stones, the kind of monument tourists ignored. But tonight the arch blazed with light from within, light the color of the space between heartbeats, and through it Wendell could see the other side: the spirit realm, vast and dim and crowded, pressing gently, patiently, like a crowd at a door.

Wendell climbed the seven steps to the arch. Behind her, the procession fell silent. Before her, the veil hung in tatters, and beyond it, the dead waited—not demanding, not threatening. Waiting. As they had waited for two hundred years. As they would have waited forever, if forever had been required.

She set the four Ember Stones on the altar-stone at the arch's center. They blazed, sang, strained toward one another like magnets, like family, like hands reaching across a table.

Sefa took her place at the arch's eastern pillar—the anchor's place, the burden too heavy for small shoulders, borne anyway. Mara stood at the base of the steps, gray coat dark with night-dew, hand resting on her sword hilt, guarding the only way up.

Wendell drew her carving knife.

The price had been named in Ashfen, and she had not flinched then, and she would not flinch now. A life's worth of memory. Everything. Her mother's face. The riverbank. Her own name. She had spent the march rehearsing goodbyes to things she couldn't yet say goodbye to, and now, with the knife in her hand and the stones singing, she found there were no adequate words, so she offered the only ones she had:

"Witness me," she said to the dead. "Remember for me. What I give, let it be given gladly."

And the dead, all together, in a voice like wind through ten thousand hollow reeds, answered:

"We will remember you."

Wendell set the blade against the first stone and began to carve.

The stone resisted, then yielded, singing louder with every stroke, and with every stroke something left her—a face going soft at the edges, a name losing its letters. She carved and surrendered, carved and surrendered, the knife moving in patterns Sefa had taught her, in the language under language, and the four stones began to move, sliding toward one another across the altar-stone, fusing, whitening, kindling—

The arch flared. The veil pulled taut like a sail catching wind. The dead on both sides cried out—one voice, joyous and grieving at once—

And Wendell Okafor, kneeling at the Hollow Gate with her memories pouring out of her like light from a cracked lantern, smiled through her tears, and kept carving, because someone had to hold the roof up, and she had finally, finally stopped hiding.

* * *

The last thing she remembered was green.

Not a place. A color, a season, a feeling—her grandmother's garden, maybe, or the memory of a memory. Warmth. The smell of cut stems. Someone's hand in her hair.

Then it was gone, and the ember on the altar-stone burned white and whole, and the Gate stood latched and gentle, and the woman kneeling before it looked up at the crowd with the polite, lost expression of a stranger waking in an unfamiliar room.

"Someone help me," she said pleasantly. "I seem to have forgotten why I climbed these stairs."

And down in the crowd, held tight in Mara Voss's arms, Sefa began, very softly, to sing the first lullaby.
