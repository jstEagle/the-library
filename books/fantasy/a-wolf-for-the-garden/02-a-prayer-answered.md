## 2. A Prayer, Answered

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth by the time Silas Blackwood noticed the knocking.

It was not the knock itself that caught their attention—people knocked at the Guild of Impossible Things at all hours, desperate and drunk and occasionally on fire—but its *quality*. Most visitors to the guildhouse knocked the way beggars beg: loudly, repeatedly, with an ear tuned for refusal. This knock came soft and slow, like rain against a shutter, like someone who had forgotten how doors worked or was no longer entirely sure they were made of anything solid enough to strike.

Silas set down their pen. The ledger lay open before them, *Debts of the Third Order, Winter Reckoning*, and they had been enjoying the particular peace of a page that balanced. Somewhere below, in the vaults, bottled memories sat in their racks like wine, humming faintly to one another in the dark. Somewhere above, Odessa Vane was asleep, or pretending to be, with a knife under her pillow and the guild's charter under her tongue. And somewhere out in the wet black streets of Corvane's lowest quarter, someone was knocking on a door that most people walked past without seeing.

Silas blew out the candle and listened.

The knock came again. Softer now. Slower. As though the hand behind it were cooling.

They went down through the guildhouse the way they always did, by memory and habit, past the shelf where Brann's locks hung like sleeping bats, past the notice board where Wren's maps were pinned with silver nails that wept when removed. The house knew them, insofar as a house can know anything, and the stairs creaked only where they wanted them to creak—a courtesy the building extended to no one else.

Silas opened the door a hand's width, chain still fastened, and looked out into the fog.

A priest stood on the step.

He was young—younger than his ruined voice had suggested—and he was dying. There was no ambiguity in it. Blood had soaked through the grey wool of his cassock from a wound low in his side, and it steamed in the cold air as though his body were already giving up its heat to the night. His face was the color of tallow. His eyes, though. His eyes were still burning, and Silas recognized that particular fire because they had seen it before, in the ledgers, in the margins of debts too large to collect: the look of a man carrying something he believed was worth more than his life.

"You're the bookkeeper," the priest said. Not a question. "They said—the whispers said there was a door here, and behind it a person who keeps accounts of things that shouldn't exist." He swayed. Caught himself against the doorframe, leaving a red handprint on wood that had absorbed worse over two centuries. "I need you to witness something."

"I don't witness," Silas said. "I record. It's different, and it costs more than you have."

"I have nothing." The priest smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man who had seen something that unmade smiling forever after. "That's rather the point. I need you to witness what I'm about to burn."

* * *

Silas should have shut the door. They knew this then, and they know it now, looking back across the years at that moment the way one looks at the first crack in a dam. Every instinct they had honed across three decades of deliberate anonymity screamed at them to close it—to let the man die on the step, to let whatever he carried burn in the street, to remain a smudge in the world's memory, unrecorded, unwitnessed, safe.

Instead they unhooked the chain.

Later, when they tried to reconstruct why, they would find only fragments: the way the priest's blood steamed; the odd, wrong quality of the fog beyond him, which seemed to lean away from his body as if afraid of contagion; and beneath all of it, faint as a finger dragged around the rim of a glass, a sound Silas could not name and could not stop hearing once they'd heard it. A resonance. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep very far below the floor of the world.

"Come in," they said, and hated themselves for it. "Quickly. If you bleed on the threshold, the house will charge me for cleaning."

The priest laughed—a short, shocked bark, as if laughter had been scraped out of him and this was the last rust of it—and crossed the threshold into the Guild of Impossible Things.

* * *

He collapsed in the front room, the one with the hearth and the taxidermied griffin that Odessa refused to explain, and Silas lowered him onto the bench beside it. Up close, the wound was worse than it had looked. Something had gone into him—not a blade, Silas thought, studying the torn edges of the cassock. Something with edges less kind than steel. The flesh around it was gray as ash, and where the blood had dried on his hands it flaked away like char.

"Who did this?" Silas asked, kneeling, pressing a folded cloth against the wound. The priest hissed but did not pull away.

"The messenger," the priest said. "Or—no. Listen. I have to say it properly, in order, because I promised Brother Anselm I'd say it properly." He gripped Silas's wrist with shocking strength. "I am Father Emeric, of the shrine of Saint Vellum in the Marshward. Three days ago a messenger came to our door. Ordinary man. Mud on his boots. He carried a letter sealed with wax the color of dawn, and he said it was a prayer—one that had been written down and sent up, the way prayers are, except this one had come back. Answered." His breath rattled. "You understand? Prayers go up. They do not come back down with instructions."

"What instructions?"

"That's what Brother Anselm wanted to know. Our prior. He read it first, and he went white, and he locked it in the reliquary, and he told us all to forget we'd seen it. But I was the one who'd copied it out—I copy everything, it's my one talent, my hand is true—and I couldn't forget it. It was in my fingers. So last night I took it out and I read it aloud, just to see, just to know if I was mad—" His grip tightened until Silas's bones ached. "And it answered me. Not words. Not exactly. More like—like standing in a doorway when something vast walks past on the other side. The candles bent. The water in the font moved against the wind. And I heard, inside my own skull, a sound like a man screaming through a wall of iron, and underneath the screaming, underneath all of it, a voice saying *yes*. Saying yes to me. To my prayer."

Silas kept pressure on the wound and kept their face still, which was the hardest thing they had done in years. "What did you pray for, Father?"

Emeric's eyes fixed on some middle distance where horror lived. "Rain," he whispered. "We prayed for rain. The Marshward wells have been salt for two seasons. We wrote the prayer and sent it up the old way, the way the country parishes still do even though the Church says the old gods are dead, and I thought—that was the point, don't you see? I thought the old gods were dead. Everyone thinks they're dead. That's why it's safe to pray to them. You can pour your heart out to a dead god the way you'd talk to a grave, and nothing answers, and you feel better for having spoken." He coughed, and the cough came up red-flecked. "Something answered."

"And the messenger?"

"Burned." The word dropped out of Emeric flat and final as a stone into a well. "This morning. In the shrine yard. He stood in the middle of the yard and he caught fire from the inside—I watched it happen, watched the light come up through his skin like a lamp behind parchment—and he burned for eleven minutes without moving, without screaming, and when there was nothing left there was a circle of ash in the shape of a man, and in the center of the ash, unburnt, was a second letter. Sealed in the same dawn-colored wax." His voice cracked. "Addressed to whoever had read the first one aloud. It named me, Master Blackwood. It knew my name. And it said: *the account is opened.*"

The fire in the hearth popped. Silas became aware, distantly, that every bottle in the vaults below had begun, very softly, to hum.

"Where is the second letter?"

Emeric fumbled at his cassock, at a pocket sewn against the chest, and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth and bound with wire. Even through the wrapping, Silas felt it—the resonance again, stronger now, that vast slow turning beneath the world, and threaded through it, thin and high and wrong, a note like a scream held so long it had become music.

"I was going to bring it to the Church," Emeric said. "To the Archprelate. But the Church burned Brother Anselm next. Sent men in grey with censers full of something that isn't incense, and they burned the reliquary and the records and the font, and they asked everyone who had heard the prayer to step forward and be cleansed." He smiled that terrible smile again. "So I ran. And the whispers said there was a door in Corvane where a person keeps accounts of impossible things, and I thought—if anyone knows what to do with a debt that comes back down from heaven, it's a bookkeeper."

He pressed the packet into Silas's hands.

"Witness it," he said. "Read it. Someone has to know what was asked and what answered, or it never happened, and if it never happened then Brother Anselm burned for nothing, and I—" His breath failed him. "I can't have that. Read it aloud. Please. Let it be witnessed by someone who writes things down."

"Emeric—"

"It's already opening my account," he said simply. "I felt it start this morning. Little pieces going missing. My mother's face. The taste of bread. Whatever it is, it takes payment as it goes, and I'd rather it took the rest while I'm holding the ledger myself." His hand found Silas's sleeve. "You keep accounts. Keep mine."

* * *

There are moments, afterward, that the memory insists on keeping whether or not one consents. Silas would carry these fragments for a long time: the oilcloth cold and damp as pond weed; the wire biting their thumb as they twisted it free; the hearth-fire leaning toward the packet like a dog toward its master; and Father Emeric propped against the bench, watching them with the fierce, exhausted attention of a man determined to stay alive long enough to be obeyed.

Inside the oilcloth was a single sheet of paper, and the paper was warm.

Not sun-warm. Body-warm. Warm the way a living thing is warm, and when Silas unfolded it, the creases resisted like joints. The handwriting was beautiful—each letter formed with a care that bordered on devotion—and the ink was the color of dawn, shifting between rose and gold as the light touched it, though the only light in the room was firelight.

It was a prayer. Silas saw that immediately; they had spent enough years handling other people's inner lives to recognize the shape of one, the specific architecture of hope laid out in lines. But it was written in reverse—every line ending where it should begin, every petition phrased as answer—and at the bottom, where a prayer should bear the name of the one praying, there was instead a seal pressed into the paper itself, a crown worked so deep into the fibers that Silas's fingertip found it like a scar.

Below the crown, in that same devoted hand, a single line:

*Received, and granted. The account is opened. Bring what remains.*

"Read it aloud," Emeric whispered.

Silas looked at the dying man. Looked at the paper. Every survival instinct they possessed, every lesson learned in a childhood they had deliberately erased, said *put it down, put it in the fire, put it in the deepest vault and salt the earth above it.*

But they were a bookkeeper. And a debt had been opened, and unrecorded debts were the one abomination the Guild of Impossible Things existed to prevent.

Silas read it aloud.

The words left their mouth strangely—not in their own voice, quite, but in their voice rung like a bell, each syllable carrying a harmonic that belonged to something larger. The candle flames stood up straight and tall. The griffin's glass eye gleamed. Below the floorboards, the bottles in the vaults sang one clear chord, and Silas felt the resonance rise through their boots, their spine, their teeth—

—and something heard.

There was no other word for it. The room did not change; the night outside did not change; and yet the whole world tilted a degree, the way a ship tilts when something immense passes beneath the keel. Silas heard, or felt, or *was briefly touched by*, a presence so far down and so deeply bound that what reached the surface was only the echo of an echo: a vast attention turning toward them through miles of stone and older things than stone, and threaded through that attention, unmistakable now that Silas knew to listen, a scream. Long. Patient. Human.

A man screaming through a wall of iron. And beneath the scream, gentle as snowfall, a voice that said:

*Yes. Yes. Yes.*

The paper went dark. The warmth drained out of it like blood from a face, and it was only paper again—except that the last line had changed. New ink, fresh as a wound, in handwriting Silas now recognized with a lurch as resembling their own:

*Witnessed. The account of Silas Blackwood is opened.*

* * *

Father Emeric died forty minutes later, quietly, with his hand in Silas's grip and his eyes on the ceiling as though reading something written there. At the end his lips moved, shaping words with no breath behind them, and Silas leaned close and caught only the last of it: "*...tell them the king remembers...*" Then nothing, and the terrible tension went out of his body all at once, and he was a weight on a bench and no longer a man.

Silas sat for a long time in the firelight, the dead priest's hand going cold in theirs, the changed letter lying on their knee.

*The king remembers.* What king? The free cities had no king; that was the whole point of them. The usurper in the east called himself King Aldric, but Aldric was new, crowned barely ten years, a conqueror with a whispering crown and an appetite the ballads were only beginning to sing about. And yet the voice beneath the scream had felt older than kingdoms. Older than the paper. Older, perhaps, than the habit of praying.

Slowly, carefully, Silas set the letter on the table, took out their pen, and opened a fresh page in a ledger they had never needed before. At the top, in their own neat hand, they wrote: *Account of Father Emeric, opened by force, closed by death. Balance outstanding.* And beneath it, because a bookkeeper records what is, not what should be: *Second party unknown. First payment rendered: one mother's face, one taste of bread. Collector: pending.*

Their own name sat at the bottom of the changed letter like a hook set in flesh. *The account of Silas Blackwood is opened.* Thirty years of erasure, of careful, meticulous invisibility—and in one night, something ancient had reached up through the floor of the world and written their name in a book they hadn't known existed.

Above the stairs, a floorboard creaked. Odessa Vane stood at the top of the steps in her nightclothes with a knife in her hand, and she was looking not at the body, nor at Silas, but at the letter on the table—at the crown pressed into its fibers—with an expression Silas had never seen her wear in twenty years of service.

She looked afraid.

"Blackwood," she said quietly, "wake the guild. All of it. And send for my ledgers of the old gods—we are going to find out what in the hells just answered you."
