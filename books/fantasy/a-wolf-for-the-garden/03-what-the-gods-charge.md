## 3. What the Gods Charge

The guildhouse had a smell for every hour of the day, and I had learned them all the way other people learned prayers.

At dawn it was ink and cold ash. By midmorning, tallow and old paper and the faint copper tang of the memory-bottles warming on their shelves. By evening—when the lamplighters came through the low streets of Vell and the guild's shutters drank in the last of the sun—it was dust and beeswax and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a locket worn against a heart for forty years.

I knew all this because I had spent thirty years making certain that no one knew anything about me at all, and a man who erases himself learns instead to memorize everything else. The rooms. The smells. The creak of the third stair from the landing, which Odessa Vane had never once stepped around in twenty years because she said a guildmaster who feared noise deserved to be robbed.

So when I heard her boots on that third stair at an hour when even the mice were asleep, I did not need her voice to tell me the world had gone wrong.

She came into the counting room carrying two things: a lantern with the shutter half-closed, so that its light fell in a single blade across my ledgers, and the dead priest's prayer, folded into a square no bigger than a coin.

"Silas." She set the lantern down. "How long have you worked for me?"

"You know exactly how long. It's in your own records."

"How long," she repeated, "have you worked for me, without anyone outside these walls being able to say what you look like?"

I put down my pen. There was no point pretending otherwise; Odessa had bought my silence before she'd ever bought my labor, and she had never once pretended the transaction was anything but what it was. *You will be no one,* she'd told me, the first night, over a ledger open to a blank page. *And in exchange, no one will ever come for you.*

"Twenty-two years," I said. "Give or take the years I've sold."

"And in twenty-two years, how many times have I woken you?"

"Never."

"Then understand what I'm about to ask, and understand what it costs."

* * *

We sat across from each other at the counting table like two debtors meeting by accident, which is more or less what we were. Odessa Vane was sixty if she was a day, built like a door someone had braced shut, with iron-grey hair cropped close as a soldier's and hands that had strangled three men I knew of and forgiven four hundred more. She unfolded the prayer between us with the care of a woman handling a live coal.

It had not burned further since the night the priest died. That should have been impossible. Paper remembers fire the way skin remembers knives. But the charred edges had stopped creeping inward, as though whatever hunger lived in the burn had eaten its fill—or been told to wait.

"The Guild of Impossible Things keeps accounts on everything," Odessa said. "Impossible favors owed. Impossible favors paid. Bottled memories held in trust. Debts of gratitude, debts of vengeance, debts of silence." She tapped the table once. "Debts of worship."

My stomach went cold in a way I recognized and hated. "There's no such ledger."

"There is now."

She rose, crossed to the far wall—the one lined floor to ceiling with the memory-bottles, each one stoppered and labeled in a hand that was mine—and pressed her palm flat against the wood. A panel sighed open. Behind it, where I had believed there was nothing but stone, stood a shelf I had never seen, holding ledgers bound in grey leather, grey as grave-clothes, grey as the rain that falls when the sky can't decide whether to weep or mourn.

"Worship-debts," Odessa said. "Every miracle performed inside a free city for the last three hundred years, recorded by whoever kept the books before me, and before her, back to the founding. Do you know why the guild exists, Silas? Not to perform impossibilities. Anyone with enough desperation can do that. We exist to keep the *ledger*. To make sure that when something impossible happens, somebody writes down what it cost."

"I audit those books every quarter," I said. "There are no worship-ledgers in the index."

"No. Because until eight days ago, they were empty. Three hundred years of blank pages." Her jaw tightened. "Do you understand what it means, when a book like that starts filling again? It means the gods have woken up. And it means they're hungry."

* * *

She opened the first grey ledger, and I read.

I am the only person alive who can read the guild's oldest records—not because of any gift, but because of a theft. The founding bookkeepers wrote in a cipher keyed to stolen memories: a word only resolves if the reader has once known the thing it describes. Read *the taste of riverwater in spring*, and if you have never knelt at a riverbank in April, the line stays smoke. It was meant to make the ledgers theft-proof. Instead it made them grief-proof. You can only read what you have lost.

I have lost a great deal. I made sure of it myself. So I read.

The entries began eight days ago, in Harrowmere, a fishing town on the northern coast. *A widow prayed for her drowned sons.* The answer was written in the same hand as the prayer itself, which troubled me before I understood why: the god had used the widow's own fingers to write its reply. *Given. Cost: the shape of their faces.*

Below it, another entry. Saltmarsh, south of here. *A farmer prayed for his blighted fields.* Given. *Cost: the memory of his wedding day.*

Another. Another. Page after page, city after city, and every single one the same shape—a prayer answered instantly, completely, generously, and a price taken that had nothing to do with gold or blood. The god took *meaning*. It took the interior furniture of a life. A woman got her husband back from a fever and could no longer remember why she loved him. A boy cured of a wasting sickness looked at his own mother and saw a stranger wearing her apron.

"They're hollowed," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, like someone calling up from a well. "Not drained. Draining takes everything evenly. This is—" I turned pages faster. "This is selective. Precise. Like a thief going through a house and taking only the silver."

"Say that again."

I hadn't meant to say it aloud at all. But Odessa was watching me with that flat, patient attention she gave to ledgers and to liars, and the words came out before I could weigh them.

"A god doesn't take payment like this. A god takes sacrifice—blood, first-fruits, burnt offerings. This is *accounting*. Someone is keeping books on the other side too. Someone is charging exact prices." I stopped. The candle guttered. "Odessa, this reads like one of ours."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lantern-blade of light lay across the open ledger between us, and in it I watched the shadow of the flame tremble, though the air in the room was perfectly still.

"That," she said finally, "is the second reason I woke you."

* * *

She showed me the rest, and the rest was worse.

The pattern, when I traced it—city by city, date by date, running my finger down columns of grief—was not random. It was not even regional. It was a *line*. Each answered prayer sat along a road, and the roads connected, and the roads all ran in the same direction: south and east, toward the capital, toward the usurper Aldric's throne, toward the crown he wore.

Miracles blooming along a marching route like flowers along a corpse.

"He's feeding them to it," I whispered. "Or it's feeding him. Every town that prays gets its miracle and loses its soul, and the army walks a road paved with—"

"With worshippers," Odessa finished. "Voluntary ones. That's the elegant horror of it. No conquest needed. Just let the cities hear that prayers are answered in the capital's direction, and let them see what happens to the ones that refuse." She closed the ledger. "One city refused. You know which."

Everyone knew which. Corvane had barred its gates and burned its shrines and declared for the old gods, the silent ones, the decent ones, the ones who took centuries to answer if they answered at all. Corvane was ash now. The fires had been seen from the hills of Vell, forty miles off, burning for two days.

"The priest," I said. "The one who brought us the prayer. He was from Corvane."

"He was the last man out. And the prayer he carried was written by a girl in Corvane three days before the city burned—a prayer that was *answered*, Silas. Answered while the army was still marching on her. Which means the thing answering isn't loyal to Aldric. Or it answers to something older than his crown." Odessa leaned forward, and the lantern-light carved deep lines into her face, lines I realized I had never actually looked at before, because looking closely at Odessa Vane had always seemed like a good way to die. "I need you to trace it. All of it. Every entry, every city, every cost. Find me the pattern under the pattern. What it takes, what it leaves, what it wants."

"No."

The word was out of my mouth before I could dress it up. She raised an eyebrow, and I pressed on, because twenty-two years of careful nothing had earned me exactly one privilege, and I spent it now.

"You know what I am. You know what I paid to be it. If I start pulling threads through those ledgers—if I go reading grief-cipher down a road that leads to the capital—people will notice. The guild's enemies will notice. *Aldric's* people will notice. Everything I built, everything you promised me—"

"—was safety," she said. "Yes. I remember the terms. I wrote them." She turned the grey ledger around and laid her hand flat upon it, the way a magistrate lays a hand on a condemned prisoner's head. "And now I'm invoking the clause you've never read, because you've never wanted to know what else you agreed to. Article One. Founding text. *When the impossible threatens the many, the keeper of accounts shall render account.*"

"That's not a clause, that's a curse."

"It's a job description." For just a moment, something moved behind her eyes that I had never seen there—something tired, and old, and almost gentle. "Silas. I have buried four bookkeepers. Every one of them thought invisibility was armor. It isn't. It's just a smaller target. The only question that has ever mattered in this guild is not *how well hidden are you*. It's *what will you do on the day hiding stops working*." She pushed the ledgers toward me, all six of them, grey and patient as headstones. "That day is today. The armies are weeks out. The miracles are moving faster than the armies. And somewhere in these numbers is the name of the thing answering prayers—which is not any god I have ever heard of, because I have heard of all of them, and none of them charge like this."

I looked at the ledgers. I looked at the prayer, folded small and scorched, humming faintly against the tabletop like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. I thought about a blank page in a ledger twenty-two years ago, and a young thief so desperate to be no one that she'd cut herself out of the world like a stain from cloth, and how safe that emptiness had felt, and how safe it still felt, and how neither of those facts changed what was walking down the road toward every city that had ever sheltered me.

Duty is a debt like any other. I have always hated that most about it.

"Conditions," I said.

Odessa almost smiled. "Name them."

"I work alone. I choose what I read and in what order. And when this is done—" I touched the blank ledger that was my own history, my own deliberate emptiness, "—you burn every record of my involvement. Again. Deeper than before."

"Done."

I drew the first grey ledger toward me, opened it, and began to read the dead.

* * *

I worked until the candles drowned in their own wax, and then I worked by lantern, and then I worked by the grey pre-dawn light coming through the shutters like a rumor of morning. The cipher fought me the way it always does—every line a locked door that opens only onto a room I once lived in and chose to leave—but grief is a key that never rusts, and I had stocked myself with grief the way misers stock cellars.

By the time the birds started, I had found it.

The costs weren't random. They weren't even cruel, exactly, though cruelty would have been easier to bear. They were *curated*. Every price taken was a memory of love—first loves, deepest loves, the faces of children, the voices of mothers, the reasons marriages held and friendships lasted. Never hatred. Never fear. Never shame. The god took only the warm things, only the things that made people *loyal*, and it left the rest standing like a house after the furniture's gone.

And the resonance—the hum in the prayer, the same hum I'd find later in a child's bottled birthday—matched, note for note, an entry in the guild's oldest index. An entry three hundred years old. An entry concerning a kingdom that no longer appeared on any map, erased so thoroughly that even the guild's records listed it only as:

*The Hollow Crown. Debt outstanding. Payment deferred.*

Payment deferred for three hundred years.

And beneath it, in a hand so old it was nearly picture, a single instruction to whichever bookkeeper came next:

*When the crown begins to collect, send someone who owes nothing. Send someone with no name to take.*

I sat very still in the grey light, listening to the prayer hum on the table like a wasp in a jar, and understood that Odessa had lied to me. Not about the danger. About the reason.

She hadn't chosen me despite my emptiness.

She had chosen me *because* of it.

Somewhere below, the third stair creaked—Tam, the kitchen-boy, up early to stoke the ovens, humming a tune he'd learned from a mother whose face he could no longer remember—and I closed the ledger, and reached for my coat, because the trail's next entry was written in a debt-book in the Lowmarket district, and it concerned a shrine, and a sale, and a boy.

It concerned Tam.
