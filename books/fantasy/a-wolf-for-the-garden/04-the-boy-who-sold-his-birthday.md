## 4. The Boy Who Sold His Birthday

The ledger entry was written in a hand that trembled.

Silas noticed that first, before the figures, before the date, before the small red sigil of the shrine at Millbrook Cross stamped crooked in wax at the bottom of the page. Guild entries were supposed to be written steady—Odessa Vane had drilled that into every apprentice with a ruler across the knuckles and a lecture about how a shaking line invited suspicion, and suspicion invited auditors, and auditors were worse than plague. But this entry shook. The ink had pooled twice where the pen had paused too long, little dark blots like eyes staring up out of the page.

*Received: one (1) memory, class joy, unclassified origin. Depositor: minor, male, approx. ten years. Consideration rendered: three silver marks and a blessing, per shrine rate. Resonance noted by receiving clerk: unusual. Referred upward.*

Silas read it four times by candlelight in the back room of the guildhouse, where the ledgers lived in iron cabinets and the air always smelled faintly of tallow and old paper and, beneath that, the cold mineral scent of bottled memory. *Referred upward.* That phrase appeared eleven times in the last month's books. Eleven memories from eleven different shrines, all flagged for a resonance the clerks couldn't name but could all feel—the way you can feel a storm coming in your teeth.

They had felt it themselves, two nights ago, when they'd held the dead priest's prayer to the candle flame and heard something on the other side of the world turn over in its sleep.

Silas closed the ledger. Sat very still. Listened to the guildhouse settle around them—the groan of beams, the tick of the great clock Odessa refused to have repaired because its wrongness kept honest time better than a right one would, the distant clatter of Brann the locksmith arguing with a lock that was winning.

Then they took out their knife, cut the page free along the spine's seam with a surgeon's neatness, folded it into eight, and put it in the inner pocket of their coat, against the breastbone, where things went that Silas did not intend to lose.

Not being recorded was a discipline. Being recorded wrongly was a death sentence. And somewhere between those two truths lay the thing Silas had built a whole life around avoiding: caring enough about an entry to carry it out of the archive.

They blew out the candle anyway, out of habit, and went to find their boots.

* * *

Millbrook Cross was half a day south by the carrier's cart, through country gone gold and tired with late summer. Silas rode on top among the sacks, hood up, face angled away from the driver, practicing the particular art of being forgettable—a slouch here, a yawn there, a murmured answer about wool prices that gave the man nothing to hold onto. It was work Silas had done so long it had become almost restful, this deliberate erosion of self in the eyes of others. Most people thought invisibility was a gift. Silas knew it was a trade, paid daily, in small installments of self.

The village announced itself by smell first—woodsmoke and river mud and, underneath, the green mineral tang of a shrine. Every shrine had it. Memory, once it left a person, decayed toward something like pond water; the shrines were all faintly scented with the ghosts of what people had given up.

It was smaller than Silas expected. Shrines to the old gods usually were, now—little stone huts with moss-eaten carvings, tended by whoever the village could spare, which in Millbrook Cross appeared to be no one at all. The door stood open. Inside, dust had claimed the altar, and the offering bowl held rainwater and one drowned moth.

But the ledger box was new. That was the thing that stopped Silas in the doorway. A squat iron strongbox, guild-stamped, bolted to the floor beside the altar—the kind of equipment the Guild of Impossible Things only issued when a site began producing volume. Someone had been collecting here. Recently. Regularly.

And someone had been sloppy, because the box's lock was a three-pin affair that Silas opened with a bent wire while barely looking at it, mostly out of professional courtesy, the way one musician taps another's instrument.

Inside: a ledger, a purse of consideration-marks, and seven bottles.

Memory bottles all look alike to the untrained eye—thumb-high glass, corked and waxed, each holding a curl of grey-gold light that moves like smoke underwater. But Silas had spent thirty years learning to read them the way other people read faces. Grief bottles sat heavy and still. Fear bottles shivered even in warm rooms. Joy bottles glowed softest and burned longest, and were, accordingly, the most expensive.

Six of these were ordinary. A woman's memory of her wedding dance. A farmer's recollection of his father teaching him to whistle through cupped hands. Small joys, sold cheap, as people did when winter came early or the tax-man came on time.

The seventh bottle hummed.

There was no better word for it. When Silas lifted it, the light inside tightened and turned—not the lazy drift of stored memory but a quick, seeking motion, like a moth against glass—and a sound came off it, felt more than heard, a low tone that Silas recognized down in the marrow because they had heard it once before, in a guildhouse back room, rising off a dead priest's stolen prayer.

The same resonance. The same note. Whatever answered prayers these days, whatever hollowed the faithful and emptied the villages, it had touched this bottle. It had touched whatever was inside.

Silas wrapped it in cloth, tucked it away, closed the box, locked it, and stood in the empty shrine feeling the shape of the crime they'd just committed settle over them like weather. Theft from the Guild's own holdings. Odessa would flay them. Odessa might also, Silas suspected, quietly approve—but that was a problem for later, and later was a country Silas visited reluctantly.

First: the depositor. *Minor, male, approx. ten years.*

Silas went to find the boy who had sold his birthday.

* * *

He wasn't hard to find, because he was sitting alone on the churchyard wall—which was itself strange, because it was a market day, and market days in villages like this ran on children the way mills ran on water. Boys Tam's age should have been underfoot everywhere, hauling baskets, chasing dogs, being shouted at affectionately. This boy sat apart with his knees drawn up, watching the market the way a cat watches birds through glass: present, excluded, unable to say why.

He was thin in the way of children who are fed but not watched. Brown hair cut badly, probably by himself. Bare feet despite the season. And when Silas sat down on the wall an arm's length away—slowly, the way you approach any frightened creature—he didn't startle, didn't scoot away. He just looked over with eyes that were doing something no child's eyes should do.

They were working. You could see the effort. He looked at Silas and Silas could feel him *straining*, reaching for something behind his own face, some piece of information that should have been automatic—who is this stranger, why is he here, am I in trouble—and finding nothing where the knowing should be.

"Hello," Silas said.

"Hello." The boy's voice was polite and lost. "I'm sorry—I know I'm supposed to know you. Everyone keeps asking if I remember them, and I keep saying yes, and then they get sad." He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve. "Did I used to know you?"

"No," Silas said truthfully. Lying to him would have been easy. It also would have been the cruelest available option. "We've never met. My name is—" and here Silas performed the small daily miracle of producing a name that belonged to no one, a name worn for an hour and discarded like a mask. "Corvin. I'm a trader. I buy old things."

"Oh." The boy considered this. "I don't think we have anything old. We had a clock but Da sold it."

"What's your name?"

"Tam." No hesitation. Names, apparently, had survived. "Tam Merric. Though—" the thread-picking again, harder now, "—sometimes I say it and it sounds like someone else saying it. Like I learned it out of a book."

"Tam." Silas let the name sit between them. "Can I ask you something strange? You're allowed to say no."

"You already asked me something strange. Whether I remembered you." But there was no sharpness in it. Children who have lost the ability to track social danger lose the ability to be offended, too, and Silas felt that loss land somewhere under their ribs like a swallowed stone.

"The shrine," Silas said. "At the crossroads. Did you go there?"

The change was immediate and total. The boy lit up—actually lit up, the way children do, the whole face rearranging itself around delight—and for one horrible moment Silas thought: *whatever he got for it, he thinks it was worth it,* and then the moment curdled, because Tam said:

"Yes! Three silver marks. Do you know what three marks is? That's—that's a whole—" and here the sentence stumbled, because the boy reached for an example of what three marks could buy and found, visibly, nothing. He frowned. "That's a lot. It's a lot of money. I know it's a lot because everyone said so, but I can't remember what for." The frown deepened, became something older than a child's face should hold. "I can't remember much lately. Ma says the doctor will fix it. Except—" he counted on his fingers, lips moving, "—except I can't remember if we have a doctor."

"What did you sell?" Silas asked, gently. "At the shrine. What did they take?"

"My birthday," Tam said, simply, the way another child might say *my tooth.*

And then, because the silence stretched and Silas didn't fill it fast enough, the boy explained, in the matter-of-fact voice of someone describing a game whose rules everyone knows: "You give them your best day and they give you money. Everybody does it now. Old Nan sold her wedding. Joss sold the day his brother was born. The priest says the god needs happy things to—" he made a vague gesture, fingers spiraling, "—to make miracles with. Rain for Harrow Field. That was us. Our village prayed for rain and the god gave it, and now everybody gives him their best days to say thank you." He smiled, bright and terrible. "It was my birthday. My tenth. There was a cake with honey, and—" 

He stopped.

Silas watched it happen. Watched the boy reach for the memory he had described a hundred times since selling it—had clearly described it to himself every night, the way you tongue a missing tooth—and watched him find, instead of the day, a hole shaped exactly like the day. His face didn't crumple. That was the worst part. It didn't crumple; it *blanked*, smooth as water closing over a dropped coin, and when he spoke again his voice had gone carefully casual in the way of someone pretending not to bleed.

"There was a cake," he said. "I think. With honey. Ma made it. She—" 

Another blank. Longer this time. His hands had gone still in his lap.

"I can't remember her face," Tam said. Not crying. Trying very hard not to cry, and losing, and not understanding why he was losing, because he couldn't find the thing that was making him sad either. "I know I have a ma. I live in her house. But when I try to picture her there's just—" he pressed his fist against his forehead, hard, "—there's just a shape. Like a person-shaped window with nothing in it. And she cries when she looks at me and I don't know why and I *can't remember why*—"

"Hey." Silas hadn't meant to speak. Hadn't decided to. The word came out low and rough, scraped from somewhere that predated all their careful training. "Look at me. Tam. Look at me."

The boy looked. Working eyes. Straining. Reaching for who this stranger was and finding nothing—and Silas understood, suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that to this child Silas was not merely a stranger. Silas was a stranger *forever*. Even if they came back tomorrow. Even if they came back every day for a year. Each meeting would be the first meeting, because the boy's reach went out and found nothing to hold, and Silas knew—knew in their thief's bones, in the place where they kept the memory of erasing their own childhood—exactly what that nothing felt like from the inside.

Something moved in Silas's chest that had no business moving. Something with teeth.

"Your mother's name," Silas said. "Tell me her name."

"Liese. Liese Merric." Instant. Relief flooded his face at having gotten one right. "Liese. I know that one. I practice it."

"Good. Keep practicing. Hold onto names—they're the last things to go and the first things to come back, if they come back." Silas stood, and heard their own voice drop into the register they used for negotiations, the flat merchant's cadence, because the alternative register—the one that wanted to promise this child the moon and the memory both—was not safe to use yet. "Where is your house? Take me to your mother. I want to ask her some questions about the shrine."

"Why?"

Because I may be able to steal it back, Silas did not say. Because the bottle in my coat is humming with the same note that answers prayers, and humming things can sometimes be unwound, and I have spent my whole life unwinding things, and I am not going to watch a child walk around inside his own life like a lodger. Because someone has to.

"Because I buy old things," Silas said instead. "And I think your family has something of mine."

* * *

Liese Merric met them in the doorway of a cottage that smelled of bread and river damp, and Silas saw the whole story written on her in the space of a breath: the reddened eyes, the hands wrung raw, the way she looked at her son with love and terror in equal measure because every morning he greeted her politely, like a lodger, like a ghost, and every morning she died a little more and smiled anyway because he needed the smile.

She took one look at Silas's coat—at the quality of it, at the guild-signet hidden but not hidden on the chain beneath the collar—and pulled them inside and shut the door and said, in a whisper cracked down the middle:

"You're from the Guild. Tell me you've come to take it back. Tell me you can give it back."

"Mother, this is Corvin," Tam offered helpfully, from the hearth. "He buys old things."

"Sit down," Liese said to Silas, ignoring him, and it broke Silas's heart in a new place to see how the boy simply accepted being ignored, as though it were weather. "Eleven families on this road have sold. Eleven. And the ones who sold the most—" her voice failed and restarted, "—old Marta doesn't know her own daughter anymore. She calls her 'that kind girl.' Joss's brother came home from the coast and Joss hid from him. Hid, in his own house, because a grown man was crying and calling him by name and Joss didn't know him." She gripped Silas's wrist with flour-dusted fingers, hard enough to bruise. "Is it supposed to do this? The priest said you sell a day, one day, and the god gives miracles. He didn't say it takes the roads out of the town behind it. He didn't say a memory isn't a *stone*, it's a *thread*, and when you pull one the whole weave comes loose—"

"I know," Silas said. Quietly. "I know what memories cost. Better than most."

And it was true, and the truth of it rang in the room, and Liese Merric—sharp-eyed, desperate, a woman who had spent a month watching her son become a stranger—looked at Silas and saw, somehow, the one thing Silas had spent thirty years ensuring no one ever saw.

"You've lost something too," she said. Not a question.

Silas said nothing. In the hearth, Tam was feeding sticks to the fire with the diligent aimlessness of a child filling an afternoon he couldn't map, and the light of it moved on his face, and Silas found they could not look away from him, and could not explain to themselves why.

In their coat, against their breastbone, the stolen bottle hummed its single low note—the note of the prayer, the note of the answered god—and inside the grey-gold curl of light, a boy's tenth birthday turned slowly over and over, honey-cake and candles and a laughing woman's face, waiting in its glass like a soul in a jar.

Someone had referred it upward. Someone in the Guild's chain had seen this resonance, this hunger, this thing that ate joy and called itself divine—and had passed it along the line like a debt to be collected later.

Silas stood, and buttoned their coat over the bottle, and made the kind of decision they had spent a lifetime structuring their entire existence to avoid making.

"Mrs. Merric," they said. "I'm going to need you to tell me everything about the priest. Where he sleeps. Where he prays. And who he reports to."

Because the trail didn't end at a roadside shrine. The trail ended wherever the prayers were being *answered*—and Silas Blackwood, who had never followed anything in their life except profit and escape, was already reaching for the road north, toward the drowned chapel and the hollowed faithful and the bound king screaming through every miracle—

—and, gods help them, they were bringing the boy.
