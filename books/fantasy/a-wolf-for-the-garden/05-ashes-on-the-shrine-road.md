## 5. Ashes on the Shrine Road

The rain began three miles before we reached the village, and it was wrong in every way a rain can be wrong.

I felt it first as a warmth against my knuckles, soft as breath, when every other mile of that road had been cold enough to ache. Tam noticed a moment later, tilting his face up with the unthinking trust of a child, and flinched. "It's warm," he said, as if the sky had broken a promise. "Why is it warm?"

"Keep your hood up," I said, which was not an answer, because I did not have one.

Then the drops came heavier, and I held out my hand in the way travelers do without thinking, and the water that pooled in my palm was black. Not muddy, not shadowed by cloud—black the way ink is black, the way the ledger's margins are black where I have blotted out a name I did not want recorded. It sat in my hand like something alive. It smelled faintly of candle smoke and, beneath that, of something older and sweeter, like incense burned in a closed room for a hundred years.

Tam looked at my palm. I looked at his face. Neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was that we had come three days following a hum in a memory bottle, and the hum had grown louder with every mile, and now the sky itself was leaking the sound of it.

"Silas," Tam said. He had taken to using my name the way children use a lamp in a dark house—frequently, and just to see it light. "Silas, are we scared yet?"

I considered lying to him. I had lied to many people in my life, mostly by omission, mostly by never being anyone worth lying to. But there was something in the question—the *yet*, the careful child's arithmetic of it—that made lying feel like a theft, and I had stolen enough.

"Yes," I said. "But we're going anyway. That's what being scared is for."

He nodded, satisfied, as though fear were a tool he'd been waiting to see used properly, and we walked on into the warm black rain.

* * *

The village was called Merrow's Bend, according to the milestone at the crossroads—or it had been, once. The stone itself seemed uncertain now, the carved letters softened as if the rain had been falling on them for years instead of hours. Beyond it, the village sat in a shallow fold of hills by a river that had run thin for two seasons, and every roof, every fence post, every apple tree heavy with unpicked fruit wore a coat of black that gleamed like lacquer.

The rain fell on it all, patient and warm, and nothing washed away. That was the first horror. Rain is supposed to take things—dust, soot, the leavings of ordinary life—and carry them to the river. This rain gave. It fell and fell and fell, and it left a shine on everything it touched, and the shine did not dull.

There were people in the streets.

I knew this because I saw them, and I did not know it, because when I tried to look at any one of them my eyes slid off like a boot on wet stone. A woman stood at a well, bucket in hand, and I could describe her hands, her shawl, the way she swayed slightly as though listening to music—but if you had asked me who she was, my mind would have presented me with a smooth gray nothing, a wall where a door should be. I tried the man stacking firewood by the inn. The same. A boy chasing a dog through puddles that reflected no sky. The same.

"Tam," I said quietly. "The bottle."

He drew it from inside his coat—the little stoppered vial we'd taken from the shrine in Lowmark, the one that had held a boy's birthday until the boy sold it, the one that hummed like a struck bell no one could hear. He held it up between us, and through the glass I saw the memory inside, the pale gold shimmer of a child's happiest day, and I saw it *leaning*. Tilting toward the village the way a flower tilts toward a window.

"It wants to go home," Tam said, and his voice cracked in the middle of *home*, because he was a boy who had sold his own happiest memory and knew exactly what it was to have a hollow where something loved used to be.

"Not yet," I said. "Stay close to me. If anyone speaks to you, don't give them your name."

"Why not?"

"Because I think," I said, and hated every word as it came out, "that names are what they take here."

* * *

The shrine stood at the village center, where a crossroads had been, older than any of the buildings around it. I had seen a hundred roadside shrines in my years of auditing debts—little stone alcoves where farmers left copper coins and dried flowers for gods who had not answered in two hundred years, gods kept out of habit the way one keeps a dead relative's chair. This one was different. This one had been *attended*.

Fresh candles burned in the alcove, dozens of them, their flames bending toward the shrine itself as if drawn by breath. Offerings lay heaped at its base: bread, wool, a wedding ring, a child's shoe. And carved into the stone above the alcove, in letters that glowed faintly with the same warm black as the rain, was a single word in the old script that I should not have been able to read and found, with a lurch, that I could:

*ASK.*

The hum in the bottle rose to a whine. Around the village square, the nameless people had begun, without any signal I could see, to drift toward the shrine. Not walking so much as *tending*—the way a tide tends a shore. They gathered in a loose ring at a respectful distance, the way one stands near a sleeping bear, and they stood there in the warm rain, faces turned up, mouths slightly open, letting the black water fall on their tongues.

An old woman stood nearest the shrine. She had been knitting—I saw the needles still in her hands, the half-finished sock dangling—and she stood as still as the stone. I made myself approach her, because someone had to, because the ledger-keeper in me needed an entry, a figure, a line of account, and because I was afraid that if I did not look at one of them closely, I would spend the rest of my life able to pretend I hadn't seen.

"Good morning," I said.

She turned to me and smiled, and her smile was warm and empty as a hearth in summer. "Good morning, stranger," she said. Her voice was pleasant. It was the voice of someone who has forgotten nothing, because there is nothing left in her to forget. "Lovely weather."

"It's—" I stopped. Started again. "It's been dry, they say. Downriver."

"Oh, it was dry," she agreed. "For years and years. The wells went bitter. The apples were small and sour. We prayed, of course. We prayed at every shrine from here to the coast." She said it lightly, a woman discussing a mild winter. "And then, in the spring, the god came back."

"Which god?" I asked, though my hands had gone cold inside the warm rain.

She frowned—a small, effortful frown, like someone reaching for a word in a language they used to speak. "That's the funny thing," she said. "We couldn't agree. Half the village prayed to the Rainmother, half to the Old King of the Springs, and the miller swore by Saint Vess of the Deep Water, and I always kept a candle for the Nameless One, the god with no name, because it seemed only polite." Her frown deepened. "I can't remember which one answered. I can't remember which one I prayed to. I can't remember—" and here her voice did not break, which was the worst part; it simply *smoothed*, the way water smooths sand, "—I can't seem to remember anything that needs remembering. But it rained, didn't it. It rained and rained, and the wells are sweet, and the apples will be fine this year."

She held up her knitting needles, pleased. "I was making this for my—" The needles stopped. The smile stayed. "For someone," she finished, and began to knit again, stitches falling from her fingers in a pattern her hands remembered and she did not.

I stepped back. My heel found the edge of the shrine's foundation stone, and the hum in Tam's bottle spiked so sharply he gasped, and every nameless face in the square turned toward us at once.

They didn't menace us. That was the thing I could not make Odessa understand later, when I told it, and could not make myself stop dreaming: they didn't menace us. They *looked* at us, a hundred warm empty faces under the warm black rain, with an expression I finally recognized because I had worn it myself, once, in a mirror, at fifteen, with a stolen memory dissolving on my tongue.

Relief. They looked at us with relief. The way the hollowed look at anything that still has a shape.

* * *

We found the priest's journal in the inn, or what passed for the inn—a common room with a cold hearth and a ledger of its own behind the bar, and I confess I looked at that ledger first, because I am what I am. The innkeeper's accounts stopped in spring, mid-entry, the ink trailing off into a small inkblot shaped like a bird. The last legible line read *paid the tinker for—* and then nothing, and the nothing went on for pages, the pen dragging, as though the hand holding it had forgotten what numbers were for.

The journal was tucked in the shrine alcove itself, wedged behind the candles, wrapped in oilcloth. A priest's hand, cramped and devout and increasingly frightened. I read it by candlelight while Tam watched the square, and I will set down what it said here, because the Guild of Impossible Things keeps accounts, and someone must.

*The god answers. Gods forgive me, the god answers. We prayed for rain and on the third night I dreamed of a door opening under a mountain, and in the morning the clouds came up from the south like a herd of black sheep, and it has not stopped raining since.*

*It is warm rain. I did not think to be afraid of warm rain.*

*The miller's daughter was the first. She came to me weeping, saying she had lost her own name—knew her face in the mirror, knew her mother's grief, but the word itself, the name her father called her across the yard, was gone, a hole shaped like her. I prayed for her. The rain fell harder.*

*Then her mother. Then the miller. Then half the village in a single night, each of them waking hollow, and those still whole too frightened to speak of it, because we understand now, we understand what the price is. It takes our names. It takes them one by one, and it does not stop when the rain is enough. I do not think it knows how to stop. I think it is a door that was opened and cannot close itself.*

*I went up to the high shrine to pray to the Rainmother to take it back, and I heard—under the rain, under everything—a voice. Not words. A sound like a king laughing, if a king were laughing in a cell, if the laughing had gone on so long it had turned into something else. I think—I think the thing answering our prayers is not the Rainmother at all. I think something is wearing the gods like a coat. I think it is answering every prayer in every shrine because answering is all it can do, and it is screaming through the miracles, and none of us can hear the screaming except when the rain is warm.*

*It has my name now. I felt it go at dawn, gentle as a thief. I am writing this so that someone, someday, knows we were Merrow's Bend. Knows we had names. I will write them all down, every soul in the village, while I still—*

The last page was a list. Forty-one names, written in a hand that deteriorated line by line, the letters going round and strange, until the final entries were only loops, the shapes of letters without the letters, a man's devotion outlasting his language. After the loops, one more line, pressed hard into the paper as if written by someone gripping the pen like a knife:

*If you can hear the laughing, do not pray. Whatever you lack, do not pray. It will give it to you and it will not be able to stop and it will take your name as the toll and the toll is not a price it chooses, it is a price it IS—*

The ink ended there. The oilcloth was stained with rain that had never dried, because it never dries, because it is not water in any way that obeys water's laws.

I sat for a long time with the journal in my hands while the candles bent toward the stone and the warm rain fell on the roof, patient as a creditor.

* * *

"Silas." Tam's voice, very small. "Silas, the woman's following us."

I looked up. The old knitter stood at the edge of the inn yard, needles stilled, rain gleaming on her shawl. Behind her, others had gathered—not menacing, never menacing, just *drawn*, the way the bottle was drawn, the way everything hollow leans toward what is full. Tam's bottle was whining now, a continuous note, and the nameless of Merrow's Bend had arranged themselves around our inn yard in a wide, gentle ring, faces lifted, mouths open, drinking the rain.

"They can smell it," I said. "The memory. It's the only whole thing for a mile." I took the bottle from him gently. "They're not going to hurt you. They can't want anything anymore. That's what frightens me."

The old woman took a step closer. "Stranger," she said, pleasant as Sunday, "you have a name, don't you? I can tell. It's all over you. It's *loud*." She smiled her hearth-in-summer smile. "It's so loud. We used to be that loud."

I closed my hand around the bottle, and the whine dulled, and a ripple of something like grief went through the ring of villagers—a sagging, a settling, forty-one hollowed people mourning a sound they couldn't name.

And then, above the shrine, the word *ASK* flared.

Not the candlelight. Not any light I have words for. The carved letters filled with that warm black, and the rain stopped—stopped all at once, everywhere, mid-fall, the drops hanging in the air around us like beads on a string, each one holding a tiny upside-down image of the village, of the ring of the nameless, of me—and through the silence came the sound the priest had written of. Not words. A laugh, deep under the world, in a cell under a mountain, a laugh that had gone on so long it had become a scream and then become a laugh again, and beneath it, so faint I will spend the rest of my life unsure I heard it, a voice saying, in the tone of a courteous host, of a king receiving petitioners:

*Ask.*

The rain resumed. The villagers sighed, all together, like a congregation after a blessing.

Tam had both fists in my coat. "Don't," he said. "Silas. Don't ask it anything."

"I wasn't going to." I tucked the journal inside my coat, next to the bottle, two debts side by side. "But Tam, listen to me. It *answered*. A village prayed for rain and something under the world gave it to them and ate every name in Merrow's Bend, and it couldn't help itself, because it wasn't granting a prayer—it was *screaming through one*." My hands were steady, which surprised me; I had thought they would shake. "Odessa said the old gods were answering all across the free cities. She was wrong. It's one god. One thing. One prisoner, answering everything, everywhere, and hollowing out everyone who kneels."

The road south from Merrow's Bend ran toward the lowlands, toward the free cities, toward the guildhouse and Odessa Vane and her ledgers full of debts. The road north ran up into the hills, toward the high shrine where the priest had heard the laughing, and beyond the hills, according to every map I'd ever audited, there was nothing at all.

Tam looked south. I looked north.

"We have to go back," I said. "We have to make Odessa understand. This isn't a pattern in the debt-books. It's a siege. Something is bound under a mountain and it is buying its way out, one answered prayer at a time, and it has just spent an entire village to show us the exchange rate." I started walking, north, because someone had to know, because someone had to write it down, because the ledger-keeper in me understood, finally and completely, what kind of entry we had been circling for three days.

Behind us, in the warm black rain, forty-one nameless people waved goodbye to strangers whose names they would never know, and the shrine's candles all bent at once, like heads bowing, toward the north.

Toward the mountain.

Toward the door the priest had dreamed of, standing open under the world.
