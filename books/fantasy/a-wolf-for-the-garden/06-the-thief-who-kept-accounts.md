## 6. The Thief Who Kept Accounts

The house on Candlewick Lane had a smell Silas would never forget, because Silas had made certain they could never forget it: tallow and vinegar and the sweet rot of pears left too long in a brass bowl. Master Corvus kept the bowl on the sideboard as a kind of test. If the smell stopped bothering you, he said, you were becoming useful. If it never bothered you at all, you were becoming dangerous.

Silas was nine years old when they arrived at the house with their name still soft in their mouth—*Sila*, just that, a river-name their mother had chosen for the water she'd been born beside—and eleven years old when they first understood what the brass bowl was really for.

The house was not an orphanage. Orphanages had ledgers too, but honest ones, written in ink that dried brown and stayed where it was put. The ledger in Master Corvus's study wrote itself in silver, and the entries moved. Debts appeared overnight. Interest accrued while you slept. And every so often, a line simply vanished, along with whatever or whomever it described.

"You will learn accounts," Corvus told them the first morning, setting the great book on the table between them. He was a narrow man, dry as a winter moth, with hands that seemed assembled from other people's hands—too-large knuckles, fingers fine as a fiddler's. "Not to read them. Anyone can read. You will learn to *keep* them."

"Whose accounts?"

"Everyone's." He smiled, and his teeth were very even, which should have been a warning. "That is what memory is, child. An account. Every kindness is a credit. Every wound is a debt. Most people are terrible bookkeepers—they misplace whole decades, they forgive debts that should ruin them, they carry credits they never earned. We are here to correct the arithmetic."

Silas looked down at the ledger. In the silver light of the candle, the pages shimmered like fish scales, and somewhere deep in the binding something turned over, the way a sleeper turns.

"What happens," Silas asked, "to the people whose accounts we keep?"

Corvus closed the book with a sound like a kiss.

"They become accurate."

* * *

The first theft happened on a Tuesday, though Silas did not know it was a theft until long after, because Corvus called it *collection*.

There was a girl upstairs named Merrin, fourteen, quick-fingered, who had tried to run twice. Corvus did not beat her. Beating, he said, was crude interest—it taught the debtor nothing but fear, and fear depreciated. Instead he sat her in the green chair in the study, and he opened a small bottle—not glass, but something older, a bottle that seemed carved from the inside of an oyster—and he said, gently, "You stole bread from me in March. I have been patient. Give me the day you learned your letters."

Merrin screamed. That was the thing no one warned you about: the screaming came *before*, not after, because some part of her knew what was about to be taken even if she couldn't have named it. Then Corvus pressed two fingers to her temple, and the room filled with a smell like rain on hot slate, and the girl went quiet in a way that quiet was never meant to be.

Afterward she sat blinking at the alphabet blocks in the corner of the nursery, turning one over and over, a small wooden A, and her face held the particular blankness of someone looking at a word in a language they almost spoke.

"She'll relearn it," Corvus said, corking the bottle. Inside, something glowed faintly, warm as a coal. "Memory is not lost, only transferred. She'll learn her letters again by summer—from someone else, in someone else's voice. But the *first* time belongs to me now. Firsts are the purest coin there is. Nothing dilutes them."

He set the bottle on the shelf, and the shelf was full—hundreds of bottles, thousands, each labeled in his crabbed hand. *First snowfall, urchin boy, age six. Mother's lullaby, twins, both. The taste of honey, widow, age seventy-one.*

"Why do you want them?" Silas asked. It was the wrong question. They knew it was the wrong question even as it left their mouth, because Corvus went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Want?" he said softly. "Child, I am owed. There is a difference. Now—" and the stillness broke, and he was dry and pleasant again, "—fetch the ink. Your lessons begin tonight. You have a talent for noticing; I saw it the moment you came through my door. Noticing is the thief's first virtue. The second is forgetting what you noticed."

* * *

But Silas could not forget. That was the flaw in them, the crack Corvus never found, or found too late.

They remembered everything. The way the bottles hummed at night if you pressed your ear to the shelf—a chord of other people's happinesses, faint and awful, like music heard through a wall. The way Merrin stopped humming songs she didn't know she'd lost. The way the urchin boy, sold away in spring, was described in the ledger afterward in past tense, and how the entry for him faded over months until only the price remained.

And they noticed the most important thing of all, the thing that took three years to notice fully: Corvus never collected from himself. His own account, kept in a separate book bound in grey leather, was perfectly, immaculately empty. No credits. No debts. No childhood. When Silas once asked him where *he* had grown up, Corvus had looked at them for a long moment with an expression Silas could not read then and understood completely twenty years later, standing over their own blank pages.

"I don't remember," he had said. And then, with something almost like tenderness: "It is the safest way to live. Nothing can be taken from a man who owns nothing. Nothing can be owed by a man who owes nothing. Remember that, child. One day you may need to be no one."

He meant it as a lesson in self-preservation. He did not mean it as an escape plan. But Silas was twelve, and thirteen, and fourteen, and they took the lesson and turned it over the way Merrin had turned the wooden block, looking for the shape of it, the use of it.

If memory was an account, then a person was a ledger.
And any ledger could be balanced.
And any balance could be zeroed.

* * *

The apprenticeship had its own cruelties, quieter than the green chair. Corvus taught them to slip into sleepers' rooms and lift memories the way other thieves lifted spoons—two fingers to the temple, a whispered word in a language that predated the language, the sensation of drawing a thread out of wet cloth. He taught them the taxonomy of takeable things: firsts, loves, griefs, names. He taught them that names were the heaviest cargo of all, a whole life compressed to a syllable, and that no one ever sold theirs willingly, which was why no one ever bought them honestly.

He also taught them, without meaning to, everything they would need to destroy him.

Because Corvus was vain about his craft. He kept his master-bottles—the ones holding pieces of himself he'd cut away over the decades, trimming his own soul for convenience—in the same cabinet as his strongest wine, behind a lock he demonstrated proudly to a fifteen-year-old apprentice. *Watch the tumblers, child. Feel them fall.* And Silas, who had spent four years learning to notice, felt all five tumblers fall into their memory and stay there, polished and perfect, forever.

That was the joke they never laughed at: Corvus had trained a memory-thief by making them memorize everything.

On the night of the escape, Silas was sixteen, and the house smelled of tallow and vinegar and rotting pears, and it smelled like home, and that was the worst part. Somewhere upstairs Merrin—who had relearned her letters and half her songs and had begun, cautiously, to laugh again—was asleep. Down the hall, two new children huddled in the nursery, freshly purchased, still crying quietly into each other's shoulders.

Silas stood in the study with the grey book open on the desk and the cabinet standing unlocked, and they understood that there were two ways out of this house.

One way was to steal enough to buy freedom: fill a sack with firsts and loves, sell them in the free cities, vanish rich. Corvus would hunt them, of course, but a thief with capital could outrun a creditor.

The other way was written in Corvus's own empty grey book.

Silas looked at the shelves. Thousands of bottles, each one a person's brightest hour, humming their terrible chord. They could not carry them all. They could not carry even a tithe. Whatever they chose, the rest would stay here, accruing interest in the dark.

So they did what a bookkeeper does. They balanced what they could.

They took the cabinet key and emptied Corvus's master-shelf first—every fragment of himself he'd excised, forty years of discarded self, and they poured them back into him while he slept, all of it at once, unfiltered. What woke in the master's bed was not Corvus. It was every man he had ever refused to be, wearing his body, and it lay there weeping and laughing and calling for people dead fifty years, and it did not rise, and it did not follow.

Then Silas went shelf to shelf with a cart, and they broke every seal, and they gave the house back its harvest. They worked until dawn pressing bottles to sleeping temples, returning first snowfalls and lullabies and the taste of honey, and the house filled up with a sound it had never made before: people dreaming their own dreams. Merrin woke mid-melody and sat up singing, bewildered and whole. The new children in the nursery dreamed of mothers they hadn't seen in years, and smiled in their sleep.

Some bottles were empty—taken too far, spent, gone. For those debtors Silas could do nothing but write their names in the front of the grey book, so that somewhere, in someone's careful hand, the loss would at least be recorded. They wrote thirty-one names. Their handwriting shook on all of them.

Last of all, Silas turned to their own account.

Here is what it held: a river with their mother's name in it. A kitchen with steam on the windows. A woman's voice singing low, off-key, patient. A hand on their hair. The word *Sila* spoken like a blessing. Sixteen years of small warm credits, every one of them real, every one of them the kind of coin Corvus had taught them was purest.

Nothing can be taken from a man who owns nothing.

Silas stood before the grey book with the stoppered bottle in their hands—the one they had prepared weeks ago, labeled in their own neat script—and they hesitated exactly as long as it takes a candle to gutter and steady.

Then they thought of Merrin turning the wooden block over and over. Of the urchin boy fading to a price. Of thirty-one names in shaking ink. Of the fact that Corvus had colleagues, creditors, competitors—that the world beyond Candlewick Lane was full of people who kept accounts, and that a runaway carrying a childhood worth stealing was a beacon, and a runaway carrying nothing was smoke.

Grief came later. Grief always comes later; that is its one mercy.

What came first was the work. Two fingers to their own temple. The whispered word. The thread drawn out of wet cloth—one strand, then another, then all of them, the river going gray, the kitchen going cold, the voice thinning to silence, the name dissolving on the tongue like sugar in rain. They poured themselves into the bottle until the bottle glowed like a captive dawn, and they corked it, and they looked at the label.

*S. Blackwood. Complete. Age sixteen.*

They did not recognize the name. That was the point. They read it the way you read a stranger's epitaph, with polite, distant sorrow, and they felt the space where the grief should be and found it swept clean, and in the clean place there was only a single instruction, the last thing they had written for themselves before the last thread came out, scrawled on the inside of the bottle's wrapping in a child's desperate hand:

*Do not open this. Do not sell this. Whoever you are now—you were loved once. Keep the account.*

Silas Blackwood—who was that? someone who had lived here, someone who had done this—tucked the bottle into their coat, over their heart, where it hummed faintly against their ribs like a second pulse, and walked out of the house on Candlewick Lane into a dawn the color of unwritten paper.

Behind them, the house was full of returned voices. Ahead of them, the city was waking. They knew the roads, the guilds, the price of bread, the five tumblers of a hundred locks. They knew everything except who had learned it all, and why that someone had wanted so badly to become no one.

They kept the account anyway. They kept it for twenty years—every debt recorded, every name preserved, their own pages deliberately, devoutly blank. It was, they told themselves, only prudence. Only good bookkeeping.

* * *

Twenty years later, in the guildhouse of the Guild of Impossible Things, Silas sat late over the ledgers with a dying priest's prayer folded beneath the inkwell, and the bottle over their heart hummed a note it had never hummed before.

They pressed their palm flat against their coat, against the small warm weight of the self they had abandoned, and for the first time since Candlewick Lane, the blankness in them ached like a missing tooth. Something ancient had stirred when they read that prayer aloud. Something had heard them. And now the bottle—their whole erased childhood, sealed and sleeping—was answering it, note for note, like recognizing like.

From the west, where the usurper's armies gathered, the horizon had begun to glow. Odessa would say it was forge-fires. The priests in the market squares were saying miracles.

Silas looked at the humming bottle, and at the prayer, and understood that the account they had kept blank for twenty years was about to be audited by something far older than any guild—

—and that whatever came to collect, it already knew their name.
