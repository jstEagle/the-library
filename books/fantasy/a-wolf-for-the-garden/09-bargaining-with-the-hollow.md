## 9. Bargaining with the Hollow

The chapel had drowned a century before either of us was born, and still the water refused to leave it.

It stood in a valley that the maps called the Sorrowfold, though Wren's forbidden atlas named it something older—*Vaelmire*, she'd said, tracing letters that no living tongue pronounced. A river had changed its mind one spring and swallowed the village whole, and when the waters receded they left behind this: a chapel of grey stone with its feet still in a shallow lake, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against the sky, its doors swollen shut with a hundred years of wet.

Tam stood at my shoulder on the shore, hugging his pack to his chest the way children hug things they're afraid will be taken. "It's wrong here," he said. "The birds won't cross the water."

He was right. Gulls wheeled along the shoreline in a neat, invisible line, as if the lake were ringed with glass. Beyond it, reeds grew in perfect silence. No frogs. No insects. The water lay flat as poured pewter, and in it the chapel's reflection was sharper than the chapel itself—as if the drowned image were the true building, and the one above the waterline only its poor, faded copy.

"The rain fell black at Ashenmere," I said. "Warm, too. Rain shouldn't be warm." I pulled my coat tighter. "Nothing about this is going to be comfortable."

"Is anything?" Tam asked, and there it was again—that dry little edge he'd developed since the shrine road, a child learning to sharpen himself against the world. It broke my heart and made me proud in equal measure, which I was beginning to understand was simply what family felt like.

"No," I admitted. "But some things pay better than others."

I waded in.

* * *

The water came to my knees at the threshold, my thighs by the third pew, and the cold of it was not an ordinary cold. It climbed into the bones of me and settled there, and I felt—there is no better word for it—*audited*. As though something beneath the surface were opening my ledgers page by page, checking my entries, finding them wanting.

Light came down through a hole in the roof in a single slanted column, and in that column hung motes of dust that did not drift. They hung motionless, each one fixed in place like stars on a chart. The altar was above the waterline still, barely—a slab of black stone furred with white silt—and behind it, the window.

Chapel windows are meant to show you saints. This one showed a king.

The glass had survived the flood intact, which should have been impossible, and it depicted a crowned figure seated on a throne of wheat and iron both, his hands open, his face turned upward as though listening to something just out of frame. The colors were strange. Not the jewel-tones of southern glass but muted things: ash-blue, rust, the gold of old honey. And the crown itself had been rendered in a color I had no name for—a dark, hungry violet that seemed to drink the light around it rather than receive it.

I had seen that violet before. In the resonance of Tam's sold birthday, humming in its bottle. In the prayer the dying priest had pressed into my hands, its ink still faintly warm after three days. In the ledger entries Odessa had ordered me to trace—the debts of worship, written in a hand that shook.

Every miracle left the same signature. I had simply never known what it belonged to.

"There," Tam whispered, and pointed.

At the base of the altar, half in the water, sat a woman.

She had been kneeling so long that her knees had grown into the posture—her legs folded beneath her in the shallows, her hands clasped before her, her head bowed. Her hair had gone the grey of drowned wood. Her dress was fine once; now it hung from her like kelp. She did not look up when we entered. She did not look up when our footsteps sent ripples lapping against her. She prayed—or made the shape of praying, lips moving over words that had worn away to nothing, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands.

I knew what I was looking at. I had seen it in miniature at every shrine between here and Ashenmere: the hollowed ones, the answered. Those who had prayed to the old gods and been heard, and paid.

But I had never seen one who had paid *this much*.

"Hallo," I said gently.

Her lips kept moving. *Please please please please*, maybe, or perhaps nothing at all anymore, just the habit of a mouth that had forgotten everything else.

I crouched at the water's edge, three pews away, close enough to see her face. It was the face of someone who had been emptied carefully rather than violently—a face with all its features present and correct and none of them connected to anything inside. Her eyes were open. They were brown, and wet, and reflected the stained-glass king perfectly, and behind them there was nothing looking out. Or almost nothing. That was the thing that made my chest tighten: not emptiness but *almost*-emptiness, the way a house isn't empty if a single candle still burns in a far room.

Something in her was still home. Something small.

"I'm going to sit down," I told her, and did, right there in the flooded nave, letting the water soak through to my skin because it seemed important to meet her where she was. Tam hesitated, then sat beside me, his small shoulder against my arm. The woman did not react.

I opened my satchel and took out a memory bottle.

Now, understand what I was doing, because I want it recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own skull: I am a thief. Stealing memories is the first thing I ever learned to do well, and for thirty years I have treated other people's pasts as inventory. Bottles, ledgers, debts, interest. I have traded a widow's wedding day for safe passage and never lost sleep over it. That was the life I built. That was the point of the life I built—to be a man who could do such things and remain unbothered, unrecorded, unknown.

But the woman at the altar had given everything to something that wore the shape of a god, and the something had taken and taken and taken, and left her kneeling in cold water mouthing *please* to no one. And I found that my hands, which have never shaken over inventory, were not entirely steady.

I uncorked the bottle anyway.

Memory doesn't pour like wine. It breathes out, a silver-grey vapor that curls in the air and seeks the nearest mind the way water seeks a low place. I let a thread of it drift toward her—not forced, just offered, the way you'd offer bread across a table.

Her nostrils flared. Her lips stopped moving.

Slowly—so slowly, like a door opening on rusted hinges—she turned her head and looked at me.

"Oh," she said. Her voice was a ruin. "Oh, you smell like *before*."

"Do I?"

"Before the answer." Her eyes fixed on the vapor curling between us with an expression I can only call hunger, and hated myself for recognizing, because I have seen that same expression on debtors' faces in the guildhouse a hundred times and always looked away. "What is it? What did you bring me?"

"A morning," I said. It was a minor thing, plucked years ago from a man who sold it to settle a gambling debt: a kitchen, sunlight, the smell of bread, someone laughing in another room. Ordinary. Worthless, by guild reckoning. "It's yours if you'll talk to me."

Her hands unclasped. For the first time, I saw what they held: a small wooden token, carved in the shape of a crown, worn nearly smooth by touch. My stomach dropped through the flooded floor.

"Talk," she repeated, tasting the word. "About him."

"About who?"

"About *him*." She lifted her chin toward the window, toward the king in ash-blue and rust. "You came to ask about him. Everyone comes to ask about him, eventually. The ones who still have questions left in them." A terrible little smile moved across her face. "Most of us run out."

* * *

She took the morning in small sips, holding the vapor to her lips like tea, and with each sip a little more of her came back—not memories, I hadn't given her those, just the *texture* of being a person, warmth and light and the sound of laughter through a wall. Enough to make the candle in the far room of her burn a little brighter. Her name, when I asked, took three tries to surface.

"Maren," she said at last, wonderingly, as if greeting a stranger. "My name is Maren. I—I had a daughter." Her face crumpled and reformed. "Did I have a daughter? There was a girl. There was a girl who called me Mama. I can't—" Her hands flew to her mouth. "I can't remember her face. I remember that I loved her. I remember the *shape* of loving her. But her face is gone, and I don't even know what I asked for. What did I ask for, that cost so much?"

"What did you ask for?" I echoed softly.

"Healing." The word came out flat. "Our village. The sweating sickness came through, the summer before the flood, and the old shrines had been silent for two hundred years, everyone knows the gods stopped listening two hundred years ago, but Father Anselm said try, try the old words, the true names, and we did, we knelt in this chapel and we said the old prayers and—" She laughed, and it was the worst sound I had heard in a year of bad sounds. "And something *answered*. You cannot imagine. You spend your whole life shouting into a well and then one day the well shouts back. It was like the sun coming up inside my chest. And the sickness lifted. Every child recovered by morning. We sang until our voices broke."

"And the price?"

"We didn't know there was one." Her fingers found the wooden crown-token and closed around it. "That's the trick of it. That's the beautiful, filthy trick. The old prayers don't mention payment. The old gods never charged. Giving was supposed to be *freely given*, that's what the words mean, that's why they're the true words. But whatever answers now—" her voice dropped to a whisper, and the water in the nave went very still around us, as if listening— "whatever answers now takes. It reached into me while I was singing. I felt it like cold fingers behind my eyes. And it took my daughter's face first. Do you understand? It took the *best thing first*. It knows where the best things are. It goes straight for them, like a magpie, like a thief—"

She stopped. Looked at me. Looked at the satchel at my hip, at the bottles within, and something flickered across her ruined face—not accusation. Recognition.

"You're a thief," she said.

"Yes."

"Good." She leaned forward, water streaming off her shoulders, and gripped my wrist with fingers strong as roots. "Then listen to me, thief, because I have been sitting in this water for a year piecing together what it took from me, trading scraps of myself back and forth with the others like beggars dividing a crust, and I have assembled enough to tell you what nobody else alive knows. Will you hear it?"

"I came a long way to hear it."

"It's not a god."

The words fell into the silence of the chapel and rang there.

"The thing that answers the prayers," Maren said, "is not a god. The old gods are dead or sleeping or gone beyond the sky—I don't know, I only know their wells are dry, I've knelt at every shrine in three provinces and felt nothing but stone. Whatever hears us now lives *closer*. It lives under the crown." She raised the wooden token. "Do you know whose face is in your window? Neither does anyone. No saint. No god. Two hundred years ago there was a kingdom here—Vaelmire, the maps that survive call it nothing, the histories call it nothing, it was *erased*, whole, every record, every grave-marker, every song—and the king of Vaelmire was beloved the way no king has been beloved since. And then one day his own crown was set upon his head, and he stopped being beloved, and his kingdom stopped being anywhere, and the prayers started being answered."

"That's a story," I said. "A bitter one. You've had everything taken from you; your mind built a villain to blame."

"Did it?" She smiled, and there was such grief in it. "Then explain the screaming, thief."

"...The screaming?"

"When the miracle comes. When the answer arrives. You feel such joy, such unbearable joy—but underneath it, underneath, there is a sound. Like a voice stretched thin across a great distance. Like a man shouting through a wall. I thought it was my imagination. Then I met the others, the hollowed ones from the other villages, and they all heard it too, and we compared what we heard, and thief—" her grip tightened on my wrist, hard enough to bruise— "we all heard the same words. The same words, in the same voice, under every miracle in every shrine. *Let me out. Let me out. Let me out.*"

The water in the nave trembled. Just slightly. Just once.

Tam made a small sound and pressed closer to me.

"It's a king," Maren whispered. "Bound beneath his own crown. And every prayer that gets answered, every healing, every rainstorm, every miracle—it's him, screaming through the bars of his cage, and the thing that locked him in holds the key and spends his voice like coin. He heals your sick because he *wants* to heal your sick—he is that kind of king, even now, even broken, even caged, he hears a child crying and he *answers*—and the usurper reaches in through the answering and takes his payment out of our hides. The god of the new age is a prisoner, and his jailer is collecting rent."

I sat very still in the cold water and felt pity curdle in my chest, thicken, darken, turn into something with teeth.

Because I knew cages. I had spent my whole life building one around myself—no name in any ledger, no face in any memory but my own, no handle by which the world could grasp me. I had thought it freedom, staying unrecorded. Sitting in that drowned chapel, hearing a bound king beg through the walls of a miracle, I understood for the first time that I had merely chosen a smaller cell and called it safety.

And anger—old, banked, patient anger, the kind I usually kept for my master in the flashback-dark behind my eyes—rose in me now on behalf of a stranger two centuries dead. Someone had done to that king what my master did to me. Had hollowed him, erased him, worn his kingdom like a stolen coat. And now wore his crown, and let him scream.

"Who?" I asked. My voice came out rough. "Who holds the crown now?"

Maren's brow furrowed. She drew back, and I watched the question travel down through the wreckage of her mind, past the places where her daughter's face used to be, past the places where her village's name used to be, down to some deep shelf where she had stored one last fragment, hoarded like the final coin of a bankrupt house.

"I traded for it," she murmured. "Last winter. From a pilgrim who'd come from the capital, from the coronation anniversary rites. He'd seen the king there—the false king, the one on the throne now. He'd seen him up close, at the ceremony, and he sold me what he saw, and I gave him—" she touched her throat— "my singing voice. Fair trade. I wasn't using it for anything anymore."

"And?"

"The crown whispers," she said. "Everyone in the court knows the king talks to his crown, prays to it, they think it piety. But the pilgrim saw the king alone, once, in a corridor, and the king was gripping the armrest of his chair so hard the wood had cracked, and his face—" she faltered— "his face was the face of a man listening to something he couldn't refuse. And the pilgrim heard the king say, very quietly, to no one: *I don't remember her laugh. What did it take today? What did it take?*" 

Maren looked up at me, and her brown eyes, empty and full at once, held mine.

"King Aldric," she said. "The usurper. The crown is eating *him* too, thief. Bit by bit, day by day, the same way it ate me. He sits on the throne wearing the cage, and he thinks he's the jailer, and the jailer is already inside him, taking his daughter's laugh, taking his mother's face, taking and taking, and he can feel it going and he cannot stop it and he cannot take the crown off, because the crown comes off for no one. Not for kings." Her voice sank to almost nothing. "Only for a price. Only for a willing gift, freely given, of equal weight to a soul. That's the old law. That's the true words. The crown was forged to be *paid off*, and no one has ever paid."

Somewhere above us, in the leaning bell tower, a rope creaked in the wind, and the silent bell gave the ghost of a toll.

Tam tugged my sleeve. His face was pale, but his jaw was set in that new, sharpened way. "Silas," he said. "The armies. Wren said the armies march in—what, weeks? Days?" He swallowed. "If the crown eats Aldric all the way down, what's left sitting on that throne when it's finished?"

I didn't answer. I didn't have to. We had both seen the hollowed ones. We had both knelt beside Maren in the cold.

I bought the rest of Maren's fragments—all of them, every scrap she'd hoarded, paying her out of my own stock bottle by bottle until my satchel hung light and her eyes had candlelight in them again. She wouldn't leave the chapel. "Someone has to keep him company," she said, nodding at the window, at the listening king. "Even a prisoner should be able to see a friendly face." So I left her a dozen mornings, a summer afternoon, a first snowfall—enough texture to live on, enough warmth to keep the far-room candle burning—and Tam pressed his own ration of honey-bread into her hands, which made her cry, which made me understand that she was the richest woman in the Sorrowfold after all.

We waded out into the grey light. Behind us the chapel stood in its mirror-still lake, the king in his window watching us go with open hands.

"Silas," Tam said quietly, when the shore was under our boots and the gulls dared to wheel overhead again. "What do we do?"

I looked south, toward the free cities, toward the guildhouse and Odessa's ledgers and the road that ran, eventually, to a capital where a man was being devoured a memory at a time in front of his entire court.

"Now?" I said. "Now we find Wren. Because if anyone has mapped the way into a kingdom that was erased from the world—"

Far to the south, so distant it might have been thunder, the horizon flickered. Once. Twice. The light of many fires, or one very large one.

"—then we'd best ask her before the world loses anything else."
