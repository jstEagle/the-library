## 10. The Usurper's Smile

The crown whispered to King Aldric before he woke.

It always did. A sound like sand poured over parchment, like a hundred voices speaking just beneath the threshold of hearing, so that a man could spend an entire lifetime convincing himself it was only his own thoughts, only the blood moving in his ears, only the wind in the high windows of the palace. Aldric had worn the crown for eleven years and had long ago stopped asking whether the whispering was real. Real things could be argued with. The whispering simply *was*, the way gravity was, the way dawn was—arriving each morning without permission and refusing every negotiation.

He opened his eyes to the grey light of the capital, which came through the windows of the royal bedchamber already tired, already sifted through coal smoke from ten thousand hearths below. Somewhere in the lower city a bell was ringing the hour, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of the faithful gathering in the Temple of the Radiant Crown for morning miracles. He could not see them from here, but he knew what they looked like: thousands of faces upturned, mouths open, eyes wet with gratitude, waiting for the priests to lay hands on their sick and their lame and their grieving. Waiting to be *answered*.

Eleven years ago, there had been no miracles in this kingdom. There had been no answered prayers at all, only the old silence that every priest called faith and every honest man called absence. Then Aldric had taken the throne—not inherited it, *taken* it, though history would be written otherwise—and the crown had begun to whisper, and the prayers had begun to be heard, and the people had fallen on their knees in the streets weeping with joy.

He had told himself, in those first years, that the price was small. That whatever the crown took in exchange, it took from him alone, and a king who wanted peace for his people could afford to bleed privately.

He no longer told himself that. He no longer remembered why he would have.

* * *

His valet dressed him in silence, as the valets were trained to do, and Aldric stood before the tall mirror while the man settled the crown upon his head. It was iron beneath the gold, though only three living people knew that—the smiths who forged it had been promoted into graves within the month. To everyone else it was simply the Radiant Crown, ancient and holy, recovered from some forgotten vault by divine providence on the very eve of Aldric's coronation. It sat heavier than any crown should. It always had. He had stopped noticing the weight the way a man stops noticing a stone in his boot, by letting part of his foot die.

In the mirror, the king looked well. That was the thing about the crown—it kept him looking well. His hair stayed dark at fifty-one. His back stayed straight. His face retained the strong jaw and clear eyes that had won him the loyalty of half the army before he'd ever made a claim on the throne. The mirror showed a king in his prime, vigorous and certain, smiling the smile that had been painted onto a thousand banners across nine provinces.

Aldric studied the smile and tried to remember when he had last used it honestly.

There had been someone—a girl—who laughed at that smile. Not courtiers laughing because laughing was survival. Someone who laughed the way children laugh, with her whole body, throwing her head back—

He reached for the thought the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark and finds one step missing. His stomach dropped. He gripped the edge of the washstand until his knuckles went white.

"Your Majesty?" the valet asked.

"Send for my daughter," Aldric said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Tell her I'll break my fast with her."

"Princess Maren departed for the summer palace at Vellamont yesterday, sire. As you ordered."

"As I—" He turned. The word *ordered* hung in the air between them like smoke. "I don't recall ordering that."

"It was Tuesday last, Your Majesty. You wished her away from the city during the mustering. For her safety." The valet's eyes were carefully fixed on the second button of the royal coat. Servants learned early which of the king's forgettings were safe to witness and which were not, and this one, apparently, sat near the border.

Tuesday last. Four days ago. Four days, and the order had gone out of him like water out of a cracked cup, leaving nothing behind but the shape of where it had been. Aldric nodded slowly and watched himself nod in the mirror, and the crowned reflection smiled its painted smile back at him, and for one vertiginous moment he was not sure which of them was doing the smiling.

"Write to her," he said. "Tell her to come home. Tell her—" Tell her what? *Tell her your father is losing pieces of himself and cannot find where they fall.* "Tell her I miss her laugh."

The valet bowed and left, and Aldric stood alone in the grey light, listening to the whispering rise around his skull like insects waking.

*She laughed,* said the crown, or something wearing the crown's voice, gentle as a nurse. *You remember.*

"I don't," Aldric said aloud, to the empty room. "That's the whole of it. I don't."

*You do,* the whisper crooned. *Here. Let us give it back to you.*

And it did—that was the horror of it, that was the trap with teeth on both sides—the crown gave him something. A sound rose in his memory, bright and ringing, a girl's laughter spilling down a stone corridor, and Aldric clutched at it with both hands like a drowning man clutching driftwood, and it was *her*, it had to be her, it was warm and alive and it filled the hollow place in his chest exactly to its edges—

Except that when he examined it, turning it over in his mind the way he'd once turned over coins to check for clipping, the laughter had no face attached. No corridor. No season. No reason for the laughing. It was laughter the way a portrait was a person—all the surface, none of the soul. A coin struck from base metal, gilded on one side only.

"You're feeding me back my own daughter in counterfeit," he said quietly.

The whispering did not deny it. It never denied anything. Denial was a wall, and walls could be pushed against. The crown preferred doors.

*It is still her laugh,* it murmured, soothing, reasonable. *Only borrowed. Only held in trust. Everything we take, we keep safe. Would you rather have nothing?*

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the hook set so deep in him he could no longer feel the barb. Eleven years of small withdrawals—the name of his first horse, the taste of his mother's bread, the color of the sky on his wedding day, the grief at his father's funeral (he knew he should have grieved; the records said he had wept; he felt nothing but a polite acknowledgment that grief was customary)—and in exchange, the crown returned facsimiles, echoes, beautiful hollow copies, until Aldric could no longer tell which of his memories were lived and which were minted. He was becoming a country of paper currency. Rich on every surface. Worthless at the treasury.

He had tried, once, two years ago, to take the crown off.

He did not like to think about that hour. The pain had been—there were no words for it that survived being spoken; the attempt itself had been taken from him afterward, he was almost sure, though the scar of it remained as a flinch in his hands whenever the valet approached with the crown. What he remembered—and he trusted this memory, because it hurt, and the crown's counterfeits never hurt—was the feeling of roots. Of the iron band grown down through his skull and into his spine and further, further, into something vast and screaming far below the floor of the world.

Because that was the other secret. The one he had never spoken aloud, the one he suspected he was not permitted to know and was permitted to know only because knowing it served the crown's purpose:

Something under the crown was screaming.

He heard it sometimes, in the deep watches of the night, beneath the whispering. Not words. Not quite. A sound like a man shouting through fathoms of water, like a bell rung inside a mountain. And the whispering, he had come to understand, was not the voice of the crown at all. It was the sound of the screaming being *translated*. Flattened. Made useful. Every miracle in the Temple below, every healing, every answered prayer in every village of nine provinces, was that distant scream bent into a shape the faithful could kneel to.

Aldric did not know how he understood this. Perhaps he had known it once, fully, and the knowledge had been taken and only the scar of it left behind. But he understood it the way his body understood falling.

* * *

The war council convened at midmorning in the Chamber of Nine Banners, where the conquered provinces hung in cloth along the walls and the tenth space waited, draped in black silk, for the banner of the free cities.

Lord Marshal Corvane spread the maps across the table—real maps, inked on vellum, though Aldric noticed with a distant unease that he could no longer read the old survey marks along the river borders. The letters swam. He had been literate since the age of five. He covered the lapse by leaning back and letting Corvane talk, which Corvane was always willing to do at length.

"The Third Legion crosses the Serein ford in six days. The Fourth follows. By month's end we will have forty thousand men on the march toward the free cities, and the priests report the roads lined with pilgrims begging to serve." Corvane's finger traced the map. "Ashford refused terms. Ashford is ash. The others have seen it. Emberfall has already sent envoys suing for mercy."

"And the guilds?" Aldric asked. "The Guild of Impossible Things sends no envoys."

A flicker crossed Corvane's face—disgust, quickly mastered. "The guilds are thieves and debt-brokers, sire. Their kind does not fight. They hide. Our spymaster has agents in their house even now."

"Good." Aldric heard his own voice say it, smooth and kingly, and hated it, and could not have said precisely why he hated it, only that somewhere underneath the hatred was an older thing, a loyalty with no object, like a dog waiting at a door for a master who had moved away years ago. "Continue."

Corvane continued. Supply lines, grain levies, the disposition of the miracle-priests who would march with each legion to heal the wounded and—this was said delicately, in the euphemism the court had agreed upon—to *fortify the faith* of the cities that wavered. Fortify the faith. Take their names, their birthdays, their mothers' faces, and leave them kneeling and grateful and empty. Aldric signed the orders as they were placed before him. His signature had changed over the years; he had noticed that much. It had grown more ornate, more confident, less like handwriting than like a sigil stamped by a seal. He wondered whose hand had taught it that flourish. He wondered if it had ever been his hand at all.

"Sire." This from Lady Isbet, the spymaster, a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes like awls. "One matter requires your attention. A priest died in the free cities last week. One of ours, planted in a shrine road parish. Before he burned—there was fire involved, the details are unclear—he passed a document to persons unknown. A prayer. An answered prayer, written in the old rite."

The room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when powerful men are afraid and pretending not to be.

"A prayer," Aldric repeated.

"Answered outside the crown's dispensation, sire. The resonance was wrong. The priests who examined the ash say the answering voice was—" she hesitated, choosing among forbidden words— "*older*. And they say the prayer was addressed to a god by name. A name that appears nowhere in our liturgy. A name that was cut from every record two centuries ago."

Two centuries. Something turned over in Aldric's chest, slow and enormous, like a leviathan shifting in deep water. Two hundred years ago. The vanished kingdom. Even the name of it had been scoured from the maps—Lady Isbet's own cartographers had once shown him the gap in the old atlases, a blank space where mountains and rivers and a capital city had been, erased so thoroughly that the vellum seemed to remember being written on. He had asked then why the blankness frightened him, and no one had been able to answer, and he had dreamed for a week afterward of a door buried under a mountain with someone knocking from the other side.

"What was the name?" he asked.

"We do not write it, sire. We do not speak it. The priests burned the transcription." Lady Isbet's awl-eyes held his, and for a moment—did he imagine it?—something in them was not reporting to a king but warning a man. "But the resonance matched something else, sire. Something from our own archives. The forging records. The vault records. The records of what the crown was made *from*."

The whispering around Aldric's skull rose sharply, a tide suddenly loud, and for a moment it was not soothing at all but urgent, many-voiced, alarmed—and beneath it, faint and far down, the screaming changed pitch. Turned, if a scream could be said to turn, toward him. Toward the mention of its name.

Every eye in the chamber was on him. He realized his hands were flat on the table. He realized he had risen halfway from his chair without deciding to. He realized that the smile was on his face—the painted smile, the banner smile—working its muscles all on its own while the rest of him stood behind it like a man pressed against the back of his own portrait.

"Then find whoever holds that prayer," the smile said, in a voice like warm bronze. "Find them, and bring them to me alive. If the old powers are stirring in the free cities, I would know what wakes them. No one answers prayers in my kingdom without my leave."

*My kingdom.* Whose phrase was that? It rang in the chamber, and the council bowed, satisfied, and the meeting dissolved into the scrape of chairs and the rustle of maps, and Aldric stood among the nine banners with the black silk hanging empty in its place, and let the smile fade from his face the way frost fades from glass.

*Well done,* whispered the crown, tender as a lover. *That was well done.*

"Who am I talking to?" Aldric asked it, barely moving his lips. "When you answer me. Who is answering?"

The whispering paused. In eleven years, it had never paused.

*We are the crown,* it said at last. *We are yours. You are ours. There is no difference worth remembering.*

*Worth remembering.* The phrasing snagged in him like a fishhook. Not *worth knowing*. Worth *remembering*—as if the difference had existed once, and been taken, and only the grammar of its absence remained.

* * *

That night he dismissed his attendants early and walked the long gallery alone, past the portraits of kings who had never existed—every dynasty older than eleven years was invention; he had commissioned those paintings himself, or someone had, using his hands—and stopped at the one portrait that was true. A small painting, unframed, kept in defiance of protocol: a girl of seven on a garden wall, mid-laugh, caught by some dead painter's honest brush.

Maren. Her name surfaced easily enough; names were shallow water. It was everything beneath the name that had gone dark. He stood before the painting for a long time, searching his own heart the way a man searches a burned house, lifting beam after beam, finding after finding nothing but ash shaped like furniture.

He could remember that she had laughed. He could remember that hearing it had been the best thing in his life—knew it the way one knows a sum is correct without being able to show the work. But the laugh itself, the sound, the *why* of it, the particular afternoon—gone. And in the hollow where it had been, the crown had packed its counterfeit, that faceless ringing laughter, and expected him to be grateful.

"They're going to take you too," he told the painted girl. "Whatever's left. When I'm finished being useful. They'll take the shape of you, and I'll sit in that chair smiling at a stranger, and I won't even know to mourn."

The painted girl laughed at him from seven years old and two hundred miles away, and did not answer, and could not.

Far below the palace—below the foundations, below the vaults, below the buried door he was not permitted to remember dreaming of—something screamed, and the crown translated the scream into comfort, and poured it into him like warm wine, and Aldric drank because there was nothing else to drink, and slept, and dreamed of a garden.

In the dream there was a wolf in the garden. It did not threaten him. It sat among the flowers with the patience of stone, watching the palace windows, and when Aldric tried to approach, he found he could not remember how walking worked. The wolf looked at him with eyes the color of ledger-ink, and its mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the distance, he understood it perfectly, the way he understood the screaming under the crown:

*Someone is coming to count what you owe.*

He woke before dawn with the taste of iron in his mouth and the whispering already smoothing the dream away, sand over parchment, tide over footprints. By the time the valet came with the crown's morning polishing, Aldric had lost the wolf, lost the garden, lost the words *count* and *owe*—but not, somehow, the cold place they had left behind, a print in the snow of him, already filling with new snow.

In the east, beyond nine provinces and a burning city and a blank space on the map, four days' ride behind an army of forty thousand, someone had picked up a stolen prayer and read it aloud.

The crown did not know that yet.

But the thing beneath it had stopped screaming, and started, very softly, to listen.
