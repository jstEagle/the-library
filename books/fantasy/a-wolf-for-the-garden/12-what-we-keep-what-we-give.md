## 12. What We Keep, What We Give

The guildhouse smelled of smoke and old paper, and beneath both, faintly, of the tincture Odessa Vane took for pain—a bitter green smell like crushed stems, like the first hour of spring before anything blooms.

Silas climbed the stairs slowly, a cup of willowbark and honey balanced in both hands, listening to the house breathe around them. The raiders had come four nights ago with fire and crowbars and the particular silence of men paid to be quiet, and the guildhouse still wore the bruises. A door hung off its hinges on the second landing. Scorch marks fanned across the wall of the archive like black feathers. Someone had nailed a sheet of canvas over the shattered window at the stair's turning, and the wind found the loose corner of it and worried at it, over and over, a soft irregular heartbeat.

Odessa lay in the narrow bed in the room that had once been the guildmaster's study and was now simply hers, propped against pillows gone gray with use. She had been a tall woman once, tall and hard as a ship's mast, and even now, hollowed by fever and the long shallow work of healing, she retained the posture of someone who had never once asked the world's permission. Her left arm was splinted and bound to her chest. Her right hand rested on the coverlet, and beside it, always, within reach of those fingers, lay the ledger.

Not the great ledger. The *small* one—the one Silas had kept, the one with their own past deliberately blank, page after page of careful nothing. She had been clutching it when they pulled her from the burning archive, they said. Clutching it and laughing at the raiders, telling them the guild's debts would outlive every one of them.

Her eyes found Silas in the doorway before Silas's foot crossed the threshold. They always did. It was the one miracle Silas had never managed to explain: Odessa Vane seeing through them as though they were glass.

"You walk like a thief in your own house," she rasped.

"It is a house full of things worth stealing."

"It's a house full of *debts* worth stealing." She tried to smile; it cost her. "Come in, Blackwood. Sit. You hover like a debt collector."

Silas set the cup on the bedside table and sat in the chair that had migrated, over four nights, from the corner to the bedside and now seemed to have always lived there. The candle on the table guttered in a draft. Outside, the city of Vell was holding its breath—the markets half-empty, the harbor bells ringing the wrong hours, and everywhere, on every corner and in every tavern, the same question passed mouth to mouth like a coin too hot to hold: *how many days?*

Three, said the merchants, who had it from a carter, who had it from a soldier's cousin.

Five, said the harborfolk, watching the horizon for sails that were not sails but smoke.

*No time at all*, said the priests of the crown, who had begun preaching openly in Vell's squares now, their voices honeyed and their eyes flat as coins. *Surrender, and be healed. Resist, and be ash. The god-king gives, and the god-king takes away.*

Silas had heard the bound king scream through a miracle. Silas knew what the god-king gave, and what he took.

"How is the pain?" they asked.

"Present. Which means I'm alive. Pain is just the body keeping its own ledger." Odessa turned her head on the pillow, and her gaze went past Silas to the door, where small feet were making an admirable but doomed attempt at silence. "And you can come in too, Tam. You've been breathing in that hallway since dawn."

Tam came in the way he did everything now—quickly, as if the room might change its mind about him. He was eleven, and he had sold his happiest memory to a shrine for bread, and he carried the absence of it the way other children carried a stone in their shoe: constantly, and without complaint, because he had learned early that complaining changed nothing. He climbed onto the foot of the bed, careful of Odessa's splinted arm, and arranged himself with the gravity of a small cat claiming territory.

"Brann's downstairs," he reported. "Being grumpy. Wren's being *weird*, which for her is normal, but she's being weird in the direction of the map table, which means she's found something."

"Did she say what?"

"She said—" Tam screwed up his face, doing the accent, Wren's flat harbor drawl, "'If the dead keep the roads, the dead can lend them.' And then she drank all the tea."

Odessa made a sound that in a healthier woman would have been a laugh. "She drinks tea like a debt collector drinks blood. Well." She looked at Silas then, fully, and Silas felt the old reflexive flinch—the instinct, trained over a lifetime, to become slightly less present, slightly less *findable*. She saw it. She always saw it. "Send them up. All of them. And Blackwood—"

"Yes."

"Bring the small ledger."

* * *

They gathered in the sickroom as the light went long and amber through the canvas-covered window: Silas in the chair; Tam on the bed's foot; Wren folded onto the floor with her back against the wardrobe, her forbidden maps spread around her like the shed feathers of some impossible bird; Brann by the door, arms crossed over a chest like a barrel, his one eye catching the candlelight and his empty socket turned to the wall, as it always was, toward the thing he refused to look at.

Brann had come back from the raid with a gash across his scalp and a silence on him that no one had been able to pick. He had locked the guildhouse door behind the raiders' retreat, and then he had stood at that door for an hour, they said, with his hand flat against the wood. Silas did not ask. Locksmiths kept their own ledgers.

Odessa waited until Wren had weighted the corners of her topmost map with a candlestick, a spoon, Tam's shoe, and Brann's boot, by way of invitation.

"You've all read the news," she said. Her voice was thin but it carried; it had carried across guild halls and debt-courts for forty years, and it carried now. "Aldric's army is on the march. Not the border raids. Not the punitive burns. The whole weight of it, coming down the King's Road toward the free cities, with the crown's priests riding ahead promising miracles. Harrow's Ford refused them." She let the name sit in the room. Everyone knew what Harrow's Ford was now. A name on a map where a city used to be. "The capital is a month's hard ride for an army. For a small company that doesn't fight fair? Wren says less."

"Nine days, if the erased roads hold," Wren said from the floor, not looking up. "Eight if we're lucky and the dead are feeling generous. They're not, usually. Generous."

"And why," Brann said, his voice like a millstone turning, "would we want to get to the capital *before* an army that's going to flatten everything between here and there? Answer me that, Guildmaster. We're bookkeepers. We're thieves and map-liars and—" his eye flicked to Tam, and something in his jaw worked, "—and children. We walk into the usurper's own city, we die in the usurper's own city, and the free cities burn anyway. That's the arithmetic."

"That's *your* arithmetic," Odessa said. "Let me show you mine."

She nodded to Silas. Silas took the small ledger from the table and opened it on their knees, and for a moment simply looked at it—their own careful hand, their own careful blankness, decades of entries in a code only they could read, and the one page, near the front, that was empty and had always been empty and was supposed to be empty forever.

The page where a name should have been.

"Every miracle the crown has worked," Silas said, "has a price. We've traced it. Rain for a village's names. Healing for a boy's birthday. Every answered prayer hollows the one who prayed. And the thing answering—" their hand pressed flat against the ledger, against the resonance they could still feel humming up through the paper from the stolen prayer locked in the archive below, "—is not a god. It's a king. Bound under his own crown. Screaming. Every miracle is him, being torn open and used."

"And the armies," Odessa said, "are the least of it. Aldric marches because the crown marches him. He hollows a little more every day. And the crown is coming *here* to feed. The free cities are not a conquest to it. We're a *harvest*." She shifted against the pillows, and even that small motion cost her; Silas saw the sweat stand out on her brow and saw her refuse, absolutely, to acknowledge it. "There is a ritual. The dead knights of the erased kingdom keep it. A memory of equal weight to a soul, given freely, unbinds him. And when he's unbound—"

"The crown shatters," Silas said. "The miracles stop. The armies halt. We've seen it in the dead knights' riddles. We've heard it in the king's own voice, under the screaming. He doesn't want rescue. He wants to be *remembered*."

Silence, except for the wind worrying the canvas corner, over and over, like a heart.

"So we go to the capital," Tam said. He said it simply, the way children say the enormous things, because no one has yet taught them the words are supposed to be heavy. "We go to the capital and we give the crown what it wants and we take the king back."

"The crown doesn't want a memory, boy," Brann said. "The crown wants a *soul's worth* of memory. You know what that means? It means someone walks into that throne room and hands over everything they are. Their name. Their mother's face. Every—" and here his voice did something none of them had heard it do, a small fracture, quickly mortared over, "—every good day they ever had. That's the price. That's the arithmetic *that* one's been avoiding writing down since the drowned chapel." His eye found Silas. "Haven't you."

It was not a question. Silas held his gaze. Somewhere in the last weeks they had stopped being able to lie to these people, and they could not locate the exact day it had happened, and that frightened them more than the crown did.

"Yes," Silas said. "That's the price. And I've been keeping accounts my whole life, Brann. I know what things cost. I know better than anyone in this room what it is to have no past." They touched the blank page. "I built myself out of nothing once already. I can do it again."

"That's not—" Brann started.

"It is exactly what it is, and I'm not asking permission."

"You're not *asking* anything," Brann growled. "That's the problem with you, Blackwood. You'd rather be a martyr than a member."

"A what?" said Tam.

"A member," Wren said from the floor, with the faintest curl of a smile. "Of a family. He's being rude about it in locksmith."

Brann's eye went around the room, and Silas watched him do the thing he had evidently been doing for four days behind his silence: counting exits. And finding, this time, that he didn't want one.

"The ford," he said at last, heavily. "When I sold your route to the spymaster. You all know. I know you know. I've been waiting for one of you to put me out. Nobody has." He rubbed his scarred scalp. "So I'll say it plain. I owe this house. I owe *her*—" a jerk of his chin at Odessa, "—for taking a one-eyed drunk with sticky fingers off the docks twenty years back. And I owe the boy for not telling the dead knights I flinched at the riddle of loyalty. And I owe *you*, Blackwood, for reasons I'll put in writing when I'm drunker. So. I can't fight, I can't map, and I can't pray. But there's not a lock in the capital that will keep me out, and I'm coming, and if anyone says otherwise they can argue with the door."

"The door agrees with me," Tam said solemnly.

"Then it's settled," Odessa said—but Silas saw her hand tighten on the ledger, and knew it wasn't, not entirely, not for her. She looked at Silas for a long moment. "You understand what you're proposing. Not the crown's price. *Mine*. I built this guild on one rule: nothing that cannot be paid back. Every debt recorded, every favor entered, so that nothing is ever truly lost. And you're about to make a debt that can't be entered anywhere, because the one who'd remember it won't exist." Her voice roughened. "I've spent forty years making sure the guild's people don't vanish. You're my best bookkeeper, Blackwood. And you're asking me to watch you write yourself out of the book."

Silas looked down at the blank page. The blank page they had made themselves, long ago, on purpose, in a different life, for different reasons. It had been a fortress then. It was something else now.

"Then don't watch me vanish," Silas said. "Watch me *pay*."

And then Tam, who had been quiet in the way that meant he was building up to something, slid off the foot of the bed and stood in the middle of the candlelight, and said, "We should do the thing. The oath thing. Like the dead knights."

"The dead knights turned people to ash for failing their oaths," Wren said.

"Yeah," said Tam. "But theirs was a bad oath. It was all *obey* and *keep* and *never*. Ours should be about giving." He frowned, working it out with the fierce seriousness he brought to everything. "That's what the king needs, right? Somebody who gives things away. So we should swear it that way. Not what we'll keep. What we'll *give*."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about Tam; nobody ever laughed, in the end.

Wren rose from the floor, unfolding joint by joint like a heron, and took the candlestick off her map. "Right," she said. "Then we'll need something to swear on. Not a book. Not a blade." She looked around the room, and her flat harbor eyes had a light in them that Silas had never seen there. "Something given."

Brann reached into his coat without a word and produced, from some inner pocket that had clearly been waiting for this, a small iron key, old and worn, its teeth filed almost smooth. "First lock I ever picked," he said. "Kept it ever since. Never told a soul why." He set it in Tam's small palm. "There. Given."

Tam looked at it, and then at the blank page of the small ledger, and something passed across his face—grief and resolve in equal measure—and he held the key out to Odessa. "You hold it. You're the one who keeps accounts. Somebody should."

Odessa took the key in her right hand, the splinted arm cradled against her, and held it up in the candlelight, and her voice, when she spoke, was the guildmaster's voice, the one that had sworn in thieves and map-liars and locksmiths for forty years, and it did not shake.

"Then swear by what you give," she said. "Silas Blackwood."

Silas rose. The candle flame stood very straight, as if listening.

"I give my name," Silas said, and the words came easier than any words had ever come, "so that a king may be called by his."

"Tam."

"I give my last good day," Tam said, "so someone else gets one."

"Wren."

"I give my maps," Wren said, "so a kingdom that was erased can be found again."

"Brann."

The locksmith's jaw worked. "I give my freedom," he said. "Seems to be the going rate. And my hands. They're good hands."

"And I," Odessa said, "give you my guild. What's left of it. Which is everything."

She closed her hand around the key and around the ledger, and for a moment the candle flame bent, though there was no draft, and Silas felt it again—the resonance, faint and far away and *listening*, the bound king beneath the world, hearing four voices and a dying woman swear to remember him.

"Then it's sworn," Odessa said quietly. "Go and pack. Wren—roads. Brann—locks and food. Tam—sleep, and that's an order. Silas." She waited until the others had gone, until the door was shut and it was only the two of them and the candle and the wind at the canvas. "Come here."

Silas came. She took their hand with her free one—her grip still strong, still a ship's-mast grip—and pressed something into it. The key. Brann's first lock.

"Given to me, I give to you," she said. "Carry it to the throne room. And when it's done—when you've paid, and you don't know why your hands are empty—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, because she was Odessa Vane, "—they'll give it back to you. And you'll hold it. And you won't know why it matters. But it will. That's the whole of it, Blackwood. That's what we keep, when we give everything away. We keep *each other*."

Silas looked at the key in their palm, at the small worn teeth, at the shape of a life someone had once locked and someone had once opened, and found they could not speak at all.

Below, faintly, through the floor, they heard Wren's voice, flat and certain: "Roads hold. We leave at first light."

Nine days to the capital.

The candle burned straight and steady, all the way down.
