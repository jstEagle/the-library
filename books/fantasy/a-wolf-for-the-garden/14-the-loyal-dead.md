## 14. The Loyal Dead

Tam had never seen a road that glowed before.

It wasn't bright, exactly. It was more like the memory of light—the way his mother's kitchen window had looked from outside on winter evenings, gold spilling across snow, promising warmth he could feel in his chest even when his toes were freezing. The road through the erased kingdom was like that. Pale gold threads ran through the old stones, and when Wren walked ahead of them with her forbidden maps held open against her chest, the threads brightened under her boots as if the road were glad to be remembered.

"Stay on the stones," Wren said, for the fourth time that morning. "Not the grass. Not the mud. The stones."

"Why?" Tam asked, because Silas had told him asking questions was how you kept from being afraid, and because he was very afraid.

"Because the grass isn't ours," Wren said. She said it simply, the way she said most things, but her knuckles were white around the edge of her map. "Nothing here is ours. We're walking through someone's grave, and the grave is still awake."

The kingdom had no name anymore. That was the first thing Tam had understood about it, back at the border, where a stone marker stood worn blank by two hundred years of rain. Wren had touched the blank stone the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder—gently, apologetically—and had whispered something Tam hadn't been able to hear. After that, the road had appeared, threading between hills that looked ordinary until you looked too long, and then they looked like hills that were pretending.

Silas walked beside him. They'd been quiet since the ford, since Brann. Tam kept glancing up at them, waiting for them to say something reassuring the way grown-ups did, *it'll be all right, little sprout*, but Silas only watched the glowing road with an expression Tam couldn't read, their thumb moving over the prayer-parchment tucked inside their coat like it was a wound they kept checking.

The fog came at midday.

It didn't roll in. It rose—out of the ground itself, out of the spaces between the stones, pale as milk and smelling faintly of woodsmoke and lilies, which was a strange combination, and Tam's eyes stung and his throat went tight, because lilies had been the flowers at his grandmother's funeral, back when he'd still had a grandmother to have a funeral for.

"Halt," said a voice.

Not one voice. Many voices, all saying the same word at slightly different times, like a wave breaking over rocks. *Halt. Halt. Halt.*

Out of the fog came horses first—great grey destriers with armor the color of old silver, moving without sound, their hooves never quite touching the road. Then the riders. They wore mail gone green at the joints and surcoats faded to ghosts of their colors, and beneath their helms there was no face. There was light instead. A soft, patient, sorrowful light, like candles burning in a chapel no one visited anymore.

There were dozens of them. Then hundreds. They filled the road ahead and behind, and the fog thickened until Tam couldn't see Wren or Odessa's empty saddle or anything at all except the nearest knight, who had reined in directly in front of him and was looking down—or seemed to be looking down—with that candlelight face.

"Travelers," the knight said. All the knights said it. "You walk the King's Road through the King's country. State your loyalty."

Tam's mouth went dry. He looked for Silas and found only fog. He looked for Wren and found only fog. His heart hammered so hard he could feel it in his ears, in his teeth, in the palms of his hands.

"I—" he started.

"You will each be tested," the knight said, and the many-voices made the words feel like they were being spoken into Tam's chest rather than his ears. "The road remembers what was sworn here. Answer truly, or turn back. There is no shame in turning back. There is only shame in lying to the dead."

* * *

They were separated. That was the worst part. The fog took them—one moment Silas's hand was on Tam's shoulder, warm and real and steady, and the next the fog closed like a door and Tam was alone on a small island of glowing stone with three knights circled around him and nothing else in the world.

He wrapped his arms around himself. He was eleven years old. He had sold his birthday to a shrine for three coppers and a hot meal, and he had spent a year not knowing his mother's face, and he had crossed half the free cities with a memory-thief and a liar-cartographer, and none of that, none of it, had felt like this.

"First question," said the nearest knight. This one's voice was just one voice now, low and rusted, like a sword drawn slowly. "When you were afraid, and no one watched—who were you?"

Tam blinked. "What?"

"The road asks. When you were afraid, and no one watched, and you could have been anything, anyone, done anything, taken anything—who were you?"

Tam thought about it. He thought about the night in Corvassa when the guildhouse raiders had come, and he'd hidden in the ledger-cellar with a bottle of somebody's wedding day clutched to his chest, and he'd heard men talking about burning everything, and he'd been so scared his whole body shook. And nobody had been watching. Nobody would ever have known if he'd crept out the back and left them all—left Silas, left Odessa bleeding, left everyone—and saved himself.

He'd stayed. He'd stayed because Silas had once sat up all night with him when he cried about his mother's face, and had said, *I can't give it back to you, sprout. But I can remember it with you, until you find it again.* You didn't leave people who did that.

"I was somebody who stayed," Tam said.

The candlelight in the knight's helm flickered—brightened. Somewhere in the fog, other voices murmured, like wind through wheat.

"Second question," the knight said. "What have you lost that you would not steal back, even now, even knowing where it lies?"

That one hurt. Because Tam knew exactly where his birthday lay—in a humming glass bottle in a shrine strongroom in Corvassa, or maybe already drunk down by whatever hollow god drank there, and some nights he dreamed about sneaking back and taking it, and in the dreams he always woke up crying because in the dreams his mother's face came back and then dissolved again when he opened his eyes.

"If I stole it back," Tam said slowly, working it out as he spoke, "it wouldn't be mine. It'd be stolen. It'd be—it'd have a crack in it forever." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "I want it back. But I won't take it from somebody else. Even from them. Even from the god. Especially from the god." He thought of the drowned chapel, of the hollowed worshipper's empty eyes, of the screaming he'd felt inside every miracle. "Because I think taking things is how all this started."

The murmuring in the fog grew louder. The second knight leaned forward, candlelight tilting.

"Third question," it said, and its voice had changed—softer now, almost gentle, almost sad. "Little one. What would you give? Not sell. Not trade. Not bargain away at fair price, weighed and measured and receipted." The word *receipted* landed strangely, and Tam wondered if the dead knew what Silas was. "What would you give freely, to a stranger, expecting nothing?"

And Tam understood, suddenly, with a cold drop through his stomach, what the third question was really asking.

Because there was only one thing left. He'd carried it across the erased kingdom pressed against his ribs like a coal: his last happy memory. The one he'd refused to sell, refused to trade, refused to let Silas even look at. The afternoon in the orchard. His mother's laugh, the only piece of her voice he had left, tangled up with the smell of ripe plums and the scratch of grass and the warmth of her hand on his hair. It was the last thing that proved he had ever been loved.

If he gave it, he wouldn't know he'd ever been loved. He'd be a boy with a hole where his heart's proof should be.

But the dead had asked. And the dead were loyal—they were *loyal*, that was the whole of them, that was what remained after two hundred years—and loyalty meant giving when giving cost. Didn't it? Wasn't that the entire shape of it?

His hands were shaking. He reached into his shirt and drew out the memory the way he carried it—not in a bottle, he'd never owned a bottle, but in the way memory-thieves taught you to hold a thing: behind your eyes, folded small, warm.

"It's an afternoon," Tam said. His voice cracked and he hated that and kept going anyway. "In an orchard. My ma was alive. She was laughing at me because I ate too many plums and got a stomachache and I wouldn't admit it. She put her hand on my head." His eyes were streaming now, and he let them. "It's the last happy thing I own. I was keeping it. I was going to keep it forever."

The knights had gone very still. Even the fog seemed to hold its breath.

"But you asked what I'd *give*," Tam said. "Not what I'd sell. So."

He unfolded it.

He didn't know how he did it—he'd never been trained, never been guilded, never learned the thief's craft—but love is its own kind of skill, and he opened himself the way you open a fist around something you've been clutching too hard, and he poured the orchard out into the fog. The plums. The grass. The laugh. Her hand, warm on his hair, heavier than a crown and worth more.

Then it was gone.

He stood on the glowing stone in the cold fog and felt the shape of the hole where it had been, and it was worse than the hole where his birthday had been, because this time he had chosen it, and this time he knew exactly what he'd lost, and he would spend the rest of his life knowing that he had known.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's all I had. So. There."

* * *

The silence lasted a long time.

Then the nearest knight dismounted. The motion was slow, rusted, careful, like a man rising from a long illness, and when the knight knelt—knelt, in front of an eleven-year-old boy in a too-big coat—the candlelight in the helm trembled.

"Two hundred years," the knight said, one voice alone now, rough with something that might have been weeping if the dead could weep. "Two hundred years we have kept this road, and tested every traveler, and turned back every army, and waited. Do you know what we wait for, child?"

Tam shook his head. He couldn't speak.

"We wait for the shape." The knight raised a gauntleted hand and pressed it, feather-light, against Tam's chest, over the new hole where the orchard had been. "Our king went down beneath his crown willingly. Did you know that? They tell it differently in the histories. They say he was conquered, trapped, bound by treachery. Some of it is treachery, yes. But at the last—at the very last—our king *chose*. He knelt in his own throne room and let them set the crown on him, because the thing that forged it promised it would spare his people if he surrendered his soul to the cage. He gave his happiness, his name, his self, freely, to a liar, expecting nothing. Expecting the lie he was promised." The candlelight guttered. "We have waited two hundred years for someone to walk this road carrying the same shape. The shape of a gift given freely, at terrible cost, without bargain."

The other knights were dismounting now, all along the fog-hidden road, dozens of them, hundreds, kneeling in the mist like a forest of stone and candlelight.

"The crown hungers," the knight said. "It eats memories, souls, names—it has eaten a kingdom and it is eating a usurper and it will eat the world unless it is fed a sacrifice of equal weight, given *freely*, as our king gave himself. We could not tell you this before. The road does not permit truth to liars, and every soul who came to us came wanting something, bargaining something, holding something back." The helm tilted. "But you. You held nothing back. You shamed them all, little one. The thief, the mapmaker, the guildmaster's shadow—all of them came ready to pay, to trade, to weigh and measure. And you simply *gave*."

Somewhere in the fog, Tam heard voices—Silas's, sharp with fear, calling his name; Wren's, lower, urgent. The fog was thinning. The knights were rising, parting, opening a corridor of candlelight and old silver down the length of the King's Road.

"They may pass," the many-voices said together, and the sound rolled down the road like thunder learning gentleness. "Bring them to the vigil-field. Bring them to the king's grave. For the armies of the false crown are three days behind you, and the coronation rite is sooner than your mapmaker dreams, and there is a truth she must see written in our dead land before she can chart the way in."

The knight who had knelt rose and stood aside, and where he had knelt, the fog parted, and Tam saw what lay ahead: a hill crowned with standing stones, and on the stones, carved deep and worn shallow by two centuries of rain, names. Thousands of names. The names of everyone the kingdom had been.

And beside the hill, faint as breath on glass, a road of gold thread continued east. Toward the capital. Toward the crown.

Small feet came pounding through the fog, and then Silas was there, dropping to their knees in front of him, hands on his shoulders, searching his face with an expression Tam had never seen them wear—raw, terrified, unguarded.

"Sprout—Tam—are you hurt? What happened? What did they—"

"I gave it away," Tam said. His voice came out flat and strange. "My last one. The orchard. They needed to know if I'd give something freely, so I did." He looked up at Silas, and his eyes were dry now, emptied out, terribly calm. "I don't remember being loved anymore. But I remember choosing it. Is that—is that enough? Does that count for anything?"

Silas's face did something complicated and broke open, and they pulled him against their chest, hard, and Tam felt them shaking.

Behind them, Wren stood at the edge of the standing stones with her maps fallen forgotten at her feet, staring at the thousands of carved names, and her lips were moving, reading, and Tam saw the color drain out of her face as she reached the newest carving—the one at the end, the stone that was not two hundred years old at all, but fresh, the letters still sharp-edged, still waiting.

She read the name aloud. Once. In a whisper.

And then she turned to Silas and said, in a voice like a woman watching her life's work burn:

"You need to see this. Now."
