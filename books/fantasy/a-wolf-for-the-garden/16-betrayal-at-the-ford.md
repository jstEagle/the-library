## 16. Betrayal at the Ford

Brann had learned long ago that a man's hands told the truth even when his mouth lied. His own hands had been telling the truth for eleven days now—trembling at odd hours, fumbling locks they could have opened blind, and once, shamefully, dropping the iron poker in Odessa's sickroom as if the weight of it had suddenly become more than he could carry.

He sat apart from the others that night, on the cold lip of the ridge above the Erased Kingdom's western road, and let his one eye do what it did best: watch.

Below, the fire crackled. Tam was asleep with his head against Wren's rolled maps, mouth open, one hand curled around the dead knights' token—a sliver of crown-iron no bigger than a thumbnail, wrapped in cloth that had been white two hundred years ago. Silas sat awake, as always, turning something over in their fingers. Not the prayer. Something smaller. A memory-bottle, maybe, or just a stone they liked the shape of. With Silas you never knew which was heavier.

Wren was sketching by firelight, her charcoal whispering against paper. She was drawing the ford.

Brann's stomach turned over, slow and sick, the way it had been turning for eleven days.

The ford at Marrow Water. Three days ahead of them, if the roads held. The only crossing wide enough and shallow enough to take them out of the erased kingdom and onto the pilgrim road to the capital—and the only place, Wren said, where the usurper's outriders would not think to look, because the maps said there was no water there at all. That was the trick of traveling through a country that had been unmade: you moved along the seams of what everyone else had forgotten.

Which meant that if anyone wanted to sell the company's route, there was exactly one thing worth selling.

*You'll tell me when you cross the Marrow Water,* Veyra Sable had said, eleven days ago, in a dream that had smelled of ink and rotting roses. *Or I'll tell the Guild of Impossible Things what their locksmith has been carrying since the winter of the ash-tithe.*

She hadn't needed to say what he'd been carrying. Brann carried it every day, in the locked box behind his left eye, in the shape of a name: Corrin. His brother. Who had taken guild coin to open a door for the wrong people, and who would hang—or worse—if Brann didn't pay the debt down. And Veyra Sable, spymaster to King Aldric, held the ledger page with Corrin's name on it the way a spider holds a fly: gently, patiently, with every intention of eating.

Eleven days ago, in the dream, he had agreed.

He had told himself he wouldn't follow through. He had told himself a lot of things. He was good at telling himself things; a man who kept other people's secrets for a living got fluent in the language of comfortable lies. But three nights past, while the others slept, he had built a message-lock—small, clever, the kind of thing only another locksmith would ever notice—out of a bird-whistle and a scrap of signal-iron, and he had sent it up from the camp in a courier hawk's talons when the hawk came circling low over the ridge, hunting.

The hawk had been hunting because someone had trained it to hunt near him. Veyra's people were already close. They had been close for days.

And now Wren was drawing the ford, and Tam was sleeping like a child who believed the adults around him were good, and Silas—who had given away more of themselves than any living soul should have to give—was sitting by the fire trusting him.

Brann looked at his hands again. Steady now. Traitorously steady.

*Tomorrow,* he thought. *I'll decide tomorrow.*

But he had known, even as he thought it, that tomorrow was a lie too. The decision had been made in a dream that smelled of roses. All that remained was the paying.

* * *

They reached the Marrow Water at midmorning, and Brann understood immediately why Wren had gone quiet when she first showed them the map.

The river was wrong. Not wild-wrong, not flood-wrong—wrong the way a room is wrong when the furniture has been moved in the dark. It ran silver and slow through a valley of grass so green it looked painted, and nothing about it matched anything around it. The hills on either bank were brown and stony, autumn-dry. The valley floor drank light and gave back none. And the water made no sound.

A river that wide, moving that fast, should have roared. This one ran silent as spilled milk.

"It remembers being somewhere else," Wren said softly. She stood at the head of the slope with her map case hugged to her chest, and for once she wasn't consulting it. "This whole valley. It's a piece of the old kingdom that didn't finish dying. The water still thinks it flows through Vael Amaranth." She swallowed. "We cross quick. Don't drink. Don't look at your reflection longer than you need to. Reflections here are... chatty."

Tam laughed, high and nervous, and asked what reflections could possibly have to say.

"Nothing you want to hear," Wren said, "and everything you're afraid they will."

Silas said nothing at all. They had gone quiet in the way Brann had learned to dread over the weeks of travel—not the silence of a person thinking, but the silence of a person listening. Their head tilted slightly, toward the water, toward the east, toward whatever ancient frequency the prayer and the bottles and the bound king all sang on.

"He knows we're coming," Silas said. No one asked who *he* was. There was only one he left. "Not us specifically. But the crown is... hungry today. I can feel it feeding."

"Feeding on what?" Tam asked.

"On whoever's nearest," Silas said, and started down toward the ford.

That was when Brann saw the soldiers.

They came out of the eastern tree line in loose column, maybe forty of them, driving a supply cart and a string of remounts—the muddy, road-worn advance of an army's trailing edge, the men nobody polished. For one heartbeat Brann felt the pure animal relief of a man whose crime is about to be discovered: *there, you see, it's out of my hands.* Then he actually looked at the soldiers, and relief died in his throat.

They were marching in step. Perfectly in step. Forty men across broken ground, through mud and tussock and stream-cut, and every boot came down on the same beat, and between the beats there was no sound at all. No jingle of harness. No rattle of the cart. No voice. Forty men and not one word, not one cough, not one curse at the mud.

And they were smiling.

It was not the smile of men glad to be nearly home. It was the smile of people being *held*—the fixed, stretched rictus of faces that had stopped belonging to their owners some miles back. As the column came down to the water's edge, thirty yards from where the company crouched in the grass, Brann saw the details that made his skin try to leave his body. Men whose eyes were dry and red-rimmed, wept empty. A boy soldier, sixteen at most, whose lips had been chewed through from the inside. One man walking with a broken ankle, the foot turned entirely backward, and his face serene, and his stride keeping perfect time.

"The crown," Wren breathed. "Gods below. That's what it does to them. That's what it does to *all of them*, isn't it? Every soldier in that army—"

"Not all," Silas whispered. "Some fight it. Some break. But enough. Enough to march."

The column reached the ford and began to cross. The silent water parted around their boots without a ripple, without a sound, and Brann watched a grown man walk into a river with a shattered ankle and feel nothing, because whatever portion of him had been allowed to feel things had been eaten, and eaten, and eaten, by a crown that fed a king's scream to ten thousand throats a day.

The boy soldier stumbled. Just once—one small hitch in the perfect rhythm, one human stutter in the machine. And for half a second his face broke. The smile cracked and underneath it was terror, raw and childlike, the face of a boy drowning in his own body, screaming behind glass. His eyes swept the bank—swept across the grass where four travelers hid—and for half a second, half a heartbeat, they met Brann's one eye.

*Help me,* said the eyes. Plainly. Wordlessly. The oldest message there is.

Then the smile closed over his face like water closing over a stone, and the column marched on, in step, in silence, up the far bank and away east toward the capital, and Brann knelt in the wet grass with his heart hammering and understood, with terrible clarity, what he had done.

He had sold these people. Not these four—these four were strangers he'd come to love, and that was bad enough, that was a wound that would never close—but he had sold the route, and the route led here, and Veyra Sable's riders would come to this ford, and they would find either the travelers or the trail of them, and behind those riders would come the army, and the army was full of boys whose eyes screamed through their smiles.

Corrin's face rose in his mind—his little brother at nine years old, gap-toothed, holding up a lock he'd picked apart just to see how it worked. *Look, Brann. Look what's inside it.* That was all either of them had ever wanted, really. To see what was inside things. Brann had made a trade of it. Corrin had made a mistake of it. And Veyra Sable had spent eleven days making sure Brann understood that the difference between a trade and a mistake was only who wrote it down.

His hands were shaking again. Good, he thought. Let them shake. Shaking meant there was still a man attached to them.

"Brann." Silas's voice, quiet, beside him. "You've gone grey. What did you see?"

What did he see. He saw a boy begging with his eyes. He saw the price of crowns. He saw, laid out plain as a diagram, exactly what he was: a lock that had been picked, a door that had been opened, a man who had spent his whole life keeping other people's secrets and had handed his own to the first person who squeezed.

"I saw what we're walking into," he said, and it was even true.

* * *

They crossed at dusk, when the light failed and the reflections, Wren hoped, would have less to work with. The water took them to the knee and then the thigh, silent as silk, and Brann kept his eyes on the far bank and did not look down, because Wren had warned them, and because he had seen enough of himself lately to last a lifetime.

Halfway across, the water spoke anyway.

It didn't use words. It used remembering. One moment Brann was wading, teeth gritted against the cold; the next he was eight years old in his father's workshop, and the smell of oil and iron filings filled his nose so completely he could taste them, and his father's hands—huge, scarred, gentle—were closing his small fingers around his first pick, saying, *a lock is just a question, lad. Every question has a shape. Find the shape.* And Brann stood chest-deep in a river that had died two hundred years ago and wept, silently, for a father and a workshop and a brother both lost to him, and the water held him up, patient, almost kind, as if to say: *this is what was taken from you. This is what you are about to take from them.*

Then Wren's hand closed hard on his arm and hauled him forward, and the remembering drained away like water through sand, and they were stumbling up the far bank into the dark, gasping, alive.

Tam was crying without knowing why. Silas stood dripping at the treeline, staring back at the water with an expression Brann couldn't read—couldn't read, and that frightened him more than the soldiers had, because Silas's silences had always been legible to him before, and tonight there was something behind their eyes that looked almost like farewell.

"We camp off the road," Wren managed. "Cold camp. No fire. We're through—that was the last seam. From here it's pilgrim road, and pilgrims, and checkpoints, and—" She stopped. Her head came up, sharp as a heron's.

Brann heard it a moment later. What she'd heard first.

Hooves. Many hooves, cantering, spread out in a sweep-line the way professionals do it—not chasing, *casting*, the way you cast for a scent. Coming down the eastern bank. Toward the ford.

"They can't have tracked us," Wren whispered. "There's nothing to track. The kingdom doesn't exist. The roads don't exist. There's no report, no map, no—"

"No map," Brann said.

The words came out of him flat and dead, and in the silence after them he felt every eye turn, and he found that he could not make himself turn to meet them. He looked at the ground instead, at his own boots, dripping river-water onto soil that belonged to a country that had been erased, and he heard himself keep talking, because apparently the part of him that confessed had been waiting eleven days for permission.

"There's one map. There's one report. There's one man who's known the route since the night I sent the whistle-hawk, because I sent it, because Veyra Sable holds my brother's debt and I paid it in the only coin I had." His voice cracked and he hated it and he kept going. "Riders will reach this ford within the hour. Behind them, the vanguard. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'd tell you I didn't know what they'd do with it but I knew. I knew exactly what they'd do with it, and I told myself tomorrow, I told myself I'd find another way, and I watched that boy soldier's face today and I understood that there is no other way, there's only what you do next."

The silence went on so long he finally raised his head.

Silas was looking at him. Not with hatred. That was the unbearable part—with a kind of weary recognition, as if Brann had confirmed something they'd always suspected about the world and were almost glad to be right. Wren's face was white with fury, her hand white-knuckled on her map case, and Tam—

Tam was looking at him the way children look at a broken thing they loved.

"You sold my birthday," Tam said, very quietly. "You sold the only thing I had left of my mother, when you sold the road she'd have walked."

"I know," Brann said. "I know, lad."

And then, distantly, across the silent river, torchlight bloomed among the trees of the eastern bank. Voices now—real voices, human voices, giving orders in the clipped cadence of Veyra Sable's household guard. A dozen riders, perhaps more, dismounting at the water's edge. Lanterns swinging low over the shallows, searching for tracks, for a ferry, for a sign.

They would find the crossing-slick on the stones. They would find it within minutes. The river gave nothing away, but the mud of the far bank held four sets of prints as clear as scripture.

Brann looked at the torchlight. Then at the company. Then at his own hands, which had finally, finally stopped shaking, because they had stopped lying to him too.

"Listen," he said, and his voice came out steady, the steadiest it had been in eleven days, because a man's hands and a man's voice both tell the truth when the deciding is done. "They're casting for four. Give them one. I know the sweep-pattern Sable's people run—I taught half of them their trade, gods rot it. If I go down to the water and let them take me, they'll log a capture, send a rider back, and stand around arguing about extraction protocol until midnight. That buys you the hours to move north along the bank, past their picket line—they'll have left the north flank thin, they always do, they think the marsh does their work. By dawn you're five miles clear and I'm a problem for Sable's interrogators instead of yours."

"You don't know what they do to captured guildsmen," Wren said.

"Yes I do," Brann said. "I've built the locks for the rooms where they do it."

"Brann—" Tam started, and his voice broke on the name, and Brann had to look away from him, fast, or he would lose the thread of the thing he was doing, the one clean thread left in the whole tangled ruin of his account-keeping.

"Corrin," he said instead. "My brother. When this is over—if any of us are alive when this is over—he'll be in a cell under the Guildhouse in Copperlane, waiting on a debt. Somebody pay it. Somebody get him out and tell him—" His throat closed. He worked past it. "Tell him I finally found the shape of the question. Tell him it was me. It was always going to be me."

He didn't wait for permission. Permission was a luxury for men with clean ledgers. He slung his tool-roll off his shoulder and dropped it in the grass—no, pressed it into Tam's arms, because the boy should have something of his to throw away later, or to keep, whichever kind of story the world decided to tell—and then he walked down the bank into the torchlight with his empty hands raised and his one eye fixed on the far side of the river, where the riders were shouting now, and the lanterns swung, and the silent water of a dead kingdom's river ran silver past his knees, carrying nothing, keeping nothing, forgetting everything, the way he had spent his whole life failing to do.

They took him roughly, as he'd known they would, and put him on a horse, as he'd hoped they would, and as they rode him back across the ford he twisted in his grip for one last look at the northern bank.

Empty. Dark. Already gone.

Good, he thought. *Run, you impossible people. Run, and win, and when you tell this story afterward—and you will tell it, Silas, you'll tell it to the boy until he believes it—leave me out of it. Or don't. Either way, it's paid. Whatever else I owed, and whoever else I owed it to, this part is paid.*

Behind him, upstream, the black rain began to fall—warm and soft and wrong, the rain of answered prayers—and the last thing Brann saw before they hooded him was the torchlight of Veyra Sable's riders glinting off the water, and beyond them, far to the east, a smudge of red on the horizon that might have been sunset, or might have been the capital burning its thousand candles for a king who could no longer remember his daughter's laugh.

Three days, he thought, as the hood came down. *They have three days. Spend them well.*

The horse splashed on through the silent river, and Brann rode into captivity the way he had done everything else in his life: with his hands steady, and the truth locked tight behind his eye, and the key thrown away at last.
