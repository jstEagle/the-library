## 17. Into the Capital

The road to Vhalcrest was paved with pilgrims.

They came in every color of desperation—farmers with bandaged sons, merchants carrying crippled daughters on litters, old men who had walked a hundred miles on knees that no longer bent right, all of them drawn by the same promise whispered from city to city like a fever: *Come to the capital. Come to the King's Healing. Come and be made whole.*

Silas moved among them wearing a borrowed face of piety, a gray wool cloak that smelled of someone else's sweat and incense, and a pilgrim's token stamped with the sun-and-crown sigil of Aldric's church. The token had cost Wren three days of forgery work and one small argument about whether Silas could pass for devout.

"You scowl like a man doing arithmetic during a funeral," she'd said, pressing the wax stamp into the soft disc.

"I *am* doing arithmetic," Silas had answered. "The arithmetic of how many ways this can go wrong."

"And?"

"The number is large."

Now, three days later, the number had not grown smaller. It had only grown louder, because the capital itself was loud—a bowl of white stone cradled between two rivers, its walls so high they seemed to hold up the clouds, its spires gilded until they burned in the afternoon light like candles lit for a god who did not deserve them.

Tam walked close at Silas's side, his small hand hooked into the back of Silas's belt the way he'd done since the ford, since Brann. He hadn't spoken much in three days. Children grieve in their bodies before they learn to grieve in words, and Tam carried his grief in the set of his shoulders and the way he counted things under his breath—steps, birds, coins, the buttons on a stranger's coat—as if the world could be held together by arithmetic if you just kept track hard enough.

"How many people do you think?" he asked now, voice pitched low beneath the hymns drifting from the walls.

"Forty thousand," Wren said. She'd been counting too, but her counting came out as maps. Her fingers twitched against her thigh, sketching streets she'd memorized from a stolen plan of the city, tracing the route over and over: gate, square, cathedral, ritual chamber. "Maybe fifty. They've been arriving for a month."

"Fifty thousand people," Tam said, "and none of them know."

"No," Silas said. "None of them know."

That was the worst of it. Not the disguise, not the guards at the gates with their mirrored helms and their long poles for hooking the fallen out of the crowd—not even the memory of Brann's face at the ford, turning toward the soldiers who took him, mouthing *go* with blood on his teeth. The worst of it was walking inside a river of hope. Fifty thousand people had come to be healed, and every single one of them would leave lighter than they arrived, and not one of them would understand what had been taken until it was gone—the way Tam hadn't understood, standing in the guildhouse with his mother's name dissolving on his tongue.

The gates swallowed them without ceremony. A priest glanced at their tokens, a guard glanced at their faces, and then the crowd's own pressure carried them through, into streets paved with marble pale as bone.

* * *

Vhalcrest was beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful, if you didn't look too closely at what it was for.

Silas looked. It was an occupational habit; a memory-thief learns to read a room the way a physician reads a body, noting where the warmth pools and where it drains away. And everywhere Silas looked in the capital, warmth was draining toward the center. The crowds flowed inward in slow tributaries. The shopkeepers along the processional way had hung garlands, but their eyes followed the pilgrims with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite envy. An old woman selling honey-cakes caught Silas watching her and said, quietly, without being asked:

"Don't stand too near the front, dear. Near the front, you come back different."

"Different how?" Tam asked, before Silas could stop him.

The woman's gaze flicked to him—sharp, quick, afraid—and then she busied herself with her cakes. "Different," she said. "Buy a cake or move along."

They moved along. But Silas turned the woman's words over all through the winding climb toward the Cathedral Square, tasting the fear underneath them. *You come back different.* The crown fed at the ceremonies—that much the drowned chapel had taught them, that much the hollowed worshipper had traded for the shape of Silas's mother's laugh. But feeding implied appetite, and appetite implied limits. What happened when fifty thousand offered themselves at once?

They found out at sundown, from the balcony of a dyer's house Wren had chosen because its upper window overlooked the square and its owner was deaf and its lock was older than the dynasty.

* * *

The square held them all.

Fifty thousand people packed shoulder to shoulder around a cathedral whose doors stood open like a mouth, and above those doors, above the whole western face of the building, stretched a mosaic of King Aldric: young, golden, smiling, crowned. The crown in the mosaic was rendered in tiles of gold leaf, and even in stillness—even as mere glass and metal—it seemed to catch the dying light and hold it, hoard it, refuse to let the evening come.

"They built him a face," Wren murmured beside Silas at the window. "Look at it. That's not how he looks anymore. I saw him once, years ago, at a treaty signing. He was handsome then, but tired. Ordinary-tired." Her fingers were moving again, drawing invisible lines on the windowsill. "This is a lie made of ten million tiles."

"People need the lie," Silas said. "It's easier to give your soul to a smiling god than to a hungry thing."

Below, the ceremony began.

It began with bells—deep bronze bells whose tones Silas felt in the sternum rather than heard—and then with light. Thousands of candles kindled at once, though no hand moved to light them; flame leapt wick to wick through the crowd like a rumor, and the pilgrims gasped, and wept, and called it grace. Silas watched the flames travel and felt the hair rise on their arms, because that was not grace. That was *drawing*. Something below, something ahead, something behind those open doors was pulling at the world the way a thirsty man pulls at a rope, and fire simply came first, being quicker than souls.

Then the king appeared.

Aldric came out onto the cathedral steps in white and gold, and the sound fifty thousand people made when they saw him was not a cheer. It was deeper than a cheer—it was the sound a drowning crowd makes when a rope is thrown, a single vast inhalation of hope, and it hurt to hear. Silas had steeled themselves to hate this man, the usurper, the tyrant whose armies had burned a refusing city to ash. Hatred would have been comfortable. Hatred would have been armor.

But Silas had met the prisoner inside him. Silas knew what wore the smile.

And now, seeing him across four hundred feet of candlelit stone, Silas saw it plainly: the way the king's hands trembled at his sides until he clasped them together; the way his eyes moved across the crowd not with love but with a terrible, searching hunger that was not his own; the way he paused, twice, mid-gesture, as if he'd forgotten what he was doing, as if pieces of him were going missing in real time. The crown sat on his head like a black star, drinking.

"He can't remember her laugh," Silas said softly. It wasn't meant to be said aloud. Wren looked at them anyway.

"What?"

"His daughter. He wrote it down—he keeps lists, hidden lists, trying to hold on to himself. *Her laugh sounds like—* and then nothing. The ink just stops." Silas's throat was tight. "He's been eaten slowly for years, and everyone in this square thinks he's a god."

The healing began.

* * *

Later, Silas would try to describe it to Odessa, and find that language failed the way a net fails to hold water. But in the moment there was no failure, only witness.

The priests opened the cathedral doors fully, and light came out—not candlelight, not sunlight, but a white radiance that moved with intention, flowing down the steps and into the crowd like milk poured into dark water. Where it touched, miracles happened. A boy's withered leg straightened with a sound like green wood bending. A blind woman cried out and clawed at her eyes and then laughed, laughed wildly, staring at the torches as if they were the dawn of creation. A man lame for thirty years dropped his crutches and wept into his hands.

And the crowd surged forward. Of course it did. Fifty thousand broken bodies pressed toward the light, screaming their gratitude before the miracle had even finished landing, offering themselves with both hands.

Silas watched the light take its payment.

It was subtle, at first. A healed farmer stood blinking in the glow, and something went out of his face—not joy, joy remained, joy blazed—but *specificity*. His features smoothed. When his wife embraced him, calling his name, he smiled at her the way a man smiles at a pleasant stranger. A girl cured of a crooked spine turned in circles, delighted, and her delight had no floor to it, no history, no self underneath; she spun like a puppet finding its strings. One by one, at the edge of the radiance, the healed stood glowing and hollow-eyed, loved ones clutching at them and finding no one home behind the miracle.

"It's not healing," Tam whispered. His face was pressed to the window bars, and he was very pale. "It's a trade. It's trading them for—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's eating them."

"Yes," Silas said.

"But they're *happy.*"

"That's the cruelest part of it. That's always the cruelest part." Silas had spent a lifetime among stolen memories; they knew the texture of a mind with holes in it, the placid blankness where a life used to be. "The crown takes the weight and leaves the lightness. People don't miss what they can't remember missing."

Wren had gone very still. She was not looking at the crowd anymore. She was looking at the cathedral—at the flow of the light itself—and her cartographer's eyes were measuring it. "Do you see the pattern?" she breathed. "The light doesn't spread evenly. It flows *inward*. Everything it takes, it carries back through those doors, down, underneath. There's a channel. A conduit." Her finger traced it in the air: steps, threshold, crypt, deep. "All of it goes to the same place. To *him*."

"To the crown," Silas said.

"To whatever the crown is *for*."

Below, the ceremony swelled toward its crescendo. The king raised his arms, and the crowd roared, and the white light thickened until it seemed the air itself had become syrup with grace, and Silas felt it then—felt it *personally*, the way one feels a hand slip into one's pocket. A tug. Faint, distant, indiscriminate, the great maw of the thing sweeping the square for loose crumbs of soul, and even here, even behind glass and distance and wards Wren had chalked on the sill, Silas felt memories shiver in their keeping like fish sensing a net.

Silas clamped down on their inner ledger, sealed every bottle, every shelf, every smuggled year of themselves, and the tug passed over them like weather. But around them, the city exhaled. Fifty thousand people sighed at once, sated and emptied, and the light withdrew up the steps and into the dark, and the bells began again, joyful, triumphant, obscene.

On the steps, King Aldric lowered his arms. For one moment—one unguarded moment before the priests closed around him—his face did something no mosaic would ever record. He looked out over his worshippers with naked horror. His lips moved. At this distance no sound could carry, but Silas read the shape of it, the way they'd learned to read lips across crowded markets while lifting a stranger's childhood from his coat pocket.

*Forgive me,* the king's mouth said. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

Then the crown flared, his spine straightened, and the smile returned like a mask settling into place, and he turned and walked back into his cathedral, into the dark that fed him, and the great doors boomed shut.

* * *

They did not speak for a long time after the square emptied. The candles guttered. Somewhere below, sweepers worked the marble, and the smell of snuffed tallow drifted up with the sound of their brooms.

Tam broke first. He was fourteen, and he had sold his birthday to a shrine, and he had watched fifty thousand people walk willingly into the same bargain tonight, and he sat down on the dyer's dusty floor and put his head in his hands and shook.

"They'll go home happy," he said. "That's the thing, isn't it? They'll go home and tell everyone. *Go to Vhalcrest, go be healed, it's a miracle, it's real.* And more will come. And more." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It doesn't even have to hunt. We line up. We *thank* it."

Silas crouched beside him. For a moment they almost reached for the old reflex—the clean theft, plucking the worst hour of the boy's night out of his head and pocketing it, leaving him lighter. They had done it a hundred times for a hundred reasons, most of them bad. Instead they put a hand on his shoulder, heavy and human.

"That's why we're here," Silas said. "Not to save the ones already fed. To close its mouth before the next meal."

"Can we?" Tam's voice cracked on it. "Really? Three of us? Against *that?*" He gestured at the window, at the cathedral, at the whole golden lying city.

"No," Silas said honestly. "Three of us, no. But we're not three. There's Odessa, bleeding but awake, and Brann buying us time in a cell somewhere, and forty dead knights holding a bridge of riddles, and a king inside the monster begging strangers to remember him. We're more than three. We're just badly organized."

That startled a wet, broken laugh out of the boy, which had been the intent.

Wren turned from the window at last. In the candlelight her face looked carved, all planes and purpose. "The conduit runs under the cathedral," she said. "Which means the ritual chamber runs under the cathedral. Which means everything—the crown, the binding, the hunger—all of it converges in one place beneath our feet." She tapped the floorboards. "And in five days, the anniversary rite. The one night a year the chamber opens for the public procession. Priests, choir, nobility—and pilgrims. Hundreds of pilgrims invited inside to witness the miracle at its source."

Silas felt it settle over them, cold and clear: the shape of the trap they had walked a thousand miles to spring on themselves.

"Pilgrims," Silas repeated. "Gray cloaks. Tokens. Faces full of faith."

"Pilgrims," Wren agreed.

Tam wiped his eyes and stood up straighter, and for a heartbeat he looked less like a child who had sold his happiest day and more like someone the dead knights might have saluted.

Below them, beneath fifty tons of marble and fifty thousand sleeping souls, something ancient shifted in its chains, and turned over, and dreamed of freedom—and Silas, thief of memories, keeper of blank pages, felt the dream touch the edge of their mind like a hand reaching up through dark water.

Five days.

They would need a miracle of their own. Fortunately, Silas knew exactly where the city kept them.
