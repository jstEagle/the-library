## 2. Prayers in the Hollow

The village announced itself to Noor's feet before her eyes confirmed it.

She had been walking since the false dawn, following a thread of path that only existed because the land wanted it to—a deer trail that widened politely where she stepped, a sheep track that curved around bog ground as though the earth itself were conspiring to keep her boots dry. That was the gift, or the curse, depending on the hour and her hunger: Noor Thornbury could feel which paths wanted to be walked. Maghda had called it *hearing*, though there was no sound in it. It was more like standing in a dark room full of sleeping animals and knowing, without looking, which ones dreamed of running.

By midmorning the paths began leaning toward each other, converging with the shy insistence of streams finding a valley, and Noor knew she was being funneled toward habitation whether she liked it or not.

She did not like it.

Three days out from Maghda's cottage, three days of cold camp and stolen windfall apples, and already the roads were trying to hand her over to people. She considered fighting them—cutting cross-country through bracken and gorse until the land gave up its opinion—but her feet ached, her food was gone, and pride made a thin soup. So when the track crested a low hill and showed her Hollowmere below, tucked into its fold of hills like a coin dropped in a purse, she stood a long moment at the top and let herself be looked at by the place before she went down into it.

It was a small village. Thirty roofs, maybe forty. A millpond gone silver with duckweed. A church with a squat stone tower whose bell hung silent in the open arch, rope frayed and knotted up out of reach, as if someone had decided some time ago that this particular voice should not be heard again. And beyond the last cottages, on a gentle south-facing slope—

Noor stopped breathing.

There was an orchard there. She knew it was an orchard because of what it wasn't: rows of dead trees, black-limbed, their bark split and peeling like old parchment, standing in the brown grass the way gravestones stand in a churchyard. Every tree was dead. Not sleeping—dead. She could feel it from the hilltop, the way you can feel a cold hearth across a cold room. Whatever those trees had been, they had finished being it years ago.

Except that half of one tree, halfway down the slope, was in bloom.

White blossom, dense as snowfall, covering every branch of the eastern half of the tree while the western half stood bare and black. The contrast was so violent, so *wrong*, that Noor's first thought was not wonder but fire—that someone had set a blossom-colored flame against the dead wood and it had caught.

Her second thought arrived slower and colder: the land beneath the orchard was humming.

Not the ordinary hum of living soil, the deep patient drone of roots and worms and water that Noor had heard all her life the way other people hear wind. This was a higher note, thinner, sweeter, like a wet finger run round the rim of a glass. It came from under the orchard and spread outward, under the village, under her feet on the hilltop, and it had in it something that made the fine hairs on her arms lift.

It sounded pleased.

* * *

Hollowmere took her in the way small villages take in strangers: thoroughly, suspiciously, and with an offer of food.

She gave her name as Thorn, said she was walking to her aunt's over the fells, and let the lie sit in the air of the inn's common room while the innkeeper's wife studied her travel-stained cloak and decided, visibly, that a lone young person was more charity than threat. By the time the barley soup arrived, Noor had learned three things from the loose talk around her: that the orchard had bloomed two nights past; that it had been Old Maren's doing; and that everyone had known it would be, because everyone had prayed for it, but only Maren's prayer had been answered.

"Because Maren *meant* it," said a broad woman with flour to her elbows, in the tone of someone explaining weather to a child. "You can pray polite your whole life and mean nothing by it. Maren meant every word. Her son planted that orchard, and her son's in the ground these six years, and the trees died the winter after him. She went out there night after last and got down on her knees in the frost and asked—" the woman paused, and her voice changed, softened into something almost embarrassed by its own sincerity, "—she asked whoever was listening. That's what she said. *Whoever is listening.*"

"And something listened," said a man by the fire, and he crossed himself in the old way, thumb to brow, thumb to breast, the gesture country people made when the church's god felt too far away to bother with formalities.

Something listened. Noor kept her eyes on her soup and her hearing on the floor beneath her, where the sweet glass-rim note ran on and on, patient as a spider.

*Whoever is listening.* Maghda's voice spoke in her memory, dry as seed husks: *That, girl, is how you invite things in. Never pray wide. Pray narrow, pray named, or don't pray at all.* Noor had been eleven. She had asked who might come if you prayed wide, and Maghda had looked at her for a long moment with those smoke-grey eyes and said, *Things that are hungry for the shape of an answer,* and had never spoken of it again.

The soup was good. Noor ate all of it and paid with the last of the coins she'd taken from the cottage jar, and hated herself a little for the theft all over again, and told herself—as she'd told herself for three days—that leaving had been the only sane thing, that Maghda's demand had been madness, that binding a person's gift shut forever was not protection but amputation.

The thought did not taste any better than it had on the first night.

* * *

She saw Old Maren at dusk.

The whole village seemed to drift toward the orchard as the light went long and gold, the way iron filings drift toward a lodestone, and Noor went with them because not going would have marked her worse than going. They gathered at the orchard gate in twos and threes, caps in hands, children hushed without being told to be, and at the center of them, small and straight-backed as a wren, stood a grandmother in a shawl the color of undyed wool.

Maren was not looking at the blossoms. That was the first thing Noor noticed, and it snagged in her like a fishhook. Everyone else was staring at the impossible white half-tree, drinking it in, but the old woman's face was turned slightly away, tilted, as though she were listening to something just behind her own shoulder.

Then she turned, and smiled at her neighbors, and the smile was so radiant, so entirely happy, that Noor felt the wrongness of it like a draft under a door.

*Come closer,* the land was humming. *Come and see. Come and be glad.*

Noor edged along the fence line, apart from the crowd, and put her palm flat against the top rail of the gate, and opened her hearing the way Maghda had taught her—one careful finger's width, no more, the way you'd crack a door in winter to listen for wolves.

The orchard roared.

Not loudly—the humming didn't change volume at all—but with everything she had cracked open even that far, the sweetness became enormous, a choir of one note sung by a throat too large for the sky above the village. Beneath the sweetness lay the trees: the true dead trees, their honest silence, the plain fact of wood that had finished. And threaded through both, wrapping the deadness like silk wound round a thorn, was the other thing. The listener. The answerer.

It was *delighted*. That was the horror of it. It was not mocking, not cruel, not hungry in any way she could name—it was delighted the way a child is delighted, wholly and without shadow, and its delight poured upward through the roots of the dead trees and out through the blossoming branches and down into the kneeling heart of the old woman at the gate, and Maren drank it like rain.

"You see," Maren was saying to the villagers, and her voice was clear and strong and carried further than an old voice should. "You see what happens when you ask truly. All these years we mumbled our prayers at a heaven that never bent down. And the moment one of us knelt in the frost and asked with her whole heart—look. *Look.*"

She gestured at the blossoms, and a petal came loose, impossibly out of season, and spiraled down onto her shawl, and the villagers made a sound like a held breath released.

"He answers," whispered the flour-woman, tears on her cheeks. "He actually answers."

*He,* thought Noor, with her hand on the gate and her gift straining against her discipline like a dog against a leash. *They've named it already. One day, and they've given it a he.*

Because here was what she could feel, what she could not stop feeling now that the door was cracked: the thing beneath the orchard had no shape of its own. It was answering Maren's prayer the way still water answers a thrown stone—with ripples in exactly the shape of the stone. Maren had needed her son's orchard alive again, needed it with the whole desperate weight of six years of grief, and so the thing had reached into the dead trees and *made* what she needed. Half a tree of blossom, out of season, on dead wood, fed by nothing, rooted in nothing, sustained by nothing except the continuing pressure of the old woman's need.

And Noor understood, with a sick lurch, that the moment Maren's faith wavered—even for a single sleepless night, even for one morning of ordinary doubt—the blossoms would fall. All of them at once. And the tree would be dead again, deader than before, because now it would have died twice.

The thing beneath the orchard did not care. Its delight had no floor in it, no bottom of understanding. It was giving the village exactly what it prayed for the way a mirror gives back a face: perfectly, instantly, and with nothing behind the glass.

* * *

She stayed three days in Hollowmere, telling herself each evening that she would leave in the morning.

It was the blossoms that held her, though she pretended otherwise. On the second day the second tree woke—not half, but whole, every black branch shouldered with white—and on the third morning a third, and the villagers had stopped calling it Maren's miracle and started calling it *the blessing*, and had begun to leave offerings at the orchard gate. Bread. Milk in a chipped bowl. A ribbon from a girl's hair. Small things, humble things, laid on the flat stone of the gatepost with the shy tenderness people usually reserve for graves.

And the humming grew stronger. Noor could feel it from the inn now, could feel it in her teeth when she bit bread, a sweetness with that hair-thin wrongness underneath, and each day the wrongness was a little less thin. Each day it was a little more *there*.

On the third evening she walked out to the orchard alone, after the villagers had gone in to their suppers, and stood among the blooming dead.

Up close the trees were worse. The living halves were flawless—every petal perfect, unblemished, identical, as if each blossom had been copied from the same original by something that did not understand why originals have flaws. There were no bees on them. She watched for a long time in the failing light and no bee came, no moth, no beetle, no bird. The blossoms had no scent either, or rather they had a scent that was almost scent, a sweetness remembered rather than smelled, and when she crushed one gently between her fingers it left no stain and no smell at all, just a cool dampness like the inside of a stone.

"They're beautiful," said a voice behind her, and Noor nearly came out of her skin.

Old Maren stood at the end of the row, shawled against the dusk, watching her with bright bird eyes. "You're the walker girl. Thorn." The old woman came closer, unhurried, her footsteps making no sound in the grass. "You look at my trees like the priest used to look at the collection plate. Like you're counting what's missing."

"I'm sorry," Noor said. "They are beautiful."

"Mm." Maren stopped beside her, close enough that Noor could smell woodsmoke and lavender and, underneath, faintly, frost. "My boy Aldous planted these. He had hands like spades. He'd have loved this." She gazed up into the white branches, and her face was soft, and for a moment Noor thought: *she doesn't know. She can't feel what I feel. To her it's simply a miracle.*

Then Maren said, quietly, without turning her head: "You hear it too, don't you."

The words went through Noor like cold water through cloth.

"The others hear the singing," the old woman said. "The lovely part. But you flinched, that first evening at the gate. I watch people. It's what old women do instead of living." Now she turned, and her eyes were not clouded at all; they were clear and grey and terribly tired. "You flinched. So you hear the rest of it. Underneath."

Noor's mouth was dry. Every instinct Maghda had ever hammered into her screamed *deny it, laugh, be a stupid walker girl asking directions to the next town*—and she opened her mouth to do exactly that, and what came out instead, in a whisper, was:

"What is it?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Out past the orchard, the first star found its hole in the sky, and somewhere in the village a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I don't know," the old woman said at last. "I knelt in the frost and I asked whoever was listening, and something listened, and my trees bloomed, and I don't know what it is." Her voice did not shake. "But I'll tell you what I know, girl, since you're the only soul in this hollow who'll understand it. When it answered me—I'd been praying to God, do you see? Forty years of praying to God. And what answered me was not God. It was—" her brow furrowed, searching for the word with a craftsman's care, "—it was *glad*. Glad the way nothing ought to be glad. Glad like a starving man glad. It was so happy to be asked for anything at all that I lay awake all night after, warm as toast, feeling its happiness, and I thought: whatever this is, it has waited a very long time for someone to knock."

The humming rose around them, sweet and vast, and Noor realized with a start that it had changed key—shifted subtly, the way a host shifts his manner when guests arrive—to welcome them. To welcome *her*. The note slid against her hearing like a hand testing a latch.

"It knows I'm here," she breathed.

"Course it does." Maren plucked a blossom from a low branch and held it in her palm between them, perfect, scentless, dead wood wearing life like a borrowed coat. "You knocked loudest."

"No." Noor stepped back, her heart slamming. "I haven't prayed. I haven't asked it for anything."

"You stood at my gate with your hand on the wood and you *listened*," said the old woman. "In my day, child, that was prayer enough for anything old and hungry. Listening is how it gets in. Asking is only how it stays."

The blossom in Maren's palm trembled—though there was no wind—and then, petal by petal, it closed itself shut again, folding inward like a fist, like an eye, like a door being politely, patiently, closed from the other side.

Above them the moon was rising, three nights past full, fat and pale over the eastern fells. Noor looked at it and felt, for no reason she could name, the way you feel a stair missing in the dark.

Thirteen moons, said a thought that was not hers, arriving whole and cold, the way true things arrive. Count them.

Behind her, in the village, the church bell that had hung silent for years gave a single soft ring, all by itself, though no rope touched it and no wind stirred the tower.

Old Maren crossed herself in the old way, and for the first time since Noor had met her, the grandmother looked afraid.

"That," she said, "wasn't me."
