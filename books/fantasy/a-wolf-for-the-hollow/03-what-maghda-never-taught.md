## 3. What Maghda Never Taught

The night Noor fled, she had stood in Maghda's doorway for a long time with her hand on the latch, and the door had not wanted to be opened.

She had known doors like that before—the smuggler's passes in the Thornfells, the ones that narrowed where a body ought to hesitate, that widened where a body ought to run. The land spoke to her that way, always had, in a language older than words: *this stone is tired, this path remembers blood, this hollow wants to be walked.* She was eleven years old the first time she understood it wasn't something everyone could do. She had told Maghda, and Maghda had gone very still over her stew, and then very busy about nothing, and for three days afterward the old woman had salted every threshold in the cottage twice.

That should have been the first lesson. It took Noor another nine years to learn it.

* * *

The cottage sat at the crook of two ridgelines above the village of Fellbrook, close enough to smell other people's woodsmoke on still evenings, far enough that no one visited without reason. Maghda had chosen it deliberately. Noor understood that now. Everything about their life there had been deliberate—a study in unremarkableness, in being the kind of people a village forgets between one winter and the next.

Maghda herself was the kind of woman who made forgetting easy. She was small and bent as a shepherd's crook, with hands like tree roots and a face that had stopped trying to be pretty sometime around her fortieth year and had settled instead into something better: a face that gave nothing away. She mended nets in the lower season and sold poultices in the higher ones, and if her poultices worked a little too well sometimes—if a fever broke overnight, if a cow's bad hoof healed in three days instead of thirty—well, villages are generous with their explanations when the explanations suit them.

Noor had grown up inside that generosity, inside its shadow, learning its rules without ever being taught them aloud.

Or so she had believed, until the autumn of her twentieth year, when Maghda finally began to teach.

* * *

It started with the bees.

The hives behind the cottage had gone quiet in late summer—not dead, but quiet, the way animals go quiet before a hard winter they can feel coming. Noor noticed it because she noticed everything the land did; it was less a skill than a condition, like tinnitus, like a second heartbeat. The mountain's mood came to her through the soles of her feet and the roots of her teeth. And that autumn the mountain was holding its breath.

"Something's waking," she told Maghda at supper, because she had never learned to keep such things inside. "Down deep. Under the Thornfells. It's not awake yet, but it's—it's turning over. Like someone in sleep."

Maghda set down her spoon. In the firelight her face did what it always did, which was nothing, but her hands—her hands Noor had learned to read the way other people read weather—her hands went into her sleeves and stayed there.

"Say that again," Maghda said. "Slower."

So Noor said it again, slower, and tried to give it shape: the pressure beneath the ridge, the taste of iron in the well water that no one else tasted, the way the old cairn up on Shepherd's Rise seemed to hum at dusk, a note too low for ears, felt only in the hollow of the throat. She had been feeling these things for months and saying nothing, because saying things was how you got salted thresholds. But the feeling had been growing, swelling like water behind a dam, and she was twenty years old and tired of swallowing.

When she finished, the fire popped. Somewhere outside an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"You will tell me," Maghda said quietly, "the moment anything changes. Not tomorrow. The moment."

"I always tell you."

"No." For the first time all evening, Maghda looked at her straight on, and there was something in her eyes Noor had never seen there before, or rather something she had seen and refused to name: fear. "You tell me the pleasant things. The paths that want walking. You have never once told me what wakes beneath us, because I think some part of you knows that once you say it plainly, we cannot unsay it." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the chest at the foot of her bed—the locked one, the one Noor had been forbidden since childhood—and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

"Then teach me properly," Noor said. "Whatever you know. Teach me all of it."

And Maghda, unwrapping the bundle with hands that were not quite steady, said the sentence that would end everything:

"That is the last thing I will ever do, child. What I'm going to teach you is how to stop hearing."

* * *

Inside the oilcloth was a needle of black iron, long as a finger, and a spool of thread that was not thread—some hair-fine filament that caught the firelight wrong, silver and then not-silver, like the surface of water deciding whether to be mirror or window.

"The rite has names in the old books," Maghda said. "I'll spare you the names. Names invite things. What matters is this: the gift you carry is a door, and doors open both ways. You hear the land. But the land—" she paused, and Noor watched her choose her words with the care of a woman laying stones across a bog, "—the land is not the only thing that speaks through it. There are older voices under the ground-voice. Things that slept so long they forgot they were things, and things that were put down so hard they cannot rise, and things that were never meant to have voices at all until men gave them one."

"The old gods," Noor whispered.

"Don't say it like that." Sharp, sudden, and Noor flinched. Maghda never raised her voice; the sharpness frightened more than shouting would have. "You don't say it at all. You don't name them, you don't pray near them, you don't hum the tunes the grandmothers hum at the shrines. Do you understand me? A name is a handle. Every prayer is a hand reaching for that handle. And you—" her voice dropped, went rough, "—you were born with your hands full of handles, girl. You hear them the way a bell hears a strike. One day something will strike, and you will ring, and whatever is listening will know exactly where you are."

"So we hide," Noor said. "We've always hidden. We're good at it."

"We have been lucky." Maghda turned the iron needle in the firelight. "Luck is not a craft. This is a craft. Tonight I teach you the binding, and tomorrow you bind yourself, and after that you will be deaf the way everyone else is deaf, and safe the way everyone else is safe, and you will live out your years as a netmender's daughter in a village that barely remembers your name. That is the whole of the inheritance I have to give you. I am sorry it isn't more."

Noor looked at the needle. Something in her chest had gone cold and small, like a snail retreating into its shell.

"And if I say no?"

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the fire to settle twice.

"Then you are a fool," Maghda said at last, gently, which was worse than anger. "And fools die loudly."

* * *

They did not speak of it again for six days. That was its own kind of teaching. Noor went about the work—nets, herbs, the winter woodpile—and felt the mountain breathing under her feet, and said nothing, and hated the saying-nothing, and did it anyway, because she had been doing it for nine years and nine years makes a groove that a soul rolls along like a cart-wheel.

But the feeling kept growing.

On the sixth night she woke with her heart hammering and lay in the dark listening to the ridge, and the ridge was listening back. That was new. All her life the land had been indifferent the way weather is indifferent—vast, present, unconcerned with her. Now there was attention in it, a slow vast turning-toward, like the owl's head swiveling. Something down in the deep stone had noticed the noticing. She couldn't have said how she knew. She knew the way she knew which path wanted walking: completely, and without evidence.

She went to Maghda's room and stood in the doorway, and the old woman was already awake, sitting up in the dark.

"It heard me," Noor said. "Last night. Whatever's down there—it heard me hearing it."

Maghda lit the candle. Her face in its light was terrible with tiredness. "Then we do the rite tonight."

"No." Noor heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "I've been thinking. Six days of thinking. You want to cut out the only true thing I have. You want me deaf and safe and small, and you call that an inheritance, and maybe it is, maybe you're right, maybe I'd live eighty years mending nets and die quiet and never know what I lost." Her hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against her thighs. "But Maghda—I felt it turn toward me. Toward *us*. Toward this valley. And you sit there with your locked chest and your salted thresholds and you know more than you've ever told me, and you're going to let it wake over our heads while I sit deaf in a corner pretending the world is only what my eyes show me."

"What would you do instead?" Maghda asked. "Walk down to it? Knock?"

"Maybe! Maybe learn its name instead of running from it. Maybe find out why it's waking now, after all these centuries. Maybe—" and here the truth came up out of her like groundwater, unstoppable, "—maybe someone has to. Because if it's waking, it's waking everywhere, in every hollow village from here to the sea, and all the grandmothers praying at all the shrines are going to get answers, and nobody—*nobody*, Maghda—is going to be able to tell whether the thing answering is a god or a wolf wearing a god's skin. Nobody except me."

The candle flame leaned though there was no draft.

Maghda looked at her for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone very soft, softer than Noor had ever heard it, and that softness was the most frightening thing in the whole terrible night.

"There it is," the old woman said. "There's the pride. Nine years I've starved it and fed it by turns, hidden it, salted it, prayed over it while you slept, and there it sits, fat as a harvest mouse. You think you're the only bell in the world, child. You think the gift makes you necessary. I knew a man who thought that, once. He could hear the land better than you—he could hear it in his *dreams*—and he went down to the deep places to learn names, and what came back up wearing his face answered prayers for eleven years in three valleys before anyone understood why the crops grew and the children sickened and the dogs would not stop howling." She closed her fist around the iron needle until Noor thought she must be cutting herself. "I buried him. I was his apprentice, and I buried him, and then I spent forty years making sure no one ever handed me another fool to bury."

"Who was he?" Noor whispered. "Your teacher?"

"My brother," Maghda said, "and I loved him, and that is exactly why you will pick up that needle."

And Noor—twenty years old, proud, terrified, certain—looked at the needle, and looked at the door, and understood with perfect clarity that she was standing at a fork the land itself could not map for her.

If she took the needle, she would be safe. Deaf, diminished, folded into invisibility like a letter no one reads. She would live. She might even be happy, in the way that a bird with clipped wings is happy in a warm room.

If she walked out the door, she would keep the gift, and keep the danger, and keep the pride Maghda feared—and she would be alone, because she would be leaving the only person in the world who had ever protected her, and leaving her in the worst way: mid-argument, unforgiven, with the last word hers.

She chose the door.

Later—much later, on roads she could not yet imagine—she would understand that this was the moment she should have examined hardest. Not the rite, not the refusal, but the choosing. Because she had told herself she walked out for the valley's sake, for the grandmothers' sake, for truth's sake. And some of that was even true. But underneath the truth ran a plainer current, cold and quick: she did not want to be small. She did not want to be safe if safety cost her the hearing, the knowing, the being-necessary. Maghda had offered her a long gray life and called it love, and Noor had called it a cage, and both of them had been right, and neither of them had been brave enough to say the third thing, which was that fear wore both their faces—that Maghda's ritual was fear of losing her, and Noor's refusal was fear of being ordinary, and the door she opened that night was opened less by courage than by cowardice wearing courage's coat.

But that understanding was years away. That night she simply took her cloak from its peg, and the door—sensible, honest door—did not want to open, and she opened it anyway.

* * *

The pass she took out of the Thornfells was a smuggler's route, unmarked on any map, one of the seventeen she carried in her memory like coins in a purse. It went up through scree and over a shoulder of bare rock and down into the next valley through a fold in the hills so narrow the stars thinned to a seam overhead. The land talked to her the whole way—*cold, high, exposed, walk fast*—and she listened, and obeyed, and wept without stopping, the way you can weep for hours on a mountainside because there is no one to see and the wind takes the sound.

At the top of the shoulder she stopped and looked back once. The cottage was a spark of banked firelight in the dark fold of the ridges. Small. Warm. Still there. She let herself imagine, just for a breath, going back down—taking the needle, taking the deafness, taking Maghda's arms around her, which had happened perhaps ten times in twenty years and each time had felt like being forgiven for something enormous.

Then she felt it again: the slow vast turning beneath the stone, the attention like an owl's head swinging toward her across the dark miles. And under it, fainter, stranger, something new—a thread of wrongness in the world's voice, off-key, hungry, *listening for listeners.*

She turned south and did not look back again.

Three days later, footsore and hollow-eyed, she came down out of the foothills into a river valley where mist lay on the water like a sheet over furniture, and a village showed its rooftops through the fog—crooked lanes, a leaning church tower, an orchard of bare black trees at the edge of the fields. A sign by the road read HOLLOWMERE, the letters worn nearly smooth.

The land here spoke softly, wearily, the voice of a place long forgotten even by itself. No paths wanted walking. Nothing wanted anything. It was, Noor thought with a relief that frightened her, the most invisible place she had ever stood.

She pulled her hood up and went in.

Behind her, far to the north, under the roots of the Thornfells, something turned over in its sleep—and smiled, though it had no face to smile with, at the sound of a bell walking away that did not yet know it was ringing.
