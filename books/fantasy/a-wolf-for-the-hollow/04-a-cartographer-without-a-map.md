## 4. A Cartographer Without a Map

The inn at Hollowmere was called the Drowned Bell, which Wren had always thought a strange name for a place so far from any water deeper than the millpond—until her third night there, when the fog came down off the Thornfells and swallowed the village whole, and she understood that everything in the hollow lived half-drowned all the time. The fog pressed against the windows like something patient. The fire hissed. And Wren sat in the corner with her back to the wall, as she had learned to sit in two years of exile, drinking small beer because she could no longer afford wine and pretending she preferred it.

She had been a cartographer once. Not the kind who copies coastlines for schoolchildren—the kind who draws the edges of things, the borders where one authority ends and another begins, the lines that decide whether a farmer pays his tithe to one crown or loses his field to another. She had been good at it. That was the bitter joke of it: they had not exiled her for being careless. They had exiled her for being precise in exactly the wrong places.

The Royal Cartographic Office kept its errors the way other institutions kept their saints' relics: carefully, reverently, behind glass. A border drawn wrong on purpose was called policy. A border drawn right against policy was called treason. Wren had spent four years drawing the eastern marches with a deliberate softness, blurring passes that existed, inventing ravines that didn't, until the maps said what the Crown needed them to say about where Aldric's kingdom ended—and then, in one week of madness or honesty, she had redrawn three sheets true. Every pass. Every ford. Every smuggler's stair cut into the mountainside by hands that had never sworn loyalty to anyone.

She had told herself it was for the king's own good. That was the part she could not stop turning over, like a stone with something underneath it. The assassination route had been real; she had found it in an intercepted letter; and if the maps showed the true paths, the guards could close them. Except the guards had not closed them. The guards had used them. Someone in the palace had read her honest maps the way a wolf reads a deer trail, and six months later King Aldric had ridden into the high country by a road that appeared on no official chart, and had come back down from it changed—or so the rumors went, the ones you only heard after enough beer, in places like the Drowned Bell, from people who lowered their voices without knowing why.

Wren did not believe most rumors. She believed in ink, in scale bars, in the stubborn truth of contour lines. But she had seen the king once, after. At the winter court, through a crowd, before her exile was formalized. He had been standing very straight in a way that reminded her of a man holding a note too long, a singer past the end of the phrase, and his eyes had gone over her face without stopping, and she had thought: *that is not a man looking at me. That is a lighthouse sweeping past a boat.*

She drank her small beer and did not think about it. She was getting better at not thinking about it, the way a scar gets better at aching.

* * *

Hollowmere suited her the way a worn coat suits a beggar—not well, but without questions. It was a village of perhaps sixty souls folded into a crease of the foothills, the sort of place the royal surveyors had marked with a dot and a name and nothing else, because there was nothing else worth the ink. One street. One mill. One shrine-house with a bell that had cracked sometime before anyone living was born, so that when it rang it made a sound like a word spoken through water. The villagers were polite in the way of people who have decided you are nobody, which was the finest gift a hiding woman could receive.

She had come here for a reason she admitted to no one, least of all herself: the Thornfells. Her false maps had hidden those passes; her true maps had exposed them; and somewhere in between, the thing had happened that unmade the king. If she could not undo what she had done, she could at least sit near it, the way you sit near a grave. She rented a room above the stable that smelled of hay and tallow, hung her one good coat on a nail, and told the innkeeper she was a widow waiting out a legal matter. The innkeeper, a broad woman named Sef with forearms like bread loaves, had looked at Wren's ink-stained fingers and said, "Aye, widow," in a tone that meant she knew better and did not care. That, too, was a kindness.

On this particular evening—the fourth night of fog, the one the old women would later call the beginning of it—Wren sat in the common room watching the door more than the fire, because two years of exile had taught her that doors were where trouble entered, and because the door kept opening on villagers shaking off the wet, and among them, lately, was someone Wren could not stop noticing.

The apprentice. Or whatever she was—no one seemed to know. A young woman, thin as a winter branch, with dark hair cut short in a way that suggested either grief or practicality, who had arrived some weeks ago and taken work with old Mother Ilsa at the edge of the village, grinding herbs and hauling water. She kept to herself. She ate alone. She sat, Wren had observed with professional interest, with her back to walls and her face to doors, and she flinched at sudden sounds, and she watched people's hands rather than their faces—all the habits of a creature that had learned the world could bite.

Wren had catalogued her the way she catalogued everything: the calluses on the right hand suggesting a staff or tool gripped often; the boots resoled twice, badly, by the wearer herself; the way she moved through the village as though memorizing it, glances flicking to rooflines and gaps in hedges. Running away from something, Wren had concluded. Running toward nothing. Another person hiding in a hollow, like a coin dropped in a well.

She had even given her a name in her head, the way you name landmarks on a private map: *the wren's shadow*, she'd almost called her, before discarding it as vanity. She settled on *the ghost of the herb-garden*. It was accurate. It was safe. Naming a thing kept it at a distance, fixed it on the page where it couldn't move.

Except that tonight, the ghost sat closer to the fire than usual, and the fog had come down heavier than usual, and the bell rang.

It rang for the evening devotion, as it did every night—six strokes of that cracked, drowned voice rolling up from the shrine-house. Wren had heard it forty-odd times now and barely registered it. But the whole room heard it differently tonight. Everyone paused. Even Sef, wiping the table, stilled her cloth. Because under the crack, under the water-sound of it, the bell had rung *wrong*—a beat late, or a tone bent, as if the metal itself had hesitated, or as if something on the far side of the sound had leaned against it.

And the ghost—the apprentice, the herb-girl—flinched. Not the ordinary flinch of a nervous creature at a loud noise. Wren had seen that flinch a hundred times and knew its shape. This was different. This was the flinch of a person who had *heard something inside the sound*. Her whole body turned toward the window, toward the shrine-house, the way a compass needle turns, and her face—for one unguarded moment before she buried it—was the face of someone listening to a voice in a language only she spoke, and hearing something obscene in it.

Then it was gone. She looked down at her bowl of barley soup. She was just a tired girl eating supper in a mountain village, and the bell was just a cracked bell, and the fog was just fog.

But Wren's heart was going like a hammer, and her beer sat forgotten, and every instinct she owned as a maker of maps—the instinct to see the true shape beneath the drawn one—stood up inside her and pointed.

Because Wren knew what it was to hear a wrongness under a rightness. She had spent four years drawing beautiful lies and one week drawing truth, and she knew the sensation of looking at an official map—gilded, certified, stamped with the royal seal—and feeling, underneath the confident lines, the sick lurch of *this is not what is there*. Most people could not feel that lurch. Most people trusted the map because trusting the map was easier. But a few—a rare, cursed few—could stand before the prettiest forgery in the kingdom and *hear* it lying.

The girl had just heard a bell lie.

Or heard something speak through it. Wren wasn't sure which possibility frightened her more, and she was a woman who had made her living being sure about terrain.

* * *

She did not approach her that night. Two years in exile teaches patience the way a river teaches stone. Instead she watched, and the watching became a discipline, and the discipline became, without her quite consenting to it, a kind of hope—which was the most dangerous supply to carry in a hollow village in early winter.

Over the following days she learned what the village knew, which was little, and what the village suspected, which was more. The girl was called Noor. She lodged with Mother Ilsa but paid her own way, money from no one knew where. She was quiet, competent, and kind in a furtive way—kind the way poachers are generous, slipping things to people when no official generosity is watching. Tam, the miller's boy, swore she'd pulled him out of the ice on the millrace in the first cold snap, and swore also that she'd made him promise never to tell, which of course meant he told everyone.

And the village talked, in the way of villages, about the orchard. Old Marta's orchard, dead these nine years—every tree blackened by some blight no herbalist could name—had bloomed overnight. Blossoms in the frost. Apples set where no apple should set, fruit heavy on bare branches while snow stood in the furrows. The priest at the shrine-house had wept. The grandmother had gone from house to house with armfuls of apples, saying the god had heard her, the god had finally heard her, after a lifetime of prayers.

They said it joyfully. But Wren noticed—and she was a woman trained to notice—that the joy had a strange texture to it. People laughed a little too quickly. They repeated the story a little too often, as if rehearsing it, as if convincing themselves. And more than one villager, telling it, glanced toward the shrine-house with an expression Wren recognized from border towns where soldiers had recently passed through: the look of people deciding, consciously, to be happy, because the alternative was unthinkable.

Something answered prayers in Hollowmere. Something made dead trees bloom in defiance of the season. And everyone agreed this was a blessing, the way everyone agrees a map with the royal seal is true.

Wren sat in the Drowned Bell each evening and watched Noor not watch the celebrations, and saw in the set of her shoulders a knowledge held like a full bladder—painful, urgent, unspeakable. The girl knew something was wrong. The girl knew it the way Wren knew a forged map was forged: not by argument but by the body, by the lurch, by the ear that catches the false note under the hymn.

And Wren understood, sitting there with her back to the wall, that she was looking at the only other person in the world—at least the only one within a hundred miles—who could hear the difference between a god and a lie wearing a god's voice.

She also understood that approaching such a person was like approaching a wild thing caught in a snare: wrongly done, it would tear itself apart trying to escape her.

So she waited. She let the fog thicken. She let the evenings shorten. She bought Noor a cup of cider one night, left at her elbow without comment, and received in return a glance sharp enough to skin a rabbit—suspicion, calculation, and beneath both, so briefly Wren might have imagined it, a flicker of terrible loneliness. The look of someone who has been alone so long that kindness feels like a trap sprung shut.

Yes, Wren thought. I know that look. I shave in its reflection every morning.

* * *

It came to a head on a night of hard frost, a week after the orchard bloomed, when the bell rang again—wrong again, more wrong, the crack in its voice widened into something like a second voice answering the first—and Noor stood up from her corner so abruptly her stool scraped, and the whole room turned to look at her, and she stood there white-faced with her hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding a door shut from the inside.

"Bad soup?" asked Sef gently.

"I need air," Noor said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she was out the door into the fog before anyone could answer.

Wren gave it the count of thirty. Then she put a coin on the table, took her cloak, and followed.

The fog outside was total, a white blindness lit dimly orange around the inn's windows. She heard breathing somewhere ahead—quick, ragged, the breathing of someone fighting something invisible—and followed it past the well, past the shrine-house whose dark bulk loomed with its cracked bell hanging silent above, out toward the edge of the village where the fields fell away toward the millrace.

Noor stood at the fence line with her fists gripping the top rail hard enough that her knuckles must have ached, staring out at nothing, at the white void where the mountains would be if the world allowed seeing.

"You heard it," Wren said.

Noor spun. In the fog-light her eyes were wide and dark and furious with fear. "Go away."

"The bell." Wren kept her voice low and level, the voice you use near spooked livestock. "Tonight. And last week. It rang wrong, and everyone heard the wrongness, and everyone decided not to have heard it. But you—" She stepped closer, slowly. "You didn't decide anything. You can't. You heard what was underneath."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Wren said. "You know exactly what I'm talking about. That's the problem."

Silence. The fog beaded on both their cloaks. Somewhere behind them the inn door opened and shut, muffled, distant, another world.

"Who are you?" Noor said at last, and it was not a question so much as a challenge thrown down like a knife.

"Wren Ashvale. Formerly of the Royal Cartographic Office, formerly of the capital, formerly of a great many things." She spread her empty hands. "Now a widow waiting out a legal matter, according to my landlady, who does not believe it."

Something moved at the corner of Noor's mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of where smiles were kept.

"And what do you want?"

"I want," Wren said, and found that the truth came out of her like a splinter long embedded, "to know if I'm mad. I've been sitting in that inn for two months telling myself the bell sounds the same as ever, because if it doesn't—if it's really changing, really being *used*—then something is happening in this kingdom that isn't on any map I ever drew or ever faked. And you're the first person I've met whose face says I'm not mad." She breathed out, and the fog took her breath. "I falsified the border maps, you know. The eastern marches. Everyone in the capital knows my name for it. I drew the passes wrong to hide them, and then I drew them right, and I still don't know which was the sin. So believe me when I tell you I know what it costs to be the only one who can see the true shape of a thing while everyone around you kneels to the pretty version."

Noor stared at her. The fury in her face was guttering, and behind it, visible now, was the thing Wren had hoped for and dreaded in equal measure: recognition. The awful relief of the drowning swimmer who sees another head above water.

"You're the map-forger," Noor said slowly. "Tam talks. Everyone talks."

"I'm the map-forger."

"And you think I can—I don't know what—hear bells."

"I think you can hear more than bells." Wren took one more step, close enough now to see the fog freezing in the fine hairs of Noor's cheek. "I think you heard something answer old Marta's prayers, and I think you know it wasn't any god your grandmother would recognize. And I think you've been carrying that knowledge alone because there is no one alive you can give it to." She paused. "Until possibly tonight."

For a long moment Noor said nothing. The fog closed around them like a held breath. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its defenses:

"If I tell you what I hear, you'll wish you hadn't asked."

"I spent four years wishing I hadn't drawn what I drew," Wren said. "I've built up a tolerance."

And that—finally, blessedly—won the ghost of a real smile, there and gone, a landmark noted and erased.

"Come to Ilsa's cottage tomorrow," Noor said. "After dark. If you're going to regret this, you can do it properly, indoors, with tea."

She turned back to the fog and the unseen mountains, and Wren, understanding she was dismissed, withdrew—but at the edge of the orange glow of the inn's windows she stopped, because Noor spoke once more, not loudly, not to her, perhaps not entirely to herself:

"It's not a god, whatever it is. Gods don't *hunger* like that. I've heard hunger before. I grew up next to it." A pause, and then, softer, almost lost in the fog: "It's learning our names."

Wren walked back to the Drowned Bell with her heart pounding and her mind already working, sketching routes, measuring distances, doing what she had always done with unbearable truths: putting them on paper, giving them edges, finding the path through.

Thirteen days' ride to the capital, if the passes were open. Fewer, if a certain forger remembered which ones she'd hidden.

She did not yet know why she was calculating. She told herself it was habit. But that night, sleepless above the stable, she took out her one concealed possession—an old field notebook, its pages soft with handling—and began, in the dark, by candle stub, to draw the first honest map she had made in two years.

It was a map of Hollowmere. And at its center, where the shrine-house stood, she wrote a single word in her smallest hand, and underlined it twice:

*Listening.*
