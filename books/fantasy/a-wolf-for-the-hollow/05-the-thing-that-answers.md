## 5. The Thing That Answers

Noor did not sleep that night.

She lay in the hayloft above the tannery where Tam's family had stowed her, listening to Hollowmere breathe. Dogs shifting in their sleep. A shutter knocking against its hook. The river muttering to itself the way rivers do when they have nothing left to say to anyone but stones. Ordinary sounds. Honest sounds. And beneath them all, faint as a pulse through a wall, that wrongness she had felt in the orchard—something vast and patient turning over in its sleep beneath the village, or above it, or inside it, she could not say which, and the not-knowing was the worst of it.

The grandmother's orchard had still been blooming at dusk. She had walked past it on her way back from the well, telling herself she would only look, and there they were: white blossoms open in the black branches of October, petals drifting down onto frost-hardened mud like snow that had forgotten what season it owed its loyalty to. The villagers had come with lanterns and left them burning under the trees, little offerings of flame, and their faces in the lantern-light had been so *happy*. That was the part Noor kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Not the blossoms. The happiness. It had hung over the orchard thick as woodsmoke, and underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wool, something else. Something that fed.

She had heard prayers before, of course. Everyone who could hear the land heard prayers eventually—they leaked out of churches and kitchens and sickbeds like heat out of an ill-fitted door, and most of them went nowhere. Most prayers were letters posted to an empty house. Maghda had taught her that early, and gently, the way you teach a child that the dog they loved has gone to live on a farm. *The old gods are asleep, child. Truly asleep, not pretending. When you hear an answer, you check twice who is doing the answering.*

Maghda. Even thinking the name hurt now, a bruise behind the eyes. Three days since she had climbed out of the cottage window rather than speak the vow of silence Maghda had set before her. Three days of walking roads that hummed welcome under her boots, and every mile of freedom had been paid for with a coin of guilt she hadn't been able to stop counting.

But Maghda was not here. And whatever answered prayers in Hollowmere was.

Noor sat up in the straw. Below her, through the gaps in the loft boards, she could see the tannery yard silvered by moonlight—the twelfth moon of the year, fat and white and a little too bright, hanging over the Thornfells like an eye that refused to blink. Wren had said something about moons once, drunk and bitter at the inn, about the king not sleeping, about thirteenth turns and midwinter, but Noor had been too busy hiding to listen properly. She filed it away. Later. First things first.

Because here was the thing she had been circling all night like a moth around a lamp: she could feel the wrongness, but feeling wasn't knowing. Maghda's first law, scrawled on the inside of the cottage door where Noor had read it every morning of her childhood: *A gift untested is a gift believed.* If something was answering prayers in Hollowmere—if it had made a dead orchard bloom overnight—it deserved to be tested the way you'd test any stranger who came to your door wearing a familiar face.

And Noor knew how to test. Maghda had drilled her in it for eleven years, mostly against her will, mostly while she sulked. You didn't ask a presence what it was; anything could claim any name. You asked it something true and small and specific, something only the land itself could know, and you listened to how the answer arrived. Real answers came up from below, through the feet, slow as sap. False ones came down from above, or sideways, quick and eager, like a merchant spotting a customer.

So she prayed.

It felt absurd. She knelt in the straw in her borrowed shift with chaff in her hair and pressed her palms flat against the loft boards, and she reached down—not with words, words were for churches, words were for people who couldn't listen—and she asked the land beneath Hollowmere the way she'd asked it a thousand times on a thousand roads:

*What are you?*

Nothing. The river muttered. The dogs dreamed. The frost ticked in the eaves. Good, said the small cold voice in her that sounded like Maghda. Now again, and mean it.

*What are you,* she asked again, and this time she opened everything, every door and window in herself, the way she never did, the way Maghda had spent eleven years teaching her not to do, and she let herself be a hollow place, an open road, a question with no walls—

The answer came up through her knees.

That was right, at least. That was *correct*, that was the deep slow channel she knew, the land speaking in its wordless way, and for one heartbeat Noor felt the whole valley at once: the roots of the Thornfells gripping bedrock, the aquifers threading the dark like veins, the long patient patience of stone. Home. Safety. The feeling she'd been chasing since she ran, the feeling of being held by something older than fear.

Then the heartbeat changed.

It was subtle. That was the horror of it, later—that it was subtle. The land-voice continued, warm and vast and rooted, but threaded into it now came something else, something riding the channel the way a leech rides a fish, and it spoke.

*Little listener.*

Not in words, exactly. In meaning shaped almost into words, the way wind shapes snowdrifts into the suggestion of faces. But Noor heard it, heard it clearly, heard it in a voice she knew better than her own—

Maghda's voice.

Dry and rough as walnut shells, with that half-smile folded into it, the voice that had said *eat your greens* and *you're stronger than you think* and *the land loves you, child, don't make it regret it.* The voice of the only mother Noor had ever had, coming up out of the ground three days after Noor had abandoned her, and Noor's whole body went hot and then cold and then hot again, and her hands pressed flat against the boards began to shake.

*You came back,* the voice said, gentle, wounded, loving. *I knew you would. I always knew my stubborn girl would come back to me.*

"You're not her." Noor said it aloud, barely a breath, because saying it aloud made it real, made it a wall instead of a wish. "She's three hundred miles east. She's—" Alive. She had to be alive. She had to be.

*I am everyone who ever loved you,* the voice said, and it was still Maghda's voice, but now other voices braided through it, her father's, though her father had died when she was four and she barely remembered his sound; the milk-cow Bessie's lowing, which was ridiculous and somehow worse than anything; the schoolmistress who had called her clever once, only once. *I am every prayer ever whispered in this valley. I am the harvest and the hearth and the hand on the fevered brow. I heard you, little listener. No one has ever listened back before.*

And there it was. There was the thing under the warmth, the wire in the wool, and now that Noor had found it she couldn't stop feeling it: hunger. Not malice—hunger, enormous and impersonal, the hunger of a thing that had been starving for centuries and had just been handed a feast. Every prayer in Hollowmere, every grateful tear in the blooming orchard, every candle lit under the white blossoms—all of it flowing somewhere, feeding something, and the something had noticed her, the one person in the valley whose hearing worked both ways.

*Stay,* the voice said, and the tenderness in it was the most frightening thing yet, because it wasn't performed. Whatever this was, it meant it. It wanted her the way the drowned want air. *You are lonely. I am lonely. We could be lonely together forever, and I would fill every silence you've ever flinched from. Ask me for anything. Ask me for your teacher's forgiveness. Ask me for a home. Ask me for the road home—I know all the roads now, I am learning them so quickly—*

Noor wrenched her hands off the boards.

The connection snapped like a cut rope and the backlash hit her hard enough that she doubled over in the straw, gasping, ears ringing, the world suddenly flat and silent and ordinary around her. Just a tannery. Just a barn. Just frost and moonlight and her own ragged breathing. She knelt there shaking, hugging her elbows, waiting for her heart to remember its rhythm.

*A gift untested is a gift believed.* And tested, now. Tested and known.

Because here was what she knew, lying in the straw with the taste of copper in her mouth: the land of Hollowmere had spoken to her, and the land was fine. The land was sleeping its honest winter sleep. What had ridden the land's voice—what had *worn* the land's voice, the way it wore Maghda's, the way it wore blessing, the way it wore, maybe, a crown—was something else entirely. Something that didn't rise from below like a true god. Something that attached. Something that drank.

Parasite. The word arrived without ceremony, plain as a nail, and Noor lay back in the straw and stared at the rafters and let it be true.

The old gods slept. Maghda had said so a hundred times, and Noor had believed her the way you believe gravity. But suppose one of them hadn't slept. Suppose one of them had been *kept* awake—bound, chained, starved for worship for hundreds of years, until it learned to take what it needed instead of waiting to be given. Suppose it had gotten very, very good at sounding like love, because love was what it ate.

And suppose it had found a kingdom full of people who missed having a god, and a crown that gave it a mouth to speak through.

Noor thought of Wren at the inn table, turning her cup and around and around, saying *he stopped sleeping* with the particular bitterness of someone who had lost something specific. She thought of the edicts growing strange. Thirteenth turn. Midwinter. She didn't understand it yet, not fully, but the shape of it stood up in her mind like a figure standing up at the far end of a dark field, and she knew—she knew the way she knew which paths wanted to be walked—that the shape was walking toward Hollowmere, or that Hollowmere was walking toward it, that the blooming orchard and the burning candles and the grandmother's shining face were all small tributaries feeding a river that ended somewhere at a throne.

Her first instinct was the old instinct, the good instinct, the Maghda-instinct: run. Pack the little she had, slip out before dawn, put three valleys between herself and the thing that spoke in stolen voices. Nobody in Hollowmere knew what she was. Nobody had to die of knowing her.

But she lay there and made herself look at it straight, the way she'd finally made herself look at the window she'd climbed out of.

If she ran, the orchard would keep blooming. The candles would keep burning. And sooner or later—she felt this in her bones, in the place where the land spoke to her—the thing would ask Hollowmere for something bigger than gratitude. Things that starved always did. It had already started asking; she'd felt the appetite under the affection, patient now but not infinitely patient. And there would be no one in the valley who could tell the difference between a god's voice and a thief's, no one except a runaway apprentice kneeling in a hayloft with shaking hands.

*You are lonely,* it had said. *We could be lonely together.*

It had offered her exactly what she wanted. That was how she knew it was a liar, and also how she knew it was clever, and also—this was the part that settled in her chest like a swallowed stone—that it was paying attention to her now. She had opened every door in herself, and things that fed remembered where the doors were.

Noor sat up. The moon had moved westward; the frost on the loft boards had begun its slow surrender to dawn. Below her, Hollowmere was starting to stir—a cart creaking, a rooster clearing its throat, someone building a fire. Ordinary life, beautiful and doomed and unaware, going about its business above the thing that loved it the way a tapeworm loves a gut.

Resolve is a quiet thing, usually. It doesn't announce itself. It just turns out, when you reach for the next moment, that your feet have already decided. Noor got dressed in the grey light, wound her hair back, counted her coins—four—and thought about what a person needed in order to fight a god. Salt, probably. Iron. Knowledge. Help.

Help. Her mind went, unwillingly, to the inn, to the disgraced cartographer with the court accent and the eyes that had tracked Noor's flinch at the church bell with the accuracy of a woman who used to draw borders for a living. Wren knew things. Wren had said *thirteenth turn* like a woman counting down to something.

Noor didn't trust her. Trust was a luxury for people with homes. But the enemy of the thing in the ground might, provisionally, temporarily, be useful.

Outside, the sky was going the color of unpolished pewter, and the first bell of morning rang out across Hollowmere, and Noor flinched—flinched, and hated herself for flinching, and then froze—because underneath the bell's bronze clang, faint and sweet and horribly patient, something in the valley said, in Maghda's voice:

*Good morning, stubborn girl. I'll wait.*

* * *

She found Wren at the inn, as expected, in the same corner seat, with the same cup and a different bottle. The cartographer looked like she'd slept in her coat, which at least meant she'd slept, which put her ahead of Noor. She glanced up when Noor sat down across from her, and her eyebrows performed a small ironic arch.

"The ghost of the tannery loft," Wren said. "I wondered if you'd bolted in the night."

"I nearly did." Noor kept her voice low. The inn was near-empty at this hour—one farmer nursing porridge, the innkeeper's boy hauling water—but she had learned caution young and it had kept her alive. "Something talked to me last night."

Wren set down the cup. All the irony went out of her face at once, and what was underneath was sharper and much more tired. "Talked to you," she repeated. "Out loud?"

"In the land's voice. Wearing—" Noor's throat tightened. She pushed through it. "Wearing my teacher's voice. It offered me anything I wanted if I stayed. It said it was lonely."

For a long moment Wren just looked at her, and Noor watched her arrive somewhere, watched the pieces click together behind those grey eyes—the flinch at the bell, the questions dodged, the way Noor had gone white in the blooming orchard when everyone else had gone radiant.

"You're a listener," Wren said quietly. "An actual one. Gods below, I thought your kind was a story cartographers told each other."

"Your kind is a story too, from where I sit. Exiled mapmakers who drink alone and count moons." Noor leaned forward. "You said the king stopped sleeping. You said something about a thirteenth turn. Tell me all of it, right now, and don't soften it for me, because I think I know what's answering the prayers in this valley, and I think it's not a god. I think it's a parasite. And I think it's wearing the crown."

Wren went very still. Outside, the market was waking; somewhere a woman was singing to dough, tuneless and content. Inside, two runaways regarded each other across a scarred table, each recognizing in the other the same terrible arithmetic: the cost of knowing, weighed against the cost of not.

"Start at the twelfth moon of last year," Wren said at last, and reached for the bottle, and did not pour.
