## 6. Thirteen Turns

The rain came in off the moor that evening like something with a grudge, and Wren watched it from the window of the Drowned Bell with a cup of wine she had no intention of drinking.

She had been in Hollowmere eleven days now. Long enough to learn which floorboard by the hearth creaked, long enough for the innkeeper's wife to stop watching her hands when she reached for the salt, not yet long enough to be anyone at all. That was the trick of small places: they forgot you quickly if you gave them nothing to hold. She had been very good at giving people nothing. Four years of practice since the capital spat her out.

Across the common room, the girl—Noor, the innkeeper's niece-by-some-tangle-of-kinship, though Wren suspected the word *niece* was doing generous work—sat hunched over a bowl of barley soup, eating with the mechanical diligence of someone who had learned hunger as an enemy rather than a companion. When the church bell rang the hour, she flinched. Not the ordinary flinch of a tired person startled by noise. Her whole body went rigid, shoulders climbing toward her ears, spoon frozen halfway to her mouth, and for three full seconds she listened—not to the bell, Wren was almost certain, but through it.

Wren had spent eight years drawing borders for a crown. She knew what it looked like when someone heard a country speaking.

She turned back to the window before the girl could catch her looking. The rain stitched silver down the glass. Somewhere under all that grey weather, east and south and half a kingdom away, the capital sat on its hill with its gilded spires and its king who did not sleep.

*Thirteen turns,* they were whispering in the taverns along the pilgrim road, in the low careful voices people used for things they feared would overhear them. *The thirteenth turn comes at midwinter.*

Wren had told herself she'd left all of that behind. She had told herself a great many things over four years, and had gotten quite skilled at believing them right up until the moment she didn't.

* * *

The trouble with Hollowmere, Wren thought later that night as she climbed the narrow stair to her rented room, was that it sat on the pilgrim road. Everything came through eventually: tinker carts and funeral processions, tax collectors and deserters, and always, always talk. Talk was the one cargo no border checkpoint had ever managed to weigh or seize.

She heard it first from two wool merchants sharing a jug near the fire, their voices pitched in the particular hush of men who wanted to be overheard by exactly one person each.

"—stopped sleeping, I'm telling you. Last year, the twelfth moon. The palace physicians say he keeps vigils now. Devotion, they call it."

"Kings don't need sleep," the other said, in the tone of a man testing how far a plank will bend.

"This one didn't used to. My cousin's wife's brother is a guard at the Winter Gate—he says the king's eyes have gone wrong. Bright, but wrong. Like a lamp in an empty house." The merchant lowered his voice further, and Wren had to lean to catch it. "And the edicts. Have you seen the edicts?"

"I've seen the taxes."

"It's not the taxes. It's the *wording*. The harvest edict last spring—it read like scripture. Like a psalm set down as law. And the one ordering every shrine in the eastern provinces rebuilt within the year—there was a line in it, my brother swears on his mother, a line about *the faithful body of the realm being gathered into one mouth.* One mouth. What kind of king writes that?"

"The kind with too many priests whispering at him."

"The kind," the first merchant said, "who isn't writing anything at all anymore. They say the scribes take dictation at odd hours. Three in the morning. The king standing at the window, not turning around, just *speaking*, and the words coming out—" He stopped. Drank. Started again from safer ground. "Anyway. Midwinter's coming. Thirteenth turn of the moon since he changed. There are those saying the coronation anniversary should be moved. Moved *off* midwinter. But you can't move a crowning, can you? It's fixed. It's been fixed for six hundred years."

"You're drunk, Ossian."

"I'm frightened," said Ossian, "which is worse."

Wren carried her candle up the stairs and did not sleep either.

* * *

She had known Aldric Vane before he was Aldric the Second of that name, before the anointing oil and the oaths and the slow submersion of a man into an institution. She had known him at nineteen, both of them junior enough in their respective services that they still took their meals in the same hall—the cartography office shared a wall with the privy council chambers, and the young clerks of both spilled out into the same courtyard to eat bread and complain about their masters.

He had been funny. That was the thing nobody remembered about kings, that any of them had ever been funny. Aldric had laughed with his whole chest, head tipped back, entirely unguarded, and he had been merciless about her maps in the way only a friend is permitted to be. *You've drawn the River Senn three feet wide,* he'd said once, tapping her draft. *I've forded it. It barely wets the knee.* And she had said, *Then your legs are longer than your honesty,* and he had laughed so hard a passing chamberlain had come to see what the disturbance was.

She had loved him. She had never said so, because he was third in line for a throne in those days and she was a cartographer's apprentice with ink-stained cuffs, and because some truths feel too heavy to hand to another person unasked. Then his brothers died—one of fever, one of a horse—and the weight of the crown rolled downhill onto him, and he had looked at her across a courtyard the week before his coronation with an expression she had spent four years trying not to remember, an expression that was almost a question, and she had answered it with a bow. A bow. Formal, correct, the bow of a subject to a king.

After that there had been the maps. After that there had been the falsification, the trial held behind closed doors, the word *traitor* spoken quietly so as not to disturb the court, and the long road west with everything she owned in one bag.

She had falsified the maps to protect him. That was the part no tribunal had ever heard, because hearing it would have required her to explain what she'd found—a route, a supply line, a chain of payments leading from certain devout lords toward a place in the mountains where the old kings had once done something old and terrible—and explaining that would have meant naming names she wasn't ready to die for naming. So she had simply altered the charts. Made the pass look impassable. Moved a ford. Bought time, she'd thought. Time to find another way.

Time had run out anyway. Whatever those lords had wanted done to their king, it had been done, and the evidence of it walked and spoke and issued edicts from the throne room every day.

Wren lay in her narrow bed listening to the rain and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing. Twelfth moon of last year, the sleeplessness began. Twelve moons since. The thirteenth full moon would rise at midwinter, and midwinter was—

She counted on her fingers against the dark, an old habit, cartographer's habit, measuring distances in nights instead of leagues.

Nine weeks. Perhaps ten, if the weather argued.

* * *

In the mornings, Hollowmere smelled of woodsmoke and wet sheep, and Wren took to walking the village's single street with a purpose she didn't have, because walking was the only prayer she'd ever been any good at.

It was on one of these walks that she saw the orchard.

She had passed it a dozen times without noting it—a dead little apple orchard behind the weaver's cottage, seven black trees that had stood skeletal for years, the way dead trees do in poor villages where no one has the heart to fell them. This morning she stopped dead in the lane, because the orchard was in bloom.

Not budding. *Blooming*. Full white blossom, heavy on every branch, absurd against the frost-browned hills, petals already beginning to fall in the cold air like snow falling upward in reverse. A handful of villagers stood at the fence in silence, and among them the grandmother Wren had noticed before—Maren, the innkeeper called her, old Maren who prayed at the shrine every dawn—her face wet and shining with a joy so naked it hurt to look at.

"She prayed," the weaver was saying to anyone, to everyone, her voice cracked open with wonder. "My mother prayed at the shrine three nights running, asked for a sign, asked for the orchard her own mother planted—and look. *Look.*"

Wren looked. And felt, beneath the wonder, beneath even her own trained skepticism, something cold slide down her spine—because the blossoms were beautiful, and the joy was real, and none of it explained why the air over the orchard tasted wrong. She couldn't have said how she knew. It was a cartographer's instinct transposed: the sense that a map had been altered, that a river had been moved three feet to the left, that something true had been redrawn by an unfaithful hand.

Old Maren turned then, and caught Wren staring, and smiled at her with such radiant, uncomplicated happiness that Wren smiled back and hated herself for it.

That afternoon, the girl—Noor—was in the inn's kitchen yard, splitting kindling with more force than the task required. Wren leaned in the gate and watched her a moment. Every stroke landed precisely; the girl's body knew exactly where it was in space, the way a surveyor's body does, the way a smuggler's does. Someone had taught her the land the way Maghda of the Thornfells taught her students—by making them *listen* to it.

"You're new here," the girl said, without looking up. Not unfriendly. Just flat, the voice of someone who counted exits.

"I'm nobody," Wren said. "Passing through. Slowly."

"No one passes through Hollowmere slowly unless they're hiding."

Wren laughed despite herself—a short, surprised sound. "And what are you doing, then? Passing through quickly?"

The axe paused. For a moment the girl looked up, and her eyes were the color of deep water, and there was something in them that Wren recognized the way one recognizes one's own handwriting in someone else's letter: the specific wariness of a person carrying knowledge that has made the world unsafe.

"The bell rang this morning," Noor said. "While the orchard was blooming. Did you hear it?"

"Heard it, yes."

"It sounded wrong." The girl said it carefully, testing the weight of each word before setting it down. "Bells sound wrong lately. All of them. Like they're ringing for something that isn't a service." She returned to her kindling, conversation evidently concluded, but as Wren turned to go, she added, quieter: "You flinched too. Yesterday. When the noon bell rang. You pressed your hand flat against the table like you were holding something down."

Wren stood in the gateway a long moment after that, rain beginning again, thinking: *she hears it too.*

* * *

That night the wool merchants were gone, replaced in the Drowned Bell's corner by a pilgrim, a lean grey man with a staff and the dust of many roads on him, and pilgrims talked the way rivers ran—constantly, and finding every low place.

"—the Devoted doubled in number since spring, that's certain. In the capital they wear white now, white and gold, and they've taken the old chapel of Saint Ferren for their barracks. There are processions every dusk. The king himself walks at their head sometimes. Barefoot, they say. Barefoot in winter."

"Barefoot," someone scoffed.

"In the twelfth month of his sleeplessness, why not?" The pilgrim drank. "They say he hasn't aged a day. That's the strangest part. Two years ago he looked his age and tired besides. Now the portraits show a man glowing. The painters are ordered to show it, of course—but my sister works in the laundry of the palace, and she says the king's linen has to be changed three times a day. He sweats, she says. As though he's *burning*. Something burning in him, using him for fuel."

"And the edicts?"

"Stranger. A decree that all maps of the northern passes be surrendered to the crown for 'correction.' A decree renaming the month of Frostmoon. A decree—" the pilgrim hesitated, and lowered his voice, and the whole room leaned in, "—a decree that on the night of the thirteenth full moon, every soul in the kingdom is to stand in their doorway and pray aloud. Every soul. It's been sent to every town and hollow and farmstead. *One voice*, the decree says. *The faithful body of the realm, gathered into one mouth.*"

Silence in the common room. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the church bell began to ring the hour, and Wren watched Noor across the room go rigid at the sound, hand pressing flat against the tabletop, holding something down.

*One mouth,* Wren thought. *One body. One vessel.*

She had seen the binding site's location once, on a chart she was never supposed to see, in the archive beneath the surveyor's office—a mountain fastness in the Thornfells marked with a symbol no living cartographer could interpret, annotated in a dead king's hand: *where the crown was first made to fit.* She had spent four years telling herself she had imagined the significance. Four years of exile bought with a lie, and she had told herself the lie was cowardice, nothing more.

But the arithmetic wouldn't stop. Twelve moons since the king stopped sleeping. One moon remaining. Nine weeks of road between Hollowmere and the capital, in gathering winter, for a disgraced mapmaker with no horse, no name, and no plan—unless the girl with the water-colored eyes and the listening hands was more than she appeared.

Wren finished her wine at last. It had gone to vinegar, and she drank it anyway, like a woman taking communion, or a vow.

Outside, the clouds were tearing apart on the wind, and through the gap the moon showed itself—fat, a night or two from full, hanging over the Thornfells like a coin someone had nailed to the sky to mark a debt.

Twelve coins paid, Wren thought. One still owed.

She went upstairs to count her money, mend her boots, and begin, at last, to plan a road she had sworn never to walk again.
