## 7. Hollowmere Burns Its Saints

The summons came at dawn, nailed to every door in Hollowmere with nails that gleamed like teeth.

Noor found it on the lintel of the miller's loft where she'd been sleeping these past three weeks—sleeping badly, in the dust and warmth of the grain room, because the inn had eyes and the road had worse. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, stamped with a seal she did not recognize: a crown made of roots, or roots made of a crown; from a distance it was impossible to say which.

*BY THE GRACE OF THE CROWNED GOD,* it began, *ALL WHO DWELL IN HOLLOWMERE ARE CALLED TO THE THRESHING FLOOR AT MOONRISE. BRING YOUR SICK. BRING YOUR DOUBTS. THE GOD WILL CHOOSE A VESSEL AMONG YOU, AND THROUGH THAT VESSEL ALL SHALL BE MADE WHOLE.*

She read it twice. The second time her hands were shaking, and not from cold.

*A vessel.* She knew what that word meant. Maghda had taught her what it meant, years ago, in the low firelight of the cottage, when Noor was young enough to still ask questions instead of swallowing them. Some things that wear divinity cannot hold their shape without borrowing flesh. They take a body the way a flood takes a riverbed—not out of malice, but because the shape is there, and the water is heavy, and no one has built anything strong enough to say no.

The old gods, Maghda said, never asked for vessels. That was how you knew them. The true ones slept in cairns and springs and the roots of hills, and if they spoke at all, they spoke like weather: indifferent, vast, honest. It was only the hungry ones who wanted to be inside you.

Noor burned the summons in the miller's stove and spent the day pretending to grind flour while her gift pressed against the inside of her skull like a hand against a door.

Because here was the thing she could not stop hearing: all day, as the village went about its preparations with a strange bright solemnity—as women baked bread for an offering table and men scrubbed the threshing floor with lye-water until the boards bleached pale—the land beneath Hollowmere was *screaming*. Not loudly. Not in any way the villagers would ever feel. But Noor's feet were bare on the earthen floor of the mill, and the earth was saying, over and over, in its slow mineral voice: *wrong, wrong, wrong, wrong*, the way a dog will whine at a door hours before the storm arrives.

Something was coming to Hollowmere tonight. Something wearing holiness the way a crow wears a stolen ring.

And everyone she had come to love in this miserable, kind, forgettable little village was going to be standing on the threshing floor when it arrived.

* * *

She had promised herself she wouldn't get attached.

That promise had lasted approximately four days—the length of time it took Maren Ostrow, the grandmother of the blooming orchard, to catch Noor stealing apples off her fence line and, instead of shouting, hand her a basket and say, "Take the ones on the north side, girl, they're sweeter, and don't think I don't know why you flinch when the bell rings." After that it was over. Maren fed her. Maren asked nothing. Maren told stories about her dead husband while Noor pruned the orchard trees that had come back from death overnight, blossoms and fruit crowding the branches in defiance of every season, and pretended not to notice that Noor never once touched the ground of that orchard barefoot.

"You hear it too," Maren had said once, quietly, shelling peas. "Don't lie to an old woman. I've buried two husbands and a son; I know what listening looks like."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." The peas rattled into the bowl. "Well. When you do know, come tell me. I'll be here. I'm always here. That's the whole trouble with me."

That was three weeks ago. Now Noor stood at the edge of the threshing floor as the moon rose fat and white over the Thornfells, and Maren Ostrow stood in the front row of the gathered village in her good shawl, and Noor understood, with a sick dropping feeling, exactly which vessel the god had chosen.

It wasn't random. That was the worst part. She could feel it now, the presence sliding through the crowd like oil through water, tasting each villager, weighing them—and passing over the young, the strong, the desperate, seeking something else. Seeking *openness*. Maren prayed every night. Maren had prayed her dead orchard back to life—or thought she had—and whatever answered that prayer had left a thread in her, a little hook of devotion, and tonight the thing on the threshing floor was reeling that hook in.

The Devoted had come too. Six of them, in grey robes trimmed with root-crown sigils, escorting a palanquin that should have looked absurd—a gilded litter carried through a mud-street village—but didn't, because the air around it bent slightly, the way air bends over a hot road, and no one laughed. From the palanquin stepped a priest. Or the shape of a priest. His face was pleasant and forgettable in the specific way of faces that have been *made* pleasant, smoothed like river stones, and his voice, when he raised his hands, reached every corner of the threshing floor without effort or echo.

"Children of the hollow places," he said. "Your god has heard your hunger."

The crowd murmured. Old Tam Ostrow—Maren's grandson, twelve years old and gap-toothed—took his grandmother's hand. Noor saw the boy squeeze it, saw Maren smile down at him, and felt something in her chest crack along a fault line she'd been pretending wasn't there since the day she ran from Maghda's cottage.

"The winter comes," the priest continued, gentle as snowfall. "The stores are thin. The coughing sickness is in the Fenlow houses—you know this is true. And your god loves you. Your god has *fed* you. Did the orchard not bloom? Do your wells not run sweet? All the Crowned God asks—" and here the pleasant face turned, slowly, deliberately, until its gaze settled on Maren Ostrow like a bird settling on carrion, "—is one open heart. One willing vessel. Through Grandmother Maren, the god will walk among you. Through her hands, the sick will be healed. Through her mouth, the god will speak, and you will never again have to wonder if you are heard."

"And after?" someone called from the back—it was the miller, Noor realized, brave stupid loyal miller. "After the god walks in her? What happens to *her*?"

The priest smiled.

"She will be glorified," he said. "Forever."

The crowd made a sound that was half sigh, half sob. Noor heard it clearly, and underneath it she heard the earth saying *wrong, wrong, wrong*, and she heard Maren Ostrow's heartbeat—she couldn't help hearing it, the woman was twenty feet away and Noor's gift had been screaming all evening—steady, steady, steady, an old heart that did not know it had been chosen for slaughter.

Maren stepped forward.

"I'm willing," the grandmother said, and her voice didn't shake. "If my god wants my house, my god can have it. He kept my Jonos's trees alive. Seems only fair."

"No," Noor said.

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The word came out of her small and flat and absolutely final, the way stones come out of avalanches, and then the whole threshing floor turned to look at her—the miller's runaway lodger, the quiet foreign girl, the nobody—and there was no path back into invisibility anywhere in the world.

"Child," the priest said kindly, "come forward. Doubt is welcome here. The god eats doubt too."

"It's not doubt." Noor walked forward. Her legs carried her the way they carried her down smuggler passes in the dark: by feel, by stubbornness, by the animal conviction that forward was the only direction that existed. "I've heard gods. Real ones. They sleep under cairns and they speak like rain and they have never, not once, in the whole history of the world, asked to borrow somebody's grandmother."

Murmuring. The Devoted shifting. Tam staring at her with wide eyes.

"And what would a mill-rat know of gods?" the priest asked, and the pleasantness in his voice thinned just enough to show the wire beneath.

"I know that thing you're serving isn't one." Noor stopped six feet from him, close enough to see that his eyes had no reflections in them—close enough to feel, through her bare feet on the frozen ground, the vast cold attention behind his face turn toward her like a searchlight. She made herself stand in it. "I know it answers prayers because answering prayers is how it hooks you. I know the orchard bloomed because it *bought* her. And I know that when it walks into Maren Ostrow, it won't heal anyone. It'll eat her up from the inside and wear her face at market, and you'll all call it a miracle right up until the wells go sour and the coughing sickness gets *worse*, because that's what parasites do. They feed until the host drops, and then they find another host, and they call every one of them blessed."

Silence. The moon stood directly overhead now, and its light lay on the threshing floor like spilled milk.

Then the priest laughed. It was a warm sound, a loving sound, and it was the most frightening thing Noor had heard in her life.

"Oh, *little listener*," he said. "We wondered where you'd gone."

* * *

Later—much later, running—Noor would try to reconstruct what happened next and fail, because the next part happened faster than memory works.

What she remembered was this: the priest's smile widening past the limits of his face. What she remembered was the earth under her feet suddenly *roaring*, every root and stone in Hollowmere shrieking the same warning, and her body moving before her mind caught up, because Maghda hadn't only taught her to listen—she'd taught her what smugglers learn and priests don't: that ground is a weapon if you know where it keeps its hinges.

The threshing floor was built over an old drainage culvert. Every village has one; every village forgets it. Noor had felt it her first week here, a hollow vein running under the east boards where the frost had been eating at the supports all season. She'd noted it the way she noted everything, idly, automatically, the map-hunger that had gotten her branded a liar and a runaway and driven out of the only home she'd ever known.

Now she dropped to one knee, slammed both palms flat against the frozen boards, and *pushed*—not with muscle, with attention, with the full weight of her gift poured into that rotten vein like water into a crack.

The culvert gave.

Not the whole floor—she wasn't that strong, and the god's attention was already bearing down on her like a collapsing mine—but a section of boards six feet across, right at the priest's feet, sagged and split and dropped into darkness with a crash that scattered the Devoted like startled birds. The priest himself didn't fall. Of course he didn't fall. He simply stepped back, unhurried, and looked down into the hole, and then looked up at Noor, and his face—for one unguarded instant—was not pleasant at all. It was old. It was *hungry*. It was the face of something that had been interrupted at dinner.

"*There* you are," it said, in a voice that was no longer the priest's, in a voice that was—gods, gods forgive her—in a voice that was almost Maghda's, warm and dry and terribly familiar. "Do you know how long I have listened for ears like yours, little one? The land sings to you. I know. I sing to the land. We should compare notes."

"Leave her alone!" Tam's voice, cracking, and the boy was between Noor and the priest with a hay fork held like a spear, and Noor's heart broke cleanly in two.

"Tam," she said. "Tam, get your grandmother and get off this floor *now*."

"But you—"

"*Now.*"

And whether it was the command in her voice or the hole in the world at the priest's feet, the boy moved—dragged Maren backward through the crowd, and the crowd itself was breaking apart now, the spell of awe fracturing into ordinary human panic, people shoving and crying out, and in that chaos Noor did the last thing, the unforgivable thing, the thing that ended her life in Hollowmere forever:

She took off her boots, threw them over her shoulder, and ran barefoot across the broken threshing floor, through the splintered gap, down into the dark of the culvert, moving by the land's own voice, faster than any robed servant of a stolen god could follow.

Behind her, above her, she heard the priest's voice rise over the panic, calm and carrying and addressed to every soul in Hollowmere:

"That girl is anathema. That girl is a liar and a witch and an enemy of your god. Bring her to the Devoted, and your orchards will bloom all winter. Shelter her, and—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk, "—we will discuss what happens to villages that shelter wolves."

* * *

The culvert ran west, out under the palisade, into the frozen ditch beyond. Noor followed it on hands and knees in freezing muck, breath tearing, boots clutched to her chest, and came up gasping in the black tangle of the alder swamp where no honest person had reason to be at midnight.

She lay in the reeds and shook.

Not from cold—from the enormity of it. Three weeks. Three weeks of being careful, of being gray and quiet and nobody, of keeping her gift leashed and her head down and her heart locked away behind the same walls that had failed her at Maghda's cottage. And it had taken one old woman stepping forward to die politely for her god, and Noor had shattered all of it in a single sentence.

They knew about her now. The god knew. The Devoted knew. By morning every village in the valley would know: a listener, a witch, a wolf in the hollows, reward for capture.

And Maren—gods, Maren. Had the interruption saved her? Had the god, exposed, slunk back to wherever it wore its other faces? Or would it take its tithe anyway, later, pettier, crueler? Noor pressed her forehead against the frozen mud and tried to feel the answer in the land, but the land was far away tonight, sulking in its winter sleep, and all she could hear was her own pulse and, distantly, the bells of Hollowmere beginning to ring—not the calling-bell, not the prayer-bell, but the alarm, rolling out over the fields, waking every farmstead for miles.

They were coming. Of course they were coming. And she had nowhere to go, because the only person in the world who might have known what to do about a god wearing a crown was a hundred miles behind her, dying of shame and silence, and Noor had burned that bridge with her own hands.

Except—

She lifted her head. In the reeds, thirty feet away, a shape detached itself from the dark and stood up. Tall, lean, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin, a face she half-recognized from the inn—the drunk cartographer, the exile who stared into his cups like a man reading something written on the bottom. Wren. That was the name the barkeep used. Wren, who drank alone, who watched doors, who had flinched—Noor remembered it now, remembered *feeling* it—at the same church bell Noor flinched at.

Wren looked down at her in the mud and said, in a low voice pitched to carry no farther than the reeds:

"If you're done lying in ditches, we need to talk about the map under the cathedral. And you need to talk fast, because those bells mean dogs."

Noor stared up at her, mud-smeared, bootless, ruined, exposed to the whole world—and felt, absurdly, for the first time since she'd fled Maghda's cottage, something that was almost relief.

"Talk fast about what?" she whispered.

"About how to kill a god," said Wren, and held out her hand.
