## 8. Runaways Together

The pass above Hollowmere had a name once, before the maps forgot it. Noor felt it as they climbed—a thread of old intention under the scree, the ghost of a drover's road worn into the mountain's memory. It wanted to be walked. Most paths did not, but this one still remembered feet, remembered the weight of sheep and salt-sacks and men who whistled to keep their courage up in the dark.

She followed that wanting the way other people followed a lantern flame.

Behind her, Wren came on without complaint, which surprised Noor more than complaint would have. A disgraced cartographer, an exile with soft city hands and boots made for cobbled streets, hauling herself up a smuggler's pass at midnight because a girl she'd known for three days had said *run*. The moon was a fingernail paring low over the Thornfells, and the wind came down off the high snowfields smelling of iron and coming winter, and still Wren climbed, breathing hard through her teeth, saying nothing.

Noor stopped where the path bent around a shoulder of granite and let her catch up. Below them Hollowmere lay in its bowl like a coin dropped in dark water—too small to see, too heavy to forget. Somewhere down there, Grandmother Sable's orchard stood in impossible bloom out of season, white petals drifting over frost-hardened ground, and somewhere down there the Devoted would be asking questions by morning. *Who saw the god's work and flinched? Who heard something wrong beneath the blessing?*

"They'll find my room," Wren said, arriving beside her, hands on knees, breath smoking. "I paid for the week. There are papers in the mattress. Maps."

"Then we don't stop until the papers wouldn't matter."

"You say that like it's simple."

"It is simple." Noor turned up the path again. "It's just not easy."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been taxed so heavily from her lately. "You know what I did for twenty years? I drew roads. I sat in warm rooms with good ink and I made mountains sit still on vellum." She scrambled over a fallen slab, cursing softly when it shifted. "Mountains don't sit still. That's the first thing they never teach you in the capital. A map is a lie you agree to believe."

Noor glanced back. In the thin moonlight Wren looked older than she probably was—forty, maybe, or fifty, hard to tell with exiles—and there was ink still stained into the creases of her knuckles, a stain no amount of washing ever fully lifted. Noor knew that stain. Maghda's hands had carried it too.

"The smuggler roads aren't lies," Noor said. "They're true ones. They only exist while someone remembers them."

"Then heaven help us," Wren said, "if you're the last one who does."

* * *

They walked until the sky went grey at its edges, then found shelter where the path wanted them to find it: a hollow beneath an overhang, floored with dry pine needles, screened by a curtain of dead bracken. Noor had felt it half a mile off, a small warmth in the land's voice, like a hand beckoning. Places could be kind. She'd learned that young, before she learned how rarely they were.

Wren sat with her back to stone and produced, from some inner pocket, a twist of paper. Dried plums. She held it out.

Noor stared at it. "You've been carrying those since Hollowmere?"

"I've been carrying them since the capital. They keep." Wren shrugged. "Eat. You've been listening to the ground all night. Listening costs something; I can see it does."

It did. Every step, Noor split her attention between the world she saw and the world she heard—the deep slow speech of stone, the thinner chatter of water under ice, the long groaning patience of roots. And threaded through it all now, fainter than a headache but constant, that wrongness from Hollowmere. A hum beneath the land's honest voices. Something singing to itself in a stolen key.

She took two plums and chewed slowly. "Ask your questions," she said. "You've been holding them since the inn."

Wren leaned her head back against the rock. "All right. First one. When the bell rang in Hollowmere, you flinched like it struck you. Why?"

"Because it wasn't ringing true." Noor turned a plum between her fingers. "Bells have a voice. That one had been... rewritten. Like a word copied out so many times the letters stopped meaning anything. The new god rings through it."

"The new god." Wren's mouth twisted. "You really believe that's what it is. Not a saint. Not a miracle. A god wearing the king."

"I hear it," Noor said simply. "Under everything. It doesn't sound like worship sounds. Worship has an ending—you give, and it's given back, and both sides rest. This doesn't end. It only takes." She hesitated, then said the harder thing. "And when it answered me, it used my teacher's voice. Maghda's. Word for word. Things only she has said."

Wren went very still. Cartographer-still, the stillness of someone measuring distances. "That's how you knew."

"That's how I knew it was a thief. Gods don't need to steal voices. They have their own."

For a long moment neither spoke. The wind worried the bracken curtain. Far below, too far to be real yet, Noor thought she heard dogs—or perhaps only the wind wanting to be dogs, wanting to frighten her. Fear made the land's voice slippery.

"Second question," Wren said at last, quieter. "Why help me? In the inn. You could have walked out and left the drunk exile to her own fate. Instead you told me about the Devoted asking after strangers. You warned me. Why?"

Because you flinched too, Noor thought. Because you hide the way I hide, in plain sight, behind a trade. But she said: "Because you looked at the church bell the same way I did. Like you were waiting for it to lie."

Wren huffed—not quite a laugh. "Twenty years drawing maps for a crown, and the thing that saves my life is a runaway apprentice with suspicious ears." She pulled her coat tighter. "Third question, then. The important one. Where are we going?"

Noor looked out through the bracken at the paleening east. "Away from what follows us. Toward whatever doesn't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." She swallowed. "I ran from Maghda because she wanted to bind my hearing shut forever. Seal it. Make me deaf to the land so we could stay invisible. And I couldn't—" Her jaw tightened. "I won't be deaf. But running meant leaving her alone with whatever comes next, and I don't know yet whether that makes me brave or a coward, and I'm tired of not knowing."

Wren was quiet a while. When she spoke, her voice had lost its wry edge. "I falsified border maps. Did I tell you that part?"

"You told me the crown says so."

"The truth is I changed one road. One route through the Thornfells that the king's party was meant to take. I moved it onto paper where it didn't exist in the world, because I'd heard there were men waiting along the true one." Her eyes fixed on some middle distance. "I thought I was saving him. Aldric. Before he was crowned—when he was just a prince who used to bring me his survey sketches and pretend he cared about contour lines because it gave him an excuse to sit in my workroom." A pause. "My false map sent him exactly where the binding waited anyway. Or the crown did. I've never learned which. Either way, whatever sits on the throne now, wearing his face—I helped carry it there."

Noor heard the guilt in it, raw as a wound kept deliberately unhealed. She recognized it the way she recognized her own reflection in dark water.

"Then we're both runaways from our own hands," she said.

"Yes," Wren said. "We are." She passed the plum twist again. "Eat the rest. Guilt travels lighter on a full stomach. My mother's proverb. She had dozens, all of them about food."

Despite everything, despite the wrongness humming under the world and the dogs that might be wind, Noor felt something in her chest loosen—one degree, no more. Enough to notice.

* * *

They made the high col by afternoon of the second day, and that was where the pursuit announced itself.

Not dogs this time. Bells. Small handbells, silver-voiced, carried by riders on the lower trail—the Devoted rang them as they rode, blessing the ground ahead of their horses, and every ring scraped across Noor's hearing like a nail drawn down slate. She doubled over, hand pressed to her ear, though the ear wasn't the problem. The problem was that the bells lied. They rang *peace, peace, peace* over ground that wanted nothing of the kind.

"Noor?" Wren's hand on her shoulder. "What is it?"

"Bells." She forced herself upright. "Six riders, maybe seven. On the drover's trail below the col. They're ringing the land quiet so it can't tell me where they are—but the horses don't like it. Horses never like it." She listened harder, past the scraping, down to the honest mutter of hooves on shale. "...Five. Five riders. They'll reach the col by dusk unless the weather turns."

"Will it turn?"

Noor closed her eyes and asked the mountain, the way Maghda had taught her to ask—politely, with attention rather than demand. The peak above them answered in its usual register: vast, indifferent, faintly amused. Snow coming. Not tonight. Tomorrow night, heavy.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tomorrow."

"Then we need five riders to be somewhere else by dusk." Wren was already pulling a stub of charcoal and a folded scrap of vellum from her coat—old habits, Noor realized, the exile's version of prayer. She sketched fast, freehand: the col, the trails, the scree slopes. "Show me what you hear. All of it. Where the ground is honest, where it's lying, where it wants to be walked."

Noor knelt beside her and listened, and spoke, and watched Wren draw. It was strange—strange and good—to have her gift treated as data instead of curse. Maghda had always framed the hearing as a debt, a danger, a door better locked. Wren framed it as a map waiting to be made. Under her charcoal the invisible became lines: the stable shelf of stone behind the eastern tor, the throat of loose scree that would sing their passage to anyone below, the narrow goat-track that switchbacked up the north face where no sensible rider would follow.

"There." Wren tapped the goat-track. "We go up and over the north face. They'll expect us to run the valley—it's the obvious line, the one any map shows. So we go where the map doesn't."

"And if the track doesn't hold?"

"Then we fall," Wren said, "and I die knowing my maps finally told the truth about something." She caught Noor's expression and softened. "It holds. Goat-tracks hold. Goats are heavier than they look."

They climbed as the light failed, the col wind rising around them, and below—faint, silver, obscene—the handbells rang their borrowed peace across a mountain that refused to mean it.

* * *

The north face nearly killed them twice, and the second time was Wren's fault, or the mountain's, depending on how you assigned blame to a ledge that crumbled.

Noor heard it go—a whisper of intent in the stone, a fraction of a second's warning—and had Wren's wrist before the ledge finished deciding to become air. For one long moment they hung against the darkening rock, Wren's full weight on Noor's grip, the valley a thousand feet of cold blue nothing below, and Noor discovered she had a very clear thought in her head, which was: *not her. Anyone else, anything else, but the mountain does not get her.*

Then she hauled, and the mountain relented, and they lay gasping on solid stone with their hearts going like festival drums.

"You heard it," Wren managed. "Before it broke. You *heard* it."

"The ledge was tired. Everything tired wants to fall. It's just usually slower about it." Noor sat up, shaking now that shaking was safe. "Don't do that again."

"Do what, almost die? I'll make no promises on a mountain like this."

"*Don't.*" The word came out harder than she intended, stripped down to bare wire. Wren stared at her, and Noor stared back, and the wind filled the silence with snow-smell, and neither of them looked away.

"All right," Wren said quietly. "I'll try to be harder to kill. For your sake." A beat. "Whose sake did you think that shout was for?"

Noor didn't answer. She got up and found the path's wanting again and followed it, and Wren followed her, and neither of them mentioned the moment again, which was its own kind of mentioning.

They crossed the shoulder of the north face in full dark and dropped into a hanging valley on the far side, shut off from the drover's trail by walls of stone, and there—at last—they heard the bells fade. Not gone. Fading. Hunting elsewhere, ringing peace over empty ground.

There was a shepherd's hut, ruined, roofless, one wall standing like a broken tooth. Noor had felt it calling from a mile out: *shelter, shelter, old kindness, cold hearth.* They wedged into its lee and shared the last of Wren's food—a heel of bread gone hard as doctrine, split with a knife smaller than hope—and watched the moon rise over the Thornfells, three nights past full and leaning toward its long winter wane.

"Twelve more," Wren said, watching it.

"What?"

"Turns. Moons. Whatever we're counting." She turned the bread knife over in her fingers. "In Hollowmere you said midwinter. The thirteenth turn. If what you heard is true—if the thing on the throne finishes its binding at midwinter—then we have twelve moons to cross a kingdom with the crown's own faithful ringing bells behind us." She laughed, short and bitter. "I spent twenty years making maps of places I'd never risk my neck to see. Now look at me."

"Is that regret?"

Wren considered it honestly, which Noor was learning was her way—she considered things the way she'd once considered coastlines, patiently, looking for the true line beneath the drawn one.

"No," she said at last. "Regret would be staying in that inn until the Devoted came, drinking myself agreeable, telling myself the crowned thing wasn't what it is. This—" she gestured at the ruin, the wind, the woman beside her, "—this is the first useful thing I've done in years. Even if it kills me." She glanced sideways. "Does that frighten you? That I'm not frightened?"

"No," Noor said. And then, because the night was dark and honest: "It's the first time since I left Maghda's door that I haven't been alone with my own head. I didn't know how loud it was until it got quiet."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. The moonlight did what it liked with her face, silvering the ink-stained knuckles, the wind-chapped cheeks, the eyes that measured everything and had finally, apparently, decided to measure this.

"We should sleep in shifts," Wren said, but she said it the way people say things they intend to break.

"I'll take first." Noor settled against the standing wall. "Wake me when the moon clears the eastern ridge. The land will tell me if anything moves closer than a mile. Nothing gets within a mile without me hearing it want to."

"And what does the land say right now?"

Noor listened. Past the wind, past the fading bells, past the great slow patience of the peaks, down to the small immediate voices—the hut's old kindness, the fireless hearth, the two heartbeats sharing its lee.

"It says we're allowed," she said. "For tonight."

Wren smiled—really smiled, the first one Noor had seen from her that reached all the way up. "Good enough," she said, and closed her eyes, and slept, trusting a runaway and a mountain to keep watch.

Noor kept the watch. And near midnight, when the moon cleared the ridge exactly as promised, she woke Wren with a hand on her shoulder—gentler than she'd planned—and Wren covered that hand with her own for a moment before letting go, and neither of them said anything about that either.

But far to the south, down in the valleys where the villages huddled and prayed, Noor heard something new threading through the land's voice. Not the impostor's hum. Something older, deeper, patient as roots—a sleeping thing, turning over beneath a cairn on a hillside they would reach in two days' time, if the paths kept wanting to be walked.

It was dreaming. And in its dream, it was calling her name.
