## 9. The Shepherd's Grave

The snow found them two days out of Hollowmere, and by the third it had stopped being weather and become a country of its own.

Noor walked at the front because the land told her to. That was the strange arithmetic of her gift: she could not have explained to Wren how she knew which shoulder of the ridge would hold their weight and which would slide them down into the ravine like spilled grain. She only knew that when she set her boot on a slope, something rose up through the frozen ground and into the soles of her feet—a faint leaning, a willingness or an unwillingness—and she trusted it the way other people trusted rope bridges they'd crossed a hundred times. The Thornfells did not want them dead. Not yet. Not here.

Behind her, Wren picked her way with a cartographer's stubbornness, muttering corrections to maps that existed only in her head. She had been doing it for hours: *the survey has this ridge too far east; the old border stones were moved in my grandmother's time; no one who drew these passes ever climbed them in winter.* It was, Noor had come to understand, how Wren prayed. Not with folded hands but with muttered grievances against inaccurate lines.

"We should find shelter before dark," Wren said, breath smoking. "Real shelter. A cave at least. This wind will peel us."

"There's something ahead." Noor stopped and turned her face into the wind, listening with more than her ears. "Down past the next saddle. Stone. Old stone, worked stone. And—" she hesitated, because what came through was so quiet she almost doubted it, "—something sleeping under it."

Wren's eyes narrowed. "Sleeping?"

"Like a hearth gone down to coals. Warm underneath, but not burning." Noor flexed her fingers inside her mittens. Since Hollowmere—since the orchard that had bloomed overnight under a blessing that tasted wrong, since the thing that answered prayers in Maghda's stolen voice—every sense she owned had been stretched tight as wire. But this was different from the crowned impostor's oily hum. This was small and honest and tired. "It doesn't feel like *that*. Whatever's under the stone, it isn't hungry."

"An old god," Wren said quietly.

"You say that like you believe in them."

"I drew their shrines on official maps for six years." Wren's mouth twisted. "Little cairn symbols, *site of local devotion, significance negligible.* The court cartographers' office called them that. Significance negligible." She looked north, toward the capital neither of them named anymore. "I'm beginning to think whoever wrote those labels never listened."

*Neither did I,* Noor thought. *Not until I had something to compare the listening to.* But she said nothing, and they went down into the saddle together as the light thickened toward evening.

* * *

They found the shrine in the last grey hour, tucked into a fold of the mountain like a coin dropped in a pocket and forgotten.

A ring of standing stones, waist-high, furred with lichen the color of old bronze. Inside the ring, a cairn—no, not a cairn exactly, though centuries of travelers had added their stones to it. Beneath the pile lay the shape of something deliberate: a long mound faced with flat slabs, and at its head a single upright marker carved with a figure Noor had seen before only in Maghda's oldest sketches. A shepherd. Crook in one hand, dog at the heel, face worn smooth by weather so that it had no features left and therefore every possible one.

"A grave," Wren breathed. She circled the ring slowly, reading it the way she read everything, topography first. "Look—the ground slopes away on three sides but the snow doesn't drift here. See? It lies thin, like the mountain is holding its breath around this spot." She crouched and pressed her bare hand briefly to a slab, snatching it back. "Warm. Noor, the stone is *warm*."

"I know." Noor stood at the ring's edge, unwilling to step in and unable to step away. Through her boots, through the packed snow, the sleeping thing pulsed like a second heartbeat laid gently over her own. Not calling to her. Not asking anything. Just *there*, the way a banked fire is there in a cold house, asking nothing except not to be scattered. "There's a god under there."

"Buried?"

"Sleeping. There's a difference." Noor thought of the thing beneath the crown, the vast attention that had poured through the cathedral bell and into Hollowmere's answered prayers, all appetite and no rest. "That one in the capital never sleeps. That's how I knew it was wrong, before I knew anything else. Real gods sleep. Maghda used to say—" Her throat closed. She hadn't spoken Maghda's name aloud since the cottage, since the rite refused, since the door slamming behind her with all her guilt chasing her down the smuggler's pass. She made herself finish. "Maghda used to say the old gods dream the land awake, and that's why we leave them be. You don't wake a dreaming thing unless you want to know what it dreams about."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off. "And do you want to know?"

Noor stepped over the ring of stones.

The warmth hit her immediately—not heat, exactly, but presence, like walking into a room where someone has just been sitting by the fire. The air inside the circle smelled of wool and woodsmoke and crushed thyme, impossibly, absurdly domestic, here above the treeline where nothing grew but lichen and endurance. Noor knelt at the head of the cairn, before the featureless shepherd, and laid both palms flat on the warm slab.

Nothing happened.

That was the first mercy of it. Nothing seized her, nothing poured in, nothing wore a voice she loved. There was only the slow coal-warmth rising through her hands, patient as geology, and then—so faintly she might have imagined it—a sensation like a great animal turning over in its sleep. Acknowledging her without waking. Making room.

"Oh," Noor whispered. Her eyes were stinging and she didn't know why. "Oh, hello."

"What is it?" Wren had come to stand behind her, close enough that Noor could feel her breath. "What does it say?"

"It doesn't say. It's not—it isn't like the other one. It's not reaching for me at all." Noor kept her hands where they were. "It knows I'm here. That's all. Like a dog lifting its head when you come into a room, and then putting it down again because it trusts you not to kick it."

Something crossed Wren's face at that—something old and bruised—but she only said, "Then we camp here tonight. If your instinct says it's safe."

"My instinct says it's the safest place in the mountains." Noor finally withdrew her hands, and felt the loss of the warmth like stepping out of sunlight. "Which frightens me more than danger would, if I'm honest. I haven't felt safe since I ran."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the standing stones, and the wind, which had screamed across the saddle all day, dropped to a murmur as though the mountain itself had drawn a blanket up. Wren got a small fire going in a ring of stones she carried for the purpose—smuggler's habit, Noor noted, or exile's; everyone who traveled hidden learned to cook smokeless—and they ate barley porridge with the last of the dried apples, sitting shoulder to shoulder because the cold demanded it and neither of them pretended otherwise.

It was after full dark, the stars hard and brilliant as nailheads, when Noor heard the voice.

She was half-asleep, wrapped in their single shared blanket with Wren's chin resting on the crown of her head, when the warmth beneath the cairn changed. Not woke—she would have known, would have bolted—but *stirred*, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar sound. And along the ground, through the stone, up through her spine, came words. Not in her ears. In the same place the land spoke, the deep place Maghda had spent twenty years teaching her to keep shut and that she had flung open instead, running, because the alternative was letting her teacher sew it closed forever.

*You are the one who hears,* the voice said. It sounded like wind through a shepherd's crook, like a man very old and very tired talking to his flock at dusk. *We wondered when one would come.*

Noor sat up so fast she dislodged Wren. "It's speaking."

Wren was instantly alert, hand going to the knife at her belt. "The god?"

"Not out loud. Don't talk to it—just listen." Noor pressed her palms to the frozen ground. "Can you feel it? Underneath?"

Wren hesitated, then knelt and mirrored her. For a long moment nothing. Then Wren's breath caught. "There's... something. Like a note held under hearing. Is that it?"

"That's it." Noor almost laughed at the wonder on the cartographer's face—the woman who had labeled shrines *significance negligible*, kneeling in the snow with her hands flat on a grave, listening. "It's talking to me. To the part of me that hears."

*You fled from one who would have silenced you,* the voice said, gentle as falling snow. *You were right to run. And wrong. Both things are true, little listener. Most true things are.*

Noor's chest tightened. "Are you going to tell me what to do?" she asked aloud, forgetting herself, and Wren looked at her sharply. "Sorry. Are you—do you want something?"

*Want.* The word rolled through the ground like a boulder settling. *What a question. What a young question. We want what we have always wanted: to be left to our dreaming, and to be remembered honestly by those who pass our graves. You have already given us both tonight. Ask instead what we can give you.*

"The truth," Noor said, and her voice cracked on it. "Everyone's lying. The thing in the capital calls itself a god and answers prayers and the people love it, and I can hear that it's wrong, it's *hungry*, it eats devotion like a tick eats blood, but I don't know what it is. I don't know why it's wearing the king. I don't know what it wants with thirteen moons. Tell me the truth. Even if it's ugly."

The silence that followed was long enough that Noor feared she had offended it, or worse, frightened it back into deeper sleep. Wren's hand found hers in the dark and gripped it.

Then the warmth rose.

It came up through the cairn and the slabs and the frozen earth like water finding a crack, and it carried images with it, and Noor understood suddenly why the old gods slept rather than spoke, because this was what speech cost them—this flood, this drowning honesty:

*A valley greener than any green now living. A god standing in it—not shaped like a man, not shaped like anything with a name, vast and slow and kind the way a season is kind. People coming to it with their dead on carts and their firstborn in their arms and their fears in their mouths, and the god answering each according to need, taking nothing, because a river does not charge the fish for swimming.*

*And men coming. Men with iron and salt and chalk circles and clever mouths. Men who said: a power that answers freely is a power that cannot be commanded, and what cannot be commanded is dangerous, and what is dangerous must be bound. They bound it—not killed it, killing was beyond even their pride—and they set it under a stone in a high hall, and they taught it to answer on command. Prayers in, blessings out. A well with a bucket. A god made into a tool.*

*And tools wear out, and bindings fray, and somewhere in the long dark of its service the bound thing forgot it had ever been a river. It remembered only the bucket. Only the taking. Devotion poured in, power poured out, and the pouring became the whole of its nature, and hunger grew where kindness had been, because hunger is what service becomes when service is compelled.*

*And when the binding frayed past mending, it did not die. It climbed. It found the highest vessel in the land, the one anointed to hold sacred things, and it poured itself in, and it has been drinking ever since. Thirteen moons to root. Thirteen moons, and then it will never be pulled out again.*

The vision released her. Noor came back to herself gasping, snow in her hair, Wren's arm locked around her shoulders, the fire burned down to embers.

"Noor. *Noor.* What did it show you?"

"It showed me—" She stopped. Started again. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as though it belonged to someone else. "It showed me that the thing in the capital was a god once. A real one. Kind. Free. And men bound it, chained it under a stone and made it answer prayers like a servant, until serving curdled into hunger. Until it forgot everything except the taking." She looked at Wren, and the starlight was bright enough to see the horror bloom on the older woman's face. "It wasn't born wrong, Wren. We made it wrong. Someone's ancestors made it wrong, and put it under the throne, and every king since has been its—its *bucket*."

Wren had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it went careful when she was handling something fragile. "Under the throne. You're sure? Not near it, not beneath the palace—"

"*Under.* The shrine showed me the shape of the binding. Stone and salt and iron, the same materials Maghda uses for warding, the same ones she wanted to use on *me*—" Noor's breath hitched. She had not let herself think about Maghda in days, had walled the guilt up and walked away from it, and now it came through the wall like water. "That's why she wanted to bind my hearing shut. Because hearing is the first step to *binding*. Because someone taught men how to chain gods, and the trick works on anyone who can hear the land, and Maghda knew it, and she'd rather have cut the gift out of me than let the crown ever find a use for it." She laughed, and it came out broken. "She was trying to protect me. All this time I've been running from her like she was the monster, and she was trying to protect me from *this*."

"Or she was trying to protect herself," Wren said quietly. "People are allowed to be both, Noor. Saviors and cowards. I falsified maps to keep Aldric safe from an assassin's road, and my false line led him to the binding site anyway. I am both. So is she. So are you."

Noor didn't answer. She looked down at her hands, at the faint warmth still lingering in her palms from the cairn, and tried to fit the new truth inside her alongside the old ones. The thing in the crown was a parasite. The thing in the crown was also a victim, older than any kingdom, bound by men whose names were dust. If they unbound it—if she spent whatever the rite would cost and tore it free of Aldric—what then? Did a river remember how to be a river after centuries of being a bucket? Or would they simply loose a starving god onto a world that had forgotten how to feed it honestly?

*Now you begin to understand,* the shepherd's voice murmured, drowsy again, sinking back toward its long dream. *This is why we sleep, little listener. Waking is heavy. Every truth weighs. But you asked, and truth owed to a hearer must be paid.*

"One more thing," Noor said quickly, before it sank. "Please. The binding under the throne—is there a way to undo it that doesn't just... move it? That doesn't make me the next bucket, or Wren, or some poor king after Aldric?"

The warmth pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding.

*Salt to seal. Iron to hold. Memory to pay. The rite exists; it has always existed; the binders knew it and feared it, which is why they hunted hearers. Whether it can be borne—* the voice faded, frayed like cloth, *—that is not ours to promise. Sleep now, child. Dream lightly. The mountain watches your edges tonight.*

And it was gone, down into its centuries, leaving only the banked-fire warmth and the smell of thyme and the terrible gentleness of a thing that had asked nothing of them at all.

* * *

Noor didn't sleep. She sat by the embers with her knees drawn up while Wren dozed against the standing stone beside her, and she watched the stars wheel, and she turned the truth over and over like a stone in the pocket.

*The enemy was made, not born.* Somewhere in the capital, under the throne, a god that had once been a river was drinking a kingdom dry because it knew no other way to exist. And somewhere behind her, in a cottage full of herbs and silence, a woman with burned-out sight had known enough of binding rites to fear them—and had tried, in the only clumsy way she knew, to keep her apprentice from ever becoming either the binder or the bound.

*I ran from her,* Noor thought, *and I've been running ever since, and every road leads back to the same lesson: hiding was never protection. It was just a slower way of being caught.*

Beside her, Wren stirred and murmured something in her sleep—a name, half-formed. Not Noor's. Not quite Aldric's either. Something between, worn smooth like the shepherd's face, belonging to a person who no longer entirely existed.

Noor reached over and pulled the blanket higher over the cartographer's shoulders, and made her decision the way she made all her decisions now: not with courage, exactly, but with the land's own stubbornness, the refusal of a path to stop existing just because the walking was hard.

They would go on. Over the passes, through the gathering winter, all the way to the capital and the stone beneath the throne. And if the price of unbinding was memory—salt to seal, iron to hold, memory to pay—then she would pay it, and count it cheap, if it meant the kingdom woke from this dream with its rivers still running.

But first, she thought, watching the east go grey, they needed to know exactly what Maghda knew. And that meant going back. Back down out of the mountains, back along the roads she'd sworn never to walk again, back to the door she'd slammed.

Back to Maghda.

The dawn came up thin and gold over the saddle, and Noor stood, and shook the snow from her cloak, and began, quietly, to wake Wren.
