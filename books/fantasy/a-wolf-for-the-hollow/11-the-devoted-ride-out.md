## 11. The Devoted Ride Out

Tam Voss had seen the crowned god with his own eyes, and that was the whole of it, the beginning and end of every argument he would ever have about faith.

He had been eleven years old when the fever came through Hollowmere, the winter before last, and he remembered it not as a season but as a color — a gray so complete it seemed to soak into the walls of his family's house, into the bread, into his mother's voice when she sang to him at night because singing was all she had left to give. He remembered the taste of willow bark. He remembered his little sister Sela's cot standing empty in the corner because she had gone first, in the autumn, and no one had explained to him why God had taken a girl who still believed the moon followed her home.

And then he remembered the rider.

The man had come up the mill road on a white horse at dusk, wearing gray and silver, and the sigil on his breast was a crown made of wheat stalks bent into a circle — the mark of the Devoted, though Tam hadn't known it yet. The man had dismounted in the yard without asking permission, the way important people do, and Tam's father had come out with a scythe in his hands until he saw the wheat-crown and dropped it in the dirt.

"Please," his father had said. Not *welcome*. Please. Like a beggar.

The Devoted had knelt in the doorway of the sickroom, which Tam thought was strange, kings' men kneeling, and he had put one hand on Tam's burning forehead and said words Tam could not follow, old words, round words, and the fever had broken like a storm passing over. Not slowly. All at once, the way a knot comes loose. One moment Tam was drowning; the next he was lying in cool sheets, watching dust turn gold in the lamplight, listening to his mother weep in the next room — not the gray weeping of that whole terrible month but the loud kind, the alive kind.

The Devoted had stayed for supper. He'd eaten plain barley soup and asked Tam's name and told him, quite seriously, that the King loved him. Not the kingdom. Him. Tam Voss, miller's son, with his chapped hands and his cowlick and the hole in his boot.

"He sees you," the man said. "That is what the crown is for. It is a lamp, and you are what it shines on."

Tam had believed him then, entirely, the way you believe fire is hot or water is wet. And three years later, kneeling on cold flagstones in the chapter house at Greyford with his forehead pressed to the stone, he believed it still.

* * *

The chapter house smelled of tallow and clean wool and, underneath everything, the faint green sweetness of wheat kept in the reliquary — living grain, blessed by the King's own hand, that never rotted. Tam knew all the smells now. He had been a novice for eight months, and in eight months he had learned that holiness was mostly made of ordinary things done exactly right: floors scrubbed, bells rung true, bread shared before it was wanted rather than after.

Brother Callen found him at the morning office, kneeling in the back row where the light from the east windows fell in long dusty bars. Callen was old, with a face like a root vegetable left too long in the sun, and he was the only brother who ever spoke to Tam as if Tam were a person rather than an instrument being tuned.

"You prayed loudly this morning," Callen said, settling beside him with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions.

"I didn't say anything."

"That's how I know." The old man smiled. "Come. The Prior wants the young ones assembled. There's news from the capital."

News from the capital meant one of two things: an edict, or a miracle. Lately they had been the same thing. Tam followed the other novices into the refectory, where Prior Edmun stood beneath the great painted crown on the wall — gold wheat on a field of blue, the King's mercy rendered in cheap pigment by some traveling artist — and read aloud from a letter bearing the royal seal.

Tam listened the way he always listened, with his whole body, hungry. The letter said that in the river towns the King's blessing had doubled the harvest. That in the mining valleys, men who had breathed stone-dust for thirty years had come down from the hills breathing easy, their lungs washed clean. That the granaries of the crown were open, truly open, and grain was moving to the famine counties ahead of the snows. That the King himself had not slept in — the letter paused here, and Prior Edmun's voice went soft and reverent — *in fourteen months*, and did not need to, because the god that wore him needed no sleep, only devotion, and devotion the kingdom gave freely now, more every day.

Fourteen months. Tam did the arithmetic without meaning to. Fourteen months ago was the twelfth moon of last year. He had heard the older brothers speak of it in low voices: something had changed in the King then, something had *opened*. Before, the miracles had been rare, a healing here, a sign there, the ordinary grudging grace that scripture promised and life rarely delivered. After, it was as if a door had been unbarred. As if someone had finally answered.

"The thirteenth moon approaches," Prior Edmun said, and the refectory went very quiet, the way a room goes quiet around a sleeping child. "At midwinter, the fullness comes. The King has called for the Ride Out — every chapter to send its faithful into the hollow villages, the back places, the forgotten valleys, so that no soul crosses into the new year unblessed. Greyford sends twelve. Brothers, and novices who have taken their first vows."

Tam's heart slammed against his ribs. He had taken his first vows in autumn. He was fifteen, and he had never ridden further from Greyford than the market at Two Fords, and he had never once been asked for anything except obedience and clean boots.

Prior Edmun's eyes found him across the room, and Tam understood, in the way you understand a thing before you can bear it, that his name was going to be spoken.

"Novice Tam Voss," the Prior said. "You are of Hollowmere."

Not a question. A summons.

"Yes, Prior."

"Then you ride home."

* * *

They rode out on the first hard frost, twelve gray-and-silver figures on good horses, and Tam at the tail of the line on a borrowed mare named Patience who had opinions about mud.

He had expected to feel triumphant. This was what he had wanted since the fever broke, since the Devoted knelt in his doorway: to be the one who rode up the mill road at dusk. To be the lamp. For eight months he had polished that want like a stone in his pocket, taking it out at night in the dormitory, turning it over, warming his hands on it.

Instead, as Greyford shrank behind them and the road climbed into country he knew — the split oak, the leaning wall, the ford where he'd caught his first trout — he felt something he couldn't name. Not dread exactly. More like the feeling of walking into your own house after a long absence and finding the furniture moved an inch to the left. Everything familiar and nothing sitting quite where memory said.

Brother Anselm rode beside him. Anselm was twenty-six, broad, cheerful, with a laugh that scared crows off three fields, and he was the best of them, Tam thought — the one who actually listened when farmers talked, who learned names, who carried sweets for children in his saddlebag.

"Hollowmere," Anselm said. "Good hunting ground, eh? Poor soil, poor folk, plenty of need. The King loves the poor ones best. Easier to see them shine."

"Aye," Tam said.

"You've family there?"

"Mother. Father. The mill." He paused. "A sister buried under the rowans."

Anselm was quiet a moment. Then: "She'll be first in line for blessing, then. The dead of the faithful sleep close to the surface this time of year. You'll see. At midwinter, when the thirteenth moon fills—" He made a gesture, opening his hand like a man releasing a bird. "Doors everywhere. That's what the brothers say who've served in the capital. Doors everywhere, and the King standing at each one."

Tam nodded and looked at the road.

Because here was the thing he had not told anyone, the thing he carried now like a second heartbeat: he had seen a door already. In autumn, in the orchard at Hollowmere, the night the dead trees bloomed.

He had been there. He had been sneaking back late from the tavern with a stolen apple in his coat, thirteen years old and full of his own cleverness, and he had heard Grandmother Ilsa praying among the black branches — heard her talking to the trees like they were people, like they were her children, begging them not to be dead, begging whoever was listening. And Tam had laughed at her, quietly, meanly, the way boys laugh, and turned to go home.

And the orchard had bloomed.

Not gradually. All at once, a sound like a held breath let go, and every bare tree had burst into white blossom under a sky with no business permitting it, petals falling warm as skin in the frost air, and Grandmother Ilsa on her knees in the drifts of flowers laughing and weeping at once, and Tam standing frozen with the stolen apple in his hand feeling — feeling *seen*. Feeling that something enormous had turned its attention toward him the way you'd turn toward a sound in the dark.

It had been the most beautiful thing he had ever witnessed. He had dreamed about it for weeks afterward, the blossoms, the warmth, the sense of a vast and loving eye.

But there had been a girl there too. Running out of the orchard like the blossoms were chasing her. Noor Thornbury, Maghda's strange apprentice, the one who kept to the edges of things, who flinched at church bells. She had looked at the flowering trees the way Tam looked at the apple in his hand — guilty. Caught. And she had looked at Grandmother Ilsa's joy with an expression Tam had spent months trying to forget, because it did not fit anywhere in the world the Devoted had built for him.

Pity. She had looked at a miracle with pity.

He had told Brother Anselm about the orchard, eventually. About the blooming. He had left out Noor. And Anselm had gripped his shoulder and said, *you witnessed a manifestation, boy, do you know how rare —* and Tam had been praised, and entered into the record, and it had helped win him his early vows.

So why, riding home now in the gray and silver, did the memory keep snagging on that one wrong thread? Why did he keep seeing Noor's face instead of the blossoms?

*Patience* stumbled on a rut and Tam gathered the reins, grateful for the small task.

* * *

Hollowmere received them the way villages receive rain after drought — with a desperate, almost embarrassing gratitude. By the time they rode into the square, word had outrun them, and half the village stood in the cold with hats in their hands. Tam's mother pushed through the line of brothers before anyone could stop her, and hugged him in front of everyone, in his vestments, weeping, and Tam endured it with burning ears and a happiness so sharp it frightened him.

His father shook Brother Anselm's hand for a long time, saying nothing, just shaking it, and Anselm let him.

They set up in the meetinghouse, because the chapel was too small, and the blessings began. Tam had imagined this part a hundred times and it was both exactly like and nothing like his imagining. It was work. Anselm laid hands on a cooper's bad hip and the hip loosened while the crowd gasped; another brother blessed the well and the water came up sweet where it had tasted of iron for years; a baby with a twisted foot was brought forward screaming and left the room walking, wobbling, its mother making a sound Tam had no word for. The grain from the reliquary was mixed into the village seed-stock, a pinch per sack, and the farmers handled those sacks afterward like they were holding sleeping infants.

It worked. That was the unbearable truth of it, the truth Tam had built his whole life on: *it worked.* The crowned god healed. The crowned god fed. Whatever else might be whispered in taverns by people who had never watched a lame child walk, the mercy was real, Tam had felt it pass through his own body once upon a fevered night, and no argument in any book could stand against the weight of a mother's face when her baby took its first steps.

On the third evening, Grandmother Ilsa came to the meetinghouse.

She came slowly, leaning on a stick, and she waited at the back of the line with her head bowed, and when her turn came she knelt before Brother Anselm and asked — Tam, assisting at the rail, heard every word — to be blessed *for remembering rightly.*

"For remembering?" Anselm asked gently.

"I'm an old woman and my mind wanders," Ilsa said. "I want to hold onto what's true. Bless me to tell the true thing from the false thing. That's all an old woman needs."

"That is a fine prayer," Anselm said, smiling, and laid his hands on her white hair, and spoke the round old words, and Ilsa bowed her head and received them.

But when she rose, she looked past Anselm's shoulder directly at Tam — at his gray and silver, his wheat-crown — and her eyes were clear as January, and she said, quietly, so only he could hear:

"They sent a Hollowmere boy to bless us. That's clever of them." She patted his arm, kindly, terribly. "You were in my orchard, child. The night it bloomed. Did you feel it too? Underneath the sweetness. Like a hand closing."

"I don't know what you mean, grandmother."

"No," she agreed. "I expect you don't yet." She shuffled toward the door, then stopped. "There was a girl running from my orchard that night. Maghda's girl. If your brothers go looking for the wicked in this village, they'll find her, or they'll find nothing, but either way they'll tear up half the ground doing it. Old woman's advice: some things bloom because they're holy, and some things bloom because something hungry wants you to think so. My trees were dead forty years, boy. Dead things don't remember how. Something remembered *for* them."

Then she was gone into the dark, and Tam stood at the rail with the incense smoke curling around him, holding his censer like a shield.

* * *

That night he could not sleep. He lay in his childhood bed under the eaves, in the room where Sela's empty cot had stood, and listened to the house settle, and tried to pray, and the prayers kept sliding sideways into questions.

Grandmother Ilsa was old. Old women said strange things; the brothers said so themselves, gently, often. But she had not sounded confused. She had sounded like someone reading a letter she'd been waiting years for.

And underneath that — underneath everything — was Noor.

He turned onto his side. He had not thought of Noor Thornbury in months, not properly, not as a person rather than a footnote in his testimony. She had been two years above him at the village school before Maghda took her fully, a quiet girl who sat at the edge of the yard eating her lunch alone and never seemed lonely about it. Once, when Tam was nine, he had fallen through the ice on the mill pond, and it was Noor who pulled him out — small as she was, wiry, furious, hauling him by the collar and screaming at him the whole time, *breathe, breathe, you idiot, breathe* — and then she had walked away before he could thank her, before anyone even came, leaving him coughing on the bank alone. He had told everyone a grown-up saved him. It was easier than explaining.

She had saved his life and never once mentioned it. And he had repaid her by telling the Devoted about the orchard and keeping her name out of it — which he had thought, at the time, was mercy. Wasn't it? He had protected her. Hadn't he?

Or had he simply protected himself, and left her standing in a field with a secret, in a kingdom that was filling up, month by month, moon by moon, with gray-and-silver riders who had been taught that secrets were the same as sins?

Through the frost-furred window, the moon hung over the Thornfells, fat and bright and not yet full. Counting down. Twelve turns behind it, one to come, and the whole kingdom leaning toward midwinter like a congregation toward the rail.

Below, in the meetinghouse, he could hear Brother Anselm moving among the sleeping villagers they'd healed, checking on them, humming. Good man. Genuinely good. They were all good, most of them; that was the trap of it, Tam thought, staring at the ceiling. You could not hate a thing made of kindness. You could only start noticing, very carefully, the way kindness could be aimed.

In the morning, Prior Edmun's messenger would arrive with instructions from the capital, and Tam already knew — the way you know weather in your knees — what they would say. That the thirteenth moon approached. That the King desired the hollow places swept clean of old superstitions before the fullness came. That there was a name of interest. A runaway apprentice of Hollowmere, daughter-adjacent to the hedge-witch Maghda, unaccounted for since autumn, possibly a witness to manifestations, possibly something worse.

Possibly, the letter would say, an impediment.

Tam lay in the dark and listened to Anselm's humming rise up through the floorboards, sweet and steady, and pressed his palms against his eyes, and understood that soon — maybe tomorrow, maybe the day after — someone was going to ask him, Novice Tam Voss of Hollowmere, son of the mill, boy pulled from the ice: *do you know where she is?*

And he was going to have to decide what a lamp was for.
