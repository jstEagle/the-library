## 13. Maghda's Door

The cottage was smaller than Noor remembered.

That was the first lie the road told her, or perhaps the first truth: distance made things grand. She had carried Maghda's door in her memory for a season now—the crooked lintel, the blue paint gone chalky with weather, the iron horseshoe nailed above it that had never once been lucky—and in her remembering it had grown into something vast and terrible, a fortress of everything she had fled. Standing before it at dusk, breath steaming in the first honest cold of autumn, she saw it was only a door. Low enough that Maghda had to duck going through. Painted by someone who had loved the color blue once and then stopped caring whether it faded.

Noor's hand rose to knock and stopped, hovering, like a bird that had forgotten how to land.

She had crossed half the Thornfells to get here. She had walked the smuggler passes by night and slept in shepherd huts by day, had eaten winter apples gone soft as sorrow, had felt her way through fog so thick the land itself seemed to have lost its voice—*and her gift had been silent for three days before that*, which should have frightened her more than it did. The paths had stopped whispering their wants somewhere past the second ridge. She told herself it was the weather. She told herself a great many things on that walk, because the alternative was to admit what Wren had said at the ford, wringing river water from her coat, not unkindly:

"You're going home, aren't you? You've been walking home since Hollowmere."

So here she was. Home. A word that had always meant this door and nothing else.

She knocked.

No answer. But the land under her feet—that deep, slow voice she had heard since childhood, older than any god the priests named—shifted beneath the cottage the way a sleeper shifts beneath blankets. Something was in there. Something breathing, but *wrong*, the rhythm ragged, like a song played on a cracked flute.

"Maghda." Not a question. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Maghda, it's Noor."

Silence. Then, from inside, a sound she had never heard her mentor make—a dry, papery cough, followed by something that might have been laughter if laughter could rust.

"The latch is broken," Maghda called, and her voice was a stranger's voice, thinned and scraped. "Has been for years. Push."

*Years.* Noor pushed, and the door swung inward on hinges that shrieked like something dying, and the smell hit her first—dried herbs and tallow smoke and underneath both the sweetish, wrong smell of a body failing. The single room was dim. Every shutter closed. The hearth held ash and nothing else, though the cold had teeth now, though Maghda had never in her life let a fire die while she drew breath.

Maghda sat in her chair by the dead hearth, wrapped in two blankets, and Noor's heart did something complicated and painful, because the woman in the chair was small. Maghda had always been *large*—large-handed, large-voiced, large in her judgments and her silences alike, a woman who filled a room the way a stone fills a stream. This woman had folded inward like a letter no one would carry. Her hair, iron-gray for as long as Noor had known her, had gone white as salt. And her eyes—

Her eyes were open, turned toward the sound of the door, and they were clouded. Not blind-blind, not white as an egg. Clouded the way ice on a pond clouds when something moves beneath it. They found Noor anyway. They had always been able to find Noor.

"Well," Maghda said. "The runaway comes home. I'd say sit down, but you were never any good at being told."

* * *

Noor built the fire. It was the only thing she could think to do with her hands, and Maghda watched her do it—or faced her doing it—with an expression Noor couldn't read, which was its own kind of horror. She had always been able to read Maghda. That had been the whole architecture of their life together: Maghda's moods were weather, and Noor had learned to read the sky.

"I heard you coming up the pass," Maghda said while Noor coaxed flame into the kindling. "Three days ago. Or I heard the mountain talking about you, which amounts to the same thing. It said you'd stopped listening."

Noor's hands went still over the fire. "How did you know that?"

"Because I'm the one who taught you to listen, girl, and I know what it sounds like when someone stops." A pause. The fire crackled into the silence, filling the room with light that made Maghda look worse, not better—the skin stretched over her cheekbones like wet paper, the hollows at her throat where muscle used to live. "And because I know what it costs to keep a listener deaf. I've been paying that price since before you could walk."

"What do you mean, paying?"

Maghda laughed again, that rusted sound. "You came all this way to ask me for help with your impostor god, and instead you're going to stand there and make me say it plainly. Fine. Fine." She pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders, and Noor saw that her hands shook—not old-woman shaking, but a deeper tremor, like something being slowly unwound. "Sit down, Noor. What I have to tell you takes longer than standing."

Noor sat on the floor, cross-legged, the way she had sat as a child receiving lessons. Some part of her hated how easily her body remembered.

"Do you know why I took you in?" Maghda asked.

"You said my mother owed you a debt."

"My mother owed *her* mother a debt, and I inherited it along with the cottage and the herb beds and the bad knees. That's the story I told you because it required nothing of either of us." Maghda's clouded eyes fixed on some middle distance where the past presumably still lived. "The truth is I heard you before I ever saw you. You were four years old, living down in Millbrook with your mother, and one night the whole valley heard the mountain groan—landslide up in the high fells, killed six people, you remember the winter. Everyone heard the groan. But you, four years old, stood up in your mother's kitchen and told her exactly where the slide had come down, and whose house it had taken, and that the miller's daughter was buried under the east wall and still alive, and they dug her out because a child said so." Her voice roughened. "Four years old. Hearing true. Do you understand what that is? There are maybe three people born in a century who can hear the land's own voice and not go mad of it. Your mother came to me weeping, begging me to take you before anyone else understood what you were. Because she knew what happens to listeners. The kings' priests used to harvest them. Bind them to stones. Use them to find things that wanted to stay hidden."

Noor's mouth had gone dry. "You never told me any of this."

"No. I told you your gift was a curse and taught you to smother it, and I let you believe I did it out of caution, out of love, out of whatever mixture keeps a teacher respectable." Maghda lifted one trembling hand and pressed it flat against her own sternum, as if holding something in. "I did it because every year I shielded you, the shielding burned a little more of me. Warding a listener isn't like warding a house, girl. The land talks *through* them. To keep the voice from reaching you, I had to stand between you and it, every hour of every day, and take what came. For twenty years I have been a wall between a mountain and a girl, and mountains are patient, and walls wear out."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea, and Noor flinched from it, and hated herself for flinching.

"That rite," Noor said quietly. "The one you asked me to perform. The binding-shut. Before I ran."

"Now you're asking the right questions."

"You wanted me to bind my own hearing shut forever. I thought—I thought it was fear. Yours. I thought you'd finally gotten too frightened of what I might hear, of what might come looking for someone who could hear it." Noor's voice cracked on the last words; she hadn't known the anger was still in her, whole and fresh as the day she'd walked out. "I thought you were a coward."

"I am a coward," Maghda said simply. "But that's not why I asked it." She leaned forward, and the effort of it was visible, terrible, like watching someone lift a weight far beyond their strength. "A listener can be warded from outside, at cost to the warder. But there's another way. If the listener binds herself—closes her own ear by her own will, freely given—the ward holds forever and costs the binder nothing but the hearing. I asked you to do it because I was dying, Noor. I had perhaps two winters left even then, and when I died, the shield died with me, and every hungry thing in these mountains—all the half-dead gods, the bound things, the *parasites* wearing crowns and calling themselves holy—every one of them would feel a naked listener like a torch in a dark field." Her hand dropped. "I asked you to blind yourself to save your life. And you chose to run instead, which saved my pride and very nearly killed us both."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire worked through the kindling and began to eat properly at the split logs, and the room warmed by degrees, and Noor sat with Maghda's words the way you sit with a wound—carefully, testing how deep it goes.

"You're dying now," Noor said at last. It wasn't a question either.

"I'm dying now. Faster than I meant to. When you ran, I had to hold the shield alone across the whole gap you left—you were frightened and moving fast and your hearing was wide open, screaming your location to anything with sense enough to listen. I spent a winter's worth of myself in a fortnight pulling it tight around you anyway, even at a distance. Even angry." Maghda smiled, and it was the old smile, dry as August. "Don't look like that. Anger and love come out of the same pot in this family. Always have."

"I met someone," Noor said, because she didn't know how else to begin carrying her half of it. "Wren. She was the royal cartographer. She's disgraced now, exiled. She says the king stopped sleeping a year ago, and there's a thing wearing him, and it answers prayers, and it's feeding, and at midwinter—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a rite. An unbinding. Wren thinks I'm the only one alive who can do it, because I can tell the false voice from the true one. But I don't know the rite. I ran before you taught me anything that mattered, and now I've come back because I need—"

She stopped again, because Maghda had raised one hand, and even diminished, even dying, that hand still commanded a room.

"An unbinding rite," Maghda said softly. "Against a crown-bound parasite. At midwinter, on the thirteenth turn." The clouded eyes seemed, for a moment, almost clear. "Do you know what such a rite costs?"

"Wren said blood. Salt. Iron. Things I can gather."

"Wren knows maps, not rites." Maghda's voice gentled into something Noor had almost never heard from her: pity. "Blood and salt and iron are the frame, girl. The price is paid in the caster. To unbind a thing that has been woven into a soul—in this case woven into a king for a year and tightening—you must offer the rite something of equal weave. Memory. The rite will take from you what it needs to cut him free, and it will not ask permission about *which* memories, and it will not give them back." She paused. "It may take your roads, runner. All of them. Every path you ever memorized, every pass, every smuggler's way carved into that remarkable head of yours. It may take more than roads. That is the price, and I am telling you now, at the beginning, so that when you agree—and you will agree, because you have your mother's stubborn heart and my worst habits—you agree with your eyes open."

Noor looked at her hands. They were steady. That surprised her.

"I already gave up staying invisible," she said. "One more thing doesn't frighten me the way it would have."

"Liar," Maghda said fondly. "It frightens you terribly. Good. Fear keeps the rite honest."

* * *

They talked until the moon rose. Small things, mostly—Noor telling her about Hollowmere, about the orchard blooming overnight, about the grandmother whose name she learned later was Sela and whose bread she could still taste. About Tam, the boy with the chapped hands, chosen as a vessel, and the trick with the well-rope that had bought him his life and cost Noor her hiding. Maghda listened the way she had always listened, without interruption, her clouded eyes turned toward Noor's voice as though the voice itself were a fire worth facing.

Once, near midnight, Noor said: "Why didn't you tell me? Any of it. The harvesting. What the shielding cost. I'd have stayed, if I'd known. I'd have—"

"No, you wouldn't have. You'd have stayed out of guilt, and guilt makes a rotten foundation for anything." Maghda shifted in her chair with a sound like dry leaves. "I kept it from you because I wanted you to have one life that wasn't shaped by other people's sacrifices. Your mother's sacrifice sent you to me. Mine shaped every day after. I thought—if she never knows the cost, she can pretend her life is her own." A long breath. "It was a selfish kindness. We are full of those, teachers. But I'll not apologize for wanting you free, even badly."

"It nearly got Tam killed. It nearly got Wren killed. My freedom wasn't free either—it just billed other people."

"Yes." Maghda said it without flinching. "That's the truth of running away, and most people never learn it. Now ask me the thing you actually walked all this way to ask."

Noor's throat tightened. She looked into the fire, because looking at Maghda was suddenly impossible.

"I left you alone," she said. "Dying, and alone, and I told myself I'd never come back, and I meant it, and then I came back anyway because I needed something. And I want—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "I want you to know that I'm sorry. And I want you to tell me whether it's forgiven, and I know that's a selfish thing to ask a dying woman, and I'm asking it anyway, because I can't carry it into whatever comes at midwinter. I can't do the rite with this sitting on me."

The wind pressed against the shutters. In the hearth, a log settled and burst apart in a spray of sparks, and in that brief flare of light Noor saw that Maghda was crying—silently, without drama, tears tracking down into the seams of her face as though they had been doing it for some time and she had simply stopped bothering to hide it.

"Oh, girl," Maghda said. "You came home to ask a blind woman for absolution, and you picked the one person in the world less qualified to give it than yourself." She reached out one shaking hand, and Noor took it, and it was lighter than a bird's bones. "I lied to you for twenty years. I let you grow up ashamed of the finest thing about you. I asked you to blind yourself rather than trust you with the truth. If you're waiting for me to say *you're forgiven and I was right all along*, you'll wait forever, because I was wrong in half the ways a person can be wrong." Her grip tightened, surprising strength in it, the old strength surfacing once like a fish. "But forgiveness isn't a ledger, Noor. Nobody balances it. You forgive the way the land forgives—completely, stupidly, without keeping accounts—and then you get up tomorrow and try to be worth it. So yes. Yes, child. Forgiven. Given too late to do either of us much good, which is the only kind of forgiveness that's ever real."

Noor bowed her head over the small hand and wept, quietly, the way Maghda was weeping, without performance, and the fire burned down, and neither of them moved to feed it.

* * *

Near dawn, Maghda made her get up.

"Enough grieving. Grief is a fine meal but a poor house." She directed Noor by voice alone to the chest beneath the window—the one Noor had been forbidden to open her entire childhood—and watched, or faced, or listened, as Noor knelt and broke a lock that surrendered after twenty years with humiliating ease.

Inside lay books. Four of them, leather gone soft as cloth, and above them a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that clinked faintly when Noor touched it. Salt. Iron nails black with age. A knife with a blade the color of a winter sky.

"The gray one is the rite," Maghda said. "Read it aloud to me tonight, all of it, and I'll correct you where the copying went wrong—it was copied by my teacher, who was brilliant and careless, which is the worst combination. The others are yours regardless of what happens at midwinter. A listener's library. There are only nine in the world that I know of, and now you own one." She paused, and her voice dropped into something almost shy, which on Maghda was so strange that Noor's head came up. "And the bundle is for afterward. Whatever afterward looks like. Don't open it until it's over. Promise me."

"What is it?"

"A promise for a promise, girl. Open it when it's done."

Noor promised. Outside, the sky was turning the color of pewter, and the mountains stood around the cottage in their old silence, and for the first time in three days Noor felt the land's voice stir faintly beneath her feet—not speaking yet, but clearing its throat, the way it did before weather, before war, before anything worth hearing.

Maghda tilted her head toward that same silence, listening with whatever remained to her, and her ruined eyes went wide.

"Noor," she said. "How many days to the full moon?"

"Six. Why?"

"Because the mountain just told me something is climbing the pass behind you," Maghda said, "and it is *not* walking like a person."

End of Chapter 13.
