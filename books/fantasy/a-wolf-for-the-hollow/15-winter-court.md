## 15. Winter Court

The road to the capital was paved with pilgrims.

Wren had not expected that. In her years at court she had thought of the city as a place one arrived at by decree or by trade—carts of wool and iron, ambassadors with lacquered cases, the king's own messengers riding hard with sealed letters. She had never once considered that the roads might fill with ordinary people walking, hundreds upon hundreds of them, bent under bundles and singing in ragged unison, all flowing toward Vael Amaranth like water finding its level.

Now she walked among them with a staff she'd cut herself from alder, her hair bound up under a gray hood, her boots crusted with honest mud. Beside her, Noor kept their head down and their hands in their sleeves, and every few miles Wren felt them flinch—the land speaking too loudly here, too many feet pressing prayers into the same packed earth.

"You're limping," Noor said quietly.

"I'm *pilgriming*," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Limping is involuntary."

Noor's mouth twitched. It was the first almost-smile in three days, since they'd passed the gibbet outside Marrow's Cross where two men swung for the crime of *unlicensed devotion*—which was to say, praying to anything other than the crowned god. The Devoted had burned the roadside shrine there too, a little stone shepherd with its face chiseled away, and Noor had stood looking at it so long that Wren had to take their elbow and turn them bodily back onto the road.

That had been three days ago. The shrine-god's fragment still slept somewhere beneath Noor's ribs; Wren could tell because sometimes, when the wind moved through the bare trees, Noor's lips shaped words that weren't theirs.

"Tell me again what we're walking into," Noor said now, low enough that only Wren could hear over the hymn-singing ahead.

"A court audience. Midwinter vigil week—the king receives petitions publicly, before the whole assembly. Anyone can kneel in the great hall if they've a grievance and clean hands." Wren adjusted the strap of her bundle. "I sat those audiences for six years. I drew the processional maps. I know which doors the guards don't watch because the architects never intended anyone to use them."

"And the king will be there. In person."

"In person." Wren heard her own voice go flat and hated it. "He sits the Sunstone Throne at the hall's head. He wears the winter regalia—white gold, sapphires the size of your thumbnail. He used to be bored during petitions. He'd do arithmetic on his cuff. Aldric hated ceremony." She swallowed. "We'll see what sits there now."

Noor glanced at her sidelong. They had learned, these weeks together, when to push and when to let silence stand like a wall between rooms. This was a wall moment. Wren was grateful for it.

The capital announced itself long before they saw it: first by smell—woodsmoke and tanneries and the river's cold iron—then by sound, a low continuous hum like a hive the size of the world. Then, cresting the last hill on the pilgrim road, the city itself: Vael Amaranth stacked white and gray against a bruised sky, the cathedral spire piercing it like a needle through cloth, and above everything the palace terraces climbing the hill in pale tiers, hung with midwinter banners of white and gold.

At the gates, the queue of pilgrims stretched half a mile. Devoted in their dove-gray robes moved down the line with censers, swinging smoke into each traveler's face.

"What is that?" Noor whispered, going rigid.

"Blessing-smoke. Frankincense and something else—I never learned what." Wren kept her eyes forward, her voice bored. "It's supposed to reveal false hearts. In practice it reveals nothing but makes everyone cough. Breathe shallowly and think about bread."

Noor breathed shallowly and thought about bread, apparently, because when the censer reached them they only coughed like everyone else, and the Devoted passed on without a second look.

Inside the walls, the city was drowning in gold. Every lintel bore a gilded sunburst. Every fountain had been draped in white cloth. Children sold wax votives shaped like tiny crowns, and beggars had been swept from the main avenues—not helped, Wren noted, just *moved*, the way you move furniture before company arrives. The whole city had been dressed for a wedding no bride had consented to.

They lodged that night in a pilgrim hospice run by an order Wren didn't recognize—new, then, founded within the last two years, its sisters young and earnest and slightly afraid of their own incense. The beds were clean. The soup was thin. On the wall above the hearth hung a portrait of the king that made Wren's stomach drop.

Aldric had always refused formal portraits. "I have a face," he'd told the painters' guild, laughing, "and it changes. Paint the harbor instead." So the court had commissioned engravings from memory, and they'd all been bad in different ways, and he'd collected the worst ones and pinned them in his study like trophies.

This portrait was not from memory. This portrait was *exact*, rendered in loving oil detail—and wrong in every way that mattered. The painter had given him eyes like lamps. Light poured out of him. His crown floated an inch above his brow, haloed, and his mouth wore a smile of such perfect serenity that it looked less like a face than a door left open into something vast.

Below it, in gilt letters: *THE FATHER OF LIGHT KEEPS HIS PEOPLE.*

"That's not him," Noor said softly. They were standing beside her, soup untouched.

"No," Wren said. "It isn't."

"It's close, though. That's the horrible part. Someone looked at him and painted what they saw."

Wren turned away from the portrait. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we petition the throne."

* * *

The great hall of Vael Amaranth had been built to make human beings feel appropriately small before the office of kingship—a lesson in proportion, in awe, in the mathematics of power. Wren had walked its length a hundred times and had long ago stopped feeling small; she'd felt, instead, the pleasant pride of belonging to the machine whose gears this architecture celebrated.

She felt small now. But not in the way the architects intended.

They entered with the third wave of petitioners, shuffled along velvet ropes past pillars wrapped in midwinter ivy—real ivy, imported green in deep winter, which must have cost a fortune and would die within the week. The gallery rose in tiers around the hall's perimeter, packed with citizens, and the floor of the nave held the kneeling petitioners: farmers with land disputes, a widow suing a miller, a guild delegation from the dyers' quarter, each waiting their turn beneath the gaze of a thousand strangers.

And at the far end, up seven marble steps, the Sunstone Throne.

He was magnificent. That was the first thing, and the worst thing, and Wren understood in one sick lurch why the portrait-painter had painted light. Aldric had always been handsome in an unassuming way—dark hair going early gray at the temples, a scholar's stoop he tried to correct whenever anyone was watching, ink stains he never quite scrubbed from his fingers. The thing on the throne shared his bones but none of his awkwardness. It sat with perfect stillness, spine straight as a drawn blade, wearing the winter regalia as though the white gold had grown there. Its face was his face, refined—every asymmetry smoothed, every line of fatigue erased—and its eyes were open and dark and utterly empty, twin wells with no bottom you could find.

The hall hushed. A herald's voice rang off the vaulting: *"His Radiance the King Eternal receives the grievances of his beloved people."*

*King Eternal.* The title had been *the King, Protector of the Realm*, forty months ago. Wren's hands curled inside her sleeves.

The first petitioner was the widow and her miller dispute. The king listened—or the thing wearing him listened—with its head tilted at a gentle angle, and when she finished, it spoke.

Its voice was Aldric's voice. That was the second-worst thing. Wren knew that voice the way she knew her own heartbeat; she had heard it angry over maps, drunk and soft on a balcony in spring, cracking with grief at his father's funeral. This voice came out of the same throat and said:

*"Mother of Sorrows, the crown sees you. The miller's greed is a stone in the river of your days. Bring your grievance to the Hall of Provision tomorrow and you will leave with grain enough for winter."*

The widow burst into tears of joy. The hall murmured approval. Somewhere in the gallery someone began to weep openly, crying *blessed, blessed,* and others took it up until the herald had to call for quiet.

Wren watched the resolution happen with a cartographer's eye. There was no deliberation. No clerk took notes. No steward was dispatched. The king simply *said* the woman would be fed, and the machinery of the court bent itself instantly around the pronouncement like water around a dropped stone, and Wren realized that this was how it ruled now—not by law, not by council, but by utterance. Whatever the crowned mouth declared became true, because no one dared let it become otherwise. A kingdom held up by the terror of disappointing a god.

The dyers' guild came next. Their grievance was tariffs. The king answered with a parable about dye and souls that made no sense whatsoever, and the guild master knelt lower and lower as it went on, nodding, agreeing, agreeing, until Wren wanted to scream. Then a farmer whose son had been conscripted into the Devoted and died at some border skirmish—*there are no border skirmishes anymore,* Wren thought wildly, *we had peace, we had eleven years of peace*—and the king looked at the farmer for a long, long moment with those bottomless eyes and said:

*"Your son walks in light. I have seen him. He carries water for the blessed dead, and he is not cold."*

And the farmer believed it. Wren watched him believe it, watched the grief in his face transmute into something radiant and terrible, and she understood that this was the true horror—not the lies, but that the lies were *kind*. Kindness as anesthesia. Grief answered with beautiful fiction until no one remembered what real comfort felt like.

Beside her, Noor had gone very still. Too still. Wren slid her hand along the rope and pressed their wrist—once, twice, a smuggler's signal she'd taught them herself—and felt the tremor running through them like a struck bell.

*I hear it,* Noor's eyes said. *Underneath. It's feeding.*

*Not here,* Wren's said back. *Hold.*

Then the herald called the next name, and Wren's blood went to ice water.

*"Petition of the brothers and sisters of the Thornfell high passes, concerning the closing of the shepherd shrines."*

No. No, no—they hadn't come to petition, they'd come to *look*, to map the hall, to count guards, to learn the rhythm of the audience. They were disguised as Thornfell pilgrims because Thornfell pilgrims were common this season and beyond suspicion. But someone in the queue had actually come to petition about the shrines, and now the king's empty eyes were turning toward the cluster of gray-cloaked mountain folk four rows ahead of them, and among those folk stood a lean man with a shepherd's crook who was already being ushered forward by the guards.

The shepherd was old and frightened and brave. He knelt on the marble and said, in a voice that shook only a little, that the shrines of the high passes had served travelers for nine hundred years, that the cairn-gods asked nothing but a stone and a kind word, that his people meant no disrespect to the Father of Light but begged leave to keep their old customs—

The king raised one hand. The hall went silent so completely that Wren could hear the ivy leaves ticking against the pillars.

*"Nine hundred years,"* the king said gently. *"Yes. I remember."*

And then—Wren's heart stopped—the thing on the throne *smiled*, and it was Aldric's smile, the real one, the crooked one, the one that had made her feel sixteen years old and seen for the first time in her life, and it said:

*"Come closer, keeper of stones. Tell me your name."*

The old shepherd crept up the seven steps. The guards did not stop him. The whole hall leaned forward like grass in wind. And the king bent from the throne—bent, the way a tree bends, with terrible slow grace—and laid his hand on the old man's head, and spoke words too soft for the gallery to catch.

What happened next happened fast. The shepherd straightened. His face had changed—washed clean of fear, of doubt, of ninety years of weather and stubbornness, replaced with a beatific blankness that Wren recognized from the portrait in the hospice. He turned to the hall and raised both arms and cried out in a voice not entirely his own: *"The Father of Light is merciful! The old stones may sleep! I have SEEN!"*

The hall erupted. Hymns. Weeping. The Devoted along the walls had gone rigid with something that looked, to Wren's trained eye, very much like relief.

Beside her, Noor whispered, so faintly it was barely breath: "He ate his memory. The god just—it reached into him and drank the doubt out, and left—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Wren. That's what it does. That's what it *is*. It eats the parts of people that resist."

"I know," Wren said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Look at the throne."

Noor looked. So did she.

For just a moment—one breath, no more—as the shepherd was led away glowing and the herald called the next petitioner, the king's composure cracked. His shoulders dropped a fraction. His head bowed. And his right hand, lying on the armrest of the Sunstone Throne, moved.

Two fingers tapped the white gold. Twice. Pause. Three times. Twice. Pause. Once.

An old court cipher. One Wren had invented herself, years ago, for love letters passed across a council table—tap-counts standing for letters, a child's code grown elegant with use. *T-H-E-N. T-H-E-N. O-N-E.*

Then. Then. One.

Her lungs forgot their work. She stared at the throne and the king's hand went still again, the mask sliding back into place, the serene hollow eyes lifting to receive the next grievance as though nothing had stirred beneath the surface at all.

But Wren had spent six years reading maps, and a map, she had always told her apprentices, is just a message written in terrain. And a message, she knew now, had just been written in the only terrain a prisoner still owned: his own body, in front of a thousand witnesses, invisible to everyone except the one person alive who knew the code.

*Then. Then. One.*

One day? One hour? One attempt remaining?

Or—her mind raced through the cipher, checking and rechecking—*one* as in *first*: the first letter of a longer word still coming, spelled out across hours of audience, tap by tap, while the thing that wore him smiled at weeping widows and never noticed its hostage scratching letters on the inside of its own cage.

"Wren." Noor's voice, urgent, very quiet. "You've gone white. What did you see?"

She tore her eyes from the throne. Around them the hall sang hymns to a hungry god; ahead of them lay the cathedral, the binding stone, the rite; behind them, seven marble steps and a hand still tapping.

"He's in there," she breathed. "Noor—he's still *in there*. And he's trying to tell us something."
