## 17. Letters in the Margins

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Wren found the first letter.

Not a letter, exactly. Not at first. She had been going through Aldric's old edicts for three nights running, spread across the floor of the safehouse above the dyer's shop in Hollowmere, pages fanned out like a hand of cards she kept losing. The edicts themselves were madness dressed in law—grain tithes doubled, then halved, then doubled again within a single season; a decree ordering every bell in the eastern provinces recast to a deeper tone; another commanding that no one in the capital speak after sundown on moonless nights, *so that He may hear His own voice clearly*. She had read them all before, in rumor and in smuggled copies, and each reading had made her feel as though she were pressing her palm against a wound.

But tonight she was reading them properly. Slowly. With the eye she had once used to catch the lies hidden in border maps—the false valleys cartographers drew to trap invaders, the rivers that bent a half-degree off true so that any army following them would arrive exhausted at a fortified bank. Falsified maps had been her crime. Finding falsifications was her craft.

And Aldric had been leaving her messages.

She saw it first in the margins of the grain decree—a small inked flourish beside the word *provision*, a curl of the pen that served no purpose except beauty. Except it wasn't beauty. Wren had spent four years of her life learning the cipher the royal chancery used for private correspondence between the king and his surveyors, a code built from cartographic notation: contour lines that meant nothing, compass bearings that meant everything. A flourish like that, drawn with a surveyor's hand, wasn't decoration. It was a bearing.

She sat very still, the way she had once sat over a map that didn't agree with itself, and let her heart go quiet.

Then she began to look.

* * *

Noor came up the stairs near midnight, smelling of woodsmoke and the cold, and stopped in the doorway when she saw the papers spread everywhere, the candle stub, Wren sitting cross-legged in the wreckage of a kingdom's paperwork with ink on her fingers and an expression Wren couldn't name.

"You've been crying," Noor said.

"I've been working."

"Your eyes are red."

"They do that when I read for too long." Wren held up the grain decree without looking away from it. "He's writing to us. Or—he was writing to someone. Look." She tapped the flourish. "This is a bearing. East-northeast, eleven degrees off true north. And here—" she turned the page, found the second mark, a small circle bisected by a line, tucked into the descender of a *g*— "this is elevation. These aren't edicts anymore. They're a map."

Noor crossed the room and crouched beside her, careful not to touch the papers, the way she was careful with everything that mattered—as though the world might startle if handled too confidently. "A map of what?"

"That's what I'm finding out." Wren pulled the next decree toward her, the one about bells. "He stopped sleeping fourteen months ago. Everyone says the king went mad. But madmen don't hide bearings in their own decrees where only a chancery-trained surveyor would ever find them. Madmen don't—" Her voice caught. She smoothed it flat. "Madmen don't leave instructions for their own rescue inside documents designed to be read aloud to crowds."

Noor was quiet a moment. Then: "You loved him."

It wasn't a question, and Wren didn't answer it as one. She kept her eyes on the page. "I knew him before the crown. Before he was anyone. We used to walk the old survey roads together when he was still just a second son nobody bothered to educate properly, and I'd teach him how to read terrain, and he'd pretend to be bored and then ask questions until midnight." She turned a page. "That was a long time ago."

"How long?"

"Long enough that I falsified a map to keep him alive, and it got me exiled instead." She said it lightly, the way you say things you have said to yourself so many times they've worn smooth. "So. Let's find out what he's been trying to tell us for fourteen months."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Wren taught Noor the cipher as they went—not the whole architecture of it, but the grammar: how a thickened downstroke meant *below*, how a repeated word three times in a paragraph marked a waypoint, how the king had a habit of dotting certain letters with a tiny triangle that meant *danger* or *do not approach alone*. Noor learned it the way she seemed to learn everything about the land—by listening rather than looking, by feeling for the shape underneath. By the third hour she was catching marks Wren missed.

"He dots the *i* in *cathedral*," Noor said suddenly, in the smallest hour before dawn. "Every time. In every decree that mentions it. But nowhere else."

Wren took the page. There it was: *the Cathedral of the Ascension shall host the solstice vigil*, and the *i* crowned with its small sharp triangle. Danger. Do not approach alone. And beneath the sentence, almost invisible unless you knew to look, a sequence of flourishes that resolved, once you had the key, into something Wren's hands remembered drawing better than her own name:

*Below the reliquary gate. Third landing. The stone is older than the crown.*

She read it aloud, and the room felt colder afterward, as though the words had taken some of the fire's warmth with them.

"The binding stone," Wren said. "Under the cathedral. He's telling us where it is." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Fourteen months. Fourteen months of decrees, and half the court thinks he's a god and the other half thinks he's mad, and the whole time he's been hiding a map to his own prison in the margins, hoping—" hoping *I* would read it, she did not say, because saying it aloud felt like a kind of vanity, or a kind of grief, or both. "Hoping someone would read it."

Noor watched her with those eyes that heard things other people only said. "You think he hoped it would be you."

"I think he dotted his letters the way I taught him to dot his letters," Wren said. "When we were twenty years old and the biggest danger in the world was a mislabeled river. So either he remembers me, or whatever's left of him remembers me, and I don't know which of those is worse."

* * *

Near dawn, Noor fell asleep sitting up, her back against the wall, her chin dropped to her chest, breathing the deep even breaths of someone who had learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing to stay safe for long. Wren draped a blanket over her and went back to the papers alone.

That was when she found the last message.

It was in the oldest decree—the first strange one, issued twelve moons past, the night Aldric stopped sleeping. It had always struck readers as pure madness: an order that the royal almoner distribute bread at the city gates each full moon, *and that none be turned away, neither stranger nor thief nor debtor, for the King desires that no hand be empty*. Charity, but strangely urgent charity, charity with an edge of desperation. Scholars had called it the beginning of his decline.

Wren read the margin.

The cipher here was different—shakier, pressed hard into the paper, as though the hand that wrote it had been fighting for every stroke. It took her an hour to work through it, and when she finished she had to set the page down twice, because her hands were not steady enough to hold it.

*If you are reading this, then you found the bearings, which means you are who I prayed you would be, which means I am sorry for what I am about to ask.*

*The stone beneath the cathedral is the door. What wears me is not me. Do not mistake its voice for mine—it will offer you anything, and everything it offers will be true, and that is how you will know it, because I would never tell you the truth so easily.*

*There is a rite. Maghda of the Thornfells knows it; I know that much from what leaks through. Unbinding. The price is memory. Whoever casts it pays in everything they have walked and known.*

*So hear me, whoever you are, and hear this most of all:*

*Do not save me if it costs another.*

*Whatever is wearing me has worn kings for centuries. If the price of my life is a life, or a mind, or a memory that belongs to someone else—then leave me. Let the thing starve slowly behind my face rather than feed it a single hour of another soul. I have been a king. I know what crowns cost. I will not be bought with coin that isn't mine.*

*I mean it. I will know if you disobey. The land tells me things now, in the cracks. And if you disobey me in this, then everything I have endured for fourteen months was for nothing, and you may as well let it finish.*

*—A.*

*P.S. If it is Wren: the river survey of 9th year. You drew the ford wrong on purpose. I have known for eleven years. I never said, because you looked so pleased with yourself.*

Wren put the page down very gently, the way you put down something sleeping.

Then she put her face in her hands and laughed until it turned into the other thing, quietly, so as not to wake Noor, her shoulders shaking in the grey light while the candle finally drowned in its own wax.

Eleven years. Eleven years he had known about the falsified ford—the one she'd altered to route his hunting party away from the pass where assassins waited, the alteration that had unraveled into the scandal that ended her career, the lie told out of love that had cost her everything and saved his life and been discovered anyway, and he had known, and he had said nothing, all these years, because she'd looked pleased with herself.

*Do not save me if it costs another.*

She thought of Noor asleep against the wall, this runaway who could hear the land's true voice, this person whose gift was exactly the thing the rite would demand as payment—every road memorized, every path known, the whole interior country of her erased. She thought of the fragment of truth the shepherd-shrine god had given them: *the impostor was made, not born; bound into service by the first kings.* She thought of Maghda dying in her cottage in the Thornfells, having already paid decades of her own sight to shield a child who wasn't even hers.

Everyone around this king paid for him. That was the shape of it. That had always been the shape of it. And now here was his voice, reaching across fourteen months of captivity inside his own body, asking—begging—to be the exception. To not be worth it. To be left.

"No," Wren said softly to the empty room, to the grey window, to a man she hadn't seen in eleven years who had somehow just spoken to her more honestly than he ever had in life. "You don't get to decide that."

She gathered the papers, squared their edges, and began, with a surveyor's precision, to draw the map.

* * *

Noor woke to the smell of fresh ink and found Wren hunched over a clean sheet, drafting by memory: the cathedral quarter, the reliquary gate, the third landing, the descent below. Around the edges, in the chancery cipher, she copied out the king's marginalia verbatim, preserving every shaky stroke.

"What's the plan?" Noor asked, rubbing sleep from her eyes.

"We go under the city." Wren didn't look up. "Through routes no royal map knows—which means I need yours. Your smuggler's knowledge of the Thornfells ends at the foothills, but the catacombs beneath the capital connect to the old mine workings in the mountains, and the mountains are your country. Between your ears and my cipher, there's a road to that stone that the Devoted will never think to guard, because it doesn't exist on any map ever drawn." She finished a line and sat back, flexing her ink-stained fingers. "We have until midwinter. Thirteenth full moon. Solstice vigil, when the whole cathedral will be crowded with pilgrims and the guards will be watching the doors, not the ground beneath their feet."

Noor came and stood over the draft map, studying it. Then she frowned. "Read me the last part again. The plea."

Wren hesitated. Then she read it: *Do not save me if it costs another.*

Noor was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet. "He doesn't know about me yet. About what I can hear. About what the rite takes." She looked up, and her eyes were steady in a way that frightened Wren more than any threat had. "When he finds out, he'll try to stop us. He'll fight us from inside his own body if he has to."

"Yes."

"And you're going to disobey him."

Wren met her gaze. "Yes."

Noor nodded slowly, and something passed between them—not agreement exactly, but recognition. Two people who understood that love, real love, the kind that survives exile and erasure, does not obey the beloved's best arguments. It stays. It pays. It refuses, gently and completely, to let the other person be the sacrifice.

"Then we'd better make sure it doesn't come to that price," Noor said. "Because I've heard what the land sounds like under that cathedral, Wren. Even from here. Even in dreams." She touched the edge of the map, where the third landing descended into blank paper. "Something down there is listening for us. And it already knows your handwriting."

Outside, the first snow of the season was beginning to fall on Hollowmere—early, too early, a white silence settling over the village like a held breath. Winter was coming ahead of schedule, the way it always did when something old and hungry leaned against the world.

Thirteen turns. The clock was running.
