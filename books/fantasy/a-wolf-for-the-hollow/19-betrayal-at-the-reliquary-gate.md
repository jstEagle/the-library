## 19. Betrayal at the Reliquary Gate

The candles in the Reliquary Gate chapel never guttered. Tam had noticed it his first week among the Devoted and had been told, gently, by an older novice named Brother Callow, that noticing such things was a gift and not a problem to be solved. The flames burned steady because the Crowned One's presence filled the cathedral quarter like water fills a cup, and fire fed on that presence the way ordinary fire fed on air. Tam had accepted this explanation the way he had accepted most explanations since coming to the capital: gratefully, completely, and without looking at it too long.

It was easier not to look. Looking had consequences.

He was stationed at the reliquary gate itself tonight—the small postern door set into the cathedral's north flank, where pilgrims of sufficient rank could pass down into the crypts to venerate the relics of the First Devoted. It was considered an honor to stand this watch. Tam had been told so four times now, once by the provost himself, and each time he felt the honor settle over his shoulders like a vestment cut for someone else.

Snow came down in slow, fat flakes through the lamplight. Beyond the gate, the pilgrims' road curved away toward the city, and beyond the city, invisible under all that falling white, lay the mountains. Lay Hollowmere.

He thought about Hollowmere every night. He had learned not to let his face do it.

* * *

They had come for him in the spring, the Devoted, two years after the orchard bloomed. Tam had been fourteen then, the winter of the choosing still sharp in him like a swallowed nail: the night the whole village had gathered in the square with lanterns, and the Voice of the Crowned One—speaking through old Mother Aldith, her body held upright by other hands—had pronounced that the god required a vessel, and that the lot had fallen upon Sela Brannigan, who was sixty-one years old and had taught half the village to read.

And a stranger—a thin, sharp-faced runaway with mud on their boots and no business speaking at all—had stood up in the crowd and said *no*.

Tam remembered the whole shape of that night. He remembered how Noor had done it: not with defiance, which would have earned them both the rod, but with a smuggler's trick dressed up as piety. They had cried out that the vessel must be *prepared*, that the scriptures of the First Devoted required three days of fasting and anointing before any vessel was worthy—and there were no scriptures that said any such thing, but who in Hollowmere knew? The village elders had conferred. The Voice had gone silent in Mother Aldith, as if consulting. And in those three days of manufactured grace, Wren the cartographer had gotten Sela out through the fens with a forged pilgrimage pass, and when the Devoted came back for their vessel they found an empty house and a cold hearth and a story about cousins in the east.

Noor had stayed. That was the part Tam could hardly bear to remember. Noor could have run with Sela; instead they'd stood in the square the next evening and let the villagers look at them, and lied so smoothly about the stranger's involvement that even Tam, who knew, had almost believed it. And then Noor had vanished into the Thornfells like smoke, and Hollowmere had breathed again, and the orchard had kept blooming, and Tam had lain awake for a season listening to his own heart argue with itself.

When the Devoted recruiters came through in the spring—robed in grey-white, gentle-voiced, offering food and purpose and a place in the light—he had gone with them. His mother had wept. His father had shaken his hand like a man seeing off a soldier. Nobody had understood, least of all Tam, that he was going to find the thing that answered prayers and ask it why.

He had asked, eventually. In his third month, kneeling in the crypt-dark with the other novices while the presence poured down through the cathedral stones like warm rain, he had opened his mouth against the singing and asked, silently, *why did you want Sela?*

And the answer had come, and it had been beautiful, and it had made sense, and by morning he could not remember what it said.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole trick. Every question dissolved by dawn like frost, leaving only the warmth behind, and the warmth was enough, the warmth was *so much*, and Tam had stopped asking questions because each one cost him something he couldn't name and left him poorer without teaching him anything.

Brother Callow said doubt was a stone in the shoe. Brother Callow said you walked it out.

Tam had been walking for two years.

* * *

The bell tolled the ninth hour, and with it came the pilgrims.

There had been word from the provost's office at dusk: a party of last-minute solstice pilgrims, delayed on the northern roads, arriving after the gates should have closed—but bearing seals of a minor northern abbey and the written indulgence of a suffragan whose name meant nothing to Tam. Such parties were to be admitted to the reliquary crypt despite the hour; the Crowned One welcomed all who hungered, especially at the turning of the year. The twelfth moon was nearly full above the snow. Everyone in the quarter could feel it, a kind of pressure behind the eyes, a tuning-fork hum in the floor joints. The thirteenth turn came at midwinter, six weeks hence, and the whole city was already leaning toward it like grass toward wind.

So Tam stood aside as the pilgrims came through the postern one by one, hooded against the snow, stamping slush off their boots on the threshold stone. He counted them, because counting was his duty: eight souls, seven tall or stooped, moving with the careful weariness of long roads.

The eighth was small. Moved wrong. Moved like someone reading the architecture rather than the icons—eyes flicking to hinges, to sight-lines, to the shadowed seam where the wall met the floor, and Tam knew that way of looking because he had grown up in a smuggling valley where everyone's children learned it with their letters.

Then the pilgrim pushed back their hood to shake off the snow, and the lamplight found a thin face, sharp-boned, older than Tam remembered and yet exactly the same, and Tam's heart went off like a dropped lamp.

Noor.

Two years, and here they were, disguised badly in a pilgrim's grey, standing six feet from him inside the holiest place Tam knew, and behind them a taller figure in a similar hood—Wren, it had to be, the cartographer, the traitor, the woman whose forged papers had carried Sela out of Hollowmere—and Noor's eyes swept the gate chamber once, twice, and passed across Tam's face the first time without stopping, because Tam was nobody, Tam was a novice in grey-white with his head bowed, Tam had been nobody on purpose for two years—

And then they looked back. Because some things don't change. Some people are built to notice what wants noticing, and Noor's gaze came around like a compass needle and fixed on him and stopped, and Tam watched recognition arrive in their face the way dawn arrives over the fells: not all at once, but absolutely.

Their lips parted. Under the hood, their eyes did something complicated—fear, yes, but underneath the fear something softer and more terrible, something that said *oh, it's you, oh, you're alive, oh, you grew up.*

"Novice." The leader of the pilgrim party—a heavy man with an abbot's ring worn openly on one gloved finger—was beckoning impatiently. "The crypt stair. We haven't got the night."

Tam's body moved. His body had been trained well; two years of drills and offices and hours on his knees had taught it obedience that outran his mind. He took the great iron key from its chain, fitted it to the crypt door, and turned the lock, and the door swung inward on breath after breath of candle-warmed air rising from below, and all the while Noor watched him from three feet away, and neither of them spoke, because what was there to say with the abbot watching and the snow falling and the twelfth moon pressing down on the roof like a hand?

*You saved me,* Tam thought at them, loudly, uselessly, the way you shout at someone across a river. *You stood up in the square and you saved Sela and you saved me, I was on the list next, everyone knew I was next after the old ones, and you stood up.*

*I believe,* he thought back at himself, in the voice of Brother Callow, in the voice of the provost, in the warm dissolving voice that answered questions by eating them. *I serve the light that feeds the hungry. The Crowned One healed my cousin's lung-sickness. The Crowned One ended the famine year in the south. I have seen it. I have handed out the bread myself.*

Both things were true. That was the unforgivable architecture of it. Both things were true at once, and there was no door between them, and Tam stood holding the open crypt door like a man holding open a door in his own chest.

The abbot led his pilgrims through. Wren went last, and as she passed Tam she did not look at him at all, which was its own kind of mercy, and Noor went second-to-last, and as they passed, close enough that Tam could smell woodsmoke and wet wool and the faint green scent of the mountains that clung to them like a second skin, Noor's hand moved—small, quick, hidden between their bodies—and pressed something into his palm and closed his fingers over it.

Then they were through, and down the stair, and the door was closing, and Tam was alone in the gate chamber with his heart hammering and his fist clenched around whatever they had given him.

He waited until the abbot's party was out of earshot below. Then he opened his hand.

A button. A plain bone button, the kind sold by the hundred in market towns everywhere—except that Tam knew these buttons. His mother sewed them. Half the youth of Hollowmere wore coats with these buttons. And Noor had pressed one into his hand because Noor remembered—of course they remembered, they remembered everything, that was their curse—that Tam's father had once traded a dozen such buttons to a peddler for the coat Tam had worn the winter Sela was chosen. It said: *I know you. I know where you're from. I know what I did.*

Or it said: *remember.*

Tam stood in the candlelight and the button sat in his palm like a pebble from a river he could no longer find his way back to, and the warmth of the Crowned One's presence pulsed up through the floor against his boot-soles, patient, vast, certain, and somewhere beneath his feet Noor and Wren were descending into the crypt with forged seals and stolen names, walking toward the one place in the world they were not permitted to be.

* * *

Here is what Tam did in the next hour, though he could never afterward assemble it into a single decision:

He finished his watch. He logged the pilgrim party in the gate register in his careful novice's hand—eight souls, abbot's seal, suffragan's indulgence, admitted at the ninth hour—and his pen did not shake, because his body had been trained too well for shaking.

He walked the corridor to the novices' dormitory as if it were any other night. He passed Brother Callow in the cloister and exchanged the nod that exempted him from questions until morning.

He sat on the edge of his cot in the dark, and he took the button out, and he held it under his blanket where no one could see, and he turned it over and over, and he thought about the answer he had received in the crypt two years ago, the beautiful answer he could not remember, and for the first time since he had come to the capital he understood that the forgetting was not a failure in him. It was a feature of the thing that answered. A god of truth would not need to eat your questions. Only a thing with something to hide required the questions to dissolve by dawn.

And if it hid its answers, what else did it hide?

Sela's empty house. The three days of grace that Noor had bought with a lie shaped like scripture. The Voice speaking through Mother Aldith's exhausted body. Tam turned all of it over in the dark, and the button turned in his hand, and the two motions became one motion, and somewhere past midnight he set the button on the sill beneath the narrow window, facing outward, the way you leave a lamp burning for someone expected home.

Then he got up, and put on his shoes, and walked bareheaded through the snow to the provost's quarters, and knocked, and gave the sign of the watch-change, and stood at attention on the cold stone with his heart very quiet and very far away, and said:

"Excellency. Forgive the hour. There is something you must know about the pilgrim party admitted to the reliquary crypt."

His voice did not break. He reported it cleanly: the small eighth pilgrim, the wrongness of their gait, the face he had seen when the hood came back—a face he knew, he told the provost, from Hollowmere, a runaway, unregistered, almost certainly the same fugitive named in the winter circulars, traveling with a companion matching the description of the disgraced cartographer Wren of the Royal Survey. He described the direction of their descent. He suggested, in the flat competent tone the provost valued, that the crypt watches be doubled and the lower galleries sealed at the second stair.

The provost listened without interrupting, which was worse than interruption. When Tam finished, the old man regarded him for a long moment with pale, unhurried eyes.

"You did well, novice," he said at last. "This is what devotion looks like. It is not warm. Remember that. Warmth is what the Crowned One gives; duty is what we give back." He was already reaching for his robe and his keys, already turning to summon the night-captain of the Devoted guard, and Tam stood forgotten in the doorway, dismissed, absolved, damned.

He walked back across the courtyard through the falling snow. The twelfth moon was setting now behind the cathedral spires, nearly round, nearly perfect, six weeks short of complete. From the dormitory wing, the other novices' windows were dark; from the cathedral's crown, the ever-burning lamps made a halo against the clouds, and the whole structure hummed faintly with the presence it held, the way a cup hums when the water in it remembers being a river.

Tam stopped in the middle of the courtyard and was sick, quietly, into a snowdrift, and then wiped his mouth and kept walking, because there was nothing else to do. He had chosen. Or he had failed to choose, which amounted to the same report filed with the same man. Loyalty had split him down the middle like a keel splitting water, and now both halves were shipping toward different horizons and only one of them could survive the voyage, and he would not know which until it was far too late to swim.

Back in the dormitory, he knelt—not at the devotional rail, but beside his own cot, in the angle of the wall where no one could see him—and he prayed. Not the liturgy. Just words, his own, the plain words of a boy from a hollow village, sent down through the floorboards into the dark where something vast and warm and hungry held a king the way a hand holds a moth.

*If you're real,* he prayed, *if any of it is real—if you healed my cousin, if the bread was yours—then hear me. Don't hurt them. Whatever they've come to do. Whatever I've just done to them. Don't hurt them.*

The warmth rose up through the stones to meet him, as it always did, infinite and gentle and utterly without a face.

And it answered.

And by morning, Tam knew, he would not remember what it said.

Below the city, in the dark beneath the cathedral, a door that had been locked was now unlocked, and two figures were moving through galleries no royal map had charted, and somewhere ahead of them, at the bottom of everything, a stone waited with a god chained to it and a king drowning slowly in the chain—and the clock of the thirteenth moon ticked on above them all, in the sky, indifferent, nearly full.
