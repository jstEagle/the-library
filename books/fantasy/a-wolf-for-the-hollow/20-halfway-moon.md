## 20. Halfway Moon

The mountain spoke first.

Noor felt it the way she always felt things now—through her boots, through the soles of her feet pressed to stone, through that strange second skin of attention Maghda had spent twenty years trying to teach her to close. The Thornfells were old and mostly honest, their paths grudging but true, and for six days Noor had led Wren up a route no royal map had ever carried: a smuggler's stair cut into the flank of Cwm Ddu, switchbacking through scree and frozen heather toward the high pass that would let them slip past the Devoted's cordon on the valley road below.

But tonight, as they made camp in the mouth of an old mine adit—dry, defensible, invisible from above—the land under her feet went quiet in the wrong way. Not sleeping-quiet. Held-breath quiet.

"Wren." She was already on her feet. "Get out. Now."

Wren looked up from the small fire she'd been coaxing, firelight catching the gray at her temples, the map-scarred hands gone still around a stick of kindling. "What is it?"

"The hillside's listening to itself." Noor grabbed the pack, threw Wren's bedroll at her chest. "There's water moving where it shouldn't be. Get *out*."

They made it eleven steps.

Noor heard it before she saw it—a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, deep and grinding—and then the slope above the adit let go. Not all of it, not a whole avalanche, just enough: a throatful of rock and frozen earth sliding down across the entrance, sealing the tunnel behind them with a boom that rolled away down the valley like distant thunder answering thunder.

Dust filled the dark. Noor coughed, blind, one hand locked around Wren's wrist, and for three long heartbeats neither of them moved.

Then Wren said, into the blackness, with the flat calm of a woman who had once redrawn a kingdom's borders and lied about it to the king's own face: "Well. That could have been worse."

"How," Noor managed, "could that possibly have been worse."

"We could still have been inside when it came down."

* * *

The moon, when they found it, was already halfway up the sky, and half itself besides—a clean-waxed coin hanging over the notch of the pass they'd meant to reach by morning. It came through a crack in the collapsed rubble high above, a blade of silver laid across the floor of the sealed adit, and by its light Noor took inventory of what they were.

Two people. One pack, half-buried and dug free. A fire built small and careful against the far wall, where the smoke could find the fissure in the roof and leak away. Enough food for four days if they were honest, five if they lied to themselves. And somewhere beneath them, according to Wren's memory and Noor's sense of the land both, another gallery of the old mine running down and sideways, maybe toward daylight, maybe toward nothing.

"Tomorrow," Wren said, sketching in the dust with a stick—the cartographer's habit, Noor had learned, the way other people prayed or bit their nails. "We dig tomorrow. Tonight we rest, because hands that shake drop rocks on heads."

"You're not my commander."

"No." Wren didn't look up from her map-in-dust. "I'm the person who's going to sit here and worry about you quietly unless you sleep, so you may as well sleep and spare us both."

Noor almost smiled. Almost. Then the half-moon slid a little further across the crack overhead, and the silver blade on the floor shifted, and she thought: *the twelfth turn is nine days gone. Three remain.* And the smile died somewhere on the way to her mouth.

Wren saw it anyway. She always did. That was the trouble with her—that was the whole entire trouble with Wren, who missed nothing, who had spent years reading terrain for a living and had apparently decided Noor was terrain worth learning by heart.

"Noor."

"Don't."

"It's three days' walk to the capital if the pass holds. Four if we're slow. Five if we're dead." Wren set the stick down. "We should talk about what happens at the stone."

"There's nothing to talk about. I do the rite. You hold the impostor's eyes. We walk out."

"And if the price takes what Maghda said it takes?" Wren's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "If you pay in memory, and what it takes is—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "If you come out from under that cathedral and you don't know me."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the mountain's bones, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"I'll know the mission," Noor said. "I wrote it all down. Every step, every name, every—everything that matters. It's in the pages. You'll read them back to me."

"That's not what I asked."

Noor looked at the fire because looking at Wren was suddenly unbearable. "You asked what I asked. I'm telling you the answer."

"You're telling me a *plan*." Wren leaned forward, and the firelight found her face—all the lines the exile had carved there, the mouth that could be so dry and was not dry now. "Plans are how you hide. You've been doing it since Hollowmere. Since before Hollowmere, I'd wager. So I'm asking the thing underneath the plan, and I'd like an answer underneath the plan too, if you can find one."

* * *

Here is what Noor could not say, though it sat in her chest like a swallowed coal:

She had spent her whole life memorizing roads. Passes and fords and smugglers' stairs, goat tracks and deadfalls and the secret ways through hedge and hollow, until her head was a country unto itself, dense with ways out. That was what her gift had been for, in Maghda's keeping—not wonder, not beauty, just escape. Every path a door left unlocked. Every road a promise that she never had to stay anywhere, never had to be caught by anything, never had to love a place so much that losing it would break her.

And then Wren had come along with her ruined reputation and her terrible jokes and her habit of talking to horses like disgruntled colleagues, and somewhere between the Shepherd's Grave and the river ford, between the shrine-god's sleeping truth and the long cold nights of two bedrolls pushed closer against the frost, Noor had realized she had done the one thing Maghda had trained her never to do.

She had stopped mapping exits.

Wren was not a road. There was no smuggler's way around her. If the rite took these weeks—if it took the cave-in and the ford and the shrine and the arguments about pace and the night Wren had sung, badly, three verses of a marching song she only half remembered—if it took all of that, then Noor would wake in the aftermath holding pages full of someone else's handwriting about a person she did not know, and the knowing would be gone, and there was no route back to it. Memory was not terrain. You could not rewalk it. You could not feel for it under your feet.

That was the coal. She held it behind her teeth and stared at the fire.

"Noor." Wren's voice again, softer. "Whatever it is—it's all right. You don't have to—"

"I love you."

It came out fast and graceless, the way water comes out when a dam finally goes, and Noor immediately wanted the mountain to fall on her after all. But she didn't take it back. She sat there in the silver-and-orange light with her heart hammering like a fist on a door, and she said it again, slower, because Wren deserved it said properly:

"I love you. And I think the rite is going to take it. Not the fact of it—I'll write the fact down, facts survive—but the *knowing*. The way your voice sounds when you're worried and pretending not to be. The way you read maps upside down without noticing. All of that lives in the weeks, Wren, and the weeks are exactly what the price eats. Maghda told me. Pay in memory, she said, and memory doesn't let you choose which memories. It takes what's nearest the wound." Noor laughed, and it came out cracked. "And you're nearest. You're the nearest thing in the whole world."

Silence. Long enough that Noor finally made herself look up.

Wren had not moved. Her hands were flat on her knees, and her eyes were bright in a way Noor had never seen—not tears, not yet, but the shoreline of them—and when she spoke, her voice was rough as scraped vellum.

"Say the first part again."

"*Wren—*"

"The first part. Please."

"I love you."

Wren closed her eyes. Opened them. "All right," she said. "Then listen to me, because I've been a coward for longer than you've been alive, and I'm not spending my last days as one. I loved Aldric. You know that. I loved him the way you love a sunrise—beautiful, and certain, and entirely out of your hands. And I told myself for fifteen years that was the shape of love: something you watched from a distance and grieved in private and never, ever touched." She shook her head slowly. "Then a runaway apprentice flinched at a church bell in a village inn, and I followed her up a mountain, and I learned that love isn't a sunrise. It's a *road*. It's something you walk. Both people, same direction, mud and all."

"Wren—"

"I'm not finished." But she was smiling now, wet-eyed, and Noor's chest hurt with it. "So here is my answer, underneath the plan. I love you. I have loved you since the ford, when you stood in freezing water up to your thighs and talked a river into drowning our enemies gently instead of cruelly, and I thought: *there is the finest person I have ever known, and she has no idea.* And if the rite takes your knowing—" Wren's breath caught, steadied. "Then I will spend every day after giving it back. Page by page. Night by night. I'll read you your own handwriting until you hear your voice in it. I'll tell you about the cave-in and the ford and this fire and this moon. And if none of it ever comes back—if you look at me forever the way strangers look at each other—then I will love you the way I loved the king: from nearby, patiently, without needing to be known. Because loving you isn't conditional on being remembered. I learned that the hard way. Love that needs to be kept isn't love. It's a binding."

The word landed between them like a dropped stone.

"Aldric used to say something like that," Wren went on, quieter. "Before. Before the crown changed his letters. He said devotion that demands payment isn't devotion, it's debt. I thought he was being clever. He was being *prophetic*, poor man." She reached across the fire, palm up. An offer. Not a demand—an offer, the way you offer a hand to someone on a ledge. "So. Underneath the plan. Underneath everything. Will you walk it with me? However many of you walks out of that cathedral?"

Noor looked at the offered hand. At the fire. At the blade of half-moon light lying across the rubble that had nearly killed them both.

"If I forget," she said, "you'll have to court me again. From scratch. Fair warning—I'm told I'm difficult."

"I'm counting on it." Wren's smile wobbled. "I already know all your favorite things. It'll hardly be fair."

Noor took her hand.

Wren's fingers were cold and sure, and she pulled—gently, questioningly—and Noor came around the small fire on her knees, and then they were holding on to each other in the mouth of a sealed mountain under a halfway moon, and Noor discovered that crying and laughing could happen in the same breath and neither one cancelled the other out.

"I'm frightened," she admitted into Wren's shoulder. It was easier to say with her face hidden. "Not of dying. Of waking up and having lost—this. Losing the *weight* of you. Knowing the facts and not the weight."

"Then we make new weight." Wren's hand moved slow and steady between her shoulder blades, the way you'd calm a horse, the way you'd calm anyone you'd learned by heart. "Weight is just time, pressed close. We'll press more. Whatever survives, whatever doesn't—we start again tomorrow, and the day after, and every day the world leaves us. That's the whole trick, Noor. That's the only trick there is."

* * *

Later—much later, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the half-moon had crossed the whole of its crack and begun to slide away—they lay side by side on the bedrolls, not sleeping, not quite talking.

"Do you hear it?" Noor asked at last. "The mountain. What it's saying."

Wren was quiet a moment. "Tell me."

"It's not angry about the collapse. It's..." Noor listened with her whole body, that second skin of attention spread wide against the dark. "Ashamed. Old workings, bad shoring—men dug it greedy and left it rotten, and it's been waiting years to fail, and it failed on *us*, and it's sorry." She huffed a small laugh. "Maghda would say I'm anthropomorphizing. She'd say stone doesn't feel sorry."

"What do you say?"

"I say everything that gets walked on long enough learns to feel the walking." Noor turned her head. In the ember-light, Wren's profile was all shadow and line, a map of a face Noor knew better than any pass in the Thornfells. "I say the land keeps faith with whoever keeps faith with it. Even when it fails. Especially then."

"Then we forgive it in the morning and dig."

"In the morning." Noor settled closer. Through the wool and the cold and the weariness, she could feel Wren's heartbeat, steady as a surveyor's chain. "Wren? If I forget the ford—tell me I was brave. Don't tell me I drowned men. Tell me I was *brave*. I'd rather meet myself that way."

"I'll tell you the truth," Wren said. "But I'll lead with the brave part."

"That's all I ask."

The half-moon slid out of its crack. The darkness came down complete and soft as snowfall, and in it Noor slept—not easily, but deeply, with her back against a mountain that was sorry and her hand in the grip of a woman who had promised to remember for both of them.

She woke once, near dawn, to Wren murmuring in her sleep. Names. Dates. The names of passes and the dates of edicts and, threaded among them, unmistakably, *Noor, Noor, Noor*—a woman rehearsing, even unconscious, even in the dark, the story she intended to give back.

Noor lay still and listened, memorizing the moment the way she'd once memorized roads: thoroughly, desperately, against the day she might need to find her way home to it from nothing at all.

Above them, muffled by a hundred tons of fallen stone, the last of the half-moon's light thinned toward morning—and far to the south, past the pass they had not reached, the spires of the capital waited under a sky already gathering its thirteenth turn.

Three days. Maybe four.

The mountain, ashamed or not, had better open soon.
