## 21. The Impostor Speaks

Sleep did not come easily to Noor anymore, and when it came, it came like a smuggler—quietly, through a window she had forgotten to bar.

That night it found her in the shepherd's shrine, wrapped in her cloak beneath the leaning cairn, with Wren breathing slow and even an arm's length away and the old god underneath them sleeping its honest sleep. She had thought that would protect her. A holy ground, however humble; a god, however small and true. She had thought the impostor's voice could not follow her here, into the hollow of a thing that had never lied.

She was wrong.

She dreamed of the road first—the road out of Hollowmere in autumn, gold leaves guttering in the ditches, her own boots printing the frost. It was a road she knew so well she could have walked it blind: seventeen turns to the ford, a split oak at the eighth, a badger sett under the ninth whose inhabitants would growl but never bite. Her feet found every stone without looking.

Then the road changed.

Not dramatically. That was what made her stomach turn over, even in dream. The stones were the same stones. The oak was the same oak. But the *wanting* was gone from it. The path lay there flat and mute as any ordinary track, a scar on the earth and nothing more, and when Noor reached out with the sense she had carried since before she had a name for it—the sense that told her which ways wished to be walked and which wished to be left alone—the road said nothing back.

It was like putting her ear to a lover's chest and hearing no heartbeat.

"You feel it already," said a voice behind her. "Good. I wanted you to understand the terms before we spoke of anything else."

She turned. The figure standing on the road wore Maghda's face.

Not quite. Not the way it had worn Maghda's voice in Hollowmere, that night by the blooming orchard, when Noor had prayed deliberately, testing, and something had answered with her mentor's stolen timbre. This was better made and worse. Maghda stood before her as Noor remembered her from girlhood—broad-shouldered, iron-gray braid, hands like root-balls—with every detail correct and none of them alive. The eyes were lamps painted to look like eyes. The mouth moved around words the way a puppet's mouth moves, from outside.

"You're not even trying anymore," Noor said. Her voice shook. She hated that it shook. "You've stopped pretending to be anyone."

"Would you prefer I tried?" The Maghda-thing tilted its head. "I have worn ten thousand faces, little listener. Kings and grandmothers and drowned girls. I can wear your mother, if you remember her. I can wear the boy who sold you the map. But you asked me once—you screamed it at me over a dead orchard—*show me your true face.* So."

It spread its hands. And for one heartbeat the shape of Maghda sloughed away like wet ash, and Noor saw—

She saw nothing she could keep. Something vast and patient and starving, older than the mountains it had been poured into, wearing its hunger the way other things wore skin. Then it was Maghda again, because even a monster knows not to show a knife to the hand it wants.

"Why my teacher?" Noor asked quietly. "Of all the voices in the world."

"Because you fled her." The false-Maghda's painted eyes held hers. "Guilt is a door, Noor Thornbury. You left it unlocked the day you walked out of that cottage. I have only ever had to knock."

The dream-road stretched away in both directions now, impossibly long, running through country she knew and country she didn't, and Noor understood suddenly that this was no ordinary dream-space. This was a road itself. The thing had built this place out of the only material it had learned to love: paths. Ways between. The binding that held it was a kind of road—a channel from crown to vessel—and roads were the one language Noor spoke fluently.

It had come to her in her mother tongue. That frightened her more than the hunger had.

"Say what you came to say," she said.

* * *

The Maghda-shape began to walk, and the road walked with it, unspooling. Noor fell into step beside it because some traitor part of her had spent nine years falling into step beside her teacher, and the body remembered what the heart had tried to bury.

"You know what I am now," the thing said. "The cartographer has guessed part of it. You have guessed more. So let us waste no time on denials." It glanced sideways at her, and for a moment the lamp-eyes flickered with something almost like respect. "I am bound into the throne of this kingdom. I have been bound for four hundred years. Every king since Aldric's great-grandsire has carried me the way a wound carries rot, and fed me whether he wished to or not, because that is what crowns are for. Devotion pours uphill, little listener. It always has."

"And Aldric."

"Aldric is the last course of a very long meal." It said it without relish, which was somehow worse than gloating. "The twelfth moon is behind us. When the thirteenth rises full at midwinter, the binding will close past opening, and whatever remains of the man will be mine entirely—not a prisoner anymore, but a room I live in. A house I own." It paused at the split oak, laid a root-ball hand against the bark, and Noor flinched because Maghda had done exactly that, ten thousand times, checking the health of a tree. "Unless, of course, someone unbinds me first. Which brings me to business."

"I don't bargain with parasites."

"No," it agreed mildly. "You bargain with liars. There's a difference, and you've always known it, because you can hear a lie in the ground itself. So here is my honesty, offered freely, since honesty costs me nothing and you are the one creature in this kingdom who can verify it." It turned to face her fully, and the whole dreaming road seemed to lean in. "Serve me willingly. Come to the capital, kneel at the cathedral, and give me your gift freely given instead of torn from you screaming. In exchange I will do two things."

The road shimmered. And Noor felt it come back—that low, warm, subterranean hum of a path that *wanted*—flooding up through the dream-soles of her feet like water into a dry field. Every road in the world woke up around her at once. Smuggler tracks and pilgrim ways, deer trails and dead kings' highways, all of them singing their small songs of desire and direction, and Noor stood in the middle of the chorus with tears freezing on her cheeks before she realized she was crying.

"First," the thing said softly, in Maghda's gentlest register, the voice that had talked her through fever nights and broken bones, "your roads. All of them. Not the memory of them—the *hearing*. I will widen it instead of sealing it. You will feel every way that wants walking from the Thornfells to the sea, forever, and no one—not Maghda, not the crown, not I—will ever make you deaf again."

"And second?"

"Second, I spare him." The lamp-eyes did not waver, and Noor listened with everything she had, the way she had listened to orchards and rivers and the ground under graves, and she heard—this was the horror of it, this was the thing that would sit in her chest for days—she heard *truth*. Clean truth, ringing like struck iron. "When you come to me willingly, the thirteenth moon need not close the binding past opening. I will release Aldric's self whole and unharmed. He will wake mortal and diminished and remembering, which is more than your rite can promise him, and more than your rite can promise *you*." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between two plates. "Yes. I know about Maghda's rite. I know what it eats. Shall I tell you what it will cost you, Noor Thornbury? Or would you rather find out midwinter, with salt on your tongue and your teacher's daughter's hands shaking?"

"There's no such title."

"There is in your heart. I've read it." The thing smiled with Maghda's mouth, and the smile was almost kind. "Your rite pays in memory. Everything you ever memorized—every road, every face attached to those roads, every village those roads led to. You would pay Hollowmere. You would pay the grandmother whose bread you'll never taste again. You might well pay the cartographer, who has been a map longer than she has been a woman." It leaned closer, and its breath smelled of cold stone and incense, cathedral-smell, throne-smell. "I am offering you the same miracle without the price. That is all. That is the entire trick, if you want to call it a trick: I simply refuse to lie to the one person who'd catch me."

Noor stood very still on the singing road and made herself think.

That was the trap inside the trap, she knew. The offer was real. She could hear that it was real, the way she could hear water under limestone. The thing meant what it said, because meaning it cost it nothing—because a willing servant was worth more than a spared king, worth more than all the roads in the world, because devotion freely given was the one coin it could never mint for itself. Four hundred years of being fed by prisoners. Of course it was hungry for a volunteer. Of course it would empty half its treasury for one.

And the alternative was the rite. The rite that ate memory. She looked down the dream-road and saw it honestly for the first time: herself at midwinter, salt and iron, Maghda's words in her mouth, and then—waking. Waking to a blank map where her life had been. Waking to a stranger with gray eyes holding her hand and knowing, from the pages in that stranger's coat, that this stranger had slept three inches from her for thirteen weeks, had argued with her and starved with her and laughed twice, badly, at nothing—and feeling nothing. Nothing at all. A king restored and a cartographer grieving for a woman still warm.

"That's the shape of your choice," the Maghda-thing said, reading her exactly, because it had read everyone exactly for four hundred years and she was no harder than most. "Serve me, and keep everything. Refuse me, and save him by unmaking yourself. I will even sweeten it further: refuse me, fail, and I will take your hearing anyway. At least this way you choose."

"Maghda used to say something," Noor said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "About gifts. About how a gift you sell stops being one."

"Maghda sealed a child's ears shut and called it protection," the thing answered, without heat. "Do not lecture me on the corruption of gifts using her as your saint. She burned her own sight out doing to herself what she did to you, and she'd have died in that cottage unremembered if I hadn't—" It stopped. The painted eyes flickered, and Noor saw, with a lurch, the first crack in all that perfect confidence.

"If you hadn't what?"

"Nothing that concerns your choice."

"You mentioned her burning out." Noor took a step forward, onto ground that sang, and hated that she needed its song under her feet even now. "You said *if you hadn't*. If you hadn't *what*?"

The Maghda-shape regarded her for a long moment. And then, because it could not lie to her, because that was the whole architecture of this ambush, it told her.

"She prayed," it said. "Three nights ago, in the cottage, alone, with her sight gone and her hands too ruined for the rite. She prayed to whatever would listen—for a road for you that didn't end where hers ended. And I was listening, because I listen to everything now, and I answered her the only honest way I could: I have been leaving you open roads ever since. The ford that wasn't watched. The pass the Devoted rode past in the fog. Every kindness of terrain you've enjoyed since Hollowmere, little listener—check them, when you wake. Check how lucky they were." It spread Maghda's broad hands. "Your teacher bargains with me. Your king feeds me. Your Tam prays to me. The whole world negotiates with the thing it hates, because the thing it hates keeps its word." A pause. "Will you be the first creature in four hundred years who doesn't?"

The road hummed. Somewhere far off, under the earth of the dream, Noor could feel the cairn—the real cairn, the honest sleeper beneath the shrine—and she fixed her attention on it like a star. Small. Dim. True. It did not sing. It only breathed, the way old stone breathes, and it wanted nothing from her at all.

That was how she knew her answer. Not by weighing the offers—all four hundred years of the thing's cunning had made the offers unweighable—but by remembering what wanting sounded like when it wasn't hunger.

"No," she said.

* * *

The road did not vanish. That was its final cruelty and its final honesty: the dream stayed exactly as it was, every path still singing, the bargain still standing, offered and open like a door held politely ajar.

"Think carefully," the Maghda-thing said. "Midwinter is nineteen days off."

"I know. I can count moons. My teacher taught me that too—along with the rite you're so worried about." She watched the painted eyes go still. "Oh, yes. She gave it to me. Before the end. Every word, every price, written fair in my own hand so I couldn't pretend I hadn't heard it. You should have come to me a month ago, when I still would have sold my soul to stop being afraid." Noor smiled, and it felt strange on her face, fierce and unfamiliar, like a key fitting a lock she hadn't known she carried. "Now I'm only afraid of keeping everything. So my answer is no. Not today, not at midwinter, not kneeling, not ever. Find another volunteer."

For a moment—one long, cold moment—the road went silent. Every path. The whole world's wanting, snuffed like candles, and Noor stood deaf in her own element while the thing that wore her teacher considered her.

Then the singing returned, warmer than before, sweeter, coaxing, and the Maghda-shape's voice followed her down into waking like a hand at her back.

"Nineteen days," it said gently. "And little listener—when you stand at the stone with salt on your tongue, and the memories start going, remember that I offered you the door. Remember which of us told you the truth."

* * *

She woke gasping in the dark under the cairn, hand already reaching for the salt at her throat, and found Wren sitting up beside her with a tinderbox already striking, because of course Wren had felt her thrash. Gray eyes, sharp with sleeplessness and fear, in the first spit of flame.

"Nightmare?"

"Worse." Noor's voice came out scraped raw. "A negotiation."

Wren went very still. Twenty years at court had taught her that word in ways Noor was only beginning to guess at. "What did it want?"

"The same thing it always wants. Me, willing." Noor pulled her cloak tighter and looked toward the shrine's low doorway, where the pre-dawn showed thin and colorless, nineteen days from midwinter. "And here's the thing, Wren—it told the truth. Every word. I checked it the way I check everything, and there wasn't a single lie in it anywhere." She swallowed. "It said my roads would sing again if I knelt. It said it would let Aldric go. And it said—" her voice caught, and she forced it through, "it said the rite will take Hollowmere from me. Take you. Everything the roads touched."

The tinderbox flared and died in Wren's fingers. In the new darkness, Noor heard her exhale, long and controlled, the sound of a woman rearranging her entire understanding of the war.

"Then we have nineteen days," Wren said at last, "to teach you my face so thoroughly it can't be taken."

But Noor was already reaching for her boots, because she had heard something else in the waking dark—something under the ground, under the cairn, faint and urgent and utterly unlike the impostor's silver tongue. The old god beneath the shrine, the honest sleeper, had opened one ancient eye.

And it was calling her name.
