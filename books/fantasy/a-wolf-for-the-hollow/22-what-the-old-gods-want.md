## 22. What the Old Gods Want

The cairn did not dream the way the living dreamed.

It had no eyes to close, no breath to slow, no heart whose rhythm could carry it down into the dark water of sleep. What it had instead was weight—the long patient weight of forty-seven stones stacked by hands that had been dust for nine hundred years—and within that weight, something that still remembered being vast.

It dreamed in seasons. That was all the dreaming left to it. In autumn it dreamed of acorns; in winter, of the hush beneath snow; in spring, of the first wet green smell of thawed earth; in summer, of the drone of bees in a heather field that had been a heather field when its name was spoken aloud and was a heather field still, though no one alive knew what to call it or why calling it had ever mattered.

But tonight—on the mountain where Noor Thornbury and the cartographer Wren had slept one night under its stones, fed it salt and barley, and gone on toward the capital—it dreamed of a girl.

Not Noor. Another girl, another century. A girl with ash on her cheeks who had climbed this same slope with a lamb too sick to live and had knelt at the cairn and said, *Old One, take what's left of her gently,* and the thing beneath the stones had reached up through the roots of the mountain with everything it had—which even then was not much—and had eased the lamb's passing until it felt less like dying and more like being set down after a long carrying.

That was what worship had been, once. Not kneeling. Not incense. Not the great gold throat of a cathedral swallowing prayers whole. It had been a girl with ash on her cheeks asking for gentleness on behalf of something small, and something old answering because it could, because answering was what it *was*.

The cairn held the memory the way it held heat from the summer sun: imperfectly, briefly, against all sense.

And then, as it always did now, the dream curdled into the other thing. The presence on the throne. The crowned hunger. The voice that had once been a brother of sorts—or an enemy of sorts; the distinction had stopped mattering centuries before the first king learned to stack stones—and that now wore a man named Aldric like a coat worn inside out, seams showing, lining torn, the wearer's arms poking through holes that should not have existed.

*You are dreaming again,* said the crowned thing, and its voice arrived not through air but through the binding itself, through the web of half-life that connected every sleeping god to every stolen altar, the way pain connects teeth. *I can feel you dreaming. Stop it.*

The cairn did not answer. Answering cost strength, and its strength was a candle-end measured against the impostor's bonfire of devotion. Ten thousand voices prayed to the crown each day in Hollowmere and the hollow villages beyond it, in the river towns, in the capital itself, and every prayer was a ladle dipped into the impostor's endless pot, and every ladleful was taken from somewhere else—from the orchard gods gone gray, from the ford-spirits silted silent, from the small honest things like itself that had once kept the edges of the world.

*You could have this too,* the crowned thing said, and there was almost tenderness in it, which was the most frightening thing about it. Tenderness was a tool it had learned from the priests it had eaten. *I remember what you were. Shepherd of the high passes. Keeper of the gentle deaths. I could give you back your name. There are shepherds on this very mountain who would kneel if someone told them to. I would tell them to.*

Beneath the stones, the old god turned the offer over the way it had turned it over a hundred times before, and found, as always, the same answer waiting at the bottom of it like a stone at the bottom of a well.

It did not want the name back.

That was the truth none of the living understood—not the Devoted in their white robes, not the frightened villagers lighting candles, not even the girl Noor with her gift for hearing the land's true voice, though she had come closer than any soul in centuries. They all believed the war was about worship. That the old gods wanted their altars rebuilt, their festivals restored, their names spoken in the market squares again. That if the impostor were cast down, some golden age of answered prayer would bloom back up like the dead orchard in Hollowmere had bloomed, overnight and impossibly.

But the dead orchard had been a lie twice over. Once because the blooming was theft. And once because no god had wanted it.

What the old gods wanted—the ones that remained, the cairn and the shrine-well at Marrow Ford and the birch-thing in the north woods and the handful of others scattered through the mountains like coins dropped between floorboards—was simpler and far more terrible.

They wanted to die.

Properly. Completely. With their names spoken one last time by someone who meant it, and then let go, the way the lamb had been let go, the way the girl with ash on her cheeks had eventually been let go, the way everything that lives is owed its ending. Half-life was not life. Half-life was a wound that had forgotten how to close. For nine hundred years—since the first kings, hungry for order, had bound the wild divinities into service, chaining them to stones and wells and crowns so that their power could be harvested like any other crop—the old gods had endured a condition worse than death: usefulness without consent. Existence as livestock.

The impostor was different only in degree. It had been bound too—that was the joke buried so deep beneath the cathedral that even the kings who profited from it had forgotten it was a joke. The thing on the throne had not crawled up out of hell to steal a kingdom. It had been *made* into what it was, bound harder and deeper than any of its kin, fused to the line of kings generation after generation until the binding and the wearing had warped it past recognition. It had begun, perhaps, as something not unlike the cairn itself. A keeper of thresholds. A god of endings.

Now it was an ending that refused to end, and it had grown so hungry in its refusal that it had begun devouring the very concept of ending—devouring death itself, offering the people false permanence, false bloom, false mercy, anything rather than the one gift the old gods still had to give, which was the permission to be finished.

*I know what you want,* the crowned thing said, and now the tenderness was gone, and underneath it was something rawer. Something that might, in a kinder telling, have been called grief. *You want me to stop. You want the long dark. You think I don't understand the longing? I invented the longing. I have been dying since before your mountain had a name, and I cannot finish, and every year the binding tightens and the finishing gets further away, and so I eat, because eating is the only motion left to me. Do you imagine I chose this?*

The cairn considered lying. It had little enough strength for speech, and lies cost more than truths.

*No,* it said at last, and the word moved through the mountain's root-system like a shudder through a felled tree. *You did not choose it. Neither did I. That is the oldest truth in this land, older than the bindings, older than the kings: nothing that suffers chose its suffering. But you have made a thousand thousand others suffer in your place. The lamb did not choose. The girl with ash on her cheeks did not choose. The man you wear—he did not choose. Whatever you were, brother, whatever they made you into, the making does not wash your hands. Only the stopping will do that.*

Silence, then. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the dreamer like snow on a roof.

*They are coming for me,* the crowned thing said finally. *The girl who hears, and the woman with the maps. I felt them pass beneath your stones. I felt you give the girl something—a fragment, a truth. Why? Why help them end me, when ending me ends you too? When the crown falls silent, the last of the bindings loosens. We will all go out together, shepherd. Every light still burning in the deep places. Is that what you want?*

And here the cairn did something it had not done in nine hundred years.

It laughed.

There was no throat to laugh with, no breath, no sound. The laughter was tectonic, a shifting of intention through stone, and if any living creature had stood upon the cairn in that moment they would have felt it in the soles of their feet and fled, or wept, or both. Because the question revealed everything—the impostor's final blindness, the one delusion that ten thousand daily prayers had polished bright and true.

*Yes,* the old god said. *Yes, that is exactly what I want. All of us, going out together, into the long dark, with our names spoken and our debts paid and our watch ended. You ask it as though it were a threat. Brother. Beloved ruined thing. It is the only mercy anyone has offered this land in nine hundred years.*

*The people need us,* the crowned thing whispered, and it sounded suddenly young, suddenly small, suddenly like whatever it had been before the first king came to it with chains and scripture and a bargain dressed as honor. *The winters are hard. The children die of fevers. If we go, they will be alone with it. Alone with the dying and the cold and the dark. How can you call that mercy?*

*Because they were never alone with us,* the cairn said. *That is what you never understood, even before the binding. They knelt to us and we answered, yes—but the answering was never the point. The point was that they asked. The point was the girl climbing the mountain with her sick lamb, choosing to carry it up through the snow rather than let it die alone in the fold. We did not save them from the dark, brother. We taught them how to walk in it. And a people who know how to walk in the dark need no gods. They need only each other, and the memory that the dark is not empty—it is only resting.*

The wind moved over the mountain. Somewhere below, in the valley, a dog barked at nothing, or at everything, or at the faint tectonic laughter still settling through the stone.

*And if they forget?* the crowned thing asked. *If the bindings loosen and the memories thin and in three generations no one remembers that the hollow places were ever tended at all?*

*Then they will learn again,* the cairn said. *Someone always learns again. Look at the girl. She was never taught—her teacher tried to bind her gift shut, poor frightened woman—and still she hears. Still she walks the paths that want to be walked. The land raises up its keepers, brother. It always has. It is doing it right now, while we speak. That is what you are truly afraid of, isn't it? Not that they will forget us. That they will not need us. That they never needed us, not the way you needed them, not the way you need them now, ladle by ladle, prayer by prayer, a hunger so old and so vast that you have mistaken it for love.*

This time the silence went on so long that the cairn wondered whether the connection had broken, whether the impostor had withdrawn into its gilded hunger as it usually did, leaving the mountain to its seasonal dreams.

But no. When the voice came again it was quieter than the cairn had ever heard it. Quieter than threats. Quieter than bargains. It sounded, for one suspended moment, like a man.

*There is a moment,* it said, *each night, when the king surfaces. He is nearly gone now—the twelfth moon is rising, and after the twelfth there is only the thirteenth, and after the thirteenth there is nothing of him left to surface. But in his last clear hour he writes. He has always written. Letters, mostly. To a woman who used to draw maps. He thinks I cannot read them. He hides them in the margins of his own decrees, in cipher, in the old script the priests forgot. He is telling them how to reach the stone. He is telling them to run, actually—telling them not to spend themselves on him. He is braver than I ever was, and I am going to unmake him, and there is nothing in me left that can grieve it properly.*

The cairn waited. It had waited nine hundred years; it could wait out one more silence.

*Why are you telling me this?* it asked at last.

*Because you asked me why I suffer,* the crowned thing said, *and no one has asked me that since the binding, and the answer is that I suffer because part of me is still what I was, and that part watches, and that part remembers the girl with the lamb, and that part knows that what I am doing to the man in the crown is what the first kings did to me. I am telling you because when they come down beneath the cathedral—and they will come, the girl will not be warned off, I have seen her kind before—I want one of my kin to know that the thing they kill will not be entirely sorry. Tell your keeper-girl, if she listens to you again. Tell her the enemy was made, not born. Tell her that when the moment comes, the killing stroke and the mercy stroke are the same stroke, and that the thing beneath the crown will meet it with something she does not expect.*

*Which is?*

The pause stretched across the whole web of half-life, from the mountain cairn to the silted well at Marrow Ford to the gray birch-thing in the north, and every sleeping god felt it pass through them like a season changing.

*Gratitude,* said the crowned thing. *Now let me dream, shepherd. Even damned things dream. It is the last honest thing we have left.*

The presence withdrew, folding itself back into the distant gold of the capital like a tide going out, and the mountain was alone with its weight again, forty-seven stones and the long patience beneath them.

The cairn settled into its seasonal dark. But it did not dream of acorns, or snow-hush, or bees. It sent what remained of its strength outward instead, down through root and rock and running groundwater, following the thread of the girl who heard—the runaway, the hearer, the keeper already forming herself somewhere on the road to the capital—and it pressed into her sleeping mind, as gently as it had once eased a dying lamb, a single image:

A stone, deep beneath a city, older than any crown.

And around it, chains. And around the chains, a shape that had once been free.

And written into the stone itself, in a script older than kings, half worn away but still legible to anyone who knew how to listen to what the land remembered:

*We were not made to be worshipped. We were made to end things well.*

Let her carry that down into the dark beneath the cathedral, the old god thought, letting itself sink at last toward the honest edge of its own long sleep. Let her know what she is really being asked to do—not to win a war, but to preside over a funeral nine centuries overdue. Let her understand that the impostor on the throne is the last prisoner of the oldest crime, and that the sword and the key are the same object, and that when she strikes, she must strike the way the girl with ash on her cheeks once asked her to strike:

Gently.

Take what's left of him gently.

Far away, in a smuggler's camp on the road to the capital, Noor Thornbury woke in the black hour before dawn with tears on her face and a sentence in her mouth that she did not remember learning, in a language she had never been taught, and beside her Wren stirred and murmured, *Bad dream?* and Noor looked east toward the distant smudge of the capital's lights and said, in a voice that did not feel entirely her own:

*No. It's a message. And I think we're almost out of time to read it.*
