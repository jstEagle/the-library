## 24. Blood on the Threshing Floor

The safehouse was a granary once, before the Devoted took the quarter and the faithful went underground like roots. Noor had learned its shape in the dark: the threshing floor worn smooth by a hundred years of flails, the loft where the grain had been kept, now stacked with pallets and smuggled salt. Forty souls slept there on the night the raid came, and Noor knew every one of their names, because she had made herself learn them. Names were a kind of road, Maghda had said once, in a life that felt like someone else's dream. Learn the names and you will never be lost among people, even when you are lost in yourself.

Sister Aveline. The cooper's widow, Marta. The twins, Ivo and Ilsa, who had never seen the sun rise over anything but a cellar stair. Old Bren with his ruined hands. The boy Tam had once been, before the robes — no, that was not right, Tam was above now, in the cathedral quarter, wearing white. Noor pushed the thought away the way she pushed a splinter out of skin.

She had been three days in the granary, teaching the underground faithful what she could: how to hear the difference between a prayer that rose and a prayer that was *taken*. How to salt a threshold. How to keep silence so complete that even the land forgot you were there. She was good at that last one. She had practiced it her whole life.

The raid came an hour before dawn, when the body's warmth is lowest and the will is thinnest.

Noor woke not to sound but to the land screaming. That was the only word for it — the stones beneath the granary shrieked in a register only she could hear, the vibration of boots, many boots, and beneath the boots the wet dragging weight of the Devoted's censers, swinging their smoke that was not smoke. She was on her feet before her eyes opened.

"Up," she said, and her voice came out wrong, flat as a blade laid on a table. "Up. Out. The river side — *now*."

They moved the way frightened people move, which is to say badly. The loft ladder jammed with bodies. Marta dropped a bundle of their salt stores and could not stop to pick it up. Ivo, the twin, began to cry and Ilsa clamped her hand over her brother's mouth and dragged him, and Noor heard all of it the way she heard everything now, too much, too clearly, the land reporting every footfall and heartbeat like a servant too eager to please.

The front doors came in with a sound like the world's spine breaking.

White robes. Censers. And behind them, in the doorway, the light of the thing they served — not torchlight, nothing so honest. It was the light of the cathedral at vigil, gold without warmth, the light Noor had seen pour from King Aldric's hollow eyes. The Devoted did not shout. That was the worst of it. They came in singing, low and sweet, a hymn Noor had heard drifting from the capital's spires for weeks, and the hymn did something to the air, made it thick, made thought move through it like a swimmer through honey.

"*The crowned one keeps us, the crowned one knows us, none who kneel are lost—*"

"Cellar," Noor said. "The old well shaft. Go, all of you, I'll hold the floor."

She did not know, even as she said it, what holding the floor meant. She only knew that her hands had already found the iron rod she kept at her belt — Maghda's iron, cold-forged, and the salt in the pouch beside it — and that the land was telling her, urgently, intimately, exactly where every one of the twelve Devoted stood. She could feel their weight through the threshing floor. She could feel the hollow places in them, the places where the god's smoke had eaten something out and filled it with gold.

Sister Aveline did not run. That was the thing Noor would carry afterward, the thing that would wake her for years: Aveline, seventy years old, who had taught half the underground faithful their prayers before the prayers turned rotten, stood in front of the twins with her arms out, and said, "You shall not have the children," in the voice she had used for decades to bless marriages and name babies, and a Devoted with a young face and an old emptiness behind it put a knife into her.

Noor heard it. She heard it the way she heard everything — the parting of cloth, the wet give, the small human sound Aveline made, the twins screaming — and something in Noor went down past fear, past grief, into a cold country she had never visited and found, horribly, that she knew the language.

The hymn faltered. The Devoted in front — the one with the knife, the young face — looked down at Aveline, and for one heartbeat something crossed his emptiness like a cloud crossing a frozen lake, doubt or horror or the ghost of the man he had been. Noor felt that heartbeat in the floorboards. She felt the way his weight shifted, the heel lifting, the balance going to his back foot. The land told her everything. The land was a traitor and a gift and she hated it and used it in the same breath.

She threw the salt.

Not a handful. The whole pouch, the stores meant to ward a hundred thresholds, flung in a wide fan with the iron rod's edge cutting through it, and Maghda's old craft did what old craft does: the salt found the hollow places and *burned*. The Devoted screamed — the first honest sound anyone had made — and the hymn broke apart, and the gold light in the doorway guttered like a candle in wind, and somewhere far above, Noor knew, the thing wearing the crown felt its fingers go numb.

She should have run then. She knew that too, in the cold country, the way you know the shape of a road you will never walk again. Instead she crossed the threshing floor over Aveline's body and put the iron rod against the young-faced Devoted's chest, where the salt had eaten through the white robe, and she said, in a voice that did not sound like hers, "Where does it hurt?"

He looked up at her. He was crying. "Everywhere," he said. "It hurts everywhere, it's been hurting everywhere since I was chosen, please—"

She pressed harder. The iron hissed against the salt-burned skin.

"*Good*," Noor said.

* * *

Wren found her there.

Later, Noor would try to reconstruct how long she stood on the threshing floor with the iron in her hands while the granary burned around her — because the Devoted, broken, had fired the grain stores as they fled, and fire was eating the old timbers with a hunger that felt almost personal. She would try to remember how many of the faithful she had gotten out and how many she had not, and the arithmetic would not come, because the cold country did not do arithmetic. It only did targets. The land had told her where the remaining Devoted were — three of them, fleeing toward the river — and Noor had been following, salt in one fist, iron in the other, walking through falling embers like a woman walking through snow, when Wren stepped out of the smoke and caught her by the arm.

"Noor."

"They're going to the ford." Her voice was calm. That was the frightening thing, she understood dimly, the thing she would examine later in the dark with Wren's heartbeat under her ear: the calm. "The land will drop them at the ford. I can feel the bank. It's rotten, it's been rotten since the spring floods, I can make it take them, I can—"

"Noor." Wren's hands were on her face now, sooty and shaking, and Wren's eyes were the grey of a map before the ink goes down, all possibility, all decision. "Aveline is dead. Marta is dead. Bren got the twins out. You got *twenty-eight people* out of a burning building and broke a raid of the god's own chosen with a pouch of salt, and now you want to drown three men in a river."

"They killed her." The calm cracked, just at the edge, like ice at a shoreline. "She put her arms out and he — she had taught *children* to pray, Wren, she was the one who—"

"I know who she was." Wren's voice broke on it, and Noor saw that Wren was crying too, had been crying, the tears cutting clean lines through the soot. "I knew her for six weeks and she called me *child* and I loved her, so don't you dare stand there and tell me what she was worth. Don't you *dare* spend her like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a reason." Wren's grip tightened. "That thing in the crown wants you cold, Noor. Do you understand that? That's the whole of its strategy, the whole of what it has done to Aldric — it takes the love in a person and it freezes it into *purpose*. It turns devotion into a weapon and hands the weapon to the devoted and lets them do the breaking. You are standing in a burning building holding iron over a man's heart and telling yourself it's justice, and it isn't. It's the hymn. It's the hymn in *your* mouth."

The words went in like a splinter going in deep, past the cold, into the living part.

Noor looked down at her hands. The iron rod was stained. The salt had eaten through her glove and the skin beneath was blistered, and she had not felt it, had not felt anything, and the land was still murmuring to her — *the three at the ford, the rotten bank, so easy, they are heavy and the water is cold and it would take so little* — and she understood, all at once, with the clarity that only arrives after the worst has already happened, that the land had not been screaming in warning at all.

The land had been *offering*.

"No," she said. Not to Wren. To the ground, to the stones, to the old listening dark beneath the granary. "No. You don't get to — you're not *his*. You're not the crown's. You're the land, you're the honest dark, you're the roads I learned when I was nine years old, and I will not — I *will not*—"

The murmuring stopped. In its place came something older and quieter, a feeling like a hand lifted off her shoulder, like a debt forgiven. The land did not apologize. The land was not capable of apology. But it withdrew, the way a dog withdraws when it has misjudged its master's grief, and Noor stood in the ash-fall and wept for the first time since Aveline fell, great ugly sobs that bent her double, and Wren held her, and the granary burned, and the three Devoted at the ford lived to see the sun rise, which Noor would never be entirely sure was the right thing, only the thing that kept her from becoming the thing she was trying to kill.

* * *

They buried what they could bury before dawn's light made the quarter too dangerous. There was not much to bury. Fire takes its tithe. They laid Aveline's remains — a scrap of robe, a shoe, the iron ring she had worn for fifty years — in the cellar of a tanner's shop whose owner had fled to the Thornfells two moons past, and the underground faithful stood in a row with their hoods up, and no one sang, because the god had taken the hymns and poisoned them, and no one spoke, because grief that deep goes past speech.

It was Old Bren who finally broke the silence, his ruined hands folded. "She'd have hated this," he said. "All this quiet. Aveline believed in noise. Singing off-key. Arguing about scripture. She'd have hated us standing here like her own funeral was a smuggler's errand."

A wet, broken laugh went down the line, and after that they could speak, and they said her name, and the names of Marta and the two others Noor still could not count, and the names did what names do. Made roads. Kept them from being lost.

Wren stood apart, sketching. Noor watched her do it — watched the exiled cartographer's hands move over a scrap of paper, mapping the granary's ruin, the Devoted's approach routes, the timing of the raid, because that was how Wren grieved, by making the disaster legible, by drawing a border around it so it could not spread. When she finished, she folded the paper into Noor's hand.

"Twelve Devoted came," Wren said quietly. "Three fled. Six are accounted dead or scattered. That leaves three unaccounted, which means the report reaches the cathedral by tonight, and the report will say what the god will want it to say: that a hearth-witch broke the hymn with salt and iron and *enjoyed* it." She met Noor's eyes. "You've stopped being a rumor, Noor. You're a name now. It will have your name."

"Let it," Noor said, and heard the cold still in her own voice, and hated it.

"No," Wren said. "Don't *let* it. That's the trap. It wants you to be the wolf at the door, the terror, the thing the faithful need saving from as much as the Devoted. Every cold thing you do makes its story truer." She took Noor's blistered hand — the burned one, the one Noor had been hiding — and turned it over, and looked at the salt-burn without flinching. "The rite Maghda left you. The unbinding. It needs a whole heart, doesn't it? That's what the warning said. Not a clean one. A *whole* one."

Noor looked at her. "How did you know the warning said that?"

"Because I read your pages while you slept." Wren said it without shame. "Because I love you, and love is not always honorable, and because I needed to know what we're walking into. And the rite needs a whole heart, Noor. Grief and all. The cold isn't protection. The cold is just the binding, small-scale, the thing we're trying to break, growing in the one person who can break it."

The words settled over Noor like ash. She looked at the tanner's cellar, at the scrap of white robe they had folded into Aveline's grave, at the row of hooded faithful already slipping away into the dark to carry the news to the other safehouses, and she felt the cold country waiting at the edge of her, patient as winter, and she understood what Wren was really saying: *Come back. Come back from there. I cannot do the next part without you whole.*

"I'm here," Noor said. It was not entirely true yet. She said it anyway, the way you plant a seed in frozen ground, on faith. "I'm here."

Wren pressed her forehead to Noor's, soot and salt and tears between them. "Then we have six days," she said. "The twelfth full moon rises in six nights. After that, the binding tightens past any rite's reach, and Aldric — whatever is left of Aldric — goes under for good. Six days to get under the cathedral."

Noor closed her burned hand around the folded map, around the names, around the whole ragged human weight of what remained.

Above them, through the tanner's broken roof, the sky was turning grey toward morning, and Noor could feel the moon coming — not risen yet, but *gathering*, the way a wave gathers — and beneath it, far to the north, the crowned thing in the cathedral turned its hollow eyes toward the south and began, softly, to sing her name.
