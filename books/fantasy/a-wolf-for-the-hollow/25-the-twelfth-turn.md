## 25. The Twelfth Turn

The moon came up over the capital like a coin dropped into a well, and Aldric felt it fall through him.

He stood at the window of the king's chamber — *his* chamber, though he could no longer remember choosing it, only that he had always been here, or had been here for so long that *always* and *here* had grown together like roots through stone — and he watched the light of it slide across the floor toward his feet. Silver. Cold. Patient.

*Twelve,* said the thing inside him, in no voice at all, in the voice of his own blood. *One more.*

Aldric had learned, over these long months, to think of himself as a note. It was not a metaphor he would have chosen; it was simply the truest shape the truth would take. Once he had been a chord — a man was many things sounding at once: grief and appetite and humor and shame and the small stubborn love of rain on hot stone. Now the binding drew all those tones out of him one by one, tightening, tuning, until what remained was a single held note, pure and thin and perfectly obedient.

He did not know what note it was. He suspected it was not a beautiful one.

But tonight — tonight the moon was full and the binding flexed, and in the space of its flexing there was a crack, and through the crack came *him*. Not much of him. An hour, perhaps. The thing that wore his crown grew careless on the twelfth turn, sated with the day's devotion, ten thousand prayers settling into it like grain into a silo, and while it digested the love of a kingdom, Aldric got to be a man for a little while.

He had learned not to waste the hour weeping. There would be time for weeping later, or there would not; either way, weeping spent the hour and bought nothing back.

He went to the desk.

* * *

The candles were already lit. They were always already lit. That was one of the small horrors he had stopped cataloguing years ago — no, months ago, how long? — the way the palace arranged itself around the thing in his skin, anticipating wants he had not yet had. The ink was fresh. The pen was mended. A clean sheet of paper lay squared to the desk's edge as if the room itself were saying, *write, write*, because the thing liked him to write. His decrees were its hymns. Every edict that left this desk went out stamped with his seal and signed with his hand, and the kingdom read them as scripture, and somewhere beneath the gold leaf of every one of them, if anyone cared to look, a drowning man was leaving messages.

Wren had cared to look. He did not know how he knew that she had, but he knew it the way he knew the weight of his own hands. Some knowledge survived the tuning. She had loved him once — before the crowning, before the maps, before whatever she had done or failed to do that sent her into exile and him onto the throne where the old crime waited under the coronation stone like a trapdoor. He remembered her laugh. He remembered it specifically, which frightened him, because so little else came back whole. Her laugh arrived in his memory like an artifact from a drowned city, intact when everything around it was silt.

He dipped the pen.

*To whoever finds this,* he wrote, and then stopped, because the candle flame leaned sideways though there was no draft, and he felt the attention of the other thing brush past him like a large animal moving in a dark room. It was checking. It always checked, on the first words. Then it settled, satisfied, and he understood why: it did not fear his writing. It *wanted* his writing. To the thing, his handwriting was just another instrument being played. It could not imagine that a man might hide truth in the margins of his own captivity, in cipher, in the spaces between the lines where devotion never thought to look.

It could not imagine it because it had eaten everyone who might have taught it caution.

Aldric wrote quickly now. The hour was burning.

*If you are reading this and you are not Wren, put it down. If you are Wren — I don't know if you're alive. I don't know if you're free. But the bell-ringers' code is still the same, isn't it? You taught me the first year, laughing at me because I kept confusing the third and fourth changes. Nine strokes means the river gate. Eleven means do not come by the river gate. I have listened to the cathedral bells every evening for a year and they have never rung eleven, which means either you are gone or you are waiting, and I have chosen, selfishly, to believe the second.*

His hand shook. Not with fear — with the effort of holding the note steady while another voice tried to sing through it. He pressed on.

*The stone is beneath the cathedral. Under the reliquary, below the crypt, below even the bones they tell pilgrims are the first saints. There is a stair that appears on no plan because I made certain of it myself, in my second year, when I still had enough of myself to lie to my own masons. I told them the stair was a cistern. I paid them to forget. Most of them did forget; men forget what kings pay them to forget. The door is iron faced with salt-lead, and it opens to no key but answers to three things spoken together: a name the god had before men named it, a grief freely offered, and a road walked honestly. I know two of the three. I never knew the name. Perhaps someone who listens to the land will.*

He paused there, pen hovering, and felt something move behind his eyes — not the impostor, something older and stranger, a pressure like weather. The land itself, perhaps, listening back. He had heard rumors, in the fragments that reached him, of a god answering prayers in the hollow villages. He had heard, too, in the thing's own gloating, of a listener it feared — some runaway who could tell a true voice from a false one. The thing did not speak of this often. When it did, its hunger went quiet and cold, and that silence told Aldric more than any decree.

*There is a girl,* he wrote, then crossed it out. *There is a person — forgive me, I am a king of an older education and my manners fail me at the end — there is a listener in the Thornfells, or there was. The thing fears them. Find them if you can. And then hear the rest of what I have to say, because it matters more than the stair, more than the stone, more than me.*

He set the pen down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations. This was the part he had rehearsed for a month of stolen half-hours, and rehearsal had not made it easier.

*Do not save me if it costs another.*

*I mean it. I know how it will look from outside — the poor enchanted king, the sleeping prince, the whole rotten romance of it. I have watched this court for a year and I know what stories people need. But I have also been inside the binding, and I know what it is. It is not a prison. It is a debt, and debts of this kind do not cancel; they transfer. Every year the thing wears me, it grows heavier with what it takes, and every rite that pulls it out of one vessel must pour it into another, unless the rite is paid otherwise. I have read the old accounts. I had a year of clear nights, once, before it finished closing its hand, and I read everything. The first kings did not bind this thing out of wisdom. They bound it out of greed — they wanted a god that obeyed, and they found one, and they chained it to the throne so that it would have to keep their kingdom fed and safe forever, and they called the chaining holiness. It has been wearing us ever since. My grandfather. His mother. All the way back. We are not victims, Wren. We are the latest payment on a very old bill.*

*So: do not save me if it costs another. If the price comes due in someone else's blood or someone else's mind, let me go. Let the note stop ringing. I have been one note for so long that I remember, faintly, what silence was, and it was not terrible. I was a boy once in the western hills and I used to lie in the grass after the harvest and hear nothing at all except wind, and I want you to know that I think of that field more than I think of the crown, and I think of you more than I think of the field.*

*That is the last secret. There. It is out. A year of composing decrees in cipher and the truest sentence I can manage is the plainest one. I loved you before the crown and I love you now, from inside this narrow place, and if I never say it with my own mouth again, this page has said it, and this page is mine — the one thing in this palace that is entirely mine.*

The candle guttered. Aldric looked up sharply.

Across the chamber, in the great mirror that the thing loved — it preened, sometimes, wearing his face, admiring the majesty of its stolen vessel — the reflection was wrong. The reflection was standing when he was sitting. It smiled when he did not.

*You are spending your hour,* it said, without sound, without lips moving, in the register of a headache beginning. *On paper. How very like you. Kings should spend their hours on kingdoms.*

*They should,* Aldric said aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's, and he hated it and did not care. *Which is why I am telling the truth for once.*

The reflection tilted its head. For a moment — a horrible, vertiginous moment — Aldric saw curiosity in his own borrowed features, genuine and almost childlike, and understood that the thing genuinely did not understand. It had been bound so long, worn so long, fed on so much mediated love, that it had forgotten what it was to be believed rather than worshipped. Truth was, to it, merely a more efficient form of incense.

*Say your goodbyes,* the reflection said, almost gently. *The thirteenth turn is coming. After that, there will be no cracks. I will close the hand, and you will be a note held forever, and it will not hurt. I have made it not hurt. That is the mercy I built into you, little king. You may thank me.*

*I will not,* Aldric said.

And the reflection laughed — his laugh, the one Wren had loved, degraded now into something that rang like a struck coin — and was gone, and the mirror showed only a tired man at a desk with an hour running out.

* * *

He wrote until his fingers ached.

Not all of it was cipher. Some of it he wrote plainly, knowing the thing would read it and find it harmless, because the thing had no use for human grief except as flavor. He wrote to his sister Maren, who had married into the hill country and stopped writing to him two years ago, and he did not blame her, because the last letter she'd received had been in his hand and yet somehow not his, warm as a coin left in sun. *Maren. The apples in the orchard at Callowmere — do you remember stealing them, the summer the steward broke his ankle? That was real. Whatever else you remember of me from these last years, weigh it against that afternoon, and if the scales tip wrong, throw the bad memories out. Keep the apples.*

He wrote to the mason Brannic, who had taken the king's coin to forget the stair and perhaps had not forgotten after all. *If you remember, forget it properly this time. If you cannot, then when strangers come asking about cisterns, be slow to answer and quick to pray, and know that a grateful dead man remembers you.*

He wrote to the kitchen boy who brought his supper, because the boy whistled off-key and it was the only music in the palace that the thing had not gotten its hands on. He wrote three lines and could not finish them and folded the page anyway.

And then, when the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, he took a fresh sheet and wrote the letter he had been circling all night, the one addressed in no cipher to no office, simply:

*Wren.*

*By the time you read this I will be nearly gone, or gone, or saved — I refuse to pretend I know which, and pretending certainty is the one vice I have not yet acquired. So I will say everything, and you may keep what you need and burn the rest.*

*You falsified the border maps. I have known since the third month, since the thing paraded your treason before me like a trophy — see, it said, see how even your loves betray you. It thought it was hurting me. It was, but not the way it meant. Because I looked at the forged lines and I saw not betrayal but care — you moved the ford, you erased the pass, you redrew the whole northern march to steer an assassin's route away from me, and you did it badly on purpose, badly enough to be caught, because a perfect forgery would have saved me and damned whoever came next along that road. You spent yourself to buy me a season. I know what that costs. I am paying the same price in a different currency.*

*We are both forgers, my love. We both altered the map to protect someone, and the world punished us both, and neither of us would unmake a single line.*

*Here is my last instruction as your king, and my last request as the man you knew, and you may choose which to obey:*

*As your king: go home. Whatever happens at the stone, walk away after. Do not stand in the falling of it. Kingdoms crush the people who stand closest when their foundations shift.*

*As the man: stay. Stay near her — the listener, the one the thing fears. I have seen the shape of their coming in the margins of my own decrees, in the way the word *hearing* keeps surfacing in documents I did not write. If anyone can pay the debt without spilling another, it is someone who knows what things truly cost, because they listen. Guard them. Argue with them. Laugh at them, once, at least — you have a laugh like a hinge that needs oiling and I have missed it every single day, and if I must be a held note forever, let the last thing I tuned myself toward be that sound.*

*The moon is setting. My hour is going. I can feel the hand closing, finger by finger, and underneath the fear there is something worse, which is gratitude — gratitude for the hour, for the paper, for the plain words, for the apples, for you. A year ago I would have said a man in my position deserves better than one hour a moon. Now I know better. No one is owed hours. We are given them, and the given ones are the sweetest, and this one —*

The pen stopped.

Aldric looked down and saw that his hand had gone still on the page, and that the stillness was not his own choosing. The binding was drawing closed around the hour like water around a sinking stone. He had perhaps a minute. Less.

With the last of himself he wrote, in letters going crooked even as the pen moved:

*— this one was enough. It was enough. Tell them the king said it was enough.*

Then the note resumed, single and pure and obedient, and the man who had written four pages of goodbye was folded away inside it like a letter into an envelope no one would open.

The thing that wore the crown rose from the desk, stretched the royal shoulders, admired itself briefly in the mirror, and blew out the candle. On the desk the pages lay drying, four sheets of a dying man's whole heart, and beside them the thing left orders for the morning — a new tithe of devotion for the solstice vigil, doubled candles, doubled choirs — because the thirteenth turn was coming, and it intended to arrive at its completion magnificent, fed, and eternal.

In the dark of the king's chamber, the ink dried slowly, and the last line of the last letter caught the moonlight as it set:

*Tell them the king said it was enough.*
