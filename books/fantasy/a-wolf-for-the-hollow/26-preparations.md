## 26. Preparations

The safehouse beneath the tanner's row smelled of old brine and colder stone, and Noor had decided, somewhere in the small hours of the night, that she would not cry about any of it.

She had made the decision the way she made most decisions now: deliberately, with her hands flat on a table, as though pressing something down would keep it from rising. The candle guttered. Wren slept badly in the next room—Noor could hear her turning, the creak of the cot, the soft percussion of a woman who had spent too many years sleeping alone and had never gotten good at it again. And Noor sat at the table with three objects arranged before her like an altar to a very practical god.

A pouch of salt, coarse and grey, bought from a fishwife who hadn't asked questions because Noor had paid in silver and looked at her the way Maghda had taught her to look at people—which was to say, not at all.

An iron nail, drawn from the doorframe of a house that had burned two winters past. Iron from a threshold was best; iron that had held a family's comings and goings remembered them, and memory, Noor was learning, was the only currency this whole miserable business traded in.

And Maghda's rite, copied out in her own careful hand onto six sheets of paper, each word triple-checked against the memory of a cottage kitchen and a dying woman's cracked voice saying *write it true, girl, write it true or don't write it at all.*

Salt. Iron. Words. That was what remained of everything she knew.

She turned the nail over between her fingers. It was rusted at the head, bright where her thumb had worried it all night. Somewhere under the city, beneath a cathedral built on top of a crime older than anyone alive, there was a binding stone that had been fed kings for centuries, and in four days—three, now, if you counted honestly, and Noor always counted honestly, it was one of the few virtues she'd kept—the thirteenth full moon would rise complete, and whatever sat behind Aldric's eyes would sit there forever.

Unless she unbound it. Unless she paid.

*The caster pays in memory,* Maghda had said, and had said it gently, which was somehow worse than if she'd said it cruelly. *The rite doesn't take what you offer. It takes what you hold.*

Noor set the nail down before she could think too hard about what she held.

Instead she did the thing she had been avoiding for two days, which was to pull a fresh sheet of paper toward her, dip the pen, and begin to write.

She started with the roads, because the roads were easiest, and because starting with the hard things would have meant starting with Wren, and she wasn't ready, and she suspected she would never be ready, and so logistics it was. Logistics were a kind of armor. She had learned that from watching Wren fold maps: crease by crease, the world reduced to something that fit in a pocket, something that could be handled.

*The Thornfell pass above Hollowmere,* she wrote. *North face. There is a shelf of rock shaped like a kneeling shepherd—you can't miss it if the moon is right, and if the moon is wrong you shouldn't be on that mountain anyway. Below the shelf, forty paces down, the path splits. Everyone takes the left fork because it looks walked. Take the right. The right fork is the smuggler's way; it wants to be walked but only by people who ask it first. Put your palm on the stone at the split and wait. If the mountain is in a mood, wait longer. It has moods. Most mountains do. People just don't listen.*

Her pen moved steadily. This was the strange mercy of it—that writing the roads out did not hurt yet. They were still hers while she wrote them. The words on the page were copies, and the originals still lived behind her eyes, warm and worn as boot-leather. It was only later—she understood this dimly, the way you understand winter is coming while standing in autumn sun—it was only after the rite that the pages would become all there was.

She wrote about the ford at Marrow Water, where the current lied about its depth. About the cut through the alder swamp that smelled of honey in spring and rot in autumn and both smells meant *safe.* About the way into the capital itself, the catacomb mouth behind the dyer's yard, the sequence of turns that no royal map knew because royal mapmakers drew what kings wanted to be true, and smugglers drew what was.

Wren had once been a royal mapmaker. Noor paused over that thought, pen hovering, ink gathering in a dark bead.

Then she shook her head and kept writing.

*If you are reading this and I am not beside you,* she wrote at the top of the second sheet, *then I got the rite wrong, or the rite got me wrong, or the moon did what moons do. Don't blame yourself. Blame me. I'm the one who insisted on doing it.*

She crossed that out. It was melodramatic. Maghda would have boxed her ears.

*If you are reading this and I am beside you but I don't know your name,* she wrote instead, *start with the bread. You'll understand when we get to the Hollowmere page.*

* * *

Wren found her there at dawn, or what passed for dawn in the tanner's row, which was a grey seepage through the shutters rather than any actual light. Noor had filled eleven sheets. Her hand ached. Her eyes felt scoured.

Wren stood in the doorway with two cups of chicory and looked at the table—at the salt, the nail, the sheaf of papers weighted down with a whetstone—and Noor watched her understand, watched it move across her face like weather.

"You're giving them to me," Wren said. Not a question.

"Someone has to keep them."

"I could copy them. You could keep the originals."

"And if we're separated? If they take the papers?" Noor slid the cup toward herself and drank. It was terrible. She was grateful for it. "You carry them. I carry the rite in my head, because the rite has to be spoken, not read. That's the division. Salt and iron go in your pack too—they're inert until the words wake them, and the words will be coming out of my mouth, so they should be within my reach, not yours."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for two days." She set the cup down. "You've thought about the cathedral approach. Someone has to think about the aftermath. Somebody has to plan for the version of this where it works."

Wren came around the table slowly. She was not a graceful woman in motion—she moved like someone perpetually correcting a course—but she was graceful in stillness, and she stood now looking down at the pages with an expression Noor couldn't name, something between reverence and grief.

"May I?"

"They're yours."

Wren picked up the first sheet. Read it. Noor watched her read, and it was unbearable, so she didn't watch; she looked at the wall instead, at the water-stain shaped like a running hare.

"The kneeling shepherd," Wren said quietly. "I mapped that pass. Years ago. Before—" She stopped. Started again. "I drew it from survey reports. Secondhand. It was wrong, you know. My map put the split thirty paces below the shelf, not forty. Some border patrol probably died trusting my lines." Her thumb traced the edge of the paper. "Yours is right. I can feel that it's right, which makes no sense, except that everything about you makes no sense, so perhaps it does."

"Wren."

"Mm."

"You're stalling. We both know you're stalling. Read them later. All of them, later, as many times as it takes. Right now I need you to do the other thing."

Wren looked up. "Which is?"

"Ask me what's not on these pages."

The silence stretched. Outside, the tanner's row woke in its ugly incremental way—a cart, a dog, someone coughing up the night. Wren set the papers down with exaggerated care, aligning their edges, and Noor recognized the gesture for what it was: the same armor, the same logistics, worn by another pair of hands.

"Noor," Wren said. "What isn't on these pages?"

And there it was. The question she had invited like inviting a wound to open.

Noor pulled a fresh sheet toward her. Her hand, which had been steady all night, was suddenly not steady at all.

* * *

She wrote slowly, and Wren sat across from her and did not help, because Noor had asked her not to, because this part had to be done alone or it would shatter her into pieces too small to perform a rite with.

*My name is Noor Thornbury,* she began, and then crossed it out, because Wren knew that, everyone knew that, it was useless, and then uncrossed it, because that was exactly the point—useless things were precisely what she stood to lose, the things so woven into her she'd never thought to write them down.

*Maghda's face. I can't draw faces. I'm going to try anyway. She had a scar through her left eyebrow, white, from a snare she said, though I never believed her. She laughed like a door hinge. When she was pleased with me she didn't say so; she just made extra porridge, and you learned to count the bowls.*

She wrote until the page filled. Then another.

*Hollowmere. The grandmother's orchard, the one that bloomed dead overnight—I can't remember her name and that frightens me more than anything else on this list, so I'm writing it here as a warning: if I come back and I don't grieve her, make me. Her name matters. Find it out. Ask Tam, if Tam lives, if Tam is speaking to any of us after the gate.*

*Tam himself. He looked at me with recognition at the ford, the soldier did, and I have carried that look ever since, and I want it written down that I did not hate him and he did not hate me and that is the whole horror of wars, that nobody in them hates anybody properly.*

*The shrine under the cairn. What the old god told me. Write it even though I already wrote it in chapter and verse on the road—write it again here, plainer: it said the impostor was made, not born, and that men bound it into service, and that the old gods don't want worship back, they want to die properly, and I believed it, and I still believe it, and if I come back believing the crown-thing was simply evil, correct me. Make me remember it was a prisoner too.*

Then the last page. She saved it for last the way you save the worst chore of the day, and her pen hovered so long that the ink dried twice and she had to re-dip it.

*Wren Dunmore—no. Wren. Just Wren, I don't actually know your surname, which is a thing I should have asked weeks ago and now seems urgent and absurd in equal measure.*

*What I cannot bear to forget:*

*The way you fold maps. Corner to center, always, like you're putting something to bed.*

*That you drink chicory like it's a punishment you've assigned yourself.*

*The night in the cave-in, halfway moon, when you said what you said. I won't write what you said here because if I lose it I want to hear it from you again, fresh, and not from my own handwriting. That's selfish. I'm allowing myself one selfishness.*

*Your hands. Steady on the pen, unsteady on everything else.*

*That you chose me over your papers. I saw you burn them. I will not let that become a story I tell instead of a thing I know.*

She signed it—not with her name, which was already written, but with the smuggler's mark, the little hooked line she'd scratched into doorframes across half the Thornfells meaning *safe passage, friend inside.* Then she blew on the ink, and stacked the pages, and tied them with string, and pushed the bundle across the table.

Wren did not pick it up immediately. She was looking at the last page, the one Noor had left open, and her jaw was working in a way that meant she was holding her face together by main strength.

"One selfishness," Wren said finally, voice rough. "Out of all of this."

"It's the only one I could afford."

"You could afford more." Wren's hand came across the table and covered Noor's ink-stained fingers. "For the record. Whatever the rite leaves you—you could afford more than one."

Noor laughed, or something adjacent to laughter, wet and short. "Don't say things like that. I have three days of composure left and you're spending them for me."

"Composure is overrated. I've met composed people. They falsify border maps and drink alone." Wren gathered the bundle at last, and Noor watched her do it—watched her tuck the pages into the oilcloth wrap, watched her settle them against her spine where a map case would ride, watched twelve years of exile and one autumn of love get packed away like cargo. "I'll read them every night," Wren said. "Whatever happens. Even if you're beside me. Especially if you're beside me."

"That's not how it's supposed to work. The pages are for if it fails."

"The pages are for keeping," Wren said. "The failing is optional. I've decided."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day preparing the body as well as the soul, because devotion without logistics was just noise. Wren sharpened knives and re-drew the cathedral quarter from memory, marking the vigil routes, the change of the Devoted guard, the window of dark between the third bell and the fourth. Noor rendered fat and salt into tallow candles the way Maghda had taught her—candles that would burn blue in the presence of unquiet things, and there would be unquiet things, there were always unquiet things, the whole city above them was a reliquary of unquiet things wearing gold.

By dusk the table was clear except for the salt, the nail, and the six sheets of the rite, which Noor read aloud once, end to end, in a whisper, to fix the cadence in her throat. The words tasted like cold iron and older cold. When she finished, the candle flame leaned toward her, just slightly, as if listening.

Neither of them remarked on it.

Later, banked fire, blankets, the narrow cot they had begun to share without ever discussing it, and Noor lay awake listening to Wren breathe and doing the arithmetic she had forbidden herself: three nights left. Three nights of knowing her own name from the inside. Three nights of the smell of chicory and the sound of maps being folded and the weight of a hand across hers on an ink-dark table.

In the morning, Wren was already up, already dressed, already standing at the table with the oilcloth bundle open and the first page in her hand—and she was reading aloud, softly, the way you read scripture or a letter from the dead.

"*The Thornfell pass above Hollowmere,*" she recited. "*North face. There is a shelf of rock shaped like a kneeling shepherd—*"

Noor sat up. "You don't have to start yet. It hasn't taken anything yet. I still know it."

"I know," Wren said, and folded the map-corner of the page over her finger, corner to center, putting it to bed. "But I promised every night, and the solstice vigil starts tomorrow, and if I'm going to learn your roads by heart before you lose them—" she looked up, and her eyes were dry and fierce and absolutely certain— "then I'd better begin tonight. Because someone in this partnership has to remember the way home, Noor. And I intend for it to be both of us."

Outside, far above the tanner's row, the moon was already fattening toward its thirteenth turn, and somewhere beneath the cathedral, the binding stone waited with a king inside it, counting down.
