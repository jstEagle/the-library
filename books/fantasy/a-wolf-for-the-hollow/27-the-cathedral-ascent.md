## 27. The Cathedral Ascent

The vigil bells began at dusk, and Wren counted every one of them the way a condemned woman counts her steps to the block.

Thirteen. Of course it was thirteen. The cathedral of Saint Vessia rang its solstice vigil in thirteen peals—she had known that once, had known it the way she knew the grain of the capital's streets, back when she had been Royal Cartographer and the city had been hers to measure. Now the sound came down through the fog like a hand pressing on her chest, each bell a small reminder: *you used to belong here. You used to be believed.*

She stood in the shadow of a dyer's shed in the Tanner's Quarter, and Noor stood beside her, and between them they had assembled a plan made of smuggler's routes, a dead woman's rite, and two sets of borrowed clothes.

"Pilgrims," Noor murmured, for the tenth time that hour, adjusting the grey wool hood over her hair. "We're pilgrims. We came for the solstice blessing. We wept at the reliquary. We gave our copper at the door."

"We gave our copper at the door," Wren agreed. Her own hood itched. The pilgrim's robe beneath her coat smelled of someone else's woodsmoke and someone else's life, purchased from a laundress who had asked no questions because Wren had paid in questions' worth of silver. "And if anyone asks where we've walked from—"

"Hollowmere." Noor's voice was steady. It had been steady for three days now, ever since she had written out her memories in a cramped hand and pressed the pages into Wren's keeping, and Wren hated the steadiness, hated what it cost, hated that she could do nothing but carry it. "Everyone walks from Hollowmere this time of year. Half the pilgrims in the square will say the same."

It was true. That was the terrible elegance of it. The impostor on the throne had filled the roads with the faithful, thousands of them funneling toward the cathedral for the longest night, and the crowd that made them invisible also made the cathedral a fortress of devotion. Wren had spent the afternoon watching the Devoted at the gates—white-robed, ash-marked, their faces lit with a joy she remembered from her childhood in the temple pews, joy that had once seemed like the purest thing in the world.

Now she knew what fed on it.

"Your hands are shaking," Noor said.

Wren looked down. They were. "Cold," she said, and Noor let the lie stand, because they had an agreement about lies that kept them walking.

* * *

They crossed into the cathedral quarter at the changing of the vigil watch, folded into a river of pilgrims moving toward the great west doors, and Wren felt the city close around her like a hand she had once trusted.

Everything was the same and nothing was. The lamplighters still worked the same corners. The chandler's shop on Vellum Row still had its crooked sign. She had drawn this quarter at nineteen, ink still wet on her commission, so proud of the little cartouche with her name in it that she had shown it to everyone in the mapping office, and Aldric had—

Aldric had held it up to the window and said, *You've made the cathedral look like it's listening.*

She had laughed and said cathedrals do nothing but listen.

She thought of that now, passing beneath the actual walls, and had to bite the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper. The cathedral *was* listening. Something beneath it was listening. Something beneath it had been listening for centuries, wearing king after king like coats, and tonight—on the twelfth turn's last night, with the thirteenth moon only days away—they were going to walk down into its mouth.

The west doors were open, blazing with candlelight, and the line of pilgrims moved through them in a slow, shuffling river. Wren kept her head bowed. Ash-mark on her brow—Noor had drawn it from memory, and her memory of sacred marks was flawless, which was one of the many things about Noor that Wren tried not to think about too closely, because there was no time and no safe place for thinking about it.

Inside, the nave rose up into darkness. Ten thousand candles. The choir already singing the long vigil, voices braided and rising, and Wren felt the old pull of it in her sternum, the pull she had felt since she was a girl: *sing along, kneel, belong, be held.* That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked. Not with terror—with comfort. It made a home in you and called the home faith.

Beside her, Noor had gone rigid.

Wren glanced over. Noor's face beneath the hood was bloodless, her eyes fixed on the altar far ahead, and her lips moved slightly—not in prayer, Wren realized, but in *listening*. Hearing whatever the land heard, and the land beneath a cathedral full of feeding devotion must have sounded like a slaughterhouse.

"Noor." Barely a breath. "Eyes down. Pilgrims don't look at the altar like that."

"I'm trying to find it." Noor's whisper was ragged. "Underneath. There's so much—*noise*. It's like standing in a river. I can't—there. There's a thread. Going down. Under the choir, northwest, it goes *down*—" She shuddered and shut her eyes. "It's hungry, Wren. It's been eating all evening and it's still hungry."

"Then we know where to go." Wren took her elbow, gently, and turned her toward the north ambulatory, where the crowd thinned toward the cloister. "One thing at a time. First we get past the reliquary gate."

* * *

The reliquary gate was the problem, and the problem had a name, and Wren had been carrying the name like a stone in her boot for two days.

*Tam.*

The boy from Hollowmere. The boy Noor had saved from the choosing, the boy whose debt had bought them passage through the catacomb door in chapter after chapter of Wren's nightmares—she had dreamed of him both ways. Letting them pass. Reporting them. She had woken more than once with his young face hovering somewhere between the two, unable to resolve.

He had become a novice of the Devoted. That much their underground contacts had confirmed: Tam, son of the fuller, white robe, ash mark, seen serving at the vigil preparations. And he would be stationed at the reliquary gate tonight, because the underground contact had also confirmed the rotation, and because the world was not kind enough to give them any other door. The reliquary gate was the only entrance to the undercroft that didn't require walking past the throne's own guard.

"He'll know me," Noor had said, when they'd planned it in the safehouse with its leaking roof. "There's no pretending otherwise. I saved his life. You don't forget the face of the person who—"

"Then we don't pretend," Wren had said. "We ask."

Now, standing in the cloister's cold with the vigil singing behind them, Wren watched the reliquary gate and waited for her heart to decide what it was going to do.

The gate was iron and black with age, set into the north wall of the ambulatory, and before it stood two figures. One was a full Devoted, tall, ash-sashed, a veteran of whatever campaigns the crowned god had waged on its own faithful. The other was slighter. Younger. His robe was new enough that the hem hadn't frayed, and he stood the way new soldiers stand—too straight, hands folded too carefully, eyes fixed on the middle distance because looking at anything specific might reveal that you were looking.

Tam.

Even at forty paces, even in a novice's robe, Wren knew him. She had seen him once in Hollowmere, a boy in a doorway with flour on his arms, and she had seen the way he'd looked at Noor afterward—like Noor was something out of a story his grandmother told. Debt did that. Debt made saints of ordinary people, and Wren knew, with a cartographer's certainty about distances, exactly how far Tam was about to fall.

"We could still go around," Noor said. "Through the choir. Try the old sacristy stair."

"The sacristy stair was bricked up in the last reign. I drew the survey myself." Wren exhaled. "It's him or nothing."

Noor was quiet a moment. Then: "Then it's him."

They walked toward the gate the way pilgrims walk—slowly, heads bowed, hands folded—and Wren felt every one of the forty paces in her knees. The veteran Devoted saw them first and raised a hand, and his voice was bored and kind at once, the voice of a man who had turned away a thousand pilgrims from a thousand closed doors.

"The undercroft is closed for the vigil. The relic of Saint Vessia receives pilgrims at dawn. You may wait in the—"

"We're not here for the relic," Wren said.

The veteran's eyes sharpened. Tam's head came up.

And there it was. The moment Wren had been dreading since the safehouse: the boy's face going through recognition like a man going through ice. His eyes found Noor's hood, and Noor, deliberately, slowly, let the hood slip back just enough—just enough—and Tam made a small sound, low in his throat, the sound of a man watching his two worlds collide.

"Brother," the veteran said to Tam, puzzled. "Do you know these pilgrims?"

And Wren watched the boy stand at the exact center of his life.

She knew what he was holding. She had held it herself, three nights ago, when she'd fed her identity papers into the safehouse stove and watched the royal seal curl and blacken—her name, her commission, her whole former self going up in a smell like burning hair. Tam was holding his own version of that paper. *Faith* on one side, *debt* on the other, and both sides were true, and only one side could survive the night.

"Brother?" the veteran said again.

Tam looked at Noor. Noor looked back, and did not plead, and did not explain. She simply stood there, the woman who had pulled him out of the choosing, and let him see her.

Then Tam smiled—the bright, guileless, terrible smile of a boy who had made his decision and was going to carry it off or die of it—and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the veteran and no farther:

"Forgive me, brother. Yes. These are the pilgrims from Hollowmere. The ones the Prior asked me to watch for." He stepped forward, all eager efficiency, and took Wren's elbow with a novice's unthinking authority. "They've come a long way. The Prior wants them taken down through the undercroft directly—the old route, past the reliquary. There's a matter of... a private blessing. You know how the hollow villages are, brother. They bring such strange needs."

The veteran hesitated. Wren held her breath and thought, absurdly, of maps—of all the borders she had ever drawn, and how every one of them was a lie agreed upon, and how this boy was drawing one right now with nothing but his voice.

"Strange needs," the veteran repeated.

"Orchard blessings," Tam said solemnly. "Grave-blessings. You know."

The veteran did know. Everyone knew the hollow villages brought strange needs, and everyone knew the crowned god answered them, and no one wanted to be the brother who delayed a private blessing. He waved them through with a flick of two fingers. "The undercroft stairs are past the second reliquary. Brother Tam will conduct you. The vigil ends at dawn—be above ground before the change of watch."

"Yes, brother," Tam said. "Blessed be the crown."

"Blessed be the crown," the veteran said, and turned back to his gate, and Wren walked past him into the dark with a boy's hand on her arm and her heart hammering like the vigil bells.

* * *

Past the second reliquary, the candlelight failed, and Tam stopped conducting and started confessing.

"I've been stationed at this gate for eleven days," he said, his voice dropping to a rush, a boy's whisper in a stone throat of a corridor. "Eleven days. I prayed every night that you'd come, and every night I prayed you wouldn't, because I didn't know which prayer I could keep." He looked at Noor, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You saved me. They chose me, and you—you did something, I never even learned what, and I've been wearing *this*—" he plucked at his white robe— "ever since, learning their songs, learning to say *blessed be the crown* like it doesn't taste like anything, waiting."

"Tam," Noor said gently. "You don't have to—"

"I know what's down there." He said it fast, before she could stop him. "I'm not a fool. The novices talk. There are rooms under the cathedral the Prior doesn't enter. There are nights the whole quarter hears the stone singing, and they tell us it's the saint rejoicing, and it isn't, is it. It isn't a saint." His jaw worked. "I'm not asking what you're going to do. I'm asking you to do it. Whatever it is."

Wren looked at the boy—this boy who had crossed half a kingdom inside his own faith, who had prayed two opposite prayers and meant them both—and felt something in her chest crack along an old seam. She had burned her papers. He was about to burn his whole self.

"Tam," she said. "When this is done, they will know. The veteran at the gate, the Prior, the whole Devoted. They'll know you let us through. Your freedom is the price, do you understand? Not your life—I'll do everything in my power to keep it your freedom—but you can't come back to this. Whatever happens tonight, you can't put the robe back on."

"I know." He said it simply. And then, with the terrible practicality of the young: "I've been leaving the gate log wrong for three days. Shifts that don't match. A door noted open that wasn't. When they look for how you got in, the record will say you came through the pilgrim door at the changing of the watch, and it will say I was at evening prayers, because Brother Ossian will remember seeing me there, because I asked him a question about the liturgy that he loves answering." He almost smiled. "I've been a novice for four months. I know exactly how much they see."

Noor made a sound that was half laugh, half grief. "You'd have made a fine smuggler."

"My grandmother said worse." Tam's face went still then, and he reached into his robe and brought out a small bundle of grey cloth, and unwrapped it, and held it out to Noor. Inside was a stub of candle, a twist of salt in paper, and a thin iron nail, black with age.

"Salt and iron," Wren breathed. The rite's components. Half of them.

"The novices carry these for warding the reliquary," Tam said. "Nobody counts them. Take them. And take—" he swallowed— "take my blessing too, if you want it. I know it's not worth much. I know what it's been feeding. But it's mine. It's the one thing they never got to."

Noor took the bundle. She closed her hands around it the way you close your hands around a coal, and she looked at the boy for a long moment, and then she said, "Your grandmother raised you right, Tam, fuller's son. When this is over, walk home. Walk to Hollowmere. There'll be bread there with your name in it."

"I'd like that," Tam said, and his voice broke on the last word, and he turned away quickly and became, in the space of a breath, a novice again—straight-backed, hands folded, face smooth. "The undercroft stairs are down and to the left. Forty steps, then the passage forks. Keep left. The right fork is the Prior's crypt, and it's warded, and the warding is *not* a formality." He paused at the edge of the candlelight, a white figure fraying into the dark, and looked back at them once.

"Blessed be," he said—and then, quieter, in the voice of a boy in a doorway with flour on his arms: "Go on, then. I've got a gate to miscount."

He was gone, back up the corridor toward the light and the singing and the life he was spending, and Wren stood in the dark a moment with her throat tight.

"Borrowed courage," Noor said softly. "That's what my mother used to call it. When someone lends you their bravery because yours ran out."

"Then we're rich," Wren said. "Come on."

* * *

The undercroft stairs were forty steps, and Noor counted them aloud in a whisper, and Wren understood why: counting was a rope to hold. At the forking of the passage, the air changed. It grew colder, and older, and it carried a smell Wren had no name for—the smell of stone that had been wept on for centuries, maybe. Or prayed on. Or fed on.

They kept left, as Tam had said, past a warded door behind which something shifted its weight and settled, patient as a debt. Wren did not look at it. Noor did not either, but her hand found Wren's in the dark, and held on, and Wren held back, and they went down.

The passage narrowed. The vigil's singing faded above them until it was only a vibration in the stone, a hymn felt rather than heard, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, wrong—Wren began to hear the other sound. A slow pulse. Like a heartbeat in the mountain's bones.

Noor stopped walking.

"It knows we're here," she whispered. Her face in the dark was grey. "It's not—Wren, it's not alarmed. It's *pleased*. It's been waiting. It says—" she pressed her free hand flat against the wall, listening to the land's true voice with her whole body— "it says the thirteenth turn is almost ripe."

Ahead, the passage opened into a darkness that was not empty. The pulse grew stronger. And somewhere in that darkness, faint as a memory of light, something that had been a king once, and a god before that, and a hunger longer than both, was waiting for them with the patience of centuries.

Wren drew the salt and the iron nail from her coat, and stepped forward into the dark.
