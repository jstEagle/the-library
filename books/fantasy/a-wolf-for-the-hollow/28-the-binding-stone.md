## 28. The Binding Stone

The stair went down farther than the city above it had ever gone up.

Noor counted the steps because counting was a thing her hands knew how to do when her mind wanted to scream. Forty-one steps to the first landing, where the smuggler's tunnel they'd followed out of the catacombs ended in a wall of dressed stone that no smuggler had built. Sixty-three more after Wren found the seam—a hairline crack in the masonry, hidden behind a saint's carved robe whose face had been chiseled away centuries ago. Eighty-nine more after that, spiraling now, the air thickening like wool pulled over the mouth.

Two hundred and six steps, and at the bottom of them, silence.

Not emptiness. Noor had grown up in emptiness—the high passes where even wind gave up, the years in Maghda's cottage where quiet was just quiet, honest and thin as well-water. This was different. This was the silence of a held breath. The silence of a room where someone is pretending to sleep.

"Say something," she whispered, and was ashamed of how small her voice came out.

"I'm saying something." Wren's hand found hers in the dark—found it without fumbling, which meant Wren had been tracking her position the whole descent, cataloguing her like a feature on a map. Her palm was cold and steady. "Cathedral crypts sit on older foundations. Everyone knows that. What everyone doesn't know—" She struck a match, and its light made a small trembling island of their faces. "—is what the older foundations are *for*."

The matchlight reached maybe ten feet before the dark ate it. But ten feet was enough to see that they stood in a corridor of black stone polished smooth as river rock, and that the walls were carved.

Noor stepped closer to the nearest carving and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

Kings. Rows upon rows of kings, each figure larger than life, each crowned, each seated on a throne—and from every throne, chains ran downward into the stone itself, rendered so finely that the links seemed to move when the matchflame moved. And below the thrones, beneath the feet of every king in every panel, coiled the same shape: vast, serpentine, neither serpent nor wolf nor anything with a name she knew, its body looping through the rock like a root through soil, its head raised toward each crown in turn with an expression the carvers had somehow made legible across centuries of granite.

It wasn't hunger in that expression. That was the worst part. It was *patience*.

"How old," Noor said. Her voice sounded wrong down here, flattened, as if the stone were drinking the sound before it could echo.

Wren had moved ahead, match held high, reading the wall the way she read everything—with a cartographer's hunger for edges and boundaries. "Look at the crowns," she said. "The earliest ones. That's not the Aldric line. That's not even the Second Dynasty. I've seen those crown-shapes in fragments, in the oldest survey records, the ones they keep locked in the map-vaults because they contradict the official histories." Her voice had gone strange too, but hers went strange by going *scholarly*, climbing into lecture-pitch the way it did when she was frightened. "These carvings predate the kingdom, Noor. The binding didn't happen to the kingdom. The kingdom happened *around* the binding."

The match burned down to her fingers. Wren hissed and shook it out, and for a moment they stood together in the breathing dark, two small warm things in the throat of the earth.

Then Noor heard it.

She had been hearing it since the stairs, she realized—had mistaken it for her own pulse, for blood in her ears, for the ordinary hum of a body afraid. But her gift stripped that excuse away the way it stripped everything. Beneath the silence there was a voice. Not words. Not yet. A drone, low and ceaseless, like a bell struck once a thousand years ago and still ringing, still ringing, the note never allowed to die.

And threaded through the drone, faint as a name called across a valley: *help me.*

Noor's knees nearly went. She caught herself against the carved wall, palms flat on ancient granite, and the land spoke to her—not the surface-land she knew, fields and roads and hills with their mild gossiping voices, but the deep stone, and the deep stone was *screaming*. Politely. Endlessly. In the manner of something that had screamed so long it had learned to make screaming sound like patience.

"Noor." Wren's hands on her shoulders. "What do you hear?"

"It's still in there." The words came out of her cracked and raw. "The king. He's not—Wren, he's not possessed, he's not worn like a coat. He's *held*. They're both held. The god and the man, chained to the same stone, and the throne above is just—" She groped for it, the truth assembling itself out of the carvings and the drone and the thing Maghda had died trying to teach her. "The throne is the lock's handle. Whoever sits it turns the key."

* * *

They followed the corridor down by touch and by Noor's gift, which grew stranger and stronger the deeper they went. The land-voice here had layers, strata, like sediment in old ice. The newest layer spoke the cathedral's language—incense and bells, the Devoted's hymns filtering down through two hundred feet of masonry as a faint golden pressure. Beneath that, dynasties of prayer, each generation's devotion laid down like silt. And beneath all of it, at the bottom of the acoustic well of the world, the drone, and inside the drone, the man.

Aldric. Still fighting. After thirteen moons of erosion, after whatever the impostor had spent of him, some kernel of the king remained at the center of the binding like a fly in amber, and he was *pushing*, and no one had ever heard him, and he had kept pushing anyway.

Noor wept without deciding to, tears running hot down her cold face, and let Wren guide her by the elbow because her eyes had become useless.

The corridor opened.

Even blind with grief she felt the change—the walls falling away, the ceiling rising, the air acquiring the vast dead stillness of an enormous enclosed space. Wren lit three matches in quick succession off each other and touched them to the torch-stub she'd carried from the catacombs, and light bloomed upward and outward and showed them the heart of it.

The chamber was a hollowed mountain. Its dome soared up beyond the torchreach into darkness, ribbed with stone like the inside of a skull. The floor was a single sweep of polished black rock, and across it ran channels—grooves cut in radiating lines, converging inward, and the grooves were stained. Dark. Old-dark, the color of things that had soaked in past any hope of cleaning.

And in the center, on a plinth of the same black stone, sat the binding stone itself.

It was smaller than Noor had imagined. That was the obscenity of it. All this architecture, all these centuries of kings and cathedrals and hymns and burned saints and Hollowmere's dead orchard given false bloom—all of it anchored to a stone scarcely larger than a millstone, round and rough-hewn, sitting on its plinth like a hearthstone someone had forgotten to build a house around.

Chains ran from it. Real ones, iron, green with age, and they ran *upward*, vanishing into the dome's darkness, following the channels' convergence. Noor understood with a lurch that the chains did not end at the ceiling. They went up through it, through the cathedral floor above, through the altar, through the throne dais, and fastened—to the chair. To the crown. To whoever wore it.

"Salt," Wren said quietly. She was already moving, pulling the packets from her coat, and her hands only shook a little. "Iron. We set the circle exactly as Maghda's pages say, and then you start, and I don't ask you how it feels, because you told me not to."

"You remembered."

"I remember everything about you." Wren said it plainly, without performance, the way she stated elevations and distances, and it landed harder than any declaration could have. "That's my whole gift, apparently. Maps and you."

Noor almost smiled. Then she stepped toward the stone, and the drone rose to meet her, and every thought in her head scattered like startled birds.

* * *

Up close, the stone was covered in writing.

Not carved—*grown*, if stone could grow anything, the letters raised from its surface like scar tissue, layer over layer, script atop script in languages dead and half-dead and one she recognized with a jolt: the formal court hand, the one used on proclamations. Someone had added to the binding within living memory. Within Aldric's memory, perhaps. His own hand, or the hand of whoever had bound him, cut into the same surface where the first kings had cut theirs.

She knelt. She read.

The oldest layer was a covenant, and the covenant was a confession. The characters were archaic but the grammar of betrayal needed no translation. *We take thee, thou eldest, thou who wert before our naming, and we bind thy death from thee.* Noor's breath stopped. *For thou didst promise the people freedom from fear, and fear is our harvest, and a god who keeps his promises starves his priests.*

"They made it immortal," she whispered. "On purpose. They took a dying god and they—" Her throat closed. She tried again. "Old gods die, Wren. The shepherd under the cairn told me—they want to die properly, they want release. And the first kings couldn't rule a people who might follow a god into honest death, into honest silence. So they chained it to life. Chained it to *them*. Made the crown its anchor so it could never leave, never fade, never rest. Every king since has been its door and its prison both."

Wren had gone very still by the edge of the torchlight. "Aldric stopped sleeping," she said slowly. "Thirteen moons ago. I thought—I told you I thought the thing had taken him recently, that it moved in during his reign—"

"It's always been there. Sleeping in the crown, wearing each king thin and slow, patient, feeding on devotion drip by drip. And something woke it. Something made it hungry enough to swallow Aldric whole instead of sipping." Noor looked down at the newer script, the court hand, and forced herself to read the last lines cut into the stone. They were instructions. Cold, procedural, recent. *Let the vessel be emptied by the thirteenth turn. Let the old self be spent entirely. Let nothing remain that remembers being a man.*

Below that, crammed into a corner in tiny, cramped strokes, in a hand shaking with effort or terror:

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still—*

The sentence simply stopped, mid-word, the groove trailing off into a scratch.

Noor put her fingers over it. The stone was cold, but beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat under snow, something pushed back against her touch. Recognition. Relief. The desperate gratitude of a drowning man feeling a rope brush his wrist.

"He wrote that," she said. "Recently. During the twelfth moon, maybe. He's been leaving messages in his own decrees and no one reads decrees closely enough and he's been *alone*, Wren, he's been alone down here in the dark for thirteen moons with that thing wearing his mouth—"

"Noor." Wren knelt beside her. Her face in the torchlight was terrible and composed, the face of a woman holding herself together with cartographer's discipline, and her eyes were wet. "We can't save him yet. We can't save anyone until the circle's set and the rite begins. So grieve fast, and work faster. That's the only mercy down here."

So Noor grieved fast, and worked faster.

They poured the salt in its unbroken ring around the plinth, Wren walking the circumference backward with her eyes on Noor the whole way, checking angles, checking gaps, a woman who had falsified border maps to protect a king now drawing a true border around the thing that had eaten him. They set the iron nails at the four quarters, heads inward. Noor unwrapped Maghda's rite from the oilcloth where it had traveled against her heart for a hundred leagues, and the sight of her teacher's handwriting—cramped, impatient, alive—nearly undid her again.

The drone changed.

She felt it before she heard it consciously: the low ceaseless note bending, warping, acquiring harmonics it should not have had. The temperature of the chamber dropped. The torchflame leaned sideways though there was no wind, leaning *toward* the stone, and in the channels on the floor the old stains seemed to darken, seemed to glisten, seemed—

Above them, muffled by two hundred feet of stone, the cathedral bell began to toll.

Not the vigil bells. Not any hour. It tolled the way a throat screams—ragged, urgent, wrong—and Noor knew with absolute certainty that it was not a bell-ringer's hand on the rope. The impostor had noticed them. Had perhaps known from the moment they passed the reliquary gate, had let them come, had watched them descend with that carved-granite patience, and now it was calling its Devoted home.

"Wren."

"I hear it." Wren was already at the salt ring's edge, checking the seal, and her voice was flat and calm and absolutely terrified. "How long do you need?"

"The rite? I don't know. Maghda never said. Maybe minutes. Maybe—" 

"Then start now." Wren drew her knife and turned to face the stair-corridor, placing herself between Noor and the darkness, between Noor and whatever came down those two hundred and six steps. "I'll hold the door. You hold everything else."

Noor looked at her—at this disgraced, brave, impossible woman who had burned her own name in a brazier three nights ago rather than give the Devoted a trail to follow—and wanted to say a hundred things, and had time for none of them.

She stepped inside the salt. She knelt at the stone. She pressed both palms flat against the layered centuries of script, against the trailing unfinished sentence of a drowning king, and she opened her gift all the way, wider than she had ever dared, wider than Maghda had ever permitted, and spoke the first word of the unbinding.

The stone answered.

Not the drone. Not the impostor. Something older and vaster stirred beneath the binding like a leviathan turning over in the deep, and the entire chamber groaned, and the chains overhead snapped taut with a sound like the sky cracking, and from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice made of every king who had ever worn the crown, the bound thing said:

*Little thief of roads. Do you know what you are unbinding?*

Noor held on. Below her palms, faint and fading, the king's scratched sentence pulsed once—*I am still*—and she poured the rite's second word into the stone like water into a wound, and far above, growing louder, came the sound of boots on the stairs.
