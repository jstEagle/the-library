## 29. Crowned and Hollow

He was a bell that someone else rang.

That was the shape of it now, after so many turns of the moon. Aldric had been a man once—tall, he thought, though he could no longer be sure the memory was his own; fond of cold mornings and bad poetry; in love, once, with a woman who drew coastlines. But those things had thinned like frost on south-facing glass. What remained of him had contracted to something small and hard and bright: a single held note beneath the great tolling of another's voice.

The note was his name. He kept it the way a drowning man keeps air.

*Aldric.*

He said it inside himself, over and over, in the dark behind his own eyes, and sometimes—only sometimes—the thing wearing him paused, as if it heard the small sound of him knocking from within, and found it amusing rather than alarming.

Tonight it was amused. Tonight it was *delighted*.

* * *

He felt them coming before he saw them, because he felt everything the body felt, even from this distance, even through the wool of his own diminishment. The binding stone sat beneath the cathedral like a heart of black glass, and the thing that wore his crown had poured itself down around that stone for thirteen moons, drinking what rose from it. Devotion, mostly. Fear, when devotion ran thin. And now, threading up through the catacombs, two small warm signatures of living will—one flickering strangely, hearing-things, land-listening, *wrong*, and one steady as a drawn line on vellum.

Wren.

His heart—that borrowed muscle, beating at the impostor's pleasure—lurched against his ribs, and for one merciful moment the thing let him have the feeling whole. It did that sometimes. It liked to let him feel. Pain was sweeter to it when he was present for it, and joy, it had discovered, made an exquisite seasoning for grief.

*She came back,* Aldric thought, and the thought was a candle flame in a cathedral of dark. *She came back, and she brought someone who can hear the land, and they are going to try to kill you, and I hope—I hope—I hope—*

*Hoping,* said the other voice, vast and patient and amused, resonating through the marrow of his jaw, *is such a human habit. I've worn eleven kings before you, little bell, and every one of them hoped. It never once changed what happened next.*

Eleven kings. He had not known that until the twelfth moon, when the binding had tightened and some of the stone's older memories had bled upward into him like water through chalk. Eleven kings before him had sat this throne with something coiled around their souls, and none of them had broken free, and all of them were dust, and their kingdoms had called it divine right.

*I am going to be the twelfth,* Aldric told the dark. *Or I am going to be the first to matter.*

*You are going to be a mouth,* the impostor said fondly. *You are going to be my finest mouth. Come. They're nearly here, and I want you awake for this part. You'll want to see her face.*

And Aldric, who had fought this thing for thirteen moons with every scrap of will he possessed, found—as always—that he could not refuse it anything. His legs carried him down the spiral stair behind the reliquary. His hands lit the lamps along the way. His mouth, that treacherous instrument, arranged itself into the mild and holy smile that had made a kingdom weep with love.

He watched from inside, screaming without sound.

* * *

The chamber beneath the cathedral was older than the cathedral. That was the joke no one in the gold-vested clergy understood: their God-in-the-Crown predated their church by a thousand years, and the church had been built over the binding stone the way a bandage is laid over a wound—partly to protect it, mostly to hide it. The room was round, hewn from mountain rock, its ceiling lost in shadow above the lamp-glow. The stone itself stood at the center: a pillar of black glass taller than a man, veined with lines that might have been writing in no alphabet still spoken, and warm to the touch, always warm, warm as a fevered brow.

Aldric's feet stopped three paces from it, as they always did. Even the impostor would not touch the stone directly. Even it remembered being bound.

And then the far door—low, arched, meant for priests' processions—did not open. Instead a section of wall that should have been solid swung inward on hinges older than the kingdom, and cold air came through smelling of wet stone and river-dark, and two figures stepped out of a passage that existed on no map Wren had ever been permitted to draw.

Noor Thornbury. He knew the name now; the impostor had plucked it from the minds of the Devoted it wore like gloves, and had shared the knowledge with him deliberately, the way a cat shares a wounded bird. A runaway apprentice. A smuggler's child. A person with a gift for hearing what the land truly wanted—and therefore the single most dangerous creature in the kingdom, as far as the crowned thing was concerned.

And beside them, hood thrown back, face pale and set and older than he remembered and exactly as beautiful as he remembered—

"Wren," said the impostor, through his mouth, in his voice, and Aldric felt the word leave his own throat like a hook leaving flesh.

It had chosen his voice well. Not the ringing proclamation-voice it used from the throne, but the low private register, the one he had used across breakfast tables and in the dark of unimportant rooms, the one only perhaps a dozen living people had ever heard. It had mined him for that voice over thirteen moons. It knew precisely what it was doing.

Wren stopped as if she'd walked into glass. Her hand went to the satchel at her hip—to the maps, he guessed, or the salt, or whatever weapons a disgraced cartographer carried into hell—and Noor's hand went to hers, quick and steadying, and something about the gesture cracked Aldric open worse than any pain the impostor had ever inflicted.

They were together. She had found someone. She had come anyway.

*Oh,* he thought, in the small dark where he lived now. *Oh, that's—good. That's good, Wren. Don't look at me like that. Look at them like that.*

"You shouldn't have come down here," the impostor continued, gentle, sorrowful, every syllable a masterwork of Aldric's own regret. "Both of you. This place is not safe for hearts."

"Neither is the throne," Noor said. Their voice was young and rough and did not shake, which must have cost them enormously; Aldric could hear the tremor underneath, the way he could hear everything now, his senses stretched thin as drumskin by the thing's attention. "We've come to take it back from you."

"From *me*?" The impostor laughed, and the laugh was Aldric's laugh, and it echoed wrong in the round stone room, doubling and redoubling until it seemed to come from the walls themselves. "Child. I am the crown. I am the throne. I am the reason your grandmother's orchard bloomed and your neighbor's fever broke and your king's grain stores never emptied through the worst winter in living memory. What exactly do you imagine you're taking?"

"The truth," Noor said. "Which is that you ate it all. Every prayer. Every answered wish. You didn't give them anything—you just showed them their own hope reflected back, and drank the difference." A pause. "I know what a real god sounds like. I've heard one sleeping under a cairn in the high passes, honest as snowfall. You don't sound like that. You sound like hunger wearing a hymn."

For a moment—for a glorious, terrible moment—the mask slipped. The smile stayed but the warmth drained out of it, and Aldric felt the thing's attention swing toward Noor like a lighthouse beam, vast and cold and considering, and he thought: *it's going to kill them, it's going to kill them right now, Wren run—*

But it didn't. Because it had seen what Aldric had seen. It had seen Wren step half in front of Noor, instinctive, unconscious, her body answering a question her mind hadn't asked yet.

And the impostor, who had fed on devotion for a thousand years, who understood love the way a leech understands blood—not its purpose, only its taste—had an idea.

*Watch,* it said to Aldric, silken, inside the skull they shared. *Watch what I can do with a heart like hers. You gave me this, little bell. Every hour you loved her and never said so, you salted the meat. Now I'll carve.*

"Noor," the impostor said aloud, and now it used a different voice—not Aldric's at all, but something older, layered, a chorus speaking through a single throat, and the lamps guttered low. "Step aside. This does not concern you. This concerns—" and his hand, his traitor hand, lifted and pointed at Wren, and his voice slid back into the intimate register, the breakfast-table voice, the *her* voice, "—this concerns what she wants. Doesn't it, Wren? You want me. You want *him*. The real him. Whole, and free, and mortal, and yours."

Wren had gone very still. "No," she said.

"I haven't finished making the offer." The impostor smiled with Aldric's mouth, and Aldric, watching from inside, felt sick with admiration and horror in equal measure, because it was doing it *beautifully*. "Here is the bargain, cartographer. Walk away. Take your hands off the apprentice. Let the rite go unperformed tonight, and at dawn I will loosen the binding—not break it, loosen it—far enough to give him back. His memories. His will. His mornings and his bad poetry and his ridiculous opinions about rivers. He will wake in his own bed tomorrow knowing nothing of these thirteen moons, believing himself merely tired, merely human, merely *king*—and he will be himself again. Truly himself. For years, Wren. Perhaps decades. All you must do is stop her."

"That's a lie," Noor said flatly.

"It is an *offer*," the impostor corrected, and its patience was more frightening than any rage. "You may verify it however you like. Ask the stone. Ask the sleeping god under your cairn whether I keep my bargains—I have kept every bargain I ever struck, in a thousand years. It is prayers I answer freely that I renege on. Bargains have *terms*. Terms are sacred." The smile widened a fraction. "You of all people should respect terms, hearer-of-the-land. Your whole gift is listening for what things truly want. So listen to me now, and hear that I am telling the truth: I will give him back to her. Whole. In exchange for your obedience, tonight, and hers."

*Don't,* Aldric screamed into the dark, knowing the scream reached no one, knowing it wasn't even meant for Wren—it couldn't be, it mustn't be—but unable to stop, unable to watch this and stay silent even in silence. *Wren, don't listen, it's lying, it always lies, the loosening is a lie, there is no version of this where it gives anything back—*

Except. Except that was the horror of it. He sifted the thing's intent the way he'd learned to sift its moods across thirteen moons of enforced intimacy, and he found—to his absolute devastation—that it was *not* entirely lying.

It would loosen the binding. Slightly. Briefly. Long enough for Aldric to wake one morning as himself, long enough for Wren to have a season—perhaps a year—of the man she had loved, before the coil tightened again and the impostor resumed its feast with interest compounded. It considered that a fair price for survival tonight. It considered a year of a man's life, sold by the woman who loved him, a reasonable exchange.

And it would enjoy, immensely, telling Aldric afterward. It would enjoy letting him know that his freedom had been purchased with Wren's betrayal, that the woman he loved had chosen a year of him over the stranger-child who had crossed a kingdom to save them both. It would wear that knowledge against him forever, the way it wore his face.

*It's true enough to be poison,* Aldric thought, despairing. *Gods, it's learned to tell truths. When did it learn to tell truths—*

*From kings,* said the impostor serenely. *Kings are excellent liars, but the best of them know that truth is the sharpest blade in the armory. You taught me that too, little bell. You and your marginalia. Did you think I didn't notice you writing in the corners of your own decrees?*

Cold went through him. The messages. The coded pleas he had spent his last clear hours scratching into the margins of royal edicts—*the stone beneath, the old alphabets, do not save me if it costs another*—he had believed them hidden, buried in cipher, invisible to the thing that read through his eyes.

It had read every word. It had simply found them useful.

*Do you understand now why I bring you to moments like this?* the impostor murmured, almost tender. *Your despair seasons the room. Now be quiet. She's going to answer.*

* * *

Wren had not moved. That was the first thing. Through the whole offer she had stood like a drawn border—straight, deliberate, defining—and Aldric realized, with a surge of love so fierce it hurt the impostor's borrowed throat, that she was buying time. Reading the room the way she read terrain. Her eyes had gone to the stone, to the lamps, to the low priest's door, to Noor's hands, cataloguing, measuring, while the monster monologued.

Some things about her hadn't changed at all.

"You've made this offer before," Wren said at last, and her voice was steady, and Aldric hung on it like a man hanging from a cliff-edge. "To the other kings. The eleven."

Something flickered across Aldric's stolen face—there and gone, a cloud-shadow over water. "The others did not have what you have."

"What, a guilty conscience?" Wren took one step forward, and Noor's hand twitched toward her, and she shook her head minutely—*stay*—without looking away from the king-shaped thing. "No. I mean you've offered this exact trade before. Freedom-for-betrayal. Someone stops the person trying to unbind you, and you reward them with a loan of the king's soul. And eleven times, someone said yes—or no one came to stop you at all—and here you are, still hungry, still wearing a new face every generation, still needing to make the same offer again." Her chin lifted. "So tell me how the last ten bargains ended. Tell me what happened to the lovers who said yes."

Silence. The lamps burned. Somewhere far above, the solstice vigil congregation was singing, a thread of sound filtering down through a thousand tons of stone, and the irony of it was exquisite: the whole cathedral full of people singing to the thing in the dark below.

"They died," the impostor said finally, and the chorus-voice came back, and it was not gentle anymore. "Everyone dies. That is not a flaw in the bargain. That is the nature of the goods."

"And him?" Wren's voice cracked, just once, on the word. "When the loan comes due—when you tighten the coil again—what happens to *him*? Does he remember the year you gave him? Does he remember that I sold out the person who saved us both to buy it? Or do you wipe that too, and give me back a man who doesn't know what I am?"

The impostor smiled, and did not answer, and the silence was the answer.

"There it is," Wren whispered. "There's the fine print. There's always fine print with you. A year of him, minus the parts that would make him hate me—which is to say, a year of a man who isn't him at all, loving a woman who isn't me, in a kingdom you'll still be eating." She turned her head, slowly, and looked at Noor, and Aldric watched her find her footing on the decision the way she'd once found footing on scree slopes above the northern passes. "You know what I spent years drawing maps for? Borders. Lines on paper that said *this is ours and that is yours*. And every one of them was a lie somebody powerful needed told. I falsified one myself, once, because I loved someone and thought a lie could keep him safe." Her breath shuddered. "It didn't. Lies never do. They just move the wound somewhere you can't see it."

"Wren," the impostor said, and now it dropped the chorus, dropped the grandeur, and spoke in pure Aldric—so perfectly, so cruelly, dredging up the exact timbre of a night neither of them had ever spoken of aloud, a night in a map-room with rain on the windows. "Come home. Put the pen down. Let the child go. I will give you back every morning we lost."

And Aldric, from inside, threw everything he had left—all of it, the last hoarded coin of himself, thirteen moons of scrimped and saved will—against the walls of his own body.

His hand spasmed. His voice broke from the impostor's control for half a heartbeat, one raw syllable torn out of him:

"*Wren—don't—*"

Then the coil snapped shut around him and he was a bell again, rung silent, hanging in the dark. But he had felt it—the impostor had felt it too—a hairline crack of fury in its composure, because he had spent himself down to nothing to say one word, and the word had landed.

Wren was crying. She was crying and she was smiling, both at once, and she wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a soldier, like a surveyor caught in weather, and she said:

"There he is."

The impostor went very still.

"All this gold and grandeur," Wren said softly, stepping forward again, past the point of safety, past the point of sense, "all the miracles and the answered prayers and the choir of voices, and do you know what just now was worth more than all of it? One cracked word. One second of the real him, spending the last of himself to warn me off saving him." She laughed, wet and fierce. "You can't counterfeit that. You've had his voice for thirteen moons and you still can't counterfeit that. And you never will. So no. My answer is no. Take your bargain and choke on it."

The impostor's face—Aldric's face—turned, slow as a closing door, toward Noor.

"Then I'll start with the child," it said, and the lamps went out.

* * *

Darkness, total, and in it the sound of the stone beginning to sing—a low subsonic hum Aldric felt in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the hollow of his chest where his self crouched small and spent and terrified. The impostor was drawing power up through the binding, gathering itself for violence, and Aldric felt the pull of it like a tide going out, dragging at the last loose threads of who he was.

*This is the part,* it said to him, almost regretful, *where I stop being careful with the vessel.*

And Aldric, who had nothing left, who had spent his final coin on a single shouted word, did the only thing remaining to him.

He listened.

Thirteen moons of listening—from inside, from beneath, from the small dark—hear the land's true voice, the runaway had said, and Aldric had no such gift, but he had spent a lifetime reading borders and rivers and the grammar of ground, and he had spent thirteen moons learning the exact topography of his own prison. And now, as the impostor surged, he felt it—faint, rhythmic, approaching through the dark of the smuggler-passage behind them:

Footsteps. Many. The measured tread of the Devoted, descending.

Tam's report. Tam's breach. The sealed quarter had not held them out; it had herded them in.

*They're here,* Aldric thought, and pressed the thought outward with everything left, a dying fire's last spark. *Wren—Noor—the Devoted are coming down—you have minutes—do it NOW—*

Whether either of them heard, he would never know. But in the darkness ahead, he heard Noor's voice begin to speak—low, steady, words in no alphabet still spoken, words that made the black stone's hum falter like a singer losing pitch—

—and the impostor screamed through his mouth, a sound with a thousand years of hunger in it, and lunged for the light of the rite.
