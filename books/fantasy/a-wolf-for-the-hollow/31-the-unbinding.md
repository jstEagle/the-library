## 31. The Unbinding

The binding stone was older than the cathedral above it, older than the city, older than the word *king*. It sat in a chamber of black rock at the root of everything, and it was not beautiful. That was the first thing Noor understood about it, standing in the cold with salt in one hand and iron in the other and Wren's voice still ringing somewhere far above her, buying her minutes with the only currency she had left.

The stone was a slab the size of a coffin, veined with channels that might have been carved or might have grown, and every channel was full of light the color of an old bruise—yellow going green going dark. It hummed. Not with power, exactly. With *hunger*, the way a belly hums when it has been fed too well for too long and has forgotten what hunger was for.

Noor had expected to feel afraid. She felt instead a terrible clarity, the kind that comes at the top of a fall, when there is nothing left to decide and only the falling left to do.

She knelt.

The floor was wet. Water seeped through the black rock from some deep vein of the Thornfells themselves, the same mountains she had crossed as a runaway, the same stone that had whispered to her all her life in its slow mineral voice. She pressed her palm flat against it and listened, the way Maghda had taught her before Maghda taught her to stop listening.

*Here,* said the land. *It is here. It has always been here.*

"I know," Noor whispered. "I'm sorry it took me so long."

She poured the salt.

It came out of the pouch in a thin white stream, and where it fell it did not scatter—it *arranged* itself, drawing a circle around the stone with the neatness of something guided by a hand steadier than hers. Maghda's rite did not need her to be precise. It needed her to be *willing*. The salt knew its work; it had been waiting in the earth for this since before there were prayers to interrupt. All Noor had to do was not flinch.

She set the iron next. Three nails, pulled from the door of a chapel in the capital's poorest quarter, blessed by no one, rusted by everyone. She drove them into the circle's edge with the heel of her hand, one at north, one at south, one where her heart pointed, which was not any direction a compass would have named. Each nail went in with a sound like a tooth breaking.

And then there was nothing left to do but begin, and nothing left to pay with but herself.

* * *

Maghda had explained it once, in the last lucid week, when her burned-out eyes could no longer see Noor but could still find her by sound, the way a blind woman finds a fire.

"The rite doesn't take what you offer," she had said. Her voice had been gravel and honey both. "Everyone thinks that. Everyone thinks you choose the price, hand it over like a coin, walk away poorer but whole. It doesn't work that way. The rite reaches into you and takes what is *loosest*. What is least anchored. And for someone like you—" she had turned her ruined face toward Noor, toward the sound of her breathing, "—for someone who built her whole self out of roads and listening, child, nothing is loose. Everything is load-bearing. You will not lose a memory. You will lose the ground under a hundred of them, and they will fall, and you will stand in the rubble and have to decide what to rebuild."

"Then I'll rebuild," Noor had said, young and certain.

"You say that now." Maghda had smiled, or Noor had heard the shape of a smile in her voice. "But you won't know what you've lost. That's the cruelty of it. You'll grieve for things you can't name, like a house mourning a room it doesn't remember having."

Noor thought of that now, kneeling in the wet dark, and made herself say the first words of the rite anyway.

They were not words in any language spoken above ground. They were older than language, syllables shaped the way water shapes stone—not spoken so much as *permitted*, let up out of her chest the way a held breath is finally let go. The salt circle brightened. The iron nails sang, one low note each, three notes braided together into a chord that made her back teeth ache.

And the stone noticed her.

* * *

It came up through her knees first, the attention of the thing in the stone, vast and slow and terribly interested. Above her, muffled by thirty feet of rock, she could hear the vigil continuing—the choir, the bells, the ten thousand voices of the Devoted singing their crowned god's praise—and she understood suddenly that every note of it was flowing down here, through the foundations, into the channels in the stone, feeding the yellow-green light. The cathedral was not built over the binding. The binding was built into the cathedral, the way a tapeworm is built into a gut. A thousand years of worship, piped straight down.

*Little listener,* the thing said, and its voice was everywhere in the chamber, in the water, in her own skull. *You came after all. I told you I would spare the king. I keep my bargains.*

"I'm not here to bargain."

*Then why are you here?*

"To unmake the bargain."

The silence that followed had texture. It was the silence of something very old recalculating.

*You do not know what you are offering,* it said, and for the first time there was something in its voice that was not hunger. Something almost like pity, if pity could be worn the way a mask is worn, by a face that had never actually made the expression. *I have eaten ten thousand years of devotion. I know what faith costs. But you—you would pay in something rarer. Pay in the self. Do you understand that there will be no one left afterward who remembers why she came?*

"There'll be Wren," Noor said. "Wren remembers."

*And if she dies? If the Devoted take her while you kneel here, hollowing yourself?*

"Then she dies knowing what I chose." Noor's hands were steady on the wet stone. "That's more than most people get."

The thing laughed. It was a horrible sound, because it was a *good* laugh—warm, rich, the laugh of a friend at a fireside, stolen from some god who had once earned it honestly. Then it stopped laughing, and the chamber went cold enough that Noor's breath showed, and the light in the channels began to move against the grain, flowing backward, gathering, resisting.

So Noor began to pay.

* * *

The rite took the mentor's face first.

She felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave the jaw—a moment of pressure, a wrongness, a socket suddenly empty. One instant Maghda's face was there behind her eyes, vivid as noon: the deep seams around the mouth, the grey braid coming loose from its pin, the way one eyebrow climbed higher than the other when she was pretending not to be amused. The next instant—

Nothing. Not darkness. Darkness would have been something. Just an absence shaped exactly like a face, a hole in the world with Maghda's outline, and Noor's grief pouring into the hole with nowhere to land, mourning a woman whose features she could no longer summon even to lose them properly.

"No," she heard herself say, and kept chanting. The syllables came without her deciding to make them. The rite was in her now, working her like a bellows works a forge.

The cottage went next. The smell of it—dried herbs, tallow, the particular must of old thatch—dissolved into a blankness that ached. The path from the cottage to the stream, the exact lean of the fence post she'd leaned against a thousand times, the sound of Maghda's cough in the winter dark—all of it, gone, gone, gone, each loss a small implosion she felt in her ribs. She was crying. She noted it distantly, the way you note weather. The tears were real. What they were *about* was being taken from her even as they fell.

*Stop,* said the thing in the stone, and now there was no pity in it at all. *You cannot mean to—*

She meant to.

The smuggler's map came apart road by road.

This was worse. This was so much worse, because the roads were not pictures; they were *knowledge in her feet*, in the soles of them, in the way her body turned without asking her mind. She felt the pass below Ravenfell go—the memory of the switchbacks, the cairn at the third bend, the place where the mountain breathed fog at dawn—and her legs twitched with the ghost of walking it, muscles firing along a path that no longer existed anywhere in the world except under her boots. Then the ford at Cold Harrow. Then the shepherd's stair, the hidden door in the Thornfells that Maghda had shown her at fourteen, swearing her to silence, the two of them wrapped in one blanket watching stars wheel over the notch. Gone. The blanket. The stars. The oath. The shape of Maghda's shoulder against hers.

She sobbed the syllables of the rite and could not stop. The salt circle blazed white now, and the light in the stone's channels was guttering, flickering, the yellow-green dimming toward grey, and the thing inside it had begun to make a sound that was not laughter and not rage but something between a scream and a hymn, because it too was remembering, dragged backward through its own long meal, and what it tasted now was not devotion but the honest worship it had once been given, freely, by people who had known its true name—

Noor didn't hear the name. She was losing Hollowmere.

* * *

Hollowmere dissolved like sugar in warm water.

The orchard first—the dead orchard that had bloomed overnight, every blossom she had seen with her own disbelieving eyes lifting away from her memory like petals off a shaken branch. Then Grandmother Sef's kitchen, the bread, the flour-dusted table, the particular crack in the blue bowl. Then Tam—Tam's face went and she grabbed for it and missed, and she was left grieving a boy she knew she had saved, knew it the way you know a fact in a book, without a single image to carry it. *I saved him. I know I saved him. Why can't I see his face? Why can't I—*

The village square. The inn where Wren had watched her flinch at the bell. The bell itself. The name *Hollowmere*, which had been a place and became, in the space of one exhaled syllable, only a sound, then less than a sound, a shape her mouth remembered making without knowing what it meant.

She was aware, in the last shreds of herself, that she was still kneeling, still chanting, that her voice had gone raw, that the salt circle was burning white as daybreak and the stone beneath her was cracking—actually cracking, hairline fractures spidering through a thousand years of prison—and that the light was draining out of it like pus from a lanced wound, and that somewhere above her the bells had stopped, and the choir had stopped, and there was shouting, and none of it mattered, none of it could matter, because she had promised herself on a night road long ago that she would never again run from a thing that needed doing.

What am I forgetting now?

She tried to hold Wren. She dug in with everything left, nails into the last intact wall of herself, and held—one image, one only: brown eyes, ink-stained fingers, the crooked half-smile of a woman who hid behind maps. *Her name is Wren. Her name is Wren. Her name is—*

The rite reached for it.

And stopped.

Not because Noor willed it. Because the rite, which was older than kings and hungrier than the hungry god, found that one memory anchored to something outside herself—to a hand that had gripped hers in the dark under the city, to a voice that had said *stay with me, stay with me, stay with me* until the words were load-bearing too—and the rite, like the land itself, honored what was truly bound. Some things come back, Maghda had said. Some things are better rebuilt than remembered.

The last thing Noor knew, in the final clear second before the knowing ended, was the taste of salt—her own tears, or the circle, or the sea the world had been before anything was carved from it—and the stone splitting open beneath her hands like an egg, and something enormous rising out of it, something that had been a god once and was about to remember it, and her own voice saying, in a language she no longer spoke:

*Be free. Be finished. Go home.*

Then the hollow opened where Noor Thornbury had been, and she swayed above it, empty-eyed, her hands still flat on the broken stone, hearing nothing at all.

Above her, far above, the bells began to ring again—but wrong, cracked, discordant, ringing the way bells ring when the god they were cast for has just died inside them.

And in the dark of the shattered chamber, the freed thing turned its attention upward, toward the throne, toward the crown, toward the last of its ten-thousand-year meal—and began, at last, to remember its name.
