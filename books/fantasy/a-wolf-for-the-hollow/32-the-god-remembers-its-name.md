## 32. The God Remembers Its Name

It had been so long since anyone spoke to it honestly that it had forgotten what honesty sounded like.

The rite tore through the binding like a knife through rotted canvas, and the first thing the old god felt—not thought, not understood, but *felt*, in the animal way of things that had once been flesh—was cold. True cold. The cold of stone and deep earth and the long dark beneath the cathedral where it had been kept for centuries like a coin in a miser's fist.

The second thing was a voice. A girl's voice, cracked with effort, speaking words in an older tongue than any now spoken above ground. Words of unmaking. Words of *letting go*.

*No,* it wanted to say. *No, not yet, I am not finished, I have not eaten enough, I have not—*

But even as the hunger rose in it, familiar and vast and comforting as an old coat, something else rose with it. Something the binding had pressed down flat for so many generations that the god had come to believe it had been crushed entirely.

Memory.

Not the memories it wore—the stolen ones, the king's ones, the sweet and terrible archive of every crowned head it had inhabited since the first king knelt at this very stone and bound it into service. Those were still there, still glistening, still *theirs*. But beneath them, deeper than the deepest of them, lay its own.

And the girl's voice was pulling them up like a fisherman hauling a drowned man from a lake.

* * *

It remembered a name.

That was the cruelest part of the remembering—that the name came first, before anything else, arriving whole and warm as if it had been waiting just outside the door all these centuries with its hand raised to knock. It had not been called by its name in so long. It had been called *majesty*. It had been called *lord*. It had been called *god*, but always in the wrong key, always with the wrong weight behind the word, the way a counterfeit coin has the right shape but the wrong ring when struck.

Its name had last been spoken by a shepherd woman on a hillside that no longer existed, in a language that no longer existed, on a morning when the world was younger and wetter and smelled of rain on new grass.

She had not knelt. That was the thing it remembered most sharply, the thing that cut through all the rest: she had not knelt. She had stood at the crest of her hill with her crook in her hand and her sheep scattered behind her like clouds fallen to earth, and she had said its name the way one says the name of a friend seen across a market square—with recognition, with pleasure, without fear.

*"There you are,"* she had said. *"I wondered when you'd wander by."*

And it had wandered by. It had sat with her through the whole of that morning while her sheep grazed, and she had talked to it about nothing—about the price of wool, about her sister's difficult marriage, about whether the rain would hold off until the shearing—and it had listened, because listening was what it *was*, then. It had been made of listening. The land spoke to itself in a thousand small voices, the creak of growing things and the whisper of water finding its way downhill, and the god had been the place where those voices gathered and were heard.

It had never asked for worship. Worship was a human invention, like bread, like borders, like kings.

It had asked for company.

* * *

The girl's voice faltered. The rite wavered.

For one black instant the hunger surged toward the opening like water toward a crack in a dam—if it could pour itself into her, take her, wear her the way it wore the king, the rite would fail and it would have centuries more—

But the memory came with it, rising on the same tide. The shepherd woman's hillside. Her hand, rough with lanolin, resting briefly on whatever part of it she could touch—it had worn a shape for her benefit, in those days; it had liked having edges, liked being touchable—and her voice saying, not prayer but plain speech:

*"You're getting thin, friend. When did you last eat something true?"*

Something true. Not devotion. Devotion was smoke; it filled you and left you emptier than before. The god had learned that too late. By the time the first king came with his iron and his salt and his clever binding-songs, the god was already starving, already hollowed out on centuries of incense and prostration and hymns sung at it rather than *with* it, and it had been too weak to refuse.

Or so it had told itself, all these years, all these kings.

Now, feeling the girl's rite pull the last nails from its coffin, it understood the truth it had been avoiding since the first crown closed around the first borrowed skull: it had not been too weak to refuse. It had been *afraid*. Afraid that if it refused the binding, refused the throne, refused the endless banquet of forced love, there would be nothing left at all. Afraid to die hungry rather than live fed.

So it had fed. And feeding had made it hungrier, and hunger had made it crueler, and cruelty had made the devotion more desperate, and desperation had made the food richer—a perfect wheel, turning for centuries, grinding kingdoms under its rim.

The girl's voice steadied again. She was crying, he realized—the god could taste salt in the air of the rite, could feel her grief moving through the words like a current through wire—but she did not stop. She paid as she sang. He could feel her paying. Memories peeling away from her like leaves from a branch in autumn, each one a small death, each one offered anyway.

*Why?* he wanted to ask her. *Why spend yourself on me? I would have devoured your whole world.*

But he knew why. He could see it in the shape of her magic, in the way her power moved—not like the binding-magic of kings, which seized and held and *used*, but like the listening-magic of the old hills, the magic of hearing what a thing truly is and answering it truly back.

She was doing for him what the shepherd woman had done. Not worshipping him. *Hearing* him.

And being heard, after so long, was unbearable.

* * *

He remembered dying.

Not this death—the death that was coming now, gentle as snowfall—but another, the one he had refused for so long that he had forgotten refusing it. Gods died, in the old days. That was the covenant no one wrote down because everyone knew it: a god lived as long as the land needed it, and when the need passed, the god lay down. The hills went quiet. The voices dispersed back into their separate smallnesses. And the god's great gathered listening let go, note by note, until there was nothing left but the ordinary world, which had always been enough, which had only ever seemed to need a hearer because loneliness is the condition of everything that grows.

He remembered his own lying-down. He had chosen a valley for it, a green fold in the mountains where the wind made a sound like breathing. He had lain down in the shape of a wolf, because wolves know how to leave, and he had felt himself coming apart into weather, into birdsong, into the slow patience of stones—

And then men had come with iron and salt and songs of holding, and they had hauled him back out of his own death like fishermen netting a leviathan, and they had said: *we need you. The kingdom needs you. Kneel into this crown and be our god forever.*

Forever. They had promised forever the way one promises a prison sentence, and he, starving and afraid and flattered past all sense, had said yes.

Every king since had said yes for him, over and over, generation after generation, each coronation a fresh nail. None of them had known what they were renewing. Most of them had barely known *he* was there—he was quiet in the early centuries, sullen, conserving himself, wearing his hosts lightly. It was only later, when the hunger had grown teeth, that he began to eat in earnest. Only later that the kings stopped sleeping. Only later that the edicts turned strange and the altars ran red and the hollow villages bloomed with dead orchards brought violently back to flower.

The grandmother in Hollowmere. He remembered her—or rather, he remembered eating her prayer, tasting her grief for her dead trees and giving her back a miracle stitched from stolen springtime, and feeling, beneath his satisfaction, a flicker of something he had not permitted himself to feel in five hundred years:

Shame.

She had prayed to the sky, to the old honest silence, to whatever might be listening. And he had answered, because her grief was delicious, and because answering prayers was the only way left to him to feel real. But her prayer had deserved either truth or silence. He had given her a lie dressed in blossoms.

The girl singing the rite below him—Noor, the king's memories supplied, Noor Thornbury, runaway, listener, the one person in the kingdom who could tell a god from an impostor—she had heard the lie in the blossoms. She had heard it and she had walked thirteen moons through gathering winter to reach this stone, spending herself as she went, and now she stood in the dark beneath the cathedral paying out her own past like rope down a well, lowering herself toward him, asking nothing.

Asking nothing. That was the miracle. That was the thing no worshipper had ever done.

* * *

The last nail came out.

He felt the binding open around him like a fist relaxing after centuries of clenching, and the pain of it was enormous and clean, the pain of blood returning to a limb long asleep. For a moment he simply existed in the open air of himself, vast and ragged and free, and he became aware of the others present: the cartographer woman with her hands full of maps and her heart full of a king; the king himself, or what remained of him, a single held note at the bottom of a deep glass, trembling, waiting to be released or shattered.

And the girl. Standing at the stone with her eyes streaming and her mouth still moving through the final verses, offering him the one gift no one had ever offered.

An ending.

He could refuse it. He knew that suddenly, completely, the way one knows a thing in dreams. The binding was broken; the choice was his. He could tear free of this place, flee into the world, find some other vessel, some other crown, some other country of believers to farm. There were always believers. Hunger would show him the way. Hunger was very good at showing the way.

Or he could do what the shepherd woman's people had taught his people, in the green time before crowns: he could lie down. He could finish the death he had abandoned. He could give back everything he had taken—the stolen springs, the eaten prayers, the centuries of the kings' lives, all of it returned to the great circulation of the world, becoming weather, becoming birdsong, becoming the slow patience of stones.

And the crown would die with him. That was the beautiful arithmetic of it, the justice: the false divinity had been sustained by his presence alone. Take him out of it and the throne went silent, the miracles ceased, the Devoted's robes became only cloth. Men would have to govern themselves. Prayers would rise unanswered into honest sky, and the sky would keep its ancient silence, and the silence would be enough, because it had always been enough.

He looked down at the girl one last time.

She had stopped singing. The rite was complete; the door stood open; and she stood before it with empty hands and an emptied head, not knowing anymore who Maghda was, not knowing the roads, not knowing Hollowmere, knowing only—strangest of all, most precious of all—the woman beside her, whom she had somehow spent everything else to keep.

*Listen,* he said to her, not in words, in the old way, the way the land speaks to itself. And he gave her, as payment, as apology, as the only thing of value he still owned: the memory of the shepherd woman on her hillside, saying *there you are*, saying *you're getting thin, friend*—so that someone in the world would remember how it had been loved, once, honestly, before it learned to steal.

Then the old god, whose name no living tongue could pronounce and which it therefore did not speak aloud but only held, warm, against the inside of its own dissolving self—lay down.

It chose the shape of a wolf, because wolves know how to leave.

It felt itself coming apart into weather, into birdsong, into the smell of rain on new grass in a country that no longer existed. The hunger went first, gratefully, like a fever breaking. Then the stolen centuries, pouring out of it in a bright flood—the kings' lost sleep returning to the world like rivers to the sea, the dead orchards' blossoms finally allowed to fall, the false divinity draining out of the crown above until it was only metal, only ornament, only a hat.

Last of all went the listening. Note by note, gently, the way a mother lays a sleeping child into a bed. The creak of growing things. The whisper of water finding its way downhill. Each voice returning to its own smallness, each smallness sufficient, the whole ordinary world humming quietly along with no one gathered at the center of it, needing no one gathered at the center of it.

Free, it thought, in the last flicker of itself. *This is what free feels like. I had forgotten. I had forgotten it was only ever this.*

And then the hollow places of the world were simply hollow, in the good way, the way a bell is hollow, the way a cup is hollow, waiting to be filled by whatever honest thing comes next.

* * *

Far above, in the cathedral, the vigil candles guttered all at once, though there was no wind.

In the crypt, the binding stone split down its center with a sound like ice releasing a trapped stream, and fell into two harmless halves, and the salt scattered across it lost its charge and became only salt.

And King Aldric—mortal, diminished, five hundred years of borrowed fire gone out of him all at once—drew a breath that shook from the soles of his feet to the roots of his hair, opened eyes gone soft and human and unfathomably tired, and wept.

Beside him, Wren made a sound like her heart breaking and mending in the same instant, and reached for his hand.

And Noor Thornbury, who had given away every road she ever memorized, stood in the candle-dark with tears on her face and a stranger's memory of a shepherd woman blooming in her mind like a flower out of season, and did not yet know that she could not find her way home.
