## 33. Thirteenth Turn

The moon came up over the cathedral like a coin pulled from a furnace.

Noor felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her sternum, a tightening in the stone beneath her knees, as if the whole city were a drum skin being drawn taut for the last stroke. She was kneeling on the binding-stone floor with salt burning in the creases of her palms and Maghda's rite running through her head in her teacher's voice, syllable by syllable, the way she had finally been taught it only days ago at a dying woman's bedside.

*Hold. Hold. Hold.*

That was the whole of the rite now, in its final hour. Everything before—the salt circle scored into the ancient stone, the iron nails driven at the four quarters, Wren's maps spread and weighted with candlesticks so that the old roads of the kingdom lay drawn out around the binding like a snare laid flat—everything before was preparation. Now there was only holding. The rite was a door held shut against a gale, and Noor was the hinge.

The god did not want to leave.

She could feel it thrashing in the stone beneath her, in the walls above her, in the bones of the mountain the capital was built on—a vast wrongness straining toward the thirteenth moon like a drowning man toward air. Except it was not drowning, Noor corrected herself, pressing her palms flat to the freezing floor. That was the lie she had almost believed in the dream, when it had worn a truthful face and offered her back every road she'd ever memorized. It was not drowning. It was starving. And it had fed for centuries on the devotion of an entire kingdom, poured into whoever sat under the crown.

*You will be empty*, it had said to her, three nights ago, in a voice like warm bread and her mentor's stolen timbre. *Empty is only another kind of free.*

"Empty is just empty," Noor said aloud. Her breath smoked. The crypt beneath the cathedral had no windows, but the moonrise reached them anyway—she could feel it come down through forty feet of masonry and bedrock, silver and absolute, and where it touched the binding stone the salt hissed like snow on a forge.

Beside her, Wren held the last line.

They had argued about this—briefly, viciously, in the way of people who love each other too much to waste time pretending otherwise—and in the end Wren had won by refusing to lose: she would hold contact with the king's hand through the whole of the unbinding. *I mapped borders that didn't exist to keep him alive*, she had said, in that flat cartographer's voice she used when something mattered past all arguing. *I can hold a hand.* So Wren knelt on Aldric's other side, her fingers laced through his slack ones, her lips moving through the counter-words Wren had copied from the margins of a king's own decrees—messages scratched in code by whatever small bright fragment of Aldric still fought inside the hollow of himself.

And Aldric knelt between them, crowned and un-crowned at once—his crown of gold set aside on the stone, because you cannot unbind a man from a thing while he wears it—and he was so still. He had been still since the rite began at dusk. His eyes were open. They were the worst part. Not hollow, not anymore—the impostor's possession had cracked like river ice these last hours, and what showed underneath was not emptiness but a man drowning in slow motion, aware, trying, his mouth shaping words that came out in the god's cadences until suddenly they didn't.

*Wren*, he had said, two turns of the glass ago, in a voice like a rusted gate. *Tell her—the garden. South wall. Tell her I kept—*

Then the tide of the thing had come back over him and he had screamed without sound, and Noor had driven more of herself into the rite, and Wren had not let go of his hand even when his grip crushed her fingers white.

Now the moon stood complete overhead, and the thirteenth turn began.

Noor felt it as a sound first—not with her ears but with the gift Maghda had tried to bind shut, the hearing that heard the true voice of land and stone and water. The binding itself spoke: a note so deep and old it was less a sound than a fact, the way gravity is a fact, the way winter is. It had been struck by the first kings, generations before anyone living was born, and it had sounded without stopping ever since, and every king who sat the throne had been a finger laid across the string, keeping the note alive with the flesh of their own selves.

*Unbind*, Noor thought at it, pouring everything into the thought—Maghda's rite, the salt, the iron, the memory of her teacher's burned-out eyes, the smell of the cottage she might already have begun to forget. *Unbind. You were never asked. None of this was ever asked.*

The note bent.

It was the strangest thing she had ever felt: a constant becoming a variable, the fixed pitch of centuries sliding, wavering, searching for somewhere to resolve. The salt circles blazed white without light. The iron nails wept rust that ran like blood toward the center of the stone. And Aldric—

Aldric opened his mouth and sang.

Not the god's voice. His. It was cracked and thin and human and it was not any song Noor knew, some court air or soldier's marching rhyme or half-remembered lullaby, and beside her Wren made a sound like a wound and gripped his hand harder, and Noor understood: he was holding himself. All those fragments surfacing in decrees and marginalia and moments of terrible clarity—he had gathered every piece of himself he could reach, and now he spent them, one held note against one held note, mortal endurance pitched against divine hunger, and whichever broke first would decide everything.

*Hold*, Noor told him, though she couldn't know if he heard. *Hold. We have you.*

The moon reached its zenith.

For a heartbeat the whole crypt went silent—the kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the eardrum, that Noor had only felt once before, standing in a dead orchard in Hollowmere the night before it bloomed impossibly. A silence like held breath. Like a page turned to blank.

Then the binding stone cracked.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible, holy thing about it—it cracked the way old ice cracks on a thawing pond, a single clean report, and then a web of hairline fractures spreading out from beneath the king's knees, and out of the cracks came not fire and not darkness but *air*. Cold, clean, ordinary night air, flowing up from somewhere deeper than the crypt, from wherever the god had been pressed down into all these centuries, and with the air came a sound Noor's gift translated for her in a language older than words:

*A name.*

The god remembered its name. Noor heard it—she would carry the hearing of it in her body long after, she knew, in the way you carry weather—and it was not a demon's name or a devil's name. It was a god's name, old and honest, the name of a thing that had once been prayed to in good faith under open skies, before men caught it and bound it into service and made it wear the crown like a bit in its teeth. And having remembered, the god did the thing Noor would think about for the rest of her life.

It let go.

The struggle simply stopped. The vast hungry pressure that had strained against her for hours, days, weeks—the presence that had answered prayers in Hollowmere and worn her mentor's voice and offered her bargains with a truthful face—released its grip on the throne, on the stone, on the man, the way a fist releases, finger by finger, deliberately, completely. The last of it flowed upward through the cracks and out—out of the crypt, out of the cathedral, out of the shape of kingship it had inhabited longer than any living memory—and Noor felt it pass overhead like a great bird leaving a tower, felt the moonlight touch it once, and felt it

*stop.*

Not destroyed. Released. A god dying properly, at last, under the honest sky, the way the old gods had always wanted. The difference mattered. Noor wept without deciding to, tears freezing on her cheeks, and she could not have said whether they were for the god or the king or the girl she had been when this started, running through a smuggler's pass with guilt at her heels.

The crypt was quiet. Truly quiet.

Aldric fell.

* * *

Wren caught him before Noor could move—of course she did; Noor would learn that about Wren over and over in the years after, that she moved fastest toward falling things. She took his full weight against her shoulder, lowered him to the stone with a care that looked practiced and was really just love wearing practicality's clothes, and Noor scrambled to keep the salt circle intact around them both, kneeling awkwardly, hands shaking now that shaking was allowed.

He was breathing. That was the first fact, and Noor held onto it like a rail in a flood. Breathing—raggedly, hiccuppingly, the breathing of someone remembering how. His face had changed. Noor realized she had never seen it change before; in the throne room, magnificent and hollow-eyed, reciting his own old love letters as scripture, the king's face had been a mask of perfect stillness. Now it moved. Now it was a face. Lines she hadn't seen, gray at temples that the court portraits smoothed away, a scar through one eyebrow no god would permit its vessel.

His eyes found Wren's face and stopped there.

"Wren," he said. His voice was wrecked—rust and gravel and disuse—but it was a voice, a man's voice, saying a woman's name.

"Yes." Wren's composure had finally broken; tears ran freely down her face and she made no move to hide them. "Yes. Here."

"I was—" He stopped. Frowned. Noor watched him grope through the fog of years the way a man gropes through a dark house after the candles die. "How long?"

"Two years," Wren whispered. "Since you stopped sleeping."

"Two—" He closed his eyes. Opened them. Something moved behind them, some enormous arithmetic of loss being done and set aside because it could not be done tonight. His hand—the one still holding Wren's, and Noor saw now that he had not released it, that his grip had gone from crushing to gentle without letting go at all—his hand tightened. "I don't remember why I'm holding your hand."

Wren laughed, wet and broken and real. "You don't have to."

"I want to." It came out fierce, immediate, a reflex from somewhere deeper than memory—the same place, perhaps, where he had kept a fragment of himself alive for two years inside a god's belly. "I don't remember why. But I'm not letting go. Whatever I was doing—whatever I was fighting for—I think it was this. I think it was always—" His gaze drifted, found Noor, sharpened with an effort. "You're not court. Smuggler's boots. Salt-burned hands." He tried to sit up; failed; settled for regarding her with the frank assessing look of a man who had once, perhaps, been worth loving before he was worth worshipping. "What did you do?"

"The rite," Noor said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The unbinding. My teacher wrote it—or kept it—and I paid—" She stopped. Touched her own temple, where something should have been. There was a smooth place in her memory, a socket where something had been removed, and she could feel the shape of the absence but not the content. Roads, probably. She had known it would take roads. "It doesn't matter. You're free. The god is dead—no. Not dead. *Finished.* There's a difference. Your priests will need to understand the difference."

"There are no priests," Aldric said quietly. "There haven't been priests in two years. Only the Devoted." Something crossed his ruined face—shame, grief, the particular horror of a man reading accounts of atrocities signed with his own hand. "Whatever was done in my name—"

"Wasn't yours," Wren said. Flat. Final. The cartographer correcting a false map. "We'll deal with it later. All of it. Tonight you're alive, and that's the entire ledger."

He looked at her for a long moment. And Noor, kneeling outside the circle with salt stinging her palms, watched something happen between them that needed no words and no memory—that was older than the binding, older than the crown, some thread that the god had stretched nearly to breaking but never severed. Aldric raised their joined hands, slowly, wincing, and pressed his lips to Wren's knuckles like a man performing a rite far older than any in the cathedral above them.

"I don't know why," he said again, wondering. "But it feels like grace."

"That," said Wren, "is roughly the correct direction to be confused in."

Noor laughed. It startled her—laughing here, in a cracked crypt, over a dying god's grave—and once started it wouldn't stop, shaky and tear-soaked, and then Wren was laughing too, and even Aldric, huddled in his own body like a man returned to a house whose rooms had all been rearranged, even he smiled, thin and wondering, and the three of them knelt together in the ruins of a two-hundred-year crime while the thirteenth moon climbed the sky above the cathedral and asked nothing of anyone at all.

* * *

Later—how much later Noor couldn't say; time had gone strange, pooled and slow like honey—they climbed.

It was Wren who insisted. "The court will be at vigil," she said, helping Aldric to his feet, bearing his weight without comment when he swayed. "The solstice vigil. Every noble house, every Devoted captain, all of them in the nave waiting for the manifestation, the blessing, the annual miracle." Her mouth twisted. "Which isn't coming. Ever again. Someone needs to tell them why, and I think—" she looked at him, and her voice gentled in a way Noor had never heard it do, not once on the road, not even at their tenderest—"I think it should come from a throat, not a rumor."

"They'll kill me," Aldric said. Not afraid. Almost curious.

"They'll try to worship you," Wren corrected grimly. "That's worse. Come on. Stairs."

The stair from the crypt was narrow, spiraled, ancient—older than the cathedral built around it, Noor realized; the whole great church had been raised over the binding like a lid over a well. Her gift read the stones as they climbed: this passage had been walked in fear for centuries. Servants bringing food to whoever was king. Kings descending alone. Once, faint as a fingerprint, the ghost of a woman's footsteps, decades old, pacing down and down and down—Wren, she guessed, or someone like Wren. Some other cartographer who had loved some other king.

The moon met them at the top.

Noor stepped out into the cathedral proper and stopped, because she had prepared for guards, for Devoted, for blades, and instead she had walked into light. Ten thousand candles, maybe more, every altar and sill and sconce lit for the vigil, and above them the great rose window, and through it the thirteenth moon—full, complete, midwinter-white—pouring down onto the flagstones in a spill of colored silver.

And the cathedral was full. Packed. Nobles in furs, soldiers in the white and gold of the Devoted, common folk crowded at the western doors—all of them kneeling, faces upturned, waiting for their god to manifest in his king.

Every face turned toward the shadowed stair.

Noor saw it happen in a hundred faces at once: recognition, disbelief, dawning terror. The king, alive, walking upright, uncrowned, leaning on a disgraced cartographer's shoulder, following a salt-burned stranger in smuggler's boots. Somewhere in the ranks of the Devoted a voice began to shake out a hymn—*he rises, he rises*—and Noor watched Aldric flinch as if struck.

"Hold," he murmured, so low only she and Wren could hear, and Noor realized he was saying it to himself, using the last word of the rite like a rope thrown back to a drowning man. Then, louder, in a voice that carried—thin, damaged, unmistakably human:

"My people."

The hymn faltered.

"I am not your god." Each word cost him; Noor could see it costing. "I was never your god. There is no god in your crown. There never was. There was only a prisoner, wearing each of us in turn, and tonight—" his voice cracked, and he did not hide it, and somehow that breaking did more than any strength could have—"tonight we let it go. Tonight it died honestly, under an honest moon. And tonight I return to you something better than a miracle: the truth. Kneel if you must. But kneel to each other. I have seen what happens when you kneel to crowns."

Silence. Vast, candle-lit, ten-thousand-hearted silence.

Then, from among the Devoted, one voice—young, cracking, familiar. Tam. Noor knew it before she found his face, the boy from Hollowmere, the debt she'd saved and been saved by. He was standing. He was taking off his white robe. He folded it, carefully, like a boy folding his schoolwork, and set it on the flagstones, and knelt—facing the king, but Noor understood, watching him, that he wasn't kneeling in worship. He was kneeling the way people kneel at gravesides.

Others followed. One, then ten, then a hundred—not all, not everywhere, and Noor knew the reckoning would be long and ugly and full of grief—but enough. Enough to begin.

Aldric's knees buckled. Wren took his arm. And over the crowd's bowed heads, as Wren steered him toward the shadowed south aisle and whatever came next, the king turned and looked back at Noor—one moment, eye to eye, mortal to mortal.

"Thank you," he said. And then, with a ghost of the man he must have been before all this, before the crown, some dry humor surviving like a seed in frozen ground: "Whoever you are."

"You'll learn my name," Noor said. "I'm told I write it down now. For safekeeping."

His brow furrowed—questioning—but Wren was drawing him away, and Noor let them go, and stood alone in the candlelight while the thirteenth moon rode high above the rose window, whole and cold and asking nothing.

Her hands were still salt-burned. Her head still had that smooth hollow where the roads used to live. Tomorrow there would be everything: the abdications, the reckonings, the kingdom learning to stand unbowed, the long work of telling truth to power and power to truth. Tomorrow she would have to find out exactly what she had lost, memory by memory, and grieve what couldn't be recovered.

But tonight—tonight the moon rose complete, and the rite held, and a man wept in a cartographer's arms without knowing why, and it felt, against all reason and all arithmetic, like grace.

Noor looked up at the moon a while longer.

Then she turned to find Wren, because whatever else had burned away in her, she knew the direction she wanted to walk.
