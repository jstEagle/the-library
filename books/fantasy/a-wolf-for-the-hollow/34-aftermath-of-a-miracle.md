## 34. Aftermath of a Miracle

Tam woke on the cathedral steps with snow in his eyelashes and no idea how long he had been kneeling.

His knees had gone past aching into that deep, distant numbness, as if the cold had climbed up through the stone and settled into the marrow like a tenant who meant to stay. Around him, the great plaza of the capital lay scattered with white-robed figures—hundreds of them, thousands, the Devoted of the Crowned God, some still kneeling where he knelt, some wandering the frozen flagstones like people searching a burned house for something that had once been theirs. Nobody was singing. That was the strangest part. For thirteen moons there had always been singing: dawn hymns and dusk hymns and the low wordless humming the novices made while they scrubbed the reliquary floors, a sound like bees in a wall. Now the silence pressed down so hard Tam could hear his own heart working, and the bells above him hung motionless in the pale morning, and not one of them rang.

The king had wept yesterday. He had come out of the cathedral hollow-eyed and mortal and small in a way no king should ever be small, and he had wept on the steps in front of all of them, and a woman named Sera from the eastern delegation had caught him when his legs gave out. Tam had watched from the third rank and felt something crack in his chest that he suspected would never knit closed quite straight.

Because Tam had known. Not everything—God, not everything—but enough. He had seen the stranger with the cropped hair and the road-worn boots slip past the reliquary gate on the solstice night, and he had let her go. He had stood aside with his hand still warm from where she'd gripped his sleeve in the tannery yard at Hollowmere, a lifetime ago, when he was only a boy with a fever and she was only a neighbor's runaway cousin who smelled of woodsmoke and pine sap, and he had chosen debt over faith in the space of one held breath. And then he had reported the breach anyway, because he was Devoted and the Devoted reported breaches, and the report had sealed the quarter, and people had died on the threshing floor of Saint Vessa's undercroft for it.

He did not know all their names. That was the worst of it. He had helped carry two of them out afterward, wrapped in altar cloths because there weren't enough shrouds, and he hadn't known their names, and they had died believing in a thing that wore his god like a coat.

*It wasn't a lie,* he told himself, the way he'd been telling himself since midwinter night. *It healed people. It fed them.* His mother's cough had gone in a single evening after she touched the hem of the king's processional robe; old Bren the miller had walked again after four years on sticks. Those things had happened. You could not unheal a healed woman. You could not tell his mother, *the thing that took your pain away was an impostor wearing a stolen crown,* and expect anything in her face but that terrible floundering loss, the look of a person whose floor has been pulled out from under them and who keeps trying to stand on air.

But it had worn someone. That was what the king's own mouth had confessed, there before the assembled court and the crowd beyond the doors, his voice cracked and human and nothing at all like the silver bell-tone the god had used through him: a thing bound by the first kings, passed down throne to throne, drinking devotion for centuries while the men inside it drowned slowly. Thirteen moons to make this vessel permanent. Twelve spent. One left—and then a runaway apprentice and a disgraced cartographer and a dead king's letters in the margins of old maps, and the thirteenth moon had risen over a god set free instead of a man erased.

Free to die, the stories said now, whispered in the markets like contraband. The god had remembered its name and chosen to die. Tam turned the phrase over the way you turn a river stone, feeling for the shape of it, and could not make it sit comfortably in his hand. Gods were supposed to be permanent. If gods could die—if they had been dying all along, or worse, wanting to die—then what had he given three years of his life kneeling for?

Snow kept falling, fine and dry as ash.

* * *

By noon the scattering had begun.

Tam saw it happen the way you see a tide go out—not any single departure but a slow universal loosening. First the eastern delegation, packing their banners with the stiff efficiency of people who have decided not to discuss something. Then whole families from the river provinces, loading carts in the temple yard, their children strangely quiet. Brother Anselm, who had taught Tam his first canticles, walked out the west gate carrying nothing but his staff, and when Tam called his name the old brother paused, looked back with eyes like wells, and said only: "I have to find out what I actually believe, lad. I'll know it when I'm far enough from here to hear it." Then he went, and the snow closed behind him like a curtain.

Others stayed. Tam understood them too, which frightened him more than the leaving did. Sister Maren still swept the north porch every morning at the same hour, though there was no longer any liturgy commanding it, her broom whispering over stone in rhythms older than her grief. The kitchen brothers kept baking the festival loaves because the ovens were hot and hands need work. Faith, Tam was learning, was less like a lamp you blew out and more like a house you couldn't stop living in even after you knew the foundations were cracked—that your body kept rising for matins whether or not anyone was listening, that your knees folded themselves at the old hours out of pure habit, that habit was its own kind of prayer, maybe the most honest kind, because nobody was watching and nothing answered.

Nothing answered. He tested it, on the second morning, alone in his cell, the way Noor must once have tested it in Hollowmere. He knelt and prayed—the ordinary words, the novice's bedtime petition, *keep me through the dark, keep the ones I love through the dark*—and he waited, listening with everything in him.

Silence. Ordinary, enormous, human silence, the same silence that had existed before the crowned thing came, the silence that was apparently what the universe had always actually been made of.

He sat back on his heels and found, to his surprise, that he was crying, and that the crying was not entirely grief. Some of it was relief, vast and guilty and clean, like a splint cut off a healed bone. Nothing was listening. Which meant nothing was watching. Which meant that whatever he did next belonged entirely to him.

That was terrifying. That was also, he was beginning to suspect, what everyone else got to feel all the time.

* * *

On the fourth day he took off the robes.

He did it in his cell at dawn, folding each piece with the care the garments deserved even if the god they'd honored hadn't—the inner tunic first, then the white surcoat with the sun-and-crown worked at the breast in gold thread, three years of careful stitches, his own among them. The wool still smelled of incense and candle smoke and the particular cold-clean smell of the cathedral crypt. He knelt for a moment with the folded pile in his lap, waiting to see if he would feel struck down, excommunicated, emptied.

He felt cold. He felt like a boy from Hollowmere who owned one good shirt and it was currently on his back.

There was a place outside the walls where he'd gone as a novice on his rare free afternoons—a plum orchard gone feral on the south slopes, trees planted by someone dead two hundred years and abandoned to their own arrangements. The ground beneath the oldest tree was soft even in winter, workable with a spade, and Tam dug until his shoulders burned, and laid the robes in the hole, and stood a long moment looking at that white rectangle against the dark earth like a bed made for someone who wasn't coming.

He should have said something. A rite, a verse, even a clumsy improvised word. But every holy phrase he knew had been written for the crowned thing, or bent by it, or breathed so often in its service that using it now felt like speaking over a grave the name of the wrong person. So he just said, "I'm sorry," which was true, and "thank you," which was complicated, and "goodbye," which was the easiest of the three, and shoveled the earth back in.

He patted the mound flat. He marked it with a river stone, because that seemed right somehow—he'd heard, somewhere in the weeks of instruction, that before the crown-thing there had been other gods, honest ones, who slept under cairns and asked only to be remembered. Maybe the robes could serve them. Maybe the ground didn't care what it was given, so long as something was finally, properly buried.

Then he shouldered his pack—three years' worth of a life reduced to a spare shirt, a carving knife, a half-wheel of cheese from the kitchen brothers who'd pressed it on him without discussion, and a letter of dismissal signed by a king whose hand still shook—and he walked out the south gate of the capital without looking back, because he'd learned from watching the others that if you looked back you would stop, and he was not going to stop.

Hollowmere was nine days' walk in ordinary weather. In the teeth of winter, with the Thornfells wearing their white crowns, it might take twelve. He had coin for an inn perhaps twice, if he was careful. He had no idea what waited for him there: whether his mother still lived in the same lane, whether the orchard the false god had bloomed overnight had held its miracle or collapsed back into honesty, whether anyone remembered the fevered boy the Devoted had taken north three years ago or whether he'd been folded into the village's past like a pressed flower, flat and fading.

He walked anyway. It occurred to him, somewhere past the first milestone, that walking toward a place you couldn't imagine anymore was its own strange kind of faith. Perhaps that was the only kind left.

* * *

The road taught him things the cathedral never had.

On the fifth day, in a village whose name he never learned, he stopped at a well and an old woman drew him water without being asked, looked at the sun-faded mark on his shoulder where the surcoat usually covered it, and said, "You're one of them that's walking home, then." Not a question. Half the roads, he was discovering, were thick with ex-Devoted drifting homeward like leaves after a storm, and the villages had learned to read them, and mostly—this undid him—they were kind. Bread handed over fences. A barn loft offered with a jerk of the thumb. Once, terribly, a woman who seized his hands and begged him to pray over her sick son anyway, because whatever had lived in the king, part of it had been real, hadn't it, surely some of it had been real—

"I can't," Tam had said, and watched hope gutter out of her face, and hated himself, and hated the truth, and then—because he couldn't do either of the things she asked, neither lie nor heal—he had sat with the boy for two hours anyway, telling him a long rambling story about a fox and a cartographer, until the fever broke on its own around midnight, as fevers sometimes do. The mother wept over Tam's hands as if divinity itself had visited. He left before dawn, shaking, because he could not carry that weight. He was not a god. He was barely, on most days, a good man. He was learning the difference, mile by mile, and the difference was heavier than the robes had ever been.

On the eighth day it snowed properly, the sky coming down in earnest, and Tam sheltered in a wayside shrine he'd have walked past a year ago. It was nothing—one room, a stone bench, a niche where some pre-crown god had presumably once sat and now sat no longer. Someone had left a stub of tallow candle and, wedged in the niche, a scrap of paper gone soft with damp. He smoothed it out. A child's handwriting, laborious: *if you are real thank you for the apples. if you are not real thank you anyway.*

Tam laughed until it turned into something else, sitting alone in that little room with the snow hissing at the door, laughing and weeping in equal measure like an idiot, like a man, like a boy from Hollowmere. Thank you anyway. There was a theology in it better than anything the canticles had taught him. He tucked the paper back in the niche, lit the candle stub for whoever came next, and slept better than he had in three years.

* * *

He crested the last ridge above Hollowmere at dusk on the twelfth day, and stopped, and stood in the falling light with his breath smoking, and let the village hit him all at once.

It was smaller than he remembered. That was the first thing—everything was smaller, the mill, the meeting hall, the crooked line of chimneys, as if the three years in the capital had swollen him and the world had not kept pace. Smoke rose from the houses in straight gray columns. Down in the hollow, lamps were being lit one by one, window by window, the way they always had, the way they would tomorrow night and the night after, and the ordinariness of it was so fierce and complete that Tam's throat closed. Here was the thing the crowned god had never understood, he thought. Here was the thing worth all those hymns and none of them: lamplight going on regardless. People keeping supper warm. The small stubborn machinery of a place that intended to survive whatever came down out of the mountains, gods or kings or winters without end.

And the orchard—Grandmother Ilse's orchard, the one that had bloomed overnight in the first impossible moon—was heavy with summer fruit in defiance of the season. Even from the ridge he could see it, a bright impossibility of blossom and fruit against the snow-hung fields, and he could see figures moving between the rows with baskets, harvesting in the blue dusk, and he heard, faintly carried up on the cold air, someone laughing.

They were eating the miracle. Whatever it had been—false blessing, parasitic gift, a god's careless flourish—they had taken the fruit and made cider and pies and stores for winter, because they were hollow-village people and that was what hollow-village people did with whatever the world dropped on them. Something in Tam's chest unlocked at the sight, some knot he'd been carrying since the capital. Maybe that was the answer to the question he'd carried down the whole road: not whether the miracle had been true, but what you did with it after. The fruit didn't care what had bloomed it. The eating was the true part.

He started down. Halfway to the first fence his mother came out of their cottage—older, grayer, squinting against the dusk—and went still, the way you go still when a shape on the road might be anything, and then she said his name in a voice scraped down to almost nothing, and then she was running, and Tam dropped his pack in the snow and ran too, and the meeting knocked both of them into the drift by the gate and neither of them cared.

Later, much later, fed and thawed and sitting in the kitchen that was exactly as small and exactly as warm as memory, she told him the news the way villages tell news—in circles, sideways, trailing off and circling back. The king had abdicated half his sacred authority and told the truth about the bindings, and nobody fully believed it yet, and everybody talked of nothing else. Grandmother Ilse guarded her orchard like a dragon and gave the fruit away to anyone who came. And the runaway—Noor, the one who'd saved him from the choosing, the one the whole village half-believed had brought the god down single-handed—she was alive, they said. She and the cartographer had been seen on the north road not a week past, walking toward the hollow with a tall thin man in plain clothes who wept at the taste of bread.

Tam set down his cup very carefully, so as not to spill it, because his hands had begun to shake.

"They're coming here?"

"So the miller's boy says." His mother studied him across the table, with the particular gaze mothers keep for exactly this. "You could wait and see. You owe her a debt, I'd think."

"I know what I owe her."

"Then you'll want to be here when she comes." His mother rose to bank the fire, and paused, and looked back at him through the ember-light. "Because she will, love. Folk like that always circle home eventually. It's the hollow places that hold them."

Outside, the winter moon was rising over the Thornfells—full, and whole, and only a moon. Tam sat in the small warm kitchen of the world that had survived, and listened to the wind coming down off the mountains, and waited.
