## 35. The Blank Map

Noor woke on a pallet that was not her own, in a room she did not know, beneath a ceiling of smoke-darkened beams, and for three full breaths she did not understand why her heart was already hammering.

Then she understood, and the understanding was worse than the not-knowing.

She lay still. The room was small and warm—a garret above some shop, by the smell of tallow and dried herbs drifting up through the floorboards. Someone had covered her with a quilt that smelled of lavender and woodsmoke and, faintly, of salt. Her body ached in the deep way that a body aches after it has been used as a channel for something enormous. Her hands, when she lifted them into the grey light from the shuttered window, were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake. Inside, everything was shaking.

She reached for the map.

Not a paper map. The one she had carried since she was six years old, the one Maghda had helped her build without either of them ever naming what they were doing: the smuggler's atlas etched into her memory, pass by pass, ford by ford, every goat-track and drainage tunnel and forgotten pilgrim stair in the Thornfells and the lowlands beyond them. She had carried it through hunger and cold and the burning of Hollowmere's saints. She had carried it under the capital itself, through catacombs no royal cartographer had ever drawn, and it had not failed her once.

She reached for it the way a hand reaches for a lamp in a familiar dark room.

And found nothing.

No. Not nothing. That was the terrible part. She reached and her mind turned over, smooth and featureless, like a stone from a riverbed. There had been a shape there—there had *always* been a shape there, the shape of the world as she knew it, the web of roads and ridgelines and water—and now there was only the place where it had been. A socket. A scar of absence. She could feel the edges of what was missing the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was, probing it again and again, hoping to be wrong.

She sat up too fast. The room tilted. She gripped the pallet frame and breathed through her nose, in and out, the way Maghda had taught her for steadying the gift, though the gift itself was quiet now, muffled, like a voice heard through a closed door.

*All right,* she told herself. *All right. You knew the price. You paid it. Now find out how much of you is left.*

She dressed. Her clothes had been cleaned—someone had washed the stone-dust and blood out of them, and the thought of who that someone must be made her chest tighten with a feeling she could not yet afford to examine. She eased open the door.

The stair creaked down into a narrow shop: dried herbs hanging in bundles, jars of salves, a counter worn smooth by a century of elbows. Empty. Through the front window she could see a street, cobbled, running downhill between tall gabled houses. Morning. Grey-gold light. The capital, then. The city where she had unbound a god three days ago—or two days, or four; her sense of time was as featureless as her map.

She stepped out onto the cobbles and turned to orient herself.

It was like turning in a circle with her eyes shut and finding that even the direction of the sun had been taken from her.

She knew the city. That was the thing—she *knew* it, she had walked it under the ground and memorized its bones, the catacombs and culverts and the old smuggler runs that threaded beneath the cathedral quarter like veins beneath skin. She had come up out of the earth into the cathedral's shadow with the rite still ringing in her blood. She had stood at the binding stone. She had paid.

But knowing a city and being able to *find* your way through it were two different things, and the second one was gone. The streets around her were just streets. They had no names in her head. They pointed nowhere. When she tried to picture the city whole—the way she had always been able to picture any place whole, a living diagram that turned in her mind like a wheel—there was only a grey blank, a page with nothing written on it, and the blankness had weight. It pressed on her from the inside.

*Which way is the river?*

She looked down the sloping street. Water runs downhill. Cities are built around water. Any fool could—

She did not know which way the river was. She could not even say, with certainty, whether the street ran toward it or away from it, because the city in her head had been a map, and the map was gone, and without the map the city was just an enormous animal with ten thousand mouths, all of them identical, all of them open.

Her breath went short.

She had been in tight places before. She had been hunted through the Thornfells by the Devoted with their white-and-gold sashes, had crouched in a frozen culvert listening to boots pass a hand's width above her head, had stood in a burning village square and made herself visible to a god that ate devotion. None of it had felt like this. Fear in the body was something she knew how to carry; you breathed, you moved, you put one foot where the land told you to put it.

But the land was not telling her anything anymore.

She walked. Downhill, because downhill was all she had. The street bent, and bent again, and she lost the direction she had been holding in her mind, and when she tried to turn back toward the herb-shop with the creaking stair she found she could not picture where it was. She had walked out of it not two hundred paces ago and it had already slid away from her like a boat cutting its mooring line. She stood in the middle of the street while carts rattled past, a woman shouting cheerfully at a mule, a boy sweeping a doorstep, and the ordinary morning went on around her while inside her the world came apart.

*Don't panic. Panicking is for people with nowhere to go. Think.*

But thinking required a place to stand, and every place she reached for was gone. Hollowmere—gone. Not faded, not dimmed: *gone*, a hole in her memory with clean edges. She could remember that there had been a village. She could remember the word *Hollowmere* and that it mattered, the way you remember a name on a gravestone matters without remembering the face. She reached for the grandmother with the dead orchard and found a shape like a person made of fog. She reached for Tam, the boy she had pulled out of the god's choosing, and found the fog had a boy's outline and nothing inside it.

She reached for Maghda.

The hole there was the deepest of all. She had known, walking into the rite, what order the memories would go in—the rite took the oldest roots first, Maghda had written, in the hand Noor could no longer picture—and she had thought she was ready. She had thought that knowing the price was the same as being able to pay it.

It was not. Nothing could have made it not be.

She was standing in the street with her hand pressed flat against a wall she did not recognize, breathing in little sips, when a man in a Devoted's undyed wool stopped three paces away and looked at her with concern.

"Sister? Are you unwell?"

The white sash at his shoulder was the plain sort worn by novices and lay brothers. His face was young and kind and utterly unremarkable, and she realized she was staring at him with the wild look of a person who has just understood that she is lost—not lost in the way travelers are lost, which is a problem with a solution, but lost in the way the dead are lost, unmoored from every landmark that ever meant *home*.

"I'm—" Her voice came out wrong. She tried again. "I'm looking for the river."

"The river?" He smiled, relieved to be of use. "You're nearly on top of it, sister. Two streets down, past the chandlery with the blue door, then follow the sound."

"Thank you." The words came out of her like something pulled on a hook. "Thank you."

She walked the way he pointed. Two streets. Past the chandlery with the blue door. She repeated it under her breath like a charm, because that was what directions were to her now: charms, spells that lasted only as long as the words were in her mouth. At the corner she looked back to fix the chandlery in her mind and found that the street she had come down had already become one street among a hundred, indistinguishable, a thread pulled from a cloth that would never be woven back the same way.

The sound of water reached her. She followed it the way a blind woman follows a hand on her arm, and came out onto a quay where the river ran wide and brown-green between stone embankments, barges sliding, gulls screaming, the far shore a smear of roofs in the morning haze.

She stood at the rail and shook.

It was not graceful. It was not the clean grief of the rite, which had at least been *hers*, chosen, each memory a coin she laid down with her own hand. This was the morning after, the bill come due, the price made real in the plain light of day. She had given up the roads. She had known she was giving up the roads. But she had thought—she had *assumed*, the way you assume the sun will rise—that underneath the roads there would still be Noor, and underneath Noor the world, and that she would walk out of the cathedral into the same life she had walked out of, only lighter.

She had not understood that the roads *were* the world. That she had never known how to be a person except as a person *in motion*, a creature of paths, a thing that moved through the land the way blood moves through a body. Take the paths away and what was left was a body standing at a rail, watching a river it could not name, in a city it could not leave.

Because that was the other thing. The thought arrived quietly, the way the worst thoughts do, and sat down beside her: *I cannot find my way out of this city.*

She had a plan, once. She and Wren had talked about it in the cave, in the half-dark, in the tenderness of the halfway moon—go south, go anywhere, let the land choose. And Noor had smiled and said *the land will tell me where to go*, because the land had always told her, because she had never in her life been lost.

Now the land was silent. And Wren was somewhere in this city of ten thousand identical streets, and Noor could not have found her if she walked until her boots wore through.

* * *

She did not know how long she stood at the rail. The barges came and went. A fishwife argued price with a cook. The gulls wheeled and screamed their one grey note. Somewhere behind her a bell rang the hour, and she flinched at it—old habit, the gift flinching at loud holy things—and the flinch told her the gift was not dead, only muffled, only sleeping behind a closed door, and that was somehow worse, because it meant the door was locked from the inside, by her own hand, and no one had given her the key.

*You could go back down,* whispered a voice that was not a voice, that was only her own fear wearing its most reasonable coat. *You could find the catacombs. You knew them. You could—*

She had known them. That was the trap in the sentence. The catacombs were in the map, and the map was gone, and the entrance she had used was a memory with clean edges around a hole.

"Noor."

She turned.

Wren was coming along the quay at a half-run, cloak flying, hair escaping its braid, and her face—her face was the first thing in the whole grey morning that Noor's heart recognized without being told. Not a road. Not a map. Something older than maps. The body knew it the way the body knows a hearth in a cold room: *that way, that is the warm direction.*

"You're up," Wren said, stopping, catching her breath, searching Noor's face with the particular attention of someone who has been afraid for days and is not done being afraid. "You're up and you're—oh. Oh, Noor." She saw it. Of course she saw it; she had spent her life reading maps, and Noor's face had always been the easiest map she knew. "You went out alone."

"I didn't want to wake you." The words sounded absurd even as she said them. "You were sleeping. You'd barely slept since the—since the stone, and I thought, I'll just walk, I'll just get my bearings—"

"And?"

Noor laughed. It came out cracked. "And the city ate me. I couldn't find the shop, Wren. I walked out of it and it *vanished*. I asked a Devoted brother for directions to the river like some—" Her voice broke and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Like some pilgrim. Like some lost farm girl. I have crossed the Thornfells in a whiteout. I have walked smuggler roads at night with the Devoted close enough to hear them singing. And I stood in a street this morning and I did not know which way the *water* was."

Wren did not tell her it would be all right. Noor loved her for that, later, when she could love things again. What Wren did was step close and take her hand—her right hand, the one that had held the salt and the iron, the one that had paid—and fold Noor's fingers closed around something small and hard.

A river stone. Smooth, grey, ordinary.

"You can't navigate," Wren said quietly. "But I can. It's what I'm *for*, Noor. It's the one thing they never managed to take from me, not the exile, not the falsified maps, not any of it. I have spent my whole life learning how places fit together, and I am not going to let you be lost in a city I have walked for ten years." Her thumb moved over Noor's knuckles. "You carried me through the Thornfells. You carried me under this city when I was ready to lie down and let them find me. You get to be carried for a while. That's how it works. That's how it's *supposed* to work."

"I don't know how to be the one who's lost."

"I know." Wren's mouth did the thing it did—not quite a smile, the shape a smile makes when it's carrying something heavier than joy. "But here's what I know, and I know it the way you used to know passes: the herb-shop is three streets north and one east of this quay. The chandlery with the blue door is on the corner where the poulterer hangs her geese. The river runs south and east, and if you follow the embankment you cannot get lost, because water only goes one way." She lifted their joined hands, the stone between their palms. "And I know where you are. I always know where you are. I've known it since the inn, since you flinched at the bell. So you're not lost, Noor. You're just somewhere you haven't memorized yet."

Noor looked down at the stone in her hand. It was nothing. It was everything. It was a landmark in a world that had been scraped bare, the first mark on the blank map, and it had been put there by the only person whose location she did not need a gift to find.

"The memories," she said. Her voice was very small. "Some of them—I can feel the edges of them. The rite took the roots first, Maghda wrote, but she also wrote that what's paid in truth can be partly—" She stopped. She could not remember the word. She could not remember whether there had been a word. "I wrote things down. Before. Everything I couldn't bear to lose. I gave you the pages."

"I have them." Wren touched the inner pocket of her cloak, over her heart, where the folded pages rode. "Every word. I've been reading them every night while you slept. And this morning I thought—" She hesitated, and Noor saw that Wren, who had faced down a god and held its attention with nothing but her voice, was afraid of this. "I thought maybe tonight I'd read some of them to you. If you wanted. Not to give them back—I don't know if they can be given back. But so you'd know what you paid for. So you'd know it's all still *there*, somewhere, even if it's not in you. A person shouldn't have to be a stranger to her own life."

The river ran. The gulls screamed. Somewhere across the city a bell rang the hour again, wrong, or Noor's sense of hours was wrong too, and none of it mattered, because Wren was standing in front of her with the pages over her heart and her hand out, and Noor took it.

"One condition," Noor said.

"Name it."

"Start with Hollowmere." She swallowed. "I know I loved it. I can feel the shape of the loving without anything inside it. I want— I want to know who I was there."

Wren's fingers tightened around hers. "Then that's where we'll start," she said. "Tonight. But first—" and here the not-quite-smile finally broke all the way through, tired and fierce and alive— "first, you're going to let me walk you home. Three streets north and one east. I'll say it as many times as you need."

They turned from the river together, and Noor did not know which way north was, and for the first time in her life she found that she did not care.
