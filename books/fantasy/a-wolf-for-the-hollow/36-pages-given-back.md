## 36. Pages Given Back

The pages smelled of woodsmoke and tallow, and beneath that, faintly, of Noor herself—of the road-dust she'd carried in her sleeves for years, of the salt she'd handled like a rosary. Wren had not expected the smell to survive. She had expected paper, ink, a task. What she got was a room full of someone.

She kept them in a lacquered box she'd bought from a stall near the fish market, the kind cartographers used for instruments. It was the only box in the city with a lock she trusted, which was its own small irony: Wren Dunmore, who had once falsified the borders of a kingdom, now guarding the truest map she'd ever held. Forty-one pages in Noor's cramped, slanting hand. *Everything I cannot bear to forget,* the first page said, and then, in smaller letters, as if ashamed: *though I suppose I won't know what I can't bear, will I.*

Wren read them alone the first night, by candle, with the door barred, and wept until her ribs ached—not for what was written but for how it was written. Noor had known. She had sat in that cold room above the chandler's shop with midwinter three days off, salt and iron stacked on the table, and she had known exactly what the rite would take from her, and she had written it all down anyway, plainly, without self-pity, the way she did everything. *Maghda's face: long, brown as a walnut, one eyebrow higher than the other. She frowned with the whole of her forehead.* And below it: *If I cannot see her face anymore, at least let me know it was worth seeing.*

Wren had loved a king who wrote love letters he no longer remembered writing. She knew what it was to hold a man's words when the man behind them had gone somewhere unreachable. But this was different. Aldric's letters had been addressed to her; these pages were addressed to Noor herself—a letter across a gap, from a woman to the stranger she would become. Wren was only the courier. The thought steadied her and broke her in equal measure.

* * *

They took a room over a bookbinder's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, because Noor said—on the third day after the thirteenth moon, when they'd finally left the palace physicians and their smelling-salts behind—that the smell of glue and leather calmed her. It was a narrow room with a window that looked out on a courtyard where the bookbinder's apprentice beat vellum every morning at dawn. Noor hated the sound. Wren learned quickly that Noor hated most sounds now, or flinched at them, the way a person flinches at a voice calling a name that is almost theirs.

"You don't have to do this," Noor said the first evening, sitting on the bed with her knees drawn up, watching Wren unfold the first page with the care of a woman handling a reliquary. "Reading them to me. I could just—" She gestured vaguely at the pages. "Read them myself."

"You could," Wren agreed. "But you'd be reading a stranger's diary. This way—" She smoothed the paper flat on her knee. "This way it's a story someone's telling you about a person you might have been. There's a difference. Trust me. I've spent my life telling people where they are. It matters who does the telling."

Noor's mouth twisted—the ghost of her old wry look, though Wren noticed she reached for expressions now the way a woman reaches for tools on a high shelf, testing each one. "And if I hate her? The person I might have been?"

"Then you'll tell me," Wren said, "and I'll put the page away, and we'll try again tomorrow."

That was the covenant, though neither of them named it that. Every night, after the lamps in the courtyard went out and the bookbinder's dog stopped patrolling, Wren would sit in the chair by the window and read. One page. Never more. Noor would lie on the bed or sit cross-legged at its foot, hands wrapped around a cup of whatever the downstairs kettle held, and listen to her own life described to her like weather in a country she'd never visited.

Some things came back.

The fourth night, reading the page headed *HOLLOWMERE*, Wren described the grandmother—Maren, wide as a doorway, hands like bread loaves—and Noor sat up so suddenly the cup nearly went over. "Her garden," she said. "There was a wall, and behind the wall—" Her eyes had gone bright and unfocused, seeing through the plaster wall of the room into something else entirely. "Apples. Dead apples, and then—" She pressed both fists against her mouth. When she lowered them she was crying without any noise at all, which Wren would learn was how Noor cried, silently, economically, as if tears were rationed. "It bloomed overnight. I heard it happen. I heard the roots drinking." She looked at Wren with an expression of naked wonder. "I remember hearing it. Not seeing it. Hearing it."

"That's yours," Wren said quietly. "Keep that one."

Some things came back wrong.

The ninth night, the page about Maghda. Wren read the description of the cottage—the crooked chimney, the herb bundles hanging from the rafters, the door that stuck unless you lifted the latch while pushing—and Noor listened with her head tilted, polite as a courtier, and when Wren finished, Noor said, "That sounds like a lovely place," in a voice with nothing behind it, and Wren understood that the memory had come back as information only, a description without a room around it. Noor heard the words and felt nothing, and worse, she knew she should feel something, and the knowing hurt her more than the absence.

"I'm sorry," Noor said afterward, staring at her hands. "You read it like it matters. I can hear that it mattered. I just can't—"

"Don't apologize for the shape of your grief," Wren said, sharper than she meant to. She softened. "My mother died when I was nine. For years I couldn't remember her face—I could remember that everyone said she had a laugh like a hinge, and I hated that I was remembering a report instead of a woman. Then one day, twenty years later, I caught myself laughing like a hinge, and there she was. Memory comes home by strange roads. You of all people should know that."

Noor laughed—a real one, startled out of her—and then pressed her hand flat over her heart as if the sound itself had been a wound. "That hurt," she said wonderingly. "Laughing hurt. Is that normal?"

"Nothing about you is normal," Wren said, "and thank every god still sleeping for it."

And some things were better rebuilt than remembered.

The smuggler roads were gone. Wren read the pages where Noor had catalogued them—the Shepherd's Cut, the Hollow Way under the capital, the ford at Saltmere and the twelve others like it—and Noor listened the way she might listen to a song in a language she'd spoken as a child, recognizing the music but not the words. On the fourteenth night she stopped Wren midway through and said, "Don't read me that one again. Please."

Wren set the page down. "It hurts?"

"It's worse than hurting." Noor was quiet a long moment. "It's like being shown a portrait of myself and being told, *this is who you are, why aren't you her?*" She looked up, and her eyes were dry and very steady. "Those roads made me. I ran down them before I could write. And now they're just—lines on paper. Someone else's lines." A pause. "But here's the thing, Wren. Here's the thing I figured out today, walking back from the market, getting lost twice." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman standing on new ground and deciding to plant something in it. "I got lost twice, and I wasn't afraid. Old me would have been terrified. Old me never got lost in her life. And I sat on a wall by the tannery and I thought: maybe I don't want her roads back. Maybe I want to find out what happens when I walk without them."

Wren felt the words go through her like a blade sliding between ribs—clean, deep, meant kindly. Because Wren had spent five weeks reading those pages hoping to give Noor back to herself, and here was Noor, gently declining the gift. Not all of it. Just the part Wren had mourned most.

"You're allowed to want that," Wren managed.

"I know." Noor reached over and took the page from Wren's lap, folded it along its old creases, and handed it back. "Keep it anyway. In case I change my mind. That's the other thing I've learned about myself—I change my mind constantly. Apparently that survived."

* * *

Winter loosened its grip by slow degrees. The bookbinder's apprentice began beating his vellum in the grey light instead of the dark, and the courtyard snow went grey, then rotten, then gone. Word came from the palace in fits and starts: the king had abdicated his sacred offices one by one, standing before the assembled court in plain wool and telling them truths that emptied cathedrals. Some priests raged. More, strangely, wept with relief—as if they had all half-known, all those years, that the thing on the throne answered prayers too easily, too warmly, the way a fire answers a moth.

Tam came once, in the second week of thaw, muddy to the knees, having walked the whole way from Hollowmere because he didn't trust horses since the ford. He stood in the doorway of the room holding his cap and looking at Noor as if checking, item by item, that she was still the woman who'd pulled him out of the choosing-circle. Noor looked back at him with the pleasant blankness of a hostess meeting a stranger, and Wren watched the boy's face fall and mend itself, all in the space of a breath.

"He doesn't remember me," Tam said to Wren, not bothering to lower his voice, because Noor had never pretended otherwise with him.

"No," Wren said.

"But she remembers saving me?" He turned to Noor directly. "Do you? The circle. The lot they drew."

Noor considered him with open, unhurried attention. "I remember that I couldn't let it happen," she said slowly, feeling for the words like a woman crossing stepping-stones. "Not the details. But there's a—it's like a scar in the shape of a decision. I know I made it. I know it cost." She tilted her head. "You were the cost's reason."

Tam nodded gravely, satisfied, as if this were better than a full memory. Maybe it was. Before he left he pressed a small cloth bundle into Noor's hands—bread, dark and dense, Hollowmere bread—and said, "From Maren. She said you'd know." Noor broke it open right there and ate a piece standing up, and something moved across her face like cloud-shadow over a field: recognition of a taste without a source. "Tell her," Noor said, with her mouth full, "that whoever she is, her bread argues with my hands. My hands remember it even if I don't." Tam grinned all the way down the stairs.

After he'd gone, Noor sat turning the last piece of bread in her fingers. "Wren," she said. "The pages. The ones about Hollowmere. Read me Maren again tonight. And the orchard. I want to try to build it—not get it back. Build it. Like the bookbinder builds a book out of signatures. Page by page."

"Reconstruction," Wren said. "I can do reconstruction. It's most of what cartography is."

"Then map me," Noor said, and lay back on the bed, and closed her eyes, ready to be told who she had been.

* * *

On a night in early spring, rain ticking against the window, Wren read the last page but one. She had saved it deliberately, the way you save the last of anything. It was dated midwinter eve, the night before the descent beneath the cathedral, and the handwriting had changed—looser, faster, the pen pressed hard enough to tear in places.

*To whoever I am tomorrow,* it began. *If the rite works, you will not know Wren. You will wake in a strange city and a stranger will be kind to you, and you will trust her, because that is apparently the one thing that survives everything: I trust her. Write that down inside yourself where the rite can't reach. Her name is Wren. She falsified maps to save a king and failed, and she crossed the Thornfells with me anyway, and she held the impostor's eyes while I paid the price, and she never once told me to stop. When you can't remember why you love her, do this: watch her hands. She talks with them when she's certain and goes still when she's lying. Watch her read—she reads with her whole body, leaning toward the page like it might lean back. You fell in love with her somewhere on the Shepherd's Cut, at night, in bad weather, and you never told her outright because you're a coward about soft things, so consider this the telling.*

Wren's voice gave out on the last line. She sat with the page trembling in her hands while the rain filled the silence, and Noor sat up slowly on the bed, watching her with an expression Wren couldn't read—had never been able to read, these weeks, because the woman who used to read *her* was gone.

"She knew," Noor said softly. "Before. She knew and she went anyway."

"Yes."

"And she loved me and didn't say it, and then wrote it down to be delivered by proxy." Noor huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Coward about soft things. That tracks. I believe that about her completely."

Wren set the page down on her knee. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone still—which meant, according to the late authority of Noor Thornbury, that she was lying. "You should know," she said carefully, "that I've been reading these to you partly for you. And partly for me. Because I lost her too, Noor. The woman who wrote these. I walked every mile beside her and she's gone the way the roads are gone, and some nights I've sat in this chair and read her handwriting and grieved her like a dead woman sitting six feet away from me eating bread."

The rain ticked. Downstairs, the bookbinder's press thumped once and went quiet.

"I know," Noor said. "I've watched you doing it. Grieving her while feeding her soup." She unfolded herself from the bed and crossed the small room and knelt in front of Wren's chair, taking the page gently from her knee, smoothing it, looking at the torn places where the pen had pressed through. "Here's what I think," she said. "I think you keep waiting for her to come back. And I think she's not coming back, Wren. Not all the way. Some of her is in me—you've seen it come up, the silent crying, the changing of minds, the hands that remember bread. But she's not going to walk back into this body and resume her seat." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and terrible and kind. "So you have a choice. Keep mourning her. Or meet me. The one who's here. Who is, I'll warn you now, also a coward about soft things, and also falling in love with you—fell, I mean, present tense, continuously, it seems to be a chronic condition—"

Wren kissed her. It was clumsy, and the chair arm dug into her hip, and the page crumpled between them, and none of it mattered at all. When they broke apart, Noor was laughing silently, tears running, both at once, economical even in joy.

"There," Wren said unsteadily. "Delivered by proxy. Consider the courier's duty discharged."

"One page left," Noor said, nodding at the box. "The last one. I've watched you skip past it every night like a stone in a stream."

Wren looked at the box. She had skipped it every night, yes. The final page was dated differently—not midwinter, not the chandler's room. The ink was different too, older, faded brown. She had found it folded inside Maghda's rite-book when they'd cleared the cottage after the funeral, and she had never yet had the courage to speak it aloud, because it was in Maghda's hand, and it was addressed to Noor, and it began: *When you are ready to hear this, you will already have lost the roads—and child, I need to tell you what I saw the night the old god first stirred, because there is another binding, deeper than the crown's, and it is waking too.*

Outside, the rain was stopping. Somewhere far north, in the hills above Hollowmere, spring water was finding its way down into the dark places of the world, and things long asleep were turning over in their beds.
