## 37. A Kingdom Without a Crown-Thing

The crown weighed less than Aldric remembered.

That was the first thing he understood, standing before the mirror in the antechamber of the Hall of Audiences while a servant named Pell hovered behind him with trembling hands and a velvet cushion. The circlet of gold and river-pearl—the same one his father had worn, and his father's mother, and the long gray line of kings stretching back past the founding of the cathedral—sat in his palms like something borrowed from a child's dress-up box. Light. Hollow. He turned it over and looked through the band at the wall, and the wall was just a wall.

For eleven years he had believed it heavy. He had felt its weight in his neck each morning of his reign, had carried it the way a man carries a yoke, and he had told himself that this was what kingship was: weight, willingly borne. Now he knew where the heaviness had lived. Not in the gold. In the thing that had worn him as its gold.

"Your Majesty?" Pell ventured. "The Council is assembled. The delegates from the hollow provinces have arrived. And—" the servant swallowed— "the woman Wren says you asked for her. She's waiting by the west door."

"Tell them all to wait a little longer," Aldric said. He set the crown down on the cushion, then thought better of it, and set it on the windowsill instead, where the morning light could get at it and show how ordinary it was. "Tell them the king is learning how to be late. It's a skill I never had the chance to practice."

Pell blinked, bowed too deeply, and fled.

Aldric stayed at the window a moment more. Below, the capital was waking into its first true spring in a year—or rather, its first spring since the twelfth moon of two winters past, when he had stopped sleeping and begun, without knowing it, to be eaten. The city looked thinner than he remembered. Thinner and more stubborn. Bakers' smoke rose crooked from chimneys that had gone unrepaired through the hungry winter; children chased a dog along the Processional; somewhere a bell rang the hour with a crack in its voice that no one had gotten around to mending. He loved it so much it frightened him. That was another thing the impostor had never let him feel properly—love with teeth in it, love that cost.

He touched the windowsill once, lightly, the way a man touches the rail of a ship that has carried him through a storm, and went out to tell the truth.

* * *

The Hall of Audiences had been built to make a single person seem enormous. Everything about it conspired toward that end: the vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of the First King into some kind of light, the pillars carved with kneeling figures, the throne itself raised on seven steps so that petitioners had to speak upward, always upward, their words climbing like pilgrims. Aldric had sat beneath that painted light for eleven years and had never once questioned it. Now he saw the room for what it was—a machine for manufacturing bigness—and he understood that the machine had been running for centuries on fuel no one had ever counted.

He did not take the throne. He had it moved.

There were gasps when the workmen came in, four of them with ropes and rollers, and levered the seat of sacred authority off its dais and dragged it, screeching, to the side of the hall, where it sat among the potted cedars looking embarrassed. In its place, on the seventh step, Aldric set a plain wooden chair—the kind farmers used, the kind he had sat in as a boy in his uncle's kitchen—and climbed up and sat down in it, so that he was elevated exactly as much as a man giving testimony ought to be, and no more.

Below him, the hall filled. The Privy Council in their chains of office, wearing the expressions of men who had rehearsed dignity for a funeral and been handed a wedding instead. The delegates from the hollow villages—Hollowmere among them, a grandmother with flour still on her sleeves who had refused, three times, the offer of a carriage. The generals. The bishops, pale as candle wax. And near the west door, arms crossed, watching him with an expression he had spent two years memorizing in another life and would now have to relearn in this one: Wren.

She had not left yet. She and the Thornbury girl were meant to be gone within the week—he had heard them arguing about routes, about roads, about whether the girl could bear to travel anywhere at all after what the rite had taken from her—but for today she was here, and her presence steadied him the way a fixed star steadies a navigator. *Look there,* it said. *You are not lost if you can find that.*

He stood. The hall went quiet the way snow quiets a field.

"My lords. My ladies. Delegates of the hollows." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He had not spoken publicly since the unbinding—had not trusted what might come out of his mouth—and even now he listened to himself the way a man listens to a floorboard that has creaked before. But the voice was only his own, tired and human, and he pressed on. "I have called you here to confess."

A bishop made a small sound, like a hiccup strangled in vestments.

"Not a confession of sin, though there is sin enough. A confession of fact. What I am about to tell you will diminish this crown. I intend that it should. Some of you will want to call it madness, or sorcery, or a trick of the Thornbury girl and the disgraced cartographer. I ask you to hold your objections until the end, and then to test everything I say against your own memories of the last two years. You will find, I think, that you already know most of it. You simply were never permitted to say it aloud."

He told them.

He told them about the binding stone beneath the cathedral—not the crypt the bishops showed to visiting dignitaries, but the true stone below that, older than the foundation, older than the city, a slab of black rock with chains of salt and iron sunk into it like roots. He told them what Noor had found written there in script older than any alphabet taught in the capital's schools: that the first kings had not been chosen by the gods but had *captured* one. That they had bound it into service—to bless the harvest, to heal the sick, to make the reign prosperous—and that the binding required a vessel, and that the vessel was whoever wore the crown.

"That is what the throne is," Aldric said. "Not a symbol. A socket. For six hundred years, every king and queen of this realm has been a door, and behind the door has stood a thing that was once a god, wearing us the way we wear these robes. It fed on devotion. Your prayers did not rise to heaven, my lords. They pooled here, in this building, and were drunk."

He watched the faces below him and gave them time. This was the discipline of it, he was learning—the restraint the moment demanded. He wanted to shout. He wanted to tear the painted First King down from the ceiling and show them all the lie in his ascending hands. But shouting would make it easy to dismiss, and dismissal would leave the machinery intact, and the machinery was the enemy now, more than any god had ever been. So he spoke evenly, and paused, and let the horror arrive at its own pace, the way frost arrives on a window: slowly, and then all at once.

"The thing is gone," he said. "Unbound, by a rite paid for at a price I will not describe in this hall because it is not mine to describe. It chose, at the end, to die honestly—which is more than my ancestors allowed it in six hundred years. The old gods of this land are not returning. They do not want worship. What they wanted, I am told, was to be permitted to rest. We will discuss what we owe them, and it is not incense."

"The crown," said Chancellor Vess, whose voice had the careful flatness of a man walking across ice, "is therefore... empty."

"The crown is therefore only metal," Aldric said. "Which is what it always should have been."

"And the king?" Vess pressed. "What is the king, if not the vessel?"

Aldric smiled, and found to his surprise that the smile was real. "Tired," he said. "And mortal. And accountable. Which brings me to the second part of the morning's business."

* * *

He had spent nine days drafting it, mostly at night, because sleep was still a country he was relearning the borders of—nine days with Wren across the table from him, correcting his instincts, striking his evasions, refusing to let him soften anything. She had been merciless. She had also, twice, put down her pen and walked to the window and stood there a long time, and he had understood that she was seeing the young cartographer she had been, the falsified maps, the assassination route that had led him to the binding site anyway, and he had let her stand there as long as she needed, because some debts are not paid in words.

Now he read the edict aloud.

It was short. Deliberately short. He had learned from the impostor's example, if from nothing else, that length was a kind of fog, and fog was how power kept people from seeing what was being done to them.

*First:* the doctrine of the King's Divinity—every liturgy, every oath, every schoolbook verse that named the monarch a vessel of grace—was declared void, retroactive to nothing, because it had never been true. The priests would need new liturgies. He offered them the assistance of honest scholars and the threat of nothing at all.

*Second:* the binding stone would be unearthed, documented, and left open to public view. "Let every subject see what was done in their name," he read, "for a secret crime belongs to everyone who benefits from the secrecy, and I have benefited longest of all." Several bishops looked as though they might faint. One actually sat down.

*Third:* the crown's authority over the hollow provinces—the right to tax their shrines, conscript their sons, and name their priests—was surrendered outright. "The hollow places have kept faith with this kingdom longer than the kingdom has deserved it," he read, "and they will govern their own worship, or lack of it, henceforth." In the front row, the grandmother from Hollowmere closed her eyes, and Aldric saw her lips move, and he suspected—not from vanity, but from having spent two years inside a thing that ate prayer—that she was saying thank you to no one in particular, which was, he was coming to believe, the only honest kind of prayer left.

*Fourth,* and here his voice almost failed him, because this was the part Wren had fought him hardest on, and won: the records. Six hundred years of royal annals, carefully curated, every inconvenient reign explained away, every vessel-king's strangeness attributed to temperament or illness. All of it to be opened. All of it to be read against the truth of the stone. "I ask the historians of the future," he read, "to judge my ancestors harshly and me gently, and I acknowledge in advance that this request is the last selfish act of my reign."

He lowered the page. The silence in the hall had a texture to it, dense and listening.

"You are asking us," said General Halloway slowly, "to tell the people that their prayers for two years—for six hundred years—went nowhere. Or worse. That their devotion fed a parasite wearing their king." The general's face was gray. Aldric knew why. Half the army had sworn oaths to the crowned god during the winter of the Devoted. Half the army had *meant* those oaths.

"I am asking you to tell them the truth," Aldric said, "and then to help them build something worth swearing to instead. Yes, there will be rage. There should be. Rage pointed at the truth is a plow; rage pointed at a lie is only a fire. We have had enough fires."

"And the Devoted?" someone asked from the back. "Those who still believe?"

"They grieve," Aldric said. "Let them. Some of them were healed, and the healing was real even if the healer was not—that is the cruelest part of all this, and I will not pretend otherwise. Give them time. Give them bread. Do not give them martyrs." He looked around the hall, and let his voice drop, and for a moment he was not addressing a council but the whole sleeping weight of a kingdom. "We spent six hundred years teaching people that salvation comes down from a throne. The only lesson that undoes that is patience. Be patient with them. God knows—I use the phrase advisedly—God knows none of us comes out of this clean."

* * *

It took until mid-afternoon. There were objections, as he had known there would be—Vess maneuvering for a regency council, a bishop demanding the stone be resealed "for the peace of the realm," a delegate from the eastern valleys asking, with devastating simplicity, who would heal his people now that the false god was dead and the true ones asleep. Aldric answered each one as honestly as he could, which sometimes meant answering *I don't know*, a phrase he suspected no king of this line had ever spoken in this hall, and which landed each time like a small clean bell.

By the end his throat was raw and his hands, he noticed, were shaking—not with fear, he realized, but with the sheer unfamiliar exertion of being entirely present. Two years of being a house with something else living in it. The body was still learning that it belonged to him.

When the hall finally emptied, only Wren remained, sitting where the delegates had left her, turning her hat in her hands.

"Well?" he said. He came down the seven steps—his last descent, perhaps, of the seven steps—and stood in front of her. "Say it. You've been composing your critique since the third clause. You do that thing with your eyebrow."

"You abdicated everything except the parts that matter," she said. "You kept the army, the treasury, the law. You gave away the god-stuff."

"The god-stuff was stolen goods," Aldric said. "I'm returning it. The rest—" he gestured vaguely at the hall, the emptied throne, the painted ceiling with its ascending liar— "the rest is a job. Someone has to sign the grain requisitions. It turns out I'm rather good at paperwork. It's very difficult to be possessed by a document."

Wren laughed—one short, surprised note—and then pressed her lips together, and he saw her eyes go bright, and he did the kindest thing he knew how to do, which was to look away and give her the window and the moment, the way she had given him hers over the drafting table.

"Aldric," she said, when she had herself again. "The Thornbury girl leaves tomorrow. North, with the maps. She asked—" Wren hesitated, and he realized with a strange jolt that she was nervous, this woman who had stared down a god's bargain without blinking— "she asked if you would receive her. Before she goes. She says she owes you an accounting. I told her she doesn't, that the price she paid—" Wren's voice caught on it, the memory of Noor's face in the binding dark, reciting roads to herself like a rosary as they dissolved— "that no one could owe anything after that. But she insists. She says a debt unnamed grows teeth."

Aldric was quiet for a moment. He thought of the girl he had seen only in fragments—through the impostor's borrowed eyes, in the margins of intercepted reports, and once, terribly, in a dream where she had stood in a flooded road and refused every offer he made her. He owed her more than a kingdom contained. She had pulled a knife out of the root of the world, and the handle had been her own life.

"Tomorrow at dawn," he said. "Before the roads fill. And Wren—" he caught her hand, briefly, in both of his, the way you catch something living that might startle— "when she comes, send word to the kitchens. Whatever she's lost, she shouldn't break a fast on an empty stomach. I've been told," and here his mouth quirked, "that memory is a kind of hunger. I intend to feed mine back into shape one meal at a time."

Wren squeezed his hand and went to make the arrangements, her boots quick on the marble, and Aldric stood alone in the great hall with the crown sitting abandoned on its cushion by the door, gathering dust already, and outside the cracked bell rang the evening hour, imperfect and true, and he found that he was humming along with it.
