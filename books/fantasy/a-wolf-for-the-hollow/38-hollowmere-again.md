## 38. Hollowmere, Again

The road to Hollowmere did not remember Noor.

That was the first thing she understood, standing at the top of the ridge where the Thornfell foothills spilled down into the valley like a loosened braid of grey and green. Once—she knew this the way a person knows a scar they cannot recall earning—once this road had lived inside her. Once she could have closed her eyes and felt it wanting to be walked, could have told you where the mud gave way to gravel, where the alders leaned over the ford, where a smuggler's shortcut split off through the bracken behind a lightning-split oak. Wren had read those things back to her from pages written in her own hand, night after night on the journey south from the capital, and each reading had been like pressing an ear to a door and hearing music through it: real, but not hers anymore.

Now she stood at the top of the ridge with the wind pushing at her back, and the road below was simply a road. Strangers' geography. A line drawn by someone else.

"It's down there," Wren said quietly. She had stopped a few paces off, giving Noor the space she'd learned—slowly, clumsily, over months—to need. Her hair was shorter than it had been in the capital; she'd cut it herself with a fish knife outside Corvane, badly, and never let anyone fix it. "Hollowmere. You wrote that the church bell has a crack in it. You said you could hear it even before you saw the village—the bell rings flat, like a coin dropped in a well."

"I wrote that?"

"You did." Wren smiled, small and careful. "You also wrote that the grandmother who saved your life smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke, and that if you ever forgot her face, you should look for hands like birch bark."

Noor looked down at her own hands, curled loose at her sides. *I forgot her face.* That was the truth of it, the plain ugly truth she had been carrying since midwinter like a stone sewn into her coat lining. The rite had taken what it took. Maghda's face first—her teacher's face, gone so smoothly she hadn't even known there was a hole until Wren read her the words *my mentor's eyes were the color of pond ice* and Noor had felt nothing rise to meet them. Then the roads, all of them, every smuggler's pass and shepherd's trail and river-ford she had ever carried in her head, dissolved like salt in warm water. Then Hollowmere itself: the orchard that had bloomed overnight for a dead woman's prayer, the neighbor chosen as a vessel, the trick with the lantern-oil and the false relic that had bought one old woman's life and cost Noor her invisibility forever.

She remembered the shape of those events now, because Wren had given her the pages back and she had read them until the ink wore soft. But remembering a story was not the same as remembering a life. She knew that Old Maren had pressed bread into her hands once before, on the morning Noor fled the village with Wren and a price on her head. She knew it the way she knew the capital had a cathedral. It was knowledge. It was not hers.

"The village will have changed," Noor said. "A year. More than a year."

"Villages don't change," Wren said. "They just get more themselves." Then, after a moment: "We can turn around. We can go anywhere. That was rather the point of everything we did."

Noor looked at her. Wren stood against the wind with her bad map-hand tucked in her coat pocket—the hand that still ached in cold weather, she'd admitted once, from years of drafting in unheated rooms—and her chin lifted in that particular stubborn angle Noor had come to know better than any road she'd ever lost. *Anywhere,* Wren had said, when they finally left the capital behind, when Aldric's abdication edicts were being nailed to church doors across three provinces. *You spent your whole life running from places. I thought maybe we could try walking toward one.*

"No," Noor said. "Down. Before I lose my nerve, which is a thing I apparently used to never have trouble with."

Wren laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and took her hand out of her pocket and held it out, palm up, an offer rather than a demand. Noor took it. They went down together into the valley, along a road that no longer whispered to her, and Noor found, to her surprise, that walking a silent road beside someone whose hand fit hers was its own kind of hearing.

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, wet earth, and underneath it something green and sweet that made Noor stop dead in the lane without knowing why.

"What is that?" she asked.

Wren breathed in. "Blossom. Late apple blossom—it's nearly summer solstice." She glanced sideways. "Your notes say the orchard died twenty years ago. Blight. The villagers burned the trees."

*The orchard that bloomed overnight for a dead woman's prayer.* Noor turned the page of herself, the way she had learned to do, reaching for the story instead of the memory. An old god wearing a crown had answered a grieving grandmother's prayer, and the dead trees had flowered white in a single night, and the whole village had fallen to its knees in joy, and only Noor had heard the wrongness underneath the blessing—something hungry dressed in grace. And yet the trees had kept flowering. Even after the impostor fell, even after the king woke mortal and weeping and the old gods went properly, honestly silent—even then, the orchard had gone on living, as though the miracle, once given, refused to be recalled.

The trees came into view around the bend of the lane, and Noor's breath caught despite everything. They were heavy with white. Petals came loose in the wind and drifted across the path like a slow snowfall falling upward, and beneath them the village of Hollowmere sat exactly as small and crooked and stubborn as her own handwriting had promised: stone walls furred with moss, thatch going soft at the eaves, geese disputing ownership of the well, a church with a squat tower and, faintly, when the wind turned, a cracked bell ringing the hour a half-tone flat, like a coin dropped in a well.

She had written that. She had heard it from the ridge on her first arrival, when she was nobody, when she wanted only to stay forgotten. And the strange grief of it was that she could not hear it now—not the way she once had, not with the land's true voice underneath, the voice that had made her run from Maghda's cottage and walk into a war between gods. The bell was just a bell. The wind was just wind. The world had gone quiet around her, or she had gone quiet to it, and she still did not know which.

Except—

She stopped again, halfway down the lane, and frowned.

"What?" Wren asked instantly, hand moving to the knife at her belt out of habit neither of them had managed to shed.

"Nothing. I thought—" Noor shook her head. "Nothing. Habit."

But it hadn't been nothing. For half a heartbeat, as the petals crossed the path, she had felt something. Not the old hearing, not the land speaking in its long slow voice of root and water. Something smaller. Warmer. Like a hand laid briefly on the back of her neck. Like recognition.

The village had seen them by then. Villages always see strangers long before strangers see villages; Noor remembered writing that too, some night by a fire, and here it was proving true. Doors opened along the lane. Faces appeared above garden walls. A boy of perhaps ten stood up in a cart of hay and pointed and shouted something, and the word carried down the lane on the blossom-wind, and it was not *stranger*, and it was not *Devoted*, and it was not any of the words Noor had braced herself to hear.

It was her name.

"Noor Thornbury!" the boy yelled, delighted, as if announcing a fair. "It's Noor! She's come back!"

And the lane filled.

Not with torches, not with the Devoted in their grey-and-gold, not with the fear she had rehearsed against on the whole long walk north. With people. A woman with flour to her elbows came out wiping her hands. Two old men abandoned a fence repair mid-post. A girl Noor did not know at all ran up close enough to touch Noor's sleeve and then shied away giggling, as though Noor were a story that had stepped off its page. And through it all the names came flying—*you're the one who fooled the Devoted, you're the one the god spoke to, Tam says you drowned the king's soldiers at the ford, Tam says you're the reason he's alive*—and every name landed on Noor like a small warm stone, and none of them belonged to anything she could feel.

Because she didn't remember them. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. These people remembered her—had kept her through a winter of occupation and a spring of freedom, had passed her name hand to hand like bread—and she stood among them holding nothing but secondhand pages. She searched their faces for anything familiar and found only the general kindness of strangers, and under that kindness, worse, a fierce particular love aimed at her like sunlight through a lens, and she did not deserve it, and she could not return it, and her throat closed.

Wren's hand found hers again without looking. Grounding. *I'm here,* the pressure said. *You're not alone in the wrong country.*

Then the crowd parted, the way crowds do, and an old woman came through it leaning on a stick.

Noor knew her immediately, and the knowing hit her sideways, because it wasn't memory at all. It was the description. *Hands like birch bark.* The old woman's hands were exactly that—pale, papery, peeling in fine curling strips at the knuckles—and they were wrapped around a cloth-covered basket, and her face was seamed and fierce and wet-eyed all at once, and when she reached Noor she did not speak. She simply pulled back the cloth.

Bread. Four round dark loaves, still warm, crusted with oats, smelling of yeast and woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath—honey, maybe, or the last of the orchard's apples grated into the dough. Noor's notes had said Maren baked bread the morning Noor fled, and pressed a loaf into her hands at the gate, and said—here Noor had made Wren read the line twice—*said: you come back when it's safe, child, and I'll have the oven hot.*

"You came back," Old Maren said. Her voice was gravel and honey. "I told you I'd have the oven hot."

"I—" Noor's voice cracked. She tried again. "I'm sorry. I don't—I don't remember you. I want you to know that. The rite took—" She gestured helplessly at her own head, at the beautiful ruined architecture of it. "It took almost everything from before midwinter. Wren's been reading my own words back to me for months. But you're not words. You should be more than words, and I can't—"

She would have gone on apologizing, spiraling, doing what she apparently had always done—which was run, even standing still—but Maren took Noor's hands in her birch-bark ones, folded them over the warm bread, and squeezed until the crusts crackled.

"Hush," the old woman said. "Do you think I remember my mother's face? Do you think I remember my husband's voice? I buried both before you were born, girl, and I kept them anyway. Memory isn't the only way to keep a person." She tapped Noor's chest with one papery finger, right over the heart. "You kept me here. Winter, when the Devoted chose me for their vessel and the whole village stood in the snow too frightened to breathe—you stood in front of me. Tam told us. He told us a hundred times, till we were sick of it. You didn't know me from Adam's housecat, and you stood in front of me anyway, and lied to armed men with a smuggler's grin, and got us both out alive." Her grip tightened. "So I don't care what's missing from your head. I know what's in your spine. That's the part I keep."

Noor could not speak. The bread was warm through the cloth, warm into her palms, warmer than anything had a right to be, and around them the villagers had gone quiet in the way of people witnessing something they'd been waiting a long time for, and the petals came down through the light, and somewhere the cracked bell rang the quarter-hour flat as a dropped coin.

"There," Maren said gruffly, when Noor's breathing steadied. "Now. You'll stay at mine—don't argue, the inn's full of pilgrims come to gawk at the orchard, and half of them snore. And you'll eat. And tomorrow you'll tell me everything, and I'll tell you everything, and between us we'll stitch the year back together. That's how it's done. Nobody remembers a life alone."

* * *

Evening found Noor alone in Maren's garden, because she had learned—was learning, slowly, the way one learns any new craft—that solitude chosen was different from solitude imposed, and she needed a little of the first to keep the second from ever finding her again.

The garden was small and ferociously tended: beans on hazel poles, onions in strict rows, a beehive humming drowsily against the south wall. Beyond the wall, the orchard glowed faintly in the dusk, white-on-grey, dropping petals into the long grass. Noor sat on the bench Maren had ordered her to sit on, with a blanket over her knees because Maren did not accept arguments about summer evenings, and watched the last light go amber and then violet over the Thornfells.

Wren appeared at the gate with two cups of Maren's cider and handed one over without asking, which was its own kind of language by now. She sat close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while they drank in silence and listened to the bees settle.

"Tam came by," Wren said eventually. "While you were washing. He wanted to see you, but Maren told him tomorrow, and he took it well, for a boy who buried his robes and walked forty miles home." She turned her cup. "He looks older. He looks—better. He asked whether you'd stay."

"What did you say?"

"That I didn't know. That it was yours to say." Wren paused. "He said something odd, though. He said the village needs a keeper. He said since the god-thing fell, the hollow places have been—restless. Quiet, but restless. Like a house after a funeral. He said old shrines in the hills are waking up, honest ones, sleepy ones, and somebody ought to walk the edges and see to them. He said his gran used to talk about such folk. Wolf-keepers, she called them. Guardians for the hollows."

Noor went very still.

Because she had felt it. All evening, sitting in the garden, she had felt it again—that touch on the back of her neck, that warm small attention, coming not from the land's old deep voice but from something nearer, newer, humbler. The orchard. The shrine-stones. The hollow places themselves. They were not speaking to her the way the land once had. They were simply *noticing* her, the way a dog notices the person who once fed it, and the noticing felt less like a burden than like—an invitation. A door left unlatched.

"Wren," she said slowly. "When we set out from the capital. When you said we could walk toward somewhere instead of running from everywhere." She turned, and in the dusk Wren's face was half shadow and half orchard-light, and Noor loved her with a completeness that needed no memories at all—this, at least, the rite had never touched, because it had never been stored in the past. It lived entirely in the present tense. "I don't think I'm finished hearing. I think it's changed. I think it's smaller and stranger and mine. And I think—" she looked out at the hills going blue-black, at the valleys between them, all the hollow places full of sleeping shrines and forgetting villages, "—I think I know what I want to be now. But I want to see it written down first. In my own hand. Will you—"

"Tonight," Wren said, already reaching into her coat for the worn leather folio, the pages, the stub of pencil she carried everywhere now like a relic of her own. "After the bread. I'll read you everything, and you'll correct everything, and we'll start a new book." She smiled, and it was the smile from the ridge, from the cave-in, from the cathedral stairs—stubborn, certain, hers. "First blank page, first new road. Where does it go, wolf-keeper?"

Noor looked out at the darkening hollows and felt, faint and unmistakable, a path waking up somewhere in the hills—unmapped, unwritten, wanting to be walked.

"I don't know yet," she said, and found that she was smiling too. "But I think it wants us to find out."
