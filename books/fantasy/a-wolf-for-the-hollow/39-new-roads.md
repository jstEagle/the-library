## 39. New Roads

The frost had come early to Hollowmere that year, silvering the orchard rows where the grandmother's dead trees still bloomed out of season—small white flowers stubborn against the cold, as if the land itself had decided to keep a promise it no longer had to keep.

Noor stood at the edge of the village green with her hands in her pockets and listened.

Not to people. Not to the wind. To the ground beneath her boots, the old listening she had done all her life without knowing what to call it—the sense of paths like veins under skin, of roads that wanted or did not want to be walked. She had braced herself for silence. She had spent a whole winter preparing for silence, writing pages in her own hand so that some future stranger wearing her face could pretend at wholeness. The rite had taken the smuggler roads first, then Maghda's cottage lane, then every track and ford and goat-path she had ever pressed into memory like flowers into a book. She remembered *that* they had been there. She could not remember *them*.

But this morning, standing on frozen grass with her breath ghosting out before her, she felt something move under the earth. Faint. New. A thread of wanting, thin as a spider's line, running north out of the village toward the fells—and it was not any road she had ever known, because if she had ever known it, it was gone now, and this was not a memory. This was the land speaking in the present tense.

*Here,* it said. Or something like here. The land did not use words so much as leanings, and this was a leaning, a tilt of the world's shoulder, an invitation written in the grammar of slope and stone.

Noor's breath caught. She looked down at her feet as though they belonged to someone else.

"You've gone pale," Wren said behind her. "Paler."

Wren came up the lane from the inn with a loaf under one arm and ink stains on three fingers, because some habits survived exile, disgrace, abdicated kings, and the end of a god. Her hair was shorter than it had been in the capital; she'd cut it herself by the fire one night in the twelfth month, badly, laughing, saying she was shedding the last of the royal cartographer's skin. What remained looked younger. Looked lighter. Looked, sometimes, when she wasn't watching, like a woman deciding what to be next.

"Wren." Noor's voice came out strange. "I can hear it."

"Hear what?" And then Wren stopped walking, because she knew. Of course she knew. She had spent a winter reading Noor's handwriting back to her by candlelight—*the pass behind Thornfell bends east after the second cairn, trust the scree, do not trust the ice*—and she had watched which words landed and which slid off like rain off slate. She knew exactly what Noor had lost, and she had learned to read Noor's silences the way she'd once read coastlines: for what they concealed, and for what they warned.

"The land," Noor said. "There's a path. North. It isn't one I memorized—it isn't one *anyone* memorized, I think. It's new. It wants—" She laughed, a small unsteady sound, startled out of her. "It wants to be walked."

* * *

They went up to the grandmother's kitchen to say it out loud, because in Hollowmere you said things out loud now. That was the whole of the new law, unwritten but absolute: nothing hidden, nothing worshipped, nothing answered in the dark. The impostor's fall had left a hunger-shaped hole in the village, and the villagers were learning, slowly and clumsily, to fill it with each other instead of with miracles.

Maren—the grandmother, keeper of the impossible orchard—was baking, because Maren was always baking, as though bread were her personal rebuttal to everything the century had done to her. She was bent and brown as a root and she had once been chosen as a vessel for a hungry god, and Noor had once smuggled her name off a sacrifice list with a forged ledger and a liar's steady voice, and between those two facts lived a debt neither woman ever mentioned aloud.

"North," Maren said, kneading. Flour drifted in the window light. "Past the fells? There's naught past the fells but more fells."

"That's rather the point," Wren said. She had spread one of her own maps on the table—a new one, drawn since the thirteenth moon, since Aldric woke weeping on the cathedral floor holding her hand without knowing why. The map showed Hollowmere and its valley in loving detail, and beyond the northern ridgeline it showed nothing at all: blank vellum, waiting. "Everything past here is unmapped. Officially. Unofficially, it's mapped wrong, because every map that ever came out of the capital was drawn to serve the crown, and the crown served—" She stopped. Finished differently. "Served something else."

"And you want to walk into it," Maren said, "in winter."

"In late winter," Noor said.

"In *early* spring," Wren said, and Noor looked at her, and Wren looked back with the particular stubbornness of a woman who had already decided and was merely negotiating the date.

Noor had expected this conversation to hurt. That was the strange part—that it didn't. Once, wanting to leave had meant fleeing: Maghda's door slamming, guilt riding her shoulder like a crow, invisibility bought at the price of never being known. Now leaving meant something else entirely. Now there was someone to leave *with*, which changed the arithmetic of departure completely. You could not be invisible beside another person. You could only be accompanied.

"I don't know what's out there," Noor said to Maren, honestly. "I don't know anything anymore. Every road I ever carried is gone. If you asked me to find my way to Thornfell I'd stand at the crossroads like a child." She turned her hands over, palms up, empty. "But I can hear new ones. They're faint. Like voices through a wall. But they're there, and they're *true*—I'd know if they weren't. I spent long enough listening to something false to know the difference now."

Maren shaped a loaf with quick, sure hands and did not look up. "Your teacher," she said. "The one who died. She walked these edges too, didn't she. Kept the hollow places."

"She kept them alone," Noor said. "She thought that was the price. I think she thought it was the *point*." She thought of the grave on the hillside above the ruined cottage, the cairn they had built together in the thaw, stone by stone, Wren passing rocks up to her while the freed king—mortal, diminished, awkward as a man waking from a decades-long dream—stood witness with his hat in his hands. "I don't believe that anymore."

Maren finally looked up. Her eyes were the color of weak tea and twice as warm. "Then take bread," she said, "and go be wrong about it somewhere interesting."

* * *

Wren's section of the packing took two days, because Wren did nothing without surveying it first.

She laid out her instruments on the inn room floor like a surgeon: compasses, a brass alidade, blank vellum rolled in oilcloth, pens, three bottles of ink wrapped in wool against the cold. She had burned her identity papers in the capital—had fed them page by page into a brazier while Devoted boots rang on the stones outside, choosing Noor over her old life with a decisiveness that still made Noor's chest ache to think about—but the maps she had saved, the ones drawn in her own hand from her own surveys, honest maps of dishonest country. Those she would not burn. Those were the closest thing she had to a self that predated both the crown and the catastrophe.

"You understand," Wren said, rolling the last of the vellum, "that this is madness. Professionally speaking. A cartographer mapping terrain she can't see from any height, guided by a listener who can't remember how she used to listen. We'll produce documents no ministry would accept. We'll be laughed out of every guild hall from here to the coast."

"Good," Noor said.

Wren paused. "Good?"

"No one will follow us," Noor said. "No one will want our maps. No one will try to wear them, or bind them, or build a throne on top of them." She sat down on the floor among the instruments, cross-legged, and picked up the little brass compass and turned it over in her fingers. "That was the whole disease, wasn't it? Everything true got taken and made to serve something. Roads became smuggling routes became invasion routes. Maps became weapons. Prayers became food." She looked up. "Let's make something nobody wants to steal."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then she sat down too, skirts pooling on the floorboards, and leaned until her shoulder rested against Noor's shoulder, and said, quietly, "You know I'd have followed you even if the maps were worthless."

"I know," Noor said. "That's why I'm bringing the good ones."

* * *

They left on a morning of hard blue sky, when the frost still held and the mud was iron-hard and kind to travelers. Half of Hollowmere came out to see them go, which would once have been Noor's nightmare entire—to be seen, to be known, to be *missed*—and which now felt like being handed something heavy and warm and told to carry it carefully.

Tam was not there. Tam had gone home ahead of them, weeks ago, robes buried somewhere on the road behind him, and the last anyone had heard he was mending fences in his father's field and refusing, politely and forever, to discuss the cathedral. Noor understood. Some debts you paid by staying; his had been paid by leaving. She left a loaf from Maren with his mother anyway, no note, no explanation. The bread would explain itself. Bread usually did.

At the northern edge of the village, where the last fence post leaned and the fell began, Noor stopped and closed her eyes and listened with her whole body the way Maghda had taught her—or half-taught her, before the lessons curdled into commands—and found it again. The thread. Running north through the frozen bracken, patient as water, wanting.

"It's still there," she said.

"Lead on, then," Wren said.

And Noor led—not along any road, because there was no road, only a leaning in the land that her feet understood and her mind did not—and Wren followed with her compass out and her notebook open, calling bearings, sketching as she walked, cursing cheerfully at the bramble, and the two of them moved up onto the open flank of the fells like a single creature with four legs and two obsessions.

By midday they had crossed the first ridge and dropped into a valley that appeared on no map Wren had ever seen or drawn. There was a stream running loud with snowmelt. There was a stand of birches gone white as bone in the sun. And there, half-swallowed by turf, a line of stones too regular to be natural and too scattered to be a wall—some older thing, a boundary or a roadbed, laid down by hands whose language no one spoke anymore.

Wren knelt and brushed the moss from one of the stones with her thumb. "This was a road," she said softly. "Once. Someone built it. Someone forgot it." She looked up at Noor, and the light through the bare birches striped her face, and she was smiling the way she smiled at a coastline that finally made sense. "Do you hear it? Under us?"

Noor knelt too. Pressed her palm flat to the cold turf. Closed her eyes.

And yes—yes, there it was, deeper than the new thread, older, drowsy, a road sleeping under centuries of grass the way the old gods had slept under their cairns, honestly, dreamlessly, asking nothing. It did not want to be walked. It was content to have been. But it noticed her noticing, the way a sleeper turns toward a candle, and something in Noor's chest cracked open along an old seam.

"They're everywhere," she whispered. "Under everything. All the old ways. Nobody killed them. They just—went quiet, when the crown-thing started eating worship. They lay down and let themselves be forgotten because forgetting was safer than being fed on." She opened her eyes. "Maghda knew. That's why she wanted me deaf. Not to hide me from the land. To hide the land from *them*." Her voice caught on the name, on the face she could no longer picture except from Wren's descriptions, told nightly, patient as restoration work. "She was guarding it alone her whole life."

"And now?"

Noor stood. Brushed the turf from her knees. Looked north, where the fells climbed away into country no honest map had ever touched, and felt the new path leaning onward through all of it, unhurried, unowned, wanting to be walked by exactly two people on exactly this morning.

"Now it has two of us," she said. "And we're going to be very slow about it."

* * *

They camped that first night in the lee of the old roadbed, under a sky rinsed clean of everything except stars. Wren cooked; Noor fed the fire; the ordinary liturgy of two people who had learned each other's habits across a winter of fear and a spring of grief.

Later, wrapped in blankets with her back against Wren's shoulder, Noor listened one last time before sleep. The land murmured on all sides—streams arguing downhill, roots drinking slow, the sleeping road dreaming its long dream of having been useful. And threading away north through all of it, faint and persistent, the new way. Unmapped. Unwanted by anyone but them. Going nowhere in particular, which was to say: anywhere.

"We should give it a name," Wren murmured, half-asleep, her chin tucked against Noor's hair. "Cartographers name things. It's how we claim them. Gently."

"Name it what?"

Wren was quiet long enough that Noor thought she'd drifted off. Then: "The way home goes in a circle," she said. "So it can't be that. Call it the way forward. Just—the way forward. Nothing at the end of it. Nothing needs to be at the end of it."

Noor smiled in the dark, where no one could see it and no one needed to.

"The way forward," she agreed, and the land under them seemed to settle at the sound of it, pleased, like a dog hearing its name spoken kindly for the first time.

Far to the north, past the second ridge, past the third, something answered—a thread of wanting, thin and bright, bending slightly westward toward a valley Noor could not see and suddenly, desperately, wanted to. She felt it the way you feel someone turn to look at you across a crowded room.

"Wren," she said softly. "There's something out there. Listening back."

In the dark, Wren's hand found hers. "Then tomorrow," she said, "we go and say hello."
