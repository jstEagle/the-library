## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The trouble with bottled grief, Freya Delacroix had learned in her third year at the guildhouse, was not the bottling. Grief bottled easily enough — it went into the glass the way rain goes into a cistern, with a sound like a long sigh and a faint darkening of the air. The trouble came later, when you had to write it down. *One grief, medium, wept-over*, she would ink into the ledger, and her pen always hesitated on the word *medium*, because no grief was ever medium, and because writing it small was a kind of lie, and Freya Delacroix had built her entire life on the principle that her lies should be confined to a single column and never spill.

She sat in the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Trades, in the guildhouse of Corvane, with the autumn light coming slantwise through leaded windows and laying pale gold bars across the open ledger before her. The ledger was bound in grey calfskin, three fingers thick, and its pages smelled of iron-gall ink and the faint mineral sweetness of the ivory wafers the guild used for its most delicate entries. Around her, the counting room did its quiet work: Master Quill's abacus clicking two rooms away, an apprentice's footsteps on the stair, the tick of the great case clock that had been four minutes slow since before Freya was born and which the guild had long ago decided was part of its character.

At her throat, beneath the high collar of her working dress, a small carved bone charm lay against her skin. It was shaped like a knucklebone, smooth as a river pebble, warm as a living thing.

It was murmuring again.

*Marguerite. Marguerite. Marguerite Delacroix.*

Her mother's name, spoken in her mother's voice, so softly that it was less a sound than a memory of one, rising through the bone and through Freya's skin and settling somewhere behind her sternum where she kept everything she could not afford to feel during business hours.

"Not now," Freya breathed, barely moving her lips. She kept her eyes on the ledger. "We agreed. Evenings only."

The bone did not agree to anything. The bone never agreed to anything. It simply continued, patient as tide, saying her mother's name over and over in that voice that had been dead eleven years and had never once, in all that time, said anything else. Freya had carved the charm herself at fourteen, from the shin of a goose that had lived too long and died unloved in the guildyard, and she had meant it as practice, a first exercise in her gift — but the bone had taken her mother's voice the way amber takes an insect, and it had kept it, and some nights that was the only mercy in the world and other nights it was the cruelest thing Freya owned.

Tonight it would be a mercy. Tonight she would go up to her narrow room under the eaves and hold the charm against her ear like a child with a shell, and listen until she slept. But that was hours away, and there was work between here and there, and the work required a woman whose hands did not shake.

She dipped her pen and finished the entry.

*Received, from the Widow Anske of Mill Lane, one (1) grief, maternal, aged nine years, decanted and sealed per standard rite. Consideration given: remission of the widow's debt to the Guild in full, plus winter coal stipend. Condition of goods: sound. Grief has kept well; no seepage at the seal.*

Below it, in the column reserved for the guild's private notes, she added in smaller script: *Widow laughed once, leaving. First time in a year, per the neighbor who walked her home. Worth noting. Not everything we take is taken.*

Master Quill would have struck that last line out. Master Quill held that sentiment in a ledger was like water in a watch — technically possible, structurally disastrous. But Master Quill was two rooms away with his abacus, and the ledger was hers, and besides: Freya had been keeping the guild's books since she was seventeen, and in all those years she had learned the single most important truth of her trade, which was that a ledger was not a record. A ledger was a story told in numbers, and every storyteller knew that what you left out was as loud as what you put in.

The clock ticked its slow wrong tick. The bone at her throat murmured. Somewhere below, in the vaults, forty years of bottled griefs and bartered years and pawned shadows sat in their careful rows, each labeled in her handwriting, each waiting for someone to come and make the impossible ordinary again.

Freya turned the page and began the next entry, and that was when the bell above the counting room door rang, and a man came in carrying his own death in a jar, though neither of them knew it yet.

* * *

His name was Tam Vossler, and he was a cooper by trade, a broad-shouldered man in his fifties with sawdust worked so deep into his knuckles that they looked dusted with flour, and he set the jar on Freya's desk with the exaggerated care of someone handling a sleeping animal.

"Guildmaster said," he began, then stopped, swallowed, started again. "The notice-board said the guild trades in — in things that can't otherwise be had."

"That's us." Freya gestured to the chair across the desk. He didn't sit. She'd learned not to insist; sitting meant committing, and most clients needed a full minute of standing first, of looking around the counting room at the shelves of labeled jars and boxes and one small locked coffer that contained, per the ledger, *one (1) apology, thirty-one years overdue, unsaid*. "Tell me what you have."

"I don't have anything." His voice cracked on it. "That's the — I want to sell time. Years. My years."

Freya kept her face still. Behind her calm, old training ran through the forms: this was a Class Three trade, mortal consideration, requiring countersignature, requiring witnesses, requiring the client be asked three times across two visits. It also required her to look at the man's remaining span, which was not something bookkeeping taught.

"You understand," she said gently, "that the guild doesn't deal in lifespans. We couldn't buy your years even if it were lawful — a year removed is a year no one lives, and what you'd get for it wouldn't—"

"Not mine to keep anyway." He said it fast, the way men say things they've rehearsed against pity. "The lung-rot. From the coopering — the steam, the oak dust. Physicker says two winters, maybe three. My girl's in Braddon's Reach with her husband and the baby, and she can't afford to travel, and I can't afford to—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I want to trade two of my winters for passage money and a wedding gift. Something decent. So I can go see my granddaughter married in spring instead of coughing alone in Corvane."

The room was very quiet. The clock ticked. At Freya's throat, the bone charm — treacherous, faithful thing — went suddenly silent, as if even the dead were listening.

"Master Quill handles mortal trades," Freya said. It was procedure. It was also cowardice, and she heard it in her own voice.

"Guildmaster Quill turned me away at the door an hour ago. Said it was against policy since the Corrwell affair." Tam Vossler looked at her then, directly, with the flat courage of a man who had already grieved himself and was only trying to arrange the funeral. "He said if I found anyone in this building with the stomach for it, they could have the commission. Ma'am. I'm not asking you to break any rules. I'm asking you to do arithmetic."

Freya looked down at her ledger. The current page held the widow's grief, neatly entered. Above it, a debt paid in years — old Maret the lamplighter, who had settled his account to the guild by surrendering six summers of memory, itemized in her hand: *the summer of the comet; the summer of his wife; the summer of his wife, again, because he asked.* The guild's whole history was pages like these. Impossible things, made lawful by being written down small.

And underneath all of it, under ten years of meticulous columns, in a cellar of herself that she visited only at night: her mother's voice in a bone, and the thing she could do that no ledger had ever recorded, and the fear that had lived in her chest so long she had stopped noticing its weight, the way you stop noticing a stone in your shoe.

If the guild would not trade this man his winters honestly, someone less scrupulous would trade them dishonestly. There were buyers in the low quarter for years of the dying; everyone in Corvane knew it and nobody said it.

"Two winters," Freya said. "For passage to Braddon's Reach, lodging for the season, a gift suitable for a granddaughter's wedding, and — " she heard herself add it before her caution could catch it — "and the guild will hold one winter in trust. If you're still alive after the wedding, it's yours back. That's not standard. But it's arithmetically sound."

Tam Vossler stared at her. Then he sat down, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and put his face in his flour-dusted hands, and cried without any sound at all, which was when Freya understood exactly what kind of grief he had carried into her counting room, and why he hadn't wanted to sell that one.

She fetched the proper forms. She ground the ink fresh. And while he composed himself she wrote, in her smallest and steadiest hand, the entry that Master Quill would pretend not to see: *Class Three, mortal consideration, countersigned F. Delacroix. Client retains dignity in full. No discount applied.*

At her throat, very quietly, the bone began to murmur her mother's name again — and then, for the first time in eleven years, it said something else.

*Tam,* it whispered, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. *Tam Vossler. Two winters. Two winters. Tam.*

Freya's pen stopped mid-word, leaving a blot like a small black wound on the page.

The bone had never done that. The bone spoke one name, one voice, hers alone, a keepsake and a curse. It did not reach ahead. It did not speak of the living or the nearly-living. It certainly did not stand in a counting room at four in the afternoon and read out the future like a line item.

*Tam Vossler,* the bone said again, softer. *Before spring. Before spring.*

"Ma'am?" Tam Vossler was looking at her strangely. "You've gone white as your paper."

"Bad ink," Freya said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; somewhere far inside, the frightened girl who had hidden this gift for twenty years took the wheel of her body and drove it smoothly through the lie. "Sit. We'll need witnesses for the countersignature — guild law, nothing personal. Wine while we wait?"

He nodded, dazed, grateful, already halfway to spring in his mind. She poured the wine with steady hands. She called down the corridor for the apprentice. And all the while, beneath her collar, the little carved bone went on whispering the shape of a man's remaining time, and Freya Delacroix smiled her bookkeeper's smile and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*Ledgers are lists. Lists have names. And someday — someday soon, if the whispers under the market-stalls are true, if what the heralds are rumored to be carrying in their sealed tubes is what I think it is — someone is going to want a list of people like me.*

She wrote the final line of the entry. She blotted it. She pressed her hand flat over the page, over all her careful columns, as if she could hold the whole ledger shut by strength alone.

Outside, the autumn light was failing early, and far off across the rooftops of Corvane, a herald's horn sounded once — thin and clear and wrong, a note she had never heard used for anything ordinary.

The bone at her throat fell silent. That silence was worse than the whispering.

Freya turned to greet the witnesses, wearing the face of a woman whose only secrets were in the margins.
