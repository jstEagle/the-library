## 2. What the Crown Announced at Dawn

The bells woke Corvane before the sun did.

Freya Delacroix had been awake anyway — she was always awake before the light came through the shutters of her room above the guildhouse, because the dead preferred to speak in the dark hours when the living's thoughts were thin as broth. She lay listening to them murmur through the bone charm at her throat: a fisherman drowned off the Sable Coast who repeated the word *cold* like a prayer; a grandmother who counted her grandchildren's names over and over, as if afraid of losing one; something older and less patient that only breathed.

Then the bells. All of them at once, every tower in the city, which meant not fire and not flood but proclamation — the kind of news that could not wait for people to gather it themselves.

She dressed quickly, fingers steady out of long practice even while her stomach tightened. In the mirror she did the small work she did every morning: tucked the bone charm beneath her collarbone where it lay against skin, hidden by linen, hidden by habit. It had been her mother's. It had been speaking since Freya was nine years old, and in twenty years no one outside this room had heard it but her.

*Today is an ordinary day,* she told herself, the way she told herself every morning. *Ledgers to balance. A debt in bottled grief due from the widow on Tanner's Row. The man who paid his rent in years will want a receipt.*

She believed it for exactly as long as it took her to reach the guildhouse steps and see the crowd.

* * *

They had come out into the street in their nightclothes and their shop aprons, bakers with flour still on their forearms, children hoisted onto shoulders, all of them tilted toward the guildhouse square like grass toward wind. Above them, on the scaffold platform the city kept for executions and tax declarations, stood three figures in white-and-gold livery, and behind them a crier whose voice was a trained instrument, built to carry across harbors and battlefields alike.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Keeper of the Sealed Borders, Warden of the Realm—"

Freya found herself at the edge of the crowd without remembering crossing the street. The guildhouse door was at her back. She should go inside. She should balance yesterday's entries before the ink dried wrong. Instead she stood among the bakers and the children and listened.

"—let it be known throughout the realm that the crown has declared a Hunt."

The word went through the crowd like a swallowed stone. *Hunt.* Not a hunt for wolves, though the season for those was coming. Not a hunt for poachers or debtors. Everyone in Corvane knew what the word meant when the crown used it, because everyone's grandmother had told them stories about the last one, two hundred years gone, and everyone had agreed those stories were old wives' comfort, relics of a frightened age.

"The gift of hearing the dead," the crier intoned, "the gift of speaking with them, of binding them, of carving their voices into flesh or bone or ivory — is hereby declared a corruption of the natural order and a threat to the peace of the realm."

A woman near Freya crossed herself with the wrong hand, the old way, the Vhalen way, and then looked around sharply to see if anyone had noticed. Someone had. Freya had.

"All persons possessing such gifts shall present themselves for registration at their nearest garrison within thirty days. Registration is mandatory. Registration is mercy." The crier let the words sit, savoring them. "Those who register shall be examined, catalogued, and — where the gift proves minor — permitted to live under covenant and watch."

He paused. The pause was the whole speech; everything before it was scaffolding.

"Those who do not register," he said, "shall be found. And those who are found unregistered shall be burned, that their corruption may not pass to blood or neighbor. So says the crown. So says the law. So says the fire."

The crowd made a sound Freya had never heard a crowd make before — not a gasp, not a cry, but a long collective exhale, as if the whole city had been holding its breath for two hundred years and hadn't known it until now.

Then, everywhere at once, people began to look at each other.

That was the thing Freya would remember later, when she tried to reconstruct the morning in the meticulous columns of her mind: not the crier's voice, not the decree, but the looking. The baker looking at the tanner. The tanner looking at the widow. Neighbors of thirty years suddenly weighing each other like merchants weighing coin, testing each for the hollow ring of a secret. Because everyone knew someone. Everyone had a cousin who talked to her dead husband's coat, an uncle who read the names off drowning victims' bones so their families could bury the right men. The gift wasn't rare. It was just quiet. It had always been quiet.

Until the crown decided quiet was the same as hidden, and hidden was the same as guilty.

Freya pulled her collar higher over the charm and walked back inside with a gait she carefully kept ordinary.

* * *

Guildmaster Osric Vane was already in his office, and he was burning paper.

Not in the hearth — in a copper bowl on his desk, the small ceremonial dish they used for sealing wax and the occasional illegal contract that needed to stop existing. He fed pages into it one at a time, unhurried, watching each one curl. He was a narrow man, sixty-odd, with ink-stained fingers and the patient face of someone who had spent forty years making impossible things look like clerical work. He didn't look up when Freya came in.

"You heard it," he said. Not a question.

"I heard it."

"Shut the door."

She shut it. The office was windowless by design — Osric had chosen this room precisely because it couldn't be watched from the street — and the firelight from the copper bowl moved on the walls like something alive and nervous.

"Sit down, Freya."

"I have the Tanner's Row delivery—"

"*Sit.*"

She sat. He finished the page he was burning, set down the stack he'd been feeding from, and folded his hands. She recognized the stack. Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up: they were ledger pages. Guild pages. Pages she herself had written.

"Do you know what I'm doing?" he asked.

"Burning records."

"I'm burning *your* records." He said it flatly, without drama, the way he said everything. "Every entry in the last eleven years where the trade involved bone. Every commission you logged for carved charms. Every payment received in 'memorial services' that we both know means you sat with a grieving family and gave them back a voice." He tapped the stack. "There are forty-one pages here. You were thorough. That was the problem. You were so gods-damned thorough that when the audit comes — and an audit will come, Freya, the crown doesn't declare a Hunt without sending auditors first — your handwriting will be the clearest confession in this building."

"They're trades," Freya said. "Registered trades. Grief-work has been a sanctioned category since the guild's founding. There are statutes—"

"There were statutes." Osric leaned forward. "Statutes are paper. Paper burns. That is the entire lesson of this morning, and I am teaching it to you early because you are the most valuable asset this guild has ever possessed and also the single biggest reason we are all going to die if you don't start thinking like a criminal instead of a bookkeeper."

The words landed like cold water. Freya stared at him. In eleven years, Osric Vane had never once called her an asset. He called her *our best pen*, *the only person who can read the Marrow Codex*, *that insufferably precise woman*. Asset was a word for things that could be seized.

"You knew," she said slowly. "Before the heralds. You knew this was coming."

"I know the shape of every wind before it reaches Corvane; that is my actual job, whatever the charter says about trades." He pushed the remaining stack toward her. "Burn these yourself. Your hand, your fire. If anyone asks, the guild suffered a chimney fire in autumn and lost portions of its older ledgers. I've already prepared the damage report. Dated three months ago."

Three months ago. He had been preparing for three months. Freya took the pages and felt how ordinary they were, how innocent — *one carved femur charm, client M. Ashwell, fee: two goats and forgiveness of a debt* — and understood that she was holding her own death warrant in forty-one pieces, and that the only difference between this paper and a pyre was time.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "You could have burned them quietly. You could have told me nothing and let me keep believing the world works."

Osric looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face — some tiredness, some guilt — surfaced and submerged again like a body in a river.

"Because you'll need to practice forgetting," he said. "And I wanted to watch you fail at it once, privately, so I know what we're dealing with."

* * *

She failed at it immediately, of course.

That was the cruelty of her gift, the joke the world had played on her: Freya Delacroix could not forget anything she had ever committed to memory, because memory, for her, was not hers alone. The dead remembered through her. Every name she had ever carved stayed carved. When she tried to put the crier's words out of her mind — *registration is mercy, those who are found shall be burned* — the words simply sank downward, past thought, into the stratum where the bone-voices lived, and joined them. By evening she could hear the decree recited in her mother's cadence, in the fisherman's broken *cold-cold-cold*, in the grandmother's counting voice, as if the dead themselves had taken up the proclamation and were learning it by heart.

She worked. That was her answer to everything, and she worked now with a fury that surprised even her. The Tanner's Row widow came at noon with her little bottle of grief, properly stoppered, and Freya weighed it, logged it, issued the receipt in her flawless hand, and never once let her eyes linger on the woman's mourning ring, which was bone, which was probably her husband's finger-bone, which probably still held a trace of him that Freya could have coaxed into speech if she pressed her thumb to it for ten seconds. The widow left lighter. Freya remained exactly as heavy as before, plus forty-one pages' worth heavier.

In the late afternoon, a young journeyman named Pell knocked at her office door — a nervous boy, all elbows, who carried messages badly and loyally.

"Beg pardon, mistress Delacroix. The guildmaster wants you to know there's a rider come up the coast road. Crown colors. Making for Corvane. Should arrive—" Pell swallowed. "Tomorrow. Maybe the day after."

"Did the guildmaster say what the rider wants?"

"He said—" Pell shifted. "He said to tell you it's an audit. And that you should sleep well tonight, because after tomorrow nobody in this building will."

* * *

Night. The guildhouse settled into its sounds — beams ticking, the cellar door swelling with damp, Pell dropping something in the kitchen and swearing with impressive fluency. Freya sat at her desk with a single candle and did not work.

Instead she took the bone charm from beneath her collar and laid it in her palm.

It was small, this thing that had ruined and saved her life: a curved sliver of rib, polished by twenty years of her thumb, carved so finely the lines were more suggestion than mark. Her mother had worn it the same way, hidden against the same skin, and her mother's mother before that, back through a line of women who had all learned the same lesson — that the gift was kept like a candle in a storm, cupped, sheltered, never shown. On the coast road where her mother had been born, in the villages that no longer appeared on maps, such gifts had once been called by gentler names. *Listening.* *Keeping.* Women who held the names of the dead so the dead wouldn't be lost. Nobody had burned anybody for it, in the stories. The stories were probably lies too. Stories usually were.

"Tell me what to do," she whispered to it, which was foolish, because the charm didn't hold her mother. It only held her mother's *voice*, the way a bell holds the shape of its ringing. What spoke through it was older and stranger than any one soul — a chorus, a sediment, the accumulated dead of her whole line, compressed into ivory.

But tonight, for the first time in twenty years, the chorus answered clearly enough that she could almost pick out words.

*Register,* something murmured, in a voice like ash settling. *Be catalogued. Be known.*

*Never,* said another voice, sharper, and Freya flinched because it was nearly her own.

*Run,* said a third, very quietly, from very far away. *While the doors are still open. While there is still anywhere to run to.*

She closed her fist around the charm until the edges bit. Outside, Corvane slept the sleep of a city that had heard a death sentence read to the whole room and convinced itself it applied to someone else. Tomorrow a rider in crown colors would come up the coast road. Somewhere beyond the hills, garrisons were lighting registration-fires, and lists were being drawn up, and neighbors were remembering suspicious things about neighbors.

And somewhere in this building, in her own precise, damning hand, forty-one pages of evidence existed that she had already burned — except that she hadn't, not yet, because her hands had refused all evening, because some part of her still believed in statutes, in paper, in the guild, in the safe world.

She lit the candle stub in the copper bowl she'd borrowed from Osric's office and held the first page over it. *One carved femur charm, client M. Ashwell.* The corner browned. Curled. Caught.

She watched it burn and felt nothing like relief. Fire, she thought, watching the letters glow orange and then black and then nothing — fire doesn't erase anything for me. I am the ledger. Everything I have ever recorded is written somewhere no flame can reach.

Which meant that when the auditors came, when the questions started, when Osric's careful fiction of the chimney fire was picked apart thread by thread — the truth of her would still be sitting intact behind her eyes, legible to anyone who knew how to read her, and the crown had just announced to the entire realm that it intended to find every single person who could be read.

The page crumbled. She fed in the next.

She was still burning them at dawn, when the rider's hoofbeats sounded on the coast road, and the bone at her throat began, very softly, to say a name she didn't recognize — over and over, urgent, like a warning:

*A stranger is coming. A stranger is coming. An oath is coming to Corvane.*
