## 3. An Oath Arrives in Winter Clothes

The oath woke Wren Halvard an hour before dawn, as it always did, and they lay in the narrow bed of the Wayfarer's Rest and let it finish.

It was not a voice. It had never been a voice. It was a wire drawn through the center of them, thin as a hair and strong as a bridge cable, and every morning the crown's seal at the base of their skull gave that wire one slow tug — *you are mine, you are mine, you are mine* — until Wren's teeth ached with the truth of it. They had learned not to fight the tug. Fighting cost more than yielding. Yielding cost almost nothing now, which was perhaps the most frightening thing about it.

They rose in the dark, dressed in the grey-and-silver of the Office of Oaths, and looked at themselves in the spotted mirror above the washbasin: a face like a closed ledger, eyes the color of river ice, thirty years old and carrying the weight of a vow sworn by a child who no longer existed.

*Audit the Guild of Impossible Trades, Corvane branch,* the summons had read, in the Huntmaster's small, precise hand. *Inventory all holdings. Note any irregularity.* And beneath that, the line that was never written but always meant: *Note any gift.*

Wren splashed water on their face, did not flinch at its coldness, and went down to where their horse waited in weather that could not decide between sleet and snow.

* * *

Corvane in winter was a city built of wet stone and bad bargains. It sat in the crook of two rivers like something the rivers had tried to drown and failed, and its streets smelled perpetually of tanneries, coal smoke, and the particular sourness of money changing hands too quickly. The guildhouse stood on Ledger Square, naturally — everything in Corvane eventually came back to ledgers — a tall narrow building of black timber and white plaster, its sign painted with the guild's arms: a scale weighing a coin against a feather, both pans empty.

Wren dismounted at the square's edge and stood a moment in the falling sleet, because there was a thing they did before every audit, a ritual the Office would have called superstition if they'd known of it. They listened.

Not with the ears. With the oath.

Every oath-keeper carried a small deadness inside them, a place where the vow had been driven in like a nail, and around that nail the world behaved strangely. Magic left residue the way fire left smoke, and the oath — cruel, precise instrument that it was — could smell it. That was half of what the crown used them for. Not the punishments, not the breaking of false vows at law-days. The smelling. A bloodhound for the gifted, wrapped in a clerk's coat.

Here, in Ledger Square, in the ordinary noise of a market city waking up, Wren smelled it immediately.

Smoke. Faint, old, worked deep into the grain of things the way smoke worked into the beams of a house where someone had smoked fish for forty years. Not the sharp reek of active sorcery — nothing so obvious, or the guild would have been shut down years ago. This was older and quieter. Residue. Habit. A gift used carefully, constantly, and hidden well, until the hiding itself had soaked into the walls.

Wren's breath fogged and hung. Somewhere behind them a cart driver swore at his mule. The smell of the gift drifted from the guildhouse like warmth from an oven door, and beneath it, fainter than faint, came something else — something Wren had never caught on an audit before and did not have a name for.

A whispering. As if many small voices were murmuring just under the threshold of hearing, the way snow murmurs when it falls on snow.

They put it down to the wind off the rivers, which lied, and walked to the guildhouse door.

* * *

The guildmaster's name was Osric Vane, and he received Wren in a room that was trying very hard to be humble and failing upward into opulence. Plain oak table, plain rush floor, plain wool hangings — all of it the finest quality money could buy, chosen by a man who understood that true power wore modesty the way a blade wore oil. Vane himself was sixty-odd, soft-spoken, with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of a man who had spent his life watching other people lie about numbers.

"Oath-keeper Halvard." He bowed exactly the correct depth. "We're honored. Corvane is a small branch; I fear we'll bore you."

"Boredom," Wren said, "is my favorite finding." They set their sealed writ of audit on the table between them. "I'll need access to all ledgers, all strongrooms, all trade-goods held in escrow, and the names and duties of every member, journeyman, and servant on the premises."

"Of course." No hesitation. No flicker. Wren noted that, and noted also that they could not tell whether the smoothness was innocence or practice. "My clerk will attend you. Freya Delacroix — she keeps our books better than I keep my conscience, if such a thing can be believed of a guildmaster."

Something moved in Wren's chest at the name. Not recognition — they'd never heard it before. Just the oath stirring, the way a dog stirs when it catches a scent under a door. They pressed it flat.

"I work alone," Wren said. "Your clerk can fetch what I ask for. She needn't hover."

"As you wish." Vane poured wine neither of them drank. "How long will this take?"

"However long it takes to be thorough." Wren unrolled the writ and began the litany of the audit, the questions that were the same in every guildhouse in the realm, and watched Vane answer each one with the serenity of a man reciting his own prayers. Bottled grief, three units, escrowed against a widow's debt. One year of life, sold by a dying farmer to pay his son's fines, held pending transfer. A dream, purchased and bottled, contents unknown even to the guild. A promise, extracted and stored, awaiting redemption by the promiser's heirs.

Impossible trades. All of it legal, all of it licensed, all of it obscene, and Wren recorded each item in their own hand with a clerk's neatness while the whispering they'd caught outside grew, very slowly, louder.

It was coming from upstairs.

* * *

They found the source on the second day.

The audit proceeded as audits did: strongrooms opened and inventoried, seals checked against the Office's registry, escrow goods weighed and sampled. Wren worked from dawn until the light failed, ate whatever the kitchen sent up untouched, and slept four hours in the room Vane provided, and through all of it the smoke-smell thickened and thinned like breath, strongest in the mornings, strongest near the counting room.

The counting room was Freya Delacroix's domain. Wren had seen her twice from a distance — a woman of perhaps twenty-eight, dark-haired, quick-moving, with the particular stillness of the hands that gifted people often had, as if afraid their fingers might give them away. She fetched ledgers when asked. She answered three direct questions with perfect, boring accuracy. She did not hover, exactly as instructed, and she watched Wren the way mice watch cats: without appearing to watch at all.

On the second afternoon, following the whispering up through the building's bones, Wren stopped outside the counting-room door and listened properly.

Voices. Dozens of them. Not words — Wren strained and got no words, only cadence, the rise and fall of speech without meaning, like a crowd heard through a wall, like a congregation murmuring through a closed chapel door. Coming from nowhere. From everywhere in that room. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that they knew to listen, the smell of the gift — but refined, disciplined, worn smooth as a river stone. Someone had been using magic in this room every day for years, quietly, in small careful doses, and had gotten so good at it that the magic itself had learned manners.

Wren pushed the door open.

Freya Delacroix sat at a slanted desk with a bone in her hand.

It was small, pale, carved — a charm, the kind peddlers sold against rheumatism, except that no peddler's charm was carved with that density of work, lines upon lines upon lines, a whole script too fine to read at distance. She was holding it the way a musician holds an instrument mid-phrase, and she went absolutely still when the door opened, and the voices — Wren heard them clearly for one full second before they cut off, snuffed like candles pinched between fingers — the voices had been coming *from the bone.*

"Oath-keeper." Her voice was level. Her knuckles were white. "Forgive me — I wasn't told you'd come up. Can I fetch something for you?"

"The fourth ledger of the autumn quarter," Wren said. "And a chair, if your master wants his auditor standing all day."

She fetched both. Her hands shook only slightly, and only once, and she hid it by setting the ledger down with exaggerated care. Wren sat, opened the book, and began checking entries against the escrow inventory, and the whole time they worked, they felt her across the room like a heat at their back, and felt the bone — tucked away somewhere in her clothing now, silent, smothered — like a coal wrapped in wool.

They wrote nothing about it. Not yet. An audit note required certainty, and certainty required proof, and proof required the Office, and the Office required the Hunt, and the Hunt required—

Wren's hand paused over the page.

The Hunt required burning. That was the new word in the realm this season, the word the heralds had begun carrying from town to town, the word that made gifted families burn their own heirlooms and flee in the night. Registration first, the proclamations said. Then judgment. Everyone knew what judgment meant now; the crown had stopped pretending otherwise months ago.

And here was a woman with a whispering bone, in a guild full of impossible trades, and Wren Halvard was sworn — wired, nailed, bound — to write her name down.

Their hand resumed moving. Neat figures. Checked sums. Nothing irregular.

*You are mine,* said the wire through the center of them, gently, patiently, the way a jailer says goodnight. *You are mine, and you know what you are.*

* * *

That night Wren did not sleep.

They sat in the provided room with the candle burned down to a thumb's width and turned the problem over the way one turns a stone looking for what lives underneath. The problem had shapes, and the shapes were these:

Shape one: duty. Wren Halvard was an oath-keeper of the crown, and the oath was not a job one resigned from. It had been sworn in blood and sealed in the marrow, and it could not be unsworn — only kept, or broken. Broken oaths punished the breaker. Every child in the realm learned the stories: the knight whose tongue rotted when he lied under seal, the magistrate whose hands curled into claws whenever she reached for a bribe, the lover who had sworn fidelity falsely and felt his heart squeeze, once, every hour, like a fist, until he confessed or died. The oath did not kill quickly. It corrected. It was patient as arithmetic.

Shape two: the woman downstairs. Freya Delacroix, clerk, bookkeeper, keeper of a singing bone. If Wren reported her, she would be registered, and registration was a list, and lists in this kingdom had lately become queues for the pyre. Wren had seen the first registrations in the capital. They had seen the faces of the people signing the rolls — the particular blankness of people who had decided that hope was a currency they could no longer afford.

Shape three, the shape Wren kept turning away from and turning back to: the whispering. Because it had not sounded like sorcery. Sorcery had a texture Wren knew, greasy and grasping. The voices in that bone had sounded like — like a room full of people waiting politely to be called. Like names on a roll being read in a soft voice. Like the opposite of harm.

Which was worse, in a way. It was easy to burn a witch. It was much harder to burn a librarian.

The candle guttered. Wren looked at their own right forearm, bare in the cold, where the skin was unmarked — the oath's brand lived deeper than skin, in the meat and the marrow, showing itself only when disobeyed. They had disobeyed once, years ago, small and stupid: ordered to break a smuggler's minor vow, they had instead let the man go with a warning. The punishment had lasted nine days. They remembered all of them. They remembered especially the fourth day, when the pain had grown so conversational, so intimate, that they had genuinely considered walking into the sea.

They had not disobeyed since. Nine days had bought eleven years of obedience, and the arithmetic had always seemed sound.

But arithmetic, Wren thought, staring at the ceiling, assumed the sums stayed the same. And the sums were changing. The crown that held their oath was the same crown lighting pyres, and the wire through Wren's center ran, strand by strand, all the way back to the throne — which meant that every name Wren wrote in an audit ledger was a log thrown on a fire they had not lit and could not put out.

Somewhere below them, faint through the floorboards, the whispering started again. Soft. Patient. Dozens of voices murmuring in the dark of a counting room, keeping their own kind of books.

Wren lay awake until dawn listening to them, and told themselves it was professional interest, and knew, in the place where the nail was, that they were lying — and that the oath heard the lie too, and filed it away, the way Wren filed everything, neatly, in a ledger of its own.

* * *

On the third morning, the audit turned to personal effects.

It was standard procedure — the Office audited guild members' declared assets as rigorously as the guild's escrow — and Vane had prepared a schedule: each member would bring their declared items to the counting room for inspection, one at a time, while Wren checked declarations against reality. Tedium, mostly. Rings, tools, savings-hoards, the occasional licensed charm.

Freya Delacroix was scheduled third.

Wren watched her come down the stairs with a small box in both hands, and watched her set it on the table, and watched her open it with fingers that were steady, steady, steady — and then saw her glance, just once, at the inner pocket of her jacket, where something the size of a thumb sat hidden against her heart.

The whispering, which had been quiet all morning, stirred like a sleeper turning over.

"Declared items," Wren said, and reached for the box, and did not yet reach for the pocket, and felt the oath tighten around them like a hand closing — patient, precise, already counting the cost of whatever Wren chose next.

Outside, the sleet had turned to proper snow, falling straight down in the windless cold, and the whole city of Corvane seemed to hold its breath beneath it.
