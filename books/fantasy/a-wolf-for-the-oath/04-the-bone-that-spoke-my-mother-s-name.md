## 4. The Bone That Spoke My Mother's Name

The trouble with hiding a gift inside a talent is that both of them want out.

I had spent eleven years teaching my hands to be ordinary. Ledger-keeping is a discipline of small, exact motions — the dip of the pen, the drag of the rule, the way you blot an entry so the ink sits flat and honest on the page — and I had made myself excellent at it precisely because excellence was loud enough to drown out everything else. When I balanced a column, the world narrowed to numbers. Numbers did not whisper. Numbers had never once, in all their gray and patient centuries, said my mother's name.

But bones are not ink. Bones remember being alive.

The charm at my throat was a knuckle-bone from a river ox, carved over with a lattice of fine lines that looked, to any passing eye, like decoration. It was not decoration. It was a cage with a door in it, and the door opened only when I let it, and for eleven years I had kept it shut with the same iron discipline I brought to double-entry bookkeeping: every name that came to me went in, was recorded in the private cipher only I could read, and stayed there, quiet, accounted for.

That morning, the guildhouse smelled of tallow and wet wool, because winter had come down off the Corvane hills overnight and found every gap in our windows. I sat at my desk in the counting room with the day's trades spread before me, and the first of them was already impossible enough to make me smile despite the cold.

A widow from Tanner's Row wanted to sell her grief.

Not metaphorically — the Guild of Impossible Trades dealt in the real article. Grief could be drawn out of a person the way a splinter comes out of a palm, if you knew the angle, and it kept, bottled, for years, darkening slowly like wine. The widow's grief was worth more than most; she had carried it thirty years without letting it change shape, which meant it was dense, well-made grief, the kind collectors paid for. I wrote the appraisal in my neat hand: *One grief, thirty-year vintage, unspoiled. Buyer's premium waived per standing arrangement with widows of the Row.* And beneath it, as I always did, I noted what she'd asked for in exchange, because impossible trades were never really about the goods.

She wanted one hour. One hour in which she would not miss him.

There was no such thing, of course. But the guild existed because people needed to trade for things that didn't exist, and someone had to keep the books honest about it. I entered the hour under *liabilities* and moved on.

The bone at my throat hummed.

It always hummed a little when death came near the ledger — and grief was death's cousin, twice removed, close enough for the dead to smell. I pressed two fingers against it through my collar, the way you'd press a hand to a dog's muzzle to stop a bark before it started. *Not now,* I thought at it. *We're working.*

The second trade of the morning was a debt paid in years. A young man from the glassworks owed Master Corvane's cousin four hundred crowns, and he had come to us with the only currency he had left: eleven years of his life, sealed in a wax tablet by a hedge-priest who charged him a month's wages for the sealing. Years were legal tender in three duchies and contraband in two, and Corvane sat neatly on the border of all five, which was why the guildhouse stood where it stood. I verified the seal, checked the tablet against the registry of licensed life-brokers, and entered the transaction with the small satisfaction I took in work done properly.

Eleven years. He'd be forty-one instead of fifty-two, and his children would eat. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the world, that counted as fair. I had learned not to look too long at the places where it didn't.

The bone hummed again. Louder this time. A thread of warmth slid up my throat and settled behind my ears, and I knew that feeling the way I knew the first ache of a fever — something was coming through.

"Freya."

I looked up. Wren Halvard stood in the doorway of the counting room, and my hands went still on the ledger the way birds go still when a hawk crosses the yard.

They had been with us six days now, the crown's auditor, sent to examine the guild's records ahead of some new regulation nobody would name. Six days of watching them move through our halls in those winter-gray clothes that fit like they'd been poured onto a different person and never quite adjusted. Six days of Wren asking quiet questions in a voice like a locked drawer, and writing nothing down, which frightened me more than any amount of writing would have. Auditors write things down. Whatever Wren Halvard was doing, it wasn't auditing.

"The third entry," they said. "The debt in years. Read it back to me."

"It's been read back once already—"

"Read it back to me."

There was no cruelty in it. That was the unsettling thing about Wren — there was almost nothing in it, no visible weather at all, just a surface like ice on a pond where you couldn't see how deep the water ran. I read the entry aloud. They listened the way other people interrogated, head tilted slightly, eyes doing the actual work, moving from my face to my hands to the ledger and back.

"And the seller," Wren said. "He understood what he was selling. You're certain."

"He signed the comprehension clause himself. Article nine."

"Article nine." Something flickered across their face — not a smile, but the place where a smile had been excavated and filled in. "You people have an article for everything."

"We have an article for everything because everything has been traded at least once." I closed the ledger, and my fingers brushed the bone charm as they came up, and that — that was the mistake. Not carelessness exactly. Habit. Eleven years of touching it for comfort, and comfort was the one thing I could never afford to take from it while anyone watched.

Wren's eyes dropped to my throat.

For a moment, nothing. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs, the guildmaster's boots crossed the floor above us, slow and deliberate, a man pacing out his own worries. The cold pressed at the windows.

Then the bone spoke.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened, because precision is the last craft I have left, and I cling to it the way drowning men are said to cling to driftwood, except that in my experience drowning men mostly cling to each other, and drag each other under, and that turned out to be true here too.

The bone spoke, and it did not speak in the whisper I had spent a decade teaching it. The cage-door I kept so carefully shut did not creak open a finger's width, the way it sometimes did at night, letting out a name like smoke. It blew wide. Every ward I'd carved into it, every line of the lattice, went hot and then went silent, silenced, overridden, and my mother's voice came out of a river-ox's knucklebone in a clear, carrying alto that filled the counting room the way her voice used to fill our kitchen:

*"Freya. Love. It's cold in here — why is it always so cold—"*

Three sentences. Perhaps four seconds. I heard them the way you hear your own heartbeat when the doctor's stethoscope goes in: shockingly loud, shockingly intimate, belonging entirely to you and yet suddenly public, suddenly witnessed.

My mother had been dead fourteen years. She had drowned — the irony of that word, that country, that whole submerged geography of my life, I would not understand until much later — and her voice was the first voice the bone had ever held, because I had carved it the week after we buried her empty coffin, a girl of sixteen with a gift she hadn't asked for and a grief she couldn't bottle, and the bone had taken her voice the way dry ground takes rain.

In four seconds, Wren Halvard heard a dead woman speak.

In four seconds, I stopped being a bookkeeper.

I got the bone in my fist. I don't remember reaching for it; my body did what bodies do, what mine had trained itself to do across eleven years of drills I'd never admitted were drills — hand to throat, charm to palm, will slamming down over the opening like a hatch cover in a storm. The voice cut off mid-word. The silence afterward was enormous.

Wren hadn't moved. That was the worst part. Most people, hearing a corpse speak, flinch, cross themselves, run, laugh nervously, do *something*. Wren stood in the doorway with the stillness of a person who has heard worse, or expected this, or both, and their eyes — pale, unhurried, terribly awake — came up from my closed fist to my face.

"You keep a talking bone," they said, "in a guild of impossible trades, and list it nowhere."

"It's a carving," I said. "Devotional. River-ox bone. The guildmaster can confirm—"

"I don't need the guildmaster." Wren stepped into the room, and the cold came in with them, or maybe the cold had always been theirs, worn like the clothes. "I need you to say what I just heard."

"Nothing spoke."

"Freya Delacroix." My full name, in that locked-drawer voice, and something in my chest turned over, because I had never told them my surname in full and neither had anyone else in the building, which meant they'd read it somewhere, which meant they'd been reading *me*, not the ledgers, since the day they arrived. "I have stood in rooms where the dead were made to speak by priests with bells and knives and hours of preparation, and it sounded like meat being persuaded. What I just heard sounded like a woman talking to her daughter. That is not a trick. That is a gift. And three days ago, the crown declared every gift in this realm subject to registration."

The word landed in the room and lay there between us.

Registration. The heralds had come at dawn two days past, brass horns splitting the frost, and read the proclamation from the guildhouse steps: *By order of the Crown, all persons possessing commerce with the dead shall present themselves for registry within sixty days, that their gifts may be examined, catalogued, and — where deemed dangerous — contained.* Contained. Everyone in Corvane had heard the word and everyone in Corvane had heard the word underneath the word, the way you hear the second bell under the first. The guildmaster had burned a page of something in his study that night, and told me to forget what I'd seen, and I had said yes, master, and had known even as I said it that forgetting was the one trade I had never managed.

"I don't know what you heard," I said.

"Yes, you do." Wren came closer. Close enough that I could see the fine scarring at their hairline, old, deliberate, ritual-cut. Close enough that I could see they were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "And I am going to ask you one more time, and I want you to understand something before you answer. I am sworn to the crown. My oath is not a formality. It lives in me. If I report a gifted person and the report is false, the oath punishes the lie. If I fail to report a gifted person and the failure is discovered, the oath punishes the omission. There is no version of my next hour in which I am not either lying or obeying. So think carefully about which lie you make me tell."

And there it was — the thing I'd sensed in them for six days without having words for it. Wren Halvard wasn't hunting me. Wren Halvard was *caught*, the way a wolf is caught in a snare it walked into willingly, and every movement pulled the wire tighter, and they knew it, and they stood there anyway, offering me, in the only language their oath allowed, a chance to make their lie an easy one.

Say nothing. Deny it cleanly. Give me a false report I can file with a steady hand.

Every survival instinct I owned voted for it. Every instinct, that is, except one — the oldest one, the one that had started all this, the one that had spent fourteen years listening to a dead woman ask why it was so cold.

"She's my mother," I said.

The words came out of me like water through a cracked dam, and once they started, the rest followed, and I could no more have stopped them than I could have stopped the bone.

"My mother's voice is in this bone. I carved it when I was sixteen. I hear the dead — not summon them, not command them, I *hear* them, the names they leave behind, and I've hidden it my entire adult life behind ledgers because ledgers are boring and boring is safe, and I know what registration means, everyone knows what it means, the word is *contained* and the word under the word is *burned*, and I'm telling you anyway because—" My fist was shaking around the bone. "Because I have been hiding for eleven years and I am so tired, Wren Halvard. I am so tired of being a locked room."

The silence after that was different from the silence before. Before, it had been a held breath. Now it was something shared.

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then they said, quietly, "You should not have told me that."

"I know."

"No. You don't." And for the first time in six days, something moved behind their eyes, something that looked almost like grief wearing duty's uniform. "Because now I have to decide what to write down. And whatever I decide, it costs."

They turned and left. Their boots went down the corridor, measured, unhurried, and I sat alone in the counting room with the bone warm in my fist and my mother's last unfinished sentence hanging in the air — *why is it always so cold* — and I felt the strangest thing, the thing I would spend the rest of this story trying to explain to myself.

Relief.

Not joy. Not peace. Relief, raw and terrible, like the first breath after a corset comes off, like setting down a weight you've carried so long your spine has grown around it. For eleven years I had been two people — the bookkeeper and the bone-carver, the woman at the desk and the woman at the keyhole of the grave — and the space between them had been filling up, year by year, with everything I couldn't say. Now the space was gone. Collapsed. Whatever came next, I would face it as one person, whole, exposed, and there was a freedom in that which frightened me far less than the pyre did.

I should have been afraid. I was, a little. But mostly I sat there and listened to the quiet, and in the quiet, faint as a coin settling at the bottom of a well, the bone whispered one word, and it was not my mother's voice this time, and it was not a warning.

It was a thank-you.

* * *

The guildmaster sold me within the hour.

I want to be fair to Aldous Corvane, because fairness is a kind of bookkeeping too, and because what he did was not simple. Nothing about Aldous was simple; he was a fat, clever, frightened man who had built the safest nest in five duchies and spent thirty years feathering it with favors and bribes and articles of incorporation, and I believe — I still believe, though belief has cost me dearly — that he loved the guild more than he loved anything living in it.

At midday, a rider in crown colors came up the valley road. I saw him from the window of the scriptorium, where I had gone to do the only useful thing my hands knew how to do: carve. Not names — not anymore, not ever again, not where anyone could find them — but the lattice pattern itself, the cage-work, practicing wards on scrap bone like a musician running scales on a stolen instrument. If they were going to come for me, I would at least understand the shape of the thing that had betrayed me.

The rider did not dismount at the gate. He handed down a sealed letter, waited while it was carried inside, and rode on toward the city. Standard procedure for crown correspondence. Except that twenty minutes later, the guildmaster's clerk came looking for me with a message that my presence was requested in the study, and when I got there, Aldous Corvane was standing at his window with his back to me, and on his desk, open, was the letter — and beside the letter, my personnel file, and beside my personnel file, a fresh sheet of guild paper bearing the guild's own seal, half-written in his hand.

I read it upside down, because eleven years of ledgers teach you to read upside down.

*...confirming for the Office of Registration that the individual named below has been observed in possession of an unregistered gift of communication with the deceased, per the auditor's report of this date...*

Below it, in the space for the name, he had written *Freya Delacroix*. In his own hand. In the same hand that had signed my apprenticeship papers when I was fifteen and orphaned and hungry, the same hand that had written my journeyman certification, the same hand that every year at midwinter wrote *to Freya, who keeps our accounts and our secrets* on the card that came with the festival wine.

"Aldous." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "What are you doing?"

He didn't turn around. "What has to be done."

"You're reporting me."

"I'm *registering* you." Still the window. His reflection watched me, though — I could see his eyes in the glass, wet and darting, a man rehearsing courage and finding the theater empty. "There's a difference, girl, whatever the heralds' tone implies. Registered gifts are examined. Examined gifts are classified. Classified gifts are—" He stopped. We both knew the sentence ended in fire, and neither of us said it, and the unsaid word sat in the room like a third person.

"They burned a woman in Harrow Fen last week," I said. "Before the proclamation was even a day old. Her neighbors registered her as a kindness, so she'd be in the system, protected by process. The process burned her."

"That was disorder. Mob law. This is order." Now he turned, and his face was the face of a man who has decided to believe his own argument because the alternative is to fall down. "Order protects people, Freya. It's the only thing that ever has. If the guild is seen cooperating — fully, immediately, *first* — then the guild survives. And a surviving guild can shelter others. Can negotiate. Can—" He gestured vaguely with the pen, scattering ink. "Someone has to be inside the room when they decide these things. I am putting myself in the room."

"You're putting *me* in the room."

"You put yourself there!" It burst out of him, and with it, finally, the truth under the truth. "Four seconds, Freya. Four seconds of a dead woman's voice in my own counting room, in front of a crown auditor, and do you know what I have spent the last three hours doing? Not deciding whether to report you. Deciding whether to report you *before* the auditor does. Because if the guild reports you, it's cooperation. If the auditor reports you, it's negligence, and negligent institutions get dissolved, and dissolved institutions get their people — all their people, every clerk and carver and apprentice — put on lists." He set the pen down with exaggerated care, the way men do when their hands want to shake. "I chose the ledger over the ledger-keeper. That is what a guildmaster is *for*. You taught me that, in your way. Every impossible trade we've ever brokered, someone paid in something they couldn't spare. Today the guild pays in you. I would that it weren't so. But I would rather it were you than all of us, and I will not pretend otherwise, because pretending is what got this realm into this mess."

There it was. No apology, because he genuinely didn't think he owed one — and that, more than the letter itself, was the wound. I could have survived his cowardice. What I nearly didn't survive was his *arithmetic*: one Freya against one hundred guild members, and the sum coming out the way sums do.

"You could have warned me," I said. "An hour's start. A horse. You could have written the letter tomorrow and told me tonight."

"And where would you go?" He said it not cruelly but helplessly, and that was somehow worse. "The Hunt hasn't even properly begun and the roads are already full of informers. There is nowhere in five duchies, girl. There is nowhere on the map." He picked the pen back up. "Go to your quarters. Pack nothing. Act as though nothing has changed. By nightfall this will be handled, and I swear to you on the guild's charter that I will use every favor I have left to see that 'registration' means examination and nothing more."

I believed he would try. That was the horror of it — I believed him completely. Aldous Corvane would spend every favor, call in every debt, burn thirty years of careful goodwill, all to secure me a marginally slower version of the same ending. He was not a monster. He was a bookkeeper of souls, and in his books, I had been entered that morning under *liabilities*.

I went to my quarters. I packed nothing. I sat on my narrow bed with the bone in my fist, and outside the window the winter light went long and gold and indifferent over the hills, and I waited to feel afraid.

What I felt instead, as the shadows climbed the wall, was the bone warming against my palm — and beneath the warmth, faint but unmistakable, new voices. Not my mother's. Dozens of them, far away, thin as wind through a keyhole, and all of them saying the same thing, over and over, in the cadence of people calling across water.

They were saying a name. Not mine. Not hers.

A country's name.

And downstairs, boots on the stair — measured, unhurried, terribly awake — Wren Halvard coming to tell me what they had decided to write.
