## 5. Arrested by the Person Who Should Have Run

The oath woke me before the sun did.

It did that sometimes — a low hum behind the sternum, like a struck bell that would not finish ringing — and I had learned to read its moods the way sailors read weather. This morning it was not humming. It was pulling. South and slightly west, toward the guildhouse district of Corvane, where the fog still sat in the streets like something that had died there overnight.

*She is awake,* the oath seemed to say. *She is packing.*

I had known about Freya Delacroix for eleven days. Eleven days of watching her through the borrowed eyes of an audit: her ink-stained fingers, her meticulous columns, the way she never once looked at the bone charm at her throat when she thought someone might be observing, and always touched it twice when she thought no one was. Twice, always twice. Once to silence it, once to thank it. You do not learn that gesture in any ledger school. You learn it in kitchens and sickrooms, in the company of the dead.

I had written my report each night and burned it each morning. The oath permitted this — it wanted the gift found, but it did not specify by whom, or when, or how thoroughly the finding must be documented. Oaths are precise the way knives are precise. They care only about the cutting edge.

But last night the guildmaster had sent his raven. And this morning the oath was pulling me south, and somewhere in the guildhouse a woman with ink on her fingers was counting the hours wrong, believing she had more of them than she did.

I dressed in the dark. My oath-brand sat under the left sleeve, cool and quiet for now, a thin silver line from wrist to elbow that the crown's clerks called *the mark of service* and I called, privately, *the leash.* It had been quiet lately. That should have warned me.

Corvane at dawn smelled of coal smoke and river rot. I took the back lanes because the front lanes already had heralds on them, and heralds meant crowds, and crowds meant questions I had stopped being able to answer honestly. At the mouth of Cooper's Row a crier was nailing up the proclamation, and a knot of shopkeepers stood reading it in the particular silence of people who have just learned which of their neighbors will be next.

*By order of the Crown: all persons possessing the gift of hearing the departed shall present themselves for registration within nine days. Unregistered practitioners will be taken. Registered practitioners will be assessed. The Hunt protects the living.*

The Hunt protects the living. Four words, printed in good black ink, doing the work of a thousand pyres.

I walked past them with my collar up. Under my sleeve, the brand began, very faintly, to warm.

* * *

The guildhouse door was unlocked.

That was the first wrong thing. A guild of impossible trades keeps locks like other institutions keep prayers — constantly, compulsively, and with an eye toward the afterlife. But the door swung at my touch, and the entry hall beyond was stripped bare: shelves empty, ledgers gone from their cradles, even the brass trade-weights pried off the counter, leaving little pale circles in the dust like the ghosts of coins.

They were running. All of them. Except one.

I heard her before I saw her — not the dead, though they were there too, a rustle at the edge of my trained senses like paper turning in another room — but her. Freya Delacroix stood in the scriptorium with a traveling cloak over her arm and a small strongbox open on the table, and she was not packing gold. She was packing bones. Dozens of them, wrapped in wool, laid in rows with the tenderness most people reserve for infants.

She looked up. And here is the thing I have turned over a hundred times since: she did not look surprised.

"Oath-keeper Halvard," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; I saw the tremor as she closed the box. "You're early. Or I'm late. One of those."

"The guildmaster sold you," I said. "An hour ago. By raven."

"I know." She said it the way you'd say *it's raining.* "He burned my page first. Did you know that? He stood right there—" she nodded at the cold hearth, "—and fed my name to the fire, and told me to forget what I am. As if forgetting were a thing you could do on command. As if I hadn't spent thirty years trying." She lifted the strongbox. It was smaller than a bread loaf. Thirty years of hiding, and everything she chose to save fit in a bread loaf's worth of bones. "Then he wrote to you anyway. Because burning the page wasn't enough. Because he needed the fire to be official."

"Why didn't you run?"

"I was going to." She looked down at the box. "But these can't run. If I leave them, whoever comes next finds them, and every one of these is a name. Names are evidence now. So I was going to carry them, and you walk faster than I do, and Corvane has one road out that isn't watched, and—" She stopped. Breathed. "And I suppose I knew you'd come. The oath pulls you, doesn't it? I can feel it from here. Like a hook through your chest."

I could feel it too. It had gone from warm to hot while she spoke, tightening with every word she said about names, about evidence, about the thing she carried.

"Freya Delacroix," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and formal, the clerk's voice, the cage's voice. "In the name of the Crown, you are placed under arrest for unregistered practice of the gift. You will be conveyed to Vaskarren for assessment."

"Vaskarren." She tasted the word. Everyone knew what Vaskarren was now; the proclamations had made sure of it. The pyre-city. "That's where they burn people."

"That's where they assess people."

We looked at each other across the empty scriptorium, and I understood that we both knew exactly what I had just done. I had lied. Not by much. Not even, perhaps, entirely on purpose. *Assessment* — the word implied a process, a weighing, a chance. There was no chance. There had been no chance since the first proclamation. Assessment at Vaskarren was a corridor between a cell and a stake, and everyone in Corvane knew it, and I had said it anyway, smoothly, in my best oath-keeper's cadence, because some animal part of me wanted her to come quietly and some worse part of me wanted to believe the sentence myself.

The brand moved.

I have felt the oath punish me before. Once, as a child, for a lie so small I've forgotten it; once, at nineteen, for hesitating a half-second too long at a door I was ordered to open. Both times it was a flare, a white wire of pain from wrist to shoulder, gone in a breath. Punishment. Correction. The leash yanked.

This was not that.

This was the brand *recoiling* — twisting under my skin like a living thing presented with spoiled meat. It crawled up my forearm in a slow cold wave, and for three full seconds I could not breathe, and the pain was not sharp but *wrong*, the way a sound can be wrong, a chord that shouldn't exist. Somewhere beneath the pain I understood what was happening with perfect clarity, the way you understand things in nightmares: the oath was not punishing the lie. The oath was trying to take it back. Some deep architecture of the vow — sworn in blood and candle-smoke by a child who no longer existed — had been built to serve justice, or what passed for it, and mercy spoken falsely out of an oath-keeper's mouth was a corruption it could not digest.

"Wren?" Freya had crossed the room. I don't remember her crossing it. Her hand was on my arm, above the sleeve, above the brand, and the wrongness eased — not gone, but dulled, muffled, as if she'd pressed a cloth against a wound. "What is it? What's happening to you?"

"Nothing." The lie came easily this time, and the brand flared again, shorter, sharper, a dog snapping at its own chain. I clenched my teeth and rode it out. "Nothing. We're leaving. Now."

"You're lying to me," she said quietly. "About something. And it hurts you when you do."

"Take up the box, Delacroix."

"And there it is again." But she took up the box. And she came with me. And neither of us said the true thing, which was that she could have run — the back window of the scriptorium opened onto a drain alley, and I knew it, and she knew I knew it, and she did not run, and I have asked myself every day since whether that was trust, or surrender, or the simple arithmetic of a woman who had calculated that her fastest route out of Corvane was attached to the person sent to collect her.

Arrested by the person who should have run. That was me, in case the phrasing confuses anyone. I should have run. From the guildhouse, from the oath, from the whole bright terrible machine of the Hunt, the moment I understood what it was building. Instead I held the door for her like a gentleman, and locked it behind us, and walked her into the dark.

* * *

We took the south road because the north road had checkpoints, and checkpoints meant registration clerks, and registration clerks meant paperwork, and paperwork meant time — time in which the brand might speak, or she might, or the bones might. The south road ran along the ridge above the Vell, through country that was all gorse and old sheep-walls, empty in the way land gets empty when the people on it have started locking their doors against strangers.

Freya walked beside the cart horse without being told to. I had put her in the cart at first, properly, like a prisoner, and she had climbed out at the first mile-marker with the strongbox hugged to her chest and simply fallen into step, and I had let her, because the alternative was binding her hands, and my hands had shaken too badly at the thought to manage the knots.

"You felt it," she said, after an hour of silence. "Last night. In the guildhouse. When you lied to me."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You're doing it right now. Lying." She glanced sideways at me. "Your jaw does a thing. Here." She touched her own jaw, mirroring. "Also your left hand closes. You're holding the reins like they owe you money."

The brand lay quiet under my sleeve. It punished lies, apparently, but not silences. Silences it tolerated the way a toll-bridge tolerates foot traffic — grudgingly, for a fee. The fee, in my case, was this conversation.

"When an oath-keeper speaks falsely in the course of duty," I said, "the vow corrects him."

"That's not what happened."

"It's what the manuals say."

"I've kept ledgers for thirty years, Wren Halvard. I know what a corrected entry looks like, and I know what a *retracted* one looks like. What happened in that room wasn't correction. Something in that vow of yours flinched." She shifted the strongbox to her other hip. "What exactly did you swear? When they gave you the brand. What were the words?"

"You don't want to know."

"I want to know everything. It's a professional failing." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "I keep accounts of impossible trades. Bottled grief. Debts paid in years. Do you know what I've learned, keeping those books? Every impossible trade has the same structure. Someone wants something so badly they'll pay more than it's worth, and someone else is willing to sell it, and the ledger's job is just to write down the price honestly so that later, when the buyer screams, there's a record that they knew." She looked at me fully then, and her eyes were the gray of the river below us. "So. What did you pay for your brand? What were the words?"

And because the truth costs nothing from the brand, and because I was tired, and because the ridge road was empty and the morning was cold enough to make confession feel like warmth — I told her.

"*I swear my ears to the Crown,*" I recited. The old words came up smooth as stones from a well bottom, worn round by ten years of handling. "*To hear the gifted and find them. To hear the dead and heed them not. To hold no loyalty above the law, no mercy above the writ, and no silence that serves the enemy of the realm. Until my death or my release.*"

"Heed them not," Freya repeated. "The dead."

"That's the clause that matters. Every oath-keeper hears them, a little. It's why we're chosen — children who already hear, taught to turn the hearing into a hunting sense. The dead talk constantly, if you're built to listen. Where they died. What they lost. Who owes them." I kept my eyes on the road. "The vow doesn't stop the hearing. Nothing stops the hearing. It stops the *heeding*. It builds a wall between knowing and caring, and it holds that wall up with pain."

"And when you lied to me about Vaskarren—"

"The wall shook." I said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have required admitting what it had felt like: that for three seconds the wall had not merely shaken but *leaned toward me*, as if it had considered, briefly, falling the other way. "A false promise of mercy is a heeding, technically. It means I weighed your suffering and adjusted my speech to ease it. The vow treats that as treason against the Crown."

"Does it hurt every time you're kind?"

The question landed harder than any blow the brand had ever dealt. I did not answer. The brand did not make me, because silence is free, and I paid nothing, and the road went on under our feet, and somewhere in the silence between us Freya Delacroix filed the non-answer away in whatever meticulous internal ledger she kept, under whatever heading she used for damning entries.

* * *

We came over the shoulder of Hallow Ridge at midafternoon, and that is where the road showed us Vaskarren.

Not the city itself — the city was still two days south, a smudge of towers on the horizon — but the fairground in its shadow, the great mustering field where the Hunt had begun its work. From the ridge we could see the pavilions, the picket lines, the long orderly rows of wagons like a market fair laid out by someone with a passion for geometry. And at the center of the geometry, a platform. And on the platform, stakes. And around the stakes, a crowd, arranged with the same loving precision, leaving sightlines clear.

"They said nine days," Freya whispered. "Registration first. Nine days to present yourself."

"They said nine days for registration." I heard my own voice come out level and hated it. "They never said the unregistered would wait."

There were three figures on the platform. Even at this distance I could see they were not struggling, which was somehow the worst detail — that they stood where they were put, in the particular stillness of people who have been assured, correctly, that running would only spread the fire to others. A clerk read from a scroll. A priest said something brief. And then a man in Hunt livery — I would learn his face later, and hate it later, but that afternoon he was only a figure with a torch — touched flame to the brushwood at the first stake's base.

The wind was from the south. It brought the smoke to us before it brought the sound, and the smoke smelled sweet, horribly sweet, like resin and honey, because they packed the brushwood with pitch and dried herbs — I knew this, part of my training, *the condemned should burn clean, the smoke is a message* — and the message drifted up the ridge and settled over the gorse and the sheep-walls and the two of us standing there like statues that had failed to become heroes.

Then the sound arrived. Not screaming. I had braced for screaming. It was worse than screaming: it was singing. The condemned of the first burning had been given a hymn — the *Litany of the Living*, the crown's new composition, four lines long, easy to teach, easy to remember — and they sang it while they burned, because their families were in the crowd, and the families had been told that a peaceful death would spare the family further assessment. So they sang. Three voices, rising on the sweet smoke, steady as choir practice:

*We give our ghosts to keep the living whole.*
*We give our names to still the restless dead.*
*What burns in us was never ours to keep.*
*The Crown receives. The Crown forgives.*

Beside me, Freya made a sound I had never heard a living person make. It came from somewhere below language — a small, folded, inward noise, grief collapsing into itself rather than out — and her hand found my sleeve without asking, gripping the fabric above the brand, and the brand went cold. Cold as river water. Cold as the far side of a grave. Whatever lived in that vow recoiled from the burning so violently that it pulled all the heat from my arm, and I stood on Hallow Ridge with a dead-cold left arm and a woman's knuckles white on my sleeve, and I listened to the second verse begin, and I understood, finally, completely, what I served.

The machinery of duty, seen in full. That's what they call it afterward, the ones who survive their oaths. You spend years inside the machine, oiling it, feeding it, believing the machine is sad but necessary, and then one afternoon the machine lets you climb high enough to see the whole apparatus at once — the gears, the belts, the sweet smoke, the geometry — and you realize the machine was never sad at all. Sadness implies reluctance. The machine was *content*. The machine was a hymn set to fire and a crowd arranged for sightlines and a clerk's neat scroll, and it would run until someone broke a gear, and I was a gear, and the gear was beginning, very slowly, with a sound like the brand recoiling from a lie, to consider jamming.

On the platform, the third stake caught.

"There are people in those wagons," Freya said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very clear, the voice I would later learn she used for reading names aloud. "Look at the wagons. They're full. Those aren't supply wagons, Wren. They're holding wagons. They're collecting faster than they're burning, which means the burning is going to accelerate, which means—" She stopped. Her grip on my sleeve tightened. "Do you hear them?"

"Hear who?"

"The dead." She turned her face up to mine, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "The ones from the pyre. They're not — they didn't disperse. Dead voices scatter, usually, they thin out like smoke. These aren't scattering. They're *gathering*. All three of them, and others, older ones, dozens, maybe hundreds, and they're all saying the same thing." Her lips parted. For a moment I thought she would not say it. Then she said it, and the word fell onto the ridge between us like a stone into a well:

"Vhalen."

The brand under my sleeve did not move. But something deeper than the brand did — some sealed chamber behind my sternum, some room I did not know I owned — and in that room a door I had never seen swung open an inch, and through the gap came a smell of green water and cold stone, and a feeling like homesickness for a place I had never been.

"What did you say?" I asked. My voice sounded far away.

"Vhalen," Freya said again, watching my face with a ledger-keeper's terrible attention, recording every line of it. "Wren. Your arm went cold. And you've gone white as the fog. You know that name."

I opened my mouth to deny it.

The brand waited, coiled, ready to punish the lie — and I found, standing on Hallow Ridge with the sweet smoke in my hair and the first burning still singing its second verse below us, that I could not afford the price.

"Yes," I said. And the word cost nothing, and that frightened me more than any pain ever had. "I know that name. God help me, Freya — I think I've *always* known it."
