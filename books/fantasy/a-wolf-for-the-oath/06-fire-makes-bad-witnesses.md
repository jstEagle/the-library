## 6. Fire Makes Bad Witnesses

The wagon smelled of tar and terror and other people's prayers.

Freya had been in it for two days. She knew this because the slats of the roof let through a slice of sky that went from grey to black to grey again, and because her body kept its own ledger now — the ache in her wrists where the manacles bit, the bruise blooming on her hip where she'd been thrown against the wheel hub, the hunger arriving on schedule like rent due. Two days. Nine prisoners when they'd loaded her at the crossroads. Seven now.

The two who were gone hadn't been taken anywhere. They'd simply stopped answering their names at the evening count, and when the guards dragged them out, the others had looked at the straw where they'd lain and understood without being told. The road to the pyre-city did not wait for a full load. It burned what it had.

Freya sat with her back against the wagon wall and counted what could not be seen: the voices.

They had started the first night, after the ridge, after Wren reined up beside the road and made them all watch the burning — a village's worth of the gifted, forty or fifty souls by the smell of it, stacked like cordwood on a headland so the whole valley could see what registration led to. *An object lesson,* one guard had said cheerfully, and Freya had thought: *yes. I am learning something.* Just not the lesson he meant.

Because from that fire, her gift had heard something new.

She had spent her whole life hearing the dead as whispers — small voices, patient voices, names murmured through bone like water through stone. Her mother's voice lived in the charm at her throat and said perhaps six words a year. That was the shape of death as Freya knew it: quiet, sparse, dignified. A name spoken once and then held.

What came off the pyre was none of those things.

It was a scream first. Then dozens of screams braided together. And under the screams — worse than the screams — a word, repeated, over and over, in every register a human throat owned, high and cracked and child-small and old-man-rough, all of them saying the same thing the way a crowd says it, the way a country says it:

*Vhalen.*

*Vhalen. Vhalen. Vhalen.*

She had pressed her palms over her ears until they ached and it hadn't helped, because the voices weren't coming through her ears. They were coming through the bone charm at her throat, which had gone hot as a coal and stayed hot, and through the ivory combs in her hair, and through the little carved fish in her pocket she'd whittled from a lamb's knuckle on a bored afternoon three years ago — every piece of worked bone on her body ringing like a struck bell, every piece of bone for half a mile around, she suspected, singing the same terrible chorus.

The dead of the burned weren't resting. They weren't even properly dead. They were *going* somewhere, all of them, streaming toward a single point on the map like sparks drawn up a chimney, and they were screaming the name of a country that no longer existed as they went.

Freya was a bookkeeper. She knew what it looked like when many separate accounts began flowing toward one destination.

It looked like a debt being collected.

* * *

On the second evening, the guards built a fire of their own beside the wagons — a small, ordinary fire, for cooking and warmth, and the irony of it sat in Freya's chest like a swallowed stone. Through the gap between the wagon boards she watched the flames and listened to the seven remaining prisoners breathe.

There was a miller's widow named Ansa who had not stopped crying since the crossroads, quietly, the way you cry when you've decided no one is going to come. There was a boy of maybe fourteen who had been caught with a scrying bowl hidden in a flour barrel, and who kept telling anyone who'd listen — no one would — that he wasn't gifted, really, the bowl had been his grandmother's, he just kept it for sentiment. There was an old man everyone called Tam who had not spoken since his arrest but whose hands moved constantly, tracing shapes in the air, and Freya recognized the shapes: letters. He was writing, endlessly, invisibly, on the inside of the world.

And there was Wren Halvard, who was not a prisoner, who rode beside the wagon on a grey horse and had not once looked at Freya directly since the ridge.

That was its own kind of information. Freya had spent years reading people the way she read ledgers — not their faces, which lied, but their entries. What they recorded. What they omitted. Where the columns didn't balance. Oath-keeper Halvard had arrested her with a face like a locked door and had told her, on that first day, in a voice sanded smooth of everything human, *You'll be treated according to law* — and Freya had watched, with the particular clarity of a person watching their own execution being scheduled, as the oath-keeper's left hand had clenched hard enough to crack knuckles at the word *mercy*, which was not a word they'd actually said aloud but which had been underneath the sentence like a corpse under floorboards.

Something in that vow punished them for lying. Freya had filed the observation away the way she filed everything, in the neat iron cabinet behind her eyes, and had not known yet what use it would be. But she was a guild woman. Nothing is ever useless. Everything has a trade value, eventually.

Now, as the camp settled into night — two guards awake, four asleep, the horses stamping against the cold — Freya heard the pyre-city before she understood what she was hearing.

It was far off, a day's ride east, maybe more. But distance meant nothing to the voices anymore. The burned from the ridge had joined whatever chorus lived out there beyond the horizon, and the chorus had grown, and grown, and tonight it was no longer screaming.

Tonight it was counting.

*Vhalen,* said the voices, but differently now — organized, rhythmic, each name a bead on a string. *Vhalen. Vhalen. Vhalen.* Not a scream anymore. A muster roll. Somewhere past the dark hills, something enormous was taking attendance of the dead, and the dead were answering, and the number was getting larger every time a pyre went up.

The bone charm at Freya's throat grew so hot she had to shift it away from her skin with one finger.

Beside her, the old man Tam stopped writing in the air. His hands fell still for the first time in two days. He turned his clouded eyes toward the eastern darkness and said, in a voice rusted from disuse, "Do you hear it too?"

The other prisoners stirred. Ansa's crying hitched and stopped.

"Hear what?" whispered the boy with the flour-barrel bowl.

"The roll call," Tam said. His lips trembled. "My wife's been dead eleven years. She never once spoke to me. Tonight—" His hand rose halfway toward the horizon and stopped there, shaking. "Tonight I can almost hear her. Almost. Like she's calling from very far off and there's a door between us."

Freya's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the winter air leaking through the wagon slats.

*Almost. Like there's a door between us.*

She closed her eyes and listened properly, the way she listened to a suspicious account, line by line, looking for the error. The dead of the burned were streaming east. The dead of Vhalen — wherever Vhalen had been, whatever had drowned it — were answering from somewhere past the edge of every map she'd ever catalogued. And between the two streams, faint but unmistakable now that she attended to it, ran a seam of pressure, a strain, like a river pressing against a dam, like a crowd packed against a bolted door.

The pyres weren't destroying the gifted dead. The pyres were *delivering* them. Every burning sent another shipment of souls eastward, and eastward something was receiving them, stacking them, adding them to itself — and the receiving thing was pressed up against a barrier that was older than the crown, older than the Hunt, older, possibly, than the kingdom doing the burning.

The crown wasn't purging the gift. The crown was *harvesting* it.

"Oh," Freya said aloud, very softly, and the sound of her own voice frightened her more than the voices had.

* * *

She didn't plan to speak to Wren. Plans were for people with options; she had a wagon, a countdown, and a growing certainty that the arithmetic of the world had changed underneath everyone. But near midnight, when the watch changed and the new sentry's boots crunched away toward the horse line, Wren Halvard appeared at the back of the wagon with a tin cup of water, as though delivering rations were simply part of the office of arrest, and stood there in the firelight with their face doing its locked-door impression.

"Drink," Wren said. "They won't feed you if you're dehydrated. It interferes with the—" A pause. The sentence hit some tripwire in the oath and died there, and Freya watched the oath-keeper's jaw tighten around whatever the rest of it had been. *Interferes with the processing,* probably. *With the rendering.* The vocabulary of butchery, dressed as bureaucracy.

Freya took the cup. Their fingers didn't touch; Wren had made sure of that, passing it handle-first with a care that looked almost like fear.

"Oath-keeper Halvard." She kept her voice low, level, the voice she used for closing out a difficult account. "How many burnings have there been?"

Wren's eyes flicked up. Grey eyes, Freya noted. The color of ash, which felt less like coincidence every day. "That's not your concern."

"Indulge me. You're sworn to the crown. The crown's numbers are public to its officers. How many?"

A muscle moved in Wren's cheek. Whatever the vow demanded of them in moments like this — silence, deflection, the smooth recitation of procedure — it apparently also punished evasion, because after a long moment they answered, flatly, as if reading from a schedule: "Four hundred and twelve registered. One hundred and sixty-three processed."

*Processed.* There it was again. Freya drank the water slowly and let the horror move through her and out the other side, into the place where she kept useful things.

"And do you know," she said, "what happens to the dead of the processed?"

"I don't discuss—"

"Because I do." She set the cup down between them, deliberately, a token placed on a table. "I hear them, Wren Halvard. I have heard the dead my whole life — small voices, patient ones, names in the bone. That's my crime. That's what I'm on your list for. And since the ridge, since your beautiful object lesson on the headland, I have heard one hundred and sixty-three people's worth of dead, and they are not at rest, and they are not destroyed, and they are not screaming at random." She leaned closer to the slats. "They are traveling. All of them. East. Toward one place. And they are saying one word."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, a horse snorted.

"They're saying *Vhalen*," Freya said.

She had expected many reactions. Disbelief. Mockery. The flat recitation of doctrine — *the drowned country is a fable, the erased lands are treason to name.* What she got instead was worse. Wren Halvard went absolutely still — not the stillness of skepticism but the stillness of a person who has been struck somewhere they have no armor, and for one unguarded instant their face was not a locked door but a door standing open onto a room where something had died a long time ago and never been buried.

"You know the name," Freya said quietly.

"I know nothing." But the oath flared — Freya saw it happen, saw the oath-keeper's left hand spasm and press flat against their thigh, saw the wince that followed, quick and brutal, there and mastered. Lying cost them. Every lie was a small payment of pain, and they were paying, right now, in front of her.

"Your vow just hurt you," Freya said. "For that sentence. *I know nothing.* It isn't true, and your oath knows it isn't true, and it made you pay for saying it." She let that sit. "Interesting accounting, Halvard. Your conscience keeps books too."

"You don't know anything about my oath."

"No. But I know about oaths. The Guild of Impossible Trades holds several thousand of them in trust — bride-price vows, debt-oaths, a blood oath between two fishing families that's been renewed every spring for ninety years. Oaths are contracts. Contracts have terms, penalties, clauses. Yours punishes falsehood." She tilted her head. "Which means whoever bound you wanted you capable of honesty, even against your own will. Think about what that says about the people who wrote it. They trusted the magic more than they trusted you."

"Stop talking."

"The dead are being sent somewhere, Wren. Burned and *sent*. One hundred and sixty-three souls, plus however many came before your count began, all flowing east like water finding a channel, all shouting the name of a country your own maps don't contain. That is not a purge. Purges end things. This is a *supply chain*." Her voice dropped further. "The pyres are feeding something. Something big enough to need feeding. Something behind a door, if the old man's right — and he hears it too, did you know that? Tam. He heard his dead wife tonight for the first time in eleven years. *Like there's a door between us,* he said. The burnings aren't silencing the gift. They're amplifying it. They're building pressure against something, and when that something gives—"

"*Enough.*"

Wren's hand shot through the slats and gripped the wagon frame, and up close Freya could see what the firelight had been hiding: exhaustion carved deep around the grey eyes, the fine tremor in the branded wrist where the cuff of the coat had ridden up — a mark like a seal stamped in old scar tissue, the crown's sigil interwoven with something older, barbed, that looked almost like thorns. The brand of the oath. It was darker tonight than it had been two days ago. Freya was certain of it. She kept excellent records, even mental ones.

"It's spreading," she said.

Wren snatched their sleeve down. "You see what you want to see. Everyone with the gift does. That's why the Hunt exists — you find patterns in smoke. You hear voices in fire. You are *evidence of nothing*."

"Then it should be easy to ignore me." Freya didn't move back. "But you came to my wagon at midnight with water, alone, when you didn't have to. And you flinched at the name Vhalen like it was a knife someone had left in a drawer you open every day. So here is my last entry for tonight, officer, and then I'll sleep, because I keep honest books even in a cage." She held the grey eyes. "Whatever the pyres are feeding — it knows your name too. I've started hearing it in the chorus. *Halvard.* Faint. Far off. Getting clearer every day, same as everything else."

The lie detector in her own bones — twenty-nine years of listening — confirmed it even as she said it. She had begun hearing it only that afternoon, threaded thin beneath the massed cry of *Vhalen*: a single syllable-name, repeated, patient, *addressed*. As though something in the east was keeping a list of its own. As though the dead had a ledger, and certain living names had recently been entered in it.

Whether it was Wren Halvard the dead remembered, or Wren Halvard the dead *wanted*, she couldn't yet say. Both possibilities were bad. One of them was much worse.

Wren stepped back from the wagon. For a moment they simply stood in the firelight, and Freya watched the war happen behind the locked door — duty on one side, whatever lived in that unburied room on the other — and she thought, with the strange detachment of a clerk observing figures refuse to sum: *this person is going to break. The only question is which direction.*

"Sleep," Wren said finally, hoarse. "Tomorrow we reach the pyre-city."

They turned and walked toward the fire, and Freya lay back in the foul straw among the breathing prisoners, and put her hand over the hot bone charm at her throat, and listened.

Eastward, the chorus rolled on. *Vhalen. Vhalen. Halvard. Vhalen.* And beneath it now, newly arrived, so faint she might have invented it — except that she had never once invented an entry in her life, and did not intend to start — a voice she knew better than any voice in the world. Her mother's voice. Not from the charm, which murmured its six annual words and no more, but from *out there*, from beyond the hills, from the direction of the door.

Her mother was not among the burned. Her mother had been dead nine years, buried properly, mourned properly, in a churchyard with a stone.

And her mother's voice was coming from the east anyway, clear across all that impossible distance, saying the one thing she had never said in life or in bone:

*"Freya. Come and see what they've done with the door."*

In the guard circle, an oath-keeper sat staring into a cooking fire with a brand crawling up their arm, and did not sleep either.

By morning, Freya knew, one of them would have to make a choice the other could not take back.
