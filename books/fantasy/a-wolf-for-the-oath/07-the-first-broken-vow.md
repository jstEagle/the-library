## 7. The First Broken Vow

The oath woke Wren before the sentry's boots did.

It always knew first. It lived under their skin like a second heartbeat, a low warm hum of rightness when they walked the crown's straight roads, and tonight it was singing wrong — a thin, high note of warning, the way it had sung wrong the night they'd lied to Freya Delacroix about mercy. The vow did not forgive lies. It did not forgive hesitation. It certainly would not forgive what Wren was about to do, standing in the frozen mud of the convoy camp with their hand already on the wagon's lock.

*Turn back,* said the hum. *You are sworn. You are kept. You are safe inside your duty like a coin inside a strongbox.*

Wren had been a coin for eleven years. They were tired of being currency.

Below the ridge, in the shallow bowl of the valley where the convoy had made camp against the wind, three wagons sat in a ring of watchfires. Two held the registered — men and women and one child, all marked by the census-takers' ink, all bound for the pyre-city of Ashfall with the patient docility of livestock that has been told, gently and often, that this is simply where they are going. The third wagon held Freya Delacroix, alone, because the soldiers had learned in two days on the road that she unsettled them. She heard things, they whispered. She talked to the bones at her throat. She'd told Corporal Ives the name of his grandmother without ever asking it, and Ives had not slept since.

Wren had watched her do it. Had watched her press her palm flat to the weeping corporal's shoulder and say, softly, *She forgave you before you left. She wants you to know the dog got out.* And then she had looked up at Wren across the fire with those ledger-keeper's eyes — eyes that tallied everything, that missed nothing, that had already counted the exact number of steps between Wren and the nearest escape from this duty — and she had said nothing at all. That silence had been worse than an accusation. Accusations could be answered. Silence just accumulated interest.

The sentry passed. Wren counted his footfalls the way they counted everything: four to the corner of the wagons, twenty-two down the line, a pause at the fire to warm his hands, and back. Ninety seconds, if the wind didn't spook him. They had spent the whole evening learning his rhythm while pretending to check the manifest by firelight, and the whole evening the oath had grown louder, pressing up through their chest like water rising in a sealed hold.

*This is mutiny,* the vow said. *This is the death of everything you are.*

"Yes," Wren murmured, and pulled the lock.

It was a cheap thing, guild-stamped iron, meant to keep frightened people in rather than determined people out. It opened with a click that sounded, in the dead hush of the camp, like a bone snapping.

Inside the wagon, Freya was already awake. Of course she was. She sat wrapped in her own coat amid the straw, the little carved charm at her throat cupped in both hands as though she were warming a bird, and her eyes found Wren's in the dark with terrible calm.

"You took longer than I expected," she whispered.

"You expected this?"

"I keep ledgers, Wren Halvard. I watched you lie to me about mercy and I watched the lie cost you — you went white to the lips and your hand shook for an hour after. A person who pays that much for a small kindness is a person who will eventually pay more for a large one." She tilted her head. "I merely had to wait for the interest to compound."

For one absurd moment Wren wanted to laugh. Then the oath understood what was happening, and stopped warning, and began to punish.

It started in the hand that had turned the lock. Not pain exactly — pain would have been merciful, pain could be bitten down on — but *unmaking*, a sensation of being peeled from the inside, as though someone had reached into Wren's arm and begun drawing out the threads of them one by one. The brand flared beneath the skin of the wrist: the crown's mark, the interlocked circles every oath-keeper carried, glowing sudden and furious white-hot through the flesh like a coal seen through paper.

Wren bit down on a scream. Freya was already moving, scrambling over the tailgate, catching their elbow as they sagged.

"What is it? What's happening?"

"The vow," Wren managed. "It knows."

"Can you stop it?"

"No." The word came out ground to powder. "There is no stopping it. There is only finishing, or—" The world whited at the edges. "—or breaking."

"Then break it." Her grip tightened, fierce and certain, and there was no fear in her face at all, which was somehow the most frightening thing about her. "Break it now, whatever it costs, because in ninety seconds that sentry comes back around the wagons and we are both dead or worse."

"I don't—" Wren's knees hit the frozen mud. The brand was climbing now, past the wrist, crawling up the forearm in branching white lines like frost given a taste for flesh, and each branch burned away something they hadn't known they were holding. The oath had been in them so long, wound so deep into muscle and memory, that tearing it loose tore loose pieces of Wren with it. They felt it take their certainty. They felt it take the clean, simple shape of their days. Somewhere in the flaying it found the memory of the child they had been, kneeling on cold stone, swearing, and ripped that open too, and Wren tasted blood and realized they had bitten their tongue through.

Freya crouched over them, shielding them from the firelight with her body, her hands hard on their shoulders.

"Look at me," she said. "Only at me. What do you want, Wren? Not the crown. Not the vow. You."

What did they want?

They wanted to never again stand on a ridge and watch a pyre. They wanted the corporal's grandmother to have gotten the dog out. They wanted to un-hear the sound the crowd had made at the first burning, two days ago — that low, awed, hungry murmur, the sound of ordinary people warming themselves at someone else's annihilation. They wanted to be the kind of person who opened locks instead of guarding them.

They wanted, with a clarity that cut through the agony like a blade through silk, to see this woman live.

"There," Freya breathed, watching their face. "There it is. Hold onto that."

And Wren broke the vow.

Not all of it — no one breaks an oath entire; it is stitched too deep — but they seized the central thread, the binding clause, the part that said *deliver her to the pyre*, and they snapped it the way one snaps a neck: quickly, deliberately, past the point of any return.

The brand screamed.

Light poured up Wren's arm, white searing branches reaching for the shoulder, the throat, the heart — and stopped. Hung there. The broken clause died, but the rest of the oath lived, wounded and furious, and it settled into Wren's flesh as a permanent brand: a blackened tracery from wrist to elbow, raised and glistening, that pulsed with every heartbeat like a thing alive. Like a leash with the far end cut off, still fastened to the collar.

Wren came back to themselves on hands and knees in the straw, shaking, drenched in sweat that froze instantly in the night air. Their arm throbbed in time with their pulse. When they flexed the fingers, the black lines shifted, faintly luminous, like veins of some dark ore.

"It didn't finish," Freya said quietly. She was reading their arm the way she read ledgers. "It's still in you."

"The rest of the vow survived. Loyalty, obedience, the general clauses." Wren's voice came out sanded raw. "I killed only the part that delivers you. The rest will punish me whenever I act against the crown — less than before, perhaps, but forever. This is what I am now. Half-sworn. Half-flayed."

"Then you're free enough to run and bound enough to be hunted." She said it without pity, the way she recorded facts. "We should go."

Boots on frozen ground. The sentry, early, the wind having changed. Wren hauled themself up the wagon's side, and Freya was already moving — quick, silent, practical, dropping from the tailgate into shadow. Behind them, in the other wagons, the registered slept on. Wren looked back once at the canvas shapes of them, at the child's small breathing, and felt the surviving half of the oath twist like a knife, whispering that these too were Wren's charge, these too were being abandoned.

*I can't carry everyone,* Wren thought, and hated the thought, and ran.

* * *

They went north through the pine dark, off the road, following a streambed because running water confused tracking dogs and Wren had spent enough years on the Hunt's edge of things to know how its machinery worked. Freya moved well for a ledger-keeper — sure-footed, uncomplaining, breathing in the measured rhythm of someone who had decided panic was an expense she would not incur. Twice she steadied Wren when the brand flared without cause, random punishments from the wounded vow, lashing out at nothing. Each time her hand on the blackened skin quieted it. Neither of them remarked on this. There was no time, and later there would be no words adequate to it.

They stopped at dawn in a hollow beneath a granite shelf, where frost ferned the stones and the pines stood close as a congregation. Wren slid down against the rock and finally let their body have its account of the night: the shaking, the nausea, the deep cellular exhaustion of a man who has amputated part of his own soul with his bare hands.

Freya built no fire. She sat beside him instead, close enough for warmth, and unwrapped the bundle she'd carried from the wagon: hard bread, a heel of cheese, a skin of water. Rations she had hoarded, he realized, against exactly this morning. She had been preparing to run since the moment the wagon door closed. Perhaps since the moment the heralds spoke.

"Eat," she said.

He ate. The bread tasted of nothing; everything tasted of nothing since the burning on the ridge. Afterward she took his arm without asking, turned it to the grey light, and studied the brand.

"It's spread," she said. "An inch past the elbow since midnight."

"Will it keep spreading?"

"I don't know. I've never—" She stopped. Corrected herself, the way she always corrected herself, mid-sentence, refusing even now to claim knowledge she didn't have. "No one has broken a crown-oath and lived to be studied. You may be the first entry in a very short ledger."

"A short book. Fitting company for my life's work."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go, in a person who had rationed them carefully for years. Then it faded, and she looked north, toward Ashfall, toward the smoke that even here, even at this distance, stained the horizon a faint bruised yellow.

"The voices are louder today," she said.

Wren went still. He had heard her speak of the voices the way other people spoke of weather — a fact of her landscape, neither confessed nor concealed. But last night, in the wagon, she had said something else. Something about the burned.

"Tell me," he said.

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness of someone reading aloud figures she wishes were different.

"When they burn the gifted, the death isn't clean. Our gift doesn't... end, Wren. It disperses. And the dead who were carrying it — the ones whose names we keep, the ones who cling to us because we're the last ones who remember them — they don't disperse with us. They're torn loose. Scattered. Screaming." Her thumb moved absently over the charm at her throat. "Since the ridge, I hear dozens of them. New voices. All saying the same word, over and over."

"What word?"

"Vhalen." She looked at him. "The pyres aren't just killing us. They're feeding something. Every gift the Hunt burns goes somewhere, and every voice it tears loose goes somewhere, and they're all going to the same place — a country that isn't on any map anymore. The dead are pointing at it, Wren. The burned are pointing at it. Whatever the crown thinks it's doing with its purges, it's building a road to Vhalen out of our ashes, and I don't think the crown knows it."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere far behind them, faint as memory, a bell rang the hour in some town where the registers were being posted and neighbors were deciding, with heavy hearts and steady hands, whom to report.

Wren looked down at their arm. The black tracery pulsed. Half-sworn, half-flayed — a man with the leash cut but the collar still burning, hunted now by the very machine he had served, unable to go back and unable, he was beginning to understand, to go anywhere at all without her. Because she was the only person alive who could touch that brand and make it quiet. Because she heard the dead, and the dead were screaming a direction. Because the oath had taken his certainty and his clean shape and his name, and what remained needed a purpose the way a wound needs a dressing.

"They'll hunt you," he said. "The oath-breaker who lost his prisoner. My face will be on the registers by tomorrow noon, next to yours."

"I know."

"They'll use the hounds. Not scent-hounds — oath-hounds. They track broken vows. I'm the loudest broken vow in the province. They won't need to search for us. We'll leave a trail like spilled ink."

"I know that too." Freya folded the bread back into its cloth with neat, economical movements, a woman putting her affairs in order. "So we don't run apart. That's the arithmetic, Wren. Alone, you're a branded traitor they can follow, and alone, I'm a registered witch they can burn. Together we're merely difficult." She rose, brushing frost from her coat, and offered him her hand — the same hand that had quieted the brand, offered now plainly, in the grey light, as a bargain and not a kindness. "Neither of us survives this alone. So we survive it together, or we don't survive it at all. Those are the entries. Take them or leave them."

Wren looked at her hand. Then at the north, where the smoke stood over the pyre-city like a pillar of witness, and where, beneath it, beneath the maps, beneath the world, a drowned country full of screaming dead was waiting for someone to write its name down.

He took her hand. The brand flared once at the contact — the wounded oath recognizing its enemy — and then, beneath her fingers, went quiet.

"North," Wren said. "Then we'd better move before the ink dries."
