## 8. A Guild of Impossible Trades, Liquidated

We walked back to Corvane the way mourners walk to a grave they have not yet admitted is a grave: slowly, without speaking, each of us hoping the ground would open first.

Freya counted her steps because it was better than counting the voices. The dead of the burned wagons had quieted somewhat since she had begun writing them down — she had filled six pages of Wren's stolen notebook with names scratched in charcoal, the letters crooked because the wagon jolted and her hands would not stop shaking — but quiet was not silence. They murmured under everything now, like a river beneath ice. *Vhalen. Vhalen. Vhalen.* Dozens of throats agreeing on a single word, the way a congregation agrees on an amen.

Wren walked ahead. Of course they walked ahead; they had been walking ahead since the night they cut her wagon loose and the oath had flayed them for it. The brand climbed their arm in slow black filigree, vine-work of pain that glowed faintly when the oath stirred, and Freya had learned to read its brightness the way sailors read weather. Tonight it was dark. Whatever the vow demanded of them at this moment, Wren was managing to disobey it without cost, which meant the vow did not know yet what they were doing. She found that thought strangely comforting. Even the crown's magic could be lied to by someone stubborn enough.

"You're limping," Wren said, without turning around.

"I'm not limping. I'm favoring my left foot, which is a completely different thing and none of your business."

"It's my business. You die, I've broken an oath for nothing."

The cruelty of it was almost restful. Wren's kindness came wrapped in barbed wire, and Freya had learned to take it by the wire. "Three miles," she said. "The guildhouse is three miles past that ridge. The chimney smoke should be visible from — "

There was no smoke. There was no chimney smoke, no hearth smoke, no forge smoke from the smithy two streets over where Tam Broadwell made the little brass scales the guild used for weighing years. From the ridge, in the gray hour before dawn, Corvane looked like a mouth with all its teeth drawn.

Wren stopped beside her. For once they said nothing, which told her more than any words would have.

"We should go around," Freya heard herself say. "Circle wide. Come in from the mill road."

"Why?"

"Because if there's no smoke, then either everyone has left, or everyone has been made to leave, and I would like one more mile of not knowing which."

Wren looked at her — really looked, the way they rarely did, their pale eyes moving over her face as if auditing her — and then, without a word, turned off the main track onto the mill road. It was the closest thing to mercy Freya had ever received from an oath-keeper, and she hated how much it moved her.

* * *

They came into Corvane through the alley behind the cooper's yard, and the first thing Freya smelled was ash — not hearth-ash, not the soft grey smell of a banked fire, but the sharp chemical reek of paper burned in quantity. Her stomach understood before her mind allowed it to. Paper, and leather, and glue. Books. Ledgers.

The guildhouse stood on Chandler's Square like a man who had been beaten and propped up against a wall to wait for questioning. Its doors hung open. Its windows were intact — that was somehow the worst part; no mob had come through screaming, nothing so honest as violence. The front hall had been swept. Someone had organized this. Someone had done it *neatly.*

"Inquisition protocol," Wren said quietly. Their voice had gone flat, official, the voice of the person they had been before the wagon in the night. "No forced entry. No property damage beyond what's necessary. They want the guild to look abandoned, not attacked. It discourages questions."

"And you know this because you've done it."

"I've read the reports." A pause. "I never did it."

"I didn't ask."

"No," Wren said. "You didn't. That's worse."

Inside, the great hall of the Guild of Impossible Trades was a body with its organs removed. The long counting tables remained, scarred oak polished by two hundred years of elbows, but the pigeonholes above them gaped empty, the deed-boxes gone, the shelves stripped to the wood. The brass scales were missing from their cabinet — Tam's scales, the ones he balanced to the grain — though someone had left one pan behind, lying upside-down on the floor like a dropped coin. The guildmaster's chair sat behind the master's desk, precisely centered, precisely empty.

Freya walked the length of the hall and touched nothing and heard her own breath and underneath her breath, faintly, the dead murmuring *Vhalen,* and she thought: *I balanced these tables every morning for nine years. I squared the ledgers every evening. Nine years, and the world can be emptied between two dawns.*

"They took the archive," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Everything. Even the — " She stopped. Swallowed. "Even the bottled grief."

"The what?"

"Grief. People trade it here. When it's too heavy to carry and too precious to throw away. We keep it in stoppered glass, cellar level, catalogued by weight." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "It's all gone. Forty years of other people's sorrow, confiscated by the crown. What do you suppose they'll do with it? Drink it? Pour it in the harbor? Or will it just sit in some warehouse in the capital, all that grief, waiting for someone foolish enough to open it?"

"Freya."

"Don't say my name like that. Like you're sorry. You're an instrument of the state standing in a room your state emptied. Save your voice for the report."

The words landed. She watched them land — saw Wren's jaw tighten, saw the brand on their arm flicker faintly warm, because somewhere in the tangled grammar of their oath, cruelty to the condemned was permitted and perhaps even required, and honesty about it was not. Freya felt a small, ugly satisfaction, and then felt sick for feeling it. This was what the Hunt did, she realized. It didn't only burn the gifted. It taught everyone else to be cruel in smaller increments, cruelty by the inch, until a woman could stand in a looted hall and feel *proud* of landing a blow.

"I'm going upstairs," she said. "To my room. Don't follow me. Or do. I don't care anymore."

She cared enormously. She hated that too.

* * *

Her room was on the third floor, the small one at the back with the dormer window, and it had been searched with the same terrible courtesy as everything else. The bed was made — she had made it, four days and one lifetime ago. Her books stood on their shelf in perfect order, untouched, because her books were ordinary books, arithmetic and grammar and a battered bestiary, and nothing about them whispered. The searcher had known exactly what to look for and exactly what to ignore, and that precision frightened her more than wreckage would have. Someone had read her life correctly from the doorway.

On her pillow lay a single sheet of paper, folded once.

She stood in the doorway a long time before she picked it up. Every instinct honed by nine years of impossible trades said: *a thing left in plain sight is bait.* But her name was on the outside, written in a clerk's hand, and beneath the name, the seal — not wax but a stamp in black ink, a circle bisected by a sword. The Hunt.

*Freya Delacroix, of Corvane. Registered. Confirmed. Condemned.*

Not a summons. Not a demand. Just the sentence, in eleven words, laid where her head would go. They had wanted her to find it. They had wanted her to know that while she ran through the winter with an oath-keeper, someone had walked into her bedroom and finished the paperwork.

She sat down on the bed she had made and held the page and did not cry, because the dead in her skull had gone suddenly, completely silent, as if even they understood that this was a moment that required attendance rather than noise.

When she came downstairs, Wren was in the master's study, standing over the guildmaster's desk, and the look on their face stopped her in the doorframe.

"What?" she said.

"They didn't take everything." Wren stepped aside.

On the desk, alone in the middle of the bare leather top, sat Master Ondrey's ledger. The great one. The master ledger of the Guild of Impossible Trades, the book in which every trade ever brokered was entered in the guildmaster's own cramped hand — forty years of bottled grief and debts paid in years and oaths resold and love traded across class lines at ruinous exchange rates. Freya knew its weight in her arms the way she knew her own heartbeat. It had been the first book she'd been trusted to carry.

They hadn't taken it. They had left it. And that was wrong in a way that made the skin of her arms crawl, because the Hunt confiscated; the Hunt did not leave gifts.

"Don't touch it," Wren said, but she was already reaching, because she was already certain, with the awful certainty of the bone-charm at her throat that had begun, very softly, to whisper again — and she opened the cover.

The flyleaf. Her own handwriting.

*Received into custody, this 3rd day of Frostmonth, one (1) charm of carved bone, provenance: maternal line of Freya Delacroix, admitted value: inestimable. Entered by F. Delacroix, ledger-keeper.*

She stared at it. It was her hand. Not similar — identical. The slight rightward slant, the way her *F*s hooked, the tiny ink-blotch after *Delacroix* where her pen always caught on the downstroke because she pressed too hard, Master Ondrey teased her about it, *you write like you're engraving, girl, ink isn't ivory.* It was her entry. She remembered making it, three years ago, the afternoon her mother's charm was formally logged into the guild's custody — except she had never made it. The charm had never been logged. She had kept it hidden, always, against her sternum, because logging it would have put her gift on paper.

Page two. Her hand again.

*Received into custody, this 11th day of Thawmonth, testimony regarding one (1) unregistered practitioner, name supplied below.*

And below, in her own ink, her own pressure, her own hooked letters:

*Freya Delacroix.*

Page after page. She turned them with fingers that had gone numb and distant, and every page was her confession, her registration, her denunciation, written in the only handwriting in the world that could not be forged — because it was hers, drawn out of her somehow, pulled up through memory like water through a wick. Dates she recognized and dates she didn't. Entries describing things she had only thought, private calculations of fear she had never spoken aloud — *considered flight on the night of the Heralds; considered burning the ledgers; considered, briefly, letting the charm speak to see if my mother's voice still knew my name* — each one set down with a clerk's impartial neatness, each one damning.

"They couldn't prove what you are," Wren said. Their voice was very low, and something in it she had not heard before — something that might have been horror on her behalf. "A gift leaves no mark. No sign a magistrate can swear to. So they needed evidence. Written evidence, in a hand no one could dispute." They swallowed. "This is how they liquidated the guild, Freya. Not with soldiers. With paperwork. Every member's file, every trade record, cross-referenced — and at the center of it, the one piece of proof that can't be challenged, because it testifies to itself."

"They reached inside me," Freya said. She was surprised at how steady her voice came out, how nearly conversational. "Some clerk in a black coat reached into my head while I slept in a hay barn and *transcribed* me. My fears. My thoughts. He copied out my thoughts in my own hand and called it evidence." She closed the ledger. Opened it again. Closed it. Her hands wanted to do something and there was nothing for them to do. "Where is Master Ondrey?"

"No one knows. He wasn't among the registered. He wasn't among the—" Wren stopped.

"The burned. You can say it. You've seen it from a ridge; I've heard it in my skull."

"He wasn't among either list. Which means he either fled, or he cooperated, or—" Wren's eyes went to the ledger. "Or he made a trade."

Freya laughed again, and this time the laugh broke in the middle and became something else, something hot running down her face that she wiped away furiously with the heel of her hand. "Of course. Of course he did. That's what he does. That's what we *do*, isn't it — the whole rotten beautiful idea of the guild, everything has a price, every impossibility can be brokered if you just find the right currency. He sold the guild to save himself, or he sold *me* to save the ledgers, or he sold the ledgers to save the apprentices — I don't know, I can't know, and the worst part, Wren, the truly unforgivable part—"

She stopped. Because her eye had caught on the last page of the ledger, the final entry, and it was not in her hand.

It was in Ondrey's. Cramped, precise, the ink-blot after the date where his nib always caught. One line only, dated the day of the seizure:

*Item: one (1) country, drowned and erased, held in trust pending collection. Location: see what is not on the map. Value: everything.*

Below it, in the margin, in smaller script, almost an afterthought — almost a plea:

*Girl, if you are reading this, the cellar. Third brick. Take nothing else.*

Freya read it twice. Then she looked up and found Wren watching her, and the brand on their arm was glowing, faint and steady, the way it did when the oath sensed disobedience gathering like weather.

"Well?" Wren said.

"He left us a map reference," Freya said. "In a country that's been cut off the maps." She wiped her face a second time, and when her hand came down, it was steady. The rage was still there — it would always be there now, she suspected, a coal banked in her chest next to the whispering bone — but rage was a currency too, and she was, whatever else had been taken from her, still a ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Trades. She knew exactly what to do with a debt.

"Come on," she said. "Third brick. And bring the light."
