## 9. The Map With a Hole in It

The guildhouse of Corvane had been gutted the way a fish is gutted — quickly, expertly, everything of value pulled out through one wound. We came back to it three days after the seizure, in the blue hour before dawn, when even oath-hounds sleep badly.

Wren Halvard crouched at the mouth of the alley behind the guild and watched the windows. Nothing moved behind them. Nothing had moved behind them for years, if Freya was right; the guildmaster had kept his true wealth where no auditor would think to look, which meant not in the vaults, not in the strongroom, but somewhere so ordinary it had become invisible.

"Tell me again," Wren said.

Freya Delacroix stood beside them with her hood down, because she had decided, in her quiet and infuriating way, that hiding was finished. Her breath fogged. At her throat, the bone charm sat still as a held tongue. "He used to say the guild trades in impossibilities," she said. "And the most impossible trade of all is selling something you don't have yet. He always kept a reserve. A cache. He told me once — drunk, once only — that he kept it *in the arithmetic*."

"In the arithmetic."

"He meant the ledgers. The numbers themselves. Somewhere in the accounts there's an entry that doesn't balance, and the imbalance is the door." She looked up at the dark windows. "I kept his books for eleven years, Wren. I found every error he ever made. He made none. Except one, if it's an error, and I could never decide."

Wren's arm ached under the bandages. The brand had gone quiet since the river — since her hand on it, which was a fact they were both pretending not to have noticed — but quiet was not gone. It sat under their skin like a coal banked in ash, waiting for them to lie, or disobey, or hope, and punish accordingly. Broken oaths did not stop working just because you'd stopped believing in them.

"All right," Wren said. "Show me the entry."

* * *

Inside smelled of ash and wet paper. The Hunt had burned what wouldn't carry: shelves, chairs, the long table where Freya had spent her working life. Scorch marks climbed the walls in black tongues. But the archive room below had survived, because archives were state property now, and the crown's men had taken the ledgers whole — every volume, every page, hauled off in wagons toward the pyre-city to be read as evidence.

Except, apparently, one.

Freya knelt in the wrecked counting room and pulled a splintered drawer from the ruins of a desk. From inside it she drew a single ledger, thin, unremarkable, its cover stained with ink and candle-grease. Wren recognized the binding. It matched the hundreds of volumes now rolling east under guard.

"This was seized," Wren said.

"A copy was seized. He kept two of everything. Always two." Her hands were steady, but Wren saw the effort of the steadiness, like watching someone hold a door shut against wind. "Quarter-day accounts, autumn before last. Here." She opened the ledger flat on the floor and turned it so the guttering lantern caught the columns.

Wren had audited a hundred guildhouses. They knew the grammar of numbers the way priests knew psalms, and they followed Freya's finger down the page with a professional's attention: receipts, disbursements, fees, fines. Ordinary. Meticulous. Then the last line.

*A gift received: 1 (one). Source withheld.*

Below it, in the margin, in the guildmaster's cramped hand, a fraction: **1/0**.

"That's your imbalance," Wren said.

"One divided by nothing." Freya's voice had gone very soft. "It isn't an error, Wren. It can't be balanced. It can't be carried forward or written off. It just sits there at the bottom of the page like a hole." She paused. "He said once that some things are worth exactly what they cost you to keep. I thought he was being sentimental. He was giving me coordinates."

Wren looked at the fraction a long moment. Then at Freya. "How does a number become a place?"

"I don't know. But he hid things in plain sight his whole life, and he taught me to do the same." She took out her carving knife — the small bone-handled one Wren had watched her use a hundred times without understanding — and set the blade's point against the margin, beside the fraction. "Hold the light steady."

She began to cut.

Not into the ledger. Into the page beneath it. Wren heard the wrongness of the sound immediately — steel on steel, almost, though there was no steel under the paper. Freya peeled back the last leaf like bark, and underneath, where the backing board should have been, there was oilcloth. Under the oilcloth, a packet wrapped in waxed linen, tied with guildmaster's cord.

For a moment neither of them touched it. The dead room ticked and settled around them. Somewhere far off, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You first," Wren said, which surprised them both, because it was trust, and trust was a currency Wren Halvard had sworn never to spend.

Freya untied the cord.

* * *

The map was drawn on vellum, large enough to cover the counting-room floor, and at first glance it was simply beautiful — a survey of the whole realm rendered in the old style, hills shaded like muscle, rivers inked in silver that caught the lantern light and ran with it. Wren had studied maps their entire adult life. They knew this coastline, these mountain ranges, these roads they had walked as an oath-keeper in service of the crown.

They did not know the hole.

There was no other word for it. In the north-central reach of the realm, roughly where every schoolbook agreed lay the province of Northmarch and beyond it "the old frontier," the vellum simply stopped. Not torn — cut. Edges scissored clean and then bound, every cut border whipped shut with thread that was not thread. Wren leaned close and felt the hair rise on their arms.

Oath-magic. Stitched into the map the way a vow is stitched into a tongue. Each stitch a tiny knot of compulsion; each knot humming faintly against Wren's brand like a tuning fork answering another. Their arm throbbed in sympathy, and they understood, with a lurch, that the map wasn't a picture of the stitching.

The stitching was a picture of the country.

"It's real," Freya whispered. "The map isn't showing us the seal. The map *is* the seal. Or part of it. One copy of it, anyway." She spread her hands above the hole, careful not to touch the stitched edge. "Look at how the rivers end. Look at the roads. They don't fade, they're *cut*. Someone took a country out of the world the way you'd take a page out of a book, and then sewed the pages together so no one would hear the paper rustle."

"Vhalen," Wren said.

The name came out of them before they could stop it, and the moment it left their mouth the room changed temperature. The lantern flame leaned. Beneath the floor, in the bones of the gutted guildhouse, in the walls that still remembered eleven years of Freya's careful handwriting, something answered.

The bone charm at Freya's throat woke up.

Wren had heard it murmur before — a dry whisper, like sand shifting, easy to dismiss. This was not that. The charm spoke now in many voices at once, dozens, scores, a congregation clearing its throat, and all of them said the same thing, over and over, with the cadence of pointing fingers:

*Through. Through. Through.*

"They mean the hole," Freya breathed. Her hand rose to the charm as if to soothe it. "They're not screaming anymore. Listen—" And Wren did listen, helplessly, because since the broken vow their ears had begun to betray them too, catching edges of that chorus like fragments of song through a wall. The voices weren't ragged now. They were organized. Urgent, but organized, the way soldiers are urgent. "*Through the stitch, past the knot, down the old road, we wait, we wait, we wait—*"

"Stop," Wren said, and hated how their voice shook. "Stop repeating it."

Freya looked at them across the map, across the hole where a country should be, and her face in the lantern light was terrible and calm. "You already know where it goes."

Wren did not answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

They worked by the lantern until the sky outside went grey, because neither of them could sleep after that, and because the map rewarded attention the way a lock rewards patience. Freya read it the way she read ledgers — systematically, hungrily, cross-referencing against memory. Wren read it the way an oath-keeper reads a crime scene, and between them, slowly, the shape of the thing assembled.

"The stitches aren't decoration," Wren said, tracing the eastern border of the hole with a fingertip held a careful inch above the thread. "There are seven knots along here. Major ones. Each one is an oath — a big one, sworn by someone with authority. You can feel the weight of them. They're not just sealing the border." They hesitated, then said the rest. "They're feeding it. Every stitch draws on something. A vow, a promise, a debt owed and unpaid."

"And the pyres?" Freya's voice was flat. Carefully flat.

Wren thought about the ridge, the burning they had watched from the road, the smoke standing up straight in the cold air like pillars holding something up. They thought about the voices in the wagons, dozens of the burned screaming one name. "Fuel," they said. "Every gifted person the Hunt burns is an oath added to the weave. The crown isn't killing you people out of fear, Freya. Fear doesn't build anything. This is construction. They're feeding the seal with bodies so it holds forever."

Freya sat back on her heels. For a long moment she said nothing, and Wren watched her do the thing she did — the ledger-keeper's discipline, grief converted to arithmetic, horror entered in neat columns where it could be examined without bleeding. When she spoke again her voice was steady.

"Then the Hunt won't stop. Not ever. It can't. A seal fed by pyres needs pyres the way a fire needs wood. As long as anyone living carries the gift, the burning continues." She looked up. "That means running was never going to save anyone. Not me, not the others like me. There is no distance from this."

"No," Wren admitted. "There isn't."

"So we don't run." She laid her palm flat on the vellum beside the hole — not touching the stitch, but near, near enough that the whispers sharpened. "We go through."

"There is no *through*." Wren heard the words come out harsher than intended, and let them stand that way, because harshness was cheaper than the alternative, which was terror. "That's the entire point of the thing. Vhalen isn't a country behind a locked door. It's a country unwritten. The maps say it never existed. The treaties say it never existed. The children born after the fall were taught to *forget*, and the teaching took, and the ones who couldn't forget were—" They stopped.

"Were burned," Freya finished quietly. "Or stitched in. Depending on which side of the border they were standing on when it happened."

"Yes."

"Then whoever cut this hole out of the map wanted someone to find it." She tapped the margin of the vellum, and Wren saw, for the first time, the small inscription inked along the lower edge in the guildmaster's hand — the same cramped script as the fraction in the ledger. Freya translated aloud, and her voice slowed as she read, the way a voice slows stepping onto ice:

"*What is removed may be returned. What is returned must be paid for. Ask the door what it costs before you knock.*"

Silence took the room. The lantern hissed. Under the floorboards, faint as a pulse, the chorus of the dead kept its patient rhythm: *we wait, we wait, we wait.*

"Hope with a price tag," Freya said at last, and laughed — a small, cracked sound with no humor in it, only amazement. "Even his miracles come itemized."

Wren looked at the hole in the map and felt the brand on their arm burn in sympathy with every stitch, and understood something they had been refusing to understand since the river, since the wagon, since the ridge: that the pain was not punishment anymore. It was orientation. The oath in their flesh knew the way the way a compass needle knows north, and it strained toward that hole like a dog at the end of a chain.

They were Vhalen-born. They had known it since the confession on the road, and knowing it had changed nothing and everything. Now the map proved it in thread and vellum: the crown had cut Wren's childhood out of the world, and the wound in the parchment fit the wound in them exactly.

"If we go," Wren said slowly, "we go as thieves. Through a stitch in the world, past oaths older than either of us, into a country that the entire authority of the realm exists to keep empty. And whatever we find there—" they met Freya's eyes "—it will want something. Doors like that always want something. His note says so. The dead say so. Everything about this says so."

"I know." Freya began rolling the map with the tenderness of someone handling a shroud. "I've done the accounting, Wren. Whatever the price is, we're already paying it. We've been paying since the day the heralds read the proclamation. Interest accrues." The roll of vellum went into her satchel, against her spine, where a ledger would ride. "All that's left to choose is whether we pay while running or pay while walking toward something."

Outside, the grey had brightened toward morning, which meant hunters, which meant movement. Wren stood, joints cracking, and slung their pack, and at the ruined doorway they paused and looked back once at the gutted counting room — the scorched shelves, the splintered desk, the drawer hanging open where a fraction divided by nothing had guarded a country for twenty years.

The guildmaster, wherever he was, had made his final entry. It said: *go north.*

"We'll need horses," Wren said. "And we'll need to be over the river before the hounds pick up the brand. And Freya—" they hesitated on the threshold, morning wind pushing at them like a hand "—when we get to the stitch. If the door asks its price before we knock."

Freya adjusted the satchel strap and did not look away.

"Then we'll finally get an honest quote," she said, "from someone in the impossible trades."

She stepped past Wren into the cold, and the bone charm at her throat whispered northward, and the dead went with them, patiently, in their thousands, keeping count of every step that brought them closer to the hole in the world where everything they had lost was waiting — and waiting, and waiting, and had begun, lately, to wonder why.
