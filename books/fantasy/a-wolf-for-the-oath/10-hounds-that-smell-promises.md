## 10. Hounds That Smell Promises

The dogs did not come for three days, and in those three days I learned that running from the crown was mostly a matter of feet.

Feet, and mud, and Wren Halvard's terrible sense of direction.

"We're going west," they said on the first morning, standing at a crossroads where four signs pointed in four directions, none of them agreeing.

"The sun is behind us," I said.

"Then we're going west by way of a detour."

"You're lost."

"I am *proceeding*," Wren said, with the flat dignity of an oath-keeper who had spent their whole life being certain and could not afford to stop now, "in a direction."

I let it go. There were larger problems than pride. My new ledger — a slim thing of birch bark and bone slivers I had begun binding together at night while Wren slept — rode against my ribs under my coat, and every few miles another voice joined the chorus inside the bones, soft as snowfall: names, names, all of them ending too soon, all of them saying *Vhalen* like a prayer or an accusation. The burned dead did not rest. They followed me the way gulls follow a fishing boat, patient and hungry, and I carried them because no one else could hear them, and because if I stopped carrying them I would have to think about what else I was carrying.

Wren's arm, for one.

The brand had not faded since the night they opened my wagon. It crawled from wrist to elbow in black filigree, like frost grown on glass, and when the oath twisted in them — which it did whenever they moved too fast toward freedom, or thought too kindly of me, or forgot, even for a breath, that they were supposed to be delivering me to a pyre — the filigree *moved*, tightening like a garrote under the skin. The first time I saw it happen they went white to the lips and sat down hard in the road and said, in a perfectly level voice, "Give me a moment."

I gave them several moments. I pretended not to count the seconds between each spasm of their jaw.

That was before the hounds.

* * *

We heard about them from a tinker at a crossroads market too poor to have anything worth taxing. He was selling dented pots and telling fortunes he made up on the spot, and when Wren paid for two apples with crown coin — a mistake; oath-keepers carry money stamped with the seal they broke — the old man's eyes went wide and sly.

"You want to keep walking fast," he said, low, folding the coin into his sleeve. "There's hounds out of Corvane. Not normal hounds." He tapped his own forearm, where a scar showed pale through rolled sleeves. "My brother ran with a broken vow once. They found him in a hay barn, forty miles from where he swore it. They don't track blood." He looked at Wren's coat, at the way they stood, at something — maybe the smell of an oath gone wrong, sour as burnt hair, that only certain people could catch. "They track promises."

Wren's face did nothing at all. That was the frightening thing about them; I had learned to read the small movements everyone else missed, and there were none. "Thank you for the apples," they said.

But I saw their hand close around the brand as we walked away, saw the knuckles go white, and I understood what the tinker had told us, in his way, without knowing it:

Wren Halvard was a bell that would not stop ringing. Every mile we fled rang them louder. Somewhere behind us, dogs with clever heads and crueler masters had lifted their noses to the sound.

"They can't smell a broken vow," I said that night, in the lee of a fallen wall, wind worrying the fire down to embers. "That's absurd. Vows aren't — they aren't *things*. They don't have a smell."

"You carve the names of the dead into chicken bones and they answer you," Wren said. "You are the last person in this country who gets to call something absurd."

Fair. I pulled my coat tighter. "So how do they do it?"

Wren was quiet long enough that I thought they wouldn't answer. Then they pushed back their sleeve, and the brand drank the firelight, and they spoke the way people speak about weather that has already ruined them.

"An oath is a shape. You say the words, and the words make a vessel, and something pours in to fill it — duty, call it, or law, or just the weight of being watched. While you keep the vow, the vessel holds. It sits quiet in you like water in a cup." Their finger traced the black lines. "When you break it, the cup cracks. And a cracked cup doesn't hold anything anymore. It *leaks*. All along its broken edges, forever, wherever you go." They looked up. "The hounds aren't hunting me. They're hunting the leak. I am the loudest fugitive in seven kingdoms, Freya. I have been shouting our position since the night I cut your wagon loose."

I thought about that. About the three days of careful footprints and cold camps and doubled-back roads, all of it theater, all of it useless, because the trail we left wasn't made of boot prints at all.

"Can you mend it?" I asked. "Re-swear the vow? Close the crack?"

The look Wren gave me then had layers. Disgust on top. Underneath, something rawer.

"Swear again to deliver you to the fire? Yes. That would silence me beautifully. They'd find us by morning." They pulled the sleeve down. "No. A broken vow doesn't heal. It can only be replaced, and everything I might replace it with is worse, or is theirs." A pause. "There's one other thing. I've heard of it. Never seen it done. Some vows can be *quieted* — not mended, just... muffled. Held shut, the way you'd hold a wound closed with your hands. But it takes another person. Someone willing to keep their hands on the break and not let go."

"That sounds like a folk remedy," I said.

"It is a folk remedy. Most of what's true is."

I should have thought longer. I should have weighed it, the way I weighed entries in a ledger, cost against cost. Instead I heard myself say, "Show me," and reached across the fire before either of us had agreed on what showing meant.

My fingers settled over the brand.

The effect was immediate and total. Wren made a sound I had never heard from them — not pain, exactly; more like the sound a person makes when a headache they've had so long they forgot it was there suddenly stops. Their whole body unlocked at once, shoulders dropping, spine curving, and they caught themselves on one hand before they folded into the fire.

"What—" Their voice came out wrecked. They cleared their throat and tried again. "What did you do?"

"Nothing. I'm just holding it." I almost took my hand away. I didn't. Under my palm the black filigree lay still for the first time since I'd known them, dormant as a winter branch. "Does it always feel like that? Like — grinding?"

"Like grinding," Wren agreed faintly. "Yes. Always. Since the wagon." They stared at my hand on their arm with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — suspicion, wonder, and a third thing underneath both that they were clearly working very hard not to have. "Why does it stop when you touch it?"

"How would I know? I balance accounts. I don't fix oaths."

"You hear the dead."

"That's different."

"It isn't different at all," they said softly, and neither of us argued the point, because I think we both felt the truth of it settle between us like a coal rolled out of the fire: gifts were gifts, whatever they touched. Mine heard what shouldn't be heard. Perhaps it could also hold shut what shouldn't be open.

I kept my hand where it was longer than necessity required. They let me.

* * *

The river saved our lives twice in one afternoon, and nearly ended them a third time.

It was the Sorrel, wide and gray-brown and swollen with early melt, running fast through a country of willow flats. We came down to it ahead of the hounds — though we didn't know we were ahead yet; we only knew the tinker's warning, and Wren's arm had been grinding all morning, worse and worse, until they walked with their teeth set. The brand had flared at dawn and hadn't calmed. Something was closing the distance.

"There," I said, and pointed at the ferry crossing: a rope strung bank to bank, a flat barge tethered at the near side, and beyond it — the far bank, and willows, and cover.

"No," Wren said. "A ferry means a ferryman means questions. And it means they know where crossings are. If I were hunting us—"

"If you were hunting us, you'd be lost in a beet field. Get on the barge."

For once in their life, Wren Halvard did as they were told.

The crossing itself was simple — pull the rope hand over hand, the current shoving at the hull, spray coming up cold. Halfway across, Wren's brand screamed.

I knew it before I saw it. They went rigid mid-pull, both hands locking on the rope, and the black lines climbed past their elbow in a rush, and the sound they made was not human. Behind us, on the southern bank, I heard dogs.

Not barking. *Singing.* Long, rising, joyful cries, the sound hounds make when the quarry is close enough to taste, and beneath the singing, fainter, the jingle of harness and the drum of hooves. I turned. Three riders came down out of the willows we'd slept below not ten hours ago, and at their heels ran shapes that made my stomach drop — hounds built wrong, too tall at the shoulder, coats dark as wet slate, moving with the terrible economy of things that have never once failed at their work.

Oath-hounds. They didn't put their noses down. They didn't need to. They ran straight at the river, straight at *us*, as though reeling in on a line.

"Pull," I said.

"I can't—" Wren's arms were shaking. The brand had reached their shoulder. I could see it pulsing now, actually pulsing, broadcasting like a lighthouse, and every pulse dragged a grunt of pain out of them and sang our location to the far shore. "Freya, the rope—"

"Forget the rope." I dropped to my knees on the swaying deck, took their face in both hands, and pressed my palms — one to their brow, one flat over the brand where it beat against their collarbone — and *held*.

Holding is not the word. It was more like listening, but with my hands. The broken vow had a texture, a ragged shrieking edge all the way around, and I could feel where it tore, and I could not mend it, but I could — I pressed down on the torn edges the way you'd press a split seam flat, and I hummed, without deciding to, a low single note, and the note was the same note the dead used when they said their names to me at night, and the brand went

quiet

like a door easing shut, like a ledger closing, and Wren sagged into me so completely I nearly went over the side.

The barge drifted. The current took it sideways. On the south bank the lead hound hit the waterline and stopped as though it had struck glass, throwing spray, singing and singing — and here was the horror of it, the thing I still dream about: it didn't lose us. It stood at the edge of the water with its head high and its song going, and it *waited*, because it could still hear Wren, faintly, through my hands. Muffled was not silent. I had bought us minutes, not freedom.

"Wake up." I shook them. "Wren. The rope. *Now.*"

They surfaced like a drowning man. Together, with my left hand never leaving their arm — I tried letting go and the brand began to climb again within three heartbeats, so that was that — we hauled the barge along the rope, inch by screaming inch, the hounds' song stretching thinner and thinner behind us, and when the keel finally ground into the north-bank shallows we fell out of it into the reeds and lay there gasping among the winter-killed cattails while the riders on the far shore wheeled their horses, looking for a ford, and did not find one in time.

* * *

We walked most of the night. By the time we stopped, in a shepherd's ruined bothy with one wall and half a roof, Wren could barely stand, and it was entirely my fault, or entirely my doing — the distinction mattered to me more than I admitted. Keeping the brand quiet took something out of me. Not warmth, exactly. More like attention. Like holding a book open at the right page for hours; the weight was nothing and everything, and by nightfall my arms ached with a tiredness that lived somewhere behind my sternum.

But Wren could walk. Without the touch, they'd have been crawling. So we made a bargain without discussing it: my hand on their arm through the daylight hours, released at night, when nothing moved and the hounds lost the thread in the general dark. Two days of this. Three. We stopped talking about it as strange.

On the fourth night, in the bothy, with a fire the size of two cupped hands and rain coming down through the missing roof in threads, I noticed Wren watching me across the flames with an expression I finally had a shelf for.

"Guilt," I said. "You're feeling guilty."

"I'm feeling practical. Your hands are shaking. You couldn't thread a needle."

"My hands shake anyway. I'm a bookkeeper. We're born trembling."

"Freya."

"Wren."

They looked at the fire. Rain ticked on the one remaining roof beam. When they spoke again their voice had dropped into the register they used for truths — lower, slower, stripped of the oath-keeper's polish.

"You should leave me somewhere and go on. It's the arithmetic. I'm the one they're tracking. You'd be invisible by comparison — a woman with a coat and a bag of bones. I'm a bonfire on a hill. Every day you spend holding my vow shut is a day you're spending yourself to keep the loudest thing in the kingdom next to you." They said it evenly, the way they said everything, and that evenness was how I knew how much it cost. "The math says go."

"I've kept ledgers my whole life," I said. "Do you know what I've learned about math?"

They waited.

"Math doesn't know anything. Math will tell you the guildmaster's books balanced perfectly the day he sold me. Every column summed. Every entry correct. And it was the most obscene document I've ever held, because balancing isn't the same as being right." I shifted closer, took their arm, laid my palm over the brand — it stilled under my hand like a bird going calm — and made myself hold their eyes. "I'm not keeping you alive because the arithmetic favors it. I'm keeping you alive because you cut my wagon loose when every honest calculation said to leave me in it. Somebody has to keep *that* ledger, Wren Halvard, and apparently it's me."

The fire popped. Rain fell. Wren looked at my hand on their arm, and then, slowly, deliberately — the way they did everything, as though courage were a discipline like any other — they covered it with their own.

Their fingers were cold. Mine were colder. Between them the broken vow lay quiet as a sleeping animal, and I understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with ledgers, that I had crossed some line I hadn't known was drawn. Not when they freed me. Not on the river. Here, in a ruin, in the rain, because they had offered to be left behind, and I had refused, and neither of us had pretended it was strategy.

"Your hands," Wren said quietly. "If it starts costing too much—"

"You'll know. I'll complain. Loudly. I'm told I'm very good at it."

"You're good at everything," they said, and then went faintly, wonderfully, catastrophically stiff, the way a man does when he has said more than he planned, and I laughed — really laughed, the first time since the heralds came to Corvane — and the sound startled us both, and neither of us took the words back.

We slept in shifts, her back to stone, my hand on their arm. In my bag, the bones murmured their names into the dark, patient as ever. Toward morning, one of them murmured something else.

I woke with my hand still on the brand and the whisper still in my ear, and it was not a name, and it was not *Vhalen*, and it was not kind. It came from the oldest bone in my bag, a sliver of rib I'd carved on the second night and never heard speak before, and what it said, in a voice like ash settling, was:

*They have stopped following. They are going ahead. They know where you are bound.*

I sat up in the dark. Wren stirred beside me, and the brand pulsed once under my palm — not pain, I realized. Resonance. It knew, the way a compass knows, and it was pulling, gently, steadily, north and east, toward the hole in the map.

"Wren," I whispered. "Wake up. The hounds aren't behind us anymore."

They were awake instantly, of course; oath-keepers sleep like knives in drawers. "Where are they?"

"That's just it. They're not *anywhere* behind us." I swallowed. "They're waiting for us at the border. Someone told them where we're going."
