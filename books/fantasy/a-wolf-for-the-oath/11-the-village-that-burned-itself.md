## 11. The Village That Burned Itself

The hounds lost us at the river, but the river did not lose the smell of what we were.

Wren Halvard noticed it first as a wrongness in the air two miles downstream of the crossing — a thinness, like a room where a fire had been and someone had opened all the windows too late. Smoke without a source. The brand on their arm answered it with a low, grinding ache, the way an old wound answers weather.

"Someone's burning something," Freya said beside them. She had not spoken in an hour. Since the crossing she had been quieter than Wren liked, her hands tucked into her sleeves as though they'd learned something at the water's edge about being taken from her.

"Not wood," Wren said.

"No." Freya's jaw tightened. "Paper."

They followed the smell because there was nowhere else to go. The road bent north along the hills, away from the pyre-city and its columns of black smoke that rose on the horizon like the fingers of a drowning man, and the road bent toward a valley where the smoke was thin and gray and constant — the smoke of things burned slowly, carefully, by people who meant it.

The village appeared at dusk: Ashenmere, though no sign said so. Wren knew the name only because every sign that should have named it had been pried off its post, and the posts themselves chopped down and burned in a heap by the gate. The houses were stone and slate, well-kept, ordinary. The chimneys smoked. A dog barked twice and was silenced by a whistle. And everywhere — on doorposts, on lintels, on the milestone at the crossroads, on the face of the old well — names had been scraped away. Not crossed out. *Removed*, gouged down to raw wood and pale stone, so that the village looked as if a giant insect had fed on it, eating only the letters.

"They've burned themselves," Freya breathed.

Wren said nothing. They were looking at the gate, where a woman stood waiting for them with a scythe held the way a soldier holds a spear — competently, unhappily. Behind her, doors were closing one by one up the lane, soft as eyelids.

"That's far enough," the woman called.

"We're not Hunt," Wren said. It was true. It was also, they knew, the kind of truth that made things worse.

"You're worse than Hunt." The woman's eyes went to Wren's sleeve, to the edge of the brand visible above the wrapped cuff, black and branching like frost on a window. "You're an oath-keeper. You carry the crown's word in your skin. There's nothing you could say that I'd believe less."

"She's right," Freya murmured.

"I know she's right."

They stood in the road while the light failed, three figures in a conversation shaped like a siege. Then a second voice came from behind the gate — an old man's voice, cracked but steady — saying, "Let them in, Marta. Let *her* in, at least. If half of what the dead say is true, we owe her more than a closed gate."

*The dead say.* Wren felt Freya go still beside them, the particular stillness of a person listening to a frequency no one else can hear.

"What do the dead say?" Freya asked.

The old man came out from behind the gate. He was small and bent and clean-shaven, with ink stains on his fingers that Wren recognized with a lurch — guild stains, ledger stains, the marks of a life spent writing things down. He looked at Freya the way a starving man looks at bread.

"He says," the old man said, "that a bone-carver is coming west with a broken oath-keeper, and that when she arrives, the screaming stops."

* * *

They gave them soup and a barn and no welcome.

The barn was warm, which surprised Wren until they understood why: the loft was full of ash. Sacks of it, barrels of it, stacked like grain against a famine. Paper ash, vellum ash, the gray ghost-flakes of ledgers and registries and parish rolls, everything a village had ever written about itself, reduced and stored as if it might someday be needed as evidence of its own destruction.

"You kept it," Wren said. The old man — his name was Aldous, he'd offered it with the wariness of a man handing over a hostage — looked up from the lantern he was trimming.

"Burning is a ritual," he said. "Rituals want remains. We burned our names out of every record in three parishes, and then we kept the ash, because—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because it felt like murder otherwise. Because a thing ought to be mourned."

"What thing?"

"Our names, oath-keeper." Aldous's smile was terrible in the lantern light. "What else does a person have?"

Wren sat on a hay bale and did not answer, because the honest answer was in their arm, itching.

Below, in the barn's main floor, Freya sat with Marta's children — three of them, drawn to her the way children are drawn to anything that doesn't look at them with fear — and she was showing them how to hold a carving knife, how to find the grain in a sheep's knucklebone, how to listen before you cut. Wren watched her hands move and thought about the river, about the moment the hounds had come down the far bank and Freya had pressed her palm flat against the brand and the pain had gone quiet as a held breath, and how Wren had done something with that silence that they had not yet examined and did not intend to examine tonight.

Aldous followed their gaze.

"She hears them," he said quietly. "The dead. Ours have been quiet since midwinter, since the burnings started. Quiet like a held breath. And then last week, all at once, they began saying one thing over and over." He leaned close. "They say a name is coming. They say a ledger is coming. They say *wait for her*."

"And if she never comes?"

"Then we wait anyway." Aldous straightened. "That's what faith is, oath-keeper. Waiting without permission."

* * *

The council met at midnight in the church, because the church was the only building left with a roof big enough and a congregation small enough.

There were forty-one souls in Ashenmere. Wren counted them in the candlelight out of habit — guild habit, audit habit, the habit of a life spent reducing people to entries — and hated themselves for it. Forty-one. Nineteen of them gifted, by Aldous's telling: hearers, finders, a woman who could sweeten milk with a word, a boy whose shadow arrived a half-second before he did. Small gifts. Village gifts. The kind the Hunt registered on Tuesdays and burned on Fridays.

"We've survived eight months," Marta said. She stood at the front with the scythe still in her hands, as if the meeting might become an ambush at any moment. "Eight months, because there is nothing left to find. No record says this village exists. No registry lists these families. The tax rolls show a fire, a forfeiture, an abandonment. On paper, Ashenmere is a ruin. We are ghosts on purpose." Her eyes found Wren like a blade finding a gap in armor. "And then *you* walked through our gate."

"I'm not hunting you."

"You don't have to be." Marta's voice didn't rise; that was the frightening thing about it. "You're a compass needle, oath-keeper. The crown points you and you swing. Every vow you carry drags the Hunt after you like a chain through deep water. You broke your oath to save her — everyone on the roads knows the story now, it's told like a romance — and the whole realm knows what follows a broken oath-keeper. Hounds. Heralds. Pyres." She let the scythe's point rest on the flagstones. "So here is what we ask. Not much. One oath."

"Name it," Wren said, because some part of them already knew, and wanted the wound open and aired rather than guessed at in the dark.

"Forget us." Marta leaned forward. "Swear that you will never speak of Ashenmere. Not its location, not its people, not its gifts, not its survival. Swear that whatever torture or trickery is used on you, whatever cell they put you in, whatever they promise or threaten — the village stays unspoken. Swear silence, and walk out of here tomorrow, and take the hounds' noses with you, because a hound tracks a broken vow, and if you swear silence and keep it, there'll be nothing to smell."

The candles guttered. Somewhere in the dark of the church rafters, a pigeon shifted.

Wren looked at the brand.

It lay quiet under its wrappings — deceptively quiet, the way a river lies quiet over a weir. They had learned its grammar in three weeks of flight. It punished lies with fire. It punished broken promises with fire. But it did something worse than fire to oaths sworn and *kept against the self* — to vows that strangled the swearer from inside while technically remaining true. Wren had seen it once, years ago, on a customs officer who had sworn never to reveal a smuggling route and then spent ten years dying slowly of a silence that grew in him like a tumor, until he cut the route into his own chest with a fish knife just to stop the pressure. The oath had been kept. The man had not survived keeping it.

An oath of total silence, sworn by a person whose conscience was already at war with their crown — that wasn't protection. That was a slow knife handed over politely.

"If I swear it," Wren said, "and keep it, the brand will eat me alive. Slowly. Months, maybe. And when I'm found — mad, mute, or dead — the Hunt will come here asking what I swallowed. My silence won't hide you. It will *point* at you. Silence is the loudest thing there is, mistress Marta. Ask any interrogator."

"Then break it," someone shouted from the back, "and bring the fire down on us anyway—"

"I'm not finished." Wren stood. Their legs ached; the brand stirred, tasting the shape of what they were about to do, and they did it anyway, because honesty was the only coin they had left and they were tired of hoarding it. "I refuse. I won't swear it."

The word went through the church like a dropped glass. Marta's face closed. Aldous bowed his head as if he'd expected exactly this and had hoped, briefly and foolishly, otherwise.

"Understand me," Wren said, and their voice carried whether they willed it or not — the trained carrying of a courtroom, a swearing-in, a sentence read aloud. "I have broken one oath in my life, and it flayed me to the elbow, and I would break it again, because the oath was wrong. But I will not swear a *new* wrong oath to balance the old one. I will not put another chain on my own neck and call it safety. What I can offer you instead is this: I will tell no one what I have seen here — freely, without vow, because I choose it, and because a choice can't be tortured out of a person the way a vow can be broken out of them. Do you see the difference? A vow is a handle. Anyone who knows your oath exists owns one end of it. I am offering you a hand with no handle on it."

"A hand," Marta repeated, flat with contempt. "We're to trust your *character*, oath-keeper. Your good intentions. The intentions of a man who spent years serving the thing that's burning us."

"Yes," Wren said. "Exactly that. Because I know precisely how the machinery works from the inside, and I know what it cannot do, and one of the things it cannot do is compel a free silence. Vows are levers. Intentions are not. Hate me for refusing if it keeps you awake — I'd hate me too, in your place. But I will not lie to you to make you feel safe, and I will not bind myself to make you feel safe, because both of those are just ways of handing the Hunt a map."

Silence. Long, ugly, complete.

Then a woman near the back — the milk-sweetener, Wren realized, a broad-faced farmer with flour to her elbows — said quietly, "He's right, Marta. About the handle. My mother swore silence once, to protect my father. The brand took four years to kill her and the Hunt took eleven days to find us after. A vow is a bell rope. Everyone knows where it hangs."

Marta didn't turn. "And if he's captured? If they take him on the road tomorrow and put him in a room with hot irons? Free silences break too, mistress Ilse. Flesh breaks."

"Flesh breaks," Wren agreed. "But here is the last piece of honesty I have, and you may spend it however you like." They rolled back their cuff.

The brand caught the candlelight — black branches climbing past the elbow now, further than it had reached a week ago, fed by every mile of flight and every small mercy of conscience. The room flinched collectively. Even Marta stepped back a half-inch.

"This is what breaking one oath cost me. If they capture me and work me over, I will hold as long as flesh holds, and no longer, and I will not pretend otherwise, and neither should you. So don't build your survival on my mouth. Build it on what you've already built — the burned records, the erased signs, the ruin you made of yourselves on paper. That work is *good* work. It's better than any oath I could give you, because it doesn't depend on anyone's honor. It depends on nothing but ash." Wren pulled the cuff down. "I'm sorry. I know it isn't the answer you wanted. But the answer you wanted was a lie wearing my face, and I've spent enough of my life being the crown's face to know one when I'm asked to wear it."

Nobody applauded. Nobody softened. That, Wren noted, was the honest measure of the speech: it had changed nothing except the temperature of the room, which had dropped several degrees.

Aldous spoke into the silence, gently. "Show them the well before they sleep, Marta. Both of them. If we're to be hated, let's at least be understood."

* * *

The well stood in the village square, and it was full of bones.

Not human ones — Wren's stomach dropped and recovered in the same breath. Sheep bones, cattle bones, the knucklebones and jawbones of a hundred ordinary animals, and every one of them carved. Tiny letters covered them, dense as moss, and Freya stood at the well's lip with her hands hovering over the stones, trembling, reading without touching.

"It's a library," she whispered. "Wren. It's a *library*. They carved every name they erased into bone and threw them down here, where paper can't burn twice." Her breath fogged in the cold. "Do you understand what this is? This is what I've been doing on the road without knowing why. This is the ledger. This is the *real* ledger."

Down in the dark, faint as wind through a keyhole, the bones were murmuring. Wren couldn't hear the words. But they saw Freya's face as she listened, and it was the face of a woman standing in a doorway with home on the other side of it.

"The dead led us here," Freya said. "All those voices screaming Vhalen — they weren't only screaming. They were *organizing*. They've been organizing me, Wren. Every village, every hiding place, every burned registry — the dead know where the names went, because the dead go where the names go." She turned, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "Forty-one people in this village erased themselves to survive, and it worked, and it's monstrous, and it's *holy*, all at once, and nobody — not the crown, not the Hunt, not Seraphine Voss with all her pretty theology — nobody has thought of doing what these farmers did. Names in bone. Unburnable. Unburnable unless you burn the *people*."

"Marta will never forgive me," Wren said.

"No," Freya agreed. "She shouldn't. You were cruel and right, which is the worst combination there is." She took Wren's hand — the branded one, deliberately, pressing her thumb to the coldest branch of it, and the grinding ache dimmed to embers. "But look at me and hear this, because I only ever say it once: what you did in that church was the bravest ugly thing I've ever watched. You chose being hated over being trusted falsely. Most people can't tell the difference. You can."

Wren looked at their own hand in hers and thought: *this is the oath I actually keep.* Not sworn on any stone. Kept daily, hourly, in the space between her palm and their ruined arm.

Behind them, across the square, a door opened. Marta stood in it with a lantern, and her face had not softened, and would not — but she jerked her chin toward the east road and said, loudly enough to wake the street:

"Rider coming. Fast. Alone. Wearing guild colors." Her eyes fixed on Freya, and for the first time there was something other than hatred in them — recognition, dread, kinship. "Bone-carver. Whatever your dead have been arranging — I think it just sent for you."

Far off down the east road, a single horse was already raising dust under the moon, and in Wren's arm, the brand woke and began, very softly, to sing.
