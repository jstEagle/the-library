## 12. Ledger-Keeping for the Fugitive Dead

The first name I carved was a child's.

I did not choose it that way. I had gathered what bones the road had given us — a knuckle of pig from an abandoned farmstead, a deer's rib snapped clean by wolves, the long pale shank of something I could not identify and did not ask about — and I laid them out on the floor of the mill loft where we'd taken shelter, and I waited for the voices to sort themselves into some kind of order. They did not. Forty-one dead from the pyre at Marrow's Ford, all screaming at once, all screaming the same word, *Vhalen*, *Vhalen*, until my skull felt like a bell being rung by someone who hated bells.

But one voice was smaller than the others. One voice kept saying a name instead — *Tamsin, Tamsin, Tamsin* — the way a child says her own name when she is lost in a market and has been told, over and over, that this is what you do, say your name, say your name until someone finds you.

So I found her.

I took the deer's rib because it was the smoothest, and I held it against my ear like a shell, and I listened until her voice came through alone, thin as wire, and then I set the point of Wren's knife against the bone and wrote her name in letters small enough to fit three times over.

*Tamsin Ostrewe. Six years old. Marrow's Ford.*

The moment the last stroke went in, she stopped screaming.

I don't know how to describe what that silence was like except to say it was the loudest thing I'd ever heard. Forty voices still shrieking, and one gone quiet, and the quiet spread outward from that little rib like oil on water, and the other voices turned — I felt them turn, the way you feel a room turn to look when someone important enters — and looked at what I had done.

Then, one by one, they began to give me their names.

* * *

Wren found me at dawn, still sitting cross-legged among the bones, my fingers split and stained with bone-dust, thirty-one names carved where forty-one screams had been. The remaining ten were patient now. That frightened me more than the screaming had. Screaming I understood. Patience was something else — patience meant they had decided I would come to them eventually, and they were willing to wait.

"You haven't slept," Wren said from the ladder. They stood there with their hood back and their brand arm cradled against their chest like an injured bird, and the light came up gold behind them through the broken slats of the mill wall, and for a moment they looked almost gentle, which I knew better than to trust.

"Neither have you."

"I don't sleep well when the oath is angry." They climbed up and sat across from me, careful not to disturb the arrangement of bones, which I noticed they had noticed was an arrangement. "What is this?"

"A ledger."

I said it before I knew I was going to say it, and once it was out I could feel how true it was, how it settled into place like a key into a lock I'd been carrying my whole life without knowing its door. I picked up the rib with Tamsin's name and held it out.

"The Hunt burns them," I said. "The pyres unmake them — I told you, you heard them, they're not passing on, they're being *shredded*, fed into something under Vhalen's border like coin into a miser's purse. Everything a person was goes into the fire and comes out as fuel. But a name—" I turned the rib in the light so Wren could read the small carved letters. "A name written in bone can't be burned. Bone remembers. That's the whole root of my gift, Wren, that's what it *is* — bone keeps what flesh lets go of. So if I write them down—"

"They stay themselves." Wren took the rib with both hands, the way you take something holy or breakable, and I watched their thumb trace the letters and watched their face do something complicated. "You're keeping count."

"I'm keeping *them*." My voice cracked and I hated it and kept going anyway. "There's a difference. A ledger isn't a memorial. A ledger is a claim. It says: these people existed, they are owed, and someone will pay."

Wren looked at me for a long time. The brand on their forearm had gone quiet — I could tell because they weren't flinching — and later I would understand why, though I didn't then: the oath punished lies and disobedience, but it had never once punished honesty, and everything I was saying was the most honest thing either of us had spoken aloud since Corvane.

"My mother used to say," Wren said finally, "that a country only dies when no one can say its name anymore." They set the rib down gently among its fellows. "She was talking about Vhalen. She didn't know she was talking about Vhalen. She wasn't allowed to know."

The ten remaining voices stirred at the word, soft as wind through wheat, and I put my palm flat on the mill floor and let them speak, and they spoke names, one after another, unhurried, each one waiting its turn like petitioners at a guild counter.

"Wren," I said. "Get me more bones."

* * *

We became, in those weeks on the run, a strange kind of guild of two.

Wren learned the roads the way they had once learned oaths — completely, obsessively, with a soldier's hunger for structure. They mapped the Hunt's patrol patterns from ash and rumor: which villages had been visited, which were next, where the pyre-carts rolled and how fast. And I learned to listen in a way I had never let myself listen before, because hiding a gift teaches you only how to muffle it, and I was done muffling.

The work had a rhythm. In the mornings we moved — always east, always toward the hole in the map, though neither of us said so yet; the dead pulled and we followed, and that was reason enough. At midday we stopped wherever bones could be found, which was everywhere, because the land itself seemed to be offering them up: cattle skulls in ditches, old graveyards whose fences the Hunt had already kicked flat, a martyrs' ossuary beneath a chapel where the priest, a shaking man with soot on his cuffs, pressed a box of relics into my hands and asked nothing except whether his brother's name might be among the ones I kept.

At night I carved.

I want to be honest about what the carving cost, because the songs — if anyone ever sings songs about this, which I doubt, since we won — should not make it pretty. Every name I took into a bone, I took also into myself. Not permanently, not the way it would end, but for the hours of the working, I *was* each person: Tamsin's terror of the dark of the wagon, the cooper Halden's fury that he had hidden his gift for fifty years and died for it in one afternoon, the twins Maret and Sefa who had held hands at the stake and whose voices, even carved, even settled, still arrived together or not at all. I gave them my hours and my warmth and sometimes, when the working ran long past midnight, I gave them blood, because a knife slips when the hand holding it has forgotten it belongs to a living woman.

Wren sat with me every night. They never offered to carve — the knife-work wanted my gift and they hadn't one — but they kept the fire low and fed, kept water at my elbow, and when my hands shook too badly to finish a letter they wrapped theirs around mine and steadied them, and we did not talk about that, and I think we both counted on not talking about it the way misers count on coin.

And the dead changed.

That was the thing I could not have predicted, the thing I still cannot fully explain. When I had met them in the wagons they had been a mob — panic given voices, grief with teeth. But a ledger does something to chaos. Ask any guild-keeper. Chaos is just information that hasn't been entered yet. And as the ledger grew — forty-one names, then sixty-three after the pyre at Dunlow Reach, then ninety, then a hundred and twelve after the terrible week at Saltmarsh where they had burned a whole river-village and floated the ashes downstream — the dead began to organize around it.

They formed lines. I mean that literally: at night, when I opened myself to listen, they queued. They waited their turns. They corrected each other's spellings — a dead schoolmistress named Ivette Corrigan took it upon herself to verify every name twice, and woe to me if I transposed a letter, because she would rap the inside of my skull like a tutor with a ruler until I fixed it. They brought me *other* names, names of the dead from years past, the quietly dead, the ones who had died ordinary deaths before the Hunt ever came, because — and this realization arrived slowly and then all at once, cold as well-water — because the erased country needed its people catalogued too. Vhalen's waiting millions had been nameless for two hundred years. Someone had to start somewhere.

"You've become a census," Wren said one night, watching me carve with the focused blankness I'd once seen on the face of a guild appraiser pricing a haunted mirror.

"I've become a guild of one," I said. "Impossible trades only. Payment deferred."

"What's the trade?"

"I keep their names. They keep me sane." I blew bone-dust off the finished line. "It's the fairest contract I've ever written."

* * *

The trouble with purpose, I learned, is that it makes you visible.

We had been careful. We traveled as a tinker and his mute sister — Wren's idea, the muteness mine, since a woman who talks to herself in forty voices draws fewer questions if she supposedly can't draw any at all. We avoided the main roads. We buried our fire-coals in river mud. But you cannot carve ninety names into bone without producing bone-shavings, and you cannot carry a ledger of the condemned in a satchel without carrying the smell of it, and there are things in this world that hunt by smell the way Wren's former colleagues hunted by vow.

The hounds found us outside a village called Emberlane, on a grey morning when frost furred every fencepost and the dead in my satchel had gone unusually still, the way birds go still before weather.

They came down the road at a trot, six of them, and they were wrong in the way the snow near the border was wrong — too large, too silent, coats like wet slate, and no eyes worth the name, just a pale shine where eyes should be. They did not cast about for scent. They came straight up the road to the exact fencepost where Wren happened to be standing, and the lead hound put its nose against Wren's branded arm and breathed in, long and slow, like a customs officer reading a suspicious seal.

"It smells the broken vow," Wren said. Their voice was very level. Their hand was not; it had found the knife at their belt. "Not me. The vow. I'm just where the vow lives."

"Can it smell me?"

"It doesn't need to." The lead hound lifted its head and looked — there was no better word — *through* me, at the satchel. "You're carrying a hundred and twelve reasons to burn. You're louder than a bonfire."

What happened next happened fast, and I remember it in pieces: Wren shouting *run*, the fence rail splintering under the lead hound's shoulder, the river at the bottom of the field running high with melt, my boots filling with ice-water, the satchel banging my spine like a second heartbeat. The hounds did not tire. Hounds that smell promises do not tire, because a promise never gets further away, it only gets *unkept*. Wren turned at the far bank to hold the crossing — I saw them stand in the shallows with their brand arm raised and their whole body lit along the fracture-lines of their broken oath, blazing faintly blue-white like a struck flint — and the hounds stopped.

All six of them stopped, at the waterline, and would not enter.

Later, drying by a peat-fire in the hills above Emberlane, Wren showed me why. The brand had changed. Where it had been a ragged burn crawling up their forearm, it was now — settled, cooled, defined — a shape. A wolf's head, stylized, ancient, the kind of mark you see on boundary stones and door-lintels in the oldest parts of the realm.

"That's not a crown sigil," I said.

"No," Wren said. "It's older than the crown." They turned their arm in the firelight, and their expression was the expression of a man reading his own death warrant and finding a love letter underneath. "Freya. I think the oath is choosing sides."

The dead in my satchel murmured, all together, softly, and for the first time since Marrow's Ford they did not sound like victims.

They sounded like a congregation.

* * *

Emberlane itself nearly undid me, and I record it here because a ledger-keeper who records only victories is a liar with good penmanship.

The village had heard the hounds. The village had seen the chase. And when we limped up its single street at dusk, the doors did not open. Faces watched us from shutters — faces I recognized, because I had begun to recognize the look: the tight, exhausted watchfulness of people who had something to hide and knew the hiding was failing. Gifted folk. A whole village of them, or near enough, tucked into the hills where the maps went vague.

An old woman finally came out, arms crossed, iron-grey braid to her waist. She looked at Wren's brand, at my satchel, at my bone-stained hands, and her face did the arithmetic and did not like the sum.

"You're the ones the hounds follow," she said. "You're bringing the Hunt down on every house between here and the border."

"We're trying to reach the border before the Hunt does," Wren said.

"Everyone says that." She spat. "Everyone who's still moving is running *somewhere*, and every road runs through somebody else's life. You want shelter? Swear you'll say nothing of this village. Swear it on whatever's left of your oath. Swear you'll forget Emberlane exists."

And Wren — I watched this happen, I stood close enough to feel the heat of it — Wren opened their mouth to swear, because it was kind, because it was the price of a roof, because lying had gotten easier for both of us than either of us wanted to admit — and the brand *seized* them. I saw their knees buckle. I saw the wolf-head flare white and the smell of scorched skin and Wren go down on one knee in the frozen mud with their jaw locked against a scream, because the broken oath would not permit even a merciful lie to pass their lips anymore. It had become, in its ruined way, incorruptible.

"I can't," Wren gasped. "I can't swear that. I'd remember you. I'd write you in my own map. I can't promise to forget."

The old woman looked down at them with something worse than hatred — with contempt, the contempt of the practical for the principled — and shut her door, and we slept in a hay-barn at the village edge with the rats and the frost, and I lay awake listening to a hundred and twelve settled names and one unsettled breathing beside me, and I thought: *this is what it costs to be honest. This is the exchange rate.* The guildmaster of Corvane had sold me with a smile and slept soundly. Wren couldn't buy a barn with the truth and shivered all night.

I chose, again, in the dark. I chose the shivering.

* * *

On the third night after Emberlane, the dead gave me a warning instead of a name.

It came during the carving — I was working the femur of an ox, entering the Saltmarsh dead, sixty-one souls, and I was deep in the rhythm of it, knife whispering, voices queuing — when the queue went silent. All of it. The way a forest goes silent.

Then a single voice, at the front of the line, a voice I knew: Ivette Corrigan, the schoolmistress, who had appointed herself verifier of all my entries and tolerated no errors.

*"Stop writing,"* she said. *"Listen. There is a rider on the north road. She carries paper sealed in green wax, and she is asking, at every house, for a fugitive oath-keeper and a woman who carves. She is not threatening anyone. She is being courteous. Be more afraid of the courtesy than of the hounds."*

My knife stopped. Outside, the peat-fire hissed. Wren looked up from sharpening their second blade and read my face the way they had learned to, quickly and completely.

"What is it?"

"She's coming," I said. "Someone's coming. A rider, asking after us by name — politely. Sealed papers." I swallowed. "Green wax."

Wren went very still. Then, slowly, they set down the whetstone, and I watched them arrive at the same place I had already arrived, the place where the dead had been pointing for weeks without words: the fact that the Hunt had not simply *lost* us. The fact that the hounds had stopped at the water. The fact that a broken oath-blaze could scatter six slate-grey horrors and leave us alive and free and *moving eastward, unimpeded, exactly where we wanted to go*.

"Hunts don't lose people," Wren said quietly. "Hunts are herded. Something is driving us." They looked at the wolf-head brand glowing faintly on their arm, then at the satchel of bones between us, a hundred and twelve lives in carved ivory, calm and ordered and waiting. "Or inviting us."

Far off to the north, too far to see, too close to ignore, a single horseman's bell rang once on the night road — the clear, patient, courteous bell of someone carrying an official invitation — and every name in my ledger turned, all together, toward the sound, and listened.
