## 13. The Huntmaster's Courtesy

The letter came by hawk, which was the first wrong thing. The Hunt did not use hawks; it used men, and men could be questioned, and questions left trails. A hawk left nothing but a shadow crossing the snow.

Wren Halvard read it three times in the lee of a dead orchard's wall, Freya watching from ten paces off with her arms crossed against the cold and her bone charm hidden inside her collar, as if fabric could muffle what had never been sound.

*To Oath-Keeper Wren Halvard, formerly of the Crown's Vigil, currently in violation of Article One. You are invited to parley. Alone. Unharmed. Under courtesy of the Huntmaster's own word. Come to the chapel at Millbrook Cross by moonrise, or do not, and we will find you anyway — but one of those options involves tea.*

No signature. None needed. The seal pressed into the wax was a wolf's skull crowned with thorns, and beneath it, in letters small enough to be missed by anyone who hadn't spent nine years learning to read the Hunt's paperwork: *S.V.*

Seraphine Voss.

Wren burned the letter in the stub of a candle and told Freya nothing except that they were moving north before dawn.

Freya, of course, knew anyway. She always knew. Not through any gift — she'd told Wren once that hearing the dead was nothing like knowing the living; the dead were honest, which made them easy — but through the ordinary, unbearable attentiveness of a woman who had spent her life reading ledgers. Wren's face was a ledger to her now. Wren had stopped being able to hide entries in it.

"You're going," she said, not a question, while she wrapped her feet in strips of blanket because her boots had died two rivers back.

"I'm going."

"Alone."

"Alone."

"And if it's a trap?"

"It's a trap." Wren checked the knife at their hip, then the second knife, then decided both were theater. Against the Hunt, knives were theater. "But it's a courteous trap, and courteous traps have purposes, and I want to know what the purpose is before it closes."

Freya stood. She was small, and cold-bitten, and had the eyes of someone who had heard too many people die and refused to be impressed by the threat of one more. "You know what I think? I think you want to go back. Some part of you. Some part that still believes there's a version of this where you walk back into their tent and they say *we understand, Halvard, it was a moment of madness, here is your oath renewed.*"

The words went in like a splinter driven under a nail. Wren didn't flinch, because flinching would have confirmed it.

"The brand says otherwise," Wren said, and held up their arm.

The broken vow had climbed from wrist to elbow now, a black filigree of burned lines that ached when the Hunt drew near and sang when Wren lied. It had been quiet for two days. That quiet frightened Wren more than pain would have. Pain meant the oath still noticed them. Quiet might mean it had finished deciding what they were.

Freya looked at the arm for a long moment. Then she reached out and laid two fingers on the worst of the scarring, and the ache dimmed, the way it always dimmed, and neither of them said anything about that either. There was a whole ledger of things neither of them said anything about.

"Millbrook Cross," she said. "I'll be within earshot. If you're not out by moonset, I'm coming in after you, and I'll bring every name in my ledger with me, and Voss can explain to forty screaming dead why her tea is cold."

"That's the worst rescue plan I've ever heard."

"I'm a ledger-keeper, not a soldier. It's supposed to be terrible. Terrible plans are honest." She turned away, then paused. "Wear the grey cloak. If you're going to sit across from the woman hunting us, at least let her look at something that isn't hers anymore."

* * *

The chapel at Millbrook Cross had been a chapel the way a corpse had been a person. Its roof had fallen inward years ago and been left where it lay, so that the altar stood open to the sky like a man who'd given up explaining himself. Snow came down through the broken rafters and settled on the altar in a thin white shroud, and no one had swept it, because everyone who would have swept it was dead or fled or registered.

Voss was already there when Wren arrived, sitting on an overturned pew with a folding table beside her and, gods help her, an actual kettle steaming over a small spirit flame. She poured two cups without looking up.

"Halvard. Sit. The chamomile is from Corvane. I confiscated it from a smuggler who was trading in grief-bottles, which tells you everything about how the war on contraband is going."

Wren stayed standing. "Huntmaster."

"Sit down, Wren." She used the first name deliberately, the way you'd use a blade you'd kept oiled for years. "You've been running for eleven days. Your boots are ruined. Your arm is dying by inches. Sit down and drink something warm before you make your next catastrophic decision."

And that was the horror of Seraphine Voss, Wren thought, finally lowering onto the opposite pew: not the pyres, not the hounds, not the register with its neat columns of names awaiting fire. The horror was that she was *kind*. Kind the way winter was kind — thorough, patient, absolutely certain of its own necessity. She was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the lined face of a woman who had done arithmetic all her life and never once liked the total. Her hands were scarred at the fingertips, old burns, decades old.

She pushed a cup across. Wren didn't take it.

"The pardon," Wren said.

"Straight to business. Good. I'd have lost respect for you otherwise." She produced a folded document from her coat and set it on the pew between them, weighted with a stone from the chapel floor. "Full restoration. Oath renewed under crown authority, brand sealed, rank of Vigil restored with seniority. The Delacroix woman delivered alive to the registry at Corvane, and your part in her escape recorded as coercion — she bewitched you, we'll say. Grief-gifts can do that. No one will question it. You'd be clean, Wren. Completely clean. By spring you could be auditing guildhouses again instead of freezing in ditches."

Wren looked at the document without touching it. "And if I refuse?"

"Then in about six days the hounds run you down somewhere in the high passes, and I burn the Delacroix woman a week later, and none of this conversation will have mattered, except to me, privately, for about an hour." She sipped her tea. "I'm offering you the only mercy in this entire machinery, Halvard. Don't insult it by pretending there's a better one hiding behind the altar."

"There isn't a better one," Wren said quietly. "There's just the true one. And you haven't told me that yet."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — not surprise; she was too good for surprise — but the recalculation of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd hoped was wrong.

"No," she agreed. "I haven't."

"So tell me. Because I've seen the pyres, Huntmaster. I stood on a ridge and watched eleven people burn, and I've been listening to their voices ever since — don't look at me like that, the brand broke something loose in me and now I hear them too, and they all scream the same word. *Vhalen.* Eleven strangers, none of them born there, none of them old enough to remember it, and they scream the name of a country that isn't on any map. So either your register is full of liars, or the burning is doing something, and I want to know what."

For a long moment the only sound was snow ticking down through the broken roof, landing softly on the altar's white shroud.

Then Voss set down her cup, and folded her hands, and gave Wren the truth with the same courtesy she gave everything.

"The pyres are fuel," she said.

The words landed in the chapel and sat there.

"Every gift we burn feeds the seal," she went on, in the tone of a lecturer who has given this explanation before and hated it every time. "A gifted person's death doesn't end their gift. It releases it. Unbound power seeks structure, and there is exactly one structure in this world built to receive it. Do you know what a country looks like when it dies, Wren? Not a battle-death. A real one. Vhalen didn't fall. It was *closed*. Sealed behind a border-stitch woven from oaths, and the stitch needs feeding, or it frays, and if it frays—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Started again, softer. "If it frays, everything on the other side comes through. And everything on the other side has been waiting thirty years, and it is not grateful."

"The dead," Wren said. "The drowned."

"The waiting," Voss corrected. "That's the word the old texts use, and it's worse. Dead things stay dead. Waiting things have intentions." She leaned forward. "Your Delacroix hears them. You've heard them yourself now, so you know I'm not lying — check me, use whatever the brand left in your ears. Am I lying?"

Wren listened. The brand was silent. That silence was its own answer, and Voss saw Wren understand it, and something almost like pity crossed her face.

"Why tell me this?" Wren asked. "You could have just shown me the pardon and the knife."

"Because you were the best oath-keeper I ever trained, and because you deserve to know what your desertion actually means. You think you freed a prisoner. You didn't. You pulled a load-bearing brick out of a wall holding back a flood." She rose, walked to the altar, and looked up through the broken roof at the falling snow. "Thirty years ago I stood on the border-stitch myself. I was twenty-two. I helped weave it. Do you know what we fed it, the first time? We fed it Vhalen itself. Every gifted soul in the country, all at once, willingly — because the alternative was watching the door open and the waiting come through. My mother was on that side of the stitch when it closed. I wove it shut with her on the wrong side, and I have fed it faithfully ever since, and I will feed it until I die, and I will sleep well every night, because the arithmetic is simple and I am not afraid of simple arithmetic."

"What's on the other side?" Wren asked. "What's actually waiting?"

Voss was quiet long enough that Wren thought she wouldn't answer.

"I don't know," she said finally. "That's the truth, and it should terrify you more than any lie. Thirty years of feeding, and the texts disagree, and the ones who wrote them are ash. Maybe it's the drowned dead, angry and mindless. Maybe it's something older that wore Vhalen like a coat. Maybe it's just a country that refuses to accept it ended." She turned. "But I know what's on *this* side if it opens. Every village you passed through getting here. Every child in them. And I know that the Delacroix woman, with her ledger and her kindness and her names, is walking toward that border intending to do exactly what the stitch was built to prevent."

"She wants to give the dead their names back."

"She wants to *open the door*, Wren. Call it whatever gentler thing helps her sleep." Voss came back and stood over the pardon document, tapping it once with a scarred finger. "So. The offer stands. Deliver her, and be clean. Refuse, and be ash — but ash with knowledge, which I suppose is its own consolation prize." She picked up her cup again, and her voice softened into something dangerously close to genuine. "One more thing, since I owe you honesty. When you catch her — *if* you catch her — ask her what her ledger costs her. Ask her what each name takes. I've read the reports. The dead calm when she writes them, yes. But she's thinner every week, and colder, and there's frost in her breath on warm days. She is spending herself, Halvard. She thinks she's keeping accounts. She's spending herself, and the dead are letting her, because the dead are patient, and patience is the most predatory virtue there is."

Wren stood. The pardon lay on the pew between them, white and official and worth less than the paper it was written on, because paper couldn't hold what Wren had already chosen on a night road beside a burning wagon.

"Keep your pardon," Wren said. "And keep your arithmetic. I've seen what it adds up to. It adds up to children on pyres."

"It adds up to *everyone else*," Voss said, and for one unguarded instant the courtesy cracked and Wren saw underneath: not cruelty, never cruelty, just exhaustion so vast it had fossilized into certainty. "That's the whole of it, Halvard. That's the entire argument. Someone pays. I chose who, thirty years ago, and I have carried that choice every day since, and I would carry it again, and I will burn every last gifted soul in this realm before I let that door open. Now go. Run. Be found in six days. And when you stand at the border and see what's waiting — if you live that long — I want you to remember that I offered you tea."

Wren walked out of the chapel into the falling snow, and did not take the pardon, and did not look back.

* * *

Freya was waiting in the tree line, half-hidden behind a blackened apple tree, and she read Wren's face the way she read everything — completely, immediately, and without mercy.

"They're not just killing us," Wren said, before she could ask. "They never were. The pyres feed a seal. On Vhalen. The whole Hunt is a feeding mechanism, Freya. Thirty years of burnings, all of it poured into a border-stitch to keep the dead country shut. And she says what's on the other side isn't dead. It's *waiting*."

Freya didn't speak. The wind moved through the bare branches, and somewhere far off a hound gave voice — one long note, patient, unhurried, the sound of something that knew exactly where it was going and saw no reason to arrive quickly.

"And there's more," Wren said. "She told me to ask you what the ledger costs you. The frost in your breath. She's been reading reports." Wren stepped closer, took Freya's hands — they were ice, they were always ice now — and held them hard. "Is it true? What does each name take?"

Freya looked down at their hands. When she answered, her voice was very quiet, and very steady, and it was the steadiness that frightened Wren most of all.

"It takes warmth," she said. "A little. Each time. I assumed it was the price of the work." She lifted her eyes. "But Wren — if the pyres feed the seal, then what do you think my ledger is feeding?"

The hound bayed again, closer now.

And in Wren's ears, faint and rising, the dead began to sing — not screaming this time, not the raw animal terror of the ridge, but something structured, something rehearsed, dozens of voices moving together toward a single word, and the word was not *Vhalen* this time.

It was *north*.
