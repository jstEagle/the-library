## 15. What Wren Swore and to Whom

The snow fell upward for three days before Wren could no longer pretend it was weather.

They made camp in the shell of a shepherd's hut a mile from where the maps went wrong — a mile from the hole in the world. The hut's roof had half fallen in, and through the gap the inverted snow rose past them in slow spirals, flakes climbing like sparks off a fire nobody had lit, until they vanished into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Freya sat near the dead hearth with her ledger across her knees, carving names into bone with the small sure motions of a woman doing arithmetic, and the bones murmured around her like a room full of sleeping people.

Wren could not sleep. That was nothing new; the brand saw to that, a dull coal of pain banked under the skin of their forearm, flaring whenever they thought too long about the oath they'd broken. But tonight the pain was quieter than usual, and that frightened them more. Out here, at the edge of the erased country, the vow felt thinner. Stretched. Like rope over a pulley bearing more weight than it was made for.

Or like something listening to it.

"You keep looking north," Freya said, without lifting her head from her work. "You've been looking north since the river."

"The border's north."

"I know what's north." She set down her knife and looked up, and in the failing light her eyes were the pale gray of bone itself. "I also know what you look like when you're lying to me, Wren Halvard. You have a face for it. It's very good. It fooled me for weeks." She turned the ledger toward them. "But I've spent my life reading what people don't write down. And lately the dead keep asking about you."

Wren's stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a body rolling in shallow water. "Asking what?"

"Whether you're coming home."

* * *

They ate what remained of the smoked fish from the burned village, and neither of them spoke while they did it, because there is no polite way to say *I think you are something other than what you told me you were*, and both of them were fluent now in each other's silences.

It was Freya who broke first, because she always did. She had never once let a silence stand when a question could be driven into it like a nail.

"Tell me about your oath," she said. "Not the words. I've heard the words — they come out of your mouth when the brand wants them to. Tell me who gave them to you. Tell me how old you were."

"Why does it matter?"

"Because oaths are like scars," Freya said. "They're all old wounds wearing uniforms. Somebody cut you, Wren. Before the crown ever put a brand on you. I want to know who held the knife."

Wren looked at the fire. There wasn't much of one — a fistful of driftwood and two chair legs from the ruined hut — but it was enough to watch instead of a person. They had rehearsed this confession, they realized, though never on purpose. They had rehearsed it every night of their life in the space between waking and sleep, in the way children rehearse the thing they are most afraid to say out loud: over and over, wordlessly, until the rehearsal wore smooth and the saying stayed impossible.

"I was born in Vhalen," they said.

The bones stopped murmuring.

It was the strangest thing Wren had ever heard — dozens of small voices, all at once, going silent as if a hand had been laid flat over an entire congregation. Freya didn't move. Her breath didn't even change. Only the bones reacted, and then only briefly, before resuming their low tide-murmur, though the sound of it had changed. Warmer, somehow. Attentive.

"My mother carried me across the border-stitch when I was four," Wren said, to the fire, because the fire couldn't die of it. "There were forty-some families in our column. I remember the count because I counted them the way children count things — lampposts, steps, sheep. Forty-one families walking out of a country that was drowning behind us. Not with water." They shook their head. "That's the part nobody understands. Vhalen didn't drown in water. It drowned in *law*. The crown wrote it off the map — decreed that no such country existed, that its borders had always been crown territory, that its name had never been spoken. And when a country is unwritten hard enough, by enough oaths, by enough sworn throats—" they gestured vaguely upward, at the snow still rising past the broken roof, "—it goes somewhere. Or stops going anywhere. The geographers call it annexation. The priests call it mercy. What it actually was, was erasure with paperwork."

"And your mother," Freya said quietly. "She walked out with you."

"She walked out with me, and she was allowed to forget." Wren heard their own voice go flat, the way it did when the alternative was not being flat. "That was the arrangement. The crown's men met the columns at the stitch — oath-keepers, the first generation of them, before they started calling it a Hunt. Every refugee child was offered a choice, except we weren't old enough to choose, so the choice was made for us and called a gift. Swear to the crown, and be free. Be *new*. New name, new papers, new memories where it mattered. The oath would hold the forgetting in place like mortar holds a wall." They turned their arm over, and even in the dimness the brand showed dark against the skin, a coil of script that never quite looked the same shape twice. "My mother swore for me because I was four and couldn't pronounce the words. Then she swore her own, and forgot me."

Freya's knife-hand had gone still on the ledger's cover. "Forgot you."

"There was a woman in the resettlement house in Corvane," Wren said. "Kind. She used to give the children honey-bread on saints' days. When I was seven I worked out that she had my mother's laugh. Exactly. The same break in it, at the same place, like a plate with the same chip. I followed her home. I stood in her doorway and said, *you sound like my mama.* And she looked at me the way you look at a stranger's dog that follows you — kindly, and with absolutely nothing behind it. She didn't know me. She had never known me. There was no ache where I should have been, because the ache had been sworn away." They smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, and let it be. "So I did the only reasonable thing a seven-year-old can do. I applied to the Oathkeepers' Academy. Twice a year, every year, until they took me at eleven."

"Why?" Freya's voice had gone very soft. Not gentle — Freya was never gentle when it mattered; gentleness would have been a lie between them — but soft, the way you lower your voice in a room where someone is sleeping. "Why would you want to serve the thing that took her from you?"

"Because the oath-keepers were the ones who remembered." Wren spread their hands. "Think about it. Everyone else got to forget — it was the mercy, it was the whole architecture of the thing, a kingdom built on amnesia and called peace. Except somebody has to maintain the forgetting. Somebody has to renew the oaths, patrol the stitch, keep the ledgers of what was unwritten. Those people aren't allowed to forget. We were the archive of a crime, Freya. I joined the archive because it was the only place in the world where anyone still knew my mother's name." They laughed, short and terrible. "Except they redacted me too. My own file. I've read it. *Refugee minor, provenance undisclosed.* Even the record of my erasure was erased. I spent twenty years inside the only institution that remembered Vhalen, hunting for one line of text about myself, and the line wasn't there."

Outside, the upward snow made its faint sound, which was no sound at all, or the memory of one — flakes passing close to the roof-gap and whispering against each other like pages being turned by someone patient.

"The pyres," Freya said at last. Each word came out placed carefully, like coins counted onto a table. "On the ridge. When we watched the first burning. You held my shoulder so I wouldn't run at them, and you were shaking, and I thought it was rage. I've been thinking for weeks it was rage."

"It was the brand." Wren's mouth was dry. "No. Both. The oath punishes sympathy for the condemned — it's designed to, that's the whole point of it, the crown doesn't trust a sword that might decide it's on the wrong side of the neck. Every time I felt something for the people in those wagons, the vow squeezed. And I thought — I told myself — it's just the design. Duty grinding against conscience, the ordinary machinery." They finally made themselves look at her. "But it wasn't ordinary, was it. Because the people in those wagons weren't strangers. They were the gift I wasn't allowed to refuse. They were the ones who got taken *after* me, when the crown stopped pretending the Hunt was anything but a harvest. Every gifted soul they've burned since the proclamation — every single one — I have been standing there feeling homesick for people I've never met, and I thought that was weakness. Conscience. It isn't. It's *kinship*. The oath has been flaying me for weeks for loving my own countrymen, and I didn't even know they were mine."

The bones began to murmur again, and this time Wren could almost pick out shapes in the sound — not words, not yet, but the cadence of words, voices arranging themselves the way a crowd arranges itself when it realizes someone at the front is about to speak.

Freya closed the ledger. She crossed the ruined room and sat down beside them, close, her shoulder against theirs, and did not say anything for a long moment, which was its own kind of eloquence. When she finally spoke, she spoke the way she read names aloud: precisely, giving each syllable its full weight.

"Say her name."

"I don't know it."

"Then describe her. Everything. The laugh, the house, the smell of her hands. All of it. Now, while I'm touching you." Freya took Wren's branded forearm in both her hands, and the coal under the skin quieted to warmth, and Wren understood what she was doing — building a channel, opening herself as a conduit, the way she had on the riverbank when the hounds came. "Because if the dead of Vhalen are on the other side of that border, and I think they are, then somewhere in that waiting crowd is a woman with your laugh's missing half. And when we get there — *if* we get there — I will need to recognize her. So tell me everything, Wren Halvard. Leave nothing out. You've spent your life keeping the crown's ledger of forgetting. Tonight you'll start paying it back into mine."

* * *

So Wren told her everything.

They talked until the fire burned down to orange eyes in black wood, and then longer, fed on driftwood and honesty. They told her about the harbor city of Vel Sarras, where they'd been born, where the tide came in green-black and the bells rang in chords instead of notes, because a single note was considered rude to the drowned. They told her about the taste of preserved figs and the way their mother sang when she mended nets, songs with no words that everyone in the street somehow knew anyway. They told her how the falling had begun — not the drowning, the *falling* — how Vhalen's last years had been spent feeding its door, and how the columns of refugees had left in good order, heads high, because their king had promised the leaving was temporary and everyone had agreed to believe him, because the alternative was unspeakable.

And somewhere in the telling, without deciding to, Wren began to cry.

It was not graceful. It was not the clean grief of a story. It was ugly and silent and it came up from somewhere below the brand, below the academy, below the resettlement house and the honey-bread and the woman with the borrowed laugh — thirty years of sediment breaking loose all at once, because they had never once, in thirty years, said any of it aloud, and the body keeps its own ledgers and audits them eventually. Freya held their arm and did not look away and did not comfort them, exactly; she simply witnessed, which was her gift and her discipline, and it was enough. More than enough. Wren had spent their whole life believing that to be seen fully was to be destroyed, and here was the proof otherwise arriving far too late to be convenient.

When it was done, when the last of it had drained out, Freya said: "Do you understand what this means? For the Hunt?"

"That they're burning my kin. Yes. I've understood since the river."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "It means more than that. Think, Wren — think like an oath-keeper, since unfortunately you are one. The Hunt registers the gifted and burns them, and the pyres feed the seal on Vhalen's door. We learned that much from Seraphine Voss. But why does the seal need feeding? Seals don't hunger. Doors don't eat." She tapped the ledger. "*Unless the door is pushing back.* Unless everything the crown erased — every name, every family, forty-one columns' worth of countries' worth of people — is still in there, still pressing. And the crown is so afraid of what's pressing that it burns its own gifted citizens to keep the wall up." She looked at them, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "You weren't a refugee who was allowed to forget, Wren. You were a hostage who was allowed to forget. A child taken from a conquered country and raised as the weapon against it. The Hunt isn't just hunting your kin. The Hunt *is* your kin — every oath-keeper of Vhalen descent, every hollowed-out child of the columns, turned around and pointed at home."

The words settled over Wren like the inverted snow settling over the land: wrong-way weather, weightless and inescapable.

They thought of Seraphine Voss's face at their last meeting, the courteous horror of her, the way she had said *you of all people should understand* — and had meant it literally, and Wren had heard it as rhetoric. They thought of every oath-keeper they had trained beside, and how many of them had the same vague non-origin, the same *provenance undisclosed*, the same unplaceable homesickness that flared at pyres. An entire order built out of the stolen children of one drowned nation, each of them holding a torch to the door of the house they'd been carried out of.

"I have to tell them," they said hoarsely. "The others. The ones still in uniform. If they knew—"

"They'd break," Freya said. "Like you broke. Some of them would shatter. And some of them would burn hotter, because it's easier to hate the evidence of what was done to you than to face it." She squeezed their arm. "Which is why, before you tell anyone anything, we're going through that door. You're going to stand in your own country, Wren. You're going to see what they sealed up. And then whatever you choose to do with the truth, it will be *yours* — chosen in full knowledge, which is more than the crown ever let you do with anything."

Through the gap in the roof, the upward snow had thickened. And Wren, watching it climb, realized the flakes were not rising randomly anymore. They were rising in lines. Ordered. Deliberate. As if something on the other side of the sky were sorting them, counting them, the way a certain kind of person counts lampposts and steps and sheep.

The bones in Freya's box had gone silent again. All of them. At once.

"Wren," Freya whispered, and her hands had gone cold on their arm. "They're not asking whether you're coming home anymore."

She lifted her head toward the north, toward the hole in the map, and in the last coals' light her face was blood and ivory.

"Now they're saying *welcome back.*"
