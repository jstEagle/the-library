## 17. A Trade in Impossible Things

The border of Vhalen was not a line. It was a seam.

Wren had known it would be something like this — the guildmaster's map had shown the hole in the world stitched shut with thread that gleamed like frozen lightning — but knowing was nothing. Nothing could have prepared them for standing three paces from a place where the air simply *ended*, where snow that fell upward met rain that fell sideways along an invisible wall of refusal, and where the country behind them — their country, though they had been permitted to forget it — pressed against the stitch like a face against frosted glass.

Freya stood beside them with her ledger strapped to her back and her bone box at her hip, and the dead inside those bones had gone quiet. Not silent. Wren had learned the difference over these weeks on the road. Silence was absence. Quiet was attention. The dead were watching the seam the way starving people watch a door.

"It's beautiful," Freya said, and her breath came out wrong, drifting upward instead of outward, joining the snow.

It was not beautiful. It was terrible. But Wren did not correct her, because they understood what she meant. The stitch rose from the frozen earth to the sky like a curtain of held breath, and through its faint shimmer Wren could see — *almost* — the suggestion of rooftops. A bell tower. Green-black light pooling in streets that had not known footsteps in twenty years.

Home.

The word arrived uninvited and sat in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone.

"The map says there's a gate," Freya said. She unslung the ledger anyway, kneeling in the snow to open it, turning pages until she found the guildmaster's cramped hand. "Here. 'The stitch is not a wall. Walls keep things out. Stitches hold things together. What holds may be asked to loosen — for a price.'" She looked up. "A price."

"Everything out here has a price," Wren said. Their brand ached under the bandages, a dull coal-glow they had carried since the night they broke their first vow. "The hounds taught us that."

"This will be worse." Freya closed the ledger. Her hands were steady; Wren had noticed that about her, that her hands never shook when the stakes were highest, as if fear only lived in her when there was room for it. "The dead have been telling me all morning. They're not afraid of the stitch. They're afraid we'll pay too much."

Wren looked at the seam again. Up close, at this distance, they could see that it was made of words. Thousands upon thousands of words, written in a script they almost recognized, wound around one another like rope, like tendon, like the fibers of a muscle holding a wound closed. Oath-magic. Someone had sworn this country shut, syllable by syllable, and the oath had become a thing with weight and hunger and — if the dead were right — a voice.

"Then let's be careful what we offer," Wren said, and walked toward the seam.

* * *

Ten paces from it, the cold changed.

Wren had endured the long winter like everyone else in the realm — had frozen fingers and frostbitten cheeks and memories of villages where the wells froze from the bottom up — but this was different. This cold came from ahead, not above, rolling off the stitch in slow tides, and it did not bite. It *listened*. It moved over their skin like fingers searching pockets.

Their brand woke.

Not the flaying agony of the first broken vow, nor the smoldering ache of the weeks since. Something else: the oath remnant inside them — the shredded remains of the vow they'd sworn to the crown, the piece still loyal, still bound — lifted its head like a dog hearing its name. And the stitch answered. The whole curtain of woven words brightened, and a voice came out of it.

It did not come from any direction. It came from everywhere the cold touched, which meant it came from inside Wren's own bones, which meant they felt every word of it in the marrow before their ears caught up.

***A trader comes.***

Wren stopped. Behind them, Freya's breath caught.

"We want to pass," Wren said. Their voice sounded very small against so many stitched words.

***Everyone wants to pass. Passing is easy. The question is what you will leave.*** The voice was neither male nor female, young nor old. It had the texture of a contract read aloud in the dark. ***I am the border-stitch. I was sworn to hold. I do not hate what I hold. I do not love it either. But I am owed, for every loosening, and I collect only in impossible things. Common payment bores me. Gold I can dig from your frozen ground myself. Years I can take from anyone. Bring me something impossible, or turn around.***

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "What counts as impossible?"

***You know,*** said the stitch, almost gently. ***You have carried impossible things across half a realm to bring them here.***

Of course they had. Wren turned, and found Freya already looking at them, and saw in her face that she had understood at the same moment.

"I'll pay," Freya said quickly. Too quickly. "My gift. My hearing of the dead — no one else has it, no one else *could* have it, it's mine alone and it can't be taken, only given. That's impossible enough. Isn't it?"

***Generous,*** the stitch mused. The cold tide paused, considering. ***A gift that hears the unhearable. Yes. That would loosen thread.***

"No," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"You'd be deaf to them on the other side. You'd walk into a country full of the waiting dead and hear *nothing*—" They heard their own voice crack and hated it. "No. Not that. Not first."

"Then what? We don't get to be precious about this. People froze to death in Tarrowmill so we could reach this place. The Hunt is three days behind us with pyres on wheels." Freya pulled the bone charm from beneath her collar and held it out toward the seam, and Wren watched her jaw set the way it did over the ledgers, the arithmetic of sacrifice already running behind her eyes. "Take it. Take all of it."

And Wren — because there was no other way to say what needed saying, because some trades you make before you lose your nerve — stepped between Freya and the stitch and spoke to the cold.

"Take my oath instead."

The seam went still. Even the upward-falling snow hung suspended, and Wren felt ten thousand stitched words lean inward to listen.

"What's left of it," Wren said. "The crown swore me as a child. The oath has been breaking since the night I freed her wagon, but it isn't gone. There's still a piece that answers to the Hunt, a piece that knows Seraphine Voss's voice, a piece that would drag me to my knees if she spoke my name. No one else can carry it. It's mine alone and it cannot be taken, only surrendered." They echoed Freya's words deliberately, so the stitch would hear the symmetry. "That's impossible enough."

***Two traders,*** the stitch said, ***two offerings.*** The cold curled around Wren's wrist, over the bandages, testing. The brand flared white-hot and then — strangely — went quiet, like a guard dog scenting a familiar stranger. ***Yes. That would loosen thread also. Choose between you which price is paid, and the way opens.***

"There's no choosing," Freya started. "We both offered, we both pay—"

***One,*** said the stitch.

The word dropped into the clearing like a stone into a well, and the silence after it had edges.

"One," Wren repeated.

***I am a stitch. I bind two things with one thread. One price, drawn once, closes the bargain. You may argue among yourselves who pays. I am indifferent.*** And then, in a tone that Wren would spend a long time trying to forget — a tone of vast, patient, bureaucratic hunger: ***Or. There is a third option.***

The snow began to fall again, but slower now, each flake hanging like a held note.

***You have both offered honestly. Honest offers interest me. I will take neither gift and neither oath. Instead I will choose my own price — a third thing, unnamed, unasked, taken when you cross. That is also within my rights. The old contracts allow it. The ones written before your crowns, before your hunts, when trade was trade and the stitching was done in good faith.***

"That's not a trade," Freya said sharply. "That's theft."

***All bargains are theft delayed,*** said the stitch, without heat. ***Choose. One of your prices, or my mystery. You have until the snow finishes falling this hour.***

* * *

They argued in the lee of a fallen boundary stone whose carved inscription had been chiseled away — another erased name, another thing the crown had unwritten — and the argument was short and vicious and exactly the same argument they had been having in different words since the night Wren cut Freya's wagon loose.

"You take the gift," Freya said. "Give them your hearing-thing, whatever it costs, and I go through whole."

"And I stand on the other side deaf while you read names to people I can't hear?" Wren shook their head. "No. Reverse it. The oath goes. It's been killing me by inches since the day I took it. Let the stitch finish the job."

"If you give up the last of your oath I don't know what happens to you." Freya's voice dropped low, urgent. "Wren. Listen to me. I've watched that brand eat you alive for weeks. It's the ugliest thing you own and it's also — it's the only proof of what you did, what you chose. If you sell the last of it, what's left? What are you, if you're not even the person who broke?"

The question landed harder than Freya could have known, because Wren had been asking it themselves in the dark hours, and had no answer. An oath-keeper without an oath. A blade with no edge and no handle. They thought of the brand burning away everything the vow demanded and leaving — what? They didn't know. They were afraid to find out on the far side of a stitched border with the Hunt at their backs.

But the alternative was Freya walking into Vhalen deaf to the very people they had come to save, and that alternative wasn't thinkable at all.

"Then it's decided," Wren said. "The oath."

"It shouldn't be decided," Freya said. "Nothing about this should be this easy—"

And that — her instinct, her ledger-keeper's nose for a false entry — was what turned Wren around.

Because she was right. Nothing on this road had been easy. Every mile had cost blood or warmth or truth. And here was the stitch offering them a clean transaction, one price, fair terms, a tidy column that balanced. After chapters of ruin, a bargain that added up.

Ledgers that balanced too neatly, the guildmaster used to say, are the ones you audit twice.

"The third thing," Wren said aloud, turning back to the seam. "Tell us what it is."

***If I named it, it would be one of your prices,*** the stitch replied, ***and we agreed I take neither of your prices. Mystery is the merchandise.***

"Then give us terms. Conditions. Anything."

***Very well. Terms, since you ask so nicely, little oath-broken one.*** The cold drew back, formal now, the voice taking on the cadence of a contract's fine print. ***The third thing will not be your gift, hers, your oath, your memories, your years, your warmth, your names, or your love. It will not be anything you would notice missing tonight. It will not be anything you would curse me for tomorrow. It may be something you miss in ten years, or something you never knew you had. When you cross, I take it. You will feel nothing. You will agree to this without knowing, which is the oldest kind of agreement there is.***

Wren stood in the falling-up snow and tried to imagine a thing that fit those terms and found their imagination failing, and realized that was the point. The stitch was asking them to sign a blank page. Every guild rule, every ledger law, every scrap of Freya's meticulous bookkeeping screamed against it.

But behind the seam, almost visible now in the green-black light, a bell tower stood over drowned streets, and somewhere inside it — or something like it — people who were not dead were waiting for someone to carry their names home. And the Hunt's pyres were three days behind, feeding whatever fed the winter, and the dead in Freya's box had gone quiet with attention, and there was no fourth option waiting behind the third.

"We could turn back," Freya said quietly. She didn't sound like she believed it.

"To what?"

She was silent. Then she laughed — a small, cracked, exhausted sound — and slung the ledger back over her shoulder. "The guildmaster always said the impossible trades were the honest ones. Everything else, somebody's cheating you slowly." She looked up at the seam of woven words, at the drowned country pressing against it, and squared her shoulders. "All right, stitch. Your mystery. But I'm keeping records." She pulled her glove off with her teeth, bared her hand, and pressed her palm flat against the nearest thread of the seam. "Witnessed. Freya Delacroix, for the Guild of Impossible Trades, agrees to your third price, unnamed, and notes for posterity that you're a thief."

The stitched words beneath her palm glowed warm gold.

***Witnessed,*** the stitch agreed, and Wren could have sworn the vast cold voice sounded pleased. ***And you, oath-keeper? I need the one who was sworn. The thread remembers who it was tied to.***

Wren stepped forward. Up close the seam was dizzying — words within words, oaths braided into oaths, and threaded all through it, unmistakable now that they looked, the script they had almost recognized. Vhalen's writing. Their grandmother's letters. The stitch had been made partly *of* their country, turned into its own lock.

They put their hand beside Freya's.

"I agree to the third price," they said. "Unnamed. Witnessed. Now open."

***Open,*** the stitch echoed, and the meaning bent strangely, and Wren had a heartbeat — just one — to understand that the word had been a confirmation and a warning at once, before the seam *unwove*.

It did not swing apart like a gate. It unraveled like a sleeve, threads pulling loose and drawing aside, and the green-black light of Vhalen poured through the gap like water finding a breach, and the smell hit them — wet stone and river silt and woodsmoke and underneath it something sweet and wrong, like flowers grown in the dark. The dead in Freya's box began to sing, dozens of voices at once, a chord of names, and Freya staggered under the sound and caught herself laughing and crying in the same breath.

They crossed together, hand in hand — Wren never afterward remembered deciding that, only the pressure of Freya's fingers as the thread of the seam brushed over their shoulders like a tailor's measuring tape, quick and impersonal and thorough, pausing somewhere inside Wren's chest to snip one small, invisible thing —

They felt nothing. The stitch had promised they would feel nothing, and it kept its promise.

The drowned country received them. Streets lay intact beneath a sky the color of deep water, buildings whole, windows dark, doors closed with a patience that had lasted twenty years. At the end of the street, figures stood in doorways and on corners, still as furniture, faces lifting toward the newcomers with the slow gravity of sleepers turning over.

Freya gripped Wren's hand hard. "Do you feel it? Did it take anything?"

Wren inventoried themselves the way they had inventoried saddlebags a hundred times on the road. Hands. Feet. The ache of the brand — still there, still banked coals. Memories of mother, of the sea, of fire. Fear. Hope. Love, startling and certain, for the woman beside them.

"I don't notice anything missing," they said.

***You won't,*** the stitch murmured — already fading, already reweaving itself behind them, thread by patient thread. ***Not for years.***

And far behind them, back across the border, in the frozen orchard where the Hunt made its camp, Seraphine Voss woke her second-in-command with a lamp and a map and a voice like a closing door, and said: "The stitch just opened. Someone paid. Saddle the hounds — they're going home, and we're going with them."

The hunt began again.
