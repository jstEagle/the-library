## 19. Names in the Bone Box

The dead of Vhalen did not rush at us.

I had braced for it — the way you brace for a wave, or a blow, or a debt come due. We had crossed through the stitch in the world with Freya's hand in mine and her ledger against her heart, and I had expected the drowned country to take us the way it had taken everything else: all at once, without negotiation.

Instead they waited.

They stood in the streets of a city that should have been at the bottom of the sea — stood on cobbles furred with green-black light, under a sky the color of deep water and old grief — and they waited, patient as furniture, patient as weather. Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe, receding down avenues into a haze that was not fog and not distance but something like *unremembering*, the far parts of the city going soft at the edges as if the country itself had begun to forget its own outline.

They were not ghosts. I want that understood. Ghosts are what the living make of the dead — pale copies, regrets with faces. These were people. They had weight enough to press footprints into the frost-slick cobbles. They breathed, or performed something so close to breathing that my body answered it, my own chest rising and falling in sympathy until I felt dizzy.

And every one of them was looking at me.

Not at Freya, whose gift had drawn them across an erased border like a bell rung underwater. Not at the bone box slung at her hip, which murmured and clicked softly, constantly, like a hive of very small bees. At *me*.

"Freya," I said quietly. "They know me."

"I can see that." Her voice was careful. She had gone very still, the way she went still over a ledger line that didn't balance — not afraid yet, but counting the ways she might need to be.

An old man detached himself from the crowd. He walked with a limp I recognized before I recognized his face, because I had spent three years of my childhood running behind that limp, trying to keep up, laughing when he pretended to shake his stick at me. He stopped ten feet away. His coat was the gray of Vhalen's winter wool, moth-eaten at the cuffs, and his hands were the hands of a man who had mended nets for forty years: knotted, scarred, certain.

"Wren," he said. My name in Vhaleni — *Vrenn*, with the soft doubled consonant, the way water says a thing twice. "Little Vrenn. You came back."

My knees nearly went. I locked them. Oath-keepers are trained to lock their knees; it is the first lesson, before pain, before silence. *You will be asked to stand when standing is impossible.*

"Master Ilven," I said. The word came out of me in Vhaleni too, unbidden, a door in my throat swinging open onto a room I had sealed when I was nine years old. "You're—"

"Waiting," he said simply. "We are all waiting. It has been a long wait." He looked past me at Freya, and then at the box at her hip, and something moved through the crowd — a shiver, a leaning-in, thousands of bodies tilting toward us by one degree, like grass before wind. "But you've brought names."

Freya's hand went to the box. "I've brought a ledger," she said. "Carved in bone. Every name the Hunt erased. Every name we could find."

"Not every name," the old man said gently. "Ours. The ones that were taken when the country fell. But names are names, little keeper. A door opened anywhere is a door opened everywhere." He turned back to me, and his eyes were wet, and the dead do not cry easily — I learned that day how much it must cost them. "You were allowed to forget. Do you understand? That was the mercy they gave the children. The crown scraped Vhalen out of you like rot from fruit, and sent you off to be their hounds. And it worked. Look at you. Look at what they dressed you in."

I looked down at myself. Crown-gray oath-cloak, the brand on my forearm still raw from the broken vow, the broken vow itself hanging off me like a shed skin. I had spent the whole journey telling myself I was a traitor to the crown. It had not once occurred to me, until this street, until this face, that I might also be a son coming home.

"I don't remember you," I said. And then, because lying had become physically difficult since the vow broke: "No. That's a lie. I remember your dog. I remember being bitten by your dog and you laughed and said it was a compliment."

"Bramble," Master Ilven said, and laughed too, a sound like a rope under strain. "She bit everyone she loved. You'll meet her. She's waiting further up the hill." He sobered. "We don't have much time, children. The cold is coming faster now, and it is not weather. When it reaches the city—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. I had seen villages frozen mid-task. I had seen a woman holding an axe she would never swing, ice in her open mouth.

"Then we start now," Freya said.

*

She chose a square near the center of the city — a market square, by its shape, though the stalls had long since become suggestions, outlines of commerce remembered rather than maintained. The dead gathered the way a congregation gathers, without being asked, filling the space in rings. There must have been two thousand of them by the time Freya opened the bone box, and the sound they made was the worst sound I have ever heard and the best: silence. Two thousand people holding their breath.

"Here's how it works," Freya said to me, low, while she unwrapped the first bundle of carved bones. Her fingers were steady. Her knuckles were white. "I read the name. All of it — given name, house-name, the little honorifics, everything the carving holds. The bone gives it back to them whole. And then—"

"And then?"

She hesitated. Freya Delacroix, who could price a bottle of grief to the drop and had never once in my hearing flinched from a hard number, hesitated.

"And then it takes something from me," she said. "Warmth. I noticed it on the road, reading the small ones — the names of the burned, the ones who died screaming Vhalen. Each name cost me a little heat. I thought it was the wagon, the night air." She looked up at me, and her eyes were level and honest and terrified in the specific way of someone doing arithmetic they don't like. "It wasn't. It's the exchange rate, Wren. A name for warmth. I don't know why. I don't know if it ends. But there are thousands of them, and the general is coming, and I am going to read anyway."

"No," I said.

"Yes," she said. "Wren. Look around you. What did you think we came here to do?"

"We came here to find another way—"

"There is no other way. There is only this way, done carefully." She took my hand — my branded hand, the one that burned — and pressed it flat between both of hers, and the brand quieted under her touch the way it always did, sullen fire banked to embers. "Listen to me. I have spent my whole life hiding what I am behind ink and ivory. Behind ledgers. Because a gift that hears the dead sounds like a curse, and I believed it. But out there—" she nodded east, toward the border, toward the pyres, toward Seraphine Voss and her seal and her terrible arithmetic — "out there they burn names. They erase people. And the only counter to erasure is witness. This is what my gift is *for*. I finally know what it's for. Don't you dare take that from me because it costs."

I wanted to argue. I want it recorded, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of me, that I wanted to argue, and that the argument died in my throat not because she was right — though she was — but because behind her, two thousand waiting people leaned forward by one more degree, and hope has a sound, and I had never heard it before that moment.

"How much warmth?" I asked. "Per name. Estimate it. You're a ledger-keeper. Give me numbers."

Her mouth twitched, despite everything. "Small names, a shiver. Full names, with the honorifics—" she flexed her fingers, already cold-looking — "a fever's worth. Maybe worse, for the old names. The deep carvings."

"So a hundred names puts you in danger."

"A hundred names puts me *cold*, Wren. Danger is later. Cold is now, and manageable, if someone keeps me warm between readings." She said it briskly, professionally, and then ruined it entirely by blushing, which in the green-black light made her look like a girl instead of a woman who had stared down a Huntmaster and won a draw.

"I can do that," I said. My voice came out strange. "Keeping you warm. Between readings. I'm — qualified. Apparently. By several recent incidents."

"*Several* recent incidents," she agreed solemnly, and then the moment broke, because Master Ilven was standing at the front of the crowd with his cap in his hands, and behind him the whole waiting city held its breath, and there was no more time for anything but the work.

*

The first name was a child's.

Freya hadn't chosen it; the bones chose, or the dead did — the box had gone quiet and then insistent, clicking, and when she reached in, the bone she drew out was small, no longer than her thumb, carved so finely the letters were barely visible except when the green light caught them. She closed her eyes. I watched her lips move, reading the silent carving, gathering the whole of it.

Then she opened her eyes and spoke.

"Maren Ostreva, called Little Sparrow, daughter of the Ostreva house, mender of sails, who taught the gulls to follow the ferry, who was eight years old when the water came—"

A girl stepped out of the crowd.

That is the only way I can describe it. Before the name, she had been one of the waiting — present, patient, but blurred somehow, a person remembered by a country rather than remembering herself. As Freya spoke, the blur burned off her like morning mist off a harbor. Color came into her cheeks. Her braid, which had been a suggestion of a braid, became a braid, crooked, tied with red thread. She looked down at her own hands and turned them over and began, very quietly, to cry — not the dead's strained almost-tears but real crying, ugly and alive, and she ran not to Freya, not to me, but to a woman in the third row, and the woman caught her, and the sound the two of them made together was the sound of a name coming home.

The cost came immediately. I saw it happen: Freya swayed, just slightly, and her breath came out white in air that had been merely cool. Frost feathered the rim of the bone box.

"Again?" I asked.

"Again," she said, teeth already chattering faintly, and held out her hand for the next bone.

I did the only thing I could think of. I stepped behind her and wrapped my arms around her shoulders, chin at the crown of her head, sharing what heat I had — and I had less than a living man should, the broken vow had seen to that, but I had some, and I gave it. She stiffened for half a heartbeat. Then she leaned back into me, exhaled, and read.

"Marta Ostreva, called the Gull-Wife, mother of Little Sparrow, who kept the lighthouse at Grey Teeth burning through the Long Dark—"

The lighthouse-keeper came back to herself mid-row, gasping, already reaching for her daughter, and the two of them collided, and Freya shivered so hard I had to tighten my grip, and the frost on the box spread to her fingertips, and I rubbed her hands between mine, and she read again.

Name after name after name.

I will not pretend I tracked them all. Grief does that — it blurs the record, and I am an oath-keeper, trained to keep records even of my own suffering, and I failed. But some of them I will carry until whatever I am carries nothing anymore:

*Aldric Vane, ferryman, who carried the last children across before the water rose, and went back for one more, and did not return.* The ferryman came back to himself already rowing, hands moving on invisible oars, and when he saw where he was he put his face in his hands and thanked Freya in Vhaleni, over and over, *thank you for the boat, thank you for the boat,* and I did not understand until later that he meant the name — that to the waiting dead, a name is a boat, the only vessel that crosses back.

*Sera Ilven, net-mender, wife of Ilven the limping, who sang the boats home in fog.* Master Ilven's wife. He made a sound when she woke that I will hear for the rest of my life, whatever length of life remains to me — a sound like a keel touching sand after a long sea.

*Tomaz Brek, called Tomaz-the-Brave by no one but his grandmother, who was afraid of eels.* The boy came back laughing, mid-laugh, as if the name had caught him in the act, and his grandmother was there, and she had been waiting, the crowd told me afterward, longer than anyone — waiting since the fall itself, holding herself together by a thread of stubbornness, just to see if anyone would ever say his name again.

And each one cost. By the twentieth name Freya's lips were blue. By the thirtieth, frost had climbed her sleeves to the elbow, and the bones were coming out of the box rimed white, and I was chafing her arms, her hands, pressing my cheek to her hair trying to feed heat into her skull, whispering between readings — *rest, rest a moment* — and she would shake her head, or say, "One more," or, worse, say nothing at all, just hold out her hand, palm up, patient as the dead.

"You have to stop soon," I said somewhere around the fortieth name.

"After this one."

"That's what you said four names ago."

"This one's different." She was squinting at the carving, and her brow furrowed. "Wren. This one's different. Listen." And she read:

*"Wren Halvard, called Vrenn, child of the Halvard house of the Saltrow district, son of Mirei Halvard, who held him on the seawall the last morning and told him to run and not look back—"*

The square went absolutely silent.

I did not step out of the crowd. I want that understood too. I was already outside it; I had been outside it the whole time, holding the woman who read the names. But the name went through me like a harpoon through water, and every part of me it touched lit up. My mother. My mother's name, in Freya's mouth, in the language I had been forbidden, in the square where — where the seawall had been, I realized, the seawall was *this square*, the market had been built on the filled land behind it, and I had stood on this exact stone at nine years old with salt spray in my face and my mother's hands pushing me toward a crown ship that promised safety and delivered forgetting.

"She's here," Freya whispered. Her voice was wrecked, half-frozen, tender. "Wren. She's in the crowd. She's been here the whole time. She's been watching you since we arrived."

I looked up.

Two thousand faces, and I found her the way you find a star you knew as a child — instantly, inevitably, as if no searching had been required at all. She stood near the back. She had not come forward. Of course she hadn't; she had pushed me away once already, spent her last act as a living woman making her son leave, and waiting dead or not, a mother does not presume. She was wearing the blue scarf. She was wearing the *blue scarf*, the one she'd wound around my neck that morning before transferring it to her own throat at the last second, and sixty years had not faded it, because the dead keep what matters.

"Mirei Halvard," Freya read, and her voice cracked down the middle, and she kept reading anyway, through the crack, giving my mother every honorific, every small true thing the carving held — *who mended other people's clothes and never her own, who lied beautifully about the quality of the fish, who loved a stubborn boy more than the country that was drowning* — and my mother came back to herself the way dawn comes back to a window.

She looked at me. I looked at her. Sixty years, an empire, an oath, a hunt, a broken vow, a frozen road, a door out of a dead country — none of it survived the looking.

"Vrenn," she said. "You got tall."

I crossed the square at a dead run, oath-keeper discipline and locked knees and all, and hit her like a wave hits a seawall, and she held on, and she was warm — the dead of Vhalen are warm, that is the secret nobody outside knows, they are warm as bread, they have been saving their warmth all this time for exactly this — and I heard Freya behind us, swaying, catching herself on the bone box, refusing help, reading the next name alone because she would not interrupt, not even for this, not even for me.

That is when I understood the shape of the ending that was coming for us. Not the details — those came later, in the orchard, at the stitch, in the storm. But the shape. Every name warmed her heart and froze her body, and there were thousands of names, and she would read every single one, and I could not stop her, and I did not want to, and both of those truths would eventually require a third truth neither of us had invented yet.

For that hour, though, there was only the work, and the warmth, and my mother's hand in my hair saying my name in the old soft way, and Freya's voice going hoser and colder, name after name after name, the frost climbing her like ivy.

It was my mother who noticed first. She pulled back from me, alert the way only someone who has lost a country can be alert, and looked past my shoulder at the reader in the center of the square — at the girl wrapped in frost, reading on, alone.

"Who is she?" my mother asked. "What is she paying?"

And before I could answer, Freya stumbled, caught the box, steadied — and every bone inside it began to scream.
