## 21. The Middle of the Hunt

I heard the seal break before I saw it.

Not with my ears — those had gone strange since the brand burned itself hollow, full of a ringing like a struck bell that would not finish its note. I heard it in the place where the oath used to live. For eleven years there had been a weight in me, a chain of words wound tight around the spine, and when Seraphine Voss shattered her own life's work from the inside, I felt the chain's absence swing loose, and through that empty space came a sound like a country exhaling.

The green-black sky over Vhalen split along a seam no one else could see. Light poured through it — not sunlight, nothing so kind — a hard white glare the color of registration papers, of wax seals, of pyres seen from a ridge at dusk.

"They're coming," Freya said.

She stood at the edge of the restored square, where the streets of Vhalen had spent three days un-drowning themselves, stone by stone rising out of memory into being. Behind her the ledger lay open on a bier of woven bone, her handwriting marching down its pages in columns so neat they looked printed. She had been reading names for hours. Her lips were gray. Her breath made fog in air that should have been mild, because each name she gave back cost her warmth, and she paid without haggling, the way she did everything — meticulously, completely, as if witness were a debt she intended to settle to the last coin.

"How many?" I asked.

"Voss came through alone." Freya's eyes stayed on the seam of light. "But she wasn't followed. She was *preceded*. The Hunt was already moving before she broke the seal. She just opened the door they'd built."

That was the thing about Seraphine Voss I still could not make fit: she had spent twenty years building a lock, and then she had broken it herself rather than let the crown use it as a gate. Dying to undo her work. Handing me the keystone with blood on her mouth and saying, *You'll know what to do with it, Halvard, or you'll know what not to do, and either way you'll do it honestly.* As if honesty were a skill a person could have. As if I had ever been honest about anything except pain.

The seam widened.

And the Hunt came through.

* * *

I want to describe them properly, because later — if there is a later — someone will ask what it looked like, and I refuse to let it become a story about glory.

It looked like an army that had been told it was righteous for so long it had forgotten how to doubt. Two hundred riders in white-and-gold, the Hunt's colors, pouring through the tear in the sky like water finding a crack. Oath-hounds loping beside them, lean and wrong-jointed, their noses full of broken promises. Wagons behind them with iron cages, and chains, and the long poles they used for pyres. And above them all, banners bearing the crowned wolf of Corvane, sewn in thread that caught the green light and made it look like rot.

They fanned out across the square with the efficiency of people who had done this many times. I watched a captain gesture, and forty riders peeled off toward the eastern quarter where the waiting dead had begun to gather — drawn, I think, by the sound of Freya's voice reading names, the way cold things move toward warmth.

"Freya." I put myself between her and the square. It was instinct, not strategy; two hundred against two is not a battle, it is an arithmetic problem with one answer. "The bone box. The ledger. We run for the border-stitch, we—"

"No." She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Wren. Look at them."

I looked. Not at the riders. At the dead.

They had come up out of the restored streets the way frost comes up through soil — everywhere at once, silent, inevitable. The people of Vhalen who were neither living nor dead but waiting. A fishwife with scales still on her hands. A boy holding a wooden horse he had carried across the border-stitch forty years ago and never set down. An old man in the robes of a Vhaleni oath-keeper, which meant he had worn the same office I had, sworn the same shape of vow, in a country that no longer existed on any map.

They stood in the open, watching the Hunt arrive, and they did not run.

"Why aren't they running?" My voice cracked on it.

"Because they've already died once," Freya said. "And because—" she touched the ledger, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal, "—they're waiting to see if their names will be read. That's all they want. That's all any of them have wanted. And the Hunt is going to burn them for wanting it."

The first pyre-pole went down in the eastern quarter. Even from the square I heard the hounds begin to sing.

* * *

Here is what I had, in that moment, laid out honestly:

One oathless disgraced knight. One bone-carver too deep in her reading to stop. A keystone I did not understand, warm against my chest where Voss had pressed it into my hands. And between us and the Hunt, several thousand people who could not be killed again but could be *unmade* — burned past the point where even a name in a ledger could call them back.

I had spent my whole life following orders. First the crown's, then the oath's, then the brand's, which had punished every disobedience with fire under the skin until obedience stopped being a choice and became a reflex, like breathing. Three days ago the brand had turned fully against the vow that made it, burning away everything the crown had demanded of me, and left me standing in the ash of my own duty asking the question I had never been permitted to ask:

*If I am not the crown's, whose am I?*

The answer arrived the way answers do — not as a thought but as a movement. I stepped forward. Off the edge of the square, down into the street, toward the eastern quarter, toward the first pyre being stacked with Vhalen's waiting dead.

"Wren—" Freya's voice behind me.

"I'm not leaving them," I said, and did not turn around, because if I turned around I would see her face and lose whatever this was, this thing moving my legs that felt suspiciously like a conscience finally getting its way after eleven years of starvation.

The captain of the eastern detachment saw me coming. I recognized him — Captain Aldery, a decent man by the standards of indecent work, who had once looked away while I gave water to a condemned woman on the road to the pyre-city. He raised a hand and his riders formed a line across the street, and forty crossbows came up, and he called out in the flat voice of procedure:

"Halvard. You're listed as oath-broken and at large. Stand down and you'll be taken alive for trial."

"I don't think I will," I said.

And then the strangest thing in a day full of strange things happened.

The dead moved.

* * *

It began at the edges of my vision, the way weather begins. The fishwife took one step forward. Then the boy with the wooden horse. Then the old oath-keeper, and a mason, and a woman carrying a bundle that might have been bread or might have been a child, and then dozens, hundreds, flowing out of doorways and alleys and the mouths of un-drowned cellars, converging — I understood with a lurch — *on me.*

They gathered around me like water around a stone. Not touching. Waiting.

Aldery's line wavered. Men who had burned the gifted without flinching looked at a crowd of the transparent dead and found their courage had opinions.

"What is this," Aldery said. Not to me. To his gods, maybe. Or to himself.

I didn't know. But I could feel them — that was the horror and the wonder of it, I could *feel* them, the way you feel a room full of people behind your back. Through the hollow where my oath had been, through the burnt-out channel the brand had carved, their attention poured into me like heat into iron. And underneath the attention, something else. A question. Thousands of voices asking one question in a language older than words, and the question was:

*What do you want us to do?*

They were asking me. The dead of Vhalen, the people I had been sworn as a child to forget, the kin I had been raised to help erase — they were looking at the one weapon the Hunt had ever made of their own flesh, and they were asking me for orders.

I should have refused. I want that written somewhere, in some ledger, in my own hand: *I should have refused.*

Instead I heard myself say, quietly, in the voice I had used for commands my whole career — parade-ground calm, the calm that makes frightened men feel the world still has rules:

"Stand between them and the pyres."

The dead of Vhalen moved like a tide.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have two sides willing to fight. This was a wall walking.

The waiting dead interposed themselves between the Hunt's riders and the pyre-poles, and the horses — horses are sensible creatures — refused absolutely to advance. Riders cursed and spurred and their mounts planted and screamed and backed. The hounds were worse; they could smell the oath-magic coming off the dead in waves, and oath-hounds are built to track broken promises, and here was a whole country of promises broken by other people, and the smell drove them mad. They howled and bit at their own handlers' legs.

"Disperse them!" someone shouted. "Steel doesn't—"

Steel went through the dead and came out the other side doing nothing at all. A soldier ran a spear through the old oath-keeper's chest, and the old man looked down at it with mild academic interest, and said something in Vhaleni that I did not need translated, because the dead's voices had begun arriving in me directly now, bypassing my ears entirely, and what he said was: *Boy, I held that rank before your grandmother was born. Put it down.*

The soldier put it down.

And I stood in the middle of it — the middle of the Hunt, the middle of the dead, the middle of everything — and felt the power move through me like a second bloodstream, and I want to be precise about what it felt like, because precision is the last honesty I have left.

It felt *good.*

That is the confession. Not the commanding — anyone might command, in extremity, and be forgiven. It felt good the way the oath used to feel good, back in the first years, when duty was still clean: the rightness of it, the certainty, the beautiful simplicity of a world divided into things I protected and things I opposed. The dead poured their trust into me and I drank it, and with every swallow the world got simpler. Forty riders became an obstacle. Two hundred became arithmetic. The men inside the white-and-gold armor — men with mothers, men with doubts, men like Aldery who had once shown a small mercy — they simplified too, in my new sight, shrinking into *obstacles*, into *pieces*, into entries in a ledger I suddenly very much wanted to keep.

*Order them to kneel,* whispered something in the hollow where my oath had been. Not the dead — the dead only asked. This was older, and colder, and it spoke in a voice like snow falling on a village at night. *Order them to disarm. Order them to remember what they've done. You can make them carry the memory the way you carried the oath. Every one of them. Forever.*

And God forgive me, I considered it.

I stood in the square with an army of the patient dead arrayed around my will like a cloak, and I imagined Aldery kneeling, and the Huntmaster's captains kneeling, and every rider who had ever chained a gifted child into a wagon kneeling in the mud of a drowned country, remembering, remembering, remembering — and the imagining was sweet, and the sweetness was the trap, and I knew it was the trap even as I stepped toward it, because cruelty always arrives dressed as justice, and it had come for me wearing my own face.

What stopped me was not virtue. I want to be clear about that too. What stopped me was a voice reading names.

* * *

Freya had come down into the eastern quarter. I don't know when — while I was drowning, presumably, while the power was pouring into the hollow of me and filling it with a certainty that felt exactly like the oath had felt, only warmer, only sweeter. She had planted herself in front of the first pyre-pile, the one the detachment had half-built before the dead closed in, and she had opened the ledger across her knees, and she was reading.

Not loudly. Freya Delacroix does nothing loudly. She read the way she balanced accounts: clearly, precisely, name by name, giving each one its full weight, and the dead around her — the ones near enough to hear — straightened as she spoke. I watched a woman who had been fading at the edges snap back into focus, whole, *named*, and lift her face toward the sky with an expression I had last seen on refugees entering Corvane: the look of someone arriving somewhere safe.

Each name cost Freya visible warmth. Fog poured from her mouth. Frost crept up the ledger's leather cover from her fingers. She read anyway.

And the cold certainty in me — the sweet simplifying power, the whispering thing that wanted kneeling armies and eternal memories — looked at her and could not simplify her. It tried. It reached for her the way it had reached for Aldery, reaching to sort her into a column, and it couldn't, because she was not an obstacle or an ally or a piece. She was a person paying for strangers out of her own body, one degree of warmth per name, and there is no column for that. There is no ledger entry for a woman who spends herself on the dead and calls it bookkeeping.

The power recoiled from her like a hand from a stove. And in the space it left, I heard myself think, in my own small human voice, *Oh. Oh no. This is what it does. This is what it did to the crown. This is what it did to me the first time, when I was nine years old and they wrapped an oath around me and called the numbness peace.*

I let go.

Not all of it — I didn't know how, and I suspect no one ever does, not entirely; I think the dead remain a little ordered around me now the way iron remains a little magnetized. But I opened my grip on the cloak of them, on the will, on the sweet certainty, and said, aloud, to several thousand waiting souls:

"That was mine wanting, not yours. I'm sorry. Do as you judge."

And the tide did not collapse. That was the miracle buried inside the warning. They stayed. They stood between the Hunt and the pyres because they chose to stand, and their choosing was worth more than my commanding had ever been, and the whispering cold thing in the hollow of me hissed like water on iron and went quiet to wait for a better moment.

There is always a better moment. That is how it hunts.

* * *

By dusk the standoff had become a siege, and the siege had become something neither side had vocabulary for. The Hunt held the western quarter, behind a barricade of wagons, their horses useless, their hounds half-mad, their doctrine — burn the gifted, feed the pyres, power the seal — rendered suddenly obsolete by the fact that the seal was broken and the pyres had nothing left to feed. Their huntmaster's banner still flew. Under it, I could see officers arguing, and the argument had the flavor of men discovering their god has stopped answering.

We held the east, and the center, and the sky, and the dead.

Freya found me at the barricade line as the green light failed. She was shivering so hard her teeth clicked, wrapped in a dead woman's coat that some Vhaleni soul had put around her shoulders with the grave courtesy of a host. She stood next to me and looked out at the fires of the Hunt's camp, and for a long time neither of us spoke.

"You commanded them," she said at last. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — assessing, the way she assessed a trade, weighing value.

"For about four minutes."

"And?"

"And it was the best I have ever felt in my life," I said, "and I have never been more afraid of anything, including the pyre." I turned to look at her. Her lips were blue. Her eyes were steady. "Freya. When I held them — the world got simple. Everyone got simple. I was starting to sort *you*, and I couldn't, and that's the only reason I stopped. Not goodness. Failure."

She was quiet for a moment. Snow had begun to fall — real snow, ordinary snow, falling down, which in Vhalen these days counted as a kindness — and it settled on her hair and did not melt, which told me how much warmth she had left.

"The general talked to me today," she said. "While I was reading. In my mother's voice." Her hand went to the bone charm at her throat, the one that had started all of this, murmuring its quiet litany of the dead. "It offered me a trade. It's going to offer again, Wren. And your new friend in the hollow where your oath used to be — it's the same thing, isn't it. Same winter. Different doors."

"Yes," I said, because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere on the road to the pyre-city, back when the oath flayed me for a merciful lie and taught me what honesty cost.

"Then we have a problem," Freya Delacroix said, "because yours wants an army, and mine wants my mother, and between them they own every single thing we love."

Out past the barricades, in the dark between the two camps, the snow changed direction.

It began to fall upward.
