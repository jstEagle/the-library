## 23. What Power Costs by the Hour

Wren had stopped counting the dead they raised.

That was the first sign, though Wren did not know it yet — that the counting had stopped. For eleven years as an oath-keeper, Wren had counted everything. Steps on patrol. Rations. The exact number of lashes the vow delivered for a lie (nine, for a small one; thirty-one for the lie Wren told Freya about mercy). Counting was how an oath-keeper stayed honest: a number could not flatter you, could not soften itself, could not pretend to be something it wasn't.

Now Wren stood on the ridge above the stitched border and looked down at four hundred dead soldiers of Vhalen standing in the snow in perfect ranks, and did not know — did not *care* to know — the exact number.

*Four hundred,* said a voice in Wren's head that was not the dead. *You counted them at dusk. It was four hundred and six.*

The brand on Wren's arm had gone quiet since the border-crossing. That was the second thing Wren should have noticed. The broken oath had screamed for weeks, a white-hot brand crawling from wrist to elbow, and now it lay still as a healed scar — and in its stillness it had opened something else. Wren could hear the dead the way Freya heard them. Not as whispers in bone. As *orders waiting to be given.*

"Third rank, forward to the stream line," Wren said, and the words came out flat as a clerk's. "Hold the ford. Nothing crosses."

Four hundred — four hundred and six — dead soldiers moved as one body, boots that had rotted to the bone finding the snow without sound, and took up position along the frozen stream. They had been dead for twenty years. Some of them had been farmers before the crown came. One of them, the tall one at the left of the third rank with the rusted gorget, had a name Wren had read aloud only two nights ago from Freya's ledger: *Ossian Vael, ferryman, drowned at the Ageing of the Waters.* Ossian Vael now held a ford in the dark because Wren told him to, and did not ask why, and did not need to.

That was the gift of it. That was the *gift* of it. No fear. No argument. No one looking at Wren with Freya's eyes, full of a question Wren could not answer.

The cold felt clean. That was the thing Wren could not have explained to anyone a month ago. The dead did not lie to you, because they had nothing left to lie with. Commanding them was like keeping a ledger: every entry exact, every line balanced, nothing wasted, nothing *felt.* Wren had spent eleven years being punished for every human impulse, flayed by the vow for every mercy, and now the vow was burned out of them and what remained was — efficiency. Certainty. The lovely, terrible quiet of a page with nothing written on it yet.

"Left flank," Wren said. "The Hunt's outriders will come at dawn through the birch stand. You will let the first ten pass. You will take the rest."

The dead did not ask what would be done with the first ten.

Wren turned back toward the camp — the ruined tithe-barn below the ridge where Freya kept the ledger, where the fire was, where the warmth was — and found that Wren's feet had been standing in the snow for four hours without moving, and the snow had not melted.

*The snow has not melted,* said the voice that was not the dead. *You are twenty-nine years old and the snow does not melt under you.*

Wren looked down at the untouched white and felt — nothing. That was the alarming part. Not horror. Not even curiosity. A distant, clerkish note: *observe, record, address later.*

Freya would be horrified. Freya would take Wren's hand and press it flat against her own throat, where the skin was always warm, and say *you're freezing, you're freezing, why didn't you say* — and Wren would have said *I didn't notice,* and it would be true, and that truth was worse than any lie the oath had ever punished.

Wren walked down toward the fire.

* * *

The tithe-barn smelled of tallow and old hay and bone dust, and it was the only warm place left in the world as far as Wren was concerned, though Wren would have said *strategically significant* rather than *warm* if asked, and the fact that Wren had started thinking in that register — that everything warm had to be justified as *strategic* before it was permitted — was the third sign, and Wren missed it too.

Freya was at the table with the ledger open, her carving knife in hand, working a new name into a fresh bone. She did not look up. She had stopped looking up when Wren came in, three nights ago. Wren had noticed that. Wren had recorded it, in the cold new ledger of the mind, under *problems, deferred.*

"You've been on the ridge since dark," Freya said, to the bone, to the knife, to the small curling shavings.

"The Hunt moves at dawn. The ford needed holding."

"You said that last night."

"The ford still needs holding."

Freya set down the knife. That was bad. Freya only set down the knife when her hands wanted to be empty for what came next. "How many did you raise tonight?"

"Enough."

"*How many.*"

"Freya—"

"Because I heard them." She stood now, and the firelight caught the bone charm at her throat, her mother's voice sleeping in it like an ember under ash. "All night. Four hundred and six people standing in a stream in the dark because you told them to, and not one of them asked you *why,* and you know why they didn't ask? Because they can't. Because they're dead, Wren, and you've stopped treating that like a wound and started treating it like a *convenience.*"

"They're holding a ford," Wren said. "Against the Hunt. The Hunt that is burning their country *again.* The Hunt that burned them the first time. If anything, they're finally fighting the right war—"

"They're not fighting. *You're* fighting. They're being *used.*" Freya came around the table, and Wren saw that her hands were shaking — not with fear. With the particular rage of a ledger-keeper who has found a falsified entry. "Do you know what I heard tonight, at the third watch? Ossian Vael. He was a ferryman. He carried people across the water for thirty years, and the last thing he did alive was put his own boat between the crown's soldiers and a family on the far bank. And tonight he stood in a frozen stream for four hours because you said *hold the ford,* and you know what he said to me? He said—" her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through it— "he said *the oath-keeper does not say my name anymore. Only my rank.*"

The barn was very quiet.

"He's not a rank," Freya said, low. "He's a ferryman. He's a *person.* He has a name, and I wrote it down, and I read it to him so he could be himself again, and you've spent three days turning him back into a piece on a board."

"They're dead, Freya." It came out of Wren flat and cold and true, and Wren heard it land between them like a dropped blade. "They are *dead.* I can give them purpose or I can leave them in the ground. The Hunt is coming at dawn with fire and hounds and Seraphine Voss, who has burned children's names off the earth, and you want me to stand at that ford and *consult* them? Ask the third rank how they *feel* about holding a line?"

"I want you to say their names!"

"THE NAMES DON'T WARM THEM."

The shout came out of Wren like something torn, and the fire guttered, and outside — Wren heard it, felt it through the brand's new quiet — four hundred and six dead heads turned toward the barn at the sound of their commander's voice raised, waiting for an order, *any* order, and that was the worst thing yet, that they waited, that they were *ready* to be aimed—

Freya had gone white. "There he is," she said, barely above a whisper. "There's the oath-keeper. I kept thinking the vow burned out of you. It didn't. It just changed what it was made of."

"That's not—"

"You ordered them like entries." She snatched the ledger off the table and held it up, and her hand was steady now, deadly steady. "This is what a name is for. This is what I *do.* And you—" she flipped it open to the page, to Ossian Vael, ferryman— "you've turned the whole country into your ledger, and every entry is *hold the ford,* and you don't read them anymore, do you? You don't read the names. You just *spend* them."

"I am keeping you alive."

"No." Her voice dropped, and it was worse than the shouting. "You're keeping yourself from feeling it. I've watched you for weeks, Wren. The vow punished you for every kindness and it kept you *human.* Pain was the price of your conscience and you paid it, every day, for eleven years. Now the pain's gone and so is the paying, and what's left spends the dead like coin because coin doesn't bleed." She stepped closer. The bone charm at her throat had begun, very faintly, to murmur — her mother, restless, hearing the cold come off Wren's skin. "When did you last feel your feet?"

Wren said nothing.

"When did you last *feel,* Wren?"

"I feel," Wren said, "what is *necessary.*"

Freya closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet and furious and more afraid than Wren had ever seen them — not of the Hunt, not of the winter, of *Wren.* "Ossian Vael held your ford all night," she said, "and then he walked out of the stream, against your order, and came to the edge of the camp, and stood there, and asked me to tell you something. Do you want to know what a dead ferryman wanted me to tell you?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, snow began to fall — falling upward, the wrong way, the old border pressing close.

"He said: *we will hold any ford you ask. We died for this country once and we will do it again. But commander — ask us while we still remember why.*"

Freya pressed the ledger into Wren's hands, closed Wren's fingers over it with both of her warm ones, and held them there.

"Read them their names," she said. "Tonight. Before dawn. Before you spend them. Because I swear to you, Wren Halvard — if you give one more order to a name you won't say, I will walk out of this camp, and I will take the ledger with me, and you can hold your ford with *nothing.*"

She let go. She went back to the table. She picked up her knife.

And outside, at the edge of the firelight, at the boundary where the snow lay unmelted, the dead of Vhalen stood in their silent ranks — four hundred and six of them, Wren counted, *four hundred and six,* and the counting hurt, and the hurt felt like the first warm thing in days — waiting to hear whether their commander still knew who they were.

Wren opened the ledger.

And the first horn of the Hunt sounded in the birch stand, hours early.
