## 24. The Guildmaster's Last Entry

They brought him in at dusk, and Freya knew before she saw his face.

She knew it the way she knew all things that had once been written down — by the weight of them arriving, by the particular hush a room makes when something unavoidable crosses its threshold. Two of Vhalen's dead carried him between them on a door torn from its hinges, and they laid him down in the frozen orchard where the pyres' glow turned every bare tree to a brand, and Freya stood at the edge of the firelight and could not make her legs move.

Master Corvane Aldery. Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Trades. The man who had taught her double-entry bookkeeping and the proper temperature for curing bone, who had paid her wages on time for nine years, who had looked her in the eye not four hours after her charm spoke her mother's name aloud and said, with an expression she had mistook for grief, *Run, girl, run now* — and then sold her within the hour.

He was dying. She didn't need her gift to know it; the dead carrying him told her without words, their hollow faces angled downward in what she had come to recognize as respect. But her gift confirmed it anyway, whispering up from the bone at her throat in a voice that was not quite her mother's anymore — the general had seen to that — but still familiar enough to hurt:

*Corvane Aldery. Three days.*

"Freya." His voice was a ruin. The Hunt had questioned him — she could see the shape of that work in the way he held his jaw, in the fingers that ended too short, in the burn scars climbing his forearms like ivy. They had asked him where the ledger was, and he hadn't told them, and this was what honesty cost in Corvane's country now. "Come here. I didn't carry myself across a stitched border to talk to you from thirty feet."

Her legs obeyed before the rest of her agreed to. That was the trouble with bodies: they kept their own ledgers, and some debts of habit ran deeper than betrayal.

She knelt in the snow beside him. Up close he smelled of smoke and old ink, the same as he had in the guildhouse on ordinary mornings, and that sameness made her furious. Men who did what he had done should smell different afterward. They should smell like what they were.

"You're supposed to be in Corvane," she said. "Safe. Counting something."

"I was safe." He smiled, and there was blood in it. "That was the problem. I've spent sixty years being safe, girl. I thought I'd spend three days being useful instead."

* * *

The story came out of him in pieces, the way stories do when the teller is rationing his breath.

The Hunt had taken the guildhouse on the second day after her arrest. Not raided — *taken*, formally, with writs and wax seals and a clerk who catalogued the inventory while oath-hounds paced the aisles sniffing for promises. Seraphine Voss herself had walked the archive floor, running her gloved finger along the spines of the trade ledgers — bottled grief, deferred debts, years purchased and sold — and Master Corvane had stood with his hands folded like a man at his own funeral, because that was more or less what it was.

"She wasn't looking for contraband," he said. Snow gathered in his grey hair and did not melt. "She was looking for the other ledger. Yours."

"The name-ledger." Freya's hand went to the bone box at her hip without her permission. It had traveled with her through everything — the wagons, the river, the upward snow, the drowned streets of Vhalen — and every name inside it had been carved by her hand so that it could not be unmade. "I never told anyone about it. Not even you."

"No," he agreed. "You didn't. But I'm the guildmaster, child. I've watched you cure bone at your desk for nine years and pretend your hands weren't moving. I've heard you humming names under your breath when you thought no one listened. You think a man who trades in bottled grief doesn't learn to recognize someone bottling their own?" He coughed, wet and long, and one of the dead attendants leaned close with a cloth, and Corvane waved them off with the stubbornness of a man who resented every kindness that cost someone else effort. "I knew. I knew for eight of those nine years. And I said nothing, because your gift was the single most valuable asset this guild ever possessed, and because — " his mouth twisted around the word — "because I told myself it was safer for you if I pretended not to see."

"That's a lie," Freya said quietly.

"Yes," he said. "It's a lie. The truth is I wanted the ledger."

The orchard crackled around them. Somewhere east, past the black trees, the Hunt's fires burned in ranks, and the wind coming off them carried voices — dozens of them, hundreds, the newly dead of the pyres all crying the same word into the dark. *Vhalen. Vhalen. Vhalen.* Freya had learned to hold those voices at arm's length, the way you hold a hot pan. She held them at arm's length now.

"When the crown announced the Hunt," Corvane went on, "the heralds read the decree off a page. You remember. But decrees are summaries, girl. The full registry order listed the sources of identification. Informants. Registration rolls. And — " he met her eyes, and did not look away, and she understood suddenly that he had rehearsed this moment on the road, that he had practiced meeting her eyes because he knew he would want to flee from it — "guild ledgers. Any record demonstrating knowledge of gifted individuals. The crown called it *evidence*. Do you understand? Our books were going to become a list of the condemned whether we surrendered them or not. Every impossible trade we ever brokered, every client whose grief we bottled or whose debt we deferred — the Hunt would pull every name out of our archives and feed them to the pyres in alphabetical order."

"So you gave them mine instead."

"So I gave them yours." No flinch. He had promised himself he wouldn't flinch, and whatever else Corvane Aldery was, he kept his promises to himself. "One name against four hundred. One girl against every grieving widow and debt-ridden father who ever walked through our doors believing we were discreet." His breath rattled. "And underneath all of that — underneath the arithmetic, which I did honestly, which I stand behind even now — there was the other thing. The true thing. The ugly thing."

"Say it," Freya said. "You owe me the ugly thing."

"The ledger matters more than you do." He said it flatly, like reading a price. "Your gift can do what no seal, no oath, no army can do. It can hold a name past the death of everyone who bore it. Four hundred names scattered across the realm, and the Hunt burning people faster than the plague ever did — and in the middle of all that, one keeper with the ability to make witness permanent. I sold you to buy time for the ledger. I hid the name-ledger inside the false bottom of my own strongbox and put a decoy in its place, and when Voss's clerks seized the archives, they took three hundred pounds of worthless trade records and missed the only book in the building that mattered. Because I sent them chasing *you*."

The snow had stopped falling upward here, near the border-stitch; it fell the honest way, down and slow, settling on Corvane's shoulders like a shroud taking measurements. Freya found that she was shaking. Not with cold.

"You let them arrest me," she said. "You let them put me in a wagon bound for a pyre. You watched me — " and her voice broke, which enraged her, so she pushed through it — "you watched me carve names for nine years beside you, you ate supper across from me, and you calculated my price and paid yourself in ledgers."

"Yes." His eyes were wet now, finally, but he did not wipe them; perhaps his hands were past wiping anything. "And every night since, I have done the sum again. And every night it comes out the same. Four hundred against one. And every night I have lain awake knowing that the arithmetic is correct and that correctness has nothing — *nothing* — to do with what I owed you." He drew a breath that sounded like tearing cloth. "That's the confession, Freya. Not that I erred. That I don't regret it. I'd do it again tomorrow. And I have hated myself for both halves of that sentence with every mile between Corvane and this orchard, and neither half cancels the other, and I am dying with the whole ugly account open on the table because you deserve to see the books balanced. All of them. Even the entries that shame me."

* * *

She did not forgive him then. She wanted the record to show that.

She sat in the snow beside a dying man who had sold her life for a filing system, and she felt the forgiveness rise in her like tide and go out again like tide, three times, four, and each time she pulled it back and examined it and found it wanting — found it too fast, too cheap, forgiveness offered the way the guildmaster himself might have offered it, as a transaction, a debt cleared, an entry closed. He had taught her that closing an entry early was how errors hid. Let the account stay open. Let it hurt. Forgiveness that didn't hurt was just bookkeeping.

What she said instead was: "How did you find us?"

"A dying man hears things," Corvane said. "Also, I bribed a ferryman with forty years of my life. I'd been saving them." A ghost of the old dry humor. "Worst trade I ever brokered. Terrible rate."

"The Hunt followed you."

"The Hunt followed everyone." His gaze drifted toward the eastern fires. "That's the part I came to tell you, actually. My confession isn't even the most important cargo I carried." His hands fumbled at his collar, and the nearer of the two dead attendants helped him, drawing out a package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the red cord the guild used for priority deliveries. "Voss found the decoy ledger within a day. Of course she did; I wanted her to. But before her clerks finished cataloguing the seizure, one of my journeymen — Tam, the quiet one, you remember Tam — he took the real ledger from the strongbox and ran south, and got as far as the Marish ferry, and the hounds caught him by the water." Corvane's voice steadied itself with visible effort. "He didn't give it up. He sank it. Wrapped in this, weighted with chain, in thirty feet of brown water. The hounds couldn't smell a promise through river mud, and Voss's divers found nothing but eels. Tam is dead now. Burned. But he died with the ledger safe, and I fished it out of the Marish two weeks ago with lungs half my age, and here — "

He pushed the oilcloth package into her hands, and Freya unwrapped it with fingers gone strangely clumsy, and there it was.

Her own ledger. Her own hand. Three years of names, carved in ivory-white bone leaves, each one cured and lettered in the hours after midnight when the guildhouse slept: the burned of the first purges, the registered, the vanished. Names the Hunt had erased from every official record, preserved in material that could not be unmade. She held it and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything, and the voices in the wind seemed briefly to lean closer, as if they knew their own names were somewhere in the neighborhood.

"He kept it dry," she said foolishly. River water and oilcloth. Grief does that, puts small words in large mouths.

"He did," Corvane said. "He was a better guildsman than I ever was. He understood the trade." He paused, gathering breath for what was clearly the hardest entry of all. "Do you know why I built this guild, girl? Everyone assumes it was greed. It wasn't. My mother died owing a debt she couldn't pay, and they took my sister for it — sold her across the sea, no record, no name attached to any paper anywhere. She simply stopped existing. I was eleven. I swore I would build a place where nothing could be made to stop existing. Where every debt, every grief, every *name* got written down so hard the world couldn't lose it." He laughed, and it became a cough, and it became silence for a long moment. "And then I grew up and became a man who valued the writing-down over the people, and I couldn't even see the moment it happened. That's the trick of principles, Freya. Left unattended, they eat the thing they were meant to protect."

* * *

Near midnight, the cold came down hard — the deliberate kind, the general's kind — and the dead attendants built up the fire and Corvane's breathing shortened, and Freya knew the three days were going to be generous by several days. She sat with his head in her lap because there was nowhere softer, and because some services are rendered not out of love but out of the refusal to be the kind of person who wouldn't.

"Keep it," he said. The snow was covering him patiently, flake by flake, and he lacked the strength to object. "The ledger. Keep it. Not for me. God knows not for me. Keep it because — " his eyes found hers, clear at the very end in a way they hadn't been in years — "because I was wrong about the arithmetic. Not the sum. The units. I counted names against people. But a name kept *is* a person, Freya. It's the last of them that survives, and the first of them that comes home. You showed me that. Your ledger isn't a list of the lost. It's a census of the returning." His hand twitched toward the box at her hip and stopped, having no right further. "Four hundred names against one girl. But the girl is holding the four hundred. So the sum was never what I thought. The sum was always infinite against finite, and I bet on the wrong side, and I am telling you this with my last breath so that you never — never — make my error. When they offer you the choice between the book and the person — "

"Don't," Freya whispered.

"— choose the person," he finished. "Every time. Choose the person, and trust someone like Tam to save the book."

His breath slowed. The fire popped. Out past the orchard, the winter ground its teeth against the border-stitch, and the voices of the pyre-dead rose in their endless refrain.

Freya bent close. "Corvane Aldery," she said, and her voice was steady now, steady the way bone is steady, "your entry is not closed. I'm leaving it open. Open accounts get revisited, and I am not done being angry with you, and I am not done — " the tide rose again, fourth time, fifth, and this time she let it — "and I am not done being grateful. Both entries. Same page. In my own hand. You taught me that much, at least: never falsify a record."

He smiled. Blood in it again, but lighter somehow.

"There's my girl," he said. "Best bookkeeper I ever — "

The sentence stayed unfinished forever. Freya sat with him until the cold took the last warmth, and the dead attendants bowed their heads, and the snow did what snow does.

Then she opened the bone box, took out a fresh leaf, and cured it over the fire with hands that only shook a little.

*CORVANE ALDERY,* she carved. *Guildmaster. Sold one life to save four hundred names, and was wrong about which of them needed saving. Forgiven in installments. Account remains open.*

She read it aloud once, softly, the way she did — and felt something in the dark lean down and take the name gently, like a coin passed across a counter.

Then, far to the east, beyond the Hunt's ranked fires, a sound rolled across the frozen country that was not thunder and was not wind: the winter general's voice, wearing her mother's stolen throat, calling clearly through the storm —

*"Ledger-keeper. I know what you carry now. Bring me the book, and before this frost releases the world, you will hear her say your name again — living, warm, and mine no longer."*
