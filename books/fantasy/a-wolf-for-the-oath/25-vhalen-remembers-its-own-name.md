## 25. Vhalen Remembers Its Own Name

The first thing to come back was a street.

I was standing in what had been, an hour before, the drowned ruin of the Marrowgate district — knee-deep in black water, watching Freya read from the ledger with her breath fogging in air that shouldn't have been cold enough for fog — when the water simply *left*. It drained upward, which is the way most things left Vhalen, sheeting off the cobbles and rising into the green-black sky like rain played backward. And beneath it: a street. Whole. Intact. Cobblestones laid in the old Vhaleni herringbone pattern I hadn't seen since I was six years old and my grandmother pointed them out to me and said, *remember this, little wren, because no one else will.*

I remembered.

"Did you do that?" I asked, and my voice came out strange, too loud in the sudden quiet of a street that had been underwater since before I could walk.

Freya didn't look up from the ledger. Her lips were moving around a name — *Ostrevane, Mirelle, weaver of the second stair* — and as she spoke it, a woman assembled out of the dimness at the far end of the street, the way frost assembles on glass. Not walking into being. Condensing. One moment there was nothing at the corner by the shuttered fountain; the next there was a woman in a weaver's shawl, standing very still, looking down at her own hands with an expression I will carry to whatever grave is waiting for me.

"She remembers her hands," Freya whispered. She'd told me this before, on the first day, when the readings began. "They forget everything else first. The names bring back the rest."

Mirelle Ostrevane lifted her head. She looked at Freya. She looked at me — and something moved across her face when she saw me, some flicker of recognition or its absence, and I braced for it the way I braced every time now. *You're one of us,* that look said, or sometimes, worse, *you're one of us and you left.* But she only pressed her hand flat over her heart, the old gesture, the one they taught children in the years before the drowning, and turned, and walked up the second stair of a house that was also assembling itself around her, beam by beam, window by window, like a memory deciding to be true.

The sky lightened. I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last discipline the oath left me, and because I don't trust anything I feel in this country anymore. The sky over Marrowgate did not turn blue. It went from the color of pond ice to the color of old pewter, and in the pewter there was a suggestion — only a suggestion — of a sun behind it, the way you can sense a lamp through a bedsheet. The green-black film that had hung over Vhalen since the drowning thinned by a degree. Somewhere east, where the capital had been, I thought I heard bells. I couldn't swear to it. I wanted to hear bells so badly that I've stopped trusting myself on the subject entirely.

"It's working," Freya said. She closed the ledger on her finger to keep her place, and looked up at me, and she was smiling, and she was also gray-lipped and shaking, because every name cost her warmth and she had read forty-one names since dawn and refused, absolutely refused, to stop. "Wren. Look at it. It's *working.*"

"I see it."

"We could have the whole city back in a month. The whole *country.*"

I didn't answer, because I was looking past her shoulder, down the length of the restored street, at the place where the light ended.

Vhalen did not come back evenly. That was the thing no one had warned us about — though I suppose no one had ever done this before, un-erased a country name by name, and so there was no one to warn us. The restoration spread outward from wherever Freya stood reading, in a rough circle, and the circle had an edge, and the edge was visible. Inside it: cobblestones, walls, windows with lamplight behind them, the smell of bread from somewhere impossibly, a city remembering itself. Outside it: the drowned dark, the green-black press, streets still lying under forty years of standing water, shapes moving in the deep places that I had stopped looking at directly after the third day.

And at the edge, where the restored world met the drowned one, the cold gathered.

I had felt it first as weather. That was the trap of it — it presented as weather, as season, as the ordinary misfortune of a northern country in the wrong month. But I had grown up in the crown's service, and I knew what engineered things looked like, and this cold was engineered. It pooled at the borders of every restoration like water finding a level. When Freya read a name and another block of Vhalen surfaced, the cold drew tighter around it, patient as a debt collector, waiting for the reading to stop.

It was not waiting anymore.

* * *

We made camp that night in the upper rooms of a chandlery that had come back with the street — three floors of candles in every color the old guilds had made, wax smell thick enough to taste, and Freya said it smelled like her mother's workshop and then went quiet for a long time. Through the windows we could watch the edge of the restoration, two streets down, holding its line against the dark. The dead of the restored quarter drifted through the candlelight doing small eternal tasks: a man re-hanging a sign that kept sliding, a girl chasing a hoop that never quite reached her foot, Mirelle Ostrevane at her loom in the upstairs window, weaving cloth that unraveled behind the shuttle as fast as it formed. They weren't ghosts. I want to be clear about that, because the crown's word for them was ghosts and the crown's word was a lie dressed as mercy. They were people, paused. Waiting for their names the way a letter waits for an address.

Freya read eleven more names by candlelight. Each one, a person condensed out of the dimness, pressed a hand to their heart, and went to take up their interrupted life. Each one, Freya got a little colder. By the ninth name her hands had gone white at the knuckles and she was reading through her teeth to keep them from chattering, and I built up the fire, and she waved me away from it without looking, because the fire didn't help, because nothing helped, because the warmth wasn't leaving her body into the room — it was leaving her body into *them*, into each restored name, paid out like coin from a purse that did not refill.

"You have to stop," I said, after the eleventh.

"One more."

"You said that four names ago."

"And it was true four times." She wet her thumb and turned the page. Bone-pale paper, ink that shimmered faintly, her own careful hand marching down the margin in columns — she had begun keeping two ledgers, she'd told me, the outer one for the Hunt's erasures and this inner one for the restorations, debit and credit, witness and return. "There's a cluster here. Three children from the same house. If I stop now they wait another night alone in the—"

"In the dark. I know." I sat down across from her. My brand ached, low and constant, the way it had ached since the border-stitch took its hidden price — not the flaying agony of the broken vow anymore, but a deeper soreness, like a bone bruised in a place I couldn't reach. "Freya. Look at your fingers."

She looked. The nails had gone the blue-gray of deep water. She curled them into her palm and kept reading.

So I did the only thing I could do, which was the thing I had discovered on the riverbank with the hounds closing in: I put my hand over hers. The brand quieted under her touch — that was the direction everyone noticed, her gift soothing my wound — but it ran both ways, and fewer people would have guessed that. Whatever the oath-magic had burned into me, it answered her. Heat moved. Not much. Enough. Her voice steadied on the twelfth name, and the thirteenth, and a boy of perhaps eight condensed onto the chandlery stairs and looked at his own small hands and laughed, actually laughed, high and delighted, and ran up into the dark to find whoever he'd been running toward when the water came.

Freya slumped against the table. "Three children," she murmured. "Same house. Told you."

"I know."

"You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you're counting something I don't want counted." She tipped her head back and looked at me upside down, eyes fever-bright with exhaustion. "Say it, Wren. You always say it eventually. Save us the hour."

I should have lied. There was a version of me, even a month ago, who would have lied — the oath-keeper who arrested her on the road to the pyre-city, who told her the wagons went to registration, who learned in one white-hot instant of the vow recoiling that the oath punished lies with pain but never once punished silence. Silence was free. Silence was the one currency I still had.

But she had put her hand in mine, and the brand was warm, and somewhere below us a boy was laughing in a house that had been underwater for forty years.

"The seal," I said. "Every name you restore weakens it. And the winter isn't weather, Freya. It's a will. And every name you give back feeds it."

* * *

I had worked it out over three nights of not sleeping, and I laid it out for her the way I'd once laid out audits for the guildmaster: evidence first, conclusion last, no adjectives. It was the only way I could say it without my voice breaking, and I am not proud of how much effort that took.

"When Seraphine Voss told us the pyres powered the seal, she told us half of it," I said. "The pyres feed the seal — burning the gifted, burning their names, feeding the stitch that holds Vhalen shut. Fine. But the seal isn't just a wall. A wall keeps things out. This keeps a whole country *down*. And something has to operate it. Something has to hold all that cold and aim it. The general."

"A general made of winter," Freya said. "Your words."

"My words. And here's what I've pieced together since. The seal and the general are the same machine. The crown didn't build a lid and hire a jailer. They built one thing — a cold that presses Vhalen shut — and the pyres are its fuel, and the erased names are its mortar. Every name the Hunt burns makes the mortar thicker." I turned my hand over, showed her the brand, the pale scar of the oath I'd broken and the darker thing underneath it that the keystone's business had awakened. "Now reverse it. Every name you *restore* takes mortar out of the wall. The seal gets thinner. And the general feels it — feels itself weakening — and it does what any besieged garrison does."

"Calls for reinforcements."

"Worse. It spends everything it has where the wall is thinnest." I nodded toward the window, toward the visible edge of the restoration two streets down, where the candlelight met the drowned dark. "That's why the cold pools at your edges. It's not resisting you randomly. It's counterattacking. You give back a name, the seal loosens, and the general pours cold into the gap to re-freeze it. You're in a siege, Freya. You just don't have a fortress. You have a ledger."

She was quiet for a long moment. In the window across the street, Mirelle Ostrevane's loom clacked its patient, unraveling rhythm.

"How much thinner," she said finally. "Can you tell? The seal."

I could. That was the part I hated most — that I could, that the brand had tuned me to the stitch-work the way a tuning fork answers its own note. "When we crossed, it was like pressing through wet canvas. Yesterday it was wet silk. Tonight—" I flexed the scarred arm, feeling for it, that vast slow pressure at the rim of the world, the lid on the drowned country. "Tonight it's a soap bubble. Still whole. Still holding. But I can feel places where it's gone thin enough to see through."

"See through to what?"

"To the other side. To everywhere the seal is supposed to be keeping apart." I hesitated, and then gave her the rest, because she'd asked and because she always knew when I withheld. "And the general knows I can feel it. Two nights ago the cold found our camp and it didn't touch you. It circled *me*. It pressed on the brand for an hour. Like it was reading me. Like I was a door it was considering walking through."

Freya sat up fully at that. "Wren—"

"I'm fine."

"You're not, and also that's not the point. If the general can use the brand—"

"It hasn't yet."

"*Yet.*" She took my scarred hand in both of hers, and the brand quieted, and for a moment neither of us said anything, and the candles burned, and downstairs a man re-hung his sign for the ten-thousandth time. "Then we go faster," she said. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it? Every name weakens the seal and strengthens the general, but only while the seal exists at all. If I finish the ledger — if every name comes home — there's nothing left to seal. No mortar. No wall. The general can spend all the cold it wants and there'll be no wall to defend."

"That's the wager. Yes."

"And if you lose the wager?"

"Then the general breaks through long before the last name is read, and everything between here and the border freezes, and it starts with us."

She nodded slowly, the way she did over a balanced column, and I understood suddenly that she had already done this math herself, days ago, and had been waiting for me to catch up — that my whole careful audit was me arriving late at a conclusion she'd made peace with alone, in the dark, while I slept. That was the worst part of loving someone who kept ledgers in their bones. They always knew the numbers before you did.

"Then we don't stop," she said. "And we don't slow down. We go *toward* it. Toward the thin place. Toward the general."

"Why would we—"

"Because a siege ends one of two ways, Wren. Either the wall holds, or the besiegers run out of siege." She smiled, gray-lipped and terrible and bright. "I want to know which."

* * *

We broke camp before dawn, and that was when the sky finished changing.

I stepped out of the chandlery into the pewter light and stopped so abruptly that Freya walked into my back. Over the eastern districts — over the direction of the capital, of the palace where I had been sworn as a child, of the great stitched seam where Vhalen had been cut from the map — the green-black film had torn. Not thinned. *Torn*, along a line miles long, and through the tear the light came down like something poured: not sunlight, nothing so innocent, but the old Vhaleni dawn-color, the amber-rose that the stained glass in my grandmother's stories had caught, the light of a country that had been told for forty years that it was a hole in the world.

Through the tear, faintly, came the sound of bells. This time I didn't doubt it. Half the restored quarter had come out into the street, the paused people, faces tilted up, and Mirelle Ostrevane stood in her doorway with her shuttle forgotten in her hand, and an old man near the fountain was weeping without any noise at all, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing to say that the light wasn't already saying.

"They can feel it," Freya whispered. "The ones still under. The ones we haven't read yet. They can feel the seal thinning." Her hand found mine. Her fingers were freezing. "Wren. They're pushing."

And then the light guttered.

It happened fast — faster than weather, faster than anything honest. The tear in the sky shrank from both ends like a wound closing, the amber-rose light cut off mid-fall, and the cold came down the streets ahead of us in a visible wave, frosting the cobbles, snuffing candles in windows, sheathing the fountain in white. The paused people didn't scream. That was somehow the worst of it. They simply folded back into dimness, all down the street at once, Mirelle Ostrevane last of all, her hand still raised toward the vanished light, and then the frost reached us and my breath tore white and the brand ignited along its whole length with a pain so clean and total that I went to one knee on the ice.

In the frost on the cobbles, in front of me, letters formed. Not written. *Condensed*, the way the dead condensed, the way the street had — the cold assembling meaning out of itself, in a hand I recognized, because I had seen it on warrant after warrant after warrant:

**STOP READING THE NAMES AND I WILL LET THE LIGHT STAY.**

Below it, smaller, almost gentle:

**CONTINUE, AND I WILL SHOW YOU WHAT A COUNTRY LOOKS LIKE WHEN IT FREEZES MID-BREATH.**

The letters held for three heartbeats. Then they sublimated upward, snow drifting the wrong way, and the green-black film sealed over the tear in the sky, and somewhere far to the east, faint and furious and unmistakable, the bells rang on — ringing, I understood at last, not in celebration.

Ringing in alarm.
