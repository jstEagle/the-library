## 26. The Choice the Dead Offered

Freya had learned to measure her warmth in names.

Seventeen since dawn. That was how many she had read aloud from the ledger before her fingers went stiff as the bone they shaped, before Wren pressed a tin cup of broth into her hands and stood over her until she drank it, watching the steam rise off her lips like a small departing spirit. Seventeen names, and for each one a person in Vhalen had remembered themselves — had turned from their long gray waiting like flowers finding the sun, had wept or laughed or simply said *thank you* in voices that hadn't been used since the water came.

Seventeen names, and seventeen small deaths of heat drawn out of her body, pooled somewhere beneath her ribs now like swallowed river stones.

She sat in the shell of what had once been a countinghouse — because of course it had; Vhalen had kept ledgers too, and the irony was not lost on her — on a street called the Sallow Mile, in a city whose name she knew but had not yet read back to it, so the city wandered its own avenues half-remembering, a dog circling a gate it could not recall how to open. Green-black light fell through a hole in the roof. The sky above Vhalen did not behave; it hung low and luminous the way pond ice does, and sometimes things moved behind it, vast and slow, like shadows of fish seen from underneath.

The waiting dead gathered at the edges of the room. They always did. Not crowding — they were too courteous for that, too old-mannered, a whole country's worth of manners pickled in the drowning — but assembling, the way congregants assemble before a service. A woman with kelp braided into her hair. A boy holding the rotted ghost of a wooden horse. An old man whose uniform belonged to no army Freya knew, buttoned to the throat against a cold that no longer mattered to him.

They came when she read. They came now because they could feel her deciding.

*That's the trouble with gifts,* her mother used to say, paring an apple in one unbroken spiral. *People can always tell when you're about to give them away.*

Freya opened the ledger on the countinghouse desk. Her own hand looked back at her from every page, names carved in tiny sure strokes into slips of bone bound edge to edge with sinew — a book that could not burn, could not be unwritten, could not be seized and entered into evidence. Every name the Hunt erased, she had kept. Every name the drowning had taken, she was carrying home.

"Viorica Ansel," she read, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Of the Sallow Mile. Seamstress. Mended sails in the last year of the war though she was never paid for them, and never asked to be."

The woman with the kelp braid stepped forward, and the gray film over her eyes thinned, and beneath it something kindled — not life, exactly, nothing so generous, but *recognition*. She remembered being Viorica Ansel. She straightened her spine the way a seamstress does after decades at the frame, and she said, "The sails," in a voice like water finding an old channel, and then she wept, which the dead of Vhalen could do without any salt left to spare.

Freya felt the warmth leave her. It went gently this time, the way blood goes when you've cut yourself on purpose — a loosening, a letting-down, cold spreading up from her fingertips into her wrists. She gripped the desk's edge until it passed.

*Sixteen more today,* she calculated, *and then I stop, and sleep, and tomorrow—*

"You will kill yourself at this pace."

Wren, from the doorway. They had a way of standing in doorways as if the doorway had invited them personally and would be offended if they didn't accept — some leftover courtesy of the oath-keeper's training that survived even now, oathless and branded and exiled from everything they'd been. Their broken brand caught the green light: the shattered sigil burned up their forearm in scarred filigree, quiet these last days, almost dormant, like a banked fire.

"I'll go slower," Freya said.

"You said that three days ago. You were reading names in your sleep that night. Do you know that? You sat up with your eyes open and read forty names to an empty room, and every candle in the house guttered, and I couldn't wake you." Wren crossed the room and put their hand flat on the ledger, not quite touching her fingers. Their hand was warm. It was the only warm thing in Vhalen besides her. "I counted your breaths, Freya. They were wrong."

"The dead waited two hundred years. They can wait while I eat breakfast."

"They are not the ones who can't wait, and you know it." Wren's voice stayed level, which was how Freya knew they were frightened; Wren shouted when they were merely angry. Outside, far off toward the stitched border, the light pulsed once, white and cold, like a heartbeat made of frost. The long winter, leaning its weight against the door. Against *her* door, now, in whatever sense the door had begun to have her in it. "The general is spending everything. Villages on our side of the stitch are freezing mid-motion — you saw the ferryman. Seraphine's seal is cracking, and when it cracks all at once—"

"Then I'll read faster."

"And be a corpse with a warm book in its hands?"

"Yes," Freya said, "if that's what it costs to hand people back their own names before the cold takes the chance away," and she heard her mother's apple-parer pause in memory, heard the old voice say, *there she is,* and hated herself a little for the pride she felt saying it.

Wren held her gaze for a long moment. Then they did the thing that undid her worse than argument ever could: they sat down across from her, folded their hands, and waited. Oath-keepers were trained to wait. Somewhere under the brand and the exile and the flayed remains of their vow, that training still lived, patient as a hound.

* * *

It was the old soldier who spoke first.

He came forward when the seamstress had finished her weeping, when Wren had given up waiting for an answer and begun instead building up the fire — a real fire, fed on driftwood from the drowned harbor that burned green and gave little heat, but Wren fed it anyway, on principle, the way other people prayed. The old man stopped at the edge of the desk and saluted, heel-click and all, a gesture two centuries out of date and performed without a flicker of self-consciousness.

"Ledger-keeper," he said. His voice was dry reeds. "You should hear what we've brought you."

"I've heard what you bring me every morning," Freya said. "Names. I'm grateful for every one."

"Not those." He glanced at the others at the walls' edges, and they nodded, all of them together, a slow tide-motion, and Freya felt the room change temperature in a way that had nothing to do with fire. "Something older. Something we have argued about among ourselves for two hundred years, ledger-keeper, in the dark under the water, while our country walked around us forgetting itself. We argued, and we voted, and I will not pretend the vote was unanimous, because nothing honest ever is. But it was decided. And we chose you to hear it."

The boy with the wooden horse came to stand by the old man's side. The kelp-braided woman took his other hand. One by one they assembled, dozens of them, the ones Freya had restored and hundreds she had not, filling the ruined countinghouse shoulder to shoulder, and the green light through the roof dimmed as if the sky itself leaned down to listen.

"Read us the city," the old man said. "Not the people. Just the city. Its name, and what it was."

Freya hesitated. Then she turned to the first page of the ledger's second gathering, where she'd copied, in her own careful hand, the names of places from the guildmaster's hidden maps — Vhalen's cities, towns, rivers, roads, salvaged from a country cut out of the world's memory. She found the entry. She read it aloud.

The name of the city moved through the room like weather.

Every dead soul straightened. Walls that had sagged for two hundred years seemed to remember their plumb line; somewhere outside, Freya heard stone grind against stone, a street settling itself back into true. The green-black sky overhead thinned by one shade, and through it, briefly, came the sound of gulls — gulls that had not existed here since the drowning, calling across water that was remembering it had once been harbor.

And warmth drained out of Freya in a single great gulp, so sudden that the room tilted and Wren's arm was around her waist before she finished falling, hauling her upright, snarling at the assembly in a voice gone ragged: "*Stop it, stop, she can't—*"

"It cost more," Freya gasped, gripping the desk, seeing white at the edges of everything. "Places cost more than people. Why—"

"Because places hold more names," the old soldier said, gentle and terrible. "A person is one name, ledger-keeper. A city is everyone who ever lived in it. Now you understand the arithmetic you've been doing with your own body. And now you understand why we must tell you what we decided in the dark."

He drew himself up. Behind him, the whole gray congregation drew itself up with him, and Freya understood suddenly that she was not in a room full of petitioners. She was in a room full of ambassadors. Two hundred years of a drowned nation's deliberation stood before her wearing kelp and rot and old uniforms, and it had come to make her an offer.

"The crown built a seal on our door," the old man said. "The Huntress built it finer than the crown knew — she wove it from pyre-smoke and stolen names, and it would have held forever, except that seals are made of rules, and rules can be met. The seal closes if the door is unused. The seal holds if no one passes. But the seal cannot close—" he paused, and the whole congregation paused with him, breathing their unnecessary breaths, "—on a living door."

The room went very quiet. Even the fire's green flames bent still.

"We are not alive," the woman with the kelp braid said, softly, to Freya alone, the way one woman tells another a hard truth in a market. "But you are. You, who carry our names in bone. You, who give them back with your own warmth. You are already half a door, child. You have been since the day you first carved a whisper into ivory and listened to it answer. All we offer is the rest of the work."

"Take us in," the old soldier said. "All of us. Every name of Vhalen — not the thousands you've gathered, but all of them, every soul the water took, every name the map erased, the complete census of a murdered country, carried nowhere but in you. Become the ledger entire. Become the door itself — living, passing, used, unclosable. No seal can shut what is flesh and walking. No winter can freeze what carries its own fire. The general would spend itself against you and break like surf."

Freya's hands had gone numb on the desk. She wasn't sure anymore whether it was spent warmth or terror.

"And me?" she heard herself ask. Her voice sounded very young. "What happens to me?"

The congregation stirred — not in refusal, but in the discomfort of honest people about to be honest.

"You remain," the old man said. "That is the word for it. You remain. You would hear every voice of Vhalen as clearly as your own — every grief, every song, every argument, forever, all of it carried in the same skull where your own thoughts live. You would walk and speak and love as Freya Delacroix, and none of that would be taken from you. But you would never again be *only* Freya Delacroix. There would be no hour of any day in which you were alone inside yourself. A country's dead would dream beside you while you slept. When you loved someone—" his gray eyes flicked, deliberate and unashamed, to Wren, "—you would love them with two hundred years of witnesses at your shoulder. Some of us sing in our sleep. We would try to be considerate. We cannot promise."

Someone in the crowd — the boy, gods, the *boy* — laughed, high and startled, and the laugh broke something loose in the room, and then the whole congregation was talking, murmuring, pressing forward a half-step, and Freya understood that they had argued about this for two centuries precisely because they knew what they were asking. That the vote had been close. That some of them thought it monstrous to ask. That they asked anyway, because the alternative was the seal, or the winter, or the pyres, and a drowned country that had already lost everything had decided it could bear being monstrous one more time if the monster it asked was kind enough to say yes.

"Refuse us and we will not blame you," the kelp-braided woman said. "We will follow you anyway. We will love you anyway. That is also part of what we decided."

*You will never again be only yourself.*

Freya thought of the guildhall in Corvane. Of balancing bottled grief against debts paid in years, of hiding her gift behind ink and immaculate columns, of a lifetime spent making herself small and legible and *only herself*, because only herself was safe, only herself was invisible, only herself could pass the audit. She had spent her whole life being only Freya Delacroix, and the Hunt had come for her regardless. The erasure had come regardless. There was no version of the arithmetic where being small had saved her.

She thought of her mother's name, still unread, still waiting in the ledger's last pages, the one entry she could not bring herself to perform because afterward there would be nothing left to save for.

She thought of Wren's hand, warm, flat on the ledger, not quite touching hers.

"I need time," Freya said.

"You may have until the winter finishes falling," the old soldier answered, and there was infinite sorrow in it, and no apology at all. "That is the one thing we cannot give you more of."

* * *

Afterward — she could not have said how long afterward — she stood in the roofless countinghouse with Wren, in the wreckage of the fire, and the dead had withdrawn to the street's ends, courteous again, watchful, giving the living room to breathe.

Wren hadn't spoken since the congregation left. They stood at the broken window frame looking out at Vhalen's wandering streets, at the harbor water running backward in the distance, at the white pulse on the horizon where the long winter gnawed at the border-stitch, and Freya watched the lamplight of the green fire move on their face and tried to read them the way she read bone.

"You're going to tell me not to do it," she said.

"No." Wren turned. Their eyes were wet, which was rare; Wren rationed their tears like a besieged city rations grain. "I'm going to tell you what I know, and then I'm going to stand wherever you decide, because that's what I'm for now. What I know is this: I was sworn to the crown as a child, and the vow ate me alive, and when it finally burned out of me I discovered I didn't know who was left underneath. So I know what it costs to let something enormous live inside you and call it duty. I know what it hollows." They crossed the room. Took her frozen hands in their warm ones, chafing them, angry-gentle, an oath-keeper's hands built for seals and swords and now reduced — elevated — to warming a carver's fingers. "But I also know that you are not me. You've never hidden from what you are the way I hid from what I was. You just hid it from other people. Maybe for you the country fits. Maybe you've got room. I genuinely don't know, and I won't pretend to."

"Wren—"

"But I want one thing on the record, ledger-keeper, before you answer them." Their grip tightened. The brand on their forearm flared once, faintly, as if even the broken remnant of the old vow wanted to witness this. "If you do it — if you take all of them in — I want to know whether *I* will still be speaking to *you*. All of you. Whether the woman who argues with me about breakfast will still be in there behind two hundred years of choir, or whether I'll be having this conversation with a committee for the rest of my life."

"That's my question too."

"I know it is. Which is why I'm asking it badly, so you'll have the dignity of asking it well."

Freya laughed — a cracked, surprised sound, the first laughter Vhalen had heard from her — and it echoed strangely off the drowned streets, and far down the Sallow Mile the waiting dead stirred at the sound like grass at wind.

She was still holding Wren's hands, still laughing or nearly, when she felt it: a new voice arriving at the edge of her hearing. Not one of Vhalen's dead — she knew their timbre now, the waterlogged patience of them. This voice came thin and cold across the intervening miles, riding the pulse of the distant winter like a note carried on a wire.

Her mother's voice. Stolen, weaponized, worn by something entombed in the seal — the general, speaking through the only mouth Freya had ever trusted.

*"Ledger-keeper,"* it said, gentle as snowfall, *"before you answer the dead — I have something of yours. Come to the border, and I will let you hear how your mother truly laughed."*

The laughter died in Freya's throat.

Wren went still beside her. "Freya. What is it. What did it say."

She looked out past the harbor, past the backward-running water, to where the horizon pulsed white — and found, horribly, that some traitorous chamber of her heart had already begun to walk.
