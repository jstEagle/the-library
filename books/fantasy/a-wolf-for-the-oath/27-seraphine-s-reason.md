## 27. Seraphine's Reason

She came alone.

That was the first thing wrong with it. Huntmaster Seraphine Voss did not come alone to anything — not to parleys, not to executions, not to the reading of a sentence. She came with hounds and banners and a retinue of oath-keepers whose brands glowed like coals under their sleeves. But when I woke on the cold stone of the orchard house, my wrists bound in silk instead of iron — silk, which does not conduct an oath's punishment, someone had thought about that — she was sitting across from me in a plain grey traveling coat, her hands folded, her hounds waiting outside like well-trained grief.

"Good," she said. "You're awake. I'd rather you heard this standing, but sleeping will do."

I tested the bonds out of habit. They gave exactly enough to remind me they were there. "If you wanted me dead, you'd have done it while I slept."

"I don't want you dead, Halvard." She said my name the way one reads a ledger entry. "Dead is easy. Dead is what everyone else gets. I want you *convinced*."

"Then you've wasted a journey."

"Perhaps." She rose, and for the first time I saw how tired she was — not the tiredness of the road but of years, decades maybe, worn down to the grain the way a stair is worn by feet. "Or perhaps I'm about to show you the only thing that has ever convinced anyone of anything."

"And what's that?"

"The truth about your country."

My country. The word landed in me like a hook finding flesh. I had confessed it to Freya weeks ago, in the dark, in the space between one breath and the next: I was born in Vhalen, before it drowned, before the map forgot it. I had been four years old. I remembered green water and a bell that rang from beneath it, and nothing else — the crown had been generous with forgetting, in those days. They let refugee children forget. It was cheaper than feeding their memories.

"You'll show me nothing," I said. "You'll tell me something, and it will be shaped like a knife, and you'll expect me to hand you Freya Delacroix because the blade has a pretty handle."

Voss smiled without warmth. "You've spent too long around bookkeepers. Everything sounds like a trade." She drew something from her coat — not a weapon, not a writ. A bone. Small, yellowed, carved so finely the figures seemed to move when the light crossed them. "Do you know what this is?"

"A bone."

"It's a door-key. One of nine hundred and forty-one recovered from Vhalen before the fall. Each one holds a name. Each name holds a person. And each person—" she turned it in her fingers, letting the light catch the grooves, "—was put into the bone *willingly*. That's the part no one tells you. The drowned country didn't take its dead. It asked for them. And they went."

The orchard outside the window stood frozen mid-rot, apples hanging like fists. Somewhere far off, the long winter ground against the world's edges. I kept my voice level. "People have always given themselves to gods. It doesn't make the gods kind."

"No," Voss agreed. "It doesn't. Which is why you should see what the god ate."

* * *

She took me through the stitch herself.

Not through the door — through the seam of it, the place where the border had been sewn shut with oath-magic older than either of us, where the world puckered like skin over a wound. She pressed her palm flat against the air and spoke three words I did not know, and the seam opened just wide enough for two people who were willing to be smaller than they were.

On the other side, Vhalen waited under its green-black sky, and I made myself look at it properly for the first time.

Freya had described it to me once, in the wagon, in the voice she used for things she couldn't stop seeing: cities intact under drowned light, streets walking, people neither living nor dead. What she hadn't managed to convey was the *patience* of it. The whole country had the quality of a held breath. Windows stood open. Tables were set. Laundry hung on lines that had not moved in sixty years. And everywhere, standing in doorways and gardens and market squares, the waiting ones — faces turned toward us, or toward nothing, or toward some interior country of their own, wearing expressions I could only call courteous. They were waiting to be named back. They had been waiting since before I was born.

"This way," Voss said, and led me not toward the city but away from it, up a hill of black grass to a structure I had taken for a monument.

It was a wall. Or the bones of one — a great arc of stone and ivory, taller than any cathedral spire, and set into it, rank upon rank upon rank, were niches. And in the niches were bones. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Carved bones, each one labeled, each one humming faintly with the sound of a held name.

"The Door," Voss said. "The original. Not the little crack Freya found — the true gate, the one the crown sealed, the one my seal feeds. Do you know what it is?"

"A grave."

"It's a *mouth*, Halvard." Her voice stayed level, but something moved behind it, deep down, like a fish under ice. "Look at the niches. Look at the dates."

I looked. The carvings beside each niche bore names, and under the names, places of origin. And the places were wrong. Not Vhalen. Not the drowned country at all. *Corvane. Mereth. The Salt Marches. The Weald.* Neighboring lands. Foreign names. Names of people who had never set foot in Vhalen, filed into its wall like grain into a granary.

"They weren't Vhalen's dead," I said slowly.

"No."

"These are—"

"Everyone else's." Voss walked along the wall, trailing her fingers an inch from the bones, never quite touching. "For six hundred years, Vhalen was not a country. It was a throat. Its priests — they called them tide-speakers — taught that death was a tax owed to the Door, and that the Door preferred foreign coin. So Vhalen traded. Grain for corpses. Peace for corpses. They would lift a siege if a town delivered its plague-dead to the border. They would end a famine, send rain, break a winter — all of it priced in bones. Whole villages sold their grandparents to keep their children fed, and the tide-speakers called it mercy, because the dead 'went somewhere better.' Went *here*." She stopped before a niche near the top, small enough to hold a child's remains, and her hand finally closed into a fist. "Went into a wall. Forever. To feed a mouth that never said thank you."

The green sky pressed down. Somewhere below us, in the patient streets, the waiting ones stood in their doorways, and for the first time I saw their courtesy differently — not patience but *habit*, the stillness of things that had learned no one was coming.

"My mother," I said. My voice came out strange. "Was she—"

"In the wall? No." Voss turned to face me. "Your mother ran. That's why you were allowed to live, and allowed to forget. The crown's bargain with the survivors was simple: forget Vhalen, and we will not ask what you knew. Most of them took it gladly. Some of them—" she paused, and I watched her choose her words with the care of a woman defusing something, "—some of them had helped fill the wall. Forgetting was a kindness to them too."

The cold went through me then, the real cold, the one that doesn't come from weather. I thought of my mother's face, which I could not remember. I thought of her hands, which I could not remember. I thought of the shape of a woman running from a country with her child under one arm and, perhaps, the memory of a price list under her heart.

"You're lying," I said, because someone had to say it.

"Am I?" Voss unclasped something at her throat — a locket, old, dented, military-issue from a war two generations gone. Inside it, a miniature portrait: a young woman with dark hair and a face I recognized with a violence that dropped me to one knee on the black grass.

Because it was my mother's face. Because it was also Seraphine Voss's face, thirty years younger.

"She was my sister," Voss said quietly. "Which makes you the only family I have left, and the reason I will stand here in the mouth of everything I hate and explain myself to a traitor. Sit down, Wren. You'll want to be sitting."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way soldiers report casualties, and somehow that was worse than any performance could have been.

Her sister — my mother — had been a tide-speaker's daughter. Had grown up counting the deliveries, learning the ledgers of the dead the way Freya learned hers. Had believed, as children believe whatever they are fed, that the Door was holy and the trade was mercy. And then, at nineteen, she had been assigned to the receiving stations beyond the border, and she had seen what arrived there: not the willing dead of pious villages but the *purchased* dead — the murdered, the enslaved, the inconvenient, shipped to Vhalen by kings who found it cheaper than burying them. She had watched a warlord pay for the end of his campaign with four thousand prisoners, alive when the wagons left, and she had understood, all at once and forever, what the mouth was for.

"She tried to stop it from inside," Voss said. "For eleven years. Sabotage, theft, testimony. No one listened, because the neighbors' peace depended on the trade, and Vhalen's power depended on the neighbors' dependence. In the end she ran with the only thing she could carry — a child." Her eyes rested on me. "You."

"And the crown hunted her anyway."

"The crown erased her. There's a difference. Erasure is cleaner." Voss closed the locket. "And then the Door grew hungry. That's the part your Freya has half-guessed from her screaming bones, and I will give her the credit — she reads evidence better than any inquisitor I've trained. The Door was never satisfied. Six centuries of tribute and it wanted more, and when the trade routes faltered — wars, plagues, simple human stubbornness — it began taking from *inside*. Vhalen's own dead. Then Vhalen's own living. The drowning wasn't a flood, Halvard. It was the Door eating its own country, from the borders inward, until there was nothing left to eat and the whole nation slid through the gate at once. Every soul in Vhalen is in that wall or behind that door. Nobody drowned. Everybody was *swallowed*."

I sat on the black grass with my mother's face in a stranger's locket and felt the architecture of my life rearrange itself around a new load-bearing horror. "So you built the seal."

"So I built the seal." She knelt across from me, close enough that I could see the fine scarring at her hairline — brand-scars, older and deeper than mine, the record of oaths sworn so hard they'd marked the bone. "I was there at the closing. I carried stones. I burned my own name off every register so that no future Hunt could find me and undo my work, and then I spent thirty years building the thing I'd burned my name to protect. The pyres you despise — every gifted soul the Hunt burns — their gift goes into the seal. Their fire keeps the Door shut. I have sent four hundred people into that flame, Wren, and I have attended every single burning, and I remember every name, because someone has to, and I am the only one left who understands what the alternative is."

"Which is?"

She gestured at the wall. At the niches. At the patient, courteous, endless hunger of it. "*This.* An empire of the dead that ate its neighbors and then its own children. A mouth that learned it could be fed. You want to open that door — you and your bone-carver and your beautiful ledger of returned names — and every soul you free makes the seal weaker, and every hour the seal is weak, the Door remembers it has an appetite. Your Freya thinks she is giving people back their names. She may be setting a table."

The wind came up the hill, smelling of green water and old stone. Below us, in the streets, the waiting ones stood in their doorways, and I searched their faces for monstrosity and found none, and that was the cruelest thing yet — that they looked like people. Tired people. People who had waited a very long time for someone to say their names aloud.

"I can't refute you," I said at last.

Voss's shoulders shifted — surprise, almost imperceptible. She had come armed for argument.

"Everything you've shown me may be true. Probably is. You don't lie well enough for it to be otherwise — liars decorate, and you haven't decorated anything." I got to my feet. My legs held. The silk bonds had fallen away somewhere during the telling; I noticed I didn't remember her removing them, and decided not to care. "But here's what I can't get past, Huntmaster. You've shown me a country that committed atrocities. You haven't shown me a single soul in that wall who committed them. The tide-speakers aren't in these niches — I'd wager my other arm they burned themselves honorable deaths in their own beds, above the waterline, above the *accounting*. The ones in the wall are the sold and the stolen and the small. Villages that traded their grandparents to feed their children. Prisoners who paid for a warlord's peace. A child's bone in a niche near the top." I pointed at it, and my hand shook, and I let it. "You're punishing the meal for the appetite."

"The meal becomes the appetite," Voss said. "That's what no one believes until they've stood where I've stood. Feed the Door one name and it learns names can be had. Free a thousand and it learns it need only wait. Mercy is a currency too, Wren. Spend it here and you are spending it into the same account that bought rain with corpses."

"Then we disagree."

"Yes." Something in her face closed, gently, like a door being shut by someone who loves the room. "We disagree. And disagreement, in my experience, is settled by whoever holds the keystone."

She turned toward the seam in the world, and I followed, because Freya was on the other side of it, and because I had finally understood the shape of the choice ahead: not truth against lies, not good against evil, but wound against wound, and only one of us could be right, and the cost of being wrong was measured in countries.

At the threshold Voss paused. "Your mother's last words were a question," she said, not turning around. "She asked whether you'd grow up to count the dead or free them. I've spent thirty years assuming the answer mattered less than the seal." Now she turned, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "I find, lately, that I'm no longer sure. Go back to your bone-carver, Wren. Tell her what I showed you. All of it. If her answer is better than mine, I'll—" she stopped, and laughed once, without humor, at whatever she saw in my face. "No. I won't say it. Saying it would make it a promise, and promises are the only currency I have left."

She stepped through the seam, and it closed behind her like water.

I stood alone on the black hill under the green sky, in the country that had made me and eaten me and let me forget, and below me the waiting ones turned their faces toward the horizon — toward Corvane, toward the living lands, toward the sound, faint and growing, of something burning.

Smoke was rising past the edge of the world.

The Hunt had found the door.
