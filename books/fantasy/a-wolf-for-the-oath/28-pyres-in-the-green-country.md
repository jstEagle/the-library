## 28. Pyres in the Green Country

The first pyre went up on the morning the sky turned the color of old pewter, and I felt it before I saw it — a name going out of my ledger like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

I was sitting on the steps of the drowned customs house with the bone box open across my knees, reading names into the thin gray air, when the voice of a girl called Maren Ostwick simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped. The way a bell stops when you drop it down a well.

I knew what that meant. I had known since the ridge above the border-stitch, where we'd watched the Hunt's outriders pour through Seraphine Voss's broken seal like water finding a crack. But knowing and feeling are different countries, and I had just been exiled from one into the other.

"Wren." My voice came out wrong, flat as slate. "Maren's gone."

Wren looked up from the keystone they'd been turning over in their hands for two days — turning it, studying it, refusing to look at me while they studied it. The brand on their arm had settled into a low amber glow since the battle at the stitch, since they'd led Vhalen's dead against the Hunt and won us this borrowed hour. It lit their face from below, which made them look like something carved.

"Gone how?"

"Burned." I closed my hand around the bone that carried her name. It was still warm. It would be warm for hours yet, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies. "They're burning them, Wren. The waiting dead. And every pyre —"

"Feeds him." Wren said it without inflection, the way you'd read a line item. "The general. Yes. Voss told us."

Voss told us, and then Voss died for telling us, and I still could not say her name without tasting ash. She had shattered her own seal from the inside to open the door, and she had pressed the keystone into Wren's hands with both of hers, and she had said, *I built the cage. You decide what it holds.* And then the winter had taken her the way winter takes everything — completely, and without malice, which is somehow worse.

Now the cage was open, and the thing the cage had been built to starve was eating again.

* * *

We climbed the bell tower of the customs house because from its ruined crown you could see half of Vhalen — the streets un-drowning themselves street by street, the green-black sky thinning toward something almost like weather, the canals running backward and then, lately, running forward, which the waiting dead told me was a good sign, though they told me everything in voices that made good signs sound like funerals.

From the tower, I counted the fires.

Seven. No — eight. They burned in a rough arc across the eastern districts, small from this distance, almost pretty, orange points stitched along the gray hem of the world. I knew what they were. I had stood beside enough of the crown's pyres in the living country to know the architecture of them: the stacked faggots, the iron stake, the ring of soldiers standing at parade rest because burning people is administrative work now, it has paperwork, it has a schedule.

But these pyres were wrong in a way that took my breath and held it. There was nothing on the stakes.

The waiting dead of Vhalen did not need stakes. They did not even need wood, not really — the Hunt had learned that in the first hour, when the first condemned soldier put his torch to an empty scaffold and the fire caught anyway, fed by something older than kindling. A name, given up. That was all a pyre needed here. Stack the wood, speak the name you meant to erase, and the country itself would provide the rest, because Vhalen was a country made of memory, and memory burns.

"They're not even catching them," Wren said beside me. Their voice had gone very quiet, the quiet they used when duty and conscience were fighting behind their teeth. "Look. They're just — walking up. Volunteering."

They were. Even at this distance I could see them: figures moving through the ruined streets toward the fires in slow processions, unhurried, hands empty. The waiting dead could not be forced — I had learned that too, in the weeks of reading names, when each name returned a person to themselves and every person so returned became, briefly, gloriously, impossible to command. So the Hunt had stopped trying to force. They had found something crueller.

They had found despair.

"The general speaks to them," I said. "In stolen voices. He tells them the door will close, that no one is coming to carry their names home, that burning is the only warmth left in this country." My throat closed around it. "And some of them believe him. Because he uses my mother's voice, Wren. He used it on the woman who raised half the orphans of the Tidewards district, and she walked into the fire calling for her mama."

Wren's hand found mine. It was cold — their hand had been cold since the oath broke, cold as the brand that had freed them and hollowed them — but it was there, and I gripped it like a rope thrown into dark water.

Below us, in the plaza, the dead who had not yet gone to the fires had gathered. Hundreds of them, standing in the gray light, faces turned upward toward the tower where they knew I sat with my box of bones. They did not shout. They did not beg. They simply waited, the way they had waited under the drowned streets for forty years, patient as sediment.

Waiting for me to choose which of them would be next.

* * *

Here is what triage looks like when the patients are names:

You spread the ledger on the floor of the customs house's counting room, which still had its great scarred table, its broken abacus, its shelves of rotted ledgers from the country's last honest days of bookkeeping. You take out your knife and your supply of bone — deer bone, ox bone, whatever the dead country offered up, and lately, God forgive me, offerings left at the tower door by the dead themselves, femurs and ribs laid in neat rows like tithes.

And then you listen.

The dead came to me all night. They came in ones and twos and sometimes in crowds that pressed against the counting room door like tide against a seawall, and each of them wanted the same thing, and none of them could have it, not all of them, not tonight, maybe not ever, and so I sat with the ledger open and I chose.

A ferryman named Aldous Brek, who had waited forty years to tell his wife he'd seen the storm coming and stayed at the tiller anyway. His wife was three streets away and the pyres were four. I carved his name first.

Two sisters, the Vane girls, who had been children when the water came and had spent the drowning holding hands, and who asked — politely, terribly politely — if they might please be read together, so as not to be separated twice. I carved them together, on one bone, their names braided around each other the way their fingers had been.

An old man who only gave me part of his name, because the rest had frozen somewhere inside him during the long winter, and who wept when I promised to come back for the missing piece. I did not promise. I heard myself promise. There is a difference, and I have to live with hearing it.

Each name I read aloud returned someone to themselves — whole, warm-eyed, present in a way the waiting dead never quite managed, present enough to laugh, to grieve, to walk out of the counting room and stand in the plaza looking at the sky like people seeing weather for the first time. Each reading drained warmth from my body. By midnight my fingers had gone blue-white. By the third watch I could no longer feel the knife, and I carved by sight alone, watching the blade bite the bone, checking my work twice because numb hands make sloppy letters and a sloppy letter in a name is a small death all its own.

By dawn I had read back thirty-one names.

The pyres had taken two hundred and six.

I know the number exactly because I kept count. That is what I am, in the end, whatever else I have become — a keeper of accounts. Somewhere in the second watch I started a second column in the ledger, and I wrote the burned names in it as I felt them go out, each one a small implosion behind my ribs, and I wrote them in red ink I made from — I don't want to record what. The dead country provides. The dead country provided that too.

Two hundred and six. And every one of them feeding him. Every pyre a spoon lifted to the general's mouth. I could feel him growing as I worked, the way you feel a storm building behind a mountain — pressure in the teeth, weight in the ears. The snow at the windows had begun falling upward again, steadily, patiently, millions of flakes climbing toward a sky that would not hold them.

And in my mother's voice, threading through the walls like smoke through a keyhole, he spoke to me all night while I worked.

*Daughter. You are counting spilled milk. Give me the ledger and I will give her back to you — not the echo, not the bone-whisper. Her. Alive. Warm. She is not beyond me; nothing in this country is beyond me. You of all people understand a fair trade.*

I did not answer him. Answering is a door, and I am learning — slowly, at cost — which doors open onto what.

But I will not lie in this ledger, and this chapter is a ledger of sorts, so I will write the truth plainly: at the hour before dawn, carving the name of a boy who had drowned saving a dog, I stopped. For eleven breaths — I counted those too — I sat with the knife motionless and let myself imagine it. My mother's true voice. Not the whisper in the charm at my throat, not the memory worn smooth by ten years of handling, but her voice, alive, annoyed at me for hunching over my work, singing off-key in the kitchen, saying my name the way only she had ever said it.

Eleven breaths. Then I carved the boy's name, and I hated the general, and I hated the crown, and most of all in that moment I hated the arithmetic — the brutal, bloodless arithmetic that said thirty-one saved, two hundred six lost, and the ratio worsening by the hour.

Arithmetic was supposed to be my safe country. I had hidden behind it my whole life, ink and ivory, columns and sums. Now it had turned on me like every other refuge, and I sat in the ruins of it, blue-fingered, surrounded by bones, doing division with the dead.

* * *

Wren found me there in the gray of morning, asleep sitting up with the knife still in my hand and the ledger open to the red column. I woke to their hand on my shoulder and flinched hard enough to cut my thumb, and the bead of blood that welled up fell on the page, on the name of someone whose name I hadn't yet learned to stop hearing.

"You're killing yourself," Wren said. Not gently. Gently would have been a mercy and neither of us had any to spare. "Freya. Look at your hands."

I looked. They were the hands of a corpse that hadn't been informed. The cold the readings took from me had been replaced by nothing; there was nothing to replace it with; I had been spending warmth I did not have, drawing it from deeper and deeper reserves, and the reserves were nearly empty, and the ledger was barely a fifth read, and the pyres were multiplying.

"There are nine thousand names in this book," I said. "Nine thousand and forty-one. I've done three hundred and twelve. At this rate —"

"At this rate you'll be dead in six days and the book will burn with you." Wren crouched to my eye level. Their face was terrible with tiredness; the brand-light under their skin made shadows move in it. "So we stop doing it this way."

"And do it what way? Tell me the other way, Wren. Tell me the clever plan. Because I've been sitting here all night listening to a man wear my mother's voice like a coat, and I have not heard a single clever plan from anyone, including you, including the keystone you keep staring at like it'll confess."

Silence. The snow fell upward past the windows. Somewhere east, a new pyre caught — I felt it, a name winking out, the two hundred seventh, and I wrote it in the red column without looking, the way a musician tunes by ear.

Then Wren said, quietly, "The keystone can close the door forever. You know that. Safety for everyone alive. Erasure for everyone here. One act, finished, done — no more triage, no more arithmetic, no more choosing."

"I know what it can do."

"And you know what I swore on it. Neither open nor shut. Hold the door with you." Their jaw tightened. "But I've been staring at it, as you so kindly noted, because I don't think it accepts oaths anymore. I think it's been listening to him. I think it's *cold*, Freya. I think the general got into it the way he gets into everything, and if I swear on it now, the door doesn't stay ajar. It closes. Slowly, politely, but it closes, and everything we've read back gets pulled under again, and everything we haven't—"

Stops. The word hung there. Stops being waiting. Starts being fuel.

I looked down at the ledger. At the red column. At the blue-black stain my blood had left on the corner of the page, already drying to the color of old rust.

"Then we read faster," I said.

"That kills you."

"Then we find another way that doesn't, and until we find it, we read faster." I picked up the knife. My fingers had begun, faintly, horribly, to warm — the general's gift, seeping in through the cracks exhaustion had opened, offering comfort the way he offered everything. I set the knife down again and wrapped my hands in my cloak instead, and made myself shiver, and made myself feel it. Cold was mine. Cold was honest. I would not take warmth from him. "Because here is what I learned last night, Wren, sitting in the arithmetic. He wants the ledger. Every trick he has — the voices, the despair, the pyres — every single one is aimed at making me surrender this book or die holding it. Which means the book matters more than he admits. Which means the names matter. Which means—"

"Which means the triage isn't just yours anymore," Wren finished. Their eyes had gone distant, calculating — the old oath-keeper surfacing, the machinery of duty repurposed. "The dead can carry names too. The ones you've read back — the three hundred twelve — they're whole enough to remember. Whole enough to *speak*."

I stared at them. Then at the window, where the plaza below was filling again, the returned dead gathering in the gray light, looking up at the tower. Waiting. Patient as sediment.

Not waiting to be read.

Waiting to be *sent*.

"Oh," I said. It was a very small word for how large the idea was. "Oh, Wren. A guild of impossible trades."

"We'll need couriers," Wren said. "Three hundred of them. And a route past nine pyres. And a general who wears your mother's face standing in every one of their paths."

Outside, the snow fell upward, faster now, and far to the east a tenth pyre bloomed against the pewter sky, and I felt the two hundred eighth name go out of the world — and this time, before the grief could close over it, I reached for the bone, and I found that the name was not entirely gone. An ember of it remained, caught, held, the way a hearthstone holds warmth.

Held by someone. Someone out there in the gray morning, carrying a stranger's name in their chest like a coal cupped in bare hands, running toward the tower through the falling-up snow.

The dead had begun rescuing each other.

I opened the ledger to a fresh page, and my hands were shaking, and I wrote at the top of it, in ink the color of ordinary honest night: *Names recovered by the returned.* And beneath it, the first entry — a name I had given back two nights ago, arriving now from the direction of the fires, borne in a dead man's keeping, coming home the long way.

The door of the counting room banged open, and the first courier stood in it, steaming with cold, eyes blazing, and said: "Ledger-keeper. I have a name for you. And you had better write fast, because there are more of us coming, and the general knows."
