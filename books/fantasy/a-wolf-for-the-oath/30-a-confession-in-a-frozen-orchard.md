## 30. A Confession in a Frozen Orchard

The orchard had been dead longer than the winter had been wrong.

I knew this because the trees were not merely bare — they were *finished*. Their branches had curled inward like fingers closing over something they refused to give up, and their bark was split along lines that looked deliberate, almost written, as though some patient hand had spent decades carving a ledger no one would ever read. The snow beneath them lay undisturbed. Nothing had walked here. Nothing had wanted to.

We came into it at dusk, when the light went the color of old ivory and the cold stopped biting and started simply *being*, the way grief does after enough years. Wren walked ahead with the keystone wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and I walked behind watching their breath and mine tangle together in the air between us — two small ghosts, mingling.

"We should keep moving," Wren said, but their voice had no conviction in it. Even they didn't believe it.

"The dead say we should stop." I touched the bone charm at my throat. It had been warm for three days now, warmer than my skin, which should have frightened me and instead felt like being held. "They say this is the last quiet place before the border-stitch. They say—" I hesitated, because what they said next was strange even by the standards of the last month. "They say we should say it here. Whatever it is we haven't said."

Wren stopped walking. The snow creaked under their boots, a sound like a door deciding whether to open.

"They talk to you about that?" they asked. "About us?"

"They talk to me about everything. They're very nosy. Death apparently doesn't cure it." I tried to make it light. It came out thin. "Most of them are rooting for you, incidentally. There's a woman from the second pyre-wagon who has money on you saying it first."

Something moved across Wren's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile used to live, before oaths and brands and burning ridgelines wore the ground down to bedrock. "And if I don't?"

"Then she loses her wager, and the dead are disappointed, and honestly, Wren, disappointing the dead is the one thing left in this country that still scares me."

That got the smile. Small, crooked, real. It was worth every word.

We made camp in the lee of the largest tree, which had split long ago and grown back around its own wound, so that its trunk held a hollow like a cupped palm. Wren built a fire from wood that had been dead so long it burned clean and nearly smokeless, giving off a smell like libraries, like paper kept safe. I spread our single blanket. We had one blanket between us now; the other had gone into the river at the crossing, along with most of my hairpins, half of Wren's rations, and any pretense that we were two people traveling together rather than one thing traveling in two bodies.

I sat against the tree's hollow and pulled out the ledger — the new one, the true one, bone pages bound in sinew — and began, as I did every night, to read names aloud. It was work. It was prayer. It was the only liturgy I had ever believed in.

*Marisol Vane. Aldric Vane, her son, aged nine. Tamsin Oduya. The twins Ferro and Fenn Oduya.*

Each name settled into the air above the fire like a bird coming home to roost. Somewhere beyond the orchard, beyond the wrong winter, the people those names belonged to stood a little straighter inside themselves. I could feel it — the faint easing, like a knot loosening one coil.

When I reached the end of tonight's page, I found Wren watching me. Not the way they'd watched me in the guildhouse, all audit and suspicion, cataloguing my gift as evidence. And not the way they'd watched me on the road, wary and hungry and pretending to be neither. This was simpler than either. This was just looking.

"What?" I asked.

"You do that differently now," they said. "In the beginning, reading the names cost you. You'd go gray. Your hands shook. Now—"

"Now it costs me anyway," I said. "I've just gotten better at hiding it. Old habit. You remember how good I am at hiding things."

"I remember." Wren looked into the fire. "I remember thinking you were the best liar I'd ever met, because your ledgers balanced perfectly. Nobody balances perfectly unless they're hiding something."

"And what do you think now?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When they spoke, their voice had the careful flatness they used when an oath was pressing on them from the inside — except the oath was gone now, burned away to nothing, and I realized with a small cold shock that this carefulness was theirs alone. No vow forcing it. Just Wren, choosing their words like a man choosing where to step on ice.

"I think," they said, "that I have been trying to write this confession for six weeks, and every draft ends badly, and I'm going to say it plainly or lose my nerve entirely."

My heart did something unhelpful. I kept my hands still on the ledger. Ledger-keeper's discipline: face smooth, breath even, no matter what numbers come through the door.

"Say it plainly," I said. "I'm a bookkeeper. Plain entries only."

"All right." They turned to face me fully, cross-legged, firelight on the ruin of their brand-scarred arm. "Here is the entry. I was sent to arrest you. I arrested you. I lied to you about mercy and my own oath flayed me for it, and I told myself the lie was duty, and then I freed you, and I told myself that was conscience, and then I stayed with you, and I told myself that was strategy, and every explanation I gave myself was a smaller lie stacked on a bigger one until the whole tower of them came down somewhere on the road past the village that burned itself." They took a breath. "Here is the truth underneath. I stayed because leaving you was the first thing my body ever refused to do. Before thought. Before choice. Like a heart refusing to stop. That's what you are to me. Not a duty. Not a debt. The thing my body knows that my mind keeps trying to catch up to."

The orchard was very silent. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath, listening — and maybe it was, maybe everything in this wrong-wintered country listened now, the dead and the snow and the stitched border all leaning in.

"Wren," I said.

"Don't answer yet." Their voice cracked on it, just slightly. "Because there's more, and it's the part I don't want to say, and if you answer kindly I'll lose the rest."

"Then say the rest."

"The rest is arithmetic." They laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the dead trees. "You taught me arithmetic, Freya. Every name you read costs you warmth. You're colder than you were a month ago — I can feel it when I touch your hands, and I touch your hands every night to check, and I tell you it's to quiet the brand, and that's another small lie, because mostly it's because I'm afraid of how cold you are. Seraphine read names for a year before she built the seal. A year. And look what's left of her. You've been reading for weeks, and the general grows stronger every day, and tomorrow or the day after we reach the stitch, and either you become the door or the seal closes or the winter takes us all, and in none of those endings—" Their voice finally broke properly. "In none of those endings is there a version where I get to keep you."

There it was. Laid out on the snow between us like goods on a merchant's table. An honest entry at last, in the plainest hand.

I closed the ledger. Set it aside. And I moved across the blanket to sit beside them, shoulder to shoulder, close enough that the brand on their arm — quiet these days under my touch — hummed faintly against my sleeve like a cat remembering it had once been wild.

"You're right," I said. "There may not be a version where you get to keep me. I've done the same arithmetic. I do it every night while you sleep. I count the names I can still read before the cold reaches my heart, and I get different answers depending on how brave I'm feeling, and none of the answers are *many*."

"Freya—"

"My turn." I took their scarred hand. Turned it over. Traced the brand's oldest lines, the ones the oath had carved first, back when Wren Halvard was a child refugee allowed to forget a drowned country. "You want a confession? Here's mine, and it goes back further than yours. I hid my gift my entire life. Twenty-six years behind ink and ivory. I carved bones in secret and burned the shavings and told no one, not even the guildmaster, especially not the guildmaster, because I learned early that the world burns what it can't file away. And then you walked into the guildhouse in your winter clothes, smelling of oath-magic and misery, and you looked at me across a table of impossible trades, and for the first time in my life I wanted to be *known*." I laughed softly. "Do you understand what a catastrophe that was? I had a perfect system. And you ruined it in an afternoon, just by standing there looking like a man carrying his own execution warrant and hoping someone would take it from him."

"I wasn't hoping that."

"No. But you were carrying it anyway, and I saw it, because I see things. It's my gift. Everyone thinks my gift is hearing the dead. It isn't. My gift is that nothing hidden stays hidden from me — not in bone, not in ink, not in a person." I lifted their hand and pressed it flat over my sternum, over the heartbeat the cold had been slowly borrowing from. "So hear this, plainly, since we're doing plain entries. I love you. Not the way the dead love — wistful, backward-facing, all memory. The living way. Forward. Stubborn. Against all sense. I loved you when you arrested me. I loved you when you freed me and screamed doing it. I loved you when you stood between me and the hounds with one working arm and no plan whatsoever. And I love you now, tonight, in this dead orchard, knowing that one of us probably doesn't survive the week — and Wren, listen to me — *knowing that changes nothing.*"

Their eyes were wet. They didn't wipe them. I think they'd forgotten how.

"That's not comfort," they whispered. "That's a death sentence with better handwriting."

"It's a ledger entry," I said. "It doesn't promise anything. It just refuses to lie. That's all a ledger ever is, in the end — a refusal to lie. Everything else is commentary."

For a long moment neither of us moved. Then Wren's free hand came up — slow, asking permission the whole way, giving me a hundred chances to pull back — and touched my face. Their fingers were cold. Mine were colder. Between us we made almost one warm person, which felt like the most honest joke the world had played on us yet.

"When I was a child in Vhalen," they said quietly, "before the drowning, before the crown, there was a festival. The Naming. Once a year, everyone in the city said aloud the name of the person they loved most, in the market square, at dusk. Publicly. So the whole city became a witness to it, and the love couldn't be taken back or denied later. My mother said my father's name every year like she was daring the sea to argue." Their thumb traced my cheekbone. "I was seven. I never got to say anyone's name. The sea argued first."

"Say it now," I said. "There's no market square. There's just dead trees and wrong snow and a nosy woman from the second pyre-wagon who's probably listening. But say it."

Wren closed their eyes. Opened them. And said, in a voice that shook and did not break:

"Freya Delacroix. I love you. Witnessed by the orchard, the snow, the dead, and whatever's left of me that's still mine to give."

The bone charm at my throat went warm as a held hand. And all around us, faint as frost forming, I heard it — the dead of Vhalen, hundreds of them, murmuring the words back like a congregation, *witnessed, witnessed, witnessed* — and the snow at the edge of the firelight stirred without wind, rising an inch off the ground and hanging there, glittering, like the whole orchard had drawn breath.

"They heard you," I whispered.

"Good." Wren leaned their forehead against mine. "Then it's on the record. No take-backs. I know how you feel about amended entries."

"I don't permit them," I agreed, and kissed them.

It was not a desperate kiss. We were past desperation; we'd spent it all weeks ago on a ridge overlooking the first pyre. It was a slow kiss, a thorough one, the kiss of two people who had decided to spend their remaining warmth generously rather than hoard it — and when we finally drew apart, the cold had crept closer around our little fire, and neither of us cared.

We lay down together under the one blanket, in the hollow of the tree that had grown around its own wound, and I fit myself against Wren's side with my ear over their heart, and their arm came around me, and the brand pulsed gently against my spine like a second, stranger heartbeat.

"Tell me something true," Wren murmured into my hair. "One more entry. Anything."

I considered. The dead murmured suggestions; I declined them all; this one I wanted to be mine alone.

"When this is over," I said, "whatever 'over' turns out to mean — if there's a world left with a corner in it, I want a house with a bad roof near water. I want a table where I can carve bones in the open, in daylight, where anyone can watch. I want a garden that isn't dead. And I want you in it, arguing with me about how to organize the ledger, because you alphabetize like a barbarian."

"By *subject* is a perfectly valid system—"

"It's chaos, is what it is."

"Freya Delacroix." Wren's voice was drowsy now, warm at the edges despite everything. "Are you proposing a future to me in a haunted orchard?"

"I'm proposing a *draft* of a future. Final copy pending survival."

"Approved," they said. "Preliminary approval. Pending review."

"Pending review," I agreed, and smiled into their collarbone, and let my eyes close.

The fire burned low. The dead kept their soft vigil around us, and for a few hours the wrong winter relented, and the snow fell the ordinary way, downward, gentle, like the world briefly remembering how it was supposed to behave.

*

I woke because the bone charm had gone hot enough to sting.

Not warm. Hot. Alarm-hot, warning-hot, the temperature of a name being spoken somewhere that names should not be spoken.

The fire was ash. Wren slept beside me, breathing slow, the keystone rising and falling against their chest. Above the black branches, the sky had changed — the green-black pressure of the drowned country's nearness had deepened overnight into something almost solid, and the stars showed through it like coins at the bottom of a well.

And at the far edge of the orchard, where the trees thinned toward the north road, the snow was falling upward in a slow, deliberate column — and within that column stood a figure made of frost and lamplight, wearing the shape of a woman.

She did not approach. She simply waited, the way a creditor waits, the way a herald waits, the way a person waits when they are certain you will come to them.

The voice that drifted across the dead orchard was gentle. Familiar. Stolen.

*Daughter,* said my mother's voice, from the mouth of the winter. *I have a trade to propose.*

Beside me, Wren's eyes opened, and I saw them hear it too — saw them go rigid, saw their hand find mine in the dark and grip it hard enough to hurt.

The general had come to make her offer. And she had chosen, with a cruelty I would spend everything to answer, to wear the one voice in all the world I could not bear to refuse.

*Come and hear the terms,* she said. *Before the week runs out.*
