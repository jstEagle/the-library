## 31. The General Offers a Trade

I had learned to read the cold the way other people read weather. There was the honest cold, the kind that came down from the mountains and minded its own business, and there was the other kind — the cold that *noticed* you, that pooled in doorways and waited at the edges of fields like a creditor who knew you were good for it.

The orchard was full of the second kind.

We had come there because Freya said the dead had told her to. *The orchard where the names are sweetest,* they had said, in that chorus-voice she carried now like a second heartbeat, and so we had walked out of the ruined city at first light, past villages frozen mid-task — a woman at a well with her bucket half-raised, two boys mid-race with their breath hanging in the air like glass — and into a grove of apple trees that had died standing, every branch silvered with frost that did not melt even where the weak sun touched it.

Freya walked ahead of me with the ledger strapped to her chest like a shield, her breath clouding, her fingers bare despite the cold because she said the bones read better against skin. The brand on my arm had gone quiet in the last mile, which I had learned to distrust more than pain. Pain meant the oath was arguing with itself. Silence meant something else was listening.

"They're close," Freya said, without turning. "Not the dead. The *other* thing."

"I know."

"Do you?" She glanced back at me, and there was frost in her lashes, and under the frost there was something I had not seen in her before — or rather, something I had seen and refused to name. A tiredness so complete it looked almost like peace. "Wren. If it offers me something —"

"It will offer you a lie."

"Everything it says will be a lie," she agreed. "But lies built out of true things are the only kind that work on me. You know that. You've watched me read a ledger."

I wanted to tell her to turn around. I wanted to tell her that we could go back to the city, that Voss's keystone was warm in the pack on my shoulders, that we had a third answer waiting for us if we could only reach the door — but I had learned, somewhere between the burning wagons and the stitched border, that Freya Delacroix did not turn around. She had been a ledger-keeper. She went toward the entries that frightened her, because an entry unexamined was a debt unpaid, and she had never in her life let a debt sit.

So I said only, "I'll be right behind you," and we went into the orchard together.

* * *

It was warmer inside the trees. That was the first wrong thing.

The frost on the branches did not lessen — if anything it thickened, each apple-tree skeleton wearing a coat of white fur — but the air itself lost its edge, the way a room loses its edge when a fire has just gone out and the walls still remember the heat. The dead light of the green-black sky filtered down through the branches and came out the color of old honey. Somewhere ahead, water was running. There was no water for thirty miles.

"Freya." I caught her sleeve. "Stop. Look."

She looked. I watched her look. And I watched the exact moment she understood, because her face did what it always did when she read something true and terrible: it went very still, and very polite, the way it had gone the day the guildmaster sold her, the way it went over any entry she could not immediately correct.

Between the trees, in a clearing where the grass was green — actually green, the green of a spring in a country that no longer had springs — stood a woman.

She was made of winter the way a statue is made of marble: not wearing it, not carrying it, but *composed* of it. Snow fell upward off her shoulders and hung in the air around her like a held breath. Her dress was the gray of ice on a river that has just learned to bear weight. Her face —

Her face was the problem. It was almost a face. It was the suggestion of a face, the way a word on the tip of your tongue is almost a word, and it kept almost resolving into someone, and the someone it kept almost resolving into was different for everyone who looked at it. I could feel it trying me first — flickering toward a shape I did not recognize, a man with a soldier's jaw, a face from Vhalen I had been allowed to forget — and then, finding nothing in me to wear, it settled into something generic and courteous, the face of any official who has ever come to inform a family of a death.

But Freya — Freya it did not have to guess about. I saw it settle on her like a bird on a branch, and I saw Freya's breath stop, and I knew.

"That's not her," I said. "Freya. That is not your mother."

"I know," Freya said, and her voice was steady, and her feet did not stop moving, and I followed because there had never been a version of this in which I did not follow.

* * *

"You came," the general said.

Its voice was the worst part. It had clearly studied voices the way it studied faces, and it had chosen — for us, for this moment — a voice that was warm and low and slightly roughened, the voice of someone who has talked all their life in kitchens and market squares and sickrooms. A mother's voice, generically. A voice like a hand on the back of the neck.

"You've been reading my names," it said to Freya. "Every night by the fire. I could hear you. Do you know what it's like, Freya Delacroix, to hear your own ledger read aloud? To hear the names you were built to bury being given back, one by one, like coins from a strongbox?" It tilted its almost-face. "I was an oath-keeper too. Did Wren tell you? The first one. Before the crown had a hundred of us, there was one, and I was it, and they put me in the seal the way you'd put a keystone in an arch. I have been holding this door shut for two hundred years with my whole body. And now a girl with a bone knife is unpicking my work name by name, and the cold is the only voice I have left."

"You have a voice," Freya said quietly. "It's stolen."

The almost-face smiled. "Everything in Vhalen is stolen. That is rather the point."

I moved up beside her. The brand on my arm had begun to ache in a way I recognized from the worst days of the oath — not the flaying pain of disobedience, but the deep, structural ache of being *addressed*, of an authority older than my vow speaking to the thing my vow had been carved from. "Say what you came to say," I told it. "And then we'll be leaving."

"Of course." The general folded its snow-white hands. It had all the time in the world, and it wanted us to know it. "But I would like to say it properly. I would like to say it to the ledger-keeper, because this is a trade, and trades should be conducted between the parties who can honor them."

Freya's hand went to the ledger at her chest. Not clutching it. Resting on it. The way you rest your hand on a horse you're afraid is about to bolt.

"I'm listening," she said.

* * *

"You have been reading the names," the general said, "and each name you give back costs you warmth. Wren has seen it. The frost that doesn't leave your fingers. The way you sit closer to the fire every night. You are spending yourself, ledger-keeper, entry by entry, and the terrible arithmetic of it is this: there are more names in your book than there are nights left in the winter. You will run out of yourself before you run out of names. We both know it. I have done the accounting." It smiled again, gently. "I was made for accounting. That is what a seal is — a debt that never comes due, paid forever in installments of cold."

"Get to the trade," I said.

"I am getting to it." The almost-face turned to me, and for a moment something moved behind it — irritation, or the memory of irritation, the ghost of a temper that had once belonged to a living person — and then it smoothed away. "Here is my offer, Freya Delacroix. Hear all of it before you answer, because I am not permitted to repeat it, and neither, I think, are you."

The orchard went silent. Not quiet — *silent*, the silence of a held note, the silence of a room waiting for a verdict. The upward-falling snow hung motionless in the air between the branches. Even the impossible water had stopped.

"Your mother," the general said, "is in my keeping."

The world narrowed to a single point.

"Her name was Maren Delacroix," it went on, in that warm kitchen voice, gentle as a knife going in. "She came to the border the year after the drowning, looking for her own mother's name, because your gift is older than you know and it came down her line like eye-color. She found the stitch. She found *me*. She was the last person to speak to me before the crown finished my entombment, and she made me a promise, and I have kept it for twenty-two years, which is longer than most living people keep any promise at all." The almost-face leaned forward. "I kept her *voice*, Freya. Not the echo of it. Not the memory of it. The voice itself — the true sound of her, alive, the sound she made at twenty-six years old on the morning she sang to you in the kitchen in Corvane while the bread rose. I have it the way I have the winter. It is mine. It is *kept*."

Freya had gone white. Not cold-white — I knew her cold-white, the draining of the readings — this was different, this was blood leaving in a rush, this was a body remembering all at once a grief it had spent twenty years paying off in small installments.

"You're lying," I said.

"I am a creature made of oath," the general said, without looking at me. "I cannot lie, Wren Halvard. You of all people should know what we are. I can only keep things, and offer them, and wait." It turned back to Freya. "Here is the trade. Give me the ledger — all of it, every name, every bone, every entry in your own hand — and let the seal close. Not violently. I will open the way for you myself. You and Wren will walk out of Vhalen alive, whole, warm, into a spring that will actually come. And I will give you her voice. Not a memory of it. *Her.* Alive, restored, the true sound of Maren Delacroix, to keep for the rest of your long and unremarkable and *safe* life. You will hear her say your name. You will hear her laugh. You will hear the thing you have been carving into bone for twenty years because you could not bear to lose the ghost of it — and you will have the real thing, and it will never, ever leave you."

The snow began to fall upward again, slowly, gently, like a celebration.

"All you must give up," the general said softly, "is everyone else's mother."

* * *

I have stood in burning fields. I have watched the Hunt's pyres from a ridge with a woman's life in my hands and my oath flaying me for the mercy I was already planning. I have felt the brand eat its way up my arm and understood that conscience has a physical cost, itemized and collected. I thought I had been present for the worst moments a person could witness.

I had never watched someone I love hesitate.

It lasted four seconds. I counted them, because counting is what I do when I cannot do anything else, and each one was a year.

In the first second, Freya did not move. Her hand stayed on the ledger, and her face stayed polite, and I understood with a horror that went all the way down into the brand that she was not refusing. She was *considering*. She was doing the thing she did with every impossible trade that crossed the guild's desk: weighing the entries, finding the true shape of the debt, refusing to flinch from the arithmetic no matter how much it cost. That was her gift, in the end. Not the bones. The willingness to look at the number.

In the second second, she closed her eyes, and her lips moved — and I realized she was not praying. She was listening. The dead were speaking to her, the thousands of names she had carried out of the pyres and the frozen villages and the burned guildhouse, and whatever they were saying, her face did not soften. That frightened me more than the hesitation. If the dead had begged her, she would have wept. They were not begging. They were *waiting*, the way they had waited under the green-black sky for two hundred years, with the patience of people who had already lost everything except hope, and their patience was a weight on her, and she was feeling it.

In the third second, I saw her hand tighten on the ledger's strap, and I saw her knuckles go white, and I saw — this is the thing I will carry to whatever grave is allowed me — I saw her *want it*. Openly. Without shame. Her whole body leaned toward the offer the way a starving person leans toward bread, and why would she not? Twenty years of carving her mother's name into ivory and bone because the voice was gone and the name was all that was left. Twenty years of a gift that made her a museum of other people's dead while her own dead had no grave, no body, no sound. And here was a creature offering her the one thing in the world that could not be bought, could not be carved, could not be read aloud by candlelight — the true voice, alive, restored — in exchange for a book.

In the fourth second, she opened her eyes.

And I saw her look at me.

Not for permission. Freya Delacroix did not ask anyone's permission, least of all mine, least of all a disgraced oath-keeper whose hands were not clean enough to hold her ink. She looked at me the way you look at a fixed point when the world is tilting — to find out which way is down, which way is true, what is real. And what was in my face, I do not know. I only know what I was thinking, which was: *say no, say no, say no* — and also, beneath it, in the place where I kept the things I was ashamed of: *if she says yes, I will still go with her. I will carry the ledger to the seal myself, and I will hate her a little, forever, and I will never leave her.*

That is what love does to a person who was raised on duty. It does not free you. It just gives your cage a nicer view.

"Freya," the general said, and its warm voice had gone warmer, the way ice going to water is warmer than ice, "the offer stands until you leave this orchard. I am not cruel. I know what I am asking you to refuse. I have had two hundred years to learn exactly what things cost." It extended one snow-white hand, palm up, open, patient. An official's hand. A hand for receiving documents. "Her voice, ledger-keeper. Her true voice. Say the word."

Freya looked at the open hand.

Then she looked down at the ledger against her chest, and she did something I had never seen her do. She unstrapped it. She lifted it — the book that had become her spine, her purpose, her whole rebuilt self since the night the guild burned — and she held it out in front of her, away from her body, into the space between herself and the general.

The world stopped. I stopped. Even the brand on my arm went silent, listening.

She held it there for the length of one long breath, her arms trembling not from the cold, and her face was the face she wore over entries she could not correct, and then —

"No," she said.

She pulled the book back against her heart so hard it made a sound, a small thump like a door closing, like a latch dropping into place.

"No," she said again, and now her voice cracked down the middle and came out on both sides of the crack at once, grief and iron in the same breath. "You don't get to make this trade. You don't get to put her voice on one side of a scale and *them* on the other. She would never —" Her breath caught. "My mother walked to this border to find *other people's* names. That's what her gift was for. That's what mine is for. You kept her voice for twenty-two years and you never once listened to what she *said* with it."

The almost-face did not change. But the orchard did. The green light guttered. The upward snow faltered, hung, and came down — came *down*, for the first time, ordinary and gray.

"You will regret this," the general said, and the warm kitchen voice was gone, and what was underneath it was the sound a glacier makes when it begins to move. "I have kept her voice safe for twenty-two years, ledger-keeper. I will not keep it safe forever. Winter is patient, but it is not *kind*, and I am running out of the one and never had any of the other. When the last freeze comes — and it is coming, child, it is coming within the week — everything in my keeping goes into it. The names. The voices. The door. All of it, closed, cold, and finished." The almost-face leaned close, and for one instant it resolved — fully, terribly, only for Freya and only for a heartbeat — into a woman's face, a real one, with Freya's own cheekbones and a mouth made for singing. "Last chance," said her mother's face, in a voice almost, almost right. "Say the word."

Freya did not step back. She stepped *forward*, into the thing's face, close enough that the frost of it breathed against her skin, and she said, in a voice I will hear for the rest of my life:

"Her name was Maren Delacroix. And I am going to say it back to you, in front of everyone, before this winter ends. And when I do, you will give her to me yourself — because that is what oaths *do*, and you are made of oath, and I have read enough ledgers to know that every debt comes due."

The face shattered back into the almost-nothing. The general straightened. And when it spoke again, its voice was the courteous official's voice, and it was worse than all the others.

"Then we will see," it said, "what you are made of."

The green light went out of the orchard all at once, like a lamp snuffed, and the cold came down on us with the weight of a closing hand, and the last thing I saw before I threw my arm around Freya and pulled her toward the tree line was the general's snow-white hand, still open, still patient, closing slowly — finger by finger by finger — into a fist.

Behind us, in the heart of the orchard, the ice began to sing.

And every name in Freya's ledger — every bone at her throat, every carved syllable against her skin — began, all at once, to scream.
