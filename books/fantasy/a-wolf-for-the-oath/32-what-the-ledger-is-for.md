## 32. What the Ledger Is For

The general wore her mother's voice the way a thief wears a stolen coat — correctly, in every seam, and therefore wrongly in every way that mattered.

*Give me the ledger, little keeper,* it said, and the cold came with the words, rolling off the frozen orchard rows like breath off a blade. *And I will give her back to you. Not the echo in your bone charms. Not the memory you have worn smooth from handling. Her. Alive. Warm. Calling your name across a kitchen.*

Freya stood in the dead orchard with the ledger open against her chest, and she let herself want it.

That was the terrible thing, the thing she would never tell Wren: for one whole breath — maybe two — she let herself want it with her entire body. She saw the kitchen of her childhood, the one that had burned with everything else when Vhalen drowned and Corvane swallowed its survivors. She saw her mother's hands, flour to the wrists, the way she'd hum when she thought no one was listening. She felt the specific weight of being a child whose mother was still alive to be disappointed in her, which was a weight Freya had carried so long without it that she had forgotten it was ever there.

Two breaths. Then she closed her eyes and did what she had done every day of her life since the drowning: she opened the ledger in her mind and checked the entry.

*Maren Delacroix. Carver's daughter. Drowned at thirty-one years old, in the third hour of the fall, holding a stranger's child above the water until the water took them both together.* The dead had told her that themselves, back in the wagons, back before the pyres, back when their voices were only screaming and not yet the organized, patient chorus they had become under her keeping. Her mother had died holding someone else's child. That was an entry. That was a fact. Facts did not bend because a thing made of winter knew how to say *kitchen* in a beloved voice.

"You don't have her," Freya said. "You have her voice the way I have her bones. A copy. A carving."

The cold laughed. It was worse than the voice. The laugh was her mother's laugh too, low and startled, the one that escaped her when Freya's father said something dry at dinner — except her father was also dead, drowned or frozen or erased, and the laugh had no right to exist outside two people who were both gone.

*I have more than a copy,* the general said. *I have the original. She is in me, little keeper, as everyone the winter takes is in me. Every name the Hunt burns comes home to my ice. Your mother is not gone. She is archived.*

"Archived." Freya tasted the word. "Like an entry in a seized ledger. Like property in a vault."

*Like something safe,* the general said, and here the voice gentled, became reasonable, became almost kind, and that was the most frightening register yet. *The crown sealed your country because what slept behind the door could not be permitted to walk. I am the seal's hand. I keep the dead where the living cannot be hurt by them. Give me the ledger — every name you have carved, every name you have read aloud, all your careful witness — and I will fold them into the ice with the rest. Safe. Quiet. And your mother's voice will be yours again, warm, in a kitchen, in spring.*

Behind her, at the orchard's edge, Wren was on one knee in the snow, still fighting the last of the general's freeze out of their lungs. She could hear them coughing. She could hear the brand on their arm hissing where the snow touched it, a sound like a wet log on a fire. She did not look back. Looking back was how people lost arguments with monsters.

"You froze villages," Freya said. "Mid-task. A woman in Hallowmere died with her hands in bread dough. A boy in the river valley died running, mid-stride, and the snow holds him like a fly in amber. Is that what safety looks like? Is that what you do with things you're keeping safe?"

*The living are collateral,* the general said, without apology, without cruelty even — just arithmetic. *The seal must close. The door must shut. You have been reading names, little keeper, and every name you return weakens the stitch. Do you know what walks through when the stitch fails? Not your gentle waiting dead. Everything. Everything that was ever drowned and ever burned and ever buried badly. I offer you mercy and you count corpses at me.*

"I count corpses," Freya said, "because counting is the work."

She opened her eyes.

The orchard stretched around her, black trees wearing sleeves of ice, and between the rows the snow stood up in places — shapes, half-shapes, the villagers the long winter had taken, held mid-motion like figures in a stopped clock. Farther off, past the orchard wall, the green-black sky of Vhalen pressed against the border-stitch, and beneath it she could feel the city, the drowned country walking, the thousands upon thousands of waiting dead whose names sat in her ledger in her own careful carving.

Her mother's name was among them. Had been among them since the beginning. Maren Delacroix, first entry, first bone, the charm that had started all of this — the one that whispered at her throat through years of guild ledgers and bottled griefs and debts paid in years, until the day it slipped and spoke aloud and cost her everything.

"You want the ledger," Freya said, "because the ledger is the only thing in the world you cannot make. Ice can hold a body. It cannot hold a name. Names have to be *carried*. Someone has to agree to remember, and remembering can't be seized — it can only be given." She lifted the book. "So no. I won't give it. And I'll tell you why, and then I'm going to do the thing you're afraid of, right here, in front of you, and you can spend your mother's voice on begging if you like."

*Little keeper—*

"Names are not mine." Her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack; there was no shame in it, there had never been any shame in grief, only in pretending grief was something else. "Not mine to hoard. Not mine to trade. Not mine to bargain with, not even for her. My mother spent her last hour holding a stranger's child above the water. Do you think she would want me to buy her back by locking every other soul in your ice forever?" She shook her head, and the motion dislodged tears she hadn't known were coming, and she let those go too. "She'd come back from the dead to slap me. And I'd deserve it."

The cold went very still. The whole orchard went still — the hanging snow, the iced branches, the half-shapes of the frozen villagers, all of it pausing as though the world itself were listening.

Then the general said, in her mother's voice, softly: *You would choose a book over her.*

"I would choose *her* over a copy of her," Freya said. "And the difference is the whole point of the work."

She looked down at the ledger.

It was a poor thing, physically. Bone-bound, hide-covered, swollen with inserted pages, stained with river water and blood — some of it Wren's, from the night of the first broken vow, when the oath had flayed them and Freya had learned that her touch could quiet the burning. She had begun it in a holding wagon with a knife and a scrap of charred wood, writing down the names of the burned while the pyres still smoked on the ridge. She had kept it through the liquidated guildhouse, the hole in the map, the hounds that smelled promises, the village that burned itself, the trade at the border-stitch that had taken a third thing neither of them had offered — she still didn't know what the stitch had taken, and some nights she woke reaching for something that wasn't there.

Every page was a person. That was the whole of it. That had always been the whole of it, from the very first ledger she'd kept hidden behind ink and ivory in the guildhouse of Corvane: behind every impossible trade was a person who needed the impossible done, and behind every name was a person who had been one.

"You asked what I'd trade," Freya said. "Here is my answer."

And she began to read.

* * *

She read the way she had taught herself to read in the wagons, loud enough to carry, clear enough to cut, each name given its full weight and its full dignity — not recited but *returned*, the way you return a coat to its owner, the way you return a key.

"Aldous Renner. Ferryman. Drowned pulling passengers from the second wave. His hands are remembered."

The nearest frozen shape — the boy mid-stride in the river valley, brought here with the rest when the winter gathered its harvest — shivered. Snow fell off his shoulders. He did not wake, not fully; the general's grip was too deep for one name alone. But he turned, slowly, like a weathervane finding wind, until his frozen face pointed toward her voice.

The general screamed.

It was not her mother's voice anymore. That was the first victory, and Freya took it and banked it like a coal: the moment she began reading, the stolen voice tore, frayed, reverted to what the general actually was — a vast grinding cold, the sound of a lake freezing all at once, the sound of the first oath-keeper's last breath entombed in the seal. Whatever human throat that voice had once belonged to, centuries of ice had eaten it. Only theft had made it beautiful.

"Betta Solm. Weaver. Froze at her loom in Hallowmere, third day of the siege. Her pattern is remembered. Her daughter wears it."

A second shape turned. Closer now. The snow around Freya's boots began to crawl upward, climbing her ankles, her knees, hungry, furious, trying to reach the book — and where it touched her, she felt the cold try the same trick it had tried with her mother's voice, offering images, offering warmth, offering her father's workshop, her childhood bed, the smell of bread—

She read faster.

"Cormac Vale. Oath-keeper, second rank. Broke his vow to carry children across the border the night of the fall. The crown called it treason. We call it what it was."

The cold clamped down on her like a fist. Frost raced up her legs, her ribs, her arms, silvering her sleeves, creeping toward her hands, toward the ledger — and she understood with sudden clarity that this was the general's real play, not persuasion but seizure, freeze the reader and the reading stops, add one more name to the archive by force.

Through the white roaring in her ears she heard Wren.

"—Freya!" Coughing, dragging, the crunch of boots breaking through crusted snow. "Freya, your hands—"

"Keep—" Her teeth were chattering so hard she could barely shape it. "Keep walking. Don't stop me. If you stop me—"

"I'm not stopping you." Wren's arm came around her from behind, the branded arm, and the brand flared against her shoulder blade — heat against the killing cold, the broken vow burning in defiance of everything its makers built, and where it touched her the frost retreated a finger's width, a grudging truce of two damaged things. "I'm standing behind you. Read."

So she read.

"Tamsin Hale. Six years old. Drowned in the third hour. Her name was the last word her brother ever said, and he is alive in Corvane, and he says it every night, and tonight he will say it and somewhere she will hear it."

The boy in the amber snow took one step. One. His foot came free of the drift with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw, and he stood swaying, half-woken, facing her, and his small frozen face was turned toward the green-black sky of his country.

The general's cold redoubled. Freya felt it in her molars, her spine, the backs of her eyes. The frost climbed past her elbows. The ledger's pages stiffened, ice threading between them, and she realized with distant horror that the book itself was freezing — her witness, her work, the only unseizable thing in the world, being seized after all, page by page, name by name—

No.

She pressed the book flat against the brand.

Wren cried out — the brand blazed, white-hot through both their clothes, the broken oath meeting the frozen archive at the point where her heart beat against the ledger's spine — and the ice *cracked*. Not melted. Cracked, like a river in thaw, like a lie under questioning, fissures racing through the frost on the pages, and the names inside stayed exactly as legible as they had ever been, because names carved in bone do not freeze, do not burn, do not drown. They wait. Waiting was the one thing the dead of Vhalen had mastered.

"Oh," Wren breathed, against her ear. "Oh, it *hates* that."

"It hates being read to," Freya said, and her lips were blue and she was smiling anyway. "Good."

She read on. Cormac Vale. Tamsin Hale. Betta Solm. Aldous Renner. The ferryman and the weaver and the traitor-oath-keeper and the six-year-old, and then the next page, and the next, dozens of names, hundreds, her voice going hoarse and finding new registers of hoarseness to keep going, and all across the orchard the frozen shapes were turning, straining, one by one finding their bearings toward her voice like iron filings toward a lodestone. The general threw everything it had. Snow rose in a howling wall around them. The sky darkened toward true black. Somewhere far away, at the stitched border, she felt the Hunt arriving — torches, pyres, Seraphine Voss's grim procession — and she felt, fainter, stranger, the waiting dead of Vhalen turning *toward* her too, thousands of faces in the drowned streets lifting at once, hearing their names come home across the ice.

*Stop,* the general roared, in no one's voice at all. *STOP.*

"No," Freya said.

It was such a small word for what it contained. She thought about that later — in the gap between this moment and whatever came next — how the whole war had narrowed to a woman with a frozen book saying *no* in a dead orchard. But in the moment there was no room for thought. There was only the next name, and the next, and the cold closing in like a hand, and Wren's brand burning steady against her spine like a second heartbeat, and somewhere in the white roar her mother's voice one last time, no longer a temptation, just a ghost of a ghost, saying something she couldn't quite hear —

— and then the ice on the ledger's final page cleared, and she saw what was written there.

She hadn't written it.

The page was in her hand, her carving, unmistakably hers — and she had never touched it. The letters were hers the way the general's voice had been her mother's: correct in every seam, wrong in every way that mattered. And the name on the page was:

**WREN HALVARD.**

Below it, in her own false handwriting, a single line: *Oath-keeper. Refugee of Vhalen. Sworn twice — once to the crown, once to the door. The second oath has no end date.*

The cold stopped.

The whole orchard stopped — snow hanging, shapes stilled, the general's roar cut off mid-grind — and in the ringing silence Freya heard Wren, behind her, read the page over her shoulder.

Neither of them moved.

"That's not your writing," Wren said quietly.

"I know."

"The stitch," Wren said. "At the border. The third thing it took." Their voice was very level, the way it got when they were most afraid. "It didn't take something from us, Freya. It put something *in*. It's been waiting in the book."

The ledger lay open in her frozen hands, and the false entry gleamed like a fresh grave, and far away at the border the first pyre of the Hunt caught light against the green-black sky.
