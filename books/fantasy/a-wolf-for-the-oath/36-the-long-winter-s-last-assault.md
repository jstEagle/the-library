## 36. The Long Winter's Last Assault

The cold did not come like weather. It came like an accounting.

Freya felt it first in the ledger — a stiffness in the pages, as though the vellum itself were holding its breath. She had been reading names since dawn, her voice worn to a thread, and now, as she reached for the next bone tile in the box beside her, she found it furred with frost. The frost had not been there a moment before. Nothing about this winter happened *a moment before*; it arrived already finished, like a verdict.

"Vhalen," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it had become a kind of bell by which she measured herself. "The winter is spending everything."

The orchard around her confirmed it. The dead trees of Vhalen's border country — trees that had begun, impossibly, to bud again as Freya read the names home — stood silvered and rigid, their new blossoms frozen mid-unfolding, each petal a small white fist clenched against the sky. An hour ago those buds had been soft. She knew because she had touched them, because touching living things had become her way of proving the reading worked, that each name spoken aloud pushed the drowned country one inch further back into the world.

Now the inch was being taken back at a rate she could watch with her own eyes.

Above the orchard, the green-black sky of Vhalen lightened — but wrongly, the way a bruise lightens, not the way morning does. And then the snow began.

It fell upward.

Freya had seen snow fall upward once before, at the old border, when the erased country pressed its face against the skin of the world. That had been wonder. This was different. This snow rose from the ground in columns and sheets, drawn up like breath drawn hard through teeth, and where it rose it *took*. It took the warmth from the blossoms. It took the color from the grass. It took, when she looked down at the ledger open on her knees, the ink.

She watched a line of her own handwriting pale. The name *Ondrel, Marta of the Weavers' Row* faded from brown to grey to the ghost of a grey, and beneath her fingers the vellum went slick and glassy, ice filling the fibers from the inside out. She snatched the page back from the wind that wasn't blowing and heard it crackle like a fired log.

"No," she said. "No — you don't get these. These aren't yours to take."

The general answered, because it always answered now. Not in words. In temperature. The air around her dropped so sharply that her next breath came into her lungs like a swallow of knives, and somewhere behind her ribs, the borrowed warmth of every name she'd read — the little heat each restored person had pressed gratefully into her hands — guttered like a lamp in a draft.

*You were warned,* the cold seemed to say, in her mother's cadence, because the general still wore her mother's stolen voice the way a soldier wears a taken coat. *Everything spends. Even witness.*

Freya gathered the ledger against her chest and ran.

* * *

The city of Vhalen — the part of it that had un-drowned, streets rising wet and gleaming from their long submersion until they stood whole under that bruised sky — was freezing street by street, and it froze in the order she had read them.

That was the cruelty of it. She understood it within three blocks of running. The general was not striking randomly. It was unwinding her work in reverse, block by block, name by name, erasing the most recently restored first — as if to show her the arithmetic of her failure in real time. Here, a fountain she had watched flow that morning stood solid, its water a sculpture of its own last ripple. There, a baker's doorway glistened with rime, and inside, visible through frosted glass, a woman of Vhalen stood with flour to her elbows, motionless, her face turned toward the bread she would never finish.

Not dead. Worse than dead. *Held.* The waiting people of Vhalen could wait centuries — that was what waiting meant here — but they waited *themselves*, named and whole. These frozen ones were being pushed backward, toward the namelessness, toward the smooth-faced nothing that Voss's seal had made of them for thirty years.

Freya skidded to a stop at the mouth of the Weavers' Row and put her palm flat against the frozen door.

"Marta Ondrel," she said, hoarse. "Weavers' Row. You mended sails for the river fleet. Your sister is waiting at the harbor steps. I read her home yesterday."

Nothing. The frost thickened under her hand, climbing her wrist, patient as arithmetic.

Her breath came out in a white flag of surrender. She had perhaps a hundred names left in her voice, and the general had thousands to spend. Every hour she had gained them by reading, the winter had matched, and now it had stopped matching and started doubling, tripling, pouring its entire treasury into one final freeze —

— because it was afraid.

That realization arrived all at once, whole and certain, and it steadied her the way only understanding ever had. Ledgers taught you to read fear in numbers: a debtor who suddenly pays in full, a creditor who suddenly forgives. Something was happening that made the general spend everything *now*, today, this hour. Something it could not afford to let reach completion.

Wren. Wren had the keystone.

Seraphine Voss had died shattering her own seal, entrusting the keystone — the heart of the stitch that held Vhalen shut — to Wren, and Wren had gone into the storm to carry it to her. To Freya. To the choice. Close the door forever and save the living, or give the keystone to her and let her become the door. One answer safe and terrible. One answer merciful and worse.

The general could stop either answer with ice. So the general was trying to stop both.

"All right," Freya whispered to the frozen door, to the frozen baker, to the whole held breath of the city. "Then we do it faster than your winter."

She opened the ledger right there in the street, shielding it with her body, and began to read.

* * *

Reading in the cold was a different art than reading in shelter.

In the guildhouse, in the wagons, in the quiet hours of flight, each name had been a small ceremony: carve, touch, speak, release. Now there was no time for ceremony. She spoke names like a woman throwing sand against a tide, two and three together, her voice cracking on the consonants, and each name bought back a few feet of street — frost retreating from a windowsill, the baker's arm twitching, the fountain groaning as one drop thawed and fell.

And each name took her warmth.

She had known the mechanism for weeks now, had mapped its economy the way she mapped any account: every name read drew heat from her body, a tax levied per syllable, paid in shivers and numb fingertips and a grayness creeping inward from the edges of her vision. In fair weather she could read forty names and rest. In this cold, the tax doubled and doubled again, and the general knew it — she could feel it adjusting the rate, leaning on her specifically, a siege engine aimed at one woman kneeling in a street reading names off ice.

Her lips split. Her blood sang thin. Somewhere past the hundredth name she stopped feeling her feet, and she kept reading, because the baker had moved, because the fountain dripped, because a child at the end of the row had unfrozen mid-step and was looking at her with eyes the color of the drowned harbor, and Freya Delacroix had never in her life left an account unbalanced.

*Freya.*

Her mother's voice, from everywhere. From the snow falling upward, from the ice in the ledger's spine, from the space between her own heartbeat.

*Look what you are doing to yourself, little keeper. All these names, and none of them yours to carry. Put down the book. Come inside where it's warm. Mothers know best — you remember how that sounds, don't you? You've been chasing the echo of it across two countries.*

"You don't get her voice anymore." Freya didn't look up. She kept her finger moving down the column. "I know what you are. You're the crown's first oath-keeper, entombed in a seal you hated, and you are *cold*, and you have forgotten that voices belong to the living. Give her back."

*Give her back?* A laugh moved through the frost, and every window in the Weavers' Row spiderwebbed with ice. *Child, I am giving everyone back. That is what your Seraphine never understood and what you refuse to understand. A sealed door keeps nothing safe. It only keeps things *stored*. I am the honest version of the seal. I am winter doing openly what the crown did in secret — ending the debt. One great freeze, and no more names owed, no more doors, no more countries pretending other countries never happened. Peace, Freya. Cold, but peace.*

"It isn't peace if nobody agreed to it." Her voice was slurring now; her tongue was stiffening in her mouth. "You sound like a ledger I once audited. Everything balanced. Nobody asked."

She read the child's name. The child stepped fully free of the ice and, without a word, without the living having words for it yet, knelt down beside her, a small shoulder pressed against hers, sharing whatever warmth a body of the waiting had to share. Then another of the unfrozen came. Then another. They could not help her read. But they could stand between her and the wind, and they did, a wall of the returned, facing outward against the storm.

Freya read until the letters swam. She read until the ledger's pages froze so stiff they cracked when she turned them, until the ink began its terrible paling again faster than her voice could hold it, until the box of bone tiles at her side rang with frost and the names carved there grew shallow, wearing away like cliffs wear to the sea —

— and through the roar of the upward snow, she heard something else.

Footsteps. Heavy, labored, human. Coming down the Weavers' Row through a storm that had frozen oath-keepers solid in their armor a mile back along the border-stitch.

"Wren," she said. Or tried to. What came out was mostly breath.

But the dead heard her, and the dead parted, and Wren Halvard came stumbling through the gap like a man crossing a river of knives.

* * *

They had walked into a blizzard on the living side of the border. Freya knew that much. She had watched them go four days ago — or was it three; time had gone strange, measured now in names instead of hours — wrapped in every scrap they owned, jaw set in that particular way that meant Wren had decided to survive whether or not survival was convenient.

What stood in front of her now had crossed a country's worth of killing cold carrying a thing that the winter itself wanted buried.

Wren was gray-lipped and staggering. Their cloak was less a garment than an architecture of ice, cracking and shedding shards with every step. Their left hand was black at the fingertips — frostbitten past feeling, she judged, and gripping anyway — and in that ruined grip, held against their chest like a coal they were smuggling through a customs house, was the keystone.

She had imagined it would be beautiful. It was not. It was a wedge of dark stone the size of a heart, seamed with hairline gold where oath-magic had been poured into it over thirty years, and it hummed — she could feel it in her teeth, in the ledger's bones, in the frost itself, which flinched and recoiled from it in a slow radius around Wren's body. Everything the stitch had ever been, folded small enough to hold. Close the door forever. Save the living. End the Hunt's purpose with a single placement, and let Vhalen sleep nameless under the ice until the end of names.

Or give it to her. Open the other road. Become the door.

Wren fell to their knees in front of her. The frozen crowd of Vhalen's returned closed in behind them, forming a windbreak, and in the sudden pocket of stillness Freya saw the brand on Wren's forearm — the mark of the first broken vow, the flayed-looking scar that had burned away everything the crown's oath demanded — blazing now, faintly, gold-gold-gold, in rhythm with the stone.

"You're alive," Wren rasped. Their voice sounded dragged behind them for miles. "The orchard — I came through the orchard. Everything in it was — I thought —"

"I'm hard to kill. I balance accounts." Freya tried to laugh and it came out as a cough, and the cough hurt all the way up from something deep in her chest that she suspected was not going to forgive her. "Give me your hand. The warm one."

"The warm one is holding the book of your face off the ground."

"Then we'll manage."

She got her arms around the ledger and hauled it against her chest, and Wren shifted the keystone into the crook of their frozen arm and reached for her with the black-tipped hand anyway — stubborn, impossible Wren, who had freed her wagon knowing the oath would flay them, who had stood between her and pyres, who had led the dead of a drowned country into battle wearing their broken vow like a banner — and pressed their palms together, and the frost on her wrists retreated half an inch at the contact, and that half-inch felt like the whole world.

"I couldn't place it," Wren said, low, urgent, the words tumbling now after days of silence. "Out there. I kept walking and I kept thinking — either choice ends someone. Seal it and I choose for every soul in Vhalen that they stay nothing forever. Give it to you and I choose for you that you stop being *only you*, and you can't choose it yourself because you're too far in the reading, you're half-ice already, Freya, look at you —"

"I'm looking."

"— and the general knows. It knows this is the hour. That's why the winter's like this. It spent *everything*, it emptied the vaults, because if it freezes us tonight there's no tomorrow where anyone chooses anything." Wren's eyes were fever-bright in the frozen face. "So it comes down to this street. To us. To right now."

Above them, the upward snow thickened, rose higher, bent inward — the whole storm beginning to curl around the Weavers' Row like a hand closing. At the far end of the street, the frost built itself upward into a shape. Tall. Thin. Crowned in icicles that hung like a regalia, wearing the suggestion of a robe and, horribly, the suggestion of her mother's posture — shoulders slightly rolled, head tilted when listening — a silhouette assembled out of every cold thing that had ever taken a voice.

The general had come to watch the last entry close.

The returned dead of Vhalen turned to face it, and did not step back.

Freya looked at the ledger in her arms. Ice had sealed it nearly shut; she could see the pale ghosts of names through the frozen cover, her own handwriting embalmed. She looked at the keystone in Wren's grip, humming its single note of *forever*. She looked at Wren — at the frostbite, the blaze of the broken brand, the exhaustion that had burned everything else away down to one bare, stubborn point of will.

Two answers. Safe and terrible. Merciful and worse. Both of them clutched in their hands in a freezing street while winter leaned in to listen.

"Wren," she said, and her voice cracked, and she found she didn't care, because her mother had taught her that a cracked voice still carries. "There's a third entry. There's always a third entry. I've spent my whole life finding them in books everyone said were balanced."

The general's silhouette took one grinding step closer, and the temperature dropped so fast the air itself crackled, and the keystone in Wren's hand flared gold against the coming white —

"What third entry?" Wren breathed.

"Put the stone down," said Freya, "and swear."
