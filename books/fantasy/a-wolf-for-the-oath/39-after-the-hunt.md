## 39. After the Hunt

Spring came wrong first.

That was how I would remember it, later — not the thaw itself but the wrongness of it, the way the world had to learn warmth again like a soldier learning to walk on a leg that had been splinted too long. In Corvane the snow did not melt so much as surrender, retreating in gray ridges along the gutters, and for three weeks the river ran brown and swollen and full of things it had no business carrying: fence posts, a cradle, an entire door from a house no one could name. The orchards bloomed in April out of sheer panic, blossoms bursting before there were leaves to hold them, and then frost came one night in May and killed every pear in the valley, and the farmers stood in their ruined rows and laughed — actually laughed — because after the winter we had survived, a dead harvest was almost funny.

I was in Corvane when the frost came. I had gone back for the ledgers.

Not the guild's old ledgers. Those were ash, or evidence, or both. I mean the new one — the one Freya had started in a holding wagon outside the pyre-city with a bone stylus and a scrap of vellum, the ledger of names given back. It had grown fat over the winter, stuffed with pages she carved at night while I kept watch and pretended not to listen to the dead murmuring through her hands like water through reeds. She had left it with me in March when she went through the door for her monthly reading, because she said — and this was Freya all over, precise even about her own sacrifice — *you are the answerer now, Wren; you should know what you're answering.*

So I carried it through Corvane's broken streets in a satchel against my chest, under my coat, the way I had once carried arrest warrants, and I felt the difference in my own body like a changed season. A year ago I had walked these same streets with a crown seal on my breast and a vow in my blood that flayed me for every kind thought. Now the brand on my arm was quiet — not gone, never gone, but turned, the way a blade is turned into a plowshare, still iron, still shaped by fire, but pointed at the earth instead of the throat.

The frost night, I sheltered in a tavern called the Drowned Bell, which had been called something else before Vhalen fell and its refugees renamed half the taverns in the borderlands without anyone quite noticing. The room was full of farmers mourning their pears and soldiers who were no longer soldiers, drinking with the specific desperation of men whose war had ended without a battle. Because that was the thing about the Hunt: it had not been defeated. It had simply been *disgraced*, which is slower and more thorough.

The order came down from the regency council in the third week of spring, read aloud in every market square by heralds who could not meet anyone's eyes. The Hunt was disbanded. Its records were seized. Its huntmasters — those who had not followed Seraphine Voss through the door and died there, or worse than died — were summoned to inquiry. And every person the Hunt had registered, every name in its lists of the gifted, was struck from those lists by official decree, as though ink could unmake what fire had already eaten.

It was not justice. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only vow I have left. Justice would have been the pyres never lit. What the council offered was apology-shaped arithmetic: they counted the burned, they paid the survivors' families in land and coin, they pulled down the pyre-scaffolds in the capital and built a fountain where the largest one had stood. Children threw copper coins into it and made wishes. I watched them do it. I stood there with my scarred arm hidden in my sleeve and my dead country's name stitched into my bones, and I could not decide whether the fountain was obscene or the most hopeful thing the crown had ever built.

Probably both. Most true things are both.

* * *

The guildhouse of Corvane reopened on a bright morning in late spring, and I was there to see it, standing across the street with my hood up like a man casing a house he means to rob.

They had chosen the building deliberately — not the old guildhall, which the Hunt had gutted and which still smelled of smoke if you knew how to smell, but a narrow merchant's house on Tanner's Row with good light and a cellar deep enough to hide a person, which everyone pretended was for storing wine. Someone had hung a sign above the door, freshly painted, the letters still tacky:

**GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE TRADES**
*All trades weighed. All debts honored. All names remembered.*

That last line was new. That last line was hers.

Freya had argued for a month about whether to reopen at all. We had fought about it in the green country, in the doorway that was becoming us, in the thin hours between readings when her hands shook too badly to carve and mine ached from answering. She said the guild had sold her. She said the ledgers had become a noose once and would again, that any institution that keeps records will eventually keep lists, and lists will eventually become orders, and orders will eventually become smoke. I said she was right about everything except the conclusion. I said the problem was never the record — it was what the record was *for*. A ledger that counts what is taken is a weapon. A ledger that counts what is returned is a promise.

She had looked at me for a long time, the way she does, as if reading my ribs like columns of figures. Then she said, "You've become insufferable since you stopped being able to lie," and kissed me, and that was how I knew she'd agreed.

The reopening was small. Maybe forty people in the street, most of them guild survivors, some of them families of the burned, a few strangers drawn by rumor — and rumor had grown strange and wonderful over the winter, as rumors do when they touch something real. They said there was a woman who could hear the dead. They said there was a door between countries where you could speak to your grandmother one last time, properly, with her own voice, if you brought her name carved in bone. They said the woman never charged for the names. They said the man who stood beside her had once been an oath-keeper of the Hunt and had torn his own vow out by the roots, and that he answered the dead himself now, spoke *to* them, and that no one who passed the door left grieving the same way they arrived.

All of it true. Even the parts that sounded like invention.

Inside, the new guildhouse smelled of sawdust and fresh ink. The counting room had been rebuilt around a single long table of pale wood, and on it lay the ledger — my burden all winter, finally home — open to its current page. Around the walls, instead of the old trade-catalogs and debt-charts, Freya had commissioned carvings: bone panels, each one bearing a single name, mounted in rows like windows. Not trophies. Windows. You could stand in front of any of them and put your hand flat against the bone, and if the name belonged to someone who loved you, you would feel it warm.

A girl of perhaps nine stood doing exactly that when I entered, her palm pressed to a panel near the window, her mother behind her with a hand on her shoulder. The panel bore the name Maren Ostwick, burned in the second week of the Hunt, registered by an oath-keeper whose name I knew because it had been mine to countersign.

I had signed that warrant. I remembered the weight of the pen.

The girl turned and saw me looking, and something in my face must have told her, because children always know, and she took her hand off the bone and stepped slightly in front of her mother, chin up, the way you guard a door.

"It's all right," her mother said quietly, though I don't think she believed it either.

"I countersigned her registration," I said. My voice came out flat and formal, oath-keeper's cadence, the old machinery grinding even now. "Wren Halvard. I can't undo it. I won't insult you by asking forgiveness. But if you ever want anything from me — anything within my power — it's yours, and I'll know it's not payment, because nothing is."

The silence stretched. Outside, someone laughed; inside, the bone panels seemed to lean in.

Then the mother did something I did not expect. She walked to the table, opened the ledger to its newest page, and asked, "How do we add a name? Her father. He died two winters ago, before all this, plain death, no burning — but we never got to say—"

"Third column," said a voice from the doorway, and Freya came in with the morning's bread and a face that dared the room to make this heavy. "Name, the words you wanted to say, and whether you want it read here or at the door. Door's better. Here's faster. Bread's free either way."

And just like that the room exhaled, and the line began to form, and grief was given shape and ceremony and a place to set down its bags.

* * *

I worked the door that first day, because that was my post now and I had chosen it the way I had once chosen duty — completely, without exit clauses.

The door itself stood in the green country, a day's walk past the stitched border, in what had been the market square of a city called Vel Sarath. Since the general dissolved — since the long winter broke apart into ordinary honest snow on the day we bound ourselves to the keystone — Vhalen had been healing at its own pace, which was neither fast nor slow but *certain*, the way a body heals. Streets un-drowned themselves in patches. The green-black sky thinned toward something like evening. The waiting dead no longer crowded the threshold; they had spread back into their city, tending gardens that grew in slow motion, repairing roofs that would never rain on them again, living their unfinished lives at the temperature of memory.

And through the middle of it, the door: a seam of light shaped like whatever the viewer needed. Most saw a gate. Some saw a kitchen doorway. One old soldier saw a trench ladder, and climbed down it weeping, and came back up an hour later clean in a way I recognized because I had felt it myself.

My work was the answering. When a name was read — Freya read, always Freya, her voice going colder with each name as the warmth drained from her into the words — the dead crossed partway, and for a few minutes they were present enough to hear and be heard. But presence is not conversation. The dead speak in currents, in pressure, in the language of before-and-after. Someone has to translate. Someone has to take the grandmother's murmur and turn it into *tell her the blue dress was the right choice*, and take the daughter's sobbing question and carry it back down as *she knows, she always knew, ask her about the garden instead.*

That was mine. The brand that had once punished disobedience now burned gently whenever a translation was needed, a compass needle pointing at truth, and I had learned to trust it the way sailors trust a wind that has never yet lied to them.

We worked until dark, and then past dark, because there were forty names waiting and Freya refused to triage anymore — not since the pyres in the green country, not since the lowest winter of her life. By the last name her lips were gray and her hands shook so badly I had to hold the bone steady while she read, our fingers laced around it like two people carrying one heavy thing, which is what we were.

Afterward we sat on the steps of a drowned cathedral that was only ankle-deep now, eating the bread she'd bought in Corvane, watching Vhalen's slow lamps come alight one by one in windows that had been dark for twenty years.

"The guild opens tomorrow," she said. "Properly. With fees."

"Fees?"

"Impossible trades require impossible prices. That was always the point. People value what costs them." She tore the bread and gave me the larger half without appearing to notice she'd done it. "But the names stay free. Forever. I'm writing it into the charter. *No fee shall ever attach to a name.* Let some future guildmaster try to sell a name and find the founding law standing in his throat."

"They'll try anyway."

"Of course. Everything gets tried." She leaned her head on my shoulder, and I felt the cold of her gradually warming, the day's spent warmth returning to her the way it always did now — slowly, through contact, through me, through whatever the keystone had braided between us. "That's why the ledger matters. Not to stop the trying. To remember what was taken when the stopping fails."

I thought about the girl in the guildhouse, guarding her mother with her chin. I thought about the fountain full of wishing-coins. I thought about Seraphine Voss, who had shattered her own seal and died believing she was paying a debt, and whose keystone now beat somewhere beneath my sternum like a second heart, patient, impartial, holding the door neither open nor shut.

"Seraphine's inquiry is next month," I said. "Posthumous. The council wants to decide whether she was a traitor or a martyr."

"What do you want them to decide?"

"Nothing. Deciding is how they got the Hunt." I watched a lamp flicker alive in a tower that had been underwater since before I could walk. "Let them argue forever. Argument is safer than certainty."

Freya made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You've become a philosopher."

"I've become unemployed. Same thing."

* * *

Summer, when it finally came, came right.

Not perfect — the pears never recovered, and the border villages stayed half-empty, and there were nights when Freya woke gasping with a name stuck in her teeth, some child's name from the pyres that she had read too late and could not stop carrying. There were nights I woke reaching for a vow that wasn't there anymore and had to find my hand in hers before I remembered what I was instead. We were not healed. We were *healing*, which is a different country with harder roads.

But summer came right in the ways that mattered. The river ran clear. The frost-killed orchards put out secondary bloom, stubborn and late, and the bees found them. And in Corvane, on the first of the high summer markets, the Guild of Impossible Trades posted its first public ledger page in the window facing the street, copied out fair in Freya's smallest hand, and the whole city came to read it.

It was not a list of trades. It was a list of returns.

*Maren Ostwick — name returned to her daughter, Ilse. Words delivered: "the blue dress was the right choice."*

*Tomas Bell — name returned to his brother. Words delivered: "not your fault; it never was."*

*Yeva Delacroix — name returned to her daughter, Freya. Words delivered:* and here the fair copy stopped, because some entries are not for windows, and below it Freya had written only: *read at the door, in full, witnessed by Wren Halvard. Account closed in peace.*

People stood before that window for hours. Old women touched the glass. A Hunt veteran in civilian clothes stood across the street all afternoon, unable to come closer, unwilling to leave, until Freya walked out and stood beside him without a word, and after a while he crossed the street and read the names of everyone he had registered, and wept, and asked if there was work for a man who knew how to keep records honestly.

There was. There always is, in a guild of impossible trades.

On the last day of summer I sat in the counting room after closing, updating the master index, and I noticed something that stopped my pen. The ledger had begun, months ago, with names of the burned — the Hunt's victims, the ones we read back first, triage and witness. But page by page, season by season, the character of the entries had shifted. Fewer names rescued from erasure. More names *given freely* — the plain dead, the ordinary grieved, the mothers and brothers and neighbors of Corvane who had lost people the old way, the human way, and had heard about a door.

The ledger of the condemned had become a ledger of the mourned. The emergency had become a practice. The wound had become, if not a purpose, then at least a craft.

I wrote that observation in the margin, because I am still an auditor at heart and margins are where auditors tell the truth. Then I closed the book, and banked the lamp, and went upstairs to where Freya was carving by candlelight — a new panel, a new name, her stylus whispering — and I stood in the doorway a moment before she sensed me, the way she always does, and looked up.

"Last entry of the season," she said, and held the bone up to the light.

And I saw the name carved there, and my blood went cold and warm at once, because it was not a client's name, not a stranger's, not anyone from the pyres or the registers.

It was a name from Vhalen. From the far side of the door. A name belonging to one of the waiting dead who had never once, in all these months, asked to be read home — because the waiting dead do not need returning; they are already where their names live.

Which meant someone had carried this name *out*. Which meant someone had crossed the door the other way.

Which meant, though I did not yet know how or why or what it would cost us, that the traffic through the door had just changed direction — and the dead of Vhalen, for the first time since the country fell, wanted something from the world of the living.

Freya met my eyes across the candlelight, and I saw she had already understood, and that her hands, for the first time in a year, were perfectly steady.

"We'd better open early tomorrow," she said.
