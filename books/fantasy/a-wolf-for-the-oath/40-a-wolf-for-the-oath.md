## 40. A Wolf for the Oath

The door did not look like a door anymore.

That was the first thing Freya had to explain to every traveler who came up the stitched road from Corvane and stopped, squinting, at the place where the map's hole used to be. They expected a gate of black iron or a seam of fire or at the very least something with hinges. What they found instead was two people standing in an orchard that had died in one winter and woken in another — two people, and between them a space of air that shimmered like heat off summer stone, though snow lay on the grass to either side.

"Where's the door?" they always asked.

"You're looking at it," Wren would say, and then, because Wren had never once in their life let a sentence stand without its counterweight: "Both halves."

Freya had learned to feel the door the way she'd once felt the bone charms at her throat — as a hum, a pressure, a second pulse under her own. When she stood in the threshold-space with her hands open at her sides, she could feel Vhalen behind her like a warm room, and Corvane before her like a cold one, and everything that crossed between passed through her first. Not through her body, exactly. Through the part of her that had been listening all her life.

The dead came through singing.

Not always. Some came through silent as falling snow, heads bowed, carrying whatever they had carried into death. But most of them sang — old Vhaleni harvest songs, gutter-work songs, the lullaby about the wolf who kept the oath, which every child in the drowned country learned before they learned their own name. Freya had heard it so many times now that she no longer flinched when the words reached her in her mother's voice. That voice had been stolen once, worn by a general made of winter, weaponized against her in a frozen orchard while she read names until her lips went blue. But the general was gone. The general had come apart into ordinary, honest snow on the day the keystone accepted a new oath, and what remained of her mother's voice in the world belonged only to the dead woman who had actually owned it.

She was coming through today.

Freya knew it the way she knew weather now. The ledger on its stand beside her — the new ledger, the one the reopened Guild of Impossible Trades had commissioned, bound in grey leather with no title on the cover because its contents were not a trade but a trust — grew warm against her hip. The pages stirred without wind. And out past the threshold, in the green-black light of Vhalen's un-drowned streets, a figure was walking toward the door with the unhurried gait of someone who had waited eleven years and could wait another hour.

* * *

Mornings began with the count.

It was Wren's ritual, inherited from their oath-keeping days and repurposed so thoroughly that the original was barely visible beneath it, the way a riverbed shapes a new river. Every dawn Wren walked the border-stitch from end to end — three miles of shimmering nothing threaded through orchard and field and the ruins of the old customs house — and counted the crossings. Dead going home to be named. Living going in to visit graves that were no longer graves but gardens. The occasional living soul of Vhalen coming out into Corvane for the first time in a generation, blinking at a sky the color of ordinary sky, weeping at the smell of bread.

Wren kept the counts in a hand that had once written arrest warrants. Freya had seen the old warrants, in the seized guildmaster's ledger, in Wren's neat merciless script: *Freya Delacroix, bone-carver, gift confirmed, disposition: pyre.* She had forgiven them for that page long ago — forgiveness being, she had learned, less a feeling than a practice, like bookkeeping, done daily whether or not one felt like it — but she had asked to keep the page. It lived now in the back of the new ledger, the way a scar lives on a body. Proof of what the counting had been for, before.

This morning the count was forty-one souls outbound, six inbound, and one living woman standing at the threshold with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for Freya to finish washing her hands.

"You're nervous," Wren observed. They sat on the customs-house step, sharpening a knife they no longer needed for anything except bread and the occasional stubborn knot. The brand on their forearm — the broken crown-oath, the thing that had flayed them from the inside when they freed her wagon, the thing that had finally burned itself clean of the crown and chosen — had settled into a pale silver mark shaped like nothing at all, or possibly like a door, depending on the light. "You've read four hundred names since midwinter. You read Seraphine's name in front of her own grave and didn't spill a drop of tea."

"That was different." Freya dried her hands on her apron. "Seraphine wanted to be found. My mother has been waiting eleven years in a country that wasn't on the map, and I'm about to tell her that her daughter became a piece of architecture."

"She'll want to know if the architecture is load-bearing."

"Wren."

"I'm serious." Wren set down the knife. Their face did the thing it did perhaps twice a year now — the careful oath-keeper's mask sliding sideways, letting through the person underneath, the refugee child from a drowned country who had been allowed to forget and had spent a lifetime paying interest on the forgetting. "Freya. She watched you carve bones your whole childhood. She hid you in a guildhouse full of impossible trades and told you to keep your head down and your ledgers clean. Whatever you are now — door, hinge, keeper, whatever word the priests are using this month — you are still her daughter doing exactly what she taught you. Names matter. Write them down. Say them out loud. Don't let anyone take them." A pause. "She'll probably just ask if you're eating enough."

Freya laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been wound tight since dawn, and she was grateful, and she hated how easily Wren could do that, and she loved it, and both of those things had stopped being contradictory somewhere in a frozen orchard with a week left to live.

"Forty-one outbound," she said, picking up the ledger. "Let's go to work."

* * *

The reading took the morning, as readings did.

The mechanism had never changed, only deepened. A name spoken by the door returned a person to themselves — that was the discovery of the green country, the miracle and the cost, the reason Freya's body had grown a permanent chill the healers of two nations could not explain. Each name read aloud gave warmth away. In the early days, in Vhalen, she had read until her teeth chattered and her vision frosted at the edges, triaging names like grain in a siege, deciding which of the waiting dead could bear to remain unnamed a little longer. Wren had held her through the worst nights, pressing their brand-scarred arm against her spine, and she had learned that her touch quieted the brand and their touch warmed her, and that this was not magic but simply what two people did when they had decided, without ever saying it in so many words, to keep each other alive.

Now the warmth came back differently. Now she was the door, and the door did not spend itself; it circulated. A name read through the threshold passed through her and out the other side, and what she lost on one side of the crossing she recovered on the other, like water finding its level. She was colder than any living person ought to be. She would always be colder than any living person ought to be. But she was not dying, and the dead were not waiting, and the seal that Seraphine Voss had built to keep a country dead had become instead a hinge that let it live.

The first name of the morning was a cooper from the Vhaleni lowlands who had drowned with his city and waited thirty years in the green dark, patient as timber. His wife had come through from Corvane last spring, one of the living who had escaped the fall as a child, and she stood at the threshold now with her hands folded, and when Freya spoke his name — *Aldric Voss-mer-Teune, cooper, father of two, mender of barrels and of quarrels* — the man stepped out of the green light and into himself, and wept, and embraced his wife across a gap of thirty years, and walked with her down the stitched road into a country he had never seen but that his wife had made a life in anyway.

The second name was a girl of nine who had been on the pyre-lists of the Hunt and had died in the holding wagons before Wren's rebellion meant anything. Her name came back to her in her mother's arms. Freya did not watch that reunion. Some things were not for the door to see.

And then the bundle of oilcloth moved, and the last name of the day stepped up to the threshold, and there was no more putting it off.

* * *

Her mother looked exactly as Freya remembered and completely unlike it, which is the trick of the dead, or perhaps the trick of memory. Eleven years older in Freya's mind; not a day older in the flesh. The same ink-stained fingers — her mother had been a scrivener before the fall, before the drowning, before she had smuggled her daughter out through a border that was busy stitching itself shut and paid for the passage with a name that the stitch took as toll. The same crooked smile. The same eyes that had looked at a small girl carving animal shapes into chicken bones and seen, instead of a childish game, a gift that would get her killed, and had begun teaching her to hide it before she taught her to read.

"Mama," Freya said. Her voice came out very small for someone who was, technically, a piece of international infrastructure.

"Little wren," her mother said — and Freya heard Wren make a sound behind her, soft and involuntary, because *wren* was not only a bird in Vhalen but a word for a small fierce thing that guards the house, and her mother had called her that all her life without knowing that one day a small fierce thing named Wren Halvard would guard her daughter across two countries and back. "Look at you. Look at this. Is this what you've been doing? Building doors?"

"It seemed useful."

"Useful." Her mother's laugh was the same too, low and rough, a laugh for private jokes. "Your grandmother carved bones that whispered. Your grandfather traded three years of his life for a boat that didn't sink. Our family does not do *useful*, little wren. We do impossible, and we apologize for the inconvenience afterward." Her eyes went past Freya, to the figure on the customs-house step, and softened in a way that made Freya's throat close. "And this is the one. The wolf."

"I'm sorry?" Wren said.

"The lullaby." Her mother's smile went crooked again. "*The wolf who kept the oath*, do they not sing it in Corvane? The wolf sworn to devour the flock who looked at the flock one night and chose instead to keep them. Every child in Vhalen knows it. We thought it was only a story." She looked at Wren the way one looks at a legend that has turned out to be tired and human and sitting on a step with a bread knife. "Sworn to deliver my daughter to the fire. And instead you carried her ledger."

"Somebody had to," Wren said. "It was heavy."

It was, Freya thought, the most honest summary of their entire history that anyone had yet produced.

Her mother stepped closer to the threshold — right to its edge, where the air shimmered — and Freya braced herself for the question she had dreaded for weeks: *What did it cost? What did they take from you? Was it worth it?* Her mother had paid a name to the border-stitch once. She knew what thresholds charged.

But her mother only reached out — not through the threshold, never through, the door's law and her own good manners — and laid her palm flat against the air, and Freya laid her palm against the other side, and between their hands the shimmer steadied into something almost like glass, almost like skin, almost like the membrane between a mother and the child she is still, after everything, trying to keep warm.

"You're cold," her mother said.

"I'm a door, Mama. Doors are cold. It's in the job description."

"And the work? Does it hurt?"

Freya considered lying. She had hidden her gift behind ink and ivory for twenty years; the habit ran deep. But this was the woman who had taught her the hiding, and mothers see through their own lessons.

"Some days," she said. "Some days I hear four hundred voices at once and I am a building and not a person. Some days I miss being only myself so much I can taste it." She turned her hand over, palm up, against the shimmer. "And some days a cooper walks out of the green dark and thirty lost years put themselves back together in front of me, and I wouldn't trade it for anything the crown ever offered, or the general, or the stitch. I keep the names, Mama. All of them. Nobody gets erased on my watch. Not ever again."

Her mother closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and she was smiling, and it was the smile from the kitchen in Vhalen, from before, from the country that had been drowned and erased and cut out of every map and was now, because of a ledger and a broken oath and a door with two names on it, simply a country again.

"Then you're not a door," she said. "Doors don't keep anything. Doors only let through. You're a keeper. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." She stepped back, gathering her skirts, and Freya understood with a jolt that she was preparing to cross — that this was it, this unremarkable morning, this was the hour toward which eleven years and forty chapters of grief had been walking. "Well. Are you going to say it properly, or must I wait in the green dark while my daughter improvises?"

Freya opened the ledger.

Her hands did not shake. Twenty years of meticulous bookkeeping had built hands that did not shake, and even becoming a hinge between the living and the dead had not undone that, and she was suddenly, fiercely grateful to the frightened girl in the guildhouse at Corvane who had balanced bottled grief and debts paid in years and thought she was only hiding. She had not been hiding. She had been practicing.

She found the page. The last page. It had been blank since midwinter, waiting, the way a chair waits.

"*Maren Delacroix,*" Freya read, and her mother's true name — the one the stitch had taken as toll, the one the drowned country had forgotten, the one that had existed for eleven years only in a daughter's memory and a daughter's carving — settled back onto her mother like a coat returning to its owner. "*Scrivener. Keeper of accounts. Mother of one. Teacher of hiding, and of hoping. She paid a name so her child could keep hers, and her child spent it keeping everyone else's.*"

The green light took her gently. For a moment her mother stood in the threshold-space with her, inside the door, inside the hum, and Freya felt her there the way she felt everything now — as warmth passing through — and her mother pressed her lips to Freya's forehead, and the warmth of it stayed.

"Read the last line," her mother whispered. "I wrote it. Before the water. I wrote it for whoever kept the accounts after me."

Freya looked down. On the last page, beneath the entry in her own hand, in faded ink she had somehow never noticed until this moment — ink that had been waiting, the way everything in Vhalen had waited — her mother's handwriting:

*A ledger is a promise that no one was here for nothing.*

*Keep it, little wren.*

*Feed the wolf.*

* * *

They closed the ledger together at dusk.

This too was ritual now. Freya carried it to the stand; Wren lit the lamp; and they stood a moment in the doorway of the old customs house, looking out at the border-stitch glowing faintly gold in the failing light, traffic of souls moving through it in both directions, slow and orderly and free. No seal. No hunt. No pyres. The Hunt itself had been disbanded in disgrace by a crown that preferred to forget its own enthusiasm, and the guildhouse in Corvane had reopened its doors under a new guildmaster — a cooper's widow, as it happened, a woman who understood accounts — with a sign in the window: *Impossible trades undertaken. Impossible debts forgiven. Names restored free of charge.*

"Do you ever regret it?" Wren asked. They asked it maybe once a season, always at dusk, always watching the door rather than her. "Being neither one thing nor the other. Neither wholly in Vhalen nor wholly out. Neither living quite as the living live, nor resting as the dead rest. Neither—" they gestured vaguely at themselves, at the space between them, at the whole improbable arrangement of their lives— "mine. Wholly mine. You share yourself with a border now."

Freya thought about it honestly, because Wren deserved honesty and had earned it at the price of an armful of brands and a lifetime of duty unlearned.

"I regret that I'll never again be small," she said. "Small enough to fit inside one life. But I was never small, Wren. I only pretended. I heard the dead whispering through my carvings when I was six years old, and I spent twenty years pretending I hadn't, and pretending nearly got me burned." She took Wren's hand — the scarred one, the branded one, the one whose touch had warmed her through the worst nights of the reading — and pressed it flat against the ledger's cover, over the heart of it, where the pages slept. "This isn't a smaller life than the one I pretended to have. It's the first one that was ever actually mine. Because I didn't choose it alone. That's the whole point. A door needs two hinges, or it's just a wall with ambitions."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then, quietly, in the voice they used for oaths — the real voice, the one underneath the oath-keeper's training, the one that had sworn a new vow on a keystone in the middle of a battle and meant it more than anything the crown had ever wrung out of them — they said:

"Then we keep it. Both of us. As long as there are names."

"As long as there are names," Freya agreed. "And after, if anyone comes asking."

"After," Wren said, "we teach the next keeper. Someone will come up that road eventually. Someone with a gift they're hiding behind ink and ivory, or behind a uniform, or behind a smile. Someone the world tried to make into a list." Their thumb moved over the ledger's cover, slow, deliberate, the way one touches a promise to confirm it is solid. "We'll be here."

Outside, the last travelers of the day passed through the shimmer — a dead soldier going home to a name, a living grandmother going in to plant roses on a grave that had become a garden — and neither of them glanced at the two figures in the customs-house doorway, because to them the door was simply the door, the way a river is simply a river, and rivers do not require thanks.

But far down the stitched road, where Corvane's lamplighters were beginning their rounds, a small figure had stopped. A child, travel-worn, alone, clutching something white to their chest. Even at this distance, even through the hum of the threshold, Freya heard it — the faint, unmistakable whisper of carved bone speaking a name that appeared on no register in any living land.

The child looked up at the glow of the door. And began, wearily, stubbornly, to walk.

Freya reached for the lamp.

"Another one," she said.

Wren was already opening the ledger.
