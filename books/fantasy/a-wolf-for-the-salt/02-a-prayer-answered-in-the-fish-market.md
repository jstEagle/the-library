## 2. A Prayer Answered in the Fish Market

The fish market of Brineholt began screaming before dawn.

Theo Almeida heard it from the window of the Salt Guild's harbor house — a sound that started as ordinary market noise, the bawling and haggling of a thousand voices opening their stalls in the dark, and then changed key. He had kept ledgers long enough to know when a number was wrong before he read it; he knew this sound the same way. It was the sound of arithmetic breaking.

He dressed quickly, took his ledger case, and went down into streets already running with water.

Not seawater. That was the first thing wrong, and every fishwife on the Salt Row would tell him so before the hour was out, each one gripping his sleeve with hands scented of brine and herring scales: *not seawater, master clerk, sweet as a well, sweet as rain on a mountain, and where did it come from, because the tide didn't do it, the tide has never done it.* Three blocks of the oldest quarter — Fishgut Lane, the Netmenders' Walk, the whole crooked sprawl around the old fish market square — stood ankle-deep, then knee-deep, in water so clear you could see the cobbles shining through it like coins at the bottom of a font.

And it was rising. Slowly. Patiently. As if something were pouring it from a height no one could see.

* * *

By full light, the Guild's local factor had given up pretending to manage the crowd and Theo had given up pretending not to be the man everyone wanted. Word traveled fast in Brineholt that the Salt Guild had sent someone. The Guild traded in impossible things, and when the impossible arrived uninvited, people looked to its clerks the way sailors look at the sky.

"Three blocks," said the factor, a damp little man named Ostrell whose ledger-ink had run down his wrists like old wounds. "Measured it myself an hour past. Three blocks flooded, four feet deep at the market square, and the water level hasn't moved since sunrise. Not risen, not fallen. Held." He wiped his face. "Held, Master Almeida. Water doesn't hold."

"Everything holds if something is holding it." Theo crouched at the edge of the flood and let his fingers break the surface. The water was cool and tasted — he spat, wiped his mouth — sweet. Not fresh exactly. *Sweet*, the way a name is sweet when you haven't heard it spoken in years. There was a taste underneath it too, faint and mineral, that made the back of his teeth ache. He knew that taste. Every Guild clerk did. It was the taste of the vaults, of bottled storms and stoppered years, of everything the Guild kept because no one else could afford to.

It was the taste of things that had been *paid for*.

"Show me where it started."

Ostrell led him through streets where shopkeepers were hauling goods to upper floors and children were being carried on shoulders, half-frightened and half-delighted, because children always are, even at the end of the world. The fish market square opened before them like a held breath.

The stalls still stood. That was the second thing wrong. A flood that comes off the sea takes stalls with it, splinters them, leaves wreckage. This water had risen around the market the way a guest rises to greet you — carefully, courteously, leaving the mackerel carts intact, leaving the awnings dry above the waterline, leaving the great stone fountain at the square's center standing in six feet of clear water like an island in a lake that had never been there yesterday.

And the fountain was running backward.

Theo stopped. He made himself look at it properly, the way he looked at a column of figures that refused to sum. The fountain's basin, dry for forty years by all accounts, was now brimming — and its central spout, the carved figure of some forgotten harbor god with a broken trident, was drawing water *up*. A thin silver thread rose from the surface of the basin, climbed the statue's chest, its throat, its open mouth, and vanished into the air above, where it spread into a fine mist that fed the flood below. The water went up. Then it came back down everywhere else.

A circuit. A circulation. Something drinking and pouring at once.

"That statue," Theo said quietly. "Whose is it?"

"No one remembers." Ostrell's voice had gone thin. "That's the trouble, isn't it? No one remembers. Old Man Corvo used to say there was a name for it, carved on the base, but the carving wore away before his grandfather's time. We've called it the Fisher for as long as anyone can recall. Just — the Fisher."

"The Fisher," Theo repeated, and wrote nothing down, because some words you don't want in ink until you understand what they cost.

* * *

He found her at noon, or she found him — Maren Osta, fishwife, fifty years old, with forearms like anchor chain and eyes red-rimmed from a night that had unmade her life's understanding of the world. She sat on the steps of the netmenders' hall with her boots off, her feet in the floodwater, and when Theo approached she didn't startle. She'd been expecting someone with a ledger eventually.

"You're the Guild's man."

"I am."

"Then write this down." She lifted her chin toward the drowned square. "Yesterday morning I knelt right there, at my stall, gutting the first catch, and I prayed."

Theo sat down beside her, slowly, the way you sit near a strange dog. "What did you pray for?"

"Water." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's the joke of it, master clerk. I'm a fishwife in a city that drinks imported well-water at a copper a pail, and my husband's been dead two years, and my knees are shot, and I was so tired of paying for water that I prayed for it. Out loud. To nobody. To whoever." Her jaw worked. "My mother prayed like that once. And her mother. All of us, down the generations — we pray to nobody because nobody ever answers, and it costs nothing to talk to nobody. It's free. It's the only free thing left."

"And now?"

"And now three blocks are drowning in the sweetest water anyone's ever tasted, and it won't rise past the fourth street, and it won't go down, and my stall is floating." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were terrible with a question. "Master clerk, what did I *owe*?"

There it was. Theo felt the question land in his chest like a hook finding flesh. Because that was the whole of the Guild's law, the first law, older than any charter: *nothing answers for free.* Every miracle in the ledgers had a price attached — thirty years here, a firstborn's name there, a memory, a shadow, a season of joy. The gods of the old contracts had been greedy, exacting, *legible*. You could trade with a thing that wanted payment. You could survive a thing that kept accounts.

But Maren Osta had paid nothing. Had offered nothing. Had talked to nobody, for free.

"I don't know yet," Theo said honestly.

"Then find out." She put her feet deeper into the water. "Because I've drunk it. Everyone has. It tastes like—" She searched. "Like being a child. Like the summer before anything went wrong. And I keep thinking, if this is a debt, I'll pay it. I'll pay anything. But if it's a *gift*—" Her voice failed her. "If it's a gift, then the world has changed, and I don't know the rules anymore, and neither does anyone else. Do they?"

"No," Theo said. "They don't."

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he did best: auditing.

The Guild's harbor house gave him access to the district records, and he went through them the way a surgeon goes through a body — looking for the wound. Bottled storms sold to sea-captains. Borrowed years pawned by gamblers. Names-that-were-never-spoken, held in trust for families who'd paid to have certain ancestors forgotten. He cross-referenced every entry against the flood: no storm unaccounted for, no year returned, no name loosed. Whatever filled the streets of Brineholt had not come out of any vault the Guild owned.

Then he pulled the older records. The very old ones. The ones written in the hand of clerks three centuries dead, back when the Guild had still called itself what it secretly was: a clearinghouse for divine debts. Back when the gods still answered prayers — and charged for it.

He found the Fisher at midnight, by lamplight, in a record book bound in eel-skin with pages soft as cloth.

*Entry 4,411. Petitioner: unnamed fishwife of Brineholt (name withheld per standard rite). Prayer: water, freely given. Response: GRANTED. Terms: none recorded. Payment: NONE.*

Payment: none. Theo read the line eleven times. His lamp guttered. Somewhere upstairs, Ostrell was pacing, unable to sleep in a city that had grown a lake overnight.

No Guild clerk in three hundred years had seen those words. *Payment: none.* It was heresy. It was impossible. It was — Theo's pen hovered over his own working notes and would not move — it was the equivalent of finding *the sea is dry* written in a trusted hand. Because if a prayer could be answered without payment, then the entire architecture of trade collapsed. Every contract, every vault, every careful price the Guild had brokered between mortals and the divine for a thousand years rested on one assumption: that the old powers were creditors, and creditors could be managed.

A god who gave gifts was not a creditor. A god who gave gifts was something you could not buy from, sell to, or bind. Something outside the ledger entirely.

And there was a postscript, in the same ancient hand, cramped and hurried as though the clerk writing it had been afraid:

*Note: petitioner's prayer answered by an agency NOT OF RECORD. The Fisher rises. Recommend the Guild observe and make NO OFFER. What gives freely cannot be bought. What gives freely may also take freely, and will not be bound by terms. May the ledgers stay shut. May we never be needed again.*

*May we never be needed again.* Theo closed the book with hands that were not quite steady.

* * *

Dawn on the second day, and the water had begun to sing.

Not loudly. If you stood on the dry streets beyond the fourth block you heard nothing at all. But inside the flood, in the drowned quiet of Fishgut Lane, the water made a sound like a choir heard through a wall — low, vast, patient, in no language Theo knew and yet somehow familiar, the way a melody is familiar when your mother sang it before you were born. People had begun wading out into it just to listen. An old man stood waist-deep among the mackerel carts with tears running down his face, saying over and over, "I know that song, I know it, my grandmother hummed it, how do I know that song—"

Theo stood at the waterline with his ledger open and wrote nothing, because there was nothing in the Guild's taxonomy for this. Wonder curdled in him slowly, the way milk curdles — imperceptibly, then all at once — into dread.

Because here was what he understood, standing there, that the singing crowds did not: the Guild's law was not greed. It had never been greed. It was a *dam*. Every price, every term, every cruel exchange rate — those were the shape of a cage built around something enormous, so that the enormous thing could only reach the world through one narrow channel at a time, metered and paid. The old clerks hadn't been merchants. They'd been engineers. And the first law — *nothing answers for free* — wasn't a rule of business.

It was a warning label on the cage.

Maren Osta had knocked on the cage door and asked for water, and something had answered *without going through the lock*. Without terms. Without the Guild. Which meant either the cage had a hole in it —

— or the thing inside had learned to open doors.

"Master clerk!" Ostrell came splashing along the Walk, gray-faced, waving a sheet of paper. "The level — it moved. Last night, while everyone slept. It rose two inches — only two inches, only once — and then it held again. But master clerk—" He swallowed. "It rose *toward the Guild house*."

Theo looked across the flooded square, at the backward-running fountain, at the mist climbing from the Fisher's open mouth into a sky gone pale and expectant. Two inches. Measured. Directed. As precise as an entry in a ledger.

Something vast and old had noticed the men who kept the books.

He snapped his ledger case shut. "Ostrell. Send word to the home office tonight. Seal it, sign it, and use the old cipher — the one from the eel-skin records. Tell them the following." He spoke slowly, engraving each word. "*The dam has a crack. The Fisher drinks without paying. And I believe it is not the only one waking.*"

Ostrell stared. "Waking? Sir — waking *what*?"

Theo looked down at the sweet water lapping the cobbles, and beneath the choir-song he thought he heard — faintly, terribly — other voices answering it. From under the hills. From under the sea. From under the world.

"Everything," he said. "Send the letter. And Ostrell — check our own vaults. Check every seal. Because if prayers are being answered for free, then somewhere, something is about to present us with a bill we can't read."

That night, packing his papers for the road he already knew he'd have to take, Theo flipped open his personal ledger to log the day's expenses — and stopped.

There, in his own handwriting, in ink he had no memory of spilling, was a single new entry, dated tomorrow:

*Brineholt account closed. Witness dispatched. — T.A.*

He read it until the candle burned down, and the water sang in the dark, and he did not sleep.
