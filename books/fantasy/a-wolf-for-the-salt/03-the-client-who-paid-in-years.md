## 3. The Client Who Paid in Years

The Guild kept its listening room on the third floor of the Brineholt house, behind a door that had no handle from the outside and no lock from the within. Theo had never understood the architecture of it until he'd learned, in his ninth year of service, that some rooms were meant to be entered only by grief. Grief found handles where there were none. It always had.

He arrived an hour before the client, as was proper. The room was small and round, walled in grey cedar that drank sound the way sand drank rain. A single table of black glass sat at its center, and upon it lay the instrument of the trade: a shallow dish of salt, a candle of tallow rendered from drowned men's funerals — the Guild bought nothing common — and a small brass horn, no longer than a finger, coiled like a sleeping adder. The horn was called a *whisperwell*. Theo had catalogued it forty times in his career and had never once been permitted to test it. Some entries in the ledgers were not for sale but for *use*, and the whisperwell belonged to that rarer class: things the Guild kept the way a surgeon keeps knives.

He set out the contract papers. He checked the salt — coarse, grey, harvested from the flats below Vessany's invisible coast, though he did not know yet that he knew that name. He trimmed the candle. And then, because the hour was early and his hands wanted occupation, he opened the day-ledger to review the file.

**Client:** Maren Ostreyke, widow, netmender, of the Saltrow district.
**Age:** Sixty-one years, by her own count; sixty-eight by her heart's.
**Payment tendered:** Thirty (30) years, drawn against remaining span.
**Service requested:** One (1) utterance. Voice of the dead. Specifics per attached memorandum.

The memorandum was in her own hand, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn through. *I want to hear my son say my name. That is all. I want him to say it once, the way he said it when he came in from the boats.*

Theo read it twice and felt the old professional numbness settle over him, the one he wore like an apron. Thirty years. She would walk out of this room at sixty-one and be ninety-one by supper, if her heart held. Most hearts didn't hold past the first decade of a paid-away life. The body knew what had been taken from it, even when the ledger didn't. He had seen clients leave this house upright and enter their carriages laughing, and then read their death-notices eleven days later, cause listed as *weariness*.

The Guild's rate card called it *temporal extraction, minor arc*. The rate card did not mention that the extracted years went somewhere. Everything the Guild traded went somewhere. That was the first law of impossible commerce: nothing is created, nothing is destroyed, everything is *relocated*.

He closed the ledger and waited.

She came at the stroke of the bell — Maren Ostreyke, small and square as a bollard, with hands knotted from fifty years of mending nets and eyes the pale washed-out blue of a sea worn smooth by weather. She wore her good shawl, the one with the embroidered gulls, and she smelled of tar and soap and, beneath it, faintly, of something floral and funeral — the lilies people put on graves in Saltrow because roses wouldn't take in the salt air.

"You're the clerk," she said. Not a question. "They told me there'd be a clerk."

"I'm the broker," Theo said. "My name is Almeida. Please sit, mistress Ostreyke."

"Ostreyke." She said it the way people say a word they've carried too long, testing whether it still holds weight. She sat. Her hands found each other in her lap and held on. "You have his voice in there?" She looked at the brass coil on the table with naked hunger, and Theo had to look away from it, the way you look away from the sun off water.

"We don't have anything *in* anywhere," he said, giving her the litany every broker gave, the one designed less to inform than to slow the client down, to make the transaction pass through the mind before it passed through the purse. "The whisperwell doesn't store voices. It *borrows* them. Your son is dead — I'm sorry, but it must be said plainly — and the dead do not speak. But the world remembers every voice it has ever heard. The walls remember. The water remembers. The well reaches into that remembering and draws up one utterance. What your son said, once, somewhere, while he lived — the well can find it and give it back. Once. For as long as the candle burns."

"And I get to choose what he says?"

"You've already chosen." Theo turned the memorandum toward her. "You wrote it down. Written requests bind the trade. That's how the Guild keeps itself honest."

She read her own words back and her mouth trembled around them. "He used to shout it from the pier," she said, to Theo or to no one. "Every boat, coming home. He'd see me on the seawall and he'd holler my name across the whole harbor, no shame in him at all, a grown man shouting for his mam like he was six years old. The other fishermen laughed. He never cared." Her hands tightened. "Then the *Kestrel* went down off the Teeth, and the sea kept him, and the sea has never once shouted anything to me except *more*."

Theo had brokered four hundred trades in his career. He knew the shape of this moment the way a locksmith knows the shape of a key. There was a precise point in every consultation where the client stopped being a client and became a cost, and the broker's whole art was to keep the two things separate in his own mind. He reached for the contract.

"Mistress Ostreyke. Before we proceed, the Guild requires me to state the terms aloud, and requires you to repeat them after me. This is not ceremony. The trade will not hold without it."

"Say them, then."

"The Guild will draw thirty years from your remaining span of life. In exchange, the whisperwell will deliver one utterance from the voice of Pieter Ostreyke, your son, deceased eleven years this past Stormmonth. The utterance will be true — spoken by him in life, remembered by the world. It cannot be new words. It cannot be words he never said. Do you understand the difference?"

"He can't tell me anything he didn't already tell me." Her voice was flat. "I know. I'm buying an echo, not a resurrection."

"Yes."

"And thirty years is the price of an echo."

"The price varies with the rarity of the utterance. Common speech is cheap. A name, spoken in love, unrepeatable—" Theo paused. He had done this math a hundred times and never liked saying it aloud. "A name spoken in love is the rarest coin there is, mistress. The Guild prices it accordingly."

She laughed then, one dry note with no humor in it, and it was worse than weeping. "Thirty years to hear my name said kindly. The gods should hear about your rates."

Something moved through the room at that word — *gods* — a pressure change, a stillness in the candle flame, gone before Theo could name it. He told himself it was the draft from under the door. He had been telling himself that for three weeks now, ever since the fish market, ever since three blocks of Brineholt had flooded knee-deep in sweet water that tasted of rivers no map contained. The Guild had sent him to investigate and he had written a report full of careful language — *anomalous hydrology, probable reservoir breach* — and both he and the man who read it had known it was a lie dressed in a clerk's handwriting. The gods were answering prayers again. Everyone felt it. Nobody said it. Saying it would make it a prayer, and who knew what would answer.

"Repeat the terms," Theo said quietly.

Maren Ostreyke straightened her spine the way he imagined she straightened nets, with economy and finality. "Thirty years from my span. One utterance from my Pieter. True words only. Once, for the length of the candle." She looked at him. "That's it? Where do I sign?"

"There is no signature." He turned the black glass table toward her. Its surface had clouded, and in the clouding her reflection was forming — but wrong, older, a woman of ninety standing in a young woman's posture. "You pay by looking. The table shows you the truth of the price. You accept by not looking away."

"That's cruel."

"It's honest. The Guild has very little else."

She looked. She looked for a long time — at the stooped ghost of herself, at the white hair she would have, at the hands like driftwood — and Theo watched her decide, watched it happen behind her face the way weather happens over water. Then she nodded, once, sharp as a cleat biting rope.

"Do it."

* * *

The ritual was simple, which Theo had always found obscene. Great costs should require great labor. Instead it required only order: salt in the dish, poured in a ring; the candle lit at its center; the whisperwell uncoiled and set upright, its flared mouth toward the client, its narrow end toward the candle flame. Then the broker spoke the opening line of the trade, the one every broker in every Guildhouse had spoken since before the Guild had a name:

"*World, you have heard this voice. Give it back.*"

The candle flame leaned. The salt ring darkened, grain by grain, as though each crystal were remembering being part of something larger. And Maren Ostreyke began to age.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. No light, no thunder. She simply *settled*, the way a house settles, the way a ship settles when the cargo shifts. The lines in her face deepened from etching to carving. Her spine curved by degrees. Her hands, already knotted, went to bark. Theo had watched this seven times in his career and it never got easier, and he had learned that it should never get easier, and that the day it got easier was the day to burn his ledger-pen and find honest work.

The candle burned down. A quarter inch. A half.

And then the whisperwell breathed.

It made a sound like the sea deciding to speak — a rush, a hush, a gathering — and out of the little brass mouth came a young man's voice, roughened by wind, bright with the particular joy of a boat coming home:

"—mam! *Mam!* Off the seawall there — MAREN! Ha, look at you, pretending you weren't watching for us—"

Maren Ostreyke made a sound Theo had never heard a client make. Not a sob. Something lower, older, the noise a keel makes when it finally comes off the rocks.

The voice laughed, and the laugh filled the round grey room the way water fills a hull, and then it said, warm and easy and eleven years too late:

"I love you, Mam. Supper on when I'm moored?"

And then the voice said:

"*Tell Theo Almeida the ledger's short one entry.*"

The candle went out.

The room went absolutely silent, in the way that a struck bell is silent in the instant after the tone — a silence full of the memory of sound. Theo stood with his hand still on the cold brass of the whisperwell and felt the words go through him like a fish-gaff. His name. His *name*, in a dead fisherman's mouth, in a dead man's last recorded laughter, pulled up out of the world's remembering by a dying woman's thirty years.

"That's not—" Maren's ancient voice cracked. She was ninety-one now, and crying, and beautiful, in the terrible way that fulfilled things are beautiful. "That's not what I asked for. He said my name. He said it. But what was the rest? Who is—"

She turned and looked at Theo, and her faded eyes went wide, wider than age should allow, because whatever she saw in his face answered her question before he could lie.

"Almeida," she whispered. "That's you. He knows you. How does my dead boy know you?"

"I don't know," Theo said, and it was the truest sentence he had spoken in nine years of service.

Because he didn't. He had never met Pieter Ostreyke. Had never brokered the *Kestrel*'s insurance, had never even walked the Saltrow piers. And yet the world's memory — the same deep water the whisperwell drew from, the same current that ran under every trade the Guild had ever made — had produced his name, unprompted, from a dead man's mouth, tagged to a phrase that made his blood run cold and then colder:

*The ledger's short one entry.*

He thought of the vault audit. Three nights ago. The entry in his own handwriting that he could not remember writing — his own name, **Theo Almeida**, dated *tomorrow*, filed under a classification code he had never seen used: **PENDING WITNESS**.

He thought: *the ledger's short one entry.* Short. As in owed. As in missing. As in a debt.

Maren Ostreyke was still looking at him, and now she was holding her own aged hands out like a woman pleading at a courtroom rail. "What did he mean? Clerk. Broker. Whatever you are. What did my boy mean?"

And Theo, who had answers for everything, who had rates for everything, who had spent nine years believing that the world was a great ledger and every entry balanced — Theo Almeida heard himself say, in a voice gone thin as paper:

"I think, mistress, that your son just paid me a debt I didn't know I owed. And I think I need to go check the books."

He gathered the contract with hands that almost shook, bowed the bow that ended every trade, and left her there in the round grey room with her thirty lost years and her one perfect echo — and as the door with no handle swung shut behind him, he was already running the numbers in his head, and every number came back to the same impossible sum:

A dead man had known his name. The ledger had a date for him. Tomorrow.

And outside the Guildhouse windows, far off across the rooftops of Brineholt, the sea was making a sound he had never heard it make in nine years of living beside it.

It sounded like pages turning.
