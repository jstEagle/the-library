## 10. The Toll-Keeper's Bargain

The road stopped being a road an hour before they reached the bridge.

Theo noticed it first as a sound — or rather the absence of one. For three days since the drowned chapel, since Ossuin's voice had poured through him like cold water through a cracked jar, the Unmaking Road had been loud with its own decay: villages sighing into white silence behind them, dogs barking at nothing because their masters had already been forgotten out of the world. But here the noise thinned, and the ground beneath his boots went smooth and pale as the inside of a shell.

"Knuckle-bone country," Ilsa said quietly.

She had not spoken much since the chapel. Since she had learned what he was. She walked half a pace ahead of him now instead of beside him, and her hand rested on the strap of her satchel — the satchel that held her maps, her life's work, the stitched-together memory of a nation — the way other people rested their hands on knives.

"The bridge," Theo said. "You've seen it?"

"Once. From the far side, when I was mapping the coast." She did not look back at him. "I never crossed it. Cartographers don't pay tolls. We *are* tolls, if we're honest about it. Everything I know was paid for by someone else."

Ahead of them, rising out of the pale plain like something grown rather than built, stood the bridge.

It was made of knuckle-bones.

Not carved to resemble them — they were knuckle-bones, actual bones, each one the size of a man's fist or larger, fitted together without mortar in a lattice so intricate it looked woven. They were yellowed and ivory-smooth, and where the light of the dying afternoon touched them they seemed to glow faintly from within, the way teeth glow in certain dreams. The bridge spanned a gorge that had no bottom that Theo could see; when he leaned toward its edge, the air coming up was warm and smelled faintly of candle smoke and old paper.

And on the near end of the bridge, in a chair made of vertebrae, sat the toll-keeper.

* * *

She was small. That was the first thing. Theo had expected — he didn't know what he'd expected. Something vast, something with too many joints, something like the thing in the fish market that had answered a fishwife's prayer with three flooded blocks of sweet water. Instead there was a woman who might have been sixty or six hundred, wrapped in a shawl of grey wool gone soft with age, her hands folded in her lap like a grandmother waiting for grandchildren who would never come.

Her eyes were the only wrong thing about her. They were the color of the blankness that followed the dissolving villages — not white exactly, but the color of a page before anything is written on it, and when she looked at Theo he felt read.

"Two travelers," she said. Her voice was dry and kind. "One mapmaker. One..." She tilted her head. "One entry."

Beside him, Ilsa went very still.

"You know what he is," she said.

"I know what everything is, dear. That's the toll-keeper's whole trouble." The old woman unfolded her hands. "Everything that comes to this bridge is carrying something it hasn't finished paying for. I only collect what's owed. Sit, sit. There's tea."

There was, impossibly, a second chair, and between the chairs a low table, and on the table a chipped clay teapot with steam curling from its spout. Theo looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at the bridge, at the gorge, at the far shore where the pale plain continued toward the salt flats and, beyond them, the Door.

"We could go around," Theo said.

"No," both women said at once, and the toll-keeper smiled, and it was such a warm, human smile that it frightened him more than the fish market had.

"There is no around," she said. "There hasn't been an around since Vessany was taken off the maps. Every road now runs through what remains. Sit down, ledger-keeper. The tea is real. It's one of the last real things left in this country, and I include myself among the unreal."

They sat. The tea was black and bitter and tasted of iron and, underneath, of salt. Theo drank because his throat was dry and because refusing seemed more dangerous than accepting. Ilsa held her cup without drinking, turning it slowly in her fingers.

"What's the toll?" she asked.

"Memory," the toll-keeper said, simply, the way a ferryman says *fare*. "One memory per traveler, paid at the middle of the bridge. Not told to me — given. There's a difference, and you'll feel it. A story told stays with the teller. A memory given..." She shrugged inside her shawl. "Goes into the bridge. That's what holds it up. Every bone in this lattice is somebody's yesterday. Somebody's first kiss. Somebody's name for their dog. Somebody's mother."

The word *mother* landed in Theo's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. It took longer than it should have.

"Why memories?" Ilsa asked. Her voice was flat, controlled, the cartographer's voice, the one she used for coordinates. "Why not blood? Why not years? The Guild trades in years. I've seen the ledgers."

"Years are cheap." The toll-keeper refilled her own cup. "Years are just time, and time is everywhere, leaking out of everything. Memory is different. Memory is the only currency that makes the payer smaller. And this bridge—" she patted the arm of her vertebrae chair almost fondly— "has to hold up against what's coming across it. Do you know what's chasing you, children? Not hunters. Not yet. *Blankness.* The erasure itself, rolling westward like weather. When it reaches this gorge, something has to be strong enough in this spot to say no. Bones alone won't do it. Bones are nothing. But a bridge built of remembered things — remembered things *refuse*. A first kiss refuses to be unwritten. A mother's face refuses. That's the whole architecture of the place."

She said it cheerfully, the way a mason talks about mortar.

"How many memories does it take?" Theo asked.

"To build? Thousands upon thousands. To cross?" She held up one finger. "One each. But choose carefully. Whatever you give, you will not miss — that's the mercy of it. You'll walk off the far side of this bridge lighter and unbothered, and you'll never know what you lost, because losing requires remembering that you had. It's the gentlest theft in all the worlds, and I've been doing it a long time, and I still think it's crueler than any violent theft there ever was." She sipped her tea. "But no one asked my opinion. Not even me."

* * *

They slept that night at the bridge's foot, because the toll-keeper said crossing at dusk was suicide — the erasure came through the gorge lowest at dusk, she said, pressing its blank face against the bones, looking for weak spots, looking for memories that had gone thin from disuse. "Give it nothing stale," she advised them, banking a small fire of driftwood that burned without smoke. "Give it something you've carried close. Those hold best."

Theo lay on his back on the pale ground and tried to inventory himself.

This was a habit now, since the chapel. Since Ossuin had told him what he was — ink, not flesh; a written thing; a witness commissioned by a god who needed someone to record Vessany's final days so that a witnessed thing could not be wholly erased. Every night since, Theo lay awake cataloguing his own life like entries in a ledger, checking each one for the telltale hollowness of invention. His childhood in Brineholt: real? Probably. The smell of tar on the docks: real. The scar on his left forearm from a gull's beak: real, he could feel it under his sleeve, absurd and specific and therefore true.

His mother.

He turned toward that entry carefully, the way you turn toward a door in a house you've just learned is haunted.

Her name had been Maren. He knew that the way he knew his own — solidly, without effort. She had kept a stall at the Brineholt fish market two rows over from the woman whose prayer had flooded three blocks with sweet water. She had sung while she worked, songs with no words, just humming, low, in a register he had never heard from anyone else. Her hands had been strong and always slightly cold from the catch. She had died when he was nineteen — fever, the wet lung, the usual harbor death — and he had paid for her burial out of his first quarter's wages from the Guild, and Corvane himself had come to the funeral, which Theo had thought strange at the time and now understood completely, because of course Corvane had come. Corvane had commissioned him. Corvane had attended the burial of a woman who may never have existed outside of ink.

Or had she? That was the question that gnawed. Was Maren Almeida a memory Ossuin had written into him for verisimilitude — a prop grief, a set dressing for a fictional past — or had there been a real woman, some harbor widow whose face a god had borrowed?

He couldn't tell. That was the horror of being written: every part of you felt equally yours.

Across the fire, Ilsa sat up suddenly, as if his thinking had woken her.

"I can hear you inventorying," she said.

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize. Just — ask me what you're circling. You've been circling it since the toll-keeper said *mother*. You flinch like a man touching a bruise he can't find."

He should have lied. He was, by trade and by nature, good at discretion; a ledger-keeper learns early that what is written down can be used against you. But lying to Ilsa Vane felt like falsifying a map, and she would catch the error anyway.

"Do you remember your mother's face?" he asked.

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere out on the plain, a wind moved through grass that wasn't quite there.

"Yes," she said finally. "Completely. She had a burn scar on her jaw, here—" she touched the left side of her own face— "from a kettle, when she was a girl. She laughed through her nose. She hummed when she drew. I could draw her right now, from any angle, at any age, and it would be exact." She looked at him across the flames, and her expression was the one she wore when a coastline refused to resolve. "Can you?"

"Yes," Theo said. "That's the problem. I can draw her perfectly. I can see her perfectly. And I cannot tell whether I'm seeing her or seeing a description of her. Whether I loved her or was *given* loving her. Whether she ever—" His voice caught, and he hated it, hated how human the catch sounded, hated that he couldn't verify the catch either. "Whether she ever actually stood in a room with me at all."

Ilsa came around the fire and sat beside him, not touching, but near enough that he could feel the warmth of her shoulder.

"Then listen to me," she said, "because I'm the cartographer and this is my trade. A map of a place that doesn't exist is still drawn by someone who does. If your mother is invented, then whoever invented her had to invent her *out of something* — out of real harbor women, real cold hands, real humming. Nothing in this country comes from nowhere anymore. Even the lies are made of truth." She paused. "So whichever she is, she's real somewhere. Hold onto that."

"And if the toll takes her?"

"Then you'll keep whatever's true and lose whatever's written." She said it gently, but he heard the edge beneath it, the thing she wasn't saying: *and maybe that's a kindness, Theo. Maybe you'd be better off.*

He didn't sleep after that. He lay watching the bridge against the stars, a black lattice full of small warm lights, thousands of them, each one somebody's yesterday burning quietly in the dark, holding up the way forward.

* * *

They crossed at dawn.

The toll-keeper met them at the first plank — the first bone — and shook their hands like a landlady seeing guests off. Up close, the bridge sang. That was the worst discovery of the morning: the bridge *sang*, a chord too low to properly hear, felt in the sternum and the teeth, and Theo realized the sound was made of all those held memories resonating together, ten thousand yesterdays humming in harmony, and that he had been hearing it all night and calling it silence.

"Rules," the toll-keeper said briskly. "Walk, don't run. Don't look down — not because you'll fall, but because you'll *see*, and seeing what holds you up is more than most people can carry. Pay at the center, at the widest bone. Give freely or don't give at all; a grudging memory makes a brittle strut, and brittle struts crack, and cracked bridges drop travelers into the Underneath, and I hate fishing people out of the Underneath." She squeezed Theo's hand, and her grip was surprisingly strong, and her page-white eyes held his. "Ledger-keeper. A word before you go."

Ilsa walked ahead. The toll-keeper drew Theo close, and her voice dropped to something private.

"You're going to want to give me something clever," she said. "Something you've decided doesn't matter. Some bad morning, some embarrassment, some debt you're ashamed of. Everyone tries it. The written ones try hardest, because you've spent your whole existence managing what's recorded about you." Her thumb pressed hard against his wrist, over the pulse that maybe wasn't a pulse. "Don't. The bridge knows the difference between a sacrifice and a disposal. Give it something you love, or turn around now, because the erasure is four days behind you and gaining, and a false payment will bring this whole span down with you on it."

"I don't know what's real in me," Theo whispered. "How can I pay with something if I don't know if it's mine?"

The old woman's terrible blank eyes softened.

"Oh, child. No one knows. That's what a memory *is*. You don't own your past — you're owned by it. Now go. Walk to the middle. And when you get there, don't think. Thinking is how the clever ones cheat themselves into ruin."

* * *

The crossing took the length of a slow hymn.

The bones were warm underfoot. With every step, the lights in the lattice around him brightened briefly, as if the bridge were greeting its material — *yes, yes, one of us, one of us* — and Theo understood with a lurch that these were not metaphors. The bridge recognized memory the way a pack recognizes its own scent. Halfway across, he passed a cluster of bones glowing gold and rose-gold, and without wanting to he felt them: a wedding on a cliff, rain and laughter, someone's uncle playing a fiddle badly. The bridge shared. That was its other cruelty. You walked through everyone else's kept things on the way to surrendering your own.

At the center, the span widened into a platform of broad flat bones, and there the toll-keeper was somehow already waiting, though she had not moved from the shore. Perhaps the bridge simply delivered her where she was needed. Perhaps she had always been in the middle. Perhaps the middle was her.

"One each," she said. "Whenever you're ready. There's no rush. There's never any rush. Rush is for people with less time than they think, and these days that's nearly everyone."

Ilsa went first.

She stood very straight, the way she stood over her drafting table, and closed her eyes, and Theo watched her lips move once — a single word, maybe a name, maybe *forgive me* — and then she exhaled, long and slow, and something left her. He couldn't see it leave. But he saw the effect: a bone at the platform's edge, dull until that moment, kindled like a lamp, and Ilsa swayed, and put a hand to her mouth, and stared at the horizon with the expression of a woman trying to recall a dream from childhood.

"What did you give?" Theo asked softly.

"I don't know." Her voice shook. "That's the whole point, isn't it. I don't know. There's a hole in me shaped like something, and I can't even tell you the shape." She laughed, a small broken sound. "Gods. It's gone and I'm grieving it anyway. Grief without an object. Is that what the rest of your life feels like?"

"Yes," said the toll-keeper, and Theo said nothing at all.

Then it was his turn.

He stepped to the wide central bone. Below him — he didn't look, he *didn't look*, but the warmth of the gorge breathed up anyway — below him was the Underneath, and the singing, and the dark. Behind him, four days east, the blankness rolled. Ahead, the Door. He thought of Ossuin saying *a thing witnessed cannot be wholly erased*, and thought: then let me witness this. Let me watch myself pay. Let this at least be entered.

He opened the ledger of himself.

Maren. The stall two rows over. The humming with no words. Cold hands. Strong hands. The burn scar — no, that was Ilsa's mother, careful, careful — Maren's hands, Maren's voice, Maren's face, the face he could draw from any angle at any age, the face that might have been real or might have been description, the face that was the oldest entry in him, older than the Guild, older than Corvane, older perhaps than Ossuin's pen—

*Don't think,* the toll-keeper had said.

He stopped thinking.

He gave.

There was no light, no sound, no dramatic tearing. One moment the entry was in him; the next moment it was in the bridge. He felt the transfer as a kind of settling, like a book sliding home onto a shelf, like a debt clearing. The great bone beneath his feet warmed and brightened, and somewhere in the lattice a new note joined the chord, low and steady, a humming with no words, and Theo stood on it, and it held his weight, and it would hold his weight forever, because it was hers now, and hers refused.

"That's paid," the toll-keeper said gently. "Both of you. Go on, then. The far side. Quickly now — dusk comes early in the gorge."

They walked. The remaining span passed in a blur. Ilsa watched him sidelong the whole way, and he knew what she was doing: reading him the way she read coastlines, looking for what had changed, for the hole in him shaped like something.

On the far shore, in the ordinary grey light of the ordinary pale plain, with the salt flats visible as a white shimmer on the horizon, Theo stopped, and Ilsa stopped with him, and she said, carefully:

"Theo. Your face."

"What about it?"

"You're crying."

He raised a hand to his cheek. It came away wet. He was crying — heavily, silently, tears running freely down his face and dripping off his jaw, and he had no idea when it had started, and no idea why.

He searched himself for the reason the way he'd search a ledger for a missing figure, running his finger down column after column: mother, mother, Maren, the stall, the humming — and found the columns intact and the figure gone, a gap in the sum, a blank where an entry should be, and no memory of there ever having been an entry at all.

"I don't know," he said. The words came out thick. "Ilsa, I don't know why I'm crying. There's nothing — I'm fine. I don't understand why I can't stop."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something she hadn't done since the chapel: she took his hand. Her grip was strong and slightly cold, like hands that had been cold once, like hands he had known.

"Come on," she said, and her voice was rough. "The flats are a day's walk. We'll get you to the Door. And Theo — whatever it was you paid —"

She stopped. Shook her head.

"It's holding up the bridge beautifully," she said. "I can hear it singing."

Behind them, faint on the wind, the chord of the knuckle-bone bridge shifted, and a new note wove itself in among the thousands — low, and steady, and wordless, and unmistakably a woman humming over her work — and Theo wept harder without knowing why, and turned his back on it, and walked toward the salt.
