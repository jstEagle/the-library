## 13. Ilsa's Map of Everything Lost

They sheltered that night in the bell tower of a church that no longer had a name, only the habit of one. The villagers below called it *the old place*, and the word *old* was doing the work of a saint's name, a founder's name, a consecration — all the names the erasure had eaten. Ilsa had slept in a hundred such places since her exile. She knew the architecture of forgetting the way other cartographers knew the architecture of coastlines: where the blankness pooled, where it thinned, where a whole street could be missing and the people who walked it would simply step over the gap in their lives without stumbling.

Tonight the tower was theirs because the priest had died in the spring and no one could recall who was meant to replace him, and so the bell hung silent, and the tower room at its top stood empty, and the door stood open to anyone desperate enough to climb.

Ilsa was desperate enough. She had been desperate for eleven years.

She waited until Theo slept — truly slept, his breath gone long and even, the ledger locked under his arm like a child's doll — before she unrolled her life's work across the bell tower's floor.

She did it in the dark. That was the point of it. The map could not be seen all at once, not by lamplight, not by any honest light. It had been made in fragments, in cellars and attics and the backs of wagons, by the light of candles that were themselves half-forgotten — tallow from sheep no one could name, wicks twisted from flax grown in fields that had slipped loose from memory. If you lit a lamp over the whole of it, the light would find only cloth: a bolt of gray linen, unremarkable, the kind sold in every market in every country that still had a name.

But in the dark, the map remembered.

It began to glow — not the way embers glow, not with heat or color, but the way a word glows when you have been trying all day to recall it and it comes to you at last in the middle of some unrelated task. The lines surfaced. Coastlines first, because coastlines were what she knew best: the whole western shore of Vessany, the salt marshes, the chalk cliffs at Verlow Point, the harbor at Brineholt rendered so faithfully that Ilsa could count, in the faint silver of the ink, the very bollards where the fishwives mended their nets. Then the rivers, the roads, the ranges of the eastern hills. Then the towns — every town, every village, every crossroads chapel and ferry landing and shepherd's bothy, each one labeled in a hand that had changed over eleven years, growing finer, growing wearier, growing at the end almost too fine to read.

Every name on the map was a name someone had given her. That was the whole of the craft, and the whole of the cost.

She had not drawn Vessany. She had *asked* Vessany to remember itself, one survivor at a time, and stitched what they gave her into cloth.

* * *

The first memory she had ever collected belonged to a woman named Dagny Holt, who kept the lighthouse at Verlow Point and who had begun, in the winter Ilsa fled there, to forget her own son.

Not the way grief forgets — not the softening, the blurring, the mercy of time. Dagny had woken one morning and looked at the boy's empty bed and felt the shape of a love with nothing inside it, like a glove after the hand is withdrawn. She came to Ilsa because Ilsa was the only person in the village who still wrote things down, and Dagny had heard — the way the erased hear things, through walls, through rumor, through the strange telegraph of the forgotten — that the cartographer Vane could *keep* things.

"Tell me about him," Ilsa had said, and spread a scrap of linen on the lighthouse table, and dipped her pen in the ink she'd begun making from salt and her own blood, because ordinary ink would not hold what ordinary maps could not.

And Dagny had talked until dawn. The boy's name — *Alden* — and the way he said it himself, with the *l* softened almost to a *w*. The scar on his chin from falling off the sea wall at seven. How he whistled through his teeth when he was concentrating, how he was afraid of gulls and ashamed of it, how he had wanted to be a ship's carpenter and had already, at eleven, built a model of the lighthouse itself, imperfect, loving, its little lamp a bead of amber glass. How he had gone out in the storm. How he had not come back. How she could not, now, remember his face except when she was dreaming, and the dreams were getting thinner, and she was afraid that soon she would dream of the lighthouse and there would be no boy in it at all, and she would not even know to grieve.

Ilsa had written it all. And when she finished, Dagny had leaned over the linen and wept — because there, in the cartographer's hand, in silver-gray ink that seemed to give off a faint warmth, was the boy's name, and the scar, and the amber bead of the lamp, and the whistling through the teeth, and it was *fixed*, it was *held*, the erasure could not take it, not from the cloth.

The map remembered Alden Holt. And because the map remembered him, Dagny did too. That was the discovery that had remade her exile into a purpose: memory, written in salt and blood on forgotten cloth, was *sticky*. It caught. It held. The erasure slid off it like rain off oiled wool.

She had spent eleven years gathering. Eleven years of knocking on doors in villages that were half-gone, of sitting with the old and the grieving and the half-erased, of saying *tell me what you are afraid to forget* and writing down everything. A fisherman's knowledge of a reef that no chart admitted existed. A baker's recipe for bread that her customers loved and could no longer name the taste of. The whole of the Brineholt fish market on a market day — the cries, the prices, the smell, the particular argument between two sisters over a stall — given to her by an old woman who had sold herring there for sixty years and could feel the market slipping out of her like water through a net. The name of the country itself, which she had gotten from a blind man of ninety who had been born in the capital and who held the word the way a drowning man holds a spar: *Vessany*. He had said it to her four times, slowly, so she would not mishear, and then he had said, "Write it large, girl. Write it large enough to see from the sea."

She had written it large. It ran across the whole northern quarter of the map in letters a hand-span tall, and in the dark they glowed like a constellation.

* * *

The problem — the problem she had carried for eleven years like a stone in her chest — was that the map could only remember. It could not *insist*.

She had learned this the hard way, in the third year, when she had tried. She had taken the map — then only a quarter finished, the western shore and the coast road — to a village that was in the last stages of going, its streets already white at the edges, its people moving through their days with the placid blankness of sleepwalkers. She had stood in the square and unrolled what she had and read the names aloud, every name, all of them, shouting them into the white silence.

And for a moment — a moment she still dreamed about — it had worked. The white had flinched. A woman in the square had looked up with sudden terror and joy and said her dead husband's name as if she had just remembered it, and a boy had begun to cry for a dog, and for one breath the village had been *there*, all of it, dense and particular and real.

Then the white had closed over it like water, and the woman's face had gone smooth again, and the boy had stopped crying mid-sob, and Ilsa had understood the arithmetic of her defeat: a map can hold a memory, but it cannot *return* one. What is written on the cloth stays on the cloth. To give it back — to make the remembering stick in the world rather than in the linen — the map would have to be *spoken* somewhere the erasure had no reach. Somewhere outside. Somewhere the words would land on ground that was not already drowning.

And there was only one outside. There was only one way out of a country that was not on any map.

The Door of Salt.

She had known it for eight years, and for eight years the knowledge had been useless, because the Door was three hundred leagues east through country that was dissolving faster every season, and because no one could carry the map through — she had tested this, oh, she had tested it — because the map was *of* Vessany, and Vessany could not cross its own border. Three times she had reached the Door herself, alone, with the unfinished cloth rolled in oilcloth against her back. Three times the white seam of light had opened for her and she had stepped toward it and the map had simply — ceased. Not burned, not torn. *Ceased*, between one step and the next, and she had stood on the far side holding an armful of blank gray linen, and the Door had closed, and she had walked back east through the unmaking country with her arms empty and her purpose intact and her hope, each time, a little thinner.

A map of Vessany could not leave Vessany. Unless —

Unless it was carried by something that was not, strictly, of Vessany. Something written from outside. Something with a ledger for a spine and a god for an author.

She had not known, when she had first seen Theo Almeida's ledger in the market at Corren's Ford, what he was. She had known only that the book made her teeth ache and the salt in her pack hum, and that a man who kept accounts of impossible things was either the Guild's hound or the Guild's ghost, and both were dangerous. She had followed him anyway. She had tested him with salt and old rites and found that he did not bleed like men do, and she had grieved — quietly, in the dark of a waystation, where he could not see — because she had been beginning to trust him, and it was easier to use a thing than a friend.

But grief, she had learned, is a luxury you schedule for later. She had looked at the man who was ink, and she had seen what he was *for*.

A ledger is an index. A ledger crosses borders, because a ledger belongs to no country — it belongs to the Guild, and the Guild belongs to trade, and trade is the one god that every nation, even an erased one, must admit at its gates. And a ledger that has already held one impossible entry — a man written into being, a name dated tomorrow — has room, Ilsa thought, has *appetite*, for another.

The map could not cross. But the map could be *entered*. Stitched, line by line, name by name, into the pages of the Guild's own book — into the one instrument in all of Vessany that was built to carry impossible things past the eyes of the world.

* * *

Theo woke an hour before dawn, the way he always did, with the sudden complete alertness of a man whose sleep has never quite belonged to him. He sat up. He looked at the map.

Ilsa watched him look. She had imagined this moment for days — since the salt flats were only a rumor, since the Door was only a direction — and she had imagined him recoiling, or going pale, or doing whatever a written thing does when it is shown its possible salvation. Instead he did what he always did. He knelt. He put his fingers an inch above the glowing lines, the way he touched ledger entries he did not trust, and he read.

"You collected all of this," he said. It was not a question, but it was not quite a statement either. It was the voice he used for entries that did not balance.

"Eleven years. Four hundred and six people gave me their memories. Some gave everything they had left. There's a woman in Sennet who gave me her whole wedding day and can no longer remember whether she was happy. I wrote down that she was. I hope I was right."

Theo's hand hovered over the northern quarter, over the great letters. "Vessany," he said, and the word seemed to weigh more in his mouth than it should have, the way a coin weighs more when you know its history.

"The blind man's spelling. He made me repeat it back to him until I had it exactly." She sat back on her heels. The map's glow lit her face from below, and she knew what it must have done to her eyes, and she did not care. "Here is what you need to understand, Theo, and then you can decide whether to hate me. This map is not a record. A record can be lost. This is an *anchor*. Every name on it is a name that someone loved enough to hand to a stranger. If it is carried through the Door and spoken aloud on the far side — every name, in order, beginning with the country and ending with Alden Holt's scar — then Vessany is no longer a thing Vessany remembers. It becomes a thing the *world* remembers. The erasure works because the forgetting is local. It lives inside the border like water in a lock. Speak the names outside, and you open the lock."

"And the map can't cross. You've tried."

"Three times. It dies at the seam. Comes through as blank cloth." She let that sit. Then, because there was no gentle way, she said the rest. "But you crossed. You crossed carrying a ledger full of Vessany's debts, and the ledger crossed *whole*. Because it's a Guild instrument. Because it was written to hold impossible things and move them where they're needed." She met his salt-pale eyes. "I want to put the map inside your book."

The silence in the bell tower was very long. Below them the nameless church settled on its stones. Somewhere a rooster that belonged to no one crowed at nothing.

"You're asking me to carry my own country's death warrant," Theo said at last, "in the same binding that holds my own death warrant."

"I'm asking you to carry the only thing in the world that can save it. And I know what it will cost to enter it — I've done the stitching, Theo, I know how the ink takes. Every name that goes into your ledger comes *out* of the cloth. The map will empty. By the time we reach the Door it will be blank linen, and everything I am will be in your book, in your keeping, in the hands of a man who is himself a debt." Her voice did not shake, though she gave it permission to. "I have spent eleven years making myself the person who could build this. I am asking you to be the person who can carry it. I don't have anyone else. There is no one else."

Theo looked down at the map — at the coastlines, the towns, the great glowing name — and Ilsa saw him do the thing that had first made her trust him, back at Corren's Ford, before she knew what he was: he *audited*. She could almost see the columns forming behind his eyes. The cost. The risk. The entry and the counter-entry. A man made of bookkeeping, weighing whether the books could bear the weight of a nation.

"The ledgers come due," he said quietly. "Everything the Guild ever sold is reverting. If I stitch Vessany into the ledger — into the *index* of the catastrophe — what happens when the gods come to collect? What if I'm carrying your country straight into their hands?"

"Then we'll have traded one erasure for another," Ilsa said. "But Theo — listen to me. The gods are reclaiming what was *sold*. Nothing on this map was ever sold. It was *given*. Freely, by people who had almost nothing left to give. There is no debt on any page of it. It is the one thing in your book that is clean." She reached out and touched the great letters, and the glow warmed under her fingers like a living thing. "Four hundred and six people gave me everything so that this could cross the water. The blind man made me promise to write it large enough to see from the sea. I intend to keep that promise. I intend for every ship in every harbor of the world to wake one morning and find Vessany on their charts, in letters a hand-span tall, and not know how it got there, and not *care*, because a country with its name back does not need to explain itself."

Theo was quiet for a long time. When he spoke again, his hand had gone to the ledger at his side, and his thumb was moving along its edge the way a man's thumb moves along a knife he is deciding to draw.

"There's a page," he said. "Near the back. I've never been able to read it. It's in my handwriting and it isn't. I think — Ilsa, I think it's where I was written. And I think if your map is going into this book, it goes in *there*. Where the author's ink is. Because that's the only ink in Vessany strong enough to hold four hundred and six souls."

"Then we open it," Ilsa said.

"Not here." He was already standing, already moving to the tower's east window, where the first gray light was finding the hills — and Ilsa saw him go still, and saw the color leave what color he had.

She followed his gaze. The eastern road, the road they had come by, the road that yesterday had held three villages and a market town and a mile-stone with the capital's distance carved on it —

The road was white. Not snowed-under, not misted. *White*. Blank as the back of a page, running to the horizon, and where the market town had been there was nothing at all — not ruins, not absence, but the specific, terrible nothing of a place that had never been.

Behind them, she understood, the unmaking had reached the church. Below, faint through the floor, she heard the villagers stirring, and heard — she pressed her hand to her mouth — heard one of them say to another, in a puzzled, pleasant voice, *what a fine old tower, I wonder who built it*, and heard the other answer, *no one, I expect. It's always been here. It's always been empty.*

"They're following us," Theo said. His voice was very steady, which was how she knew he was afraid. "Or the country is simply running out. Either way, we're out of time. We do it on the road. We do it walking."

Ilsa began, with hands that were not quite steady, to roll up her life's work — the map, the map of everything lost, four hundred and six gifts, one blind man's promise — and fold it into the oilcloth, and sling it against her back, and take up her staff.

"East, then," she said. "Toward the Door. And Theo — when we stop, and I start cutting the stitches, and the map starts coming apart in my hands —" She paused at the tower door, with the white horizon behind her and the gray dawn before her. "Don't let me stop. Whatever I say. Whatever I beg. Don't let me stop."

Below them, the bell that had no name hung silent in its tower, waiting, like everything else in Vessany, to find out whether it would ever be rung again.

They went down the stairs together, into the last of the dark.
