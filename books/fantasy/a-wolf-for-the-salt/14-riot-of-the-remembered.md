## 14. Riot of the Remembered

The news reached the capital before we did, carried the way bad news always travels in Vessany — by mouth, from neighbor to neighbor, each retelling adding weight until the word *erased* fell on the city like a dropped anvil.

We came down off the Unmaking Road at dusk, Ilsa limping beside me with her map-case strapped tight across her back, and the first thing I saw was smoke. Not cooking smoke, not chimney smoke — the greasy black of burning paper and varnish, rising in three columns from the direction of the Guild house.

"They found out," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question. Word of what we'd learned at the drowned chapel — that Vessany had been cut from every map, every memory beyond its borders, that its people lived inside a wound no one outside could even see — had moved faster than we had. Someone had talked. A priest, a toll-keeper, a child who overheard too much. In a country held together purely by remembering itself, the truth was a lit match thrown into a dry barn.

"They found out," I agreed, "and they're blaming the Guild."

"Shouldn't they?"

I didn't answer. The Salt Guild had kept Vessany's ledgers for two hundred years. If anyone should have known the country was unwritten, it was us. And if Corvane knew — and Corvane knew everything — then he had known and said nothing, and there were perhaps ten thousand people in this city tonight deciding what to do about that.

The streets near the harbor were already full.

* * *

I have seen riots before. Brineholt had them every few years when the fish runs failed or the Guild raised its tithes. They had a shape: anger first, then noise, then broken glass, then the watch, then silence. This was not that shape. This was something older and stranger, and it frightened me more than anything on the Unmaking Road had.

The people of the capital were not smashing windows. They were standing in the squares and calling out their own names.

An old man stood on the fountain rim in Tallow Square, gripping his hat to his chest, shouting "Berendt Osk! Berendt Osk, of the Tallow Street chandlers, son of Marta Osk!" over and over into the dark, as though the name might fly out of him and land somewhere safe. Beside him a woman held her daughter's face in both hands and made the girl repeat her full name, her street, her grandmother's trade, faster and faster, like a prayer against forgetting. Down the whole length of the square, families had formed circles and were reciting their lineages aloud — names nested inside names going back six generations, seven, ten — because someone had told them, rightly, that a thing remembered cannot be wholly erased, and they intended to remember themselves so hard that erasure would break its teeth.

"They're fortifying," Ilsa said quietly. "Against the blankness."

"It won't work."

"No. But look at them do it anyway." Her voice caught on something. She turned away and pretended to study the smoke.

A stone came through a window above a moneylender's shop. Then another. Then the crowd found its second shape — the shape I recognized — and the night tipped over into fire.

* * *

The Guild house in the capital was called the Counting Room, a granite pile three stories tall on Ledger Street, and by the time we fought our way to it the ground floor was fully alight. I understood immediately how it had happened. The rioters hadn't come for coin; the Guild kept little of value in coin. They'd come for the records — the deeds, the contracts, the entries of borrowed years and bottled storms — and when they couldn't get the vaults open they had done the next best thing. They had burned everything that proved the trades had ever happened. If the ledgers burned, maybe the debts burned. Maybe the years came back. Maybe the storms went quiet in their bottles again.

Maybe. But I had seen what happened when impossible things reverted without order. I had watched a buyer age thirty years in a market square while the Guild's clerks stood helpless around him. The ledgers weren't the disease. They were the only map of the disease anyone had.

"Theo." Ilsa grabbed my sleeve. "Tell me you're not thinking what your face says you're thinking."

"I'm going in."

"The building is on fire, Theo."

"I know exactly what it is. I've audited it." I pulled my coat closed and looked at her. "Everything in those vaults is indexed in my head and half-indexed in my handwriting. If the ledgers burn, every debt in Vessany comes due at once with no one holding the list. You've seen the villages on the road. Now imagine all of it, everywhere, tonight, unmanaged."

She stared at me, and I watched her weigh it — the cartographer in her hating risk, the exile in her understanding exactly what it meant to lose the only record of a place. "The side door," she said finally. "Off the coal yard. Every Guild house has one; the clerks sneak out through it to drink."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I mapped this city before they exiled me for it. Coal yard, east side. Go."

* * *

The coal-yard door was unlocked, which told me the clerks had already fled, and hot, which told me how far the fire had come. I went through anyway, into a corridor full of rolling gray smoke, and the heat hit me like a hand pressed flat against my chest.

Here is what I will say about that walk, and I have had time to think about it since: I did not feel afraid in the way a man should feel afraid. The smoke stung my eyes and the floor scorched my boots and somewhere above me a beam groaned like a living thing in pain, and underneath all of it there was a small, cold voice saying *you are ink, Almeida, ink does not burn the way flesh burns.* I hated that voice. I have never been sure whether it was Ossuin's or my own.

The counting hall was a cathedral of shelves, and it was dying beautifully. Flames ran up the shelving in bright sheets, and the ledgers — two hundred years of them, calf-bound and iron-clasped — were catching one by one, pages curling into black lace, entries of bottled storms and borrowed decades lifting off as ash and drifting up toward the broken skylight like the souls of the traded things themselves. I grabbed the nearest intact case off the shelf and felt its weight, felt the particular dense gravity of a ledger that has recorded many impossible things, and stuffed it under my arm.

Then the ceiling came down across the main aisle, and the way I'd come in stopped existing.

I stood in a burning library with one book under my arm and forty more within reach, and I did the arithmetic every auditor learns first: you cannot save everything, so decide now what matters most. The old ledgers — the historical ones, the ones recording trades already settled — were ash either way, and their loss hurt but would not kill. What mattered was the current register. The open debts. The index of everything still owed and still owing, the single volume that said *this storm lives in this bottle, this woman's thirty years are held in trust, this name belongs to this man.*

The current register lived in the vault. Of course it did. Corvane had built the vaults to survive anything short of divine attention, and divine attention was currently busy elsewhere.

I crossed the hall with my coat over my head and my lungs full of knives. The vault door was iron, round as a millwheel, and it was open — flung wide, actually, which stopped me cold, because the vault was never left open. Inside, the racks of preserved hours sat untouched in their glass cases, glowing faintly amber, each sealed hour a little jar of somebody's afternoon. And on the central table, squared neatly as if waiting for collection, lay the current register.

Beside it lay a note in a hand I knew better than my own.

*Almeida. You'll come for this. Take it. Take the hours too if you can carry them. Burn nothing — you'll understand why soon. I am sorry about the fire; I did not light it, but I did not stop it either, and that is the same crime in a smaller font. When you are ready to hear the rest, follow the salt. — C.*

I read it twice while the room roared around me. Corvane had known I'd come. Had perhaps arranged for me to come. Had left the most dangerous book in Vessany sitting out like bread on a windowsill, and had signed his apology in a joke only the two of us would understand.

I took the register. I took four cases of preserved hours, hooked onto a carrying frame meant for exactly this purpose, and I did not let myself think about how heavy they were or how the heat behind me had begun to sound like the sea.

* * *

Getting out was worse than getting in. The main aisle was a river of flame now, and I went along the east wall where the shelving had collapsed outward, climbing over fallen ledgers whose pages burst into sparks under my hands — I remember that specifically, the way the entries died under my palms, *one storm, sold to Harrow of the Fen Coast, terms: until recalled,* flaring white and gone. Each one was a promise ending without being kept. Each one was a debt with no record left to say it existed.

I came out through the loading bay into the coal yard with my eyebrows singed and my arms shaking, and Ilsa caught me before I fell, and together we dragged the frame into the alley behind the tanneries where the smoke wouldn't show.

Behind us, the Counting Room burned like a lantern with the country inside it. People had gathered at the mouth of Ledger Street to watch, and they were cheering. That was the part I couldn't look away from. They were cheering the fire — men and women who had sold years to the Guild, bought luck, pawned memories, traded names — celebrating the destruction of the only proof of what they'd paid. A woman near the front was dancing, actually dancing, holding her hands up to the sparks, and I heard her shout to her neighbor: "It's gone! The book is gone! Nobody owns my years now!"

And I stood in the alley with that exact book under my arm, and I understood — truly, finally, with the clarity that only arrives in the middle of a disaster — that she was wrong, and that everyone cheering with her was wrong, and that the difference between wrong and dead was going to be whether anyone believed me in time.

Burning the ledgers did not free the debts. I had seen it on the road: the moment an entry lost its binding, the debt didn't vanish, it *reverted* — it went home to the gods who'd lent it out, and the gods were not gentle creditors. Every year the Guild had ever brokered, every storm ever bottled, every name ever held in escrow, was a loan, and the ledgers were not the chain. The ledgers were the *record of the chain*, and a chain you cannot see is still a chain, except now nobody knows where the links are.

They thought they were burning their chains. They were burning the receipt that said the chains could be returned.

"Theo." Ilsa was watching my face. "You've got that look. The one where you're about to say something everyone will hate."

"They have to stop burning them." My voice came out rough with smoke. "Not just here. Everywhere. Any town with a Guild annex, any clerk with a satchel of loose entries — if those burn, the reversion happens blind. No index, no recall order, no mercy schedule. The gods take back everything at once and nobody can even tell them what they're taking."

"So tell them."

"With what authority? I'm a clerk whose office just burned down. Half the city thinks the Guild caused the erasure, and the other half thinks the ledgers caused the plague of answered prayers, and both halves are cheering right now."

"Then don't tell them." Ilsa put her hand flat on the register's cover, on the salt-stained calfskin, and her fingers were steady. "Show them. You keep saying these are just books, Theo, but I've seen your handwriting move. I've seen an entry dated tomorrow. These aren't records of the debt — they're the mechanism. Which means they aren't only a weapon pointed at us." She looked up, and the firelight made her eyes look gilded. "They're a tool. You said it yourself on the road — rewritten, not burned. Well. Here's your rewrite, clerk. Ten thousand people in this city just learned they're written in someone else's book. Show them the book has margins. Show them they can hold the pen."

I looked at the register. Under my hands it had the warm, faintly pulsing weight of a sleeping animal, and I thought of the entry I'd found in my own handwriting, dated tomorrow, and of Ossuin in the drowned chapel saying *a thing witnessed cannot be wholly erased,* and of Corvane's note saying *follow the salt.*

"You want me to teach a rioting city to edit its own existence."

"I want you to teach them to *sign their own names.*" She almost smiled. "Start with the woman who's dancing. She looks like she'd make an excellent first student."

Above the rooftops, the columns of smoke bent eastward on the wind, and beneath the crackle of the burning Guild house I heard — or imagined I heard, though I have never since been able to convince myself it was imagination — a low sound from the direction of the harbor. A long, patient exhalation, like the sea drawing breath to speak.

Something vast had noticed the fire. Something vast was coming to see what had been taken out of it.

"We need to move," I said. "Now. Before whatever's listening finishes listening."

Ilsa shouldered the map-case. I hugged the register to my chest, feeling its heartbeat against mine, and we went down into the alleys, toward the salt.
