## 16. Betrayal at the Salt Flats

Ilsa Vane had drawn the Salt Flats four times in her life, and every time she had lied about them.

Not on purpose — no cartographer alive could render them truly. The flats resisted accuracy the way water resists a closed fist. They were a hundred miles of white crust over a dead sea, cracked into polygons vast as village squares, and the horizon did not curve so much as *dissolve*, so that a traveler walking east might walk forever and arrive where they started, going the other way. On her best map she had shaded them gray and written *here the land forgets to be land*, which was true and useless and all she had.

Now she crossed them on foot with a god's made-man at her side and half of Vessany folded against her heart, and she thought: *if I survive this, I will draw them right. Whatever it costs.*

The map rode in a leather tube strapped across her back. She felt it there constantly now, the way one feels a wound — a warm pressure between her shoulder blades, an awareness of weight that had nothing to do with pounds. Half her life's work. Half of everything. The torn edge had been cauterized by Theo's ink when he fed it fragments of himself, and where it had been mended it glowed faintly at night, like paper remembering fire.

Theo walked ahead, reading the salt.

That was the only way Ilsa could describe what he'd begun doing since the toll-keeper's bridge. He would stop mid-stride, crouch, and press two fingers to the crust, and his eyes — salt-white now, white as the ground beneath them — would go distant. "The ledger runs thin here," he'd said an hour ago. "Whole stretches with no entries. Blank pages." He meant the country itself. Vessany was running out of record, and the unmaking road behind them was proof: villages gone not to ruin but to *nothing*, white silence where memory used to stand.

"Anything?" she called.

"Entries pending," he said, which was becoming his joke and his prayer both. "Salt. More salt. A fault line under the eastern pan—" He stopped.

Ilsa stopped too. Not because he had — because the light changed.

It was late afternoon, the hour when the flats turned from white to rose to a bruised gold, and the shadows of the crack-polys ran long and violet like the seams of a stitched wound. But along the southern horizon something interrupted the dissolve. Something that should not have been there, because nothing stood on the flats; she had never once drawn a feature there, not a cairn, not a marker, because the flats ate features.

Figures. Seven of them. Walking without hurry, in a line as straight as any rule she had ever cut.

"Theo."

"I see them." He rose from the crust. His hand went to the satchel where the ledgers rode — three books now, swollen with rewritten entries, humming faintly like hives. "They're not leaving footprints."

She looked. He was right. Seven figures crossing a surface made entirely of footprint, and behind each of them the salt lay unbroken, smooth as fresh vellum.

"Corvane's?"

"Who else walks like accountants?" Theo's mouth was dry humor but his eyes weren't. "He always said the Guild's last instrument was collection."

*Hunters.* The word arrived in Ilsa's mind with the cold weight of certainty, though no one had said it yet. She had spent eleven years in exile learning to read threat the way she read terrain — the set of shoulders, the economy of pace, the way men moved when they were paid by the mile and not by the hour. These moved like that. And they were not angling to pass them, or to flank them. They were walking directly at the point where Ilsa stood, as if Corvane had given them coordinates. As if, she thought with a lurch, *he has read my maps. He knows exactly where I'll be, because I am a person who goes where she says she will go.*

"We run?" Theo asked.

"Where?" She swept her arm across the horizon. "The flats don't permit running. You can sprint for an hour and be twenty feet from where you started if the salt dislikes your direction." It was one of the flats' truths, the kind she'd had to lie around on paper. "We hold the high ground we have, which is none, and we choose the terms."

"And what terms does white nothing offer?"

"One," she said. "The cracks."

She turned and led them at a hard walk toward the nearest polygon-fault, where the crust had heaved and split and dropped away into shadowed fissures deep enough to lose a horse in. Behind her, distantly, she heard Theo mutter something that might have been arithmetic. He did that now — counted things, tallied things, the ledger leaking out of him through his lips. She had stopped asking what the numbers meant. Mostly she didn't want to know.

The fissure was eight feet across at its lip and went down farther than light bothered to follow. Ilsa peered over the edge. Brine-slick walls, a floor of jumbled plates. Bad ground to fight on and worse ground to fall into, which was precisely its value. She unslung the map tube, checked the straps, checked them again.

"They'll split," Theo said beside her. "Four and three. Two coming down each flank of the fissure, the rest holding the rim." He frowned, pressing fingers to his temple as if pressing fingers to a page. "I don't know how I know that."

"Because you're written from the same ink that wrote their orders, maybe." She hated the bitterness in her voice the moment it left her. Since the chapel, since the drowned god's revelation, she had been careful — so careful — not to let him hear how the knowledge sat in her. That her friend was ink. That his warmth was calligraphy. But fear wore her courtesy thin, and here came seven men across a dead sea to collect her, and the thin places tore first.

Theo didn't flinch. That was somehow worse. He simply nodded, the way he nodded at entries that balanced, and said, "Then we give them the flanks they expect, and you keep the map moving. If I'm ink, Ilsa, let me at least be ink in the right margin."

She wanted to answer that. She wanted to say a dozen things, some apology-shaped, some shaped far more dangerously, and she got as far as opening her mouth—

—when the world went wrong.

There was no thunder. The flats did not permit it; sound died out there the way footprints did. What happened instead was that the seven figures were suddenly not seven figures on a distant horizon but *present*, one of them standing ten feet away on the unbroken salt, close enough that Ilsa could see the Guild's gray badge at his throat and the long needle of a collector's pin in his fist. No footprints. No approach. Between one blink and the next, the flats had simply *filed them forward*, the way Theo filed entries, and Ilsa understood with a horror that tasted of copper that Corvane had taught his hunters to use the blankness itself — to write themselves across it, short distances, the way a clerk moves a mark down a column.

"*Ledger-keeper,*" the near hunter said. His voice was flat as the ground. His eyes found Theo, then slid off him as if he were glare on the horizon, and fixed on her. On the tube on her back. "*And the cartographer. Master Corvane regrets the necessity.*"

"He regrets nothing," Ilsa said. "He foresaw everything. That's different."

Something that was almost amusement crossed the man's face. Then he moved.

The fight, if it could be called that, lasted less time than a held breath. Collectors did not brawl; they administered. The needle flicked toward her throat — toward the artery, she realized later, the way one cancels an account cleanly — and she was already dropping, because eleven years of exile teaches the body lessons the mind never consents to. The needle passed through the space her neck had occupied. Theo shouted something. The fissure yawned at her left shoulder. She rolled, came up with her knife out — a cartographer's knife, honed for cutting vellum, absurd against a collector — and the hunter regarded it with the mild patience of a man being shown an inaccurate drawing.

Then Theo hit him.

Not bravely. Not skillfully. Theo Almeida, who had never in his recorded existence thrown a punch, launched himself like a man falling out of a burning building, all weight and desperation, and bore the collector sideways toward the fissure's lip. They struck the edge together. Crust cracked like dropped porcelain. The hunter twisted — effortlessly, terribly, the way a strong swimmer twists in the grip of a weak one — and the needle reversed its arc, finding not Ilsa anymore but Theo, driving toward the center of his chest, toward whatever a made-man kept where a heart went.

Everything in Ilsa Vane narrowed to a single line.

Later she would try to reconstruct the decision and fail. There was no decision. There was geometry — the angle of the needle, the position of the map tube on her back, the arc of her own body — and geometry was the one language she had spoken fluently since before she could talk. She crossed the distance. She turned so the line of the strike would meet not his chest but hers.

The needle went in below her collarbone, through the strap of the map tube, and out beneath her shoulder blade.

Sound returned to the world all at once, as if the flats had decided to permit it again: Theo screaming her name, the hunter's grunt of surprise, and underneath it a tearing noise like sailcloth giving way in a gale, which was the strap, which was the tube sliding from her back, which was half of everything she had ever loved tumbling end over end across the white salt toward the fissure's edge.

The hunter wrenched the needle free. Pain arrived late, enormous, impersonal, like weather. Ilsa went down to one knee and watched — from very far away, from the cool altitude of shock — the tube strike the crust, burst its cap, and disgorge her life's work into the wind.

Half a map. Stitched from a nation's memories, anchored in Theo's surrendered past, the only object in existence that could carry Vessany's name out through the Door of Salt and speak it back onto the earth. It unrolled across the flats like a living thing, forty feet of sewn vellum and memory-ink, and the wind that the flats permitted for nothing else took its torn edge and *lifted* it.

"No," Ilsa said. It came out very small.

The second hunter reached the map before anyone else. He did not run — collectors never ran — but the blankness carried him, filing him forward in blinks, and his boot came down on the vellum's center with the deliberate pressure of a man stamping a document. Then he gathered the trailing edge in both fists and pulled.

The tear was the loudest thing Ilsa had ever heard, and it made no sound at all.

She saw the map part along a line of weakness — the old seam, the place where her stitching joined the western sheets to the eastern, the join she had reinforced three times and trusted twice. She saw forty years of collected memory become twenty and twenty. She saw the western half, the harbors, the capital, the drowned chapel and the door-road, go taut in the wind and lift free over the hunter's head, rising, rising, white vellum against white sky, and for one heartbeat it hung there whole and beautiful and *theirs*—

—and then the third hunter raised the collector's pin and threw it, and the map was pinned to the salt like a moth to a board, and the wind died, and it was only paper again. Only half of paper. Torn in half, trampled, transfixed, its glowing mend-line guttering like a lamp running dry.

Somewhere above her Theo was fighting. She heard it — the ugly sounds of a scholar brawling for his life, the crack of crust, a hunter's voice saying flatly, "*Master Corvane wants the keeper alive. The woman is optional.*"

Optional. Eleven years of exile, every friendship unwritten before it could be betrayed, every map smuggled out in flour sacks and false bottoms, every night she'd redrawn her homeland from memory alone so that somewhere, somewhere, it would continue to exist in ink — optional.

Ilsa put her hand flat on the salt and pushed herself up.

The wound in her shoulder announced itself, and she declined to listen. Blood ran warm inside her sleeve, and she noted, with the detached precision that fear lent her, that where a drop fell on the white crust it did not soak in; the salt refused it, beaded it, the way the flats refused footprints. Even her blood was being unwritten here. Fine. Let the country refuse her. She had been refusing to be erased for eleven years, and she had learned the trade from masters.

She walked to the pinned map. The second hunter turned, surprised — collectors are surprised by almost nothing, and this cost him a full second — and she was inside his reach before the second ended, her vellum-knife laid open his forearm from wrist to elbow, and the pin came loose in her other hand.

She knelt on the map itself then, kneeling on her own life's work, and drew the pin out of the vellum with a care that made her hands shake. The hole went through the Sea of Glass district. Through the Street of Nine Fountains. Through, she realized, her own childhood house, or where her childhood house had stood before the erasure took even the lot it stood on. Of course, she thought, and almost laughed, and the laugh came out as blood on her lips. Of course Corvane's pin would find her home. He had always known exactly where people lived. It was his gift.

"*Ilsa!*"

Theo, backed against the fissure's lip, blood at his hairline, two hunters closing. The leader, forearm gushing, kneeling on the salt and writing something in his own blood with two steady fingers — and where he wrote, the blankness *answered*, the air thickening, the distance between him and Theo shortening in blinks. Filing himself forward. Preparing to administer.

Ilsa looked down at the torn halves of the map beneath her knees. Twenty and twenty. The western half still glowed faintly along its mended edge — the edge Theo had fed with pieces of his recorded past, the fairs he'd never attended, the mother whose face he'd paid to the toll-keeper, every fragment making him fainter so the country could become more. The eastern half lay dark. Dead vellum. The Door of Salt, if it appeared anywhere, would appear on the western sheet — but a half-map could not anchor a nation. A torn covenant bound no one. She had told Theo as much herself, three nights ago, in a village that no longer exists: *if it tears, it's over. Memory doesn't mend.*

But that was before she had knelt on it. Before she had bled on it — before the flats refused her blood and it ran instead in a thin line along the seam, along the tear, filling the gap the way water finds a crack, and where it touched the mended glow the glow *flared*.

Memory doesn't mend, she had said. But she had spent her whole life proving otherwise — every map an act of mending, every accurate line a stitch in a country's wound. Memory didn't mend itself. It waited for someone willing to pay the thread.

The lead hunter stood. Wiped his bloody fingers on his coat. Looked at her across the white distance with the first expression she had seen on any of their faces, which was interest. "*The map is spoiled, cartographer,*" he said. "*Torn goods bind nothing. Give us the keeper, take your wound home, and live to draw again. Master Corvane is generous with those who stop resisting arithmetic.*"

Ilsa rose. She gathered the western half of the map in her good arm, pressed it against the wound below her collarbone, and felt the vellum drink — felt it *drink*, the memories in its fibers waking at the salt-taste of her blood the way the old gods woke at prayers. The glow spread a hand's width. A foot.

"Torn goods," she agreed, and her voice carried across the flats the way nothing else did, the way a true bearing carries. "You're right. Paper binds nothing."

She looked at Theo. He was watching her with salt-white eyes, and she saw in them the thing she had been refusing to see since the chapel: not a made thing, not ink, not an entry — but a friend, which was worse, and better, and the only currency she had left.

"But I'm not done writing," she said.

And the fissure behind him — the deep dark crack she had chosen for its badness, its wrongness, its refusal to behave like ground — shivered along its whole visible length, top to bottom, rim to root, as if something far below had turned over in its sleep and recognized the taste of blood on salt. As if the dead sea under the dead sea had felt the map drink and remembered that it too had once been entered in a ledger.

The lead hunter took one step backward. In eleven years, Ilsa had never once seen a collector step backward.

"What," he said quietly, "is under us?"

Ilsa smiled, and it hurt, and she meant it more than anything she had ever drawn.

"That," she said, "depends entirely on who remembers asking."
