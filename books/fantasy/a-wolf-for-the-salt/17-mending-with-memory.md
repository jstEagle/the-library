## 17. Mending with Memory

The map lay between them on the floor of the ruined waystation like a wounded thing.

Ilsa had spread it flat, weighted its corners with stones from the broken hearth, and knelt over it in the grey light of a Vessany dawn that came later every day now — as if the country itself were rationing what remained of its mornings. The tear ran diagonally across the whole of it, from the Salt Flats in the south to the hills above Corvane's vault in the north, and where the blade had passed, the map did not simply gape. It *forgot*. The parchment along both edges had gone blank and smooth, the way snow goes smooth after wind, and when Theo touched it his fingers found nothing — not paper, not absence, but a soft refusal, like pressing on the surface of a dream you are trying not to wake from.

"It's dying," Ilsa said. She said it the way she said everything since the ambush: evenly, precisely, with her hands steady and her eyes somewhere else. The wound meant for him had been taken by her shoulder instead, and she had bound it herself without once letting him help, and now she moved her left arm as if it belonged to someone she was politely tolerating. "A map made of memory doesn't bleed. It just stops being remembered. Half of Vessany is already gone from this side." She laid her palm flat against the torn edge. "I can feel it going. Like names slipping out of a wet hand."

Theo sat back against the wall and looked at his own hands.

He knew what he was now. Ossuin had told him in the drowned chapel; Ilsa had confirmed it with salt and old rites; and every hour since, he had felt the truth of it settling into him the way silt settles in still water. He was an entry. A line of ink given breath so that a god could have a witness. And an entry — this was the part that mattered now, the part he had been turning over all night while Ilsa slept badly against her pack — an entry could be *spent*.

"Give me the pen," he said.

Ilsa looked up. "What pen?"

"The one I've been carrying since Brineholt and didn't know I was carrying." He held out his right hand, palm up, and let the thing rise through his skin the way a splinter works toward light: a slender quill, grey-white, its barbs like frost on a window, its point dark with ink that was not ink. It had been inside him all along. Of course it had. You could not be written into existence without carrying the instrument somewhere in your substance. "Ossuin wrote me with this. Which means it writes what I am. Which means—" He stopped, because saying it plainly would make it real, and some cowardly remainder of the man he'd thought he was wanted one more hour of unreality.

Ilsa understood anyway. She always did. Her face went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"No," she said.

"Ilsa—"

"*No.*" She rose, and even hurt she was formidable, a woman built out of borders and defiance. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand there with your little ghost-quill and your martyr's arithmetic and decide that you're the price. That's not how maps work, Theo. That's not how *anything* works."

"How does it work, then?" He kept his voice gentle, which only made her angrier. "Tell me another way to mend it. Tell me where we find more memory in this country — real memory, witnessed memory, the kind that holds. Every village behind us is white silence. Every person we meet is losing their grandmother's face, their own wedding day, the taste of their mother's bread. The gods are drinking it all back. There is exactly one reservoir of recorded life left in Vessany that isn't nailed down, Ilsa, and it's standing in front of you asking for the quill."

"That reservoir is a *person*."

"The reservoir," Theo said quietly, "is a ledger entry. That's rather the point."

* * *

They argued until the light failed, which by then took no time at all.

She argued that they could reach the Door with the map half-mended, that half a country remembered might be enough to anchor the rest, that hope was a currency too and he was spending theirs recklessly. He argued that half a map was a lie with corners, that a nation restored at half-remembering would come back wrong, its rivers running through towns that weren't there, its people waking with gaps in themselves shaped like other people — and hadn't they seen enough of that on the Unmaking Road? Hadn't they walked through the village of Sennet-low, where a woman had stood in her doorway calling a name that no longer attached to anyone, over and over, polite and pleasant and hollow-eyed?

In the end it was not argument that decided it. It was the map itself.

Near midnight, the blank edge crept. Not much — a finger's width, maybe less — but they both saw it happen, the forgetting moving like frost across glass, and Ilsa made a sound low in her throat that Theo had never heard from her before and hoped never to hear again. It was the sound of a cartographer watching geography die.

"One fragment tonight," she said at last, in the voice of a woman negotiating with a flood. "One. And we see what it costs before we speak of any others."

* * *

Choosing the first piece was harder than he had expected.

He had assumed he would give away the dross — the dull years, the unloved hours. But when he closed his hand around the quill and reached inward for the ledger of himself, he found there was no dross. That was the first cruelty of it. Everything recorded in him had been recorded *on purpose*, by a god who needed a witness, and a witness does not keep filler. His childhood in the Guild house at Brineholt, the smell of tallow and brine, old Corvane teaching him to hold a pen so the words would not smudge — all of it load-bearing. All of it him.

"You have to choose something true," Ilsa said softly. She had drawn close without him noticing, kneeling beside him, her good hand hovering over the map's torn edge. "Not something big. Something *true*. Memory-maps mend with truth the way flesh mends with blood."

So Theo chose the fish market.

It was small, as lives go. A morning in Brineholt, years ago, before everything — the gulls screaming over the stalls, the silver slap of herring on split boards, an old fishwife laughing at him because he'd haggled over three coppers and then tipped her five. Salt on the air. Sun on wet stone. The particular bell-note of a harbor city waking up, a sound he had loved without ever thinking to love it, the way you love your own pulse.

He set the quill's point against the torn edge of the map and gave it away.

There was no pain. That was the second cruelty. He had braced for pain, and instead there was only a sensation of *opening*, like a door unlatching in a room he hadn't known was locked, and then the memory went out of him and into the parchment — he watched it go, actually watched it: sunlight blooming across the blank vellum, gulls stitching themselves into the margins, the fishwife's laugh becoming a curl of coastline, the herring-silver becoming a river he had never seen but which the map remembered anyway, because the map knew that all mornings were cousins of each other.

And then it was gone from him.

Not dimmed. Gone. He reached for the fish market and found a smooth place where it had been, the way his tongue reached for a tooth that had been pulled. He knew — because the ledger-knowledge in him told him so, coldly, usefully — that he had once loved a morning in Brineholt. He could not remember loving it. He could not remember the morning.

"Oh," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Oh, Theo."

"It worked," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself, a voice heard through a wall. "Look at it. Look."

The tear had closed for two full spans of the map's length. Where the blankness had been, Vessany stood again in ink and certainty: the hills above Corvane's vault, the road they had walked, the white wound of the Flats. Isla ran her fingers over it and wept without noise, the tears simply falling, as if her body had decided crying was logistics.

"One more," Theo said. "Tonight. While the mend is warm."

"No." But her voice had no conviction in it, because they could both see the map breathing easier, and they could both see him sitting very straight and very pale, like a candle burned down to its collar.

* * *

He gave four fragments before they slept, and woke fainter.

That was the word for it, and he hated how accurate it was. Fainter. In the grey of the next morning Ilsa looked at him twice, and the second look lingered, and he knew why: the light seemed to pass through him a little more readily than it had. His shadow on the waystation floor had thinned at the edges. When he spoke, Ilsa sometimes tilted her head, as though listening for him under the sound of him.

"What did you take?" she demanded, when she saw the new stretch of mended map. "What did you give, I mean. Tell me what you gave."

"A winter," he said. "Our first winter on the road together. The inn at Coldharbour, the night you taught me to read contour lines and I pretended to be slower than I was so the lesson would last."

Ilsa went white. "I remember that night."

"You would. It was yours too. But it isn't mine anymore." He smiled, and found the smile cost something, some small muscular honesty he no longer owned. "Do you see? That's the trade. You keep the memory. I keep the fact of it. You'll carry our winter, and I'll carry the knowledge that I carried it once. It's not nothing. It's almost enough."

"It is not *almost enough*," she said fiercely, and gripped his hand — his hand, which he noticed distantly felt cooler than hers, smoother, more like paper warmed by a lamp. "Listen to me. We do this my way now. One fragment at a time, and I choose them, and we stop the moment I say stop, and if you so much as touch that quill without me watching, I will break your writing hand, ghost or no ghost. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Theo said, and meant it, and loved her a little for the ferocity, and did not tell her that he had already spent the night cataloguing himself, deciding what he could live without, arranging his own dissolution like a man packing a bag for a journey only he would take.

* * *

They traveled by day and mended by dusk, and the country came back around them stitch by stitch.

It was strange to watch. Each fragment he surrendered restored not just the map but, in some small way, the land beneath their feet. After he gave the memory of his mother's kitchen — the copper pots, the smell of cardamom, her humming off-key — a farmhouse appeared in a valley that had been empty an hour before, and smoke stood up straight from its chimney, and a woman leaned out a window beating a rug, solid as bread. After he gave his first sight of the sea, a bay filled itself in to the west, glittering, complete with a lighthouse whose lamp turned and turned.

"You're rebuilding it," Ilsa said, awed and grieving at once. "Out of yourself. You're literally re-membering the country."

"Not me." He shook his head. "The ledger. I'm just the ink it flows through. Ossuin wrote me to witness Vessany's ending — but a record can be read backward, Ilsa. Every entry in me is a proof that this place existed. The map is learning to cite its sources."

On the fifth evening, at the edge of a town called Merrowgate that had returned to existence only that afternoon, he gave away the memory of Ilsa herself — the first meeting, the border at dusk, her torchlight and her suspicion and the exact way she'd said *you're holding a god's ledger, man, do you know what that makes you?*

Afterward he looked at her across the fire and felt the smooth place where that memory had been, and it was the worst one yet, worse than his mother's kitchen, because she was sitting right there, alive and scarred and stubborn, and he could not remember how he had come to trust her. He knew that he trusted her the way he knew the sum of a column he could no longer see. The answer was there. The working was gone.

"Why are you crying?" Ilsa asked sharply. "What did you give? Theo. *What did you give?*"

"I don't know," he said, and it was almost funny, and neither of them laughed. "That's rather the design of it. I can't miss what I can't remember. Except apparently I can."

She crossed to him and took his face in her hands, and studied him like territory, like a coastline changing under an old chart. Whatever she saw frightened her, because her jaw tightened and her eyes went bright and hard.

"How much of you is left?" she whispered.

He considered honestly. He reached inward and audited, the way he had audited vaults and ledgers all his life, and reported the figures without flinching, because someone had to keep the books even now. Especially now.

"Enough," he said. "Enough to walk to the Door. Enough to do what has to be done there. Ilsa — listen. This is the arithmetic and you know it better than anyone: a country of two million souls, weighed against one entry. One line of ink that was only ever written to watch them die. If I spend myself so they don't have to, that's not sacrifice. That's just *bookkeeping*."

"It's not bookkeeping," she said. "Bookkeeping doesn't hold your face like this." But she didn't let go, and outside, in the reborn streets of Merrowgate, people who had not existed that morning were lighting lamps against the early dark, and every lamp was lit with a little of his light, and none of them would ever know it.

* * *

On the seventh night, the last night of mending, the map pulled.

He felt it before he understood it — a tug behind his sternum, insistent, directional, like a hook set gently in him and paid out toward the north. The map, nearly whole now, lay glowing faintly on the ground between them, and at its center, where Ilsa had stitched her life's work together from a thousand borrowed memories, one region still pulsed blank: a shape he recognized with a lurch.

The Door of Salt. The one exit from Vessany. The destination.

"It won't mend there," Ilsa said quietly. She had noticed it too; her finger hovered over the blank seam without touching. "I've tried. Every fragment you've given has gone everywhere except that one place. The Door won't take your memories, Theo. It's not part of Vessany. It's the edge of it. The Door doesn't want to be remembered." She looked up at him, and the firelight moved in her eyes, and beyond her the recovered country lay quiet under the stars, whole and named and breathing, bought with seven nights of his diminishing self. "Which means whatever happens there — whatever Ossuin wants, whatever the Door demands — we arrive with the map finished everywhere except the one place that matters most."

Theo looked down at his hands. They were pale as vellum in the firelight. When he turned them over, he could almost see the grain of the paper in them, the faint ruled lines of a page, and somewhere beneath the skin, the grey gleam of the quill waiting.

"Then we'd better hurry," he said. "I'm spending fast, and I want to be there for the last entry."

Far to the north, past the hills, past the Flats, a seam of white light waited at the edge of the world — and as they broke camp in the dark, Theo felt it pull again, patient and vast, like a ledger opening to a fresh page with his name already fading at the top.
