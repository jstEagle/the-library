## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The temple of Corren Hollow kept its accounts the way it kept its gods: carefully, quietly, and with a great deal of arithmetic.

Sable Villalobos had copied eleven thousand, four hundred and six ledger entries in her nine years as a debt-scribe, and she had developed a theory about all of them. The theory was this: every debt in the valley was small on purpose. A debt was never so large that a debtor might despair of it — despair made people run, and running debtors were bad for the ledgers — nor so small that they might forget it. No, a Corren Hollow debt was calibrated to sit precisely at the weight of a stone in the shoe. Noticeable every single step. Never quite enough to justify taking the shoe off.

She was copying one now. Entry 11,407: *Marta Ellswicke, weaver's widow, owes the Temple of the Quiet Hand three coppers per week for the blessing of her roof-beam, term indefinite.* Marta had been paying that three coppers for nineteen years. The roof had not fallen. Sable suspected it never would have. But the blessing had been *offered*, and in Corren Hollow an offered blessing was a door you could not close without owing rent on the doorway.

Her quill scratched. Outside the scriptorium window, the valley did what the valley always did, which was nothing whatsoever, beautifully.

That was the official word for it, anyway. *Nothing happens in Corren Hollow* was printed on the waybills that travelers carried out over the pass, and the travelers always nodded when they read it, because it was true. No storms worth naming. No feuds worth recording. No magic — none at all, not a spark, not a whisper, not so much as a milkmaid's charm against churned butter. The valley was famous for its peace the way a pond is famous for its stillness, and for roughly the same reason: nothing got in, and nothing much moved.

Sable had spent nine years learning to love that stillness the way you learn to love a room you cannot leave. Which is to say: thoroughly, sincerely, and with occasional moments of wanting to put her fist through the wall.

"Entry 11,407," she said aloud, because the scriptorium was empty except for Brother Osmund, who was deaf in one ear and pretended to be deaf in both when he wanted to avoid conversation. "Marta Ellswicke. Three coppers. Term indefinite."

"In-definite," Osmund agreed comfortably, not looking up from his own page. He was copying out the liturgy of the Quiet Hand for the four hundredth time. His handwriting had gotten so automatic it was practically calligraphy's ghost.

"You know she's paid for that roof eleven times over."

"Mm."

"If we charged by the actual value of the blessing instead of the fear of needing it, the whole ledger would fit on a napkin."

Osmund turned a page with the serenity of a man who had learned, decades ago, that agreeing with Sable Villalobos was more dangerous than disagreeing with her, and considerably less interesting. "The Temple does not charge for blessings," he recited. "The Temple accepts gratitude."

"The Temple accepts gratitude at three coppers a week."

"Sable." He finally looked up, and his eyes were kind in the tired way of old men who have watched clever young women wear grooves into their own patience. "Copy the entry."

She copied the entry.

This was the arrangement, and Sable understood the arrangement down to its bones, because she had helped write it — or rather, her mother had signed it, and Sable had inherited the ink. Her father had borrowed against the Temple's granary during the blight year, a debt of some two hundred crowns, which should have been cleared in five. But debts in Corren Hollow did not clear; they *transferred*, and when her father died the debt had flowed downhill like water finding its level, into the name of his daughter, along with his chair, his ink-stained desk, and his habit of talking back to Brother Osmund.

Nine years served. Six remained. She counted them the way other people counted coins or sins or grey hairs: constantly, privately, and with a small cold satisfaction each time the number dropped. Three thousand one hundred and forty-two days, give or take a festival day here and there, and then the ledger would release her name and she could walk out over the pass and see whatever it was that existed on the other side of *nothing happens in Corren Hollow*.

She did not know what was out there. Nobody in the valley knew, really. Merchants came through twice a season with salt and dyes and stories they told in the tavern for the price of a meal, but their stories had a secondhand smell to them, like coats worn by too many shoulders. Kingdoms, they said. Mountains with snow on them in summer. A place called Vael where the king wore a crown older than the kingdom and nobody thought that was strange.

"A crown," Sable muttered, dipping her quill. "Who's impressed by a hat."

"Entry 11,408," said Osmund, who heard everything he chose to hear.

*Entry 11,408: Jorin Petch, cooper, owes one day's labor for the mending of his cart-axle, term indefinite.* She wrote it out. She drew the little sigil of receipt beside it — a closed hand, the mark of the Quiet Hand, the only symbol permitted in a valley where symbols otherwise did nothing — and she pressed it flat with her blotter, and somewhere in the middle of doing this perfectly ordinary task she felt the feeling she always felt, the one she had long ago decided to stop having.

It arrived the way it always did: not as longing, exactly. Longing was dramatic. This was subtler. It was the sense, while copying someone else's small debt, that her own life was also an entry in a ledger — *Sable Villalobos, scribe, owes six years, term definite* — and that somewhere a hand was holding a quill over her name, patient as winter, waiting to write the next line.

She had gotten very good at folding that feeling up small and putting it in the drawer with her spare nibs. Most days it stayed folded.

Most days.

* * *

The book arrived on a Thursday, in the company of a rain that couldn't decide whether to be serious.

Corren Hollow got perhaps ten days of true weather a year; the rest was a mild gray courtesy, drizzle and mist and the occasional apologetic sunbeam. So when real rain came — fat, committed rain, drumming on the scriptorium roof like a creditor at the door — everyone remarked upon it, and the novices ran about closing shutters, and Brother Osmund said "hm" in a tone suggesting the weather was personally letting him down.

The courier came up from the gatehouse soaked to the collar, carrying a oilcloth bundle under his arm as if it were a cat he didn't trust.

"For cataloguing," he said, setting it on Sable's desk with more relief than care. "Came down with the salt caravan. No manifest. Master Verel says you're to look at it before anyone else touches it."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who reads things," the courier said, with the flat logic of the young, and left before she could point out that reading things was, in fact, her entire job description and therefore not a special honor.

The bundle was tied with cord gone stiff with age. Sable untied it the way she'd been trained to untie everything in the archive: slowly, noting the knots, in case the knots meant something. They didn't. Inside the oilcloth was a book.

It was a large book — folio-sized, bound in leather the color of dried blood, gone soft and scuffed at the corners like a dog's favorite toy. There was no title on the cover. There was no clasp. There was, however, a sigil stamped into the leather near the spine: a closed hand.

Sable frowned at it. The Quiet Hand's mark appeared on temple property throughout the valley, but this was not temple property. Nothing came *into* the valley bearing the mark; the seal kept such things out. That was rather the entire point of the seal, though nobody spoke of the seal directly, the way nobody in a family speaks directly about the uncle nobody mentions.

"Hm," she said, in Osmund's own tone, and opened the book.

The first thing she noticed was that the pages were wrong.

Not damaged-wrong. Not foxed or torn or water-swollen, though the book was plainly old — the leather alone argued a century, maybe more. Wrong in a subtler way. Archive paper had a feel she knew better than her own heartbeat: dry, inert, faintly cool, paper being paper. These pages felt like something else entirely. They felt *expectant*. And when she laid her palm flat against the first leaf —

— warm.

Not sun-warm, not fresh-from-the-press warm. Warm the way a hand is warm. Warm the way the nape of a neck is warm under hair. The page held a temperature that had no business existing in a book that had ridden a week through mountain passes in an oilcloth wrap.

"Osmund," she said.

"Mm."

"Feel this."

"I don't feel things on Thursdays." But curiosity was older than his policy, and he shuffled over, wiped his hands on his robe with archival ceremony, and touched the page. He stood there a moment. His eyebrows performed a slow ascent.

"Well," he said.

"Well," Sable agreed.

"It's warm."

"It's warm."

"That's not possible."

"No," Sable said. "It isn't." She found she was smiling, and made herself stop, because smiling at impossible things was how novices got assigned to the candle-tallow inventory. "Catalogue it anyway. Everything gets catalogued. Especially the impossible things — those are the ones that go missing."

They worked through it together, and it was slow going, because the book resisted being known. The text inside was dense, handwritten in an iron-gall brown that had faded to the color of old tea, and it was in no alphabet either of them recognized — looping, vertical, full of ligatures that knotted letters together like fingers laced in prayer. Marginalia crawled up the edges in a different, smaller hand, annotations upon annotations, generations of readers arguing with the text in ink of varying ages. Some of the marginal notes were in trade-common, the valley tongue, which was itself impossible, because trade-common had been spoken in Corren Hollow for barely eighty years.

And there were drawings. That was the strangest part. Diagrams, mostly — circles within circles, lines radiating from centers, and at the heart of each diagram a sigil that Sable's eye kept snagging on and refusing to leave. She knew sigils. She drew the closed hand forty times a day. Sigils in Corren Hollow were decoration, punctuation, the shape tradition gave to meaning that had drained out of them centuries ago. Everyone knew that. It was the first thing they taught you: *ink is ink, child. It holds words. Nothing else.*

But these sigils seemed to hold something. Looking at them was like looking at a locked door in a house where you'd lived your whole life and never noticed the door before.

"Verel will want this in the restricted case," Osmund said at last, straightening up with a wince. "I'll fetch the chain. Don't — " he pointed a finger at her, " — don't read ahead. You always read ahead."

"I never read ahead."

"You read the funeral registry ahead. You read the tithes ahead. You read *my* correspondence ahead, which is why I now write my correspondence in cipher."

"That was one letter, and it was about me, and I stand by it."

He shuffled off toward the storeroom, muttering, and the rain filled the silence behind him, steady and silver on the roof.

Sable looked at the book.

Here is the thing about restless longing disguised as contentment: it doesn't stay disguised forever. It waits. It sits in the drawer with the spare nibs, folded up small, and it waits for a Thursday in autumn when the rain is serious and the scriptorium is empty and a book lies open on your desk, warm as a living thing, full of writing nobody can read.

Sable told herself she was only checking the binding condition. She told herself she was assessing water damage along the fore-edge, which was a genuine concern given the courier's soaking. She told herself several other professional-sounding things, and then she turned past the diagrams, past the knot-lettered text, into a section where the pages changed character entirely — where the entries became dated, ordered, sequential, a chronicle of some kind, and the dates, converted with a scribe's reflexes into the valley calendar, went back a hundred years and more.

Prophecies, she realized. Or predictions. Or a madman's diary with delusions of structure. Entries written *before* their dates, describing events after them — a coronation, a fire, a flood, a wedding, each entry initialed by a different hand, each one marked afterward with a small notation in the margin: *fulfilled*, or *failed*, or, in one case, *partially fulfilled, see corrections*.

Even prophecy, apparently, required revisions.

She was smiling again. She let herself smile this time, alone, rain on the roof, because it was funny — a hundred years of solemn prediction and somebody still had to write *see corrections* in the margin. Whoever they had been, those annotators, those keepers of this warm impossible book, they had been scribes. Like her. People who sat indoors while weather happened and made records of a world they mostly weren't allowed to touch.

The last dated entry was a century old. After it, the pages continued — blank, blank, blank, dozens of them, waiting — except that they weren't quite blank. As she turned them, Sable began to notice ghost-faint impressions, as if the pages remembered pressure. As if something had been written on them once and erased. Or as if something intended to be written on them and hadn't yet gotten around to it.

The rain thickened. Somewhere below, the bell for evening offices rang, muffled and dutiful. Osmund would be back with the chain any moment. The sensible thing — the Sable-Villalobos-with-nine-years'-seniority thing — was to close the book, log it as *unidentified folio, provenance unknown, contents illegible, storage: restricted*, and go eat supper.

Her hand rested on the next page. It was warm. All the pages were warm, but this one — the first after the hundred-year gap, the first page of the unwritten stretch — this one was warmer than the rest, the way a chair is warm when someone has just left it.

Or the way a chair is warm when someone is just about to arrive.

"One page," she told the empty scriptorium, in the reasonable voice she used on novices and herself. "Condition assessment. Then I catalogue it, chain it, and forget it."

She turned the page.

The ghost-impressions on it were stronger than the others — strong enough that she leaned close, tilting the page toward the lamplight to catch the depressions, the way she'd been taught to recover erased text from palimpsests. Light raked across the surface. Shadows pooled in the old pressure-marks. And letterforms rose out of them, half-formed, suggestions of words, and among the suggestions her eye caught shapes she recognized — trade-common, the valley tongue, emerging like footprints in frost:

*...the scribe comes through the ink...*

Sable went very still.

The lamplight flickered. Under her palm, the page grew warmer — not gradually, not politely, but all at once, fever-hot, and the impressions deepened as she watched, ink surfacing through the paper like bruises rising on skin, letters filling in stroke by stroke, the ghost-text becoming black, becoming certain, becoming *addressed*:

*To the one whose name is already here.*

"What name?" Sable whispered. "Whose name is — "

The ink moved.

Not flowed — *moved*, deliberately, the way a snake moves through grass, coiling up from the depths of the page toward her fingertips where they rested on the margin, and Sable had nine years of training and a healthy respect for the impossible and every instinct a person could pack into one body, and all of it said *let go*.

She did not let go.

Later — much later, in another country, under a hungrier sky — she would try to explain this to someone who loved her, and the closest she ever got was this: *it was the first time anything in my life had reached for me on purpose. Everything else I'd ever touched, I'd touched because I owed it.*

The ink touched her fingertips.

The world turned like a page.

Somewhere behind her, faintly, through the roar of a sound that was not wind and not rain, she heard Brother Osmund come back into the scriptorium with the chain, and say, mildly, to the empty room:

"Hm."
