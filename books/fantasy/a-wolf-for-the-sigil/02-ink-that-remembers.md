## 2. Ink That Remembers

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of Sable's thumb, which meant one of two things: either she was diligent past all reason, or she was hiding from her own bed.

She suspected it was both.

The scriptorium of the Temple of Quiet Accounts smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular sourness of old glue — a smell she had once found oppressive and now found, to her private embarrassment, comforting. Around her, the shelves of the archive rose into shadow like patient mountains, each one holding the valley's memory in careful columns: harvest ledgers, marriage contracts, debt tallies going back four generations. Somewhere in those pages was the record of her own binding, entered in her mother's trembling hand when Sable was nine years old and the winter had eaten everything they owned.

*Three hundred and eleven days,* she thought, dipping her pen. *Give or take.*

That was the arithmetic that lived behind her ribs. Three hundred and eleven days until her debt cleared and she could walk out of Corren Hollow a free woman — free to do what, exactly, she had never quite worked out. The temple trained scribes beautifully and then gave them nowhere to go. But freedom didn't need a destination. Freedom just needed to be *hers*.

"Late again," said Brother Fenwick from the doorway, in the tone of a man who had been saying the same thing for eleven years and would say it for eleven more.

"You're later," Sable said without looking up. "I heard you arguing with the cat on the stairs."

"The cat started it."

"He always does." She finally glanced over. Fenwick was seventy if he was a day, built like a soft-bellied teapot, with ink stains on his fingers so old they'd become part of his skin. He was carrying a crate. That was unusual. New acquisitions came maybe twice a year, and usually they were duplicates — another copy of the Almanac of Tithes, another hymnal nobody sang anymore.

"What is it?"

"Donation," he said, setting the crate down with a grunt. "From the Halloway farmstead. Old Marta Halloway died last month, and her nephew went through the attic and found *this*, and being a sensible man, he brought it here rather than burning it." He lifted out a book wrapped in oilcloth, and even across the room, Sable felt something shift — a faint pressure against her ears, like the moment before a storm breaks.

"That's odd," she said.

"What is?"

"It's warm."

Fenwick frowned and touched the cover himself. "Feels like paper to me. You've been at the candle too long, girl. Your eyes are cooking." He set the book on her desk anyway, because despite forty years of temple discipline, Brother Fenwick had never once done actual work when there was a younger person available to delegate to. "Catalogue it. I'm for bed. Don't stay past the candle, or I'll tell Mother Anselm you've been communing with demons again."

"She only believes that about you."

"And she's right." He shuffled out, still grumbling about the cat.

Sable looked at the book.

It was bound in leather gone dark with age, cracked along the spine, no title on the cover — which was strange in itself, because every book in the archive was titled, stamped, and numbered within an inch of its life. The temple loved order the way other institutions loved gold. A book without a label was a child without a name; it made the archivists itch.

She unwrapped the oilcloth. Underneath, pressed flat between two boards someone had added long ago as reinforcement, the book sat with the heavy stillness of something that had been waiting.

"Warm," she murmured again, and laid her palm flat on the cover.

It wasn't body-warm. It wasn't the warmth of something left near a hearth. It was the warmth of a stone that has sat in sunlight all afternoon — warmth held deep, warmth with *history* to it. And beneath her palm, so faint she might have imagined it, she felt a rhythm. Slow. Patient. Like a pulse that beat once a minute instead of once a second.

"Well," she told the empty scriptorium, because talking to books was a habit she'd stopped apologizing for years ago, "you're either very interesting or I've finally lost my mind. Fenwick will be pleased either way. He's been saying it's coming since I was fifteen."

She opened it.

The first pages were ordinary enough — a ledger, actually, in a fine antique hand. Debts recorded, debts paid, debts forgiven. Names she half-recognized from the valley's older families. But the handwriting changed partway through, loosening, growing more urgent, and the entries stopped being about money. One line read: *Paid in full: the east field, three daughters' worth of luck.* Another: *Owed and unpaid: one true name, to be collected by the wood.* Another simply said *forgiven* over and over, filling a page, as though the writer had needed the practice.

Then the ledgers ended, and the prophecies began.

Sable had seen prophecy before, of course. Every temple kept its shelf of them — mostly the fevered scratchings of farmers who'd eaten the wrong mushrooms, dutifully catalogued under *Apocrypha, Agricultural*. They were good for a laugh on slow afternoons. This was not that. This was precise. This was *dated*.

The date stopped her cold.

One hundred years ago. To the season. To the month.

She turned the page, and the hair on her arms stood up, because the ink was fresh.

Not looked-fresh. Not well-preserved. *Fresh* — black and wet-looking, catching the candlelight the way new ink catches light, the way her own ink caught light at this desk every night. A century-old book should have browned, faded, flaked. This looked like it had been written while she was climbing the stairs.

And the writing described tonight.

She read it twice before her hands stopped shaking enough to hold the pages steady. The entry was short. Prophecy, apparently, did not waste words:

*A scribe comes at the turn of the century. Bound, but not yet broken. Her name is written in the temple's debt-book: Sable Villalobos. She will come on a night of late frost, alone, by candlelight, and she will find this page. Beside her walks a wolf who hates what he serves. Through her hand, the hundred-year hunger ends — or begins. The ink decides.*

Below it, in smaller letters, almost an afterthought:

*Her face is the face on the next page.*

Sable sat very still.

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind moved through the bare orchard trees with the dry rattle of late frost — exactly the kind of night the entry described, which proved nothing, because most nights in Corren Hollow were nights of late frost.

But her name. Her exact name, spelled correctly, including the accent her mother had insisted on and the temple clerks always got wrong. Nobody spelled it right. Nobody *cared* enough to spell it right.

Slowly — the way you approach a dog that might be friendly or might be rabid — she turned to the next page.

There was a drawing there.

It was her.

Not approximately her. Not a flattering guess. It was her face rendered by someone who had studied it closely and honestly: the slight asymmetry of her brows, the ink stain on the side of her right hand where she rested her wrist, the stubborn set of her jaw that Mother Anselm called pride and Sable called survival. Even the small scar above her eyebrow from the time she'd fallen off the tithe-wagon at age seven. The artist had gotten *the scar*.

The figure in the drawing stood beside a wolf — huge, dark, its head turned toward the viewer with an expression that was unsettlingly intelligent, unsettlingly *tired*. Between them, drawn in lines that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them, ran a thread of sigils. She recognized the style. It was debt-script. Binding-script. The language of contracts and obligations that the temple used to keep its accounts — and, if you believed the older stories, to keep rather more than its accounts.

Except the sigils in the drawing were wrong. Or rather, they were *unfinished*, trailing off mid-stroke, as though whoever drew them had been interrupted. As though the picture itself was waiting for something to complete it.

"This isn't funny," Sable said quietly. Her voice sounded thin in the big empty room. "If Fenwick put you up to this—"

But Fenwick couldn't have. Fenwick couldn't draw a straight line with a ruler, and besides, the book had come from the Halloway attic, sealed in oilcloth, older than anyone alive. Unless old Marta Halloway had spent her long farming life practicing portrait art of a girl born sixty years after her death, this was—

This was impossible.

Sable was a creature of ledgers. She believed in columns and sums and the satisfying closure of a balanced account. Impossibilities made her nervous the way uncounted coin made a moneylender nervous. So she did the only thing her training allowed: she reached for her catalogue pen, to enter the item properly, *Anomalous text, provenance Halloway farmstead, contents apocryphal, condition—*

Her hand hovered over the page.

And the ink moved.

It was subtle at first — a shimmer, like heat off summer stones, running through the letters of the prophecy. Then the words themselves began to rearrange, sliding across the page with the smooth deliberateness of ants relocating a colony, and re-formed into a single new line:

*Touch it, Sable.*

"No," she said.

The ink waited. The whole page seemed to wait. Even the candle flame leaned toward the book, elongating, attentive.

"I don't touch things that talk," she informed it. "It's rule one of the archive. Well, rule four. Rules one through three are about not spilling tea. But it's a *strong* four."

The ink shimmered again, and she could have sworn — she would swear later, when it mattered, when people asked her how it happened — that the letters formed something almost like amusement.

*You already touched it. You've been touching it since you opened the cover. The question was never whether, little scribe. Only when.*

"That's—" She stopped. Swallowed. Because the voice wasn't a voice at all; it was arriving somewhere behind her eyes, in the same place memories lived, and it knew her name, and it was patient in the way that the pulse beneath the cover was patient. One beat a minute. It had been beating like that for a hundred years. It could certainly wait out one frightened woman with a stub of candle.

She should close the book. She should carry it to Mother Anselm's door and wake the whole convent and let three generations of senior clergy argue about it until morning. That was procedure. Procedure was safe. Procedure was the entire architecture of her life: do the task, balance the column, count the days, three hundred and eleven, three hundred and ten.

But the drawing looked up at her from the page — her own face, wearing an expression she didn't recognize yet but somehow knew was coming, an expression of someone standing at the edge of a story much larger than herself — and beneath it the wolf watched her with those tired, knowing eyes, and Sable felt something in her chest that had nothing to do with fear.

It felt like the moment before opening a letter you've waited your whole life for.

"Oh, this is a terrible idea," she whispered.

She touched the page.

The ink took her hand.

Not grabbed — *took*, the way water takes a dropped stone, folding around her fingers, up her wrists, warm and utterly wrong, because ink should be wet and this was neither wet nor dry but something in between, something alive. The sigils in the drawing flared white. The unfinished strokes began, at last, to finish themselves, racing toward completion, and Sable had one lucid, furious thought — *I never agreed to any of this, put it in the ledger, I want it in the ledger* — and then the scriptorium folded up around her like a page being turned.

The candle went out.

The archive vanished.

She fell through darkness that smelled of every book she'd ever loved, and somewhere below her — impossibly far and impossibly near — she heard the pulse of the book quicken at last, one beat a minute becoming many, a heart waking from a century's sleep, and the last thing she felt before the world broke open was the sensation of being *read*: every debt, every longing, every restless night at this desk, turning over in some vast attention like pages in a patient hand.

Then: light. Cold air. Stone under her knees.

Sable gasped and caught herself before her face met the ground — cobbles, she realized distantly, worn smooth, nothing like the packed earth of Corren Hollow. Noise crashed around her, enormous and layered: bells, drums, thousands of voices chanting in a language that was almost hers but bent strangely at the edges. Above the rooftops of an unfamiliar city, banners streamed in a sky the color of a bruise.

And down the great avenue, moving toward her through the crowd, came a procession — and at its center, on a throne carried on the shoulders of twelve men, sat a king in a crown of black iron, and the king's shadow fell the wrong way, stretching against the sun, reaching.

Reaching toward her.

The crowd began to chant a name. *Aldric. Aldric. Aldric.*

Sable, still on her knees in the middle of the avenue, in a dead man's borrowed century, did the only reasonable thing left in her inventory.

She looked down at her ink-stained hands, and said, "Well. This is going in the ledger."
