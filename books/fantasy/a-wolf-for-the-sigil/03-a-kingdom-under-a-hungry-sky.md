## 3. A Kingdom Under a Hungry Sky

The first thing Sable noticed about the kingdom of Vael was that it smelled like a festival.

The second thing she noticed was that she was lying in a gutter.

She sat up too fast and immediately regretted it, pressing a palm to her temple as the world performed a slow, insolent rotation. Cobblestones. Bunting. The distant bellow of a crowd. Somewhere a fife was playing, badly, and a vendor was shouting about candied chestnuts in an accent that made the word *chestnuts* sound like a threat.

"Right," she said to no one. "So I've either died, or I've had a stroke, or the book was *real*."

She looked down at her hands. Ink-stained, as always. The right one still bore the faint brown ghost of the sigil she'd touched — the one that had reached up out of the page and *pulled*, the way a current pulls a swimmer, the way a debt pulls a debtor. Her satchel was still on her shoulder, which felt absurdly domestic. She checked it anyway. Ledger. Quill. The little tin of dried plums she rationed like a miser. All present.

The prophecy book, however, was gone.

That should have frightened her more than it did. What frightened her, actually, was that she couldn't summon a single memory of the passage itself — only the sensation of it, like the afterimage of a lightning strike, and a word pressed into the back of her teeth that she hadn't put there: *Vael*.

She got to her feet, using a shuttered window for balance, and took stock of her surroundings with the methodical calm of a woman who had spent eleven years cataloguing other people's chaos for a living.

She was standing in a narrow street that opened, thirty paces ahead, onto a wide avenue. The avenue was lined with banners — white and gold, stitched with a sigil she recognized with a lurch of the stomach, because she had *copied* it last night, over and over, in a ledger that had no business existing. A crown. But the crown on the banners was drawn wrong, or rather drawn *too right*, with too many points, seven of them, and the seventh point curled like a talon.

The crowd along the avenue was dense and festive and chanting.

"*Aldric! Aldric! Aldric!*"

Coronation, then. She had landed in the middle of a coronation. Sable closed her eyes and performed the small exercise her temple tutor had taught her for moments of crisis, which consisted of listing five things that were true. One: she was in Vael. Two: Vael was a hundred years and a whole cosmology away from Corren Hollow. Three: she had no money, no papers, and no idea what the local currency was, though given the candied chestnut economy she suspected chestnuts were involved. Four: the prophecy had said *a wolf at her side*, and she did not currently see any wolves. Five: the chanting was getting louder, which meant the procession was getting closer, which meant she should probably stop standing in a gutter looking like exactly what she was — a foreigner who had fallen out of nowhere.

She was halfway through composing a plan — find a side street, find food, find out what year it was — when she saw the king.

The procession came around the corner in a wash of trumpets and horseflesh: knights in white-and-gold, a rank of priests swinging censers, and at the center, on a white horse that deserved a better rider, a young man in a cloak of silver thread. King Aldric. He was handsome in the way that new coins are handsome, all clean edges and honest shine, and he was smiling and waving, and the crowd loved him — she could feel their love, warm as bread.

And his shadow was moving wrong.

Sable had spent her life looking at ink. She knew what shadows were supposed to do. They were supposed to be loyal. They were supposed to follow, obedient as a well-trained hound, one heartbeat behind their owner. The king's shadow did not follow. The king's shadow *led* — leaning ahead of the horse, ahead of the trumpeters, its seven-pointed crown-shape stretched long across the cobbles even though the sun was nearly overhead. And when the king raised his hand to wave, the shadow's arm rose a half-second *before* his did, like a puppeteer testing the strings.

No one else saw it. The crowd was too busy chanting, the priests too busy swinging their censers, the king too busy smiling. Sable watched the shadow's crowned head turn — slowly, deliberately, with the unhurried confidence of something that had been waiting a very long time — until it was looking directly at her.

She did the only reasonable thing. She got out of there.

* * *

The side streets of the capital were narrower and less festive and considerably more honest about their smells. Sable walked with the brisk purpose of a woman who knew exactly where she was going, which is a skill every scribe learns, because the alternative is admitting you're lost and being charged accordingly.

Her plan, such as it was, had three steps. Step one: food. She had a few coins from Corren Hollow in her satchel, and while they might be worthless here, vendors were famously flexible about currency when the customer looked hungry and pitiable enough. Step two: information. Step three: the book. It had pulled her here; presumably it hadn't dragged her across a century of nowhere just to abandon her in an alley. Books that went to the trouble of kidnapping a person generally wanted something.

She had completed step one — a meat pie purchased through an elaborate pantomime and two of her temple tokens, which the vendor had squinted at and accepted with the expression of a man deciding it wasn't his problem — and was deep into step two, which consisted of loitering near a well and listening, when the city changed key.

The chanting stopped. In its place came bells — not the bright bells of celebration but a lower, heavier peal, and beneath it a sound she'd only ever read about in the temple's oldest records and had always assumed was decorative: the *hollow note*, a bell rung with no clapper, resonating from its own emptiness. It was the sound, the records said, that Vael's kings used to call the realm to attention. The records had also said the hollow note could only be rung by the true king, and Sable, chewing her pie, thought: *well, that's ominous, because it sounds like it's being rung by something with a lot of teeth.*

A crier mounted the fountain. "By decree of the Crown Regent!" he shouted. "The coronation is suspended for the Rite of Vigil! All pilgrims and foreign persons will present themselves at the Gate of Scribes for counting! The realm is *watched*! The realm is *kept*!"

The crowd around the well dispersed with the smooth efficiency of people who had done this many times before and did not enjoy it. Sable stood very still, pie in hand, and did the arithmetic. *All foreign persons will present themselves for counting.* She was, at this precise moment, the most foreign person in the entire kingdom. She had appeared out of thin air in a gutter on coronation morning. She had no papers, no accent they'd recognize, and — she realized, with a cold slide in her stomach — she was still wearing the ink smudge of a sigil on her right hand that she had last seen in a forbidden prophecy book.

A guard would take one look at that hand and reach exactly the wrong conclusion.

Or, she thought optimistically, the *right* one. It was hard to say which was worse.

"Ma'am." A hand on her shoulder. "You'll come with me."

She turned. The guard was young, earnest, and armed with a halberd that looked better polished than practiced. Behind him stood two more, less young, less earnest, considerably more practiced.

"Of course," Sable said, because eleven years of temple service had trained her for exactly one response to authority, and it was *compliance with excellent posture*. "I'm a scribe. I was just—"

"Foreign scribe, on coronation day, unregistered." The older guard took her arm, not roughly, but with the impersonal grip of a man moving a piece of furniture. "That's a cell and a question, at minimum. Possibly a question and *then* a cell. Depends on the Wolf."

"The Wolf?" Sable asked, and then immediately regretted showing interest.

The older guard's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You'll see."

* * *

The cell was, by Sable's professional assessment of confined spaces, a three out of ten. Stone walls, a straw pallet, a slot in the door, and a window too high and narrow to be anything but an insult. But it was dry, it was quiet, and — this was the part that genuinely unsettled her — the walls were covered in sigils, old ones, worn nearly smooth by time, and every single one of them was a *binding*.

She knew bindings. She had copied thousands. Debt-contracts, vow-seals, the little knotwork marks that kept a temple's promises enforceable and its debtors in place. The sigils on these walls were the same family, the same grammar — but older, wilder, written in a hand that had been afraid. Someone had warded this cell against something getting *out*.

Or, she amended, sitting down slowly on the pallet, against something getting *in*.

The meat pie, she noted, had been confiscated at the gate. She was still bitter about it.

Hours passed, measured by the movement of a stripe of light across the floor. Sable catalogued, because cataloguing was what she did when the world came apart: she had crossed a hundred years and a sealed border by touching a page. The prophecy had been accurate to the day. The king's shadow moved wrong, which meant the crown was already feeding, which meant the hundred-year hunger was real, which meant she was standing — sitting, technically — in the opening chapter of something that had been written before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

And the prophecy had said *a wolf at her side*.

The bolt in the door slid back with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

The man who entered was not what the word *guard* had prepared her for. He was younger than she expected, somewhere in the gray country between twenty-five and thirty, with dark hair that looked like it had been cut by someone in a hurry and a face that was all sharp planes and stubborn angles. He wore the white and gold of the crown's service, but the way he wore it was wrong — like a coat belonging to someone else, buttoned by someone else's hands. His eyes were amber. Not brown-with-amber-in-it. *Amber*, the color of resin and lantern-light, and they caught the cell's dimness and held it.

He did not sit. He did not introduce himself. He stood by the door with his arms folded and looked at her the way she imagined a wolf looked at a snare: checking where the wire was, and whether it was worth chewing through.

"You're the gutter-woman," he said.

"I'm the scribe," Sable said. "The gutter was circumstantial."

One eyebrow moved a fraction. "Scribes don't fall out of the sky on coronation morning."

"This one did." She held up her ink-stained hand, sigil-ghost and all, before she could talk herself out of it. Defiance, she had learned long ago, was mostly a matter of choosing which fear to show. "And before you ask — no, I'm not a spy. Spies have handlers, and handlers have plans, and no plan on earth involves arriving face-first in a gutter with no money and no idea what year it is. I'm a *scribe*. I copy ledgers. I was cataloguing a book, and the book disagreed with being catalogued."

The amber eyes went to her hand. Stayed there. Something moved behind them — recognition, or its close cousin, dread.

"Show me the sigil again," he said quietly. "Slowly."

Sable traced it in the air, from memory, the way she'd traced it a hundred times in ledgers: the seven points, the curling seventh, the knot at the base that bound the whole design to a single word. When she finished, the man had gone very still, in the specific way of a creature deciding whether to flee or fight.

"Where did you learn that mark?" he asked.

"In a book," Sable said. "A prophecy book. A hundred years old. It has my name in it. It has my *face* in it, drawn by someone who never met me. And it has a wolf in it, which I assume—" she looked him in the eye, because there was no point pretending she hadn't seen the amber, the stillness, the way he held his weight like something four-legged wearing a man loosely, "—is you."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the hollow bell rang again, long and low, and the sigils on the walls seemed to shiver in their old stone.

"You don't know what you've walked into," the man said at last.

"No," Sable agreed. "But I know what I *saw*. Your king rode past me this morning, and his shadow bowed to the crowd before he did. I know that's not a figure of speech. I know the seventh point of the crown is a talon, and I know the thing wearing that crown has been hungry for a hundred years, and I know—" her voice did not shake, which she counted as a professional triumph, "—that according to a book older than this cell, I'm the one who's supposed to do something about it. So you can stand there looking at me like I'm a trap, or you can tell me your name, because I've been in this cell for six hours without a meat pie and I am *not* in the mood for dramatic silence."

The man stared at her. And then — briefly, unwillingly, like a crack in ice — he almost smiled.

"Wren," he said.

"Wren," Sable repeated. "Of course it is. The book didn't mention that part. The book was very thorough about the dying, though, so if you're the wolf in question, we should probably talk."

Something flickered across his face at the word *dying* — quick and cold, there and shuttered. He glanced at the door, at the sigils, at her, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped low enough that only she could hear it.

"Then talk fast, scribe. Because the crown asked for you by name an hour ago." His amber eyes held hers, and there was no warmth in them at all — only a warning, and under the warning, faint as a heartbeat, something that might have been fear. "And the crown *always* gets what it asks for."
