## 4. The Wolf Who Hates His Leash

The cell smelled of wet stone and old fear, which was standard for the undercroft of Vael Keep. What was not standard was the prisoner.

She was sitting cross-legged on the cot when Wren came down the stairs, and she was *reading*. Not pacing, not weeping, not clawing at the bars like the last three spies the crown had fished out of the coronation crowds. She had found — gods knew where, because he'd personally watched them strip her of every possession — a loose thread on the blanket's hem, and she was worrying it between her fingers with the deep concentration of a scholar deciphering a difficult text.

"You're going to unravel that," Wren said.

"I'm going to *catalogue* it," she said, without looking up. "It's what I do."

He stopped at the bars. Up close, in the light of his lantern, she looked even less like a spy than she had in the courtyard, where she'd been hauled past him dripping river water and shouting that she wasn't finished, that the book hadn't let her finish. Her hair was dark and badly cut, the kind of cut a person gave themselves with a knife and a grudge. Her wrists were thin. There was ink on her fingers — not the smudge of a forger, but the stain of someone who wrote all day, every day, until it got into the skin.

"Name," he said.

"Sable Villalobos." She looked up then, and her eyes were the problem. Spies looked at you sideways, calculating exits. She looked at him the way people look at weather they've read about but never seen.

"Villalobos," he repeated. "That's not a Vaelish name."

"No. It's Corren Hollow. We don't really do surnames so much as debt-designations, but the temple liked the sound of it."

"The temple."

"The Temple of the Quiet Ledger." She said it the way other people said *home*, and something flickered across her face that made him think maybe she didn't mean it kindly after all.

Wren leaned one shoulder against the stone beside the bars, which was a thing he did to make prisoners relax, because relaxed prisoners talked, and talking prisoners could be weighed. It usually worked. It was not working now, because she was watching him do it with an expression of mild professional interest, like a fellow tradesman noting a technique.

"You crossed three hundred miles of sealed border," he said, "in a single night, into a kingdom on the most heavily guarded day of its century, and appeared in the middle of a coronation procession. Walk me through how that happens by accident."

"It's a long story."

"I have the watch shift."

"It's a *strange* story."

"Those are my favorite kind," Wren said. "I'm a wolf who takes orders from a man wearing a haunted hat. My standards for strange are very forgiving."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The beginning of one, aborted, like a page torn out before the ending was written. He noticed that he wanted to see the rest of it, and he filed that want away in the drawer where he kept things that were none of his business.

"There was a book," she said finally. "A prophecy ledger, a hundred years old. It came into our archive for cataloguing. The pages were warm." She paused, and her hands curled slightly, remembering. "My name was in it. Written a century ago. And my face — there was a drawing, and it was *me*, down to the scar on my chin from falling off the temple roof when I was nine. And tonight's date. And—" she stopped.

"And?"

"And a wolf at my side," she said quietly. "Standing over me. Like it was guarding me."

The lantern flame bent, though there was no draft. Wren kept his face still, which was easy, because he'd had thirty years of practice keeping his face still around people who would kill him for what moved behind it.

"Prophecies are cheap," he said. "Every hedge-witch in Vael sells one for a copper. They're vague on purpose. A wolf could be anyone. Half the northern houses have a wolf on their sigil."

"Not this wolf." She stood, crossed to the bars, and held up her wrist. On the pale inside of it, faint as breath on glass, was a mark: a line of script in no alphabet he recognized, coiled like a sleeping animal. "This appeared when I touched the page. Do you know what it is? Because I've been sitting here for four hours deciding whether to be terrified of it, and I'd love a second opinion."

He should have said no. He should have said *I have no idea, stranger, sounds like a curse, we'll fetch a priest.* That was the oath talking, the leash-voice, the smooth cold instruction that lived behind his teeth ever since the day he'd knelt on the coronation stones and sworn his pack's survival into the crown's keeping.

Instead what came out of him was: "Don't scratch it."

Her eyebrows went up. "I wasn't going to—"

"You were. Everyone scratches it. It's a binding-mark. Old magic. Older than the crown, or the same age; nobody alive knows which. Scratching it just makes it settle deeper." He heard himself and hated himself, a little, for the fluency of it. "Where did you learn about the rites of the Hollow Crown?"

She blinked. "I didn't say anything about—"

"You said 'haunted hat' didn't bother you. You looked at my sword-belt — left hip, where the ceremonial dagger sits — and you flinched. That dagger is used in exactly one rite, and it isn't performed publicly. So either you're the best-trained spy I've ever met, or you know things you shouldn't." He straightened off the wall. "Which is it?"

For a long moment she just looked at him. Then, absurdly, she sat back down on the cot and folded her hands like a schoolgirl preparing to be reasonable, which was somehow more unnerving than anything else she'd done.

"In the book," she said, "there were marginal notes. A different hand than the main text. Someone annotating the prophecy as it unfolded, year by year, decade by decade. Notes about the king. About the crown. About—" she hesitated again, and he watched her decide something, watched the decision cost her, "—about feeding it."

The undercroft was very quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled through six feet of stone, the city was still singing its coronation songs, bright and stupid and joyful.

"What did the notes say about feeding it?" Wren asked. His voice came out flat.

"That it eats memories. That it started small — a king forgetting a servant's name, a queen losing the tune of a lullaby — and that it grows bolder the longer it wears. And that the last annotator wrote, forty years ago, that the current king was already 'half gone,' and that the court had learned not to notice, because noticing was dangerous." She looked up at him. "Is that true?"

*Say no,* said the leash. *Say she's mad, say it's propaganda, say whatever keeps her contained and keeps your pack breathing.*

What Wren actually thought of, unhelpfully, was last winter. Aldric standing at a window overlooking the training yard, watching young soldiers drill, and asking Wren — genuinely asking, with the ghost of the man he used to be looking out of his eyes — *"Who are they mourning?"* There was nothing to mourn. It was a Tuesday. The king had forgotten Tuesdays existed.

And he thought of the queen-regent's smile, which never reached any further than it needed to.

"The king is ill," Wren said. "It's been a long reign. Illness is common in long reigns."

"That's not a no."

"That's not a question I'm permitted to answer."

Her head tilted. "Permitted by whom?"

"By my oath." He said it simply, because dressing it up would be worse. "I swore to the crown. Body, nose, and fang. There are perhaps nine people in Vael who hold oaths the way my people do, and I am one of them, and what that means is that there are questions I cannot answer even when the answer is chewing through my ribs trying to get out."

"That sounds awful," she said, with such frank sympathy that he almost laughed.

"It has its moments." He crouched down to her level, forearms on his knees, and lowered his voice even though the corridor was empty. This was the part where he was supposed to press her. Extract names, contacts, the route of entry, the paymaster. Every question the protocol demanded sat in his mouth like stones.

Instead he heard himself ask: "The prophecy. The wolf at your side. Did the book say what happens to him?"

"Why?"

"Because in my experience," Wren said, "books like that don't put a wolf in a story unless the wolf is about to be asked to do something, and I would very much like to know if it's something I've already agreed to."

Sable studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy some internal ledger of hers, because she nodded slowly.

"He dies," she said softly. "Near the end. Protecting me. It's written very plainly. The annotator underlined it twice."

The cold that went through Wren then was an old, familiar cold — the same one from the day the crown's soldiers burned the Thornwood den and called it pest control. He'd survived that by telling himself it was fate, that wolves were made for dying in other people's stories. Apparently the universe had taken notes and intended to be consistent.

"Well," he said. "At least it's plain. I hate ambiguity."

"You're joking."

"Wolves joke. It surprises people. Keeps them off balance." He stood, because staying crouched near her was doing something inconvenient to his judgment. "Here's what happens now. I report to the Lord Marshal that you're disoriented, untrained, and almost certainly not a spy — which is true, incidentally. No spymaster alive would send someone who volunteers information this fast. You'll be held as a curiosity rather than a traitor, which is a better category. Curiosities get fed."

"They also get dissected," Sable said.

"Occasionally. Try to stay interesting in a survivable way." He turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the stair rail, because there was one more thing and the leash couldn't stop him saying this much: "The mark on your wrist. If it starts to burn — truly burn, not itch — you call for me. Not the guards. Me. Say my name is Wren and that the wolf told you to ask."

"Why would I trust you?"

"Because I just told you I die in your story," he said, "and I'd rather like to argue with the author before the ending gets copied fair."

He climbed the stairs two at a time, heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional, and told himself firmly that it was the stairs.

* * *

The report took him less time than the walking away did.

Lord Marshal Cade received it in his office above the armory, a room that always smelled of oil and iron and the particular ambition of a man who had never once in his life been surprised by anything he'd allowed to happen. Cade listened without interrupting, which was his way, and then set down his pen.

"A temple scribe from beyond the seal," he said. "Appearing out of empty air in front of ten thousand witnesses."

"So she claims."

"You believe her."

Wren kept his face still. "I believe she believes herself. Whether that's truth or delusion, the archivists can sort out."

"Mm." Cade looked at him a moment too long. "There's a matter you should hear before the council convenes. His Majesty has requested an audience with the prisoner."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Requested."

"Asked for her by description. Before she was even brought inside the walls." Cade smiled, and it was the smile of a man handing someone a live coal. "The king says he's seen her before. In a dream, he says. He says the crown showed him her face, and that she is 'the ink the prophecy is waiting for.'"

Outside the window, the city sang on, bright and oblivious. And beneath the singing, so faint that only a wolf's ears could have caught it, Wren heard the keep itself make a sound — a low, grinding hum from the direction of the throne room, like a great jaw shifting in its sleep.

Like something tasting a new word.

"When?" he asked.

"At dawn," said Cade. "Clean her up. The crown does dislike dirt."
