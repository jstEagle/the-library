## 6. Old Debts, Older Magic

Wren had guarded the cells beneath the White Spire for three years, and in all that time he had learned one thing with absolute certainty: nobody escaped. Not because the walls were thick, though they were, or because the guards were vigilant, though they were. Nobody escaped because the crown did not *want* them to escape. The cells held exactly as tightly as the crown's hunger required, no more, and when a prisoner stopped being useful, the door simply opened and the prisoner walked out hollow-eyed and wrong, and was never spoken of again.

So when he came down the stair at moonset with a stolen ring of keys sweating in his fist, Wren felt less like a traitor than like a man doing something the building expected of him. That didn't make it comfortable.

The stranger was awake. Of course she was. She'd been awake every time he'd checked on her for two days — sitting cross-legged on the cot with her back straight as a ruled line, watching the door with those ink-stained fingers laced together, like a woman waiting for an appointment rather than a prisoner awaiting judgment. Scribes. He'd known a few. They were all the same: terrified and stubborn in equal measure, and convinced that if they could just get the words right, the world would behave.

"Up," he said through the bars. "Quietly. We have perhaps forty minutes before the changing of the watch, and I intend to spend thirty-nine of them running."

She stood. She didn't ask questions, which unsettled him more than screaming would have. She simply looked at him — at his face, then lower, at the oath-cord knotted at his wrist, gray wool shot through with a single thread of crown-gold — and something in her expression shifted from fear to arithmetic.

"You're the wolf," she said. "The one they set to watch me."

"I'm the guard assigned to your cell."

"The prophecy says a wolf at my side." Her voice was flat, factual, the voice of someone reading aloud from a ledger. "I assumed it meant something else. A herald's badge, perhaps. A dog."

"A dog," Wren repeated.

"It would have been tidier."

He unlocked the door because his hands were already moving and arguing with them would waste time. "Listen to me, scribe. I'm not doing this for you. Understand that first, last, and always. The crown wants you. It sent knights to fetch you before you'd even been properly questioned, which means it has plans for you, and I have seen what happens to people the crown has plans for. There is a woman in the east tower who used to be the king's nurse. She still smiles. That's all she does now. Smiles, and asks after your health, forever, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. I am not going to stand by while the crown does that to someone else. Not because I care about *you*."

"Naturally," she said.

"Don't be flippant."

"I'm not being flippant, I'm being efficient. You've rehearsed this speech. I could hear the rehearsal in it. Let's go."

Wren stared at her for a moment. Then, despite everything — despite the oath-cord on his wrist and the three years of careful, obedient grayness — he felt the corner of his mouth twitch. He smothered it immediately. Trusting this woman was dangerous. Liking her would be fatal.

* * *

They went up, not out. Every escape route Wren had ever mapped led down and toward the river gates, and every one of them was watched, because the crown knew how wolves thought and had built its prison accordingly. So instead he took her into the heart of the Spire itself, through the laundry galleries where steam hung thick enough to hide a shadow, past the scriptorium annex where the night scribes dozed over their copies, and out onto the roof of the low cloister that connected the Spire to the outer wall.

Sable — she'd given her name finally, on the second day, in the tone of someone paying out information in small grudging coins — kept pace without complaint. But halfway across the cloister roof she stopped dead, and Wren nearly went over the eaves before he caught himself and turned.

She was looking at the palace.

From here, above the laundry smoke, you could see the whole of the coronation city laid out under the hungry sky: the banners, the bonfires, the procession route still strewn with petals from three days ago, and above it all the White Spire's crown-tower, where King Aldric sat with the thing on his head that was eating him alive one memory at a time.

"He looked at me," Sable said quietly. "When they brought me before him. And it wasn't him looking. There was something behind his eyes, working him like a puppet works a hand, and it *offered* me things, Wren. It offered me my freedom. My debt, gone, just like that. All I had to do was finish what the book started." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Do you know what my debt is? Do you know what I gave up?"

"No," Wren said. "And I don't want to. Debts are chains either way, and I don't chain myself to strangers."

"That's very wise," she said, in a voice that suggested it was nothing of the kind. "But you should know that I refused it. Whatever your crown wanted from me, whatever bargain it whispered through that poor man's mouth — I said no. So whatever you think I am, I'm not its creature. If anything, I'm the opposite of its creature. I'm its disappointment."

Something in Wren's chest loosened half a degree. He hated that. He turned away and kept moving. "Roof ends here. There's a drain-spout wide enough for a slim person and a lot of faith. After that it's rooftops to the tannery lane, and after that we're outside the walls and I stop knowing the ground."

"And after that?"

"After that," Wren said, "we go to the last place in Vael the crown will ever look for us. We go to the Thornwood."

* * *

The Thornwood began where the king's maps ended.

That wasn't poetry; it was policy. Three generations ago, the crown had struck the forest from every chart and survey in the kingdom, erased the roads, burned the bridges, and declared the whole of it void — not wilderness, but *nothing*, a blank space with thorns in it. Officially, no one lived there. Officially, it was haunted, cursed, diseased, pick your rumor. Unofficially, the crown had emptied it of people the way you'd empty a house of rats before fumigating, and the people it had emptied it of were wolves.

Wren's people.

He didn't tell Sable any of this as they walked. He told her nothing at all for the first six hours, which she bore with a patience he found increasingly suspicious, until finally, at dusk, when they'd crossed the tannery flats and the wall behind them was a gray suggestion against the sky, she sat down on a fallen log and refused to move another step.

"I need to say something," she announced, "and you're going to hate it."

"Probably."

"You walk like a man leading a funeral procession for one. You haven't spoken since the wall. You keep touching that cord on your wrist like you're checking it's still there, and every time the road bends toward the trees ahead, your shoulders climb up around your ears. So either you're taking me somewhere terrible, or somewhere sad, and I'd genuinely like to know which before my legs give out entirely."

Wren looked at her. Moonrise was coming up behind her, silvering the edges of her, and she sat there on her log with her ink-stained hands folded and her chin up, exhausted, hunted, a stranger in a world that wanted to use her, and absolutely refusing to be managed.

"My pack lived there," he said. "In the Thornwood. Before the crown exiled us. Before—" He stopped. Started again, rougher. "There were ninety-three of us when the edict came. I swore to the crown to spare them. Knelt in the throne room and put my neck under the oath-cord and promised my loyalty, my nose, my teeth, all of it, if the pack was left in peace. And the crown accepted. And then, four winters later, it sent soldiers anyway, and burned the den, and what was left of us scattered so far that most days I couldn't name where any of them are. So yes, scribe. Somewhere sad. Are your legs equal to it?"

To her credit, she didn't offer sympathy. Sympathy would have been unbearable. Instead she stood, brushed off her skirt, and said, "Then we'll walk faster. Sad places are worse in the dark."

And that — that absurd, practical, unsentimental sentence — was the moment Wren first felt the dangerous flicker of it. Not trust, exactly. Something smaller and more survivable. The sense that this woman's spine and his own were made of similar timber.

They walked faster.

* * *

The Thornwood received them the way an old wound receives a probing finger.

By full dark the trees had closed overhead and the king's roads had dwindled to deer paths and then to memory, and Wren let his nose take over, because his nose remembered even if his heart wished it didn't. He smelled the den before he saw it: char and old ash, sweetened by twenty years of moss, and underneath it, faint as a signature, the musk of his own bloodline, ghosted into the bark of the trees.

Sable stopped at the edge of the clearing and put her hand over her mouth.

The great den had been built into the roots of a hill — an entrance of woven timber wide enough for ten wolves abreast, now collapsed and blackened, furred over with ivy that had done its patient work trying to knit the wound shut. Someone had raised cairns. Dozens of them, small and careful, scattered through the clearing among the nettles, each stone stacked by some survivor who had come back, once, to say what couldn't be said aloud.

"We don't have to—"

"I'm fine," Sable said, in the voice of someone who was not fine, and walked into the clearing anyway, and knelt at the nearest cairn, and did the thing scribes apparently did: she read it. There were no words on the stones, but she read it anyway, the way they were placed, the way the largest stones sat at the top like bowed heads. When she rose, her eyes were wet, and she wiped them briskly, angrily, as though tears were an inefficiency.

"I'm sorry," she said. "Not — not the groveling kind. The useful kind. I'm sorry in the way that means I understand why you're taking me somewhere the crown won't look. You brought me to a grave because graves are the one place hunger doesn't follow. That's clever, and it's terrible, and I'll try to be worth the trouble."

Wren found he couldn't answer. He busied himself checking the collapsed den's side passage — the one the soldiers had never found, the one his mother had shown him when he was small, saying *always know two ways out of anywhere, little wolf* — and it was still clear, still dry, opening into a root-chamber that smelled of earth and old safety. He spread his cloak there. Built a fire small enough to hide. And when he finally spoke, his voice came out lower than he intended.

"Why did you refuse it? The crown's bargain. Most people would have taken freedom. Most people would have taken it with both hands and asked for seconds."

Sable sat down across the fire from him and held her palms toward the heat, and for a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "Because I've spent my whole life being *used*, Wren. My temple owns eleven years of my labor against a debt my mother signed before I could walk. Every page I copy, every ledger I balance, goes into their coffers, and they call it holy, and they call it fair, and they call it my honor to pay. I know what it feels like when something large and ancient tells you it's being generous while it tightens its grip. The crown offered me freedom the way my temple offers me dignity. On a leash, priced in obedience." She looked up, and firelight caught the stubborn set of her jaw. "Freedom that's given to you by the thing that wants to eat you isn't freedom. It's seasoning."

Wren laughed. It startled him — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose — and he covered it by poking the fire harder than the fire deserved.

"You should sleep," he said. "First watch is mine."

"You'll wake me for mine?"

"I said first watch is mine. I didn't say anything about yours."

"Wolf," she said, settling onto his cloak with the dignity of a duchess claiming a throne, "if you carry the whole night yourself, you'll be useless tomorrow, and if you're useless tomorrow, we die, and if we die, I will haunt you with *extensive* commentary on your decision-making. Wake me for my watch."

He woke her for her watch. It seemed safer.

* * *

It was near dawn, during her watch — he knew because he wasn't sleeping, merely resting his eyes with the vigilance of a soldier and the paranoia of a wolf — when Sable made the small sound. A breath drawn in through teeth. He was on his feet before the sound finished.

"What is it?"

She was sitting with the prophecy book open on her knees — the book she'd carried through the ink, the warm book, the wrong book — and the firelight was doing something ugly to her face. Making it young. Making it frightened.

"The pages moved," she said. "While I was reading. The text — Wren, the text rearranged itself. Like it was... making room."

He crossed to her and looked over her shoulder, and the hair along his arms lifted, because he could smell the book from here: hot metal and fresh blood and something older underneath, something like the inside of the crown-tower. New lines had surfaced on the page, ink still glistening, curling around the older prophecy like a vine around a fence post:

*The wolf leads her beneath the thorned boughs, believing the dead will shelter them. The dead cannot. What hunts them knows the forest as the wolf knows it — for the forest taught the crown to hunt.*

Below that, in smaller letters, almost apologetic:

*Run.*

Wren was moving before the word finished registering, snuffing the fire, ears straining. And there — far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of *smelling* — came the thing he had dreaded and half expected: the bell-note clang of hollowed knights in black steel, many sets of boots on the forest floor, moving through the Thornwood with the easy certainty of creatures who had been here before.

Who had learned these woods the way he had.

Who had burned his den, and stacked his dead, and come back now for the woman holding the book.

"Sable," he said quietly. "Take the book. Take my hand. And whatever you hear behind us — however close it sounds — do not look back."

She took the book. She took his hand. Her grip was ink-stained and steady, and as they slipped into the deep dark between the thorned boughs, running together into the blank place on the king's map, Wren realized with grim clarity that the flicker of trust in his chest had just become something larger and considerably more inconvenient.

He was starting to believe in her.

And the forest, all around them, began to scream.
