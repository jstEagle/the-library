## 10. Two Kinds of Chains

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Wren worked up the nerve to say it.

He'd been rehearsing the words all evening — through the rabbit he'd snared and skinned, through the stew Sable had improved with some dried herb she'd found in the archivist's stores ("It's called *thyme*, Wren. The valley has it too. I refuse to believe you've never heard of thyme"), through the long silence after they'd eaten when she'd sat turning the prophecy book over in her hands like a woman deciding whether to keep a letter or burn it.

*Take her home.* That was the whole of it. Take her back to Corren Hollow, before the crown's hunters regrouped, before the thing on Aldric's head finished whatever slow meal it was making of him. Before the pages at the end of that cursed book got any closer to being *now*.

Simple. Sensible. The kind of plan a man made when he was thinking clearly instead of thinking about the way firelight caught in a scribe's ink-stained fingers.

"Sable," he said.

"Hm." She didn't look up from the book.

"I know a way out of Vael."

That got her attention. She closed the book on her thumb and looked at him across the flames, one eyebrow doing the skeptical arithmetic she did so well. "You know a way out of a kingdom you were exiled from, past knights who can smell us, in the dark, in winter."

"The Thornwood goes further than anyone in the capital remembers. There are old paths. Pack paths." He kept his voice level, practical. "They end at the mountains. Past the mountains there's a pass, and past the pass—" He almost said *home*, and had to swallow the word, because it wasn't his anymore and never would be again. "Past the pass is your valley."

She was quiet for a moment. "Corren Hollow."

"Magicless. Sealed. Nothing in Vael can follow you there — not the crown, not the knights, not the book." He nodded at the thing in her lap. "You'd be safe. Truly safe. I could have you there in nine days if the weather holds."

"And then what?" she asked.

"Then I come back here."

"Alone."

"It's what I know how to be."

She set the book down on her knee, carefully, the way she handled everything — as though even dangerous things deserved gentleness. "Wren. Walk me through this. I go home. And then what happens to Aldric?"

He'd known this question was coming. He'd been dreading it since the first rehearsal. "The crown finishes eating him."

"And then?"

"Then it has a hundred years of a king inside it, and no one left to resist it, and it starts looking outward. At the Thornwood. At what's left of my people. At everything." He poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. "I'm aware of the flaw in the plan, Sable."

"You're aware of the flaw," she repeated. "Good. Because from where I'm sitting it looks less like a flaw and more like the entire foundation."

"It's not your foundation to worry about."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It's just written across my face in a century-old book, so I take a certain professional interest."

* * *

This was the trouble with arguing with a scribe: they remembered everything you said, organized it, and handed it back to you with citations.

Wren shifted, putting more of his weight on his bad knee — the one that ached when the weather turned, which it had been doing all week, as if the sky itself were taking the crown's side. "You said it yourself, three nights ago. You're not a soldier. You're not a mage. You're a debtor with good handwriting. Every day you stay in Vael is a day the book gets closer to its ending, and I've read that ending. It has you crowned and me dead, and I don't care much for either role."

"I don't care for them either," Sable said. "Which is why I tore out the page."

"And the world screamed."

"The world flinched."

"It screamed, Sable. I heard it. The trees heard it. The hunters heard it, which is rather the point." He leaned forward. "You changed something that shouldn't be changeable, and now reality is improvising around us, and you want to talk about foundations? My plan is the only one with a floor in it."

Her jaw did the thing it did when she was deciding whether to fight fair. Usually she chose otherwise. "Your plan requires me to abandon a man to be devoured alive over the course of months while I sit in a warm valley copying ledgers."

"Yes."

"And you think I could do that."

"Yes," he said again, and hated how much he wanted her to prove him wrong, and hated more that he wanted her to agree, because agreement meant safe, and safe meant alive, and alive mattered more than anything else he could name. "You could. People do it every day. It's called surviving."

"Not to me." Her voice stayed soft, but it acquired that edge she got — the ledger-keeper's edge, the one that could cut a contract into confetti. "Wren, I spent eleven years copying other people's debts. Do you know what I learned? That everyone believes their chains are load-bearing. The temple thought its rules held up the world. The king thinks the crown holds up the kingdom. You think your oath holds up—"

"Don't." The word came out rougher than he intended.

"—your oath holds up your guilt," she finished anyway, because she always finished. "And none of it is true. Chains don't hold anything up, Wren. They only hold *in*."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"My pack died because I broke faith," Wren said quietly. "I swore to the crown to spare them, and they died anyway, and the only thing I have left — the only thing that makes their deaths mean *anything* — is that I keep my word now. Perfectly. Completely. Even when the word I gave was to a thing that eats kings. If I start choosing which promises matter—"

"Then you'd be a person," Sable said, "instead of a leash."

He stood up. It was a mistake; standing put him too close to her, close enough to see the firelight move in her eyes and the stubborn set of her mouth and the smudge of ink still on her jaw from three days ago that she didn't know was there and that he had, embarrassingly, been looking at ever since. He took a step back instead of forward, which felt like the hardest thing he'd done all week, including the running fight with the hollowed knights.

"You should sleep," he said. "We move at first light. South, toward the pass."

"We're not going toward the pass."

"We are if you say yes."

"I'm not saying yes."

"Then we're going toward the pass anyway, because I'm bigger than you and I know the way."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then, infuriatingly, she smiled — small and tired and entirely without fear, the smile of someone who had looked at a hungry ancient crown and told it *no*. "You could carry me, I suppose. But you'd have to explain to the hunters why the wolf-shifter is traveling with a scribe slung over his shoulder like a sack of turnips, and honestly, Wren, the gossip alone would follow you longer than the prophecy would."

Despite himself — despite everything — a laugh escaped him, low and unwilling. This was the other trouble with Sable Villalobos. She made the worst hours feel survivable, and survivable felt dangerously close to *good*.

"Sit down," she said, patting the ground beside her. "Please. I'm not done arguing, but my feet hurt and I'd rather do it comfortably."

* * *

He sat. Of course he sat. His body had apparently defected to her side of the argument sometime during the second week and hadn't seen fit to inform him.

For a while neither of them spoke. The fire settled. Above the black lattice of the Thornwood branches, a few stars managed to push through, thin and cold.

"When I was eight," Sable said eventually, "the temple assigned me to copy the debt rolls. Thousands of names. Every person in the Hollow and what they owed and to whom. And I noticed something — I was young, nobody had taught me yet that noticing things was insubordination — I noticed that the interest columns didn't add up. Not fraud, exactly. Just... rounding. A grain here, a coin there, always flowing uphill. Enough that no one ever paid off their debt. Enough that the temple owned everyone forever, gently, politely, with beautiful handwriting."

"That sounds familiar," Wren said.

"Mm. So I corrected them. Quietly. Recopied the rolls with honest sums." She turned her hands palm-up, studied the ink stains as if they were maps. "They caught me in a month. And instead of punishing me, they promoted me. Gave me better work, better food, a room with a window. And I was so pleased with myself for years before I understood: they hadn't forgiven the correction. They'd *acquired* the corrector. They took the one person who saw the flaw and bound her to maintaining it." She closed her hands. "My whole life, Wren, I have been the rounding error that got promoted. Useful precisely because I see what's wrong. Kept precisely because I might fix it."

"The temple designed your contract," he said. It wasn't news — the archivist's confession had seen to that — but saying it aloud in the dark made it heavier.

"They designed me. Or tried to. A successor bred in a sealed valley, raised on debt-magic, ready to be harvested whenever the seal needed renewing." She shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "And the joke of it is, they nearly succeeded. I was *content*, Wren. Eleven years of contentment. I had a window."

He wanted to say something. Everything he thought of sounded like pity, and she'd flay him for pity. So instead he said, "What was the view?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The window. What did it look at?"

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a real one, startled out of her. "A wall. Mostly. And above the wall, a slice of sky, and sometimes, in autumn, geese." Her voice softened. "I used to watch the geese and think: they know where they're going, and no one owns the sky they cross. I thought that was the freest thing in the world."

"And now you've crossed the sky yourself."

"And now I've crossed the sky myself," she agreed, "and landed in a kingdom with a hungry hat, and discovered that freedom mostly means choosing which chains you'll carry." She looked at him sidelong. "Yours is guilt. Mine is duty. The crown's is hunger. Everyone's dragging something."

"Geese drag nothing."

"Geese also eat from garbage," Sable said. "Freedom isn't free of cost, it's just free of *ownership*. Different thing entirely."

He stared at her. "Did you just make that up?"

"I'm a scribe. Making things up is most of the job. The rest is pretending you didn't."

* * *

The fire was nearly ash when he finally said the true thing, because the dark made it possible and because he was tired and because if he didn't say it tonight he was going to spend the rest of his short, prophesied life not saying it.

"If I take you home," he said, "and come back, and die at the end of that book the way it says I do — I want you to know it wouldn't be the oath that sent me back. It hasn't been the oath for a while."

Sable went very still beside him.

"I keep telling myself it's duty," he went on, watching the embers because watching her was impossible. "Duty to Aldric, duty to my dead, duty to — something. But duty doesn't check the road behind us every time you laugh. Duty doesn't learn what *thyme* is. Duty doesn't—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "When the knights had you at the river, and I thought for about ten seconds that they'd taken you, those ten seconds were worse than the day my pack died. And I've carried that day for six years. So. That's what I'm dragging. In case you were keeping a list."

Silence. Long enough that he heard his own heartbeat and disliked how loud it was.

Then Sable moved — not away, which he'd braced for, but closer, until her shoulder rested against his arm, light as a bird landing. "I am keeping a list," she said. "I'm a scribe. It's what I do." Her voice was steady, but he could feel, faintly, that hers wasn't the only heartbeat being loud. "Here's mine. When you stood between me and the hollowed knight at the ward-line, I didn't think *my guard is brave*. I thought, very clearly, *oh no*. Just that. *Oh no.* Like watching a page tear." She exhaled slowly. "I know what that feeling is, Wren. I catalogued it under a false name for weeks because acknowledging it seemed catastrophically impractical given the circumstances."

"The circumstances being the apocalypse."

"The circumstances being the apocalypse, yes. Very good, you've been listening."

He huffed something that was half laugh, half surrender. "So we're both —"

"Distracted. Catastrophically. Yes."

"And your solution is—"

"To defer it." She said it simply, without apology. "File it. Under a date we survive to reach. Because if I let myself have this — you, this, whatever it is — while Aldric is underground in his own skull and the crown is hunting us and the book is screaming our location, then I'll start protecting it. Protecting *you*. And protected things make careful choices, and careful choices right now look exactly like a pass through the mountains and a kingdom left to starve." She tilted her head back against the stone behind them. "I won't make careful choices. Not these ones. Not yet."

Wren looked down at her — at the ink stain on her jaw, at the book on her knee, at the whole improbable shape of her — and felt the argument go out of him like heat from the last ember. She was right. That was the unbearable part. She was right the way a blade is right.

"So we go east," he said. "Toward the capital. Toward the Feast of Binding."

"Toward the capital," she agreed. "Disguised, ideally. Among pilgrims. I can fake piety; I grew up around professionals."

"And the list? The filed item?"

Her shoulder pressed a fraction more firmly against his arm. "Cross-referenced," she said. "Pending."

Above them, far to the east, the sky had begun to pale — and beneath the paling, faint as a bruise, a glow that did not belong to any dawn. Wren sat up slowly, ears pricking, wolf-senses reaching through the dark.

Smoke. Too much smoke, and too far south for any hearth.

The capital was burning early this year — or something in it was.

"Sable," he said quietly. "I think the feast started without us."
