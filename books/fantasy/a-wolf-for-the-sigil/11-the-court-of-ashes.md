## 11. The Court of Ashes

The capital of Vael smelled like a bakery that had committed a crime.

That was Sable's first thought as she passed beneath the Ashgate with the pilgrim column, her scribe's robe — borrowed, ill-fitting, and smelling faintly of someone else's onions — scratching at her neck. Woodsmoke and roasting meat and honeyed bread rolled down from the palace terraces in fat, celebratory waves, and underneath it all ran something else. Something scorched. Like a fire had happened here once, long ago, and the city had decided the politest thing to do was cook over the memory.

"Stop making that face," Wren murmured beside her. He wore the grey hood of a lay-brother, which should have been absurd on a man built like a drawn bow, but he'd managed to arrange himself into an expression of such profound monastic boredom that two guards had already looked straight through him.

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face you made when you found out the prophecy book had a sequel."

"That was a *reasonable* face." She kept her eyes forward, hands folded, the picture of a humble ink-worker come to witness the Feast of Binding. "And for your information, I'm cataloguing. That's what I do. There's a difference between being horrified and taking notes."

"Are you taking notes?"

"I have taken three notes."

Wren exhaled through his nose, which was how he laughed when laughing would blow their cover. It was a sound she'd learned to listen for the way sailors learned weather. Three weeks in the Thornwood, one botched ambush by hollowed knights, one rewritten ward-sigil performed mid-sprint with her heart hammering like a temple bell, and somewhere in all of it she had stopped thinking of him as *the wolf* and started thinking of him as *Wren*, which was a far more dangerous category of person to think about.

Ahead of them, the pilgrim column wound up the Processional Way toward the palace square. Hundreds of them — scribes from every province, summoned by royal decree to attend the Feast of Binding, where King Aldric would renew his coronation oath before the assembled orders of the quill. Royal decree. Sable had read the summons twice and both times felt the words reach into her chest and tap on something.

*All bound scribes are bidden to attend.*

Bound scribes. Debt-magic. The crown wasn't inviting pilgrims. It was inviting *her kind*.

She hadn't told Wren yet. She'd tell him tonight, or she'd tell him never, depending on what she found.

* * *

The pilgrims' quarters were a converted granary with straw pallets, one communal washbasin, and an acoustics problem: you could hear everything. Which meant that by sundown Sable knew more about the provincial scribes of Vael than any archivist alive — who owed whom, who resented it, who had a cough, who snored like a landslide (Brother Odo of Marrow Fen; she'd begun keeping a tally), and who was quietly terrified of tomorrow's ceremony.

"You're new," said the girl on the next pallet. She was maybe sixteen, round-faced, with ink stains to the elbow and the defiant posture of someone who'd been yelled at recently and often. "I'm Tansy. Temple of Little Harrow. Third-year binding."

"Sable. Corren Hollow."

Tansy's eyes went wide as communion cups. "The Hollow? But — everyone says there's no magic there at all. How do you have a binding?"

*Carefully,* Sable thought. *Very carefully.* "It's a quiet valley," she said aloud. "Quiet bindings."

"Oh." Tansy absorbed this with the magnificent ease of the young, then leaned in, dropping her voice to a conspiratorial whisper. "Have you heard what happens at the Feast? My mistress went twenty years ago, before I was bound. She won't talk about it except to say—" Tansy glanced around the granary, lowered her voice further, "—she says the king doesn't renew the oath. The *crown* does. And the crown takes something in exchange. A tithe."

"A tithe of what?"

"That's the part she wouldn't say." Tansy hugged her knees. "She just held my hand really hard and told me that if they ever call me up to the dais, I should think about my mother's kitchen. Nothing else. Just the kitchen. As hard as I can."

Sable lay back on her pallet and stared at the rafters, where swallows' nests crowded the beams like soot-colored thoughts.

Think of something small and beloved, and hold onto it while something enormous fed.

She thought about Wren, three rows away, pretending to sleep with his breathing arranged into careful, counterfeit slowness. She thought about how he'd stood between her and the hollowed knights without hesitation, and how his death was written on the final pages of a book she'd torn, and how tearing it had changed nothing except the shape of her nightmares.

Then she thought about the dais, and the tithe, and she did not sleep at all.

* * *

The Feast of Binding began at moonrise, because of course it did. Everything in this kingdom seemed to prefer the dark.

Sable had expected to be kept in the pilgrims' section, far from the throne, among the hundreds. Instead, at the granary door, a chamberlain in ash-grey livery consulted a ledger — an actual ledger, and some deep scribe instinct in her itched to correct its marginalia — and read names. Provincial scribes with heavy bindings were called forward, row by row, and escorted up the Processional Way toward the palace square, where ten thousand candles burned in honor of a hundred years of peace.

"Sable of Corren Hollow," the chamberlain read, and did not look up.

Her stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, and kept going.

Beside her, Wren shifted — a tiny motion, hood tilting, wolf-instinct rising under the monk's disguise like water under ice. She caught his sleeve. *Not here.* His jaw worked. He let go.

They climbed together in the stream of chosen scribes, past terraces hung with white banners, past courtiers in their feast-finest, past musicians playing something slow and sweet that sounded, if you listened wrong, like a dirge learning to dance. And all the way up, Sable watched the crowd and took her fourth note:

*Everyone is smiling too hard.*

It wasn't festivity. It was effort. Courtiers smiled the way people hold a plank — strain dressed as grace. A duchess laughed at a joke and the laugh went on two beats too long, ringing hollow as a dropped pot. An old lord wept quietly into his wine and those around him pretended not to notice, or perhaps genuinely didn't. And everywhere, everywhere, the same small gesture: a hand drifting up to touch the temple, the brow, the space behind the eyes, the way you might touch a bruise you couldn't remember earning.

"They're missing something," Wren breathed. He'd drifted close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers, two monks sharing devotions. "Look at their eyes. It's like — like rooms after a theft. Nothing gone that you could name. Just rearranged."

"I know." Her mouth was dry. "I've seen that look before."

"Where?"

"In a mirror. The morning after I first touched the prophecy book." She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now with terrible clarity. *The crown had already reached across a hundred miles and a sealed valley to taste her.* It had known what she was before she did.

The square opened around them, vast and candlelit, and there was the palace — a mountain of pale stone crowned with balconies — and before it, the dais.

King Aldric sat upon it.

Even at fifty yards, even through the shimmer of torchlight, Sable saw what the crowd was trained not to see. The king was young still — thirty at most — and handsome in the way of portraits, all clean lines and ceremonial stillness. But he sat *wrong*. Not slumped; worse than slumped. Arranged. Propped the way a puppeteer props a marionette between scenes, dignity maintained by geometry rather than will. And his shadow —

His shadow moved before he did. A half-second early, always. When a herald stepped forward, the king's shadow turned its head first.

"There," Wren said, barely a sound. "You see it."

"I see it."

"And you still want to walk toward it."

"Wren." She kept her voice light, her eyes forward, two pilgrims chatting. "I walked *through a book* to get here. Walking toward things is the only skill I have."

* * *

The ceremony itself was beautiful, which was the cruelest thing about it.

A hundred voices rose in the Binding Hymn, old and low, and the words were ones Sable knew from her own temple — the same liturgy, the same cadences, sung in Corren Hollow over debt-contracts and tithes and the slow arithmetic of servitude. Hearing it here, in a capital square full of candlelight and stolen smiles, she felt the hymn reorganize itself in her understanding. It had never been about devotion. It had been about *anchoring*. All of it. Every verse a nail.

The high archivist called the tithe.

One by one, the chosen scribes ascended the dais steps, knelt before the king, and bowed their heads. And one by one, Sable watched the crown take from them.

There was no flash of light. No thunder. That was the horror of it — the *domesticity*. Each scribe knelt, the crown leaned — the crown *leaned*, a motion like a bird bending to drink — and the scribe's face went briefly, terribly blank. Not unconscious. Empty. A page wiped clean. Then they rose, smiling, and descended, and the smile stayed on their faces afterward like paint that wouldn't dry right.

A woman ahead of Sable came down clutching a small carved bird. She stared at it, turning it over and over. "Someone gave me this," she said to no one, delighted. "Isn't it lovely? I can't remember who. Isn't that funny?"

Nobody laughed. Everybody smiled.

Sable's hands had gone cold. Beside her, Wren had gone very still in the way that preceded either flight or violence, and she slid her fingers around his wrist — pulse jumping under her grip like a trapped moth — and held on.

"Not yet," she whispered. "Watch the king. Watch what it *does* with what it takes."

He watched. So did she.

Each time the crown drank, King Aldric's eyes cleared. Just slightly. Just for a moment. The arrangement fell out of his posture; his shoulders drew; his gaze sharpened and swept the square with something that looked agonizingly like *himself* — a man surfacing from deep water, seeing the sun, knowing he was about to be pulled back down. Once, only once, his eyes found the line of kneeling scribes and moved along it, searching, until they snagged on her.

He knew her. Or the thing wearing him knew what she was. With Aldric, at this point, Sable suspected the distinction had collapsed entirely.

His lips moved. No sound reached her. But she was a scribe, trained since childhood to read mouths across a scriptorium, and she caught it anyway, three silent words shaped with desperate precision:

*Don't kneel.*

Then the crown drank again, and he was gone behind his own face, and the herald called the next name.

"Tansy of Little Harrow."

The girl went up the steps pale as paper, and Sable remembered — *think about your mother's kitchen* — and saw Tansy's lips moving as she knelt, saw her fists clench, saw the crown bend to drink and find... resistance. Small, stubborn, sixteen-year-old resistance. The crown paused. Recoiled a fraction, like a tongue touching something bitter. It took less from her — a sip instead of a draught — and Tansy came down the stairs crying without knowing why, holding her head, saying "I'm fine, I'm fine, why am I crying?"

But she still had her kitchen. Whatever else the night took from her, she still had that.

*Hold onto something small,* Sable thought, *and it can't take the whole of you.*

She looked at Wren. He was looking at the king. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the whole candlelit square wasn't already saying in a hundred stolen memories: *this is what a hundred years of peace costs, and the bill comes due tonight, and it has been coming due every night for a century.*

Grim clarity settled over her like a well-tailored coat. Not courage exactly. Something colder and more useful. She had spent her whole life copying other people's debts into ledgers, believing the numbers were fixed, believing the contract was the contract. Now she stood in the middle of the largest debt in the kingdom — a hundred years of payments, extracted nightly, from a king and a court and a country — and she knew, with a scribe's certainty, that debts could be *rewritten*. She'd proven it in the Thornwood with a ward-sigil and shaking hands.

The crown was a contract. Every contract had terms. Every term had been written by someone.

Which meant it could be unwritten by someone else.

"Tonight," she said to Wren, so quietly it was mostly breath. "While it's feeding. While it's *distracted*. We find the archive."

"The archive's inside the palace. Inside the—"

"I know where it is. I know what's in it." The original Binding Accord. The first contract. The root text. If the crown was anchored by a hundred years of hymns and tithes, then the anchor chain was forged somewhere, signed somewhere, and filed — because everything was filed, that was the one law of the universe Sable had never once seen broken. "And I know how to read it."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Torchlight moved in his eyes, and under the monk's hood, under the duty and the grief and the leash he hated, something fierce and glad flickered awake.

"You know," he said, "most people, when they see a monster eat someone's childhood, decide to leave."

"I'm not most people. I'm a scribe." She was already moving, drawing him with her into the shadow of the colonnade, away from the hymn, away from the dais, away from the king whose silent warning rang in her ears — *don't kneel* — as though she ever would. "We file complaints in person."

They slipped along the colonnade's edge, two grey figures in a river of celebration, and behind them the crown bent to drink again, and again, patient as winter, hungry as a hundred years.

Neither of them saw the queen-regent turn her head and follow them with eyes that were not hers.

Neither of them heard her whisper, in a voice like dust settling on a grave:

"*Hurry.*"
