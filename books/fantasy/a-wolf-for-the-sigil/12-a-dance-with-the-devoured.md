## 12. A Dance with the Devoured

The Feast of Binding smelled of roast boar, spilled wine, and something underneath it all — a faint sweetness like fruit going soft in a cellar. Wren had been smelling that sweetness since they'd passed the city gates three days ago, and he was beginning to suspect it wasn't the food.

It was the crown.

He stood at the edge of the great hall with a pilgrim's hood shadowing his face and a pilgrim's staff that had never seen a shrine, watching six hundred people celebrate their own slow devouring. The courtiers laughed. The musicians played. Somewhere above them all, on the dais beneath the banners of Vael, King Aldric sat at his high table like a man carved from tallow left too near a fire — still shaped like a king, but softening.

*Don't stare,* Sable had told him before they'd split up among the pilgrim scribes. *Staring is how people get noticed. Noticing is how we get caught.*

*I'm a wolf,* he'd said. *Noticing is what I do.*

*Then notice quietly.*

He was noticing quietly now. He was also holding a cup of wine he hadn't drunk from, because the last time he'd accepted hospitality in this palace, the hospitality had come with an oath chained to his throat. He'd learned his lesson about cups.

"You're not drinking."

Wren turned. The speaker was a young courtier in green silk, cheeks already flushed, swaying slightly to music that hadn't started yet. "I'm on duty," Wren said.

"On duty? At the Feast?" The courtier leaned in, conspiratorial, breath thick with honeyed wine. "No one works tonight. Tonight the crown *keeps* us." He said it the way other men might say *tonight the gods bless us*, with a smile that didn't quite reach anywhere useful.

"Then I'm keeping myself," Wren said, and moved away before the man could decide whether to be offended.

The hall was built for awe: vaulted ceiling painted with the hundred-year history of Vael's crown, pillars wrapped in silver leaf, chandeliers dripping light like fat off a spit. Every surface celebrated the same thing — the Binding, the ancient rite by which the first Aldric had taken up the crown and saved the kingdom from the Long Dark. That was the story on the ceiling, anyway. A hero-king lifting a circlet of iron and starlight while shadows fled before him.

Nobody had painted what came after. Nobody ever did.

Wren drifted along the wall, cataloguing exits out of habit. Three main doors, two servant passages behind the tapestries — he could smell the draft through them — and one narrow stair up to the minstrels' gallery where a guard stood more asleep than awake. Sable was somewhere across the hall in her borrowed scribe's robes, doing whatever it was scribes did at feasts (he suspected: standing near walls, memorizing everything, judging everyone silently). Their plan was simple, which meant it would probably fail. Get close to the crown. Learn how it fed. Find the seam.

What he had not planned — what no plan could have survived contact with — was the queen-regent deciding he looked lonely.

"Wolf."

The word reached him before she did, low and amused, and every hair on the back of his neck stood up. Not because of the tone. Because of the word itself. She'd said it softly, under the music, pitched for his ears alone — and there was only one person in this hall who knew what he was, and she was supposed to be across the room pretending to copy down seating arrangements.

He turned. Queen Maerlin swept toward him through the crowd, and the crowd parted for her the way water parts around a keel. She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, all sharp lines and pale gold, wearing a gown the color of frozen rivers and a circlet of silver that sat above her brow like a second, smaller crown — a crown's herald, a crown's promise. She was perhaps fifty years old and carried it like thirty, and her eyes were gray as slate and just as warm.

She stopped in front of him. Smiled.

"Dance with me," she said.

Every instinct Wren owned screamed at once, a full chorus. *This is the woman who runs the regency. This is the woman whose guards hollowed knights in the Thornwood. This is the woman who has been feeding the court to the crown one memory at a time.* And underneath all of that, quieter but older, the wolf's voice: *she smells wrong.*

Because she did. Under the perfume and the wine and the roasted meat, Queen Maerlin smelled like nothing. Like a room long shut. Like a body that had been emptied and something else poured in, and the pourer hadn't quite gotten the proportions right.

"I don't dance," Wren said.

"Everyone dances at the Feast of Binding." Her smile widened a fraction. "It's tradition. And you wouldn't want to insult tradition, would you... Master Corvin?" His false name, delivered perfectly. Of course she knew it. She probably knew the names of every pilgrim in the hall, because knowing things was how you kept a kingdom, and keeping kingdoms was apparently what she did instead of sleeping.

Around them, the music shifted into a slower measure, and couples began moving onto the floor, and Wren understood with sinking certainty that refusing the queen-regent publicly would end their infiltration approximately forty minutes after it began.

"One dance," he said.

"How generous," said the queen-regent, and took his hand.

Her fingers were cold. Not cool, not elegant-and-chilled like some noblewoman affecting delicacy. Cold, like river stones in thaw-month, like the underside of a grave marker. He masked the flinch by bowing over her hand, and when he straightened, she was looking at him with those slate eyes and something flickered behind them — quick, drowned, gone.

They joined the dance.

Wren was, in fact, a passable dancer. Pack gatherings in the old den had involved music more often than not, and Rill had drilled him mercilessly on the grounds that a shifter who couldn't waltz would embarrass the bloodline. He'd resented it then. He resented it less now, though he suspected even Rill would draw the line at dancing with whatever this was.

The queen-regent moved beautifully. Too beautifully. There was no weight to her, no human stumble, no small corrections. She flowed through the turns like smoke given a gown, and when the dance brought them close, close enough that he could see the fine powder dusting her throat, he saw what the powder was hiding.

A thin line at her jaw. Old. Healed wrong. The kind of mark a body gets when it's been broken and put back together by someone who wasn't a healer.

"You're staring," she murmured.

"Habit," Wren said. "I'm told it's rude."

"It's honest. I find honesty refreshing at these affairs. Everyone here lies so beautifully." She turned under his arm, silk whispering. "Tell me, Master Corvin. What brings a pilgrim scribe so far from his valley?"

"The usual. Devotion."

"Mm. Your devotion smells of pine and wet fur."

His heart stopped, restarted badly. Her expression didn't change; she was smiling at him exactly as she'd smiled at the courtier in green, exactly as she'd smiled at everyone, a fixed pleasant mask — and yet the words kept coming, quiet, under the music, each one placed like a knife between ribs.

"Don't worry. No one else can hear me. I've gotten very good at speaking quietly. It's one of the few skills they leave you." Another turn. Her cold hand tightened fractionally in his. "You came here with the little debt-scribe. The one who reads ahead. She's marked now, you know. Marked things are easy to find. You should both have stayed in your hole in the world."

"If you know who we are," Wren said carefully, "why am I still dancing?"

"Because I asked you to." For half a heartbeat, the fixed smile cracked — not into menace but into something worse, something pleading — and then sealed over again. "And because I have so few chances to ask for anything anymore."

The music swelled. They turned, and turned, and Wren let himself look at her properly, wolf-slow, taking in details he'd have missed a year ago. The way she never blinked quite in time with her words. The way her breathing ran shallow and even regardless of exertion or emotion, like a bellows worked by someone else. The way the courtiers nearest her leaned subtly toward her as she passed, faces slackening into contentment, the way flowers lean toward a lamp — except lamps don't feed.

She saw him seeing. And this time the crack in the smile stayed open.

"She's not here," the queen-regent said, and her voice had changed — lower, rougher, fraying at the edges like rope dragged over stone. "Maerlin. The woman whose face this is. She's been gone ninety years. I wear her. I've worn her since I wore the girl before her, and the girl before *that*—" A spasm crossed the beautiful features, quickly mastered. "Listen to me. Listen. You have to—"

"Your Majesty!" A steward materialized at the edge of the floor, bowing. "The toast. They wait on you."

The queen-regent's head turned toward him with a smoothness that made Wren's skin crawl, and when she spoke again her voice was silk once more, perfect, pleasant, empty. "Of course. One moment." The steward bowed again and withdrew, satisfied, and Wren understood that whatever the steward had seen, it had been exactly what he expected to see: his queen, pausing mid-dance, gracious as always.

She turned back to Wren. Up close, in the chandelier light, he could see that her eyes weren't gray at all. Gray was a color. These were the color of ash after the fire's gone out, and deep in them, impossibly far down, something moved. Something pressed against the inside of her gaze like a face against frosted glass.

"*End it,*" she whispered.

The music played on. Six hundred people drank and laughed and danced themselves deeper into the crown's ledger, and the queen-regent of Vael gripped Wren's hand with fingers gone suddenly desperate-strong, and begged him in fragments.

"*End it. Please. It uses my mouth. It uses my hands. Ninety years, I've watched it eat them, course by course, and it keeps me to do the serving — I remember my daughters, I remember my garden, I remember being afraid of spiders, and it takes them one at a time to keep its anchor, and I can feel them going, I can feel myself going, and I can't — I can't even scream, it smiles with my mouth when I try—*" Her breath hitched, a human sound, horribly human. "*You're sworn to it. I can taste the oath on you, wolf. Use it. Oaths are doors. Break the door.*"

Wren's throat had gone dry as August. "I don't know how," he said, barely a breath.

"*Find out.*" The desperation banked, controlled, banked again — he could feel her fighting for every word, spending strength she must have hoarded for decades. "*There's a seam. Everything bound has a seam. The first king knew — he wrote it down — he hid it — the archive, the black cabinet, the book with the—*"

"Your Majesty." The steward again, closer now, patient the way only the very powerful or the very frightened are patient. "The toast."

"*Go,*" she breathed, and the ash-eyes shuttered, and Queen Maerlin smiled her fixed pleasant smile and released his hand.

Wren bowed — deep, correct, everything a loyal subject owed — and stepped back into the crowd, and the crowd closed around him, and the queen-regent glided toward the dais to raise her glass to the crown that was eating her alive.

He stood in the press of bodies with his heart slamming and his mind racing, replaying it. *The archive. The black cabinet. The book with the—* With the what? She'd been cut off. Cut off on purpose, he realized — the steward hadn't interrupted by chance. The crown had felt her slipping and sent its leash.

Across the hall, he found Sable by instinct rather than sight — the particular stillness of her, the way she held her head when she was listening to a room. She'd seen the dance. Of course she had. Her eyes met his through the crowd, and her eyebrows lifted in a question.

Wren touched two fingers to his chest — *alive* — then let his gaze flick, deliberately, toward the tall doors at the hall's northern end. Beyond them lay the corridor to the royal archive. He'd scouted it yesterday, telling himself it was thoroughness. Now it was a map someone had drawn for him without knowing.

Sable's chin dipped, almost imperceptibly. Then she turned to the pilgrim beside her and began, with the serene confidence of a lifelong scribe, to complain about ink quality — the perfect cover for two people drifting toward the same door.

Wren took one step, then another, letting the current of the feast carry him northward. Behind him, the toast was raised. Six hundred voices cried *"To the Binding!"* and the sound rolled through the vaulted hall like a wave, and above it all, clear and sweet and utterly wrong, the queen-regent's laughter rang out — perfect, pleasant, and if you had ears like his, you could hear the screaming underneath it.

*The black cabinet,* he thought. *The book with the—*

Whatever it was, the crown had killed the sentence halfway. Which meant it mattered.

Which meant, Wren thought grimly, shrugging deeper into his pilgrim's hood and following the scribe who was following him, that they had maybe until dawn to steal a secret a hundred years dead — from the one room in this palace the crown guarded like a wound.

* * *

They found the corridor to the archive empty, which was the first bad sign.

They found the archive door unlocked, which was the second.

And they found, waiting in the dark between the shelves, sitting calmly in a chair as though she'd been expecting them for hours, a small elderly woman in temple-gray robes, with Sable's own face grown old — and a voice like dry paper when she said:

"Took you long enough. Sit down, child. We need to talk about what my sister wrote in the margins."
