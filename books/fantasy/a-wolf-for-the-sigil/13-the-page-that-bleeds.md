## 13. The Page That Bleeds

The royal archive smelled of dust and candle tallow and, faintly, of old lies.

Sable had been in archives her whole life — the temple's scriptorium at Corren Hollow had been her whole world once, all cedar shelves and beeswax and the soft rasp of quill on vellum — and she knew the way rooms like this breathed. This one breathed wrong. It was too quiet, the kind of quiet that was being *maintained*, the way a courtier maintained a smile. The books here were arranged not by subject or age but by usefulness to whoever had last curated them, and someone had curated them recently, and recently was the problem. A hundred years of inconvenient history had been shelved somewhere out of reach, and Sable intended to find it before the Feast of Binding reached its crescendo and the crown found something new to eat.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Wren murmured from the doorway, where he stood with the particular stillness of a man pretending to be furniture. He'd borrowed a pilgrim scribe's grey robe for the infiltration, and it fit him badly across the shoulders, which was the only reason she could tell him apart from actual furniture. "Besides the obvious answer, which is that you can't help yourself."

"The obvious answer is sufficient," Sable whispered back, running her fingers along a shelf of tax ledgers. Boring. Deliberately boring. "But if you want the strategic answer: the prophecy book came *from* somewhere. Books don't walk."

"This one did," Wren said. "It walked you into a kingdom mid-coronation. I have opinions about that book."

"You have opinions about everything. It's one of your best qualities." She pulled a ledger free, fanned it, slid it back. Nothing. "The archivist said the crown's people purged the archive after the last unbinding attempt. But purging isn't destroying, not for scribes. You don't burn dangerous books. You *hide* them. You misfile them so thoroughly that even you forget where they are." She tapped her temple. "I spent nine years cataloguing a temple that owned my debt. Misfiling is a love language, Wren. I'm fluent."

He crossed the room without seeming to cross it — he never made noise when he didn't want to, which she suspected was most of the time — and looked over her shoulder at the shelf. This close, she could feel the warmth of him through two layers of stolen robe, and she sternly informed her heart that now was not the moment. Her heart, as usual, filed her instruction under things to consider later.

"And what does fluency get us?" he asked.

"A book that's warm to the touch," Sable said. "In a cold room."

They worked the shelves together after that, and it was almost pleasant, in the way that burglary is almost pleasant when you're doing it with someone whose breathing you've learned to read. Wren took the high rows; she took the low ones. He passed her volumes down with exaggerated care, like they were sleeping animals, and she caught herself smiling at the absurdity of it: two fugitives, disguised as pilgrims, looting a royal archive during the holiest week of the Binding calendar, whispering jokes about filing systems.

Then her fingers found a spine that wasn't cold.

It was wedged behind a run of agricultural almanacs — *On the Husbandry of Northern Goats*, forty years of them, the single most effective hiding place she had ever seen, because no one in the history of reading had ever voluntarily touched a goat almanac. The spine beneath them was plain black leather, unmarked, and warm the way a hand is warm. Not sun-warm. *Alive*-warm.

"Wren."

He was beside her instantly. He didn't touch the book. He'd learned that much about it, and about her, in the weeks since the Thornwood: when Sable went still in a particular way, you waited.

"That's it," he said quietly. Not a question.

"It's been waiting for me." She worked it free of the goat almanacs. The leather sighed under her hands, and somewhere in the room, every candle flame leaned toward her at once, like flowers toward sun, and then remembered itself and stood straight again. "Or it's been waiting for *this*. The Feast. Whatever the crown is doing tonight downstairs, the book knows. Books always know first. That's the whole trouble with books."

"You could put it back," Wren said. She heard what it cost him to say it — heard the offer underneath it, the same offer he'd made on the road, twice, in different words: *we could still run, we could still go, I would take you.* "Goat almanacs are very restful."

Sable laughed despite herself, a small cracked sound in the hush. "One day I'm going to write an entire treatise on northern goats just to punish you for that joke."

"One day," he said, "I'm going to hold you to it."

And there it was again, that fault line under their feet, the thing neither of them would name because naming it would make it a debt, and debts were the only magic either of them truly feared. She opened the book instead.

* * *

The pages were the same as she remembered: heavy, cream-colored, inked in a hand that shifted between centuries like a voice changing speakers. Prophecies in columns. Marginal notes in three dead languages and one living one. And dates — that was the cruelty of it, the dates, each entry stamped with the night it described, so that reading it felt less like divination and more like a diary written by time itself.

She turned past the coronation. Past the Thornwood. Past entries she recognized with a lurch, nights she had lived, rendered in flat prophetic prose that somehow made even Wren's laughter sound ominous. (*The wolf runs ahead; the scribe follows; the wood pretends not to watch.*)

Past the Feast of Binding.

Her hand stopped.

The next page was dated tonight.

She should have stopped there. Some part of her, the well-trained scribe part, the part that had spent nine years learning that some ledgers are closed for a reason, raised its hand politely and suggested they close the book, re-shelve it behind the goats, and go steal something safer, like the crown jewels.

But another part of her — the part that had torn loose from Corren Hollow through a page of ink, the part that had rewritten a ward-sigil mid-battle while hollowed knights closed in, the part that had looked at a hundred years of prophecy and thought *I am a scribe, and this is just text, and text obeys* — that part turned the page.

The entry was short. Prophecy is economical; it has no need of adjectives. It said:

*The wolf stands between the blade and the scribe, and does not step aside.*

*The scribe lives.*

*The wolf does not.*

*And the crown, finding the scribe's blood acceptable, sets its sigil upon her brow, and she reigns a hundred years, and the valley forgets her name, and she forgets it first.*

Below the words was an illumination, painted in colors that hadn't faded because they'd never been entirely in this world. It showed a great hall — she knew it, she'd seen it from the outside, the throne room of Vael with its windows like spear-blades — and in it, a figure lay crumpled on the steps of the dais. Grey-robed. Dark-haired. One arm flung out, as if reaching for something just beyond the frame.

Wren's face. Rendered with terrible tenderness, the artist had given him his face in the moment of letting go — eyes half-closed, mouth soft, the expression of a man who has decided something and is glad of it.

And above him, ascending the dais, crowned in black fire, wearing robes that pooled like spilled ink, was a woman with Sable's face.

Not similar. Not suggestive. *Hers* — the small scar on her chin from falling out of the pear tree at eleven, the way her left eyebrow arched higher than the right when she was afraid and trying not to be. The painted woman wore the crown the way a wound wears a bandage, and her painted eyes were dry, and that was the worst detail of all, because Sable knew — she knew with the certainty of her own heartbeat — that the woman in the picture had cried until there was nothing left, and then the crown had drunk that too.

"Sable." Wren's voice came from very far away. "You've gone white. What does it say?"

She couldn't answer. Her throat had closed around something large and jagged. The candles were leaning again, all of them, straining toward the open page like they wanted to read it too, and the warmth of the book had become heat, and the heat had become something like hunger, and she understood suddenly, with a scholar's horrible clarity, why the crown's people hadn't destroyed this book.

They couldn't. It was load-bearing. Tear down a prophecy and the future it describes comes down with it — unless the future finds another way to happen. The book wasn't a record. It was a *rail*, and the world ran along it, and everyone she loved was strapped to the track.

Including the man standing next to her, asking her, gently, what was wrong.

"*Nothing,*" she said, and her voice came out steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. Nine years of copying ledgers had taught her exactly one useful form of lying: the flat, clerical, nothing-to-see-here tone. She deployed it now like a weapon. "A list of names. Feast attendees. It's nothing."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. He had eyes the color of winter oak, and they were not fooled, and she watched him decide — deliberately, visibly, the way he decided everything — to let her lie. Because he trusted her. Because he had sworn himself, page by page, chapter by chapter, to a woman who was currently lying to his face to keep him from knowing the date of his own death.

*The wolf stands between the blade and the scribe, and does not step aside.*

She could feel the words sitting in her chest like swallowed stones.

"Put it away," Wren said softly. "Whatever it says. We'll deal with it tomorrow. Tonight we have a feast to infiltrate and a king to save, and I'd rather do both without you looking like you've seen a ghost."

"I haven't seen a ghost," Sable said. "Ghosts are past tense."

She smiled at him. He smiled back, tired and crooked and real, and something in her chest tore cleanly down the middle, and she understood that she was going to do a terrible thing.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about hubris: it doesn't feel like pride. It feels like love.

Sable had spent her whole life believing that knowledge was safety. In Corren Hollow, ignorance was the currency of the powerless — the temple kept the ledgers, the temple kept the truth, and the debt-bound kept their heads down and their questions small. Freedom, she had always believed, would look like *knowing*. Knowing the terms. Knowing the trap. Knowing the ending in advance, so that no one could ever again hold it over her.

So when Wren drifted to the far window to check the courtyard below — because he always checked, because vigilance was the leash he'd traded for the one he hated — Sable stayed at the lectern with the book open to tonight's page, and she did the thing that the well-trained scribe in her had begged her not to do.

She read it again. Slowly. Like a scholar.

And then she reached for the tools of her trade.

Her kit was still in her sleeve where it had been since the Thornwood — pen knife, bone folder, a stub of red sealing wax, the little pot of ink she'd refilled from the archivist's stores. Ordinary tools. Except that in Vael, in her hands, ordinary tools had stopped being ordinary the night she'd rewritten a ward-sigil while knights tried to kill her. Her debt-magic worked here. Her *craft* worked here. Words obeyed her, if she paid for them.

*I am a scribe,* she thought, staring down at the painted hall, at the crumpled grey figure, at her own crowned face. *This is just text. Text obeys.*

The plan assembled itself in her mind with the smooth, seductive logic of all catastrophic plans. If the page was a rail, she would bend it. If the prophecy demanded a wolf's death, she would strike the clause. Scribes amended documents all the time — errata, corrections, marginal revisions. She had spent nine years watching her temple masters alter contracts to favor themselves. Why should the future be any different? Why should *this* document, of all documents, be held sacred?

Because it was written in a century-old hand?

She had better handwriting than that.

She picked up the pen knife.

The page seemed to lean into the blade. The candles all leaned with it. Somewhere below, muffled by floors of stone, the Feast of Binding was beginning — music, voices, the low collective hum of a court feeding itself to its king — and Sable told herself that whatever happened in this room was a mercy compared to what was happening down there.

"Forgive me," she whispered. Not to the book. To the boy in the pear tree, the girl in the scriptorium, the woman she'd been a month ago who still believed debts could be paid honestly. To Wren, who was ten feet away and would never forgive her for this, and whom she was doing it for anyway, which was perhaps the definition of the whole cursed situation.

She set the blade against the top of the page.

And the page bled.

Not ink. Blood — thin and dark and shockingly warm, welling up along the cut line before the blade had gone deeper than a hair's width, running down the painted hall, pooling in the painted Wren's open palm as though he were catching it. The illumination began to move. The crowned version of herself turned her head — slowly, with the weight of a hundred years — and looked up out of the page at Sable, and her dry painted eyes said, without words, in a voice like a ledger closing:

*You cannot cut yourself out of me. I am what you buy.*

The world flinched.

There is no other word for it. Every candle in the archive guttered at once; the shelves shuddered like a struck bell; the dust on the floor jumped a half-inch into the air and hung there, confused. For one impossible instant Sable saw the room *twice* — once as it was, and once as it would be if the page remained whole: the same room, but older, darker, the windows fogged with a century of ash, and in the second version she was sitting on the throne with the crown eating her memories one by one, and she was smiling, because she had forgotten there was anything to grieve.

Then both versions collapsed into one, and the present won, barely, the way a man wins a fight against the tide.

The book screamed.

It was not a loud scream. It was worse than loud — it was *textual*, a shriek of language, every line of prophecy on every page rewriting itself at once in frantic scrawls, dates dissolving, certainties smearing, a hundred years of fixed future thrashing like a netted fish. The warmth became fever. The leather bucked under her hands.

Footsteps, somewhere above. Then below. Then everywhere — the whole palace waking to a sound only half of it could hear, guards stirring, warders lighting lamps, and beneath it all, rising from the direction of the feast hall, a note of the crown's attention swinging toward them like a searchlight.

"Sable!" Wren was already moving, knife out, positioning himself between her and the door with the automatic, infuriating, heartbreaking precision of a man standing between a blade and a scribe. "*What did you do?*"

She looked down at the page.

The tear had gone through. Only a few inches, only a slash across the illuminated hall — but through the cut she could see not the next page but *nothing*, a seam of raw white where the future should have been, and from the edges of the tear, the blood was still seeping, and the crowned woman's face was split neatly down the middle, half of her smiling and half of her screaming, and Sable could no longer tell which half was hers.

"I read ahead," she said. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing very far away. "And then I edited."

Wren stared at her — at the bloody knife, at the convulsing book, at the seam of white nothing bleeding futures into the air — and she watched him arrive, with the terrible speed of a man who has survived many bad nights, at the understanding that the world had just come off its rails, and that she had taken it off, and that everything from this moment forward would be improvised.

The doors at the end of the archive began to glow. Ward-light, gold and hungry, tracing sigils she recognized — the crown's sigils, the binding-script, the same hand that had written her contract and her valley and a hundred years of hunger.

"They know where we are," Wren said. Calmly. Almost gently. He rolled his shoulders, and beneath the stolen grey robe something vast and patient stretched and woke. "The book told them."

"Yes," Sable whispered, clutching the screaming thing to her chest, because it was hers, because she'd made it, because you don't abandon your own text no matter what it costs. "Wren — I'm sorry — I only wanted—"

"I know what you wanted." He stepped backward, putting his body squarely between her and the burning doors, and for one unguarded moment he smiled at her — crooked, tired, real, the exact smile from the painting, and she felt the floor drop out of her heart. "Stand behind me, scribe. Let's find out what happens on a page nobody wrote."

The doors came down.
