## 14. Torn Pages Have Teeth

The world flinched.

Wren felt it in his teeth first — a wrongness like a bone set crooked, the whole sky grinding a half-inch out of true. The heretic archivist's shelter was a burrow of stacked books and candle-smoke beneath the roots of a dead oak, and every book in it shuddered at once, spines rattling like startled birds.

Sable stood in the middle of it with torn paper in her fist and blood on her thumb where the page had bitten her.

"You tore it," Wren said. Not a question. He'd watched her do it. He'd *let* her do it, which was worse.

"I tore it," she agreed. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "It said you die on Thursday."

"It's Wednesday."

"Then we have time to fix the rest of it."

He wanted to laugh. It came out as something closer to a growl, which, in his experience, was how most of his laughter came out anyway. "Fix it. You tore a hole in a hundred-year-old prophecy and your plan is to *fix* it."

"I'm a scribe." She pressed her bleeding thumb against her skirt, leaving a rust-colored crescent. "Amendments are what we do."

Old Maren, the archivist, looked up from her desk of mismatched candles and made a sound that might have been a prayer or might have been indigestion. With Maren it had always been difficult to tell. "Child," she said, "when a prophecy bleeds, you don't patch it. You run."

And then the book screamed.

There was no better word for it. The prophecy volume, lying open on Maren's table where Sable had left it, opened its throat and *screamed* — a sound like a hawk and a hinge and a woman mourning all at once, high and thin and carrying, pouring out through the cracks in the earth above them into the night air of the Thornwood.

Wren's ears — his other ears, the ones he couldn't put away — flattened before his human ones even registered the sound. Every wolf instinct he owned stood up at once and pointed north-northeast, toward the capital road.

"They can hear that," he said. "Whatever she sends, they can hear that."

"How far?" Sable asked.

He closed his eyes and listened past the screaming, past the wind, past his own hammering heart. Hooves. Many. Iron shoes on old forest paths, moving fast, fanning out in the particular disciplined spread that meant knights who no longer needed to sleep or eat or doubt. Hollowed. He'd heard them hunt once, years ago, from a ridge while his pack scattered below him like leaves. He had hoped never to hear it again.

"Half a league," he said. "Maybe less. They'll be here before the moon clears the trees."

Maren began blowing out candles with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had been raided by better governments than this one. "Back way's through the root cellar. Mind the third step; it bites."

"Sable." Wren caught her wrist. Her pulse jumped under his fingers — quick, frightened, alive. He held on anyway. "We run. Now. We can outrun iron if we keep to the streams, break scent—"

"No."

The word landed between them like a dropped stone.

"We've been running since the Thornwood," she said. "Since before the Thornwood. Since I fell out of a book into your kingdom, everything we've done has been *away*. And look what it's gotten us. Aldric is still under that crown. Your pack is still dead. And now—" she lifted the torn page, and the edges of it curled and blackened as if remembering fire, "—now reality doesn't know what happens next, so it's making things up as it goes, and I don't think it's very good at improvising."

As if to prove her point, the floorboards rippled.

Not metaphorically. The actual boards, old oak, warped upward in a slow wave like something swimming beneath them, and for one vertiginous moment Wren saw grain-patterns rearrange themselves into letters — half-formed sigils, an alphabet trying to be born — before settling back into ordinary wood with a groan.

"See?" Sable said. "*That.*"

"That could also be an earthquake."

"It's not an earthquake, Wren. Earthquakes don't rhyme."

He opened his mouth to argue and found, to his considerable irritation, nothing to say. She was right. It had rhymed. Something about the boards' groan had carried a rhythm, a couplet almost, and the scholar in her had *noticed*, and the soldier in him wanted very badly to be anywhere else.

Outside, the screaming of the book rose to a new pitch, and beneath it, closer now, came the sound of hollowed knights dismounting.

*

They came up out of the root cellar into the cold and found the clearing already ringed.

Six of them. Wren counted without wanting to: six shapes in gray-lacquered armor, visors down, no breath fogging in the frost despite the cold. Their horses stood behind them with the patience of furniture. At their center, one knight stepped forward, and where a crest should have been there was only a smooth blank plate, polished to a dull shine — the mark of the crown's own hand, its personal hounds.

Wren had a knife, a short sword, and one working plan, which was to put himself between the knights and Sable and be extremely inconvenient about dying.

"Sable," he murmured, "the cellar. Maren knows a way out through—"

"I'm not going in the cellar, Wren."

"You are going in the cellar."

"I'm really not." She stepped up beside him instead of behind him, which was so entirely characteristic that it made his chest ache. She had the prophecy book clutched to her chest, and it was still screaming, though softer now, more like keening. "It screams because of me. I tore its page. If they're going to follow anyone, they follow me, and I'd rather they did it where I can see them."

"That's the worst tactical reasoning I've ever heard."

"Thank you. I learned tactics from a wolf who swears loyalty to a crown he hates, so I had low expectations for my teachers."

Despite everything — the iron ring closing, the frost, the screaming book — he laughed. Actually laughed, a rough bark of a sound, and saw the nearest knight tilt its head at the noise like a bird hearing something unfamiliar. Maybe the hollowed remembered laughter. Maybe it hurt them somewhere under all that lacquer.

The lead knight raised a gauntleted hand. When it spoke, its voice came out flat as slate, three voices speaking in unison slightly out of phase:

"*Return the text. Return the vessel. The crown requires completion.*"

"The vessel," Sable repeated. "That's me, I assume."

"*The crown requires completion.*"

"Yes, you mentioned. I'm going to need you to take 'no' back to your employer, along with the following message." She drew herself up, ink-stained and furious and five feet nothing of temple scribe, and Wren fell a little bit further in love, which was frankly terrible timing. "Tell it the prophecy is *amended*."

The knights moved.

Later, Wren would struggle to describe the fight, because the fight refused to behave. Reality was improvising, and it improvised like a drunk musician — brilliant in flashes, catastrophic in between. The first knight lunged and mid-lunge *skipped*, stuttering sideways as if the world had lost its place in the sentence, ending up ten feet left of where it meant to be and burying its blade in a log. The second knight's sword turned briefly into a flock of starlings. Actual starlings. They wheeled once, confused, and then became a sword again, but the knight hesitated a half-second too long checking its own hand, and Wren's knife found the gap beneath its helm.

No blood. Only a sigh, like a house exhaling, and the armor folded down empty.

"Left!" he shouted, because the third and fourth were coming around the book-shelves' root-maze, moving in the paired pattern he remembered from the purge — one to pin, one to kill. He took the pinner, shoulder-low, shifting as he went, letting the wolf come up through him like cold water, and met the second one's blade with his own teeth—

—and the blade sang.

Not rang. *Sang.* A clear note that went through his skull and rearranged something, and suddenly he wasn't in the clearing anymore, he was in the den, the old den, smoke and his mother's voice and then fire, always the fire, the memory dragged up and played at full volume—

A page tore.

The song cut off. He shook his head, wolf-eared ringing, and saw Sable standing over the fallen knight with a handful of ripped parchment and blood running freely down her chin where she'd bitten her tongue.

"Don't listen to them," she gasped. "They fight with memories. The crown taught them to — Wren, they fight with *memories*, that's what the hollowing is, they're full of stolen—"

The fifth knight hit her.

Wren crossed the clearing faster than he had ever moved in his life, wolf-shaped, and took the knight off her in the air. They went down together in a tangle of armor and fury, and he was dimly aware of doing damage — great tearing damage, the kind his kind were made for — but the knight didn't stop, didn't bleed, didn't fear, just kept its gauntlet locked around Sable's ankle and *pulled*, dragging her toward the tree line even with two hundred pounds of wolf on its back.

She screamed — not in pain. In *scribing*.

"Stop!" she shouted at the world itself, and drove her bleeding thumb against the knight's breastplate, and *wrote*.

One word. He saw it only as a smear of red light, a sigil drawn backwards, hastily, the handwriting of someone amending a contract under duress. But the word was *stop*, and the knight stopped. All of it. Armor, grip, the awful patient machinery underneath. It simply ceased, kneeling in the frost like a penitent, and did not move again.

Silence. The last knight stood at the edge of the clearing, and it looked — Wren would swear this later, looking into those blank black visor-slits — it looked *uncertain*. Its head turned from Sable to the book to the frozen knight to the empty armor he'd left strewn across the snow.

Then it spoke, in that triple-flat voice, and for the first time the voices were not in unison. They argued.

"*Completion is amended. Completion is amended. Report: the text fights back. Report: the text fights—*"

It turned and walked into the dark, unhurried, and the horses followed it like good furniture.

*

Wren shifted back and sat down hard in the snow, because his legs had opinions and none of them were favorable.

Sable knelt beside the frozen knight, breathing in long deliberate pulls, the way she did when she was refusing to fall apart in front of witnesses. The book lay in the snow beside her, quiet at last, its remaining pages fluttering gently like a creature recovering its breath.

"You rewrote a knight," he said.

"I rewrote a knight," she agreed faintly.

"With your blood. In the snow. While being dragged."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"When you say it like *that* it sounds reckless. Any other way it sounds suicidal." He crawled over to her — dignity was for people whose legs worked — and took her hands. Her thumb was a mess. He tore a strip from his sleeve, wrapped it clumsily, tied it off with his teeth because his fingers were shaking worse than hers. "You could have bled out over a piece of armor."

"It was going to drag me into the trees. Trees are where the plot kills people. I've read enough prophecies to know that."

"Sable."

"Wren."

"You could have died."

"So could you." Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "That page said you die on Thursday, and I tore it out, and I'd tear out every page in that book before I let it have you. Do you understand? That's not recklessness. That's *editing*. I am choosing what this story gets to do to us, and I choose — I choose—"

She stopped. The clearing was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, the real starlings — the ordinary ones, the ones that had always been starlings — settled back into the branches.

"Thursday," he said quietly. "You tore out my death."

"And apparently the crown's whole script with it. Did you see them? They couldn't finish a *sentence*." A wet, exhausted, triumphant little laugh escaped her. "Reality doesn't know how this ends anymore. Which means for the first time in a hundred years, nobody does. Not the crown. Not the temple. Not the book."

"Not even us," Wren said.

"Especially not us." She looked up at him, snow in her hair, blood on her chin, eyes bright as new ink. "Isn't it wonderful?"

It was not wonderful. It was terrifying. It was the single most dangerous thing either of them had ever done, and the hunters would regroup, and the crown would send worse than six knights next time, and somewhere in the burning palace a king was drowning inside his own skull while they sat in a snowy clearing playing god with stationery.

But she was laughing. And she had torn out his death with her bare hands.

So Wren did the thing he had sworn never to do again, the thing that had gotten his pack killed, the thing that fatalism had forbidden him for eleven bitter years.

He chose.

"We go to the capital," he said. "Tonight. Before they regroup. Before the crown figures out what you did and sends something that doesn't argue with itself. We stop running, we stop hiding, and we walk straight through the front gate during the Feast of Binding, and we end this."

Sable blinked at him. "That's insane."

"Yes."

"The Feast of Binding will be crawling with hollowed courtiers and the queen-regent and every trap the crown has spent a century building."

"Yes."

"And you want to walk in the *front gate*."

"Front gates," Wren said, hauling himself upright and offering her his hand, "are the one door no one guards, because no one is stupid enough to use them." He grinned, and felt his own ears flick forward, wolfish and wild and lighter than they had been in years. "Fortunately for us, I know someone stupid enough."

She took his hand. She was still laughing when he pulled her up, and the sound of it went out into the improvising, unfinished, unwritten night like a signature on a brand-new page.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the prophecy book lay open in the snow — and where the torn page had been, fresh words were slowly surfacing in the paper, ink rising like something breaching water:

*And so the story, wounded, turned and wrote itself anew.*

And beneath that, smaller, in handwriting neither of theirs:

*They are coming to the Feast. Let them come.*

*She will be waiting.*
