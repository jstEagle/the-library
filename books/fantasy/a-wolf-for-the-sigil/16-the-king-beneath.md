## 16. The King Beneath

The throne room of Vael had been built to make a person feel small. Wren had always assumed that was the point of architecture — kings liked their visitors humbled. But standing in the doorway now, with Sable's breath quick and shallow beside him and the hall stretching out before them like the throat of some enormous patient beast, he understood for the first time that the room had never been designed for visitors at all.

It had been designed for the thing on the throne.

Aldric sat beneath the crown's arching weight, still as carved stone. The Feast of Binding was over — or rather, it had ended badly, in fire and screaming and the queen-regent's stolen body walking out of the great hall trailing memories like severed threads — and the court had fled. What remained was the king, alone on his dais, lit by a hundred candles that guttered in a wind Wren couldn't feel.

His shadow did not match him.

It sprawled up the back wall, too tall, too many-jointed, its head tilted at an angle no living neck would allow. As they watched, it moved — not with Aldric, but independently, slowly, like a hand drumming fingers on a table. Waiting.

"Wren." Sable's voice was barely more than air. "The book said—"

"I know what the book said."

He did. He'd read it over her shoulder in the archivist's shelter, by candlelight gone grey with smoke: *The wolf must speak the words he swore, and unspeak them, or the king is lost.* At the time it had seemed almost mercifully simple. Words. He'd been swearing and unswearing things his whole life; it was practically a hobby for a man with his particular talent for bad decisions.

Standing here, the simplicity curdled. Because the oath he'd sworn to the crown wasn't just words. It was the leash he'd put on himself so the crown would spare his pack — and when the pack died anyway, the oath was all he'd had left of them. Breaking it felt like digging up graves.

Sable touched his arm. Her fingers were ink-stained even now, even here; some things were permanent. "We can still go around," she said, though they both knew there was no around. "There might be another way."

"There isn't," Wren said. "And you'd hate me if I took it."

"I would," she agreed, with such immediate honesty that he almost laughed. That was Sable. She'd promise you the truth even when a kind lie would have served better. It was one of approximately four hundred things he loved about her and refused, for the moment, to count aloud.

"Then stay behind me until it's done."

"Absolutely not," she said. "I'm the anchor. The prophecy's clear. You break the oath, I hold him to himself. If I hide behind your shoulders, he'll drift." She squared her small shoulders and looked at the throne the way she looked at an overdue ledger — with grim professional resolve. "Besides, I've rewritten a ward-sigil mid-battle and torn a page out of destiny itself. A fading king is frankly a step down in difficulty."

"You tore one page out of destiny and half the Thornwood caught fire."

"Yes," she said fondly. "Imagine what I'll do with a whole king."

* *

Crossing the throne room took longer than crossing the Thornwood ever had. There were no obstacles — the hall was bare, flagstones swept clean by terrified servants who had not come back — but the shadow noticed them. Wren felt its attention like a cold finger drawn down his spine, and every step forward made the pressure worse, a slow tightening, as if the room itself were a fist closing.

The crown was the worst part up close. From a distance it was merely a crown — gold, old, ugly in the way of things made heavier than they needed to be. Up close he could see it *move*. Not visibly. More that his eyes kept sliding off it wrong, the way they slid off a page of numbers that didn't add up. It sat on Aldric's brow and it *drank*, and what it drank leaked out of the king's face in other ways: skin gone papery, lips cracked, eyes open and fixed on nothing with the terrible patience of a man who has been shouting in a soundproofed room for years.

"Aldric," Wren said.

Nothing. Not a blink.

"Aldric. Your Majesty." He climbed the three steps to the dais. They felt like three hundred. "It's Wren. Of the Thornwood pack. You gave me this oath when I was nineteen years old and stupid enough to think loyalty was a place to live."

The king's throat moved. A sound came out — dry, small, like wind through a keyhole. It might have been a word. It might have been the last word, worn down to nothing by a hundred years of the crown chewing through every other word he owned.

"He's still in there," Sable breathed. She had come up beside him after all — of course she had; hiding behind anyone's shoulders had never once succeeded in her life — and her hand hovered over Aldric's wrist without touching. "Wren, look at his eyes. There's something moving behind them. Like someone pacing at a window."

"Can you reach him?"

"Not from out here." She bit her lip, thinking, and then her face did the thing it did when she found a ledger error — a small fierce satisfaction entirely inappropriate to the circumstances. "But I brought paper."

She always brought paper. She had brought paper into a burning palace. She knelt on the dais steps, spread the prophecy book open on her knees, and began to write — not in it, beside it, on a loose sheet, sigils flowing off her nib quick and sure. "Debt-magic," she murmured. "Everything owed. Everything given. A story is just a debt between teller and listener, did you know that? Someone owes you the ending. Hold on, Your Majesty. I'm coming to collect."

Light rose off the page, soft and gold, the color of lamplight in a library. Sable pressed her palm flat against Aldric's wrist.

The king screamed.

Not aloud. That was the horror of it — his mouth never opened, his body never moved, but the scream filled the room anyway, everywhere at once, the sound of a man remembering he had been buried alive. The shadow on the wall reared up, all its wrong joints flexing, and the candles bent sideways in a wind that still wasn't there, and Sable held on with both hands now, white-knuckled, saying, "I have you, I have you, I'm here, follow my voice—"

And Aldric's eyes changed. Just slightly. Just enough. The pacing figure behind the window turned toward the glass.

* *

What happened next, Wren watched from one step below, useless, while Sabel of Corren Hollow did the thing she had been born — bred — designed to do.

She talked.

Not magic. Not yet. Just stories, low and steady, the way you'd talk to something wild you didn't want to startle. "Your Majesty, my name is Sable Villalobos, and I'm a scribe, and scribes are keepers of accounts, so let me give you yours. You were crowned thirty-one years ago, young, younger than you wanted to be. Your first act as king was to end the tithe on river villages because you'd seen a flood when you were nine and never forgot the bodies." 

Aldric's cracked lips trembled.

"You kept a garden. Everyone thought it was odd. An herb garden, behind the east wing, where the wall blocked the wind. You went there every morning before council and pulled weeds yourself because — say it with me if you can — *no one else does it properly.*"

A sound. Rough, ruined, but a laugh. A ghost of a laugh, dragged up from somewhere very deep.

"There," Sable whispered. "There you are." And she kept going, faster now, feeding him himself: his chancellor's name, the treaty with the southern isles, the year of the bad winter when he'd opened the granaries against everyone's advice, the small kindnesses, the private jokes, the shape of a life the crown had spent decades eating. Wren stood guard and listened and realized, with a strange ache in his chest, that he was hearing about a good king. A genuinely good one. The crown hadn't chosen Aldric despite his goodness.

It had chosen him *for* it. Goodness was rich soil. Hunger grew best in the generous.

"The garden," Aldric rasped. His voice came out in pieces, rust and gravel. "Does it... still..."

"We'll plant it again," Sable said instantly, lying without hesitation, which Wren noted for later appreciation. "First thing after. I'll hold you to it. That's what I do — I hold people to their debts."

The ghost-laugh again, thinner. "You sound... like my mother."

"She sounds wise and terrifying. I accept the comparison."

The shadow struck.

Wren saw it a half-second before it moved — the whole sprawling wrongness of it gathering like a wave pulling back from shore — and he threw himself between it and Sable, arms out, utterly aware of how little good human arms would do. The cold hit him like deep water, like the bottom of the winter lake he'd swum in as a cub on a dare, pressure and dark and a voice that was not a voice pressing against the inside of his skull:

***MINE. HE IS MINE. SHE WILL BE MINE. ALL DEBTS COME TO ME.***

"No," Wren said, through chattering teeth. "He really, really isn't."

And then he did the thing he had come to do.

* *

An oath, he had learned at nineteen, was a simple structure. You named yourself. You named the power you knelt to. You surrendered something specific: your service, your pack's safety, your life laid at the crown's discretion. Three parts, like a sigil. Sable had explained it to him weeks ago, in the ruins of the den, drawing the shape of it in spilled tea on a tabletop: *Anything bound can be unbound, Wren. You just have to unmake it in order. Backwards.*

So he started at the end.

"I, Wren of the Thornwood pack," he said, and his voice shook, "release my life from the crown's discretion. It was never yours to spend. It was theirs." *Thiers* — the dead pack, the green dens, the names he carried like stones in his pockets. Saying it hurt like pulling a knife out of his own ribs. Above him, the shadow shrieked without sound, and somewhere in the walls of the palace something old groaned.

"Keep going!" Sable shouted. She was grey-faced, both hands locked around the king's wrist, holding the line while the crown fought to swallow Aldric back down. "Two parts left!"

"I, Wren of the Thornwood pack, release my pack's safety from the crown's mercy." His voice broke on *pack*. It had broken every time, all his life, whenever he said that word. "You never had any mercy to give. The bargain was rotten from the start, and I knew it, and I signed anyway, because I was afraid. I release the bargain. I release the fear. What I owed them, I paid. What they owed me—" His throat closed. Sable, kneeling in her own light, looked up at him with eyes that were wet and furious and certain.

"What they owed you," she said gently, "you forgive. Say it."

"What they owed me," Wren said, "I forgive."

The second bond snapped. He felt it go — a hook tearing free somewhere behind his sternum, and with it twenty years of leash-weight he'd stopped noticing, the way a man stops noticing a stone in his boot until it's gone. He nearly staggered. The shadow was smaller now, he realized. Dimmer. Feeding, and finding less to drink.

One part left. The hardest one. The naming.

"I, Wren..." He stopped.

*Say it,* whispered the crown, silk-soft, inside his skull. *Wren of the Thornwood, sworn servant. That is who you are. Without me, what are you? A stray. An exile. Nothing that belongs anywhere.* And gods help him, it found the sore place, the old one, the one that had never healed — *you will be alone.*

"He was never alone," Sable said. She said it to the crown, but she was looking at Wren, and her chin was up, and there was ink on her cheek and fury in her spine and he thought, absurdly, *I am going to survive this, because how could I not, she won't allow it.* "He has a pack. It's smaller than the old one. It has terrible handwriting and excellent instincts and one member so far, but we're recruiting. Say it, Wren. Name yourself."

Wren drew a breath that went all the way down.

"I, Wren," he said, "of the Thornwood pack, of the den under the hill, son of Maera, brother of the fallen, friend of Sable Villalobos — release my name from the crown. My name was never yours. My loyalty was loaned, not given, and the loan is called due, and I am *calling it due.*"

He spat the last word the way his mother had spat curses, and the third bond shattered like ice on a warm morning, and King Aldric screamed — aloud this time, a real scream, a living man's scream — and the crown began, at last, to lift.

No. Not lift. To *pull*, dragging its hooks out of him inch by inch, and the shadow on the wall thrashed and shrank, and Wren lunged up onto the dais and gripped the thing with his bare hands, gold searing cold against his palms, hauling it upward while Sable poured light down the king's arm and Aldric's body arched and the whole throne room rang with a sound like a century ending —

And the crown came free.

* *

For a moment nobody moved. The candles stood upright. The shadow was gone — truly gone, or fled deeper into the palace, and Wren's hands were burned silver where the gold had bitten them, and he found he could not make his fingers let go of the crown, which lay in his grip suddenly ordinary. Just metal. Old, ugly, heavy.

"Drop it," Sable croaked. "Drop it drop it drop it—"

He dropped it. It hit the flagstones with a sound like a coin falling in an empty temple, and rolled, and stopped, and did nothing at all. It looked so small.

On the throne, King Aldric breathed.

It was a ragged, awful, beautiful sound, the breathing of a man surfacing from deep water, and then his eyes focused — actually focused, present, *his* — and traveled from Sable's ink-streaked face to Wren's smoking hands to the crown on the floor, and he said, in a voice like a dry gate opening:

"Well," said the King of Vael. "That took long enough."

And Sable laughed, high and shaky, and Wren sat down abruptly on the dais steps because his legs had unionized and gone on strike, and for one whole minute it was over, it was *over*, they had done it—

Which was when every bell in the palace began to ring at once, and the temperature dropped, and from the direction of the great hall came a sound Wren knew from his worst dreams: the measured, hollow tread of armored feet, hundreds of them, and above it a woman's voice that was not a woman's voice at all, sweet and ancient and starving, calling down the corridor:

*"Give me back what is mine. I know which of you it wants to keep."*

Sable and Wren looked at each other.

"That's us," they said together.
