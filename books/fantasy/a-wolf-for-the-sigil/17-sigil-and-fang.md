## 17. Sigil and Fang

The palace was burning from the inside out.

Not with ordinary fire — I'd seen ordinary fire in the temple kitchens, when Brother Hallow's porridge ambitions exceeded his patience. This fire burned *wrong*. It climbed the tapestries without consuming them, hung in the air like curtains of orange glass, and where it touched the murals of Vael's kings, it ate only their faces.

"The crown's memories," Wren had said, back when we still had breath for talking. "It's burning what it can't keep."

Now we were running through a corridor that had been the Hall of Ancestors an hour ago and was now a throat of smoke and falling embers, and I was holding a book that screamed.

That was not a figure of speech. Since I'd torn page thirteen — since I'd committed the sin every archivist is weaned on warnings against — the prophecy book had developed opinions about our location, and it shared them loudly, in a frequency only things made of hunger could hear. Every hunter in Vael knew exactly where we were. The book told them. It told them *gleefully*.

"Left," Wren said, shoving me sideways as a section of ceiling came down where my head had been. He'd been half-shifted since the feast — ears flat, eyes gone amber, claws where a courtier's hands should be. "Sable. *Left.*"

"I heard you." I hadn't. I'd been reading the book while running, which is exactly as graceful as it sounds, and I went down hard on one knee among the embers. They didn't burn me. That should have been comforting. It wasn't; it meant the fire recognized something in me and was deciding whether I was kindling or kin.

The book lay open across my forearm, its remaining pages fluttering though there was no wind. Page after page of prophecy, and now — because reality was improvising, because torn pages have teeth — new text was writing itself even as I watched, ink blooming up through the vellum like bruises:

*The scribe runs. The wolf holds. The crown is patient.*

"I hate this book," I announced to no one.

"It hates you back," Wren said. "Feelings mutual all around. Move."

* * *

We'd lost the queen-regent somewhere between the feast hall and the west stair, which was like losing a lighthouse. She had been Regent Maerlin for thirty years — or rather, her body had been. What wore it now was older. The crown's first victim, the thing beneath the gold, the hunger that had eaten a hundred years of kings and finally grown tired of eating slowly.

When it had stood up from the throne at the feast, wearing Maerlin's stolen face like a mask two sizes too small, the entire court had frozen mid-toast. And then it had smiled with teeth that did not fit the mouth, and said, in a voice like a ledger being balanced:

*"One debt remains."*

It had looked directly at me when it said it. Of course it had. Everything in this kingdom looked at me like I owed it money. In a sense, I did.

Wren had gotten us out through the servants' corridor — he knew the palace's bones better than its masters did, a legacy of years spent guarding a king he despised, learning every crack in the walls he might someday need to escape through. I'd asked him once if that counted as pessimism or hope. He'd said, "Preparedness," in the tone of a man who refuses to examine himself on principle.

Now the preparedness was saving our lives, and the pessimism was probably justified, because behind us, unhurried as a creditor at month's end, came the queen-regent's body walking through fire that parted for it like courtiers.

"She's not running," I panted. We'd reached a landing above the great gallery, and I risked a look back. Below, amid the burning portraits, the regent strolled. Her gown was untouched by flame. Her head turned slowly, taking in the murals, the banners, the century of Aldric's line rendered in paint — and everywhere she looked, the faces went dark. "Wren, she's not chasing us. She's *reading*."

"She's erasing," Wren corrected grimly. "Every face she takes down is a memory of a true king. She's clearing the way."

"For what?"

He didn't answer, but the book did, ink crawling up its margins: *For the crowning. There must always be a crowning.*

I slammed it shut. It hummed against my palms like a trapped hornet.

* * *

The archive was our destination, though getting there required crossing the Long Gallery, which required a plan, which required the one resource neither of us had left in any quantity: time.

So we improvised. Improvising, it turns out, is what happens when you tear a page out of fate and the world stops knowing what you're supposed to do next. Reality had been guessing at us ever since. Guessing wrong, mostly, in ways that tried to kill us — doors opening into walls, stairs that forgot to end — but occasionally in ways that helped, like the moment a burning curtain had simply decided to be a waterfall instead, drenching three hollowed knights who had cornered us on the east stair.

Wren had put them down efficiently and without pleasure. "Your book is drunk," he'd said, shaking ichor off his claws.

"My book is *rewriting itself around a hole I made*," I'd snapped. "Everything near the tear is improvising. Including us."

"Including them." He'd nodded at the knights. Hollowed men — courtiers whose memories the crown had drunk to stay anchored, leaving bodies that moved like puppets with one string. They didn't get up. For the first time since the Thornwood, the crown's hunters stayed down, because whatever animated them had been written by prophecy, and prophecy, near my torn page, had stopped being sure they existed.

Which gave me an idea. A terrible, scribe-brained, blasphemous idea.

"Wren." I crouched at the mouth of the Long Gallery, the book open on my knees, and pulled the stylus from my hair — temple-issue bone, worn smooth by ten years of other people's ledgers. "The ward-sigils on the archway. Can you see them?"

He squinted. Wolf-eyes saw more than human ones, but sigil-work was invisible to anyone untrained. "Barely. Faint. Why?"

"Because they're protection wards keyed to the royal line, and the royal line currently includes a man buried alive in his own skull and a body-snatching hunger wearing his grandmother's face, which means the wards don't know who to protect anymore." I licked my thumb, pressed it to the stone, and felt the old sigil shiver under my touch like a dog hearing its name. "They're waiting for instructions. So I'm going to give them some."

"You're going to *rewrite the palace's wards*. Mid-battle. While the palace burns."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"It sounds like the Thornwood, except vertical and on fire."

"The Thornwood worked."

"The Thornwood nearly killed us."

"And yet." I was already working, thumb and stylus moving together, striking through the old binding-phrase — *protect the blood of Aldric* — and drafting beneath it, in the quick shorthand the temple used for corrections: *protect those who flee the crown.*

The sigil flared white. The whole archway lit up like dawn, and the Long Gallery beyond it changed — the fire dimming to embers along its length, the smoke parting, a clear road opening through the heart of the burning palace. Not extinguished. *Redirected.* The wards couldn't stop the crown's fire, but they could decide what it was allowed to touch, and I had just told it, in the oldest legal language in Vael, that it was not permitted to touch people running away.

Wren stared at the open road, then at me. Something moved through his expression too fast to name — awe wearing fear's clothing, maybe, or the reverse.

"You're going to get us killed," he said.

"Probably. But interestingly."

"*Interesting* is how you describe tax disputes."

"I contain multitudes." I grabbed his arm. "Run."

* * *

We ran the Long Gallery like thieves, which, given that I was rewriting the architectural law of a kingdom as I went, we essentially were. Behind us the fire roared, held at the threshold of each warded archway like a tide against a seawall — and behind *that*, patient as arithmetic, came the regent.

She did not run. She walked through her own flames, and where the wards barred her, she *paused*, and considered, and then spoke — and her voice carried through stone and fire alike, found us in the gallery's far reaches, and settled over my shoulders like a debt collector's hand.

*"Scribe."*

I kept running. Wren kept running. The voice continued, unhurried, intimate, the tone of someone who has read your contract more carefully than you have.

*"You rewrite so beautifully. Did your temple tell you why? Did they show you the first draft — the valley, the seal, the little prison of peace they built and called kindness?"* A pause, and when the voice resumed it was almost gentle. *"You were made for this. Ink in the blood. Debts in the marrow. A hundred years of careful breeding, and they sent you to me wrapped in a bow of ignorance. Give me the book, child. Give me the crowning. And I will unbind you from everything — the temple, the valley, the prophecy. You will owe nothing. To anyone. Ever again."*

And there it was. The offer again, dressed in new silk. Freedom. The word I had copied ten thousand times in ledgers without once understanding its weight.

My feet slowed. Just slightly. Just enough that Wren noticed.

"Sable." Low. Not a command. Worse — a question.

"I know." I swallowed. "I *know*." And I did know — knew the freedom it offered was the freedom of the hollowed knights, empty vessels owing nothing because nothing remained to owe. Knew it the way you know a forged signature: instantly, in the gut. But knowing a trap doesn't stop your legs from remembering every year you knelt on cold temple floors copying other people's accounts of what you cost.

The book chose that moment to help, because the book hated everyone equally. New ink surged up its open page:

*The scribe hesitates. The wolf decides.*

"What does that mean?" I demanded. "What do you *decide*—"

Wren stopped, turned, and put himself between me and the fire.

All of him. Not the half-shift, not the amber-eyed courtier's compromise he'd worn since the feast — he let the wolf come up through him whole, the change rolling over him like water, until a grey shape stood in the burning gallery with its hackles raised and its teeth bared at a hundred years of hunger, and every line of him said what he would never waste words saying aloud: *through me first.*

The regent's body tilted its head. Even possessed, even ancient, even hungry, something in that stolen face registered surprise.

*"You are sworn to the crown, little wolf,"* it said. *"Body and oath. You cannot raise fang against me. It is written."*

The wolf's answer was a sound I felt in my sternum — not quite a growl, not quite a word. But the book translated, because the book was generous with everyone's doom:

*Written,* says the wolf, *by you. And she* — the grey head swung toward me — *holds the pen.*

* * *

After that, the gallery became a battle, and the battle became a blur, and the blur became the longest hour of my life compressed into minutes.

The crown could not break its own law — not directly. Prophecy bound it tighter than it bound anyone; a hundred years of written certainty had made it rigid, and rigidity, my temple masters had taught me, is just another word for brittleness. So it sent what it could still bend: the hollowed ones, dozens of them now, pouring up the grand stair with the regent gliding behind, and it set them against the wolf at the warded archway, and it trusted weight of numbers to do what law could not.

Wren held the archway.

I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe what that looked like. He fought the way water fights — flowing around blades, breaking against armor and reforming, always, always coming back. Three knights at once, four, five, and he took wounds I could see — a gash along the shoulder that bled dark, a limp that came and went — and gave back worse, and never once retreated past the line of the archway, because behind him was me, and the book, and the last unwritten pages of everything.

And I wrote.

Kneeling at the archway's base with embers raining around me, I rewrote the palace one sigil at a time. The floor ward became a wall of force that tripped the charge of twelve hollowed men. The banner-hangers' binding-sigils became nets of golden light that dropped from the ceiling and cocooned two knights mid-lunge. When a knight broke through — fast, silent, sword already falling toward my spine — I struck through the ward on the nearest window and redrafted it as *open*, and the resulting implosion of wind took the man off his feet and out into the night, screaming a sentence the wind finished for him.

The book drank it all in. Pages turning themselves, recording, prophesying backwards — writing down what had just happened as if it had always known, which was the whole obscenity of prophecy, and also, I was beginning to understand, its fatal flaw. Because a book that records everything can be *edited*.

A page tore loose. Then another. The book, overfull, overfed on improvised history, was shedding its own certainty, and the pages rose around me like embers — hundreds of them, a hundred years of inevitability catching the fire-light and burning as they flew, and where they burned, the hollowed knights faltered, because the words holding them upright were ash before they hit the ground.

"No," said the regent's body, and for the first time the voice cracked. "*No — those are mine — those are* MINE—"

It abandoned its slow walk. It ran.

"WREN!" I screamed. "THE ARCHWAY! GO, GO—"

He came through the collapsing line of knights like a grey bolt, bleeding from a dozen cuts, seized the book's spine gently in his jaws — gently, always gently with the things that mattered — and we ran, the two of us and a storm of burning prophecy, up the west stair toward the coronation dais and the king buried underneath it, while behind us the crown's first victim tore through her own servants to reach the last pages that named her ending.

* * *

We burst onto the dais landing with the book smoking, my hands cut and ink-stained, Wren limping badly, and the door to the king's chamber standing open before us — open, and dark, and breathing.

Because the room breathed. Slow, vast inhalations, like the belly of something sleeping. The crown's work. The crown's prison. Somewhere in that dark, a young king who had once laughed at his own coronation rehearsal lay folded up inside a hundred years of someone else's appetite.

Wren shifted back — staggered, caught the doorframe, bled onto the ancient wood. His eyes found mine, amber fading to brown, and in them was the question he'd been carrying since the Thornwood, since the den, since the dance, since every moment we'd both pretended we weren't keeping count.

"Tell me you have a plan," he rasped.

I looked down at the book in my arms. Its final pages were blank now — burned away, rewritten, waiting. My contract sat against my heart where I'd carried it for ten years, the temple's neat script naming every year of my life as another man's property. And beneath both, faint as a heartbeat, I could feel the seal on Corren Hollow, three kingdoms away, thin as old paper.

"I have ink," I said. "I have debts. And I am *so* done paying other people's bills."

I stepped through the breathing door.

Behind us, up the stair, fire — and footsteps that did not hurry, because nothing that certain ever hurries — began to climb.
