## 18. The Unbinding

The palace was burning, and Sable had never felt more like a scribe.

That was the absurd truth of it. She had crossed a century of prophecy, outwitted hollowed knights, danced — badly, once, at Wren's insistence — with a queen made of stolen skin, and now, standing on the coronation dais with ash drifting down like grey snow, her first coherent thought was about *margins*.

The dais was a wide crescent of white stone at the heart of the throne room, and it had been built for exactly this: a hundred years ago, the last scribe of Corren Hollow had stood here and written the crown into its hunger. Sable could feel it in the stone under her boots, the old sigil-work humming like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. The original binding was here, sunk into the dais the way ink sinks into paper, and every binding could be unbound — that was the first law of her craft, the one they taught you before they taught you your letters.

*Every debt can be paid. Every page can be turned.*

The crown was coming.

She could hear it — not the crown itself but the thing wearing the queen-regent's body, moving through the smoke with the wrongness of a puppet whose strings had been cut and who had learned to walk anyway. Wren was somewhere behind her, in the throat of the burning corridor, holding the line in his other shape. She had seen him shift only twice, and both times her heart had done something embarrassing and unprofessional. A wolf the color of storm-water, standing between her and the dark, snarling at a hunger older than kingdoms.

*Sable.* His voice arrived in her head, rough and breathless, the bond between them fraying with distance. *How long?*

*As long as it takes,* she said. *Stop asking. You'll jinx it.*

*I don't know what a jinx is, but I'm sure it's a scribe thing and I'm sure it's annoying.*

*It is, and I love that about you. Now be quiet and bleed quietly.*

There was a pause, and then, faintly, the mental equivalent of a laugh that was mostly teeth. *Bleed quietly. I'll put it on my tombstone.*

*You don't get a tombstone. That's an order.*

The queen-thing came through the smoke.

Up close it was worse than at the feast. The body was still beautiful in the way of a portrait left too long in the sun — the features correct, the posture regal, but the eyes had gone the color of the inside of the crown, a molten hungry gold, and the mouth smiled a fraction too wide, the way a mask smiles when the person underneath has stopped being consulted.

"Little scribe," it said, in a voice like a page turning in an empty room. "You came all this way. You tore my book. You broke my knights." It tilted the stolen head, considering her. "And now you stand on my dais with nothing in your hands."

"I have something in my hands," Sable said.

She did. It was folded inside her coat, against her heart, where it had ridden since the Thornwood: her debt-contract. The original. She had gone back for it in the capital's chaos, through the archive where she'd first found the prophecy book, and she had taken it off its shelf the way you take a splinter out of your own skin — quickly, and with a sound you pretend not to make.

The contract was a single page of temple vellum, dense with the small, careful script of her life. Every year of her servitude. Every loaf of bread, every candle, every hour of copying counted and weighed against the sum that owned her. Her name at the bottom, and above it, in older ink, the name of the temple, and above *that*, in ink so old it had gone brown, a line she had never been permitted to read closely:

*Bound until the debt is paid in full, by any instrument of equal weight.*

*Any instrument of equal weight.*

The archivist had wept when she'd understood it. Sable had understood it on the dais, with the crown's hunger breathing down her neck, and understanding had felt like the moment a fever breaks — terrible and clean.

Her debt was her whole life. And a whole life was exactly the weight of a hundred-year binding.

"You have nothing," the crown said again, and the queen-regent's body raised one elegant hand, and the air filled with the sound of the palace's bones shifting. "You are a debt. You have always been a debt. Give me the prophecy and I will unmake the debt. I will make you free. I will make you *mine*, which is the same thing, and you will never have to count another candle—"

"See, that's where you're wrong," Sable said, and her voice only shook a little, which she counted as a professional triumph. "You keep offering me freedom like it's a gift. But freedom isn't a gift. Freedom is a *payment*. And I pay my debts."

She unfolded the contract.

The smoke bent toward her. The crown's hunger, that vast patient thing that had eaten a century of kings and courtiers and memories, turned all of its attention on the little page of vellum like a wolf turning on a rabbit — and Sable, who had spent eleven years being the rabbit, stood very still and did not run.

Because here was the thing the crown could not know. Here was the thing the temple had never understood, the thing the last scribe had built into her valley's seal without realizing she was building a weapon: a debt-bound scribe's magic was not in spite of the debt. It was *made of* the debt. Every year of servitude had been ink. Every counted candle had been a stroke of the pen. Sable's binding was the largest single working of debt-magic in a hundred years, and it was *hers*, and she was standing on the dais where bindings were written, holding the original text in her own two hands.

You couldn't erase a binding that big. But you could *amend* it.

She bit her thumb, the way she'd done ten thousand times to soften ink for a stubborn page, and pressed the blood to the vellum.

The contract woke.

It had never done this before — had never been *allowed* to — but the whole palace seemed to lean in as the small careful script began to move, her life's ledger rearranging itself under her bleeding thumb like a flock of birds deciding on a new direction. Words she had written at fourteen, at nineteen, at twenty-five, swam up to the surface and offered themselves. She took them. She had spent her whole life copying other people's words; now, in the last minute that mattered, she wrote her own.

*Whereas the debt of Sable Villalobos has been counted in full—*

The crown screamed. The queen-thing lunged, and smoke became a wall, and the temperature dropped as though winter had been waiting in the wings for its cue — but the dais remembered what it was for. The old sigils flared white under her feet, the hundred-year-old binding rising to meet its amendment, and the queen-thing struck the edge of the dais and *bounced*, the way a wave bounces off a seawall, and Sable kept writing.

*—and whereas said debt was incurred in service of a seal now broken by her own hand—*

Somewhere behind her, Wren howled. Not the bond-voice — a real howl, raw and furious and afraid, and she knew without turning that he'd broken through, that he was coming for her, that he'd felt what she was about to do through the bond the way you feel a door close in a house you love.

*Don't,* he said, and it wasn't a word so much as a wound. *Sable. Don't. There's another way, we'll find another way—*

"There isn't," she said out loud, because some things you had to say with a mouth, even if only one of you could hear it. "There never was. That's what the archivist was trying to tell me. That's what the whole stupid book was trying to tell me. Prophecies aren't predictions, Wren. They're *invoices*."

*Then I'm coming up there and I'm—*

"Wren." She closed her eyes for half a second, ink and blood and smoke, and let herself feel all of it — the storm-colored wolf, the burned palace, the ridiculous gentle humor of a man who'd threatened to put *bleed quietly* on his tombstone. "When you find the book — the prophecy book — look at the last page."

*The last page is your crowning. I've seen it. I'll tear it out myself—*

"It's not anymore." She opened her eyes and kept writing, and her hand was steady, and that steadiness was the freest thing she had ever done. "I tore that page out weeks ago, and the world has been improvising ever since. Which means, my dear wolf, that the ending is *unwritten*. Which means it isn't prophecy anymore. It's a first draft. And first drafts—" she pressed her thumb down for the final line, "—are for *revision*."

*Whereas the crown of Vael, being a debt unpaid for one hundred years, is hereby—*

She wrote the last words the way she had written everything that had ever mattered: small, and careful, and true.

*—bound to its debtor, and its debtor's debt being paid in full, the crown is UNBOUND.*

The world went white.

Not the white of fire — the white of a page. For one impossible moment Sable stood inside the moment before writing, the clean emptiness where every story starts, and she felt the crown's hundred-year hunger meet the weight of her eleven-year debt, and the two of them balanced on the old scribe's scales like a coin on its edge.

Then they weighed the same. Of course they did. Debts always weigh exactly what they cost.

The crown shattered.

It did not shatter like glass. It shattered like a *word* — the way a word shatters when you finally understand it, all its hidden meanings coming apart into plain light. Sable felt it go, felt the hunger unclench from the mind it had held for a century, felt it scatter into the smoke as a hundred years of stolen memories came loose and flew — into the burning courtiers, into the knights collapsing in the corridors, into the queen-regent's poor stolen body, which folded down onto the dais steps like a dress slipping off a hook, and lay still, and was only a body, and was finally, at the end, *empty in a kind way*.

And Aldric woke.

She heard it before she saw it — a gasp from the throne where the king had sat motionless through the whole battle, a man surfacing from a hundred years underwater. He came up the dais steps on legs that shook, young and old at once, his eyes his own for the first time since his crowning, and he looked at Sable — at the scribe standing in a circle of dying sigil-light with a contract crumbling to ash in her hands — and he said, in a voice rusty with disuse:

"Did you just... amend me?"

"I unbound you," Sable said. "The amendment was implied."

The king of Vael began to laugh. It was not a kingly laugh. It was the laugh of a man who has just been handed back his own head and found it still fits, and it cracked halfway through into something else — grief, probably, for the century he'd lost — and he sat down right there on the dais steps next to the empty body of the queen-regent and put his face in his hands and let it happen.

Sable let him. She was busy.

Because she could feel it — the other thing, the far thing, the thing she had known was coming since the Thornwood and had refused to say out loud even to herself. Her contract was ash. Her debt was paid. And the seal on Corren Hollow had been built on her debt the way a roof is built on a load-bearing wall.

She felt it crack from a hundred leagues away. Felt the valley's long magicless silence develop its first leak in a hundred years — magic seeping in like spring water through a foundation, patient, unstoppable, already changing everything her temple masters had believed was permanent. The peace she had been bred to preserve. The peace she had just, with one bloody thumb and eleven years of counted candles, ended.

*Triumph,* she thought, *is mostly paperwork. And the paperwork is terrible.*

The dais was quiet now. The fire was guttering — the crown's death had taken the strange hungry wind with it, and ordinary fire, unassisted, was remembering how to be ordinary. Ash drifted. Somewhere below, people were shouting, but it was the shouting of the living, which has a different music.

Wren came up the steps.

He was human again, mostly — shirtless, burned, bleeding from a dozen places, walking with the careful lurch of a man whose body had recently been a wolf's and hadn't finished renegotiating the terms. He stopped at the top of the dais and looked at her, and his face was doing several things at once, none of them organized.

"You paid it," he said. "Your debt. Your *freedom*. You—" His voice failed, restarted. "You wrote yourself out of your own life to unbind him."

"I wrote myself *into* a new one," Sable said. "That's what writing is. You should try it. You'd be terrible at it. I'd have to teach you."

"Sable."

"And the spelling would be a disaster. All those extra consonants you shifters use."

"*Sable.*"

"I'm fine," she said, and then her knees informed her, in the traditional manner, that they had been running on prophecy and stubbornness for three days, and both accounts were overdrawn. Wren caught her before the dais did, and for a moment she was just a tired woman being held by a burned man in a burning-adjacent palace, and it was, all things considered, the best moment of her life.

"The valley," he said quietly, into her hair. "I felt it. The seal. What did you do?"

"Cracked it." She pulled back enough to look at him. "It'll keep leaking. Magic's going to seep into Corren Hollow until there's no Hollow left to corren. My temple masters are going to wake up one morning and find their powerlessness has become... symmetrical." She laughed, and it came out wet. "They'll be furious. They'll be *powerless and furious*. It's going to be wonderful."

"And you? What happens to you? The contract was the binding. If it's ash—"

"Then I'm unbound too." She said it carefully, testing each word like a step on ice. "Free. Actually free. Which means—" and here her voice did finally shake, because some truths deserved it, "—which means I don't have to be anywhere. I don't *belong* anywhere. Not the valley, not the temple, not even the prophecy. The prophecy's over, Wren. We finished it. We finished it *wrong*, on purpose, and now there's no book telling me what I am."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Ash drifted between them. Below, King Aldric was giving orders in a voice that gained strength with every word, and the kingdom of Vael was beginning, painfully, to turn over in its sleep.

"Come with me," Wren said.

"Where?"

"Anywhere. Everywhere. I've spent my whole life on a leash — oath, pack, crown, prophecy, take your pick. You've spent yours on a ledger. And now neither of us—" his voice caught, and he, who never stumbled, stumbled, "—neither of us is *owned* anymore. That's terrifying. I'd like to be terrified with you."

Sable opened her mouth to answer — yes, obviously yes, the yes was already written, she could feel it waiting in her chest like ink waiting for the pen —

And the ash on the dais moved.

Not drifted. *Moved* — swirled inward, against the wind, gathering itself into a thin spiral above the shattered crown's last fragments. And in the spiral, for a moment, letters formed. Old letters. The last scribe's letters. A hundred years old and still, stubbornly, refusing to be finished:

*THE SEAL IS CRACKED. THE VALLEY IS WAKING. COME AND SEE WHAT YOU HAVE DONE, DAUGHTER OF THE LEDGER.*

Then the ash fell, ordinary again, and the words were gone.

Sable looked at Wren. Wren looked at Sable.

"Well," she said, "I suppose I'd better see what I've done."

* * *

They left at dawn, when the palace was still smoking and the king was still laughing at things that weren't funny, and the roads east were full of pilgrims coming the other way to see the place where the hundred-year hunger had died. Sable walked with a satchel of blank paper, a stub of ink, and a wolf who kept looking at her like she was a page he couldn't stop reading.

Behind them, far behind them, in a valley that had forgotten it was ever anything but safe, something old was waking up — and it was writing back.
