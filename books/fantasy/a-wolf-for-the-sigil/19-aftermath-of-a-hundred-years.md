## 19. Aftermath of a Hundred Years

The palace smelled like burnt sugar and old secrets.

Wren stood in the great hall three days after the end of the world — or the end of the crown, which amounted to nearly the same thing — and watched servants sweep ash into buckets as though ash were a normal thing a hall produced. Perhaps it was. A hundred years of the crown's hunger had to go somewhere, and apparently it had gone into the rafters, the tapestries, and the hair of every courtier who'd survived the Feast of Binding. They kept finding memory-slag in the corners: little glassy beads where stolen recollections had dripped out of the crown's feeding channels and cooled. The servants collected them in jars without asking what they were. Wren decided not to tell them. Some questions were kinder unanswered.

"You're standing in my light," said King Aldric.

Wren stepped aside. The king shuffled past him toward the window, moving like a man made mostly of suggestions. He was thinner than he'd been before the unbinding — which shouldn't have been possible, since he'd been a walking skeleton wrapped in ceremony for months — but the crown had been holding him upright the way a puppeteer holds a marionette, and now that it was shattered across the coronation dais in forty pieces (thirty-nine; Wren had pocketed one, purely for evidence, he told himself), Aldric's body had remembered its own weight all at once.

He looked, Wren thought, like a man who had just woken from a hundred-year nap and been handed the bill.

"The eastern lords are petitioning," Aldric said. "They want to know if the pardon for the shifters includes their hunting grounds."

"It does."

"They want to know if it includes *revenge*."

"It doesn't."

Aldric smiled faintly. It was a good smile, when it came — crooked, tired, human. "You're very certain for someone who spent most of his life being told what to think."

"I'm practicing," Wren said. "You named me truth-speaker. Apparently that means I get to be certain out loud."

"Truth-speaker." Aldric turned from the window. His eyes were still wrong sometimes — too old, too patient, a hundred years of watching his own mind from the inside — but they were *his* eyes again, and Wren had learned the difference. "Do you know why I chose that title? Not advisor. Not chancellor. Every king has those, and every king learns to stop hearing them."

"Why?"

"Because you told me the truth when it was useless to do so. In the cell. You sat there and told me I was dying, and I couldn't even hear you, but you said it anyway." Aldric's voice roughened. "The crown could make anyone flatter me. It couldn't make anyone mean it."

Wren looked at the floor. There was a scorch mark shaped vaguely like a swan. "I meant it badly."

"You meant it *exactly* right." The king lowered himself onto the window seat with the care of a man negotiating with his own knees. "Sit down, Wren. You've been standing at attention for three days. It's making the guards nervous. They think you're about to attack something."

"I'm always about to attack something. It's a wolf thing."

"*Sit.*"

Wren sat. The window seat was warm from morning sun, and against every instinct he owned, his shoulders dropped an inch.

Outside, Vael was doing the thing cities do after surviving: rebuilding loudly, arguing about it, and selling soup. The Thornwood's edge was visible from here, a dark green line on the horizon, and somewhere past it the shifters were walking back onto land that hadn't been theirs for sixty years. Wren had sent word ahead. He still didn't know what he'd say when he got there. *Sorry I left* seemed insufficient. *Sorry I left and also I fell in love with a scribe who then vanished into a book-shaped hole in reality* seemed like it would derail the apology entirely.

"Aldric," he said. "The woman who broke the crown. Sable."

The king's face did something careful. "Yes."

"She was on the dais. After. Everyone saw her write the unbinding — the whole courtyard saw the contract burn — and then she was just..." He gestured. Words failed him, which was rare and humiliating. "Gone. Like ink drying backward."

"I remember." Aldric folded his hands. "I remember everything now, actually. That's the strange gift of it. A hundred years of memories the crown ate are coming back to me in pieces, like letters delivered late. And I remember her standing over me, telling me about my own reign — the harvest festival where the dam broke and nobody drowned because you ordered the levees opened early. My mother's lullaby. The name of my first horse." His mouth twitched. "*Buttercup.*"

"She told me that story," Wren admitted. "She used it as an anchor. Said kings hold on better to ridiculous things."

"She wasn't wrong." Aldric was quiet a moment. "You're going to ask me if I saw where she went."

"I'm going to ask everyone until someone gives me an answer that isn't a shrug."

"Then let me save you some walking." The king leaned forward. "When the binding broke — when she paid her debt with her freedom — I felt it leave. The crown, I mean. It didn't die screaming the way I'd always imagined. It went *home*. Whatever it was, whatever hunger drove it, it had a place it was bound to, and her unbinding sent it back there. And she—" He hesitated, choosing words like a man crossing ice. "She went with the current. Or the current took her. I genuinely don't know which. But it pulled *east*, Wren. Toward the valley. Toward whatever sealed place your temple scribes came from."

Wren's heart did something complicated. "Corren Hollow."

"If that's the name. The magicless valley." Aldric's gaze sharpened. "Which is no longer magicless, I'm told. Our weather-mages woke up two days ago complaining that their bones were humming. The wards around that valley cracked like an eggshell the night the crown shattered. Every diviner in the capital felt it."

"So she's alive."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You looked east when you said it. Kings only look meaningful when they're hiding bad news or delivering hope, and you don't have enough energy left to hide anything."

Aldric laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and then coughing, because his lungs were still renegotiating their contract. "You'll do well as truth-speaker. You're insufferable already."

"I have references."

*

The pardon ceremony was held in the outer courtyard, because the great hall still smelled like burnt sugar and the kitchens refused to be blamed for it.

Wren stood beside the throne — not on it, never on it, though half the court kept looking at him like they expected him to sprout fur and claim it — while Aldric, propped up by ceremony and stubbornness in equal measure, read the proclamation aloud. Sixty years of exile revoked. The shifter packs restored to the Thornwood and the high meadows beyond. Compensation for seized lands, to be assessed by — Wren's mouth went dry — *the King's Truth-Speaker, who shall hear all claims and speak them plainly to the crown.*

They'd given him paperwork. They'd given the outcast wolf-shifter whose pack died under a broken oath *paperwork.*

"There are four hundred and eleven claims," the under-clerk whispered to him afterward, pressing a ledger into his arms with the reverence of a man handing off a live snake. "Some of them are duplicates. Some of them are forgeries. One of them, my lord, is written on a turnip."

"On a—"

"We believe it's symbolic, my lord."

Wren stared down at the ledger. Four hundred and eleven claims. Somewhere in Corren Hollow, a scribe had once counted her debts in ledgers exactly like this one, column by column, year by year, believing each entry brought her closer to freedom. She would have known what to do with a turnip. She probably would have catalogued it.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it, and the under-clerk blinked like he'd been handed a compliment by a thunderstorm.

That evening, the first of the pack arrived.

They came through the south gate at dusk — twelve of them, travel-worn, wary, led by Maera, who had been a cub when the exile happened and had grown up learning to hate the crown from stories. She stopped ten paces from Wren in the torchlight, sniffed the air with deliberate insolence, and said, "You smell like palace soap."

"It's a hazard of employment."

"You have *employment.*"

"Truth-speaker. It comes with a chair."

Maera looked him over — the fine grey cloak, the sigil of office, the shadows under his eyes — and something in her stance shifted from challenge to something almost gentler. "The den elders said you'd gone soft."

"The den elders are welcome to inspect me personally."

"They will. With teeth." But she was smiling now, thin and real, and behind her the others were looking at the courtyard walls, the banners, the open gates — looking at *belonging* the way starving people look at bread. "We heard what you did. All of it. The oath-breaking, word by word, in front of the whole court." Her voice caught. "Nobody's ever broken an oath *for* us before. Only on us."

Wren's throat tightened. "It was overdue."

"It was *impossible.*" She stepped closer. "And the scribe? The one the songs are already lying about?"

"The songs are lying already?"

"There's one where she rides you into battle. As a horse."

"That's — no. Absolutely not. She'd never let me hear the end of it."

Maera's grin widened. "*Hear the end of it.* So she talks."

Wren opened his mouth. Closed it. The courtyard torches crackled. And the truth, which was supposedly his profession now, came out of him sideways and quiet:

"She talked constantly. She narrated everything. She argued with prophecy like it was a badly organized filing system. She rewrote a ward-sigil in the middle of a battle and complained the original calligraphy was sloppy." He swallowed. "She's gone. The unbinding took her east, toward her valley. I'm going to find her."

Maera studied him for a long moment. Then she said, gently, in the tone wolves use for wounded packmates pretending not to bleed: "I know."

*

He left for Corren Hollow nine days later, over the objections of three chancellors, one under-clerk, and the entire concept of the claims ledger.

"The kingdom needs stability," Chancellor Ovid protested, wringing his hands in the way that had clearly gotten him promoted.

"The kingdom needs me to stop pacing," Wren said. "Trust me. I've seen myself pace. It frightens the staff."

"You can't simply *leave*—"

"I can, actually. Watch." He shouldered his pack. "The truth-speaker position, as I understand it, requires me to speak truths. Here is one: I am useless to this kingdom until I know whether the woman who saved it is alive. Every decision I make until then will be half a decision. Half decisions make bad policy. Bad policy makes rebellions. Therefore, finding her *is* statecraft."

Ovid opened his mouth, found nothing, and closed it. From the throne, Aldric hid a smile behind his hand with the skill of long practice.

"Take the eastern road," the king said. "And Wren — take this." He held out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside was a shard of the shattered crown, no bigger than a coin, dull and dead as river glass. "Every piece we recovered went inert except this one. The mages can't explain it. It's cold in summer and warm in winter, and it hums when—" He stopped.

"When what?"

"When it faces east."

Wren closed his hand around it. It was warm, faintly, like a page that had been recently touched. Like a book that remembered hands.

"I'll bring back your answer," he said.

"Bring back *her,*" Aldric said. "The answer can walk."

*

The journey east took nineteen days, and Wren used every one of them to rehearse.

This was, he reflected somewhere past the Thornwood's edge, deeply stupid behavior for a grown predator. He had faced hollowed knights in the dark. He had broken a century-old oath syllable by syllable while the crown screamed in his skull. He had danced — *danced* — with a queen-regent wearing a stolen body, and kept his face pleasant. But he could not, despite forty separate attempts, compose an opening sentence for the moment he found Sable Villalobos.

*I came as fast as I could* sounded like an excuse. *I never doubted you were alive* was a lie; he'd doubted hourly, in the specific grinding way of a man chewing the same worry like gristle. *The turnip claim remains unresolved* was true and important but perhaps not the emotional note required.

By day twelve he'd settled on saying her name and improvising. By day fifteen he'd abandoned even that and settled on picking her up, which solved the sentence problem entirely and created several new ones.

The valley announced itself before he saw it.

The road climbed through hills that grew greener with every mile — unnaturally greener, now, since the seal had cracked. For a hundred years Corren Hollow had been a wound of ordinary in a magical world: no wards, no charms, no shifting blood, no singing stones. Farmers had settled there precisely because nothing *happened.* Now the fields rippled with small strangenesses. A flock of birds wheeled in a perfect spiral, holding the shape like dancers. A creek ran briefly uphill, giggled — actually giggled — and thought better of it. Wildflowers along the roadside turned to follow travelers, not hungrily, just curiously, like dogs.

Magic, seeping home after a century of exile. The valley was remembering what it was.

So, apparently, were its people. In the first village he passed, a crowd had gathered around a well where the water was glowing faintly gold, and an old woman was shouting, delighted and furious, "Seventy years I prayed for rain and NOW it wants to be *miraculous*—"

Wren bought bread, asked directions, and learned that the temple at the Hollow's center had gone very quiet lately. Its scribes had lost their authority overnight — the debt-contracts they held had all burned to ash on the same night, every one, in a wave that rolled outward from the temple itself. The villagers were torn between terror and a giddy, disbelieving joy.

"And the scribe," Wren asked the innkeeper, keeping his voice casual with heroic effort. "Sable Villalobos. Was she there? Is she—"

"Oh, the Whitlock girl?" The innkeeper waved a floury hand. "Came back the night the sky cracked, slept for two days straight, and then walked out of town yesterday morning with a bag and that old black book of hers. Headed east, if you can believe it. Back toward the capital, someone said. Or maybe just... away." She squinted at him. "Friend of hers?"

Something in Wren's chest unlocked so suddenly he nearly staggered. "Yes," he managed. "Something like that."

"Well." The innkeeper looked at his face — really looked — and softened. "East road. She can't have more than a day on you. She walks like a woman reading while moving."

That, at least, was accurate. Wren was out the door before his bread was paid for, tossed the coins over his shoulder, and ran.

*

He found her at dusk on the third day, in a meadow where the returning magic had made the grass grow in slow silver waves, and every rehearsal he'd ever done evaporated on contact with reality.

She was sitting cross-legged in the middle of it with the prophecy book open on her knees, writing — actually writing, quill moving, adding text to pages that had been fixed for a century. She'd cut her hair short and practical. She wore traveling clothes and an expression of profound concentration, and she was muttering to herself in the cadence he knew better than his own heartbeat: *"No, that's wrong, the meter's all off, whoever wrote this section was being paid by the adjective—"*

A wolf could have crossed that meadow silently. Wren had been a wolf for most of his life. Instead he stepped on a branch.

Her head snapped up. Their eyes met across fifty feet of impossible grass.

For a moment neither of them moved. The meadow waved. Somewhere a bird sang with suspicious virtuosity.

Then Sable said, in a voice that wobbled only slightly, "You're supposed to be in the capital, learning to file things."

"The filing system here is terrible," Wren said. "I came to complain."

And she laughed — that sharp, surprised, helpless laugh he'd heard maybe five times and hoarded like treasure — and was up and running across the meadow, and he was running too, and the last twenty feet happened much faster than either of them had planned. She hit him hard enough to knock the breath out of him and he held on, one hand fisted in her coat, face buried in her cropped hair, breathing like a man surfacing.

"You *idiot,*" she said into his chest, muffled and fierce. "You absolute — I tore a hole in causality for you people, the least you could do was send a *letter*—"

"You tore a hole in causality. I walked. We all contribute differently."

She pulled back just far enough to glare at him, eyes red-rimmed and blazing, and Wren finally understood what the truth-speaker's job actually was, because the truth came out of him plain and unfiled:

"I searched the ashes for three days. I found your book. The last page was blank and I thought—" His voice cracked. He let it. Truth-speaker. "I thought I'd lost you to the story."

Sable's expression gentled. She reached into her satchel and drew out the black book, worn soft at the corners, and opened it to the final page. Blank white paper. Except now, in fresh brown-black ink, in a careful new hand, there was a single line:

*A wolf crosses the valley at dawn, owing nothing to anyone.*

"I wrote it last night," she said quietly. "Or — it wrote itself, and I let it. The book's not finished with us, Wren. Prophecies end. Stories don't. I checked the margins." Her mouth curved. "There's room."

Wren looked at the blank space below the line — all that clean, unwritten white — and felt the future open like a door.

"Room for what?" he asked.

Sable dipped her quill, held it out to him handle-first, and smiled.

"That," she said, "is the best question anyone's asked all year. Sit down. Let's find out."
