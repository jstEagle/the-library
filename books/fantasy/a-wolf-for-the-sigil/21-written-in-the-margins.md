## 21. Written in the Margins

*Sable*

The valley had begun to dream.

That was how Tam, the youngest ledger-scribe, put it one morning over the porridge pot — not magic, he said quickly, because saying *magic* in Corren Hollow was still like whistling in a sickroom, but dreams. Odd ones. His grandmother had dreamed of a river that sang. Old Master Corvel had dreamed of a door where his pantry wall should be.

Sable kept her own dreams to herself, mostly because hers were of a burning palace and a man with a crown eating him alive from the inside out, and she did not think porridge was the place for it.

Weeks had passed since the unbinding. Weeks since she had woken on the floor of the temple archive with ink-stained fingers and no debt at all, in a valley whose edges had gone soft as wet paper. The mountains still stood. The temple still stood. But the *seal* — the great quiet thing that had pressed down on Corren Hollow for a hundred years, keeping it magicless and safe and small — had cracked the way an egg cracks: invisibly, irrevocably, all at once.

She felt it most in the mornings now. A hum under the flagstones. A taste of iron in the well water. The candle flames leaning, very slightly, toward her when she walked past, like dogs hoping to be fed.

"You're doing it again," Tam said, appearing at her elbow with the smugness of the young. "Staring at nothing."

"I'm staring at the window."

"The window's fine. You're staring at what's *past* the window." He set a bundle on her desk with a thump. "Master Corvel says you're to catalogue this. He says you have a talent for strange books." A pause, and then, gentler than Tam ever managed anything: "He means it kindly. Mostly."

The bundle was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the plain hemp cord the temple used for everything, because the temple believed beauty encouraged pride. Sable untied it and knew before the cloth fell open what she would find.

The prophecy book.

It had come back to her the way things came back to people in stories — found on the road by a tinker, sold for bread, bought by a pilgrim, carried here. She had held it once already, wept over it once already, read its final page once already and found it blank as a held breath. Wren's last written act had been to survive. The book had simply stopped keeping score.

Now it sat on her desk, warm.

Not metaphorically warm. Not the warmth of a book that had been sitting near a fire. It warmed against her palms the way a sleeping animal warms, faintly and unevenly, and when she pressed her hand flat to the cover she could have sworn she felt something on the other side press back.

"Tam," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Has anyone else touched this?"

"Corvel looked through it. Said it was blank rubbish." Tam frowned. "Why? Is it cursed? Should I get the salt?"

"Get the salt," Sable agreed absently, already opening the cover, "and then go away."

The early pages were as she remembered them — the tight, ancient script of the first scribe, prophecies dated a century past, including the one that had described her face and her name and a wolf at her side. She turned past them quickly, past the middle pages where her own story had been written out ahead of her like a trap, past the torn stub where page thirteen had been — tearing it had cost her three days of nosebleeds and a permanent tremor in her left hand, and she'd do it again — and arrived at the end.

The final page was not blank anymore.

There were only two words on it, in handwriting she knew better than her own heartbeat, handwriting she had watched scratch out ward-sigils by firelight and carve her name into a tree with a claw because he'd been too embarrassed to say it aloud:

*Still coming.*

Sable sat down. Then, because sitting didn't seem sufficient to the occasion, she stood up again, knocked over her inkpot, caught it, put it down, and sat back down.

"Still coming," she repeated to the empty room.

The book warmed under her hands, patiently, the way a wolf waits at the edge of a clearing.

* * *

*Wren*

The Thornwood had grown back wrong, which was to say it had grown back honest.

Wren crouched at the edge of the old den-site and let the wind tell him what it could. Ash and rain and green things pushing up through ruin. Somewhere north, a stag. Somewhere south, the distant sour smell of the capital, which even at this distance made his hackles rise. And threaded through all of it, so faint he might have imagined it except that he had never once imagined it in twenty-nine years of not imagining it —

Her.

Not her scent, exactly. Something stranger. Like the memory of her voice written into the air itself, arriving sideways, the way their whole story had always arrived: backwards, in fragments, in a dead woman's book.

He shifted back to skin and crouched by the pack-rolls. Maren, the eldest survivor, was sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, which was how Maren prayed.

"You're going," she said without looking up.

"I'm going."

"To the valley. Through the crack in the seal. Where the crown's hunters can't follow and the king's writ doesn't run and nobody has sworn anything to anybody in a hundred years." She tested the blade's edge with her thumb. "You understand that if you cross, you can't carry your oath across with you. The seal won't permit it. You'll be oath-free for the first time since you were nine years old."

"I know."

"Do you?" She finally looked at him, and her eyes were the yellow of old paper. "I've watched you wear that oath like armor, boy. I've watched it choke you. And I've also watched it hold you together on nights when nothing else would. Take it off, and what's left?"

Wren thought about it honestly, because Maren deserved honesty and because lying to her had never once worked.

"A man who owes a scribe his life," he said, "and intends to spend a long time repaying it."

Maren snorted. "That's not love, that's accounting."

"It's both. She'd appreciate that. She was a debt-scribe." He shouldered his pack. "Besides — the book told me to come."

That got her attention fully. "The book *spoke* to you?"

"Not spoke." He pulled the prophecy book from his pack — his copy, or rather the original, which had chosen him after the palace burned, appearing in the ashes of the royal archive with its pages warm and its final page suddenly, smugly legible. "Writes. Look."

He opened it to the end. Below *Still coming*, new text had appeared overnight in his own handwriting, though he had written none of it:

*Three days to the border. The valley will try to turn you around twice. Let it. The third time, walk straight.*

"She wrote that?" Maren asked.

"No. That's the maddening part. Neither of us wrote it." He closed the book gently. "The bond did. Whatever our story wrote into the world when she tore the page and broke the contract and shattered a hundred-year hunger — it's still writing. It uses my hand. It knows the road." He hesitated, then admitted the part he'd been carrying alone: "Sometimes I wake up and there are words on my arm. Directions. Like a compass that loves me."

Maren was quiet a long moment. Then she reached out and gripped his forearm, hard, the way shifters blessed a departure.

"Then go be directed," she said. "And boy — if the valley tries to turn you around, don't fight it angry. Fight it *certain*. There's a difference, and the seal can taste it."

* * *

*Sable*

The valley tried to hide the book from her on the fourth day.

She woke to find it gone from her desk, which was impossible, because she had locked it in a drawer, and the drawer was still locked, and the room's only other exit was a window three floors up. She found it eventually in the temple's deep archive, shelved among tax ledgers from a century nobody cared about, cold to the touch for the first time since it had arrived, sulking like a cat that had been put outside.

"All right," she told it, hauling it back to her room. "I see how this is going to be."

Because she understood now — had understood since the second morning, when the words *the bridge at Marrow Ford is out; take the eastern road* appeared on her forearm in handwriting that made her chest ache — that the book was a thread, and threads ran in two directions, and somewhere on the far side of the cracked seal a wolf was walking toward her while the valley itself tried to protect her from him.

That was the bitter joke of it. The seal had been built to keep magic out. Now, half-broken, it seemed determined to keep *love* out instead, as if some lingering fragment of the old scribe's intent had decided that of all the dangerous things in the world, a wolf-shifter with kind eyes was the one Corren Hollow must never contain.

The words came at odd hours. On her arm, in the margins of her ledgers, once — memorably — in the steam on the kitchen window, so that Tam dropped an entire tray of bread.

*Cold night. Found a barn. The farmer's dog and I have an understanding.*

*The bridge at Marrow Ford is out. Take the eastern road.* (This one she wrote back, pressing her pen to her own skin until the letters bloomed red: *I KNOW. THE BOOK TOLD ME THREE DAYS AGO. YOU'RE SLOW.*)

*Did you just scold me through space and time?*

*Someone has to. You once fought six hollowed knights with a soup ladle.*

*It was a GOOD ladle.*

She laughed until she cried, alone in her room over a book that purred warmth against her ribs, and if any of the temple elders had seen her they would have said she was bewitched, and they would have been exactly, precisely right.

But the valley pushed back too. Twice she woke with the book missing and had to hunt it down — once in the well-house, once wedged behind the altar where it radiated offended heat like a stove. Twice the words on her arm smeared into nonsense, and she spent anxious hours re-inking them from memory. Once, worst of all, she dreamed of the old scribe — the first one, the one who had sealed the valley and bred Sable's line like prize hens — standing at the foot of her bed with a face like wet parchment.

"He cannot cross," the scribe said. "The seal remembers what it was for."

"The seal is broken," Sable said.

"The seal is *healing*. Cracks close, little successor. Give it time, and the valley will be quiet again, and safe, and small, and you will grow old counting debts that mean nothing—"

"I tore your page," Sable said. "I tore every page you meant me to live inside. Write that in your margins."

She woke with her hand throbbing and the book open on her chest, warm as a heartbeat, and on the final page, in fresh ink, in his hand:

*Two days. The valley turned me around at the ford. I went certain. It worked. Also I may have frightened a census-taker. Long story. Save it for the fire.*

* * *

*Wren*

The border of Corren Hollow was not a wall or a gate. It was a feeling.

Wren felt it a mile out: a pressure like deep water, a whisper at the base of the skull saying *turn back, turn back, nothing for you here, nothing you want, nothing you remember wanting.* The old scribe's voice, worn smooth by a century of repetition. He recognized it the way you recognize a lie you've lived inside — because he had heard its cousin his whole life, in every oath-swearing, every leash, every *know your place, shifter.*

He stopped at the treeline and took out the book.

The last page was full now, top to bottom, in both their hands — his directions, her replies, arguments about roads and ladles and whether wolves could taste sarcasm (they could; she owed him an apology). At the bottom, fresh that morning, in her writing:

*I'll meet you at the eastern gate at dawn. Not as scribe, not as debtor, not as whatever the prophecy thought I was. Just me. If that's acceptable to the famous Wren of the Thornwood, hero of the soup-ladle engagement.*

Below it, unbidden, the bond added a line neither of them had written, in a hand like neither and both:

*Dawn. Eastern gate. Come certain.*

Wren closed the book and walked at the valley's whispering edge until sunset, and when the whisper swelled to a shout he did what Maren had taught him without knowing she was teaching it: he thought of her laugh, her terrible handwriting, the exact weight of her head against his shoulder in the ruined den while she cried over his death-page, and he walked *straight.*

The seal parted around him like a crowd making way.

It hurt. He wouldn't pretend otherwise — it felt like pulling a splinter the length of his life out of his chest, oath and leash and hundred-year hunger and every chain he'd ever called duty, all of it snagging on the way out. He knelt in the border grass shaking, empty, lighter than he had ever been and terrified of it, a wolf with no pack-law and no crown-oath and no orders from anyone alive or dead.

Free.

The word was too big to say. So he shifted instead — let the wolf take the shape of everything he couldn't articulate — and ran east through the healing dark, toward dawn, toward a gate, toward a woman who had torn the world open to rewrite one page, carrying a book that had learned, somewhere in its long hungry century, how to hope.

Behind him, faint as a closing eye, the seal sighed — and did not close.

Ahead of him, on the eastern road, a lamp was already lit.
