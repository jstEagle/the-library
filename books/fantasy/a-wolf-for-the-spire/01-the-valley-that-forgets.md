## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink froze first. It always did.

Jude Lockridge blew on her pen to warm the nib and watched the gray light come up over the eastern ridge of the valley, where the mountain stood with its shoulders in cloud. Three hundred years of prayers sat in the scriptorium behind her, copied in a hand so fine it could pass for print, and not one of them — not a single line of petition or praise written since the Quiet Flame settled over this place like snow — had ever been answered.

She knew that the way she knew the weight of her own hands. She had copied eleven thousand of them herself.

"Page forty," said Mother Ansel from the doorway, without looking up from her own ledger. "The widow Corren's petition goes on page forty. Third column. Mind the spacing; the archivists complain."

"The archivists always complain."

"The archivists are dead," Mother Ansel said, "which is why they complain less than the living. Page forty, Jude."

Jude turned the page. The paper was temple stock, heavy and cream-colored, milled from reeds that grew only in the valley's slow water. Everything here was slow. The river took a full day to cross the valley floor. The bread rose overnight whether you asked it to or not. Even grief moved at the pace of geology, worn smooth by repetition until it stopped cutting anyone.

Widow Corren's prayer was short, as most were by the time they reached the copying stage. *For my sister's boy, taken with fever. Let him keep his breath through the night.* Below it, in the margin, a clerk three generations dead had noted: *Standard intercession fee, four marks. No answer recorded.*

No answer recorded. The phrase appeared so often in the ledgers that it had its own abbreviation — N.A.R., stamped in red, a little block of letters like a door slammed shut. Jude wrote it perhaps sixty times a day. She had once tried to calculate how many times it had been written since the founding and gotten a number so large she'd had to sit down.

She dipped her pen. Wrote the widow's words out clean. Stamped the red letters beneath them.

And then, because it was morning and her hands needed something to do besides obey, she let herself think about the boundary.

* * *

Every child in the valley learned about the line before they learned their letters. You could walk to it in half a day if you were strong — up the shepherd's path past the last farm, through the black pines, to a place where nothing marked the edge except the way things behaved there. Birds turned back. Not all at once, not dramatically; they simply would not cross, wheeling in the air as though the sky itself ended at an invisible wall. Bees did the same. Once, when Jude was nine, she had watched a hawk fly straight at the line, stall, and drop like a stone into the pines, unhurt but defeated, before climbing back the way it came.

Her mother had told her why, in the flat voice she used for doctrine. "When the old gods went to war, the Quiet Flame was lit to keep the ashes out. Magic cannot follow you here. Nothing answers here. That is the price of peace."

Peace. Jude had stood at the line many times since, on errands to the high farms, and felt nothing at all — no hum, no hush, no sense of a held breath. Just wind, and pines, and the strange obedience of birds. But she had never been able to shake the thought that a peace which required birds to forget how to fly in a straight line was a strange kind of peace. A peace that looked, from certain angles, like a cage with very tall bars.

"You're drifting," Mother Ansel said.

"I'm copying."

"You're copying slowly, which is drifting with extra steps." The old woman set down her pen and rubbed her knuckles. Her hands were maps of veins and old ink stains. "Page forty-one after this. And Jude — the Corren account. Check the debt column before you close it."

Jude checked it. That was how she found the problem.

The widow Corren owed the temple eleven marks — standard fees for intercessions that had never once been answered, accruing interest at the gentle, merciless rate of one mark per season. Eleven marks was two sheep. It was a winter's firewood. It was, for a woman alone with a dying nephew, impossible.

And under temple law, unpayable debts did not simply sit. They converted. After three seasons of default, the debtor's household entered indenture — service to the temple until the balance cleared, which, given the interest, meant generations. There was a boy in the tannery now whose grandfather had copied prayers to clear a debt of six marks. The arithmetic never worked. Everyone knew it didn't work. The ledgers weren't designed to be paid; they were designed to be kept.

Three seasons of default. Jude ran her finger down the widow's column and counted. Two. One more season and the temple would come for whatever hands it could use, and the only hands in that house belonged to a boy of twelve who was, according to the physician's note clipped to the file, burning with fever.

The temple did not take sick children. The temple waited for them to get well, and then it took them.

Jude looked at the entry for a long time. Around her, the scriptorium scratched and breathed — thirty copyists, heads bowed, pens moving, the great windows letting in light the color of dishwater. Mother Ansel had returned to her own ledger. Nobody was watching. Nobody ever watched, because nobody had ever had a reason to.

There was a clause. There was always a clause; the temple loved clauses the way other institutions loved saints. Clause nineteen of the Debt Ordinance: *Where the debtor demonstrates good faith through charitable labor rendered to the temple, the steward may abate accrued interest at discretion.* Discretion. The word sat there like an unlocked door in a house full of locked ones.

The widow Corren had labored for the temple. Everyone in the lower parish had, at some point — scrubbing the pilgrim floors, hauling water, sitting through the long grey services. It was never recorded, because recording it would have created obligations, and the temple did not care to owe anything to anyone.

But it could have been recorded. That was the thing Jude turned over in her mind, feeling the shape of it like a coin under a blanket. It *could have been.*

She pulled a fresh slip of record paper toward her. In a clerk's hand — not hers, deliberately not hers, a plain round hand like a hundred others in the archive — she wrote: *Received of Tessa Corren, labor rendered: six days' washing of temple linen, autumn season past. Witnessed. Entered per Clause Nineteen.*

Six days of washing. Plausible. Verifiable, if anyone cared to verify it, which no one had in living memory. Six days of washing abated the interest and knocked four marks off the principal. Seven marks remained — still crushing, still unfair, but no longer convertible. No longer a hook in a child's skin.

Jude blotted the slip, carried it to the intake shelf where such slips waited for the evening filing, and slid it into the middle of the stack, third from the top, where it would look like it had always been there.

Her heart was going like a bird at the boundary line.

She walked back to her desk. Sat down. Turned to page forty-one.

It had taken ninety seconds. Somewhere in the valley below, a boy was burning with fever, and the entire machinery of the Quiet Flame — thirty copyists, four elders, three hundred years of immaculate records — had done exactly nothing for him, and would have taken him the moment he recovered. And the only force standing between that boy and the tannery was a forged slip of paper worth six days of laundry.

Jude thought: *I have just committed fraud against God.*

She thought: *God wasn't using it.*

Then she picked up her pen and copied the next prayer, because the next prayer was waiting, and the one after that, and the eleven thousand after those, and the day did not stop for the conscience of a scribe.

* * *

At midday the bell rang for the meal, and the copyists filed out into the cloister in their grey robes, thirty identical silhouettes against thirty identical shadows. Jude fell in beside Dellis, who was young and loud and the closest thing to a friend the rules allowed.

"You've got ink on your chin," Dellis said.

"It's a prayer mark. Very holy."

"It's a crime scene, is what it is." Dellis grinned. She was the only person in the scriptorium who treated the work as a joke, carefully, the way you'd pat a sleeping dog. "Elder Maren was asking about you this morning. Asking what you'd been filing lately."

The bird hit the invisible wall of Jude's chest. "What did you tell her?"

"That you file what you're told to file, same as everyone." Dellis shrugged. "Which is true, isn't it?"

"Same as everyone," Jude said.

Dellis studied her a moment too long, then let it go, because Dellis understood the first law of the valley better than any elder: nothing changes here, and people who look like change get watched. They walked the rest of the cloister in silence, boots ringing on stone, and above them the swallows wheeled in the courtyard air — free, contained, turning back at the eaves as though even the temple roof had its own small boundary line.

* * *

The afternoon passed in columns. Widow Corren's page closed with its red stamp and its hidden mercy buried three shelves away in the intake stack. Jude copied a farmer's plea for rain, a bride's plea for a safe birth, a dying man's plea for forgiveness from a god who had not spoken in three centuries. N.A.R. N.A.R. N.A.R. The stamps fell like small red doors closing.

Toward dusk, Mother Ansel paused at Jude's desk on her rounds. She stood there a moment, reading the finished pages upside down, which she claimed kept her honest.

"Good spacing," she said finally.

"Thank you, Mother."

"The Corren account," Mother Ansel said. "Closed clean?"

Jude kept her eyes on her pen. "Clean as the ordinance allows."

"Mm." The old woman's shadow lay across the page a moment longer than it needed to. Then she moved on, and Jude could not tell, and would never be able to tell, whether the pause had been suspicion or simply the slowness of age — everything in the valley moved slowly, even doubt.

That evening Jude climbed the bell tower, which was permitted, and looked east, which was tolerated. The mountain stood black against the last light, higher than anything had a right to be, wearing its clouds like a hood. Somewhere under all that stone, the doctrine said, the old gods slept the sleep of the defeated, and the Quiet Flame burned to keep them sleeping, and the valley forgot them so thoroughly that forgetting had become a form of worship.

The birds were coming home across the valley floor in long dark ribbons, thousands of them, every one of them obediently turning back from a line no eye could see.

"They don't even know it's there," Jude said aloud to the empty tower. "They just know not to cross."

She stayed until the cold drove her down. Below in the scriptorium, the evening lamps were being lit, and tomorrow's pages waited blank and patient, ready to receive another sixty unanswered prayers, another handful of red stamps, another season of a world that had traded wonder for order and called the trade peace.

On the intake shelf, third slip from the top, a forged record of six days' laundry waited to become true.

And far to the east, deeper than any doctrine reached, something under the mountain shifted in its sleep — though no one in the valley felt it, because the valley was built, stone by patient stone, out of not feeling things.

Jude blew out her lamp. The ink froze. The dark came on.

Three hundred years of silence, she thought, falling asleep. And somewhere in her chest, quiet as a forged signature, the first crack had already opened.
