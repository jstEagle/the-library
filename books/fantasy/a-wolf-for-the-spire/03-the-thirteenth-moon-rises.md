## 3. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The envoy arrived on a Tuesday, because of course he did. The temple received all its catastrophes on Tuesdays — it was the day the ledgers were audited, and the universe had apparently decided to keep to the schedule.

Jude was in the scriptorium when the bells went wrong. She knew every bell in the valley the way she knew her own heartbeat: dawn bell, copy bell, meal bell, vespers. This one didn't belong to any hour. It was lower, older, and it came not from the bell tower but from the front gates, from a horn that someone was blowing with the patience of a man who knew the sound itself opened doors.

She put down her pen. The ink kept moving for a half-second after she lifted it — a thin thread of black crawling on without her — and then stopped, as if embarrassed.

*Not again,* she thought, and pressed her thumb flat over the word until it dried obedient.

Nine days since Widow Marren's candle had relit itself mid-prayer. Nine days since Elder Cassian had sealed the scriptorium, posted two guards at the door, and announced — in the tone of a man nailing shut a window that had already flown open — that the incident had been "a draft." Nine days of copying prayers that no one believed were going nowhere anymore. The whole valley had gone quiet in a new way, the way a field goes quiet before something crosses it.

The horn blew again. Jude's inkwell shivered.

She was out of her seat and at the scriptorium door before she remembered she wasn't supposed to leave. Brother Anselm, one of her guards, stepped into her path with his hands raised.

"Sister Jude. You know the rules."

"I'm not a sister," she said automatically. "I'm contracted labor. There's a difference, and you know it, because you helped write my contract."

"Then contracted labor stays in the room."

Below, in the courtyard, voices. Not the usual Tuesday shuffle of debtors and petitioners. Something with weight to it. Anselm glanced toward the window despite himself, and Jude saw the exact moment his resolve loosened: his eyes went wide, his mouth made a small shape, and he stepped sideways without meaning to.

Jude went past him and down the stairs.

* * *

The courtyard was full but silent. Half the temple stood in a ragged crescent, and at the center of it, alone, stood a man who did not fill space so much as *claim* it.

He was tall and narrow, dressed in a coat the color of wet slate, cut in a fashion Jude had only seen in old engravings — high collar, long lines, buttons of dark horn running like vertebrae. His hair was gray but not aged; it looked gray the way stone is gray. He carried no weapon. He carried, instead, a ledger.

It was bound in leather gone brown-black with handling, brass corners worn to shine, and it was thick — thick the way Bibles are thick, thick the way law books are thick, thick enough that holding it should have required both arms. He held it in one hand, balanced on his palm, the way a waiter balances a tray.

"Good morning," he said, and his voice reached every corner of the courtyard without being raised. "I apologize for the hour. I've come from beneath the mountain, and the roads are long."

Nobody answered. Somewhere behind Jude, a chicken made an aborted noise and thought better of it.

Elder Cassian came forward the way men come forward toward large dogs, sidelong and smiling. "Honored envoy. The Temple of the Quiet Flame receives you. We were not informed—"

"You were not informed," the envoy agreed pleasantly, "because notice is a courtesy extended to peers. I am not here on courtesy. I am here on contract." He let that sit. Then he opened the ledger.

Jude had spent eleven years reading ledgers. She knew their grammar the way farmers know weather. But when this one opened, she felt the reading happen *to* her — the columns pulling her eye down them like water down a channel — and she understood, in the place where understanding becomes instinct, that this book was not a record of debts. It was where debts lived.

"Contract of Sale," the envoy read, "numbered four thousand ninety-one in the third register, executed in this valley — yes, in this very valley, how convenient — upon the occasion of a birth imminent. The seller: one Maren Iversen, resident of the village of Aldersbrook." A pause. A ripple through the crowd. Jude heard someone whisper *Marren*, and someone else correct, *no, Iversen, old Iversen's girl.* "The goods conveyed: the soul of issue unborn. The buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Terms: collection upon the completion of thirteen turns of the thirteenth moon."

He looked up. His eyes found Jude across the courtyard as if she had been standing under a lamp.

"Jude Iversen," he said. "You are the issue."

* * *

Afterward, she could never say why she walked forward. Cowardice, probably. It is easier to step into a thing than to have it come for you.

The crowd parted the way crowds do, reluctantly, everyone wanting to watch without being close. Jude crossed the stones she'd scrubbed a thousand times as penance and stood in front of the envoy, close enough to see that his ledger's pages were not paper exactly. They had a grain to them, like skin. Close enough to see that her name — when he turned the book so she could read it, and he did, courteously, the way you show a dog its own reflection — her name was written in ink that moved. Not crawling, not alive, just... patient. Resting.

"That isn't my handwriting," she said, because her mouth needed to say something and that was truest.

"No," the envoy said. "It's hers."

"My mother has been dead eleven years."

"Yes. Sales survive sellers. It's rather the point of sales." He closed the book gently, fingers flat along the spine, and studied her with something she might almost have called sympathy if it hadn't been so well organized. "You are wondering why now. Thirteen moons, girl — there is a thirteenth moon coming, the first in three hundred years, and contracts written against it wake when it rises. Your mother knew. Your mother signed anyway. Desperation makes excellent ink." He tilted his head. "You have questions. Everyone does. But questions are not my department. Collection is."

"I'm not going anywhere with you."

"You misunderstand the arrangement entirely." He smiled. It was the worst thing Jude had seen in eleven years of copying other people's suffering into tidy columns. "I am Vess. Witness-Envoy, Third Rank. I don't take anyone anywhere. I arrive, I read the contract aloud before witnesses, per protocol, and I inform the debtor of the term of fulfillment. You have until the thirteenth moon completes its thirteenth turn — some weeks yet. On that night, the contract executes itself. Wherever you are. Whatever you're doing." He glanced up at the sky, gauging it. "Walking, sleeping, mid-sentence. It doesn't care. Contracts are very egalitarian that way."

The courtyard made a sound — not a gasp, something flatter, the sound of forty people exhaling at once.

Elder Cassian found his voice somewhere behind her. "Surely — honored envoy — surely there are procedures. Petitions. The temple has standing, the temple can appeal—"

"The temple," Vess said, without looking at him, "can pay for passage."

A silence of a different kind.

"Pardon?" said Cassian.

"Passage. Escort. Provision." Vess finally turned, and the whole crescent of temple seemed to lean back an inch. "Your institution holds numerous liens against the persons in this valley. Those liens remain valid regardless of what happens to any individual debtor. However — and I say this as a courtesy, since you offered me one — if the court's business here concludes messily, there will be an audit. Audits are unpleasant for everyone. Whereas if your debtor presents herself at the mountain on schedule, healthy and punctual..." He shrugged, elegant as a closing door. "No audit. Some institutions prefer certainty. I assumed yours did."

Jude turned and looked at Elder Cassian. Really looked. Eleven years she had copied prayers beside this man, mended his robes, taken his corrections, swallowed his sermons about the sanctity of obligation and the holiness of paying what you owe. And she watched his face do the arithmetic. Watched it openly, shamelessly, the abacus clicking behind his eyes: one troublemaker, the girl who'd falsified a ledger, the girl who'd asked questions about the widow's candle, weighed against forty years of clean audits.

"We will of course provide escort," Cassian said. "And provisions. And — the standard fee, I assume?"

"The standard fee," Vess agreed.

Jude did not remember walking back up the stairs. She remembered the door of the scriptorium closing behind her, and Anselm hovering uselessly, and her own voice saying, very evenly:

"Get out."

* * *

She packed by lamplight, though packing took ten minutes because she owned nothing. Three shifts. Her good boots. The bone-handled pen that had been her mother's, which the temple had let her keep because it was worthless to anyone who didn't write. A stub of wax. The little knife she used for scraping errors.

At the bottom of her bag she put the one thing she had never been able to throw away: a scrap of paper, soft as cloth with age, in her mother's handwriting. *For Jude, who will understand when she is older.* No signature. Her mother had died before explaining anything to anyone.

*Who will understand.*

Jude sat on the cot and laughed once, a hard ugly bark at the empty room. Understand. She understood plenty. She understood that the temple would pay good coin to be rid of her, that eleven years of service converted to nothing the moment her existence became a line item in someone else's book. She understood that her mother — gentle, exhausted, always cold, always rubbing her hands over the stove — had sold her before she drew breath. Sold her soul. Not metaphorically. There was a number. Four thousand ninety-one.

And underneath the grief, quieter, working like water under ice: *she was desperate. People sign terrible things when they're desperate. I've watched them sign for years.*

She hated herself for defending her already.

Near midnight, footsteps on the stairs — the slow, deliberate kind. Vess didn't knock; the lock simply gave up, which told her everything about how doors worked where he came from.

He stood in her doorway with his impossible ledger and looked around the cell-sized room with an expression she couldn't read.

"You're leaving in the morning," he said. "I'll be at the west gate at first light. Not a moment after; the schedule is contractual too."

"I heard."

He didn't go. He stood there a moment longer, head tilted, and said, in a different voice than the courtyard voice — smaller, more human, or better at pretending — "You falsified a ledger entry. Last spring. A child named Ostin, seven years old, marked for indenture over a debt of nine coppers. You changed the column."

Jude went still. "That's none of your business."

"Everything written is my business. That's what witness means." He considered her. "I mention it because it interests me. In three hundred years of collection, I have read perhaps a million entries, and most people, given a ledger and power over another life, use it the way you'd expect. They don't commit small mercies at personal cost and tell no one." He adjusted his grip on the book. "Debtors are usually boring, Sister Jude. You may not be."

"Contracted labor," she said. "Not sister."

"Not for much longer," said Vess, "either way," and the door closed behind him so softly it was worse than a slam.

* * *

She meant to slip out before dawn. Of course she meant to. She got as far as the chapel, because it stood between her cell and the wall, and because eleven years of habit pulled her feet through its doors even now — especially now.

The eternal flame burned in its iron cradle, low and orange, the way it had burned her whole life. The Quiet Flame. The god who did not answer, who did not speak, who asked only order and payment and quiet. She knelt because her knees decided without consulting her, and she stared at the fire, and she waited to feel something like farewell.

What she felt instead was the flame lean.

Just slightly. Just the way a candle leans when a draft comes in — except there was no draft, and it leaned *toward* her, the way flowers lean at windows, and for one heartbeat the whole chapel smelled of snow on far mountains, and something vast and old and buried turned over in its sleep and almost, almost spoke her—

Her real name. The thought arrived whole and cold: *that's not my name it's reaching for. My name is borrowed. It's been borrowed my whole life.*

Jude stood up so fast the bench complained. She did not pray. She walked out of the chapel of the god who had kept her eleven years and never once, not once, opened his mouth — while the god beneath the mountain sent horns and envoys and moving ink to claim her like a parcel.

Loyalty, it turned out, was just debt you hadn't read closely enough.

The west gate at first light. Vess was there, of course, precisely on schedule, slate coat immaculate, ledger balanced on his palm. Behind him the road wound down out of the valley toward the pass, toward the world she had never seen, toward the mountain that stood on the horizon like a filed document.

Behind her, on the chapel steps, the entire temple had gathered to see her off. Nobody spoke. Cassian raised one hand in a benediction performed entirely for the benefit of the envoy, and Jude looked at the faces of people she had copied prayers beside for eleven years and found nothing there she recognized.

"Time, Sister Jude," Vess said mildly.

She shouldered her bag. She took one step, then another, and felt the moment she crossed the shadow of the gate arch — felt it like a page turning.

Above the eastern ridge, pale as a fingernail paring, hung a moon that did not belong in any sky she knew. The thirteenth moon. Counting.

Thirteen turns, the envoy had said. She could see it already beginning to move.
