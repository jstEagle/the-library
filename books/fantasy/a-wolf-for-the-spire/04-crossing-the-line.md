## 4. Crossing the Line

The boundary was not a wall. That was the first thing Jude understood, standing before it with her bundle on her shoulder and Vess waiting three paces behind her like a man at a funeral he'd been paid to attend.

It was a held breath.

She had walked to the valley's edge a hundred times as a girl — every child did, dared by the older ones, dared by their own hearts — and every time the world simply stopped mattering past a certain line of stones. Not frighteningly. Politely. The way a host ends an evening. The birds turned back mid-wing. The wind lost interest. Your own feet, somehow, always found a reason to turn around, and you never once thought to ask why, because asking why was not something the valley taught.

Now she stood at that line of stones knowing why, and the knowledge sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

*Because of you,* she told herself. *Or because of what they think you are. Either way.*

The morning was gray and thin, the kind of light that made everything look provisional. Behind her, down the long green throat of the valley, the Temple of the Quiet Flame smoked gently from its morning fires, and she could just make out the scriptorium's roof where she had spent eleven years bent over prayer-copies no god had ever read. They had given her a blessing at the gate. Elder Maren had pressed two coins into her hand — passage money, not protection — and said, "May your accounts balance," which was the closest thing to tenderness the temple permitted itself, and even that had felt like a receipt.

Vess cleared his throat. "If we're going to do this, do it. The moon doesn't wait for courage."

"I know how moons work."

"Do you?" He said it without malice, which was worse. He was a narrow man in a narrow coat, gray as the morning, with ink-stained fingers and the patient posture of someone who had spent his life standing behind other people while they made terrible decisions. "You've lived thirty years inside a place where nothing answers. You're about to step into a world where everything does. I've seen grown men kneel in the road and weep at the color of grass."

"Then you can catch me when I fall."

"That," said Vess, "is technically not my function." He produced a small ledger from his coat, licked his thumb, and turned to a fresh page. "But note the date and hour: the sold one crosses willingly. That will matter later. It always matters later."

Jude looked at the stones one more time. Gray, lichen-crusted, unremarkable. On the far side, the land rose into hills she had seen from the temple tower her whole life and never once considered visiting, because considering them was like considering the far side of the moon.

Her mother had crossed this line once, carrying a secret the size of a soul.

Her mother had never come back.

"Fine," Jude said, mostly to herself. "Fine."

She stepped over.

* * *

The world hit her like cold water.

Not gradually. Not like opening a door. Like being thrown into a winter river — a full-body shock that stopped her heart, started it again, and left her gasping on the bank of her own senses. She heard herself make a sound, high and involuntary, and then the sound itself became strange, because she could hear it *twice* — once in her ears and once in some deeper ear she hadn't known she owned, where it rang out silver and enormous.

Color came first, or rather too much color. The gray morning tore open. The grass on the hillside wasn't green; it was a riot of greens, each blade arguing its own case, and above them the air itself was *full* — threads and currents and slow drifting shapes that might have been weather or might have been attention. Something vast moved overhead, formless and curious, and she felt it look at her the way you feel rain before it falls.

Then the voices.

They were everywhere. In the stones under her feet, drowsy and mineral. In the wind, gossiping. In the old boundary-stone beside her hip, muttering its single duty over and over across centuries — *turn back, turn back, turn back* — except now, with her on the wrong side of it, the words slid off her like water off oiled cloth, and the stone seemed to notice, and its muttering faltered into something very much like surprise.

"*Stop,*" Vess was saying, somewhere far away. "*Stop walking—*"

She couldn't have stopped if the valley itself had grabbed her ankles. Everything was arriving at once, decades of silence breaking like a dam, and underneath all of it — beneath the colors and the voices and the vast slow attention of the sky — there was a current running through her own blood, warm and rising, a tide answering a moon she'd never been allowed to see.

The world had been holding its breath for three hundred years.

So, apparently, had she.

She raised her hand — she didn't decide to; her body simply wanted to touch it, the way a drowning woman reaches for light — and the air *reached back*. Threads of that impossible color gathered toward her fingers like iron filings toward a lodestone, humming, eager, and she felt the hillside lean toward her, felt the grass bend though there was no wind, felt every voice within a mile swing toward her at once like a congregation hearing its name spoken—

"—*DOWN!*" Vess hit her legs from behind, arms locked around her knees, and drove her into the earth.

The world snapped.

What she had been gathering — what *she* had been gathering, some part of her understood with cold horror, this was coming *out of her* — discharged upward in a column of pressure that she felt more than saw. The grass flattened in a ring twenty feet wide. The boundary-stone cracked from crown to base with a sound like a struck bell, and its centuries-old muttering cut off mid-word. Overhead, the vast curious shape recoiled and fled, and the birds — real birds, ordinary birds, brown and frantic — burst from the hillside in a shrieking cloud.

Silence rushed back in like water filling a boot-print.

Jude lay face-down in the flattened grass, heart slamming, and discovered she was laughing. Small, broken, terrified laughter, muffled against the dirt, because her body didn't know what else to do with what had just happened.

"That," Vess said from directly beside her, flat as a ledger line, "was nearly my death."

She rolled over. He was sitting in the grass beside her, gray coat gray with dew, hair disordered for the first time since she'd met him. He was looking at the cracked stone with the expression of a clerk who has found an error so large it breaks the arithmetic.

"What did I do?"

"You reached," he said. "You reached, and the world answered, and the world does not answer gently. Do you understand what almost happened? That stone has held its post since before the temple had a name. You broke it by *wanting*." He pulled his ledger out, checked it for damage, and seemed relieved to find it intact, which told her something about his priorities. "This is why the court sends witnesses. This is why the contract specified collection rather than simple... acquisition." He looked at her then, really looked, and for one unguarded moment there was something in his face that was not procedure. "You have spent your whole life in a room with the door bricked shut. Now the door is open and the river is coming through. You cannot reach like that again. Not until you learn what you are reaching *with*."

"And who teaches me?" Her voice came out ragged. "You?"

"I record," said Vess. "I don't instruct." A pause. "But I have watched people die of exactly what you just did, and I would prefer to finish this assignment with my subject alive. So. Rules." He held up ink-stained fingers. "One: you do not reach. Two: when you feel it rise, you breathe and count, as you would count copies. Three—" he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him visibly, "—three: you tell me when it rises. Even if telling me feels like handing a weapon to a stranger. Especially then."

"Why would I trust you?"

"You shouldn't," he said. "Trust is not in my remit. But I am the only person between here and the mountain whose job requires you to arrive breathing."

Jude lay back in the crushed grass and stared up at the sky, which was still too colorful, still threaded and alive, still looking at her. Her hands were shaking. Underneath the shaking, quieter but unmistakable, the tide in her blood had not gone back down. It waited. It *wanted*. And some traitorous part of her — the part that had falsified a ledger to save a child, the part that had watched a candle relight itself and felt wonder before fear — that part was already aching to reach again.

That frightened her more than the near-death. The terror she understood. The wanting was new.

* * *

They walked.

The hills took them in folds and shoulders of green, and Jude learned to walk with her eyes half-lidded, the way you learn to walk in bright sun. Everything was still too much, but it was becoming a *manageable* too-much, the way a loud room becomes bearable if you stop trying to hear each conversation. She catalogued instead of drowning. A stream argued with itself over a fallen branch. A stand of birches hummed an old, old patience. Once, crossing a ridge, she felt something enormous pass beneath the ground — deep, slow, asleep, or pretending to be — and she gripped Vess's sleeve without meaning to and he, without comment, let her.

By late afternoon they crested a long moor, and Jude stopped counting breaths and counted something else instead: how many times the tide in her blood surged toward the horizon ahead of them. Toward the mountain. It happened on every rise. North, always north, the pull faint but constant, like a hook set behind her sternum.

"The mountain," she said.

"The court," Vess confirmed. "Where contracts go to be kept." He walked with his ledger open against his forearm, writing as he went, a habit she was beginning to suspect he couldn't turn off. "Two days' travel to the waystation at Harrow Ford, where your paperwork awaits. Then the descent."

"My paperwork."

"Every soul in the court's keeping has a file. Yours is..." he turned back a page, checked, "...thick. Older than the temple. Older than the valley's founding, if the marginalia is honest, and the marginalia is usually honest because clerks are lazy liars." He glanced at her sidelong. "There is a word written in your file that I am not authorized to speak aloud. I will say only that it is not 'Jude.'"

The bottom dropped out of her stomach so smoothly she kept walking through it, one foot and then the other, automatic, while the sentence rearranged everything around it.

*Not Jude.*

Thirty years of copying prayers in her own careful hand. Thirty years of a name signed in ledgers, whispered in blessings, stitched into her cloak. And it was borrowed. A coat hung on a hook that belonged to someone else.

"When," she said, and her voice sounded far away to her own ears, "did my mother sign?"

Vess closed the ledger gently, like a man closing a door on a sickroom. "That," he said, "is in the archive. And the archive, Miss Lockridge, is where we're going."

Ahead of them the moor rolled north into dusk, and the first star came out — except it wasn't a star, she could see now, it was something older and closer and watching, and it flickered, very slightly, when she looked directly at it.

The tide in her blood pulled north.

And far behind them, at the edge of a valley that had just lost its reason for holding its breath, the broken boundary-stone began — very softly, in a language no temple had ever taught — to sing.
