## 5. The Debt-Collector's Road

The valley let her go easier than it had ever let anything else go.

Jude had expected the boundary to fight — the way birds turned back at the invisible line, the way the temple's oldest maps simply stopped drawing the world beyond a certain contour. But when Vess's caravan crossed it on the third morning, there was no thunderclap, no wall of force. Just a sensation like stepping through a curtain of cold water, and then the world *opened*.

She gasped. She couldn't help it.

Color came first — not brighter exactly, but deeper, as if everything she'd ever seen in the valley had been painted on vellum and this was oil. The grass was green the way fire is hot. The sky went on forever and seemed to know it. And underneath it all, faint as a pulse, she felt something moving through the ground, through the air, through her own blood: a current, vast and slow, like a river that had been waiting three hundred years for a stone to be pulled out of its throat.

"Close your mouth," Vess said from the driver's bench. "You'll swallow a moth."

She didn't close her mouth. She couldn't have explained to him what she was feeling if she'd tried — that every prayer she'd ever copied, every line of careful script about the Quiet Flame who does not answer, had been written by someone standing in a room with the windows painted shut. And now the windows were open, and the wind was coming through, and it knew her name.

Her real name. The one in the contract.

"Vess." Her voice came out strange. "What did you say this feeling is?"

"I didn't say anything about it." He flicked the reins. The mules plodded on. He was a narrow man, gray-robed, with a face like an unsent letter — nothing wasted, nothing revealed. "But since you ask: the old gods are waking under the mountain. What you feel is their attention. Try not to attract more of it than necessary."

*Their attention.* Jude sat back against the crates of temple goods — candles, ledgers, a sealed letter from the Abbot that she suspected was less a recommendation than a bill of sale — and tried to breathe normally while her skin hummed like a struck bell.

They traveled east along a road that followed a river down out of the highlands. In the valley, roads were things you walked quickly and quietly; here, they were things. They had weight. Wagons creaked under loads of salt and wool and, once, a covered cart that made Jude's teeth ache when it passed, though she couldn't have said why until Vess said, without looking at her:

"Prayer-carts. Shrine deliveries. Don't stare."

"What's in them?"

"Answered prayers." He said it the way a man says *grain* or *iron*. "Bottled. For transport to the mountain."

Jude thought of the widow Marta, whose candle had relit itself mid-copying, whose prayer for an easy death had been answered within the hour. She thought of how the elders had sealed the scriptorium, how they'd looked at Jude afterward — not with wonder but with arithmetic.

"And the children?" she asked. Because she'd seen them too, these last two days. At the first village past the line, a shrine stood at the crossroads, new-built, its stones still pale. Children in white smocks sat in a row before it, very still, very clean. Their parents stood behind them with the expressions of people paying taxes: grim, resigned, faintly proud.

"You noticed those," Vess said.

"I noticed."

"Then you've noticed the whole country." He reined the mules around a rut deep enough to drown a boot. "Three hundred years of silence, and then the mountain starts answering prayers again — selectively, generously, and always with a price column. The villages figured out the terms fast. A good harvest costs a tithe. A cured fever costs a tithe. And the tithe the shrines want most—" he nodded back toward the children in white, already shrinking behind them, "—isn't grain."

"They're selling their children."

"They're *tithing* them. It's in the new liturgy. Very official. The priests call it 'offering up the first fruits.'" His voice stayed flat, but something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "The parents get a receipt. The shrine gets a child. The child gets carried to the mountain in a prayer-cart, and what happens after that is above my station."

"Above your station," Jude repeated. "You're an envoy of the court beneath the mountain. What station is above yours?"

He didn't answer. The road unrolled ahead of them, and somewhere far off, a shrine bell rang with a sound that Jude felt in her back teeth.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a ruined waystation. Vess built a small fire and cooked barley with dried plums, and it was better food than the temple had served in years, which Jude found obscenely fitting.

She watched him across the flames. He ate precisely, neither fast nor slow, and when he finished he took out a slim black book and began writing in it with a silver stylus.

"What is that?"

"My testimony record." He didn't look up. "Court protocol. Every collection must be witnessed and documented. Conditions of the subject. Condition of the route. Deviations, delays, irregularities."

"Subject," Jude said. "That's me."

"That's the term."

"Am I a deviation, Vess? An irregularity?"

Now he looked up. Firelight made his face older and younger at once. "You're the strangest collection I've made in forty years of service, if that helps. Most subjects weep. Some run. One bit me." He turned back to his book. "You falsified a ledger entry to save a child from indenture, and when your own contract came due, you packed a bag. I've been trying to decide what to write about that."

"What did you write?"

"'Subject displays atypical composure. Recommend observation continue.'"

"Observation," Jude said slowly. "Not escort. Not custody."

Vess's stylus stopped.

"The temple told me you were my captor," she pressed. "They said the court sends collectors, and collectors don't take no for an answer, and I should go quietly because the alternative was worse. That's what the Abbot said. Almost word for word."

"The Abbot," Vess said, "was eager to see the back of you, and dressed it up as theology. Which is, in fairness, his profession." He closed the book. "I am not a collector, Jude Lockridge. Collectors are another office entirely, and you should pray — carefully, cheaply, to something small — that you never meet one. I am a witness. Court protocol requires that every contract collection be observed by a neutral officer of the record, who documents the fulfillment and certifies it before the clerks. I certify. I do not compel."

"So if I ran—"

"I would write it down." A pause. "And then I would write down that you had cause. My testimony isn't a leash. It's the only honest thing in this entire arrangement, and I'd thank you to remember that, because in the months ahead it may be the only honest thing available to either of us."

Jude stared into the fire. Somewhere in the dark, the river talked to itself. The current she'd felt since crossing the line pulsed faintly in her fingertips, and she curled her hands closed around it, ashamed of how much she wanted it.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You've known me three days."

Vess was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he reached into his robe and drew out something on a cord: a small tin medallion, worn nearly smooth, stamped with a name she couldn't read in a script older than any she'd copied.

"My sister's," he said. "Sold to a shrine sixty years ago, when the old gods first stirred and our village needed a winter without famine. I was seven. I held the receipt." He tucked it away again, fast, like closing a wound. "I took this office because someone has to write down what happens to them. Someone has to keep the record true, even if the record changes nothing. Especially then."

The fire cracked. Jude thought of the ledger entry she'd falsified — one line, one child, saved — and how small and enormous it had felt.

"Your sister," she said. "Is she—"

"I don't know. No one knows. That's rather the point of the spire." He banked the fire with two efficient motions. "Sleep, subject. We reach the toll-shrine at Harrow Ford tomorrow, and you'll want your wits. The shrine there has been answering a great many prayers lately, and its appetite has grown accordingly."

* * *

Harrow Ford was worse than the first village, and worse in a way Jude understood in her bones, because she had spent her life copying the language of devotion and recognized it here wearing armor.

The shrine dominated the town. It had been a granary once — she could see the outline of the old doors bricked over — and now it rose three stories, hung with bells, its walls papered inside and out with prayer-flags gone gray from handling. A queue stretched from its entrance down the street: farmers with offerings, mothers with children, an old man holding out a rooster like a man surrendering a sword.

And beside the queue, a board nailed to the shrine wall, headed in fresh paint:

**RATES OF ANSWER**

*Rain for the season: 1 child, aged 7–10.*
*Fever lifted (household): 1 child, aged 5–8.*
*Debt forgiven (to any lender): 1 child, any age, plus firstborn status.*
*All other petitions considered. The Flame provides. The Flame requires.*

Jude read it twice. The words swam. Not because of grief — though there was that, a cold weight settling behind her ribs — but because the letters themselves *moved*, subtly, the way the widow Marta's candle-flame had moved, the way the words on the page had moved under her fingers in the sealed scriptorium. This was living text. Contract-text. She knew it the way she knew her own handwriting.

"Vess," she whispered. "That board. That's not paint."

"It's statute. The shrine writes its own rates now. The mountain lets them." He was watching her watch the board, and his hand had drifted, unconsciously, to the medallion under his robe. "Don't touch it. Reading is safe. Bargaining is not."

A woman at the front of the queue was handing over a boy of maybe eight. The boy went quietly, which was the worst part — he climbed the shrine steps on his own feet, paused at the door, and waved to his mother. She waved back. She was smiling. It was the smile of someone who has decided, in advance, not to feel a thing, and Jude had seen it before: on debtors signing indentures, on widows renouncing claims, on every face that had ever passed the temple counters where she worked.

The door swallowed him. The bells rang once, sweetly, like a transaction concluded.

Jude's hands were shaking. Not with fear. With the current — it had risen in her since the ford came into view, rising with every step closer to the shrine, and it wanted *out*. She could feel words forming behind her teeth, half-legible, urgent, the way a sneeze forms. If she opened her mouth, something would come out. Something written. Something that would rewrite the rate-board, or the shrine door, or perhaps just herself, catastrophically, in front of forty witnesses including a court officer sworn to document deviations.

She clenched her jaw. She counted. She did what she'd done her whole life in the scriptorium: she put the storm in a box and labeled it.

"Jude." Vess's voice, low, at her shoulder. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"There's light in them. Like ink catching flame." He glanced around — the queue, the bells, the shrine's shuttered windows. "Walk. Now. Slowly."

They walked. Past the board, past the queue, past a girl of six in a white smock sitting alone on a step, waiting for her family's turn, swinging her feet. Jude met her eyes and the girl smiled at her, gap-toothed, unafraid, and Jude had to grip Vess's sleeve to keep walking, because every instinct she had screamed to pick the child up and run, and running would kill them both and save no one.

At the edge of town, she finally let go of his sleeve. Her breath came ragged.

"I could have done something," she said. "Back there. I don't know how I know it, but I could have. The words were right there."

"Yes," Vess said. "And the shrine would have felt it, and the shrine reports to the mountain, and the mountain would have sent collectors instead of witnesses. Forty years, I've kept my record clean by writing down atrocities I couldn't stop. You will not make it dirtier on your second week out of a valley." He paused. His voice dropped. "But I'll tell you what I've never written in any testimony, subject: the day comes for everyone on this road when writing it down isn't enough anymore. When it was enough, I told myself, my sister would still be home. Choose your day carefully."

They made camp that night within sight of Harrow Ford's bells, which rang at odd hours, each ring marking another bargain struck. Jude lay awake listening to them, feeling the current move in her blood like a wolf pacing a fence line.

Near midnight, Vess spoke from his bedroll, without preamble, as if continuing a conversation hours old.

"The archive at Waystation Nine," he said. "Day after tomorrow. Contracts going back nine hundred years. Including, I expect, yours."

Jude sat up. "You'd show me? The original?"

"Court protocol permits a subject to review her own instrument before fulfillment." A pause. "And I permitted myself to want to show you. Write that down as a deviation, if you like."

In the dark, Jude almost laughed — a startled, rusty sound. "You're a strange man, Vess."

"I'm a thorough one. There's a difference, and the clerks hate it." The bells rang again, distant, patient. "Sleep, Jude Lockridge. Tomorrow we walk into the mountain's paperwork, and I have a feeling — and I do not put feelings in my record — that what you find in your contract will change what you think you are."

Above the eastern ridge, low and wrong and beautiful, a thirteenth moon was rising where no moon had any business being.
