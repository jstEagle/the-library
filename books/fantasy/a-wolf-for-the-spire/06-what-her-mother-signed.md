## 6. What Her Mother Signed

The waystation sat where the road split — one branch climbing toward the mountain passes, the other bending south toward country Jude had only seen as ink on maps she'd copied for temple tithe-rolls. It was built of grey stone that had been white once, with a slate roof sagging in the middle like a tired back, and it had the particular smell of all old buildings: dust and lamp oil and the ghost of a hundred thousand travelers' meals.

Inside, it was warm. That was the first thing that undid her.

Three hundred years of answered silence in the valley had taught Jude to expect coldness from institutions. The Temple of the Quiet Flame kept its scriptorium at a temperature calculated to keep the fingers stiff enough to discourage conversation. But this place — this archive, Vess called it, though it looked to Jude like an inn that had swallowed a library — had a hearth going in the front room, and behind the desk a small round woman named Odile who took one look at their travel writs and said, "Court business. Archive access, then. Third floor. Don't touch anything with a red ribbon."

"Why not?" Jude asked.

"Because it'll touch you back," Odile said, and went back to her stew.

* * *

Vess led her up two flights of stairs that creaked in a rhythm, like something walking ahead of them. He carried his testimony case under one arm — the flat black box he'd carried since the valley road, the one he opened every evening to speak into, recording his observations in the flat court-dialect that made everything sound already decided.

"You don't have to come," he said on the landing. "You could wait downstairs. Most debtors don't read their own contracts. They sign the acknowledgment page and we proceed."

"I copied prayers for eleven years," Jude said. "I'm not afraid of paper."

"That," said Vess, "is because you've never met court paper."

The third floor was one long room running the length of the building, shelved floor to ceiling, lit by lamps that burned without oil — Jude felt them now, the faint wrongness of flame fed on something other than tallow, a hum in her teeth she hadn't been able to feel a week ago. Beyond the boundary of the valley, her senses kept opening like eyes uncrusting after long sleep. She could hear the shelves breathing. Not metaphorically. The archive was alive in the slow vegetative way of very old things that had been fed too much paper.

"Contract archives are organized by debtor surname," Vess said, walking the aisle with the confidence of a man who had done this a hundred times. "Lockridge. Lockridge. Here."

He stopped. And then he didn't move.

Jude came around him and saw why.

The shelf held perhaps forty volumes bound in grey leather, each spine stamped with a name. And there were more names than she expected. Lockridges went back six generations in the valley records — farmers, mostly, a stonemason, one disgraced midwife. But the volume Vess's hand hovered before wasn't stamped *Lockridge*.

It was stamped *Vael*.

"What is that?" Jude asked.

"Your mother's maiden name," Vess said slowly. "Maren Vael. Before she married." He pulled the volume free and it was heavier than its size suggested, the way wet things are heavy. "Every contract is filed under the name the signer held at signing. I should have thought of it. I apologize. Court procedure assumes people know their own filing history."

He set it on the reading table beneath the oilless lamp, and Jude looked at it and did not open it.

Her mother's name. Maren Vael. Jude had known it, of course — it appeared on festival documents, on the deed to their cottage, in the temple rolls where her mother's debts were recorded in Jude's own careful handwriting. But knowing a name and seeing it stamped in court leather on a book that breathed were different things. This was her mother filed. Catalogued. A line item in someone else's memory.

"Open it," Vess said gently. "Or I can summarize—"

"No." Jude put her hands flat on the cover. The leather was warm. "If I'm going to be collected like a bad debt, I want to read the invoice myself."

She opened it.

* * *

The first pages were procedural. She recognized the format instantly, and that recognition was its own small horror: the contract was laid out like a prayer transcription. Header, invocation, body, seal. The same architecture the temple used for the prayers Jude had copied ten thousand times without answer. Someone had designed both. Someone had taught the temple scribes to write in the shape of a contract without ever telling them what they were imitating.

*Contract of Sale and Conveyance,* the header read. *Executed under the authority of the Broker of the Deep Court, in the four hundredth year of the Quiet.*

Then the parties.

*Vendor: Maren Vael, of the Valley of the Quiet Flame, copyist, second grade.*

*Purchaser: The Court Beneath the Mountain, by its agent and broker.*

*Subject of conveyance:* —

And here Jude's eye snagged, because the word was not what she expected. She had imagined, in the nine days since Vess arrived, a hundred versions of this document, and in every version the subject was simple. *One soul.* Her soul. A thing, singular, like a lamb or a plot of land.

But the contract said:

*Subject of conveyance: One (1) soul entire, together with one (1) name pre-natal, held in trust by the vendor, to be surrendered upon the completion of the thirteenth moon.*

"Together with one name pre-natal," Jude read aloud, because saying it made it no more real but at least made it audible.

Vess had gone very still beside her. In nine days of travel she had learned his stillnesses — the stillness of calculation, the stillness of boredom, the stillness of a man deciding how much truth a situation could hold. This was the third kind.

"Keep reading," he said quietly.

*Terms of conveyance:* The vendor surrenders all claim, title, and interest in the subject's soul and name, in perpetuity, throughout all courts and jurisdictions. The purchaser agrees to withhold collection until the thirteenth moon following the subject's majority, and further agrees to concealment clauses as follows:

There followed three full pages of the concealment clauses. Jude read them the way she read everything — fast, complete, twice — and each pass made the room colder.

The clauses forbade the vendor from speaking the subject's true name aloud. Forbade her from writing it. Forbade her from teaching it to the subject. Required her to register the subject under a substitute designation of the vendor's choosing, subject to court approval. Required annual confirmation that the substitute remained in use. Required — clause fourteen, and Jude read it four times — required the vendor to *forget the sound of the true name*, by means of a working performed at signing, so that even under compulsion she could not surrender what she did not remember.

"She named me," Jude said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else copying in another room. "She picked my name. Everyone always said — Aunt Ilsa said Maren chose it from the temple calendar, the saint's day, Jude, the faithful one. She told me that story herself. She said she held me and picked the name off the calendar because—" Because it was the nearest one. Because it was pious. Because her mother had smiled when she said it, and Jude had treasured the smile for years, the proof that even a woman drowning in debt had wanted to give her daughter something holy.

Clause fourteen meant the story was cover. Clause fourteen meant her mother had stood in some room like this one, or worse, and let a god burn the real name out of her memory, and then walked home and invented a lie about a calendar and told it to a baby for sixteen years.

"Jude." Vess's voice, careful. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing." She turned the page. Her fingers left damp prints on the old paper, and the paper drank them, and she tried not to think about what else these pages might drink.

*Consideration received:* The vendor receives discharge of all debts outstanding against the Vael household, totaling — and here was a figure, itemized across two columns, and Jude knew every entry. The winter her father died. The seed debt. The roof. The fine for the unsanctioned candle, which her mother had never explained and which had doubled overnight through some clerical cruelty. Eleven years of interest, compounded. It was all there, priced to the copper, and beside each entry the temple's own ledger numbers, matching exactly, because of course they matched. The temple's books and the court's books had always been the same book. The Quiet Flame had just been the front counter.

Eleven years of copying prayers. Eleven years of her mother's bent shoulders and silent suppers and the way she flinched whenever Jude asked about the past. All of it purchased. All of it *priced*, down to the last candle-fine, and the total came to less than the temple spent annually on lamp oil.

"They sold me cheap," Jude said.

"The price isn't the point of a contract like this," Vess said. "The price is the receipt. What your mother bought wasn't the discharge." He tapped the consideration column, near the bottom, where a final line sat apart from the itemized debts, written in a different ink — darker, almost black, the ink Jude had seen on Vess's own writs. *Plus one (1) additional consideration, sealed per Appendix C.*

"Appendix C," Jude repeated.

"Sealed appendices can only be opened by the contracting parties," Vess said. "You're the subject, not a party. Your mother would have to—"

"My mother is in the valley." Jude heard her own voice go flat and hard, a voice she didn't recognize, a scribe's voice, the voice that read terms aloud before signatures. "Under temple supervision. Where the elders who sold me my passage can watch her not tell me anything else."

Vess said nothing. Which was, from Vess, an argument.

She kept reading. There was a section on enforcement — standard, Vess had explained on the road, meaning the court's right to collect by whatever means the thirteenth moon required. There was a section on jurisdiction. And then, near the end, before the signature block, a single paragraph that was not boilerplate at all. It was written in the same dark ink as the sealed appendix line, and it was short, and it was the only sentence in the whole contract addressed to anyone besides lawyers and gods.

*A note for the subject, should she ever read this: the name was not taken from you. It was hidden in you. These are different things, however much they may feel alike. Ask the wolf.*

Jude stared at it.

"What wolf?" she said.

Vess leaned over, read it, and frowned — genuinely frowned, the first expression she'd seen on him that wasn't procedural. "That's not standard. That's not court drafting at all. Someone added that by hand." He checked the signature block. "After sealing. Look — the ink sits on top of the seal wax. Someone amended a sealed contract after execution, which is—" He stopped himself, recalibrated, and said, in his flat court voice, "Which is impossible."

"Who signed?" Jude asked, though she could see it. She'd been avoiding looking at it the way you avoid looking at a wound.

The vendor's signature was her mother's — the careful, cramped hand Jude knew from the deed to the cottage, from the rare notes left on the table, *gone to market, feed the hens*. Maren Vael. Below it, the purchaser's mark: not a name but a sigil, a spiral coiled tight around a vertical line, like a thread wound on a spindle. The Broker's mark, Vess had called it, when she'd seen it on the collection writ. *Every contract in the deep archive carries it. Every soul in the spire was signed under that spiral.*

And below both, in a third hand, in ordinary brown ink, casual as a grocery list, a witness's signature:

*Ilya.*

Nothing else. No surname. No title. Just the name, written by someone who had apparently witnessed the sale of a child's soul and couldn't be bothered to add an honorific.

"Do you know that name?" Jude asked.

"No," Vess said. Then, because he was honest in the narrow ways functionaries are honest: "It doesn't appear in any filing I've processed. Witnesses are usually court clerks. They sign with their full registry designations." He touched the edge of the page, not quite touching the name. "This person signed like someone who didn't expect to be audited. Or like someone who didn't care."

*Ask the wolf.* Jude closed the book, and the shelves around them exhaled, a long papery sigh, and somewhere on the third floor a lamp flickered as if amused.

* * *

They didn't speak on the stairs. In the front room, Odile looked up from her stew, read their faces the way innkeepers do, and ladled out two bowls without being asked. "Archive days," she said. "Everyone comes down looking like they've read their own obituary. Eat. It's mutton."

Jude ate. She was surprised to find she could. Grief, she discovered, did not actually stop the body; it just moved in alongside it, an unpaid lodger.

Vess opened his testimony case across the table and spoke his evening record into it. *"Day nine. Subject reviewed original conveyance instrument without incident. Emotional affect consistent with disclosure protocols. No irregularities observed."* A pause. His eyes flicked to the impossible amendment, the wolf, the witness named Ilya, and Jude watched him decide — visibly, deliberately — not to mention any of it.

*"No irregularities,"* he repeated, and closed the case.

"You lied to your box," Jude said.

"I recorded accurately," Vess said. "Irregularities are defined by court protocol. A hand-amended clause on a sealed instrument isn't an irregularity if no protocol covers it." He tore his bread and didn't eat it. "You're going to ask me what happens now."

"What happens now?"

"Now you sleep, because tomorrow we climb, and the pass takes four days, and the thirteenth moon is nineteen nights from full." He hesitated. It was such a small hesitation, but from Vess it was a speech. "And now you understand what you weren't supposed to understand yet, which is that the thing being collected isn't only your soul. Names have weight, Jude. In the deep court, a name is a handle. Whoever holds your true name holds the grip by which you were made to be lifted. Your mother didn't sell what you are." He looked at the fire. "She sold where you can be picked up from."

Jude sat with that while the mutton went cold in front of her. Somewhere upstairs, the archive breathed. Somewhere north, under the mountain, a spiral-coiled sigil waited on a desk for her arrival, and a drawer — she was suddenly certain of it, certain the way you're certain of weather — a drawer already held a card with her real name on it, waiting to be matched to her face.

Her whole life, she had been Jude Lockridge the way a house is an address. Correct. Functional. Assigned.

*Hidden in you,* the note said. *Not taken.*

She went to bed and lay in the dark listening to the building breathe, and she practiced, silently, being a person whose own name she did not know. It was like practicing being a room with the furniture removed. You could stand in it. You could see the outlines on the walls where things had been.

Near dawn, she finally slept, and dreamed of a valley boundary where birds turned back — except in the dream, one bird crossed. It was grey, and heavy-chested, and it flew low over the snow with its ears pinned forward, and it was not a bird at all.

It sat at the edge of the dream, watching her, and it did not leave.

She woke with the word already in her mouth, tasting of ash and patience.

"Wolf," she said, to the empty room.

Downstairs, Vess was already packing, and when she came down he handed her a bowl of porridge and a piece of news in the same motion, the way he delivered everything, as if emotion were a filing error. "Riders came in the night," he said. "Road's watched between here and the pass. Shrines on the southern route are burning their tithes rather than deliver them — the locals call the ones responsible the god-touched." He cinched his case shut. "The court wants us to take the northern road. Officially."

"And unofficially?"

Vess looked at her for a long moment, and she realized he was doing the thing he did — weighing how much truth a situation could hold — and that for the first time, the scales were tipping her way.

"Unofficially," he said, "the northern road leads to the court, and the court has never once in my career returned a debtor improved. The southern road leads to people who burn shrines." He shouldered his pack. "I'm a witness, Jude. I get to observe. Nobody says which direction I observe from."

Jude looked at the door, and the grey morning beyond it, and the road bending south toward everything her mother had paid to keep hidden.

"South, then," she said.
