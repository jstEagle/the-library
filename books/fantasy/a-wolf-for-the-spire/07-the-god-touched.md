## 7. The God-Touched

The ambush came at the ford.

Jude would remember that detail later — how ordinary it looked, the water braided silver over brown stones, the mules drinking with their ears half-drooped. She had been three days on the Debt-Collector's Road by then, and she had learned its grammar: the way Vess rode ahead and spoke to toll-keepers in a voice that made ledgers open themselves; the way villages watched the caravan pass and did not wave; the way the sky over the eastern hills had begun, faintly, to glow at dusk, like a lamp left burning behind the world.

She was copying. That was the thing about her hands — they needed to be doing it. Vess had given her a slate and a stub of chalk as a kindness or a test, and she had filled eleven slates since the valley line, copying prayers from memory because there were no books out here worth the name. The words came off her fingers easier than they ever had in the scriptorium, and that frightened her more than anything else on the road, so she didn't think about it. She copied.

The first arrow took the lead mule through the neck.

It didn't scream. Mules don't. It just folded, and the cart behind it lurched, and one of Vess's clerks — a gray man named Ossen who had not spoken to Jude once in three days — went backward off the bench with a second arrow already standing in his chest like a quill someone had planted in him for later use.

"Down," Vess said. He said it the way he said everything, without hurry, and then he was moving fast, hauling Jude off the cart by her collar and into the shallows of the ford, where the water hit her knees like a slap. "Stay under the bank. Don't run. Running is what makes them certain."

"Certain of what?"

"That we're worth killing."

The reeds along the bank were tall enough to hide a kneeling woman, and Jude crouched in them with the water soaking through her skirt and her slate clutched against her chest like a child she'd decided to save. Across the ford, the remaining clerks had formed a ring around the second cart, and Vess stood in front of them with his hands *open* — empty, palms out, the posture of a man making an argument rather than a fight.

They came down out of the trees on the far side, and Jude's first thought was that they were wrong. Everything about them was wrong. They wore no colors. Their weapons were farm tools and hunting bows and one curved sword that had seen better centuries. But it was their eyes —

Their eyes were lit.

Not glowing, exactly. Not the way candles glow. It was more that light was happening *behind* them, somewhere deep, the way the dusk hills glowed, the way the scriptorium candle had relit itself three hundred years of silence after it should have stayed dead. Seven of them, maybe eight, fanning out across the water with the stones barely ringing under their feet, and every one of them looking at the caravan the way you look at a debt you've come to collect.

"Envoy," said the one with the curved sword. A woman, broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair cut short as a soldier's. Her voice carried over the water without effort, the way a bell carries. "Vess of the Ninth Bench. We know your seal. We know your route. We've been reading your road for two days."

"Then you know I travel under truce," Vess said. "Witness protocol. I carry no enforcement writ."

"You carry *her*." The sword tipped toward the reeds. Toward Jude. "We felt her before we saw the carts. Something under the mountain is moving up the Collector's Road wrapped in borrowed flesh, and we are done waiting to learn what."

"They'll kill you," Vess said quietly, sideways, to the reeds. "Don't argue. Don't explain. Explanations read as guilt."

But Jude was already standing up.

She didn't decide to. Later she would try to reconstruct the decision and find nothing there — just the water running off her skirts, the chalk dust still on her fingers, and eight pairs of god-lit eyes turning to her all at once, and some animal part of her understanding that crouching in the reeds made her look exactly like what they thought she was: something hidden, something sent.

"I'm not a court agent," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it. "I'm being *collected*. There's a difference."

The woman with the sword crossed the ford toward her. Up close the light behind her eyes was worse — or better; Jude couldn't decide — because up close she could see it wasn't constant. It flickered. It guttered like a flame deciding whether to stay, and when it guttered the woman's face underneath was just a face, tired and sunburned and maybe forty years old, with a scar through one eyebrow.

"Hold still," the woman said.

"I'd really rather—"

"*Hold still.*"

A hand closed around Jude's jaw, callused and strong, tilting her face up to the gray afternoon light. The woman studied her the way Vess studied contracts — line by line, looking for the clause that mattered. Then her grip shifted, caught Jude's chin, and turned her head, and her thumb pressed hard against the skin below Jude's left ear.

Jude hissed. It hurt — it hurt like a bruise she'd never known she had, a deep ache waking up under pressure.

And the woman went very still.

"Sefa?" said one of the others, wading closer. "What is it?"

Sefa — because that was clearly who this was, the name arriving in Jude's mind with the certainty of a copied word — Sefa pulled her thumb back and stared at it as if it had told her something. Then she looked at Vess, and the light behind her eyes flared bright enough that Jude stepped back without meaning to.

"She's marked," Sefa said. "The sold-mark. Old style. Pre-Reform ink." She spat into the water, which seemed to be a whole sentence in whatever language these people spoke among themselves. "You're transporting *property*, Envoy. You stood there talking truce while carrying a soul with a court brand on it."

"I'm transporting a witness," Vess said. "The mark is why. Read your own histories, Sefa of the Ford. The court doesn't send witnesses for property. It sends them for *claims*."

"And whose claim?"

Vess paused. Jude watched him weigh it — actually watch the weighing happen behind his face, the scales tipping — and then he said, "That isn't mine to disclose. Ask her."

Eight god-lit faces turned to Jude again.

Her mouth was dry. The water was cold. Somewhere behind her a clerk was doing something merciful with the dead mule, and the sound of it kept trying to pull her attention away from the moment she was standing in, which was the moment, she understood dimly, where her whole life tilted onto a new axis.

"My mother signed a contract," Jude said. "Before I was born. I found out nine days ago. I spent my whole life before that copying prayers in a temple where nobody's prayer has been answered in three hundred years, and I didn't know why that felt like drowning until the prayers started getting answered again." She stopped. Steadied. "I'm not the court's. I'm the court's *cargo*. And if any of you can tell me the difference matters, I'd genuinely like to hear it, because I have not heard a single convincing argument yet from anyone with power over me."

Silence on the water. The reeds ticking against each other. One of the rebels laughed — short, surprised, quickly smothered.

Sefa didn't laugh. She reached out again, slower this time, and touched the mark below Jude's ear with one fingertip, feather-light, and Jude felt the ache answer it — felt something under her skin lean toward the touch like a plant toward sun, and saw Sefa feel it too, saw her breath catch.

"It answers us," Sefa said softly. To her people, not to Jude. "The mark answers the god-touched. Do you understand what that means? The old ink was cut with living fire, back when the gods still walked. It remembers them." Her eyes came up to Jude's, and for a moment the light behind them steadied instead of guttering, and Jude felt seen in a way that had nothing to do with her face. "Girl. What's your name?"

"Jude Lockridge."

"Is it yours?"

The question went through her like the arrow had gone through the mule. She thought of the contract she hadn't read yet — Vess had it sealed in his dispatch case, and she had asked twice and been refused twice — and she thought of her mother's face when Jude had asked her, only once, *what did you sign*, and the way her mother had looked at the floor as if the floor were a ledger she could read her way out of.

"I don't know," Jude said. It was the most honest thing she'd said aloud in years, and it cost her, and she watched the cost land on Sefa's face and be recognized there, matched, answered.

Sefa turned to Vess. "The witness travels with us."

"The witness travels with *me*," Vess said. "Protocol—"

"Your protocol protects the court's claim. I'm telling you the claim is void. Sanctuary law predates your benches, Envoy. Any soul bearing the sold-mark may claim sanctuary with the god-touched, and no collector may cross our fire to take them." She smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile, but it was an honest one, which after nine days of Vess's courtesies felt like clean water. "Unless you'd like to test whether your truce holds against eight of us and whatever's left of the god of fords, who woke up last spring and is *extremely* territorial."

Vess looked at the dead mule. At Ossen, whom no one was going to bring back. At Jude, standing in the shallows with chalk dust on her fingers and a brand under her ear that leaned toward strangers like a child toward a lit window.

Something in his expression settled, the way a ledger settles when the columns finally balance.

"Witness protocol requires me to record voluntary cooperation," he said. "If the witness states she goes willingly, my report survives. If she states otherwise—" he glanced at the curved sword "—my report survives differently. Witness. For the record. Do you go willingly?"

Jude looked at Sefa. At the seven others on the far bank, their borrowed fires guttering and flaring, farmers and poachers and one very young one who couldn't have been older than sixteen and was staring at Jude's mark with naked, hungry hope. Strangers. All of them strangers. Every person she had ever known had known her as the temple's quiet girl, the debtor's daughter, the one who copied beautifully and asked nothing — and not one of them had ever offered her anything except another page to copy.

These people had known her ten minutes. They had put an arrow through a mule and a clerk to reach her, which was terrible, and they had looked at the brand on her soul and called it a *claim*, which was the first time in her life anyone had treated what was done to her as something done *to* her rather than something she simply was.

"I go willingly," Jude said.

* * *

They burned the carts. Sefa's people were efficient about it — the toll records, the dispatch case's outer seals, everything with the court's stamp went into the flames while Vess stood watching with the expression of a man memorizing damages for later reimbursement. The dead clerk they buried above the waterline, and the young one — Doss, his name was Doss — said words over the grave that weren't in any rite Jude knew, words that made the ground hum faintly, like a struck string, and Jude had to look away because her eyes were stinging and she refused to examine why.

They walked west as the light died. Sefa set a pace that assumed everyone had something to outrun, and Jude, who had never in her life walked more than the distance between the scriptorium and the refectory, discovered muscles she had been ignoring her entire existence and made a private vow never to complain where anyone could hear.

Toward dusk they came up out of the river valley into hills full of pines, and there — tucked into a fold of land like a coin in a palm — was the camp.

Jude had expected tents. What she found was a village that had learned to hide: low stone houses roofed with turf, smoke channeled through split logs so it rose cold and scentless, children — actual children, dozens of them — moving between the buildings with the quick purposeful silence of people practicing being invisible. And everywhere, everywhere, the light. Behind eyes, cupped in lamps that burned without oil, threaded through a loom where an old woman wove cloth that shimmered faintly, wrong-colored, like a memory of color rather than color itself.

"We're what the shrines spit out," Sefa said, walking beside her now. She had given Jude dry socks and bread and neither kindness nor apology for the mule. "Every village on this road tithes somebody. Firstborn, sickbed-cured, dream-touched — whoever the shrine marks, the shrine takes. Most of them come back changed. Some come back hollowed. The ones who come back *lit*—" she gestured at the camp, at all of it, "—find each other. That's all we are. The leftovers of the gods' return."

"And the court?"

"The court calls us heresy." Sefa's voice flattened. "Three hundred years they filed the gods asleep and sold the silence, prayer by prayer, and now the gods are waking up and every contract written in the dark is coming due, and the court would rather burn us than admit what they owe." She stopped at the door of a long low house, firelight leaking gold under its lintel, and turned to Jude. "You'll eat. You'll sleep. Tomorrow we talk about what your mark can do, because I've felt it answer and I think you haven't even scratched it. But tonight—" and here her voice did something unexpected, dropped its edge, went almost gentle, "—tonight you're just a sold girl under sanctuary fire, and nobody under this roof will ask you to sign anything. Ever. That's the law here. The first law."

Jude stood in the doorway with the warmth on her face and the bread going cold in her hand, and understood that she was crying only when a tear hit the crust.

She couldn't have explained it. Nobody had struck her. Nobody had threatened her. It was only that *nobody will ask you to sign anything* was the single kindest sentence anyone had spoken over her in twenty-two years, and it had come from a stranger with a stolen sword and a god flickering behind her eyes like a lamp in wind.

Inside, the long house was full — thirty people, forty, eating from shared bowls, arguing softly about grain and routes and whether the god of the eastern hills was benevolent or merely bored. A space opened for her near the fire without anyone appearing to arrange it. Someone put a bowl in her hands. Someone else, a girl not much older than the youngest scribes back home, touched Jude's arm below the mark — lightly, questioningly — and when Jude didn't flinch, smiled at her with eyes full of banked light and said, "Welcome, sister. Who woke in you?"

"I don't know," Jude said. "Nothing. Yet."

"Not nothing." The girl shook her head, certain as scripture. "The mark doesn't answer for nothing. Something in you is listening."

Jude ate. The fire was warm. Around her, strangers argued theology the way her temple elders had argued punctuation, and for the first time since the candle relit itself in the scriptorium, since the envoy arrived with her name in his case, since the boundary line and the cold flood of the world's true colors — for the first time since any of it, the fear in her chest went quiet.

Which was, of course, when Sefa sat down beside her, close enough that the light behind her eyes lit them both, and said:

"Now. Tell me about your mother's signature. Because I've seen that contract before, Jude Lockridge. And the last soul it was written for didn't survive the learning of it."
