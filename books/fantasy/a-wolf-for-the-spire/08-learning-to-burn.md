## 8. Learning to Burn

The rebels' camp lived in the hollow of a dead river, and Jude learned its grammar before she learned its people.

The riverbed itself came first — a wide scar of gray stone and pale silt winding down from the hills, dry for so long that trees had grown in it, their roots gripping cracked clay like fingers around a throat. The rebels had built their shelters into the banks, half-buried, roofed with turf. From the ridge above, the camp was invisible. From inside, it felt like living in the throat of something that had once spoken and chosen silence.

Sefa's people numbered perhaps sixty. Jude counted them the way she counted everything now — automatically, in columns, because that was how her mind had been rebuilt. Forty-one adults. Eleven children. Nine elders. A cook named Brannic who sang to his pots. A girl of about ten who followed Jude at a distance for two full days before working up the courage to ask if the mark of the sold hurt.

"No," Jude said. "It itches."

That was a lie. It burned, some nights, low in her sternum, like a coal that remembered being a fire. But the girl's face had been so hopeful that Jude had reached for comfort before honesty, and only afterward did she notice what she'd done — that she had *chosen* a sentence the way she now chose everything, weighing it, testing its weight, aware of its power. A year ago she would have simply spoken.

She was still deciding whether that was growth or damage.

* * *

Her training began on the third morning, in the riverbed's widest bend, with a woman named Kess who had hands like a carpenter and no patience whatsoever.

"Show me what you did to Vess," Kess said.

"I didn't *do* anything to Vess. I was falling and he caught me and I — the world went loud, and then he was somewhere else."

"Somewhere else." Kess looked at the sky as if petitioning it. "You threw a grown man thirty feet with a word you don't remember saying, and your account is *somewhere else*."

"I was terrified."

"Good. Terror's honest. Now do it again on purpose."

Jude spread her hands. Nothing happened. She tried to feel what she'd felt on the road — that cold-water plunge, the sense that the air was full of waiting — and got nothing but the ordinary morning, birdsong, the smell of woodsmoke from Brannic's fires.

Kess watched her fail for an hour. Then she sat down on a boulder, pulled bread from her coat, and said, "You're reaching for it wrong. You're reaching like it's outside you. It's not. It's language."

"Language?"

"You're a scribe. You spent your life copying prayers. What is a prayer?"

"A petition. A request directed to—"

"A *sentence with force behind it*." Kess bit the bread. "The old gods answered prayers. That means prayers were operations. Instructions. The valley didn't block magic, girl. It blocked *grammar*. Nothing you wrote in there could take, because nothing you wrote had anywhere to land." She pointed at Jude's chest, at the place where the mark burned. "You've got landing space now. So stop reaching and start *drafting*."

Jude stared at her. "Drafting what?"

"Reality. It's a text. Everything is. The stone, the air, the fire in the cookpot — all of it is written, all the way down, in a hand older than gods. Most people can't read it. The god-touched can read a line or two. But you—" Kess leaned forward. "You spent twenty years copying sacred text without ever once being permitted to change a word. Don't tell me you don't know what it is to look at a sentence and see exactly how it should be different."

Jude's whole body went still.

Because Kess was right, and the rightness of it landed in her like a key turning. She had spent her life correcting other scribes' errors in her head. She had spent her life reading a ledger entry and knowing — instantly, helplessly, with a clarity that had gotten her in trouble — how it *ought* to read instead. The falsified entry that saved Tam's child had not been an act of magic. It had been an act of grammar.

"Try this," Kess said. She set a river stone on the flat rock between them. "Don't push it. Don't *want* it to move. Look at it, and find the sentence it's sitting in. Then amend."

"Amend."

"You're a copyist. Use your own tools."

Jude looked at the stone.

And this time, instead of reaching, she *read*.

It was hard to describe even to herself, afterward. The stone did not grow words on its surface. But looking at it with the intention Kess had named, she perceived — beneath the gray, beneath the weight, beneath the plain fact of its stoneness — a kind of sentence. Not in any alphabet. More like the *shape* of a sentence, the way a ledger entry has a shape before you read it: this, here, thus, now. *Stone. Ground. Resting.*

*Resting*, she thought. And felt, with a copyist's instinct, the small false note in it — the way a forged signature has a false note. The stone wasn't resting. Resting was a living thing's word. The stone was only *being placed*.

She reached into the sentence the way she'd have reached for a pen, and struck the word, and wrote *falling*.

The stone dropped through the rock beneath it as if the rock had become smoke, and kept falling, and Jude felt the world lurch — and caught herself, horrified, and hastily rewrote *resting* back into the sentence, and the stone hung in midair six feet down, embedded in nothing, humming faintly.

Kess said nothing for a long moment.

Then she started to laugh — a low, delighted, slightly frightening laugh. "Oh," she said. "Oh, Sefa is going to either love you or be very afraid. Possibly both."

* * *

The price revealed itself that evening.

Jude had spent the afternoon practicing small things — turning a cup's *empty* to *full* and back, warming water by amending *cold* to *warm*, teaching herself the feel of the sentences under the world. Each working left her breathless and giddy, drunk on a fluency she'd never been permitted in ink. She was still high on it when she sat down by the fire across from Vess, who was writing in his testimony book by firelight, and reached for the bread at her elbow and found her own hands strange to her.

"Jude," Vess said.

She looked up. He was watching her with an expression she'd learned to read on the road: the envoy's careful face with something human showing through the seams.

"You've been casting since noon."

"Yes."

"How many workings?"

"I don't — a dozen? Twenty. Why?"

He closed his book. "What did you eat this morning?"

"Porridge. Why?"

"What did Brannic sing while you watched him cook?"

Jude opened her mouth. Closed it. Reached for the memory and found — nothing. Not a fog, not a blur. A clean absence, shaped exactly like a memory, the way a removed entry leaves a gap in a ledger that is itself a kind of record. She knew she had once known. She could not know what she had known.

"What's the name of the girl who asked about your mark?"

"Tam—" No. That was the child in the valley. The girl here, the follower, the one with the hopeful face. Jude searched. The search came back empty. She knew the girl existed. She could not retrieve her. "I don't know her name."

Vess was quiet. Then he said, "The god-touched who burn out young — Sefa's people call it *spending*. Every working is paid for. The court's magic runs on contracts because contracts are *recorded* — the debt exists on paper, so it persists. Your magic has no paper. So it takes the payment out of the only ledger you carry." He tapped his own temple. "It takes memories. The ones you feed it."

"The ones I—"

"Choose," Vess said. "That's the cruelty of it. It won't take what you refuse to give. It waits for you to pick. And you'll always pick something you've decided you can live without. And you'll be wrong, every time, because you decided *before you knew what it was worth*."

Jude sat with that by the fire, and the fire cracked, and somewhere in the camp the girl whose name she had lost was laughing at something, and the sound was lovely and anonymous.

*Why didn't anyone tell me?*

But she knew why. Because she'd been drunk on it. Because a person drunk on power does not read the terms. Because she had spent her whole life watching desperate people sign things they hadn't read, and now she had signed herself, twenty times over, without once looking at the price column.

*Every prayer ever answered has a price column,* whispered a thought she didn't yet know where she'd gotten. She filed it away, uneasily, like an entry in someone else's ledger.

* * *

She got better at choosing. That was the trap of it.

Kess taught her to work small. Vess taught her to work *recorded* — he began writing down her memories before she spent them, transcribing her dictations into his testimony book, so that at least a copy existed outside her head. "It's not the same," he warned. "A written memory is a body without a ghost. You'll know the facts and feel nothing." But it was something, and she took it.

She spent the taste of her mother's stew rather than the memory of her mother's face. She spent the smell of the temple scriptorium — ink and tallow and cold stone — rather than the memory of Tam. She spent the sound of her own voice at nine years old, which she had been hoarding without knowing it, and did not notice its absence for two days, and when she noticed, she sat down in the riverbed and cried for an hour, and Kess sat beside her without touching her, which was somehow exactly right.

And still — still — the power was *beautiful*.

That was the thing she could not make Vess understand, though she tried, there by the fire, night after night. It wasn't that she was weak. It was that the world was a draft, and she could *revise*. Every injustice she had ever copied into a ledger and been powerless to touch — she could touch now. A debt was a sentence. A sentence could be amended. She lay awake at night feeling the sentences of the camp around her, the *owing* and the *fleeing* and the *hiding*, and her fingers itched with the grammar of a better world.

"Then why do you look frightened?" Vess asked her once.

"Because I'm starting to understand the court," she said. "If you can rewrite anything, the question stops being *can you* and becomes *who decides*. And I've seen what happens when the answer is *whoever holds the pen*."

Vess wrote that down. She didn't ask him not to.

* * *

On the sixth night, Sefa came to her fire.

The rebel leader sat down across from Jude without invitation, which Jude had learned was her way — she treated space the way she treated people, as territory to be entered. Up close, by firelight, the god-light in Sefa's eyes was quieter than Jude had first taken it for. Not a flame. An ember. Something being carefully kept alive.

"Kess says you're a natural," Sefa said. "Vess says you're a danger to yourself. They're both right, which is why I'm here and not them."

"Sefa—"

"You spent a memory today. I watched you do it. The one about the sound of your own voice." Sefa's gaze didn't waver. "You gave it up for a working that warmed a kettle. That's not discipline, Jude. That's waste. You're spending like a woman who's never been poor."

The words stung because they were true.

"You think I don't understand the hunger," Sefa went on. "The gods are waking, and their power is coming loose in the world like coins from a torn purse, and everyone who can grab a handful thinks they've been *chosen*. The god-touched burn out by thirty. The shrines are eating villages. And here you are, the most fluent natural scribe I've ever seen, burning pieces of yourself to heat water." She leaned forward. "You want to spend? Spend on something that matters. There's a shrine two days east that's hoarding answered prayers — souls, Jude, stored like grain in jars. We're going to hit it. When we do, I want your grammar on our side."

Jude looked into the fire.

She thought of the girl whose name she had spent. She thought of Tam, and the ledger entry she had falsified in another life, and how small that mercy had been and how much it had cost her to make it and how it had been, nevertheless, the only thing she'd ever done that felt like herself.

"Souls in jars," she said quietly. "Children?"

"Some of them."

Jude looked up. "Then I'll come. But Sefa — if we get them out, we get them *out*. Not studied. Not used. Not filed somewhere for the rebellion's own ledger. Out."

Sefa smiled, and it was the smile of someone agreeing to terms she had no intention of honoring, and Jude — who had spent her life reading signatures — saw it, and told herself she was wrong.

"Out," Sefa said. "I swear it by the ember."

That night Jude lay awake under the cold stars, doing inventory. She listed what she still had: her mother's face. Tam's laugh. The weight of the first pen she'd ever held. Her own name — *Jude*, though the word had begun to feel like a coat that fit strangely, and she did not yet know why.

She listed what she had spent, and found the list already going soft at the edges, entries fading into the clean blank gaps that were themselves a record.

Then she amended the last item in her mental ledger — *I am careful* — striking it, and writing in its place the only sentence she trusted anymore:

*I am a woman with a pen, and I must decide, every day, what it is for.*

Two days east, a shrine full of stolen souls was waiting. She slept, at last, and dreamed of nothing at all, which frightened her more than any dream could have.
