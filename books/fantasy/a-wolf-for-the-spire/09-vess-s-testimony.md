## 9. Vess's Testimony

*Entry the ninth. The subject sleeps.*

Vess dipped his pen a fourth time and watched the ink crawl down toward his knuckle before writing. The caravan fire had burned low. Across it, Jude Lockridge lay curled in her cloak with one hand open against the dirt, palm up, like a beggar or a woman catching rain. Her magic had settled after the incident on the boundary road — she had not incinerated anyone since — but he could still see it in her sleep: a faint shimmer at her fingertips, the way heat shivers above a summer road.

He wrote: *The subject demonstrates uncontrolled thaumaturgic discharge consistent with an unschooled inheritance of the first order. Recommend continued observation under Article Twelve. Recommend no intervention in subject's dreams, which appear to be monitored by parties unknown.*

That last part was true and also useless. Everything he wrote was true and also useless. He had been composing this testimony for eleven days now, and every line of it performed the same trick: it said exactly what the court required while saying nothing at all.

*The subject asks questions. The subject shows undue interest in the terms of her own contract. The subject has begun to ask about other contracts.*

There. That was the sentence he should not have written.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at the rest of the page — the careful, bloodless entries above it, nine days of them, each one technically accurate and arranged like bricks in a wall — and understood what he had been building. A case. Not for Jude Lockridge. Against her. Every observation he recorded would be read by clerks who did not watch her eat, did not hear her apologize to her guide for nearly boiling the marrow in his bones, did not see her flinch when she passed a shrine and felt its hunger lean against her like a drunk leaning on a stranger.

The court did not read testimony to learn things. It read testimony to find permission.

*Correction,* Vess wrote, and struck the last clause through with a single clean line. *The subject has begun to ask questions about the nature of contractual obligation itself. This observer declines editorial interpretation.*

But that was a lie, wasn't it. That was exactly what he'd been doing since the waystation. Interpreting. Sitting up past the second watch with her contract-copy spread on his knee — he was permitted to carry it; he was the witness; the witness carries the evidence — reading the clauses the way he'd been trained to read them, and finding, underneath all that beautiful archaic formal language, the same thing he always found.

A frightened woman. A bad year. A signature pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear it.

*She was desperate,* the contract said, in the old formula, *and being desperate, did sell—*

And the court's position, three hundred years of precedent thick, was that desperation did not void a sale. Desperation *established* the market. Desperation was why the buying desk existed at all.

Vess put down the pen and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands until the fire turned to orange ghosts.

His sister's name had been Maren.

* * *

Not his sister, properly. His great-great-aunt, four generations back, though in Vess's family she had never once been referred to as anything but his sister, because that was how the family kept the wound open — you didn't let it scab over with genealogy. You called her *sister* so that every child born into the Ives line understood that the sold girl belonged to them, personally, forever.

He was seven when they told him. Family instruction was conducted at the kitchen table in their village house outside Drennmark, and his grandmother did the telling, because his mother could never get through it without weeping and his father refused to be present at all.

"Maren was twelve," his grandmother said, laying a scrap of yellowed paper on the table between the bread and the salt. It was a copy — the original sat in the deep stacks beneath the mountain, where all originals went — but copies were legal things in their world, and this one had been carried out of the notary's office in 1187 and never surrendered. "The harvest failed twice. Your great-great-great-grandmother had four children and a husband coughing blood into a rag. A man came from the mountain. He wore grey. He was very polite."

"What did he say?"

"He said, *Madam, the court is prepared to offer relief.*" His grandmother's voice went flat and precise, doing the Broker's cadence the way you do a snake's rattle. "Relief. That was the word. Debts forgiven. Grain advanced. A physician for the father. And all it would cost was one daughter's soul, collectible upon her natural death or the completion of the thirteenth moon, whichever came first, per Schedule C."

"What's Schedule C?"

"The schedule," his grandmother said, "that makes everything legal."

Seven-year-old Vess had looked at the paper, at the signature of a woman he knew only from a grave-marker, and asked the question that had apparently been asked in his family at that table for four generations, by every child, always:

"But couldn't she have said no?"

And his grandmother had given the answer every child got, which was yes, of course she could have said no, she signed freely, the court does not compel, and then she had added the part that no clerk beneath the mountain would ever write down, the part that Vess would spend forty years trying to forget and one night in a caravan finally failing to:

"Yes. She could have said no. And her husband would have died in the spring, and two of your aunts would have died in the famine winter, and Maren would have grown up hungry and buried half her family, and she would still have been ours." His grandmother folded the copy along its old creases. "They don't force you. They just make sure that by the time they arrive, saying yes is the only door left standing. Then they take the door as evidence of your consent."

He had gone to work for the court anyway. People always asked that part later, with a particular tilt of the head, and Vess had a stock of answers — the salary was good, the training was unmatched, what else does a clever boy from a debt-haunted family do — and all of them were true and none of them were the truth.

The truth was that at nineteen, standing in the recruitment hall in Drennmark, he had believed something almost unbearably naive: that if people like him *went inside*, the system would have to look at people like Maren. That a witness with a conscience was a crack in the wall. That the ledgers were so vast only because no one who loved the sold had ever learned to read them.

Forty years later he knew better. The ledgers were vast because everyone who read them learned to call Maren *a completed transaction*. The wall did not crack. The wall hired you, gave you a grey coat and a title and a pen that never ran dry, and let you tell yourself that recording a thing accurately was the same as serving it neutrally was the same as being innocent of it.

He had risen to witness-of-record, third circle. There were perhaps thirty witnesses in the court's service. Their function was ancient and specific: when the court collected a soul, protocol demanded an observer, someone outside the transaction whose testimony established that collection had occurred lawfully. The witness did not guard the asset. The witness did not negotiate. The witness *watched*, and wrote, and his writing made it real.

For three centuries, witnesses had escorted assets to the mountain. Most of them wrote nine days of brick-wall testimony just like his.

None of them, as far as Vess knew, had ever written what he was about to write.

* * *

Jude stirred in her sleep and murmured something. The shimmer at her fingertips brightened, and the nearest stone in the fire ring glowed briefly cherry-red, and then faded, and she slept on. Even her dreams leaked power. The guide, Hallik, had taken to sleeping upwind of her, which Vess considered wise.

*Addendum,* he wrote. *This observer notes that the subject's power responds most readily to written and spoken language, and that her instinctive castings take grammatical form — commands, declarations, amendments. This observer further notes that no such aptitude is recorded among the god-touched rebels currently active in the northern provinces, and that the subject's contract, reviewed in full at the Waystation of Saint Audren, contains an unusual rider: the purchased item is specified as "the soul entire, together with whatsoever name it bore before its naming."*

He stopped. In eleven days of testimony he had not once committed that rider to the record. It sat in his memory where he had filed it the night he read it, in a drawer marked *things that change the meaning of everything else*, and he had kept it there because he did not yet know whose purposes disclosure would serve. That was the functionary's oldest skill, knowing which drawer to use, and tonight it disgusted him.

*Whatsoever name it bore before its naming.*

Somewhere under the mountain there was a filing cabinet — he had seen the stacks once, on his oath-day, endless aisles of them receding into dark — and in one of those cabinets was a slip of paper with a girl's true name on it, the name her mother sold before her mother ever spoke it aloud, the name Jude Lockridge had never heard and could not miss. The court held it the way it held everything: as property.

And Vess, keeper of drawers, had decided somewhere around midnight that he was tired of holding things for them.

*Private memorandum, non-evidentiary,* he wrote, in the small cipher he had not used since his oath-day, the cipher of a boy who had entered the service intending to crack walls. *Observations withheld from official record, to be produced at such time as this observer judges the court's interests and justice's interests to have permanently diverged: One. The Lockridge contract specifies purchase of a pre-natal name, indicating the seller knew or suspected the child's nature. Two. The court's collection timeline coincides with the thirteenth moon's completion, suggesting the collection is not routine recovery of an asset but retrieval of a thing the court believes will soon become dangerous or valuable beyond price. Three. The court does not send witnesses on routine recoveries.*

Four, he wrote, and his pen hesitated over the page the way it had not hesitated over anything in forty years.

*Four. This observer's grandmother once asked him what he intended to do with his life, and he said he intended to matter, and she said, mind you don't only matter to them. This observer has now served the court for four decades without once asking himself whom he mattered to. The subject asked him yesterday, on the road, whether he had chosen this duty. He answered that duty chooses. This was a coward's answer. The truthful answer is that he chose, at nineteen, to believe that watching was a neutral act, and has spent forty years declining to test that belief. The test begins here.*

He read it back. It was not testimony. Testimony was facts arranged for a reader. This was a confession, and confessions were not admissible in any court he knew, including the one that employed him.

Good, he thought. Let it stand as the first thing I've written in forty years that no clerk can file.

* * *

"You're still awake."

Jude's voice, rough with sleep. She had turned toward the fire without opening her eyes — he could tell from the angle of her face — and for a moment he considered closing the memorandum book, tucking it away, resuming the grey-coat posture that was more armor than clothing by now.

Instead he set it open on his knee, facing the fire, and let her see the cipher.

"You write in your sleep," she said. "I mean — your hand. When you think. You move the pen against your leg like it's still going."

"A habit. Forty years of habit."

"That's a long time to keep records."

"It's a long time to keep anything." He fed a stick to the fire. Sparks climbed. "You asked me yesterday if I chose this duty."

Her eyes opened. In the firelight they were the color of strong tea, ordinary, except that when she looked at anything too long the ordinary color seemed to deepen, as though the world behind her eyes were coming into focus through her. "You said duty chooses."

"I lied." He heard himself say it and felt, absurdly, lighter. "Or rather, I gave the answer I've given for forty years, and it occurred to me tonight, writing, that it was never true. I chose. Nineteen years old, full of the idea that I could be a good man inside a bad machine."

"And can you?"

"I'll tell you when I find out." He turned the memorandum toward her, then thought better of it and closed it. Some things needed to be offered whole or not at all. "I had a sister. Not by blood — by grief. Sold, four generations back, for grain and a physician's visit. My family kept her memory the way other families keep saints' days. When I took the court's coin, my grandmother told me to mind who I mattered to."

Jude sat up slowly. The shimmer at her fingers was steady now, a low warm light, like banked coals listening. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because in nine days of official testimony I have described you as an asset with anomalous properties, and because tomorrow or the day after, this road takes us past shrines that are doing things the court will want recorded favorably, and because I have begun to suspect that the difference between a witness and an accomplice is roughly the width of a pen." He looked at the fire rather than at her; looking at her had started to feel like looking at the sun. "I am not yet your ally, Miss Lockridge. I want to be honest about that. But I am no longer certain whose testimony I'm keeping."

She studied him for a long moment. He had the peculiar sensation of being read — not observed, *read*, word by word, like a document someone was deciding whether to amend.

"All right," she said at last. "Then answer me one question honestly. The contract. The one with my name on it. Is mine the only kind in those archives? Or are there thousands?"

Vess thought of the deep stacks. The aisles of cabinets receding past torchlight. The soft sound the clerks made, all day, every day, the sound he had always taken for industry and recognized only lately as something closer to appetite.

"There are hundreds of thousands," he said. "Every one of them signed freely. That's the first thing they teach you. Signed. Freely."

"And the second thing?"

"The second thing they teach you," Vess said, "is not to ask about the circumstances."

Far down the valley road behind them, a bell began to ring — a shrine bell, tolling the hour, except that shrines had stopped keeping hours weeks ago and this one had been ringing for some time. Jude's head came up. The light at her fingertips sharpened to a point.

"That's not an hour bell," she said. "That's a summons. Something's calling people in. Vess — that village we passed this morning, the one with the empty fields. All the children were at the shrine. All of them."

He was already reaching for his boots, and already, traitorously, automatically, reaching with the other hand for his pen — because a witness records, because recording is reflex, because forty years of brick walls do not come down in a night —

—and then he left the pen where it lay, and picked up his coat instead, and followed the girl whose soul his court owned toward whatever the shrine wanted.

Some testimonies, he decided, were best gathered running.
