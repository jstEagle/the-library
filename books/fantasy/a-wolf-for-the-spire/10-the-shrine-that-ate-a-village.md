## 10. The Shrine That Ate a Village

The village of Harrow's Bend had no name for what it had become, so the rebels called it a shrine, and the shrine called itself nothing at all.

Jude saw it first from the ridge road, three days after Sefa's camp moved out of the high pines. It sat in the crook of the river valley like a thing that had grown there rather than been built — a mound of white stone studded with candles, tiered and terraced, rising maybe forty feet above the fields. Every surface burned. Not one candle guttered. From this distance the light should have been beautiful, and was, in the way a fever is beautiful: the whole structure glowing against the dusk like a coal that refused to die.

"It ate them," Sefa said beside her. She said it without drama, the way she reported weather or distances. "Whole village. Four hundred souls, give or take. They prayed for a good harvest two winters back. The god answered. Then it answered again. And again. Kept answering until there was nothing left of them to answer *to*."

"Four hundred people don't just kneel down and get eaten."

"No." Sefa's jaw worked. "They do it slowly. One answered prayer at a time. First your crops come in heavy, then your sick mother lives, then your husband's luck turns, then your child stops crying at night. And every time, you give a little more. A prayer here. A name there. A memory you won't miss. The shrine takes payment in whatever's closest to hand." She turned to Jude, and in the last light her eyes were flat as coins. "You've seen ledgers. You tell me. What happens to a debt when the debtor runs out of things to pay?"

Jude didn't answer. She already knew. In the temple scriptorium she'd copied ten thousand prayers that never got answered, and she had always believed the silence was neglect. Now she understood it might have been mercy.

"We go in tonight," Sefa said. "Small team. Me, you, Bren, the twins. Everyone else holds the ridge."

"Why me?"

Sefa smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "Because you read contracts better than anyone I've ever met, and shrines are contracts. Somebody wrote this one, and somebody signed it, and if we're going to break it open we need someone who can find the seam." She clapped Jude on the shoulder, brief and hard. "Besides. You wanted justice for the sold. Here's four hundred of them."

---

* * *

They went down through the barley fields in the blue hour before full dark, moving between the rows like wading through shallow water. The grain was wrong — Jude noticed it immediately, the way she noticed everything now that magic lived in her blood. The stalks grew too straight, too uniform, each head identical to its neighbor, a field printed rather than planted. No birdsong. No insects. The silence had texture, like held breath.

The first villager they passed was kneeling in his own doorway.

He was alive. That was the worst part. His chest rose and fell; his eyes were open; a thin line of drool caught the candlelight on his chin. He knelt facing the shrine with his hands folded and an expression on his face that Jude could only call *satisfied*. Around him the house was filthy, dishes crusted, a dog skeleton by the cold hearth — but the man himself was clean, tended, almost luminous, as though something were keeping him the way you keep a tool you intend to use again.

"Don't touch him," Bren whispered. "Last team tried. He screamed for six hours. Not in pain. In *protest*."

"They're not prisoners," Jude said quietly. "They're collateral."

Ahead, the shrine's light spilled across the packed earth of the village square, and Jude felt it before she understood it — a pull, gentle and enormous, like the tide taking hold of her ankles. Her blood stirred. The words rose unbidden behind her teeth, half-formed sentences that wanted to be spoken into the world, and she clenched her jaw against them. Since crossing the valley line she had learned that her magic responded to language, that reality around her was a text waiting to be edited — and that every edit cost a memory. She had fed it small things so far. The smell of her mother's kitchen. The tune of a lullaby. She had told herself the price was manageable.

Standing in that square, she felt how easily a person could feed it everything.

* * *

The shrine's interior was hollow.

That surprised her. From outside it looked solid, monumental, but they entered through a crack between two foundation stones and found themselves in a vast dark chamber ribbed with white stone, lit from within by the same sourceless glow. And stacked along the walls, filling alcoves, heaped in bins carved directly from the rock —

Grain.

That was Jude's first thought, and it made her stomach turn, because it was accurate. Bins of it. Amber-colored, faintly luminous, pouring softly over itself when Bren thrust his torch near. Each kernel pulsed with a tiny inner light, and each kernel, when Jude looked closer — truly looked, with the new sight the valley had never let her have — had a face.

Not a picture of a face. A face. Folded small, folded impossibly, a whole human soul compressed into something the size of a barleycorn, and there were thousands upon thousands of them, harvests of them, vintages, *villages* of them, stored in the dark like seed corn against some future planting.

"Oh," Bren said, very quietly. "Oh, gods."

"The answered prayers," Jude breathed. "This is where they go. This is what the price is." She reached toward a bin and stopped herself, hands shaking. "Every prayer that gets answered — the answer isn't free. The god takes the *asker*. Folds them up. Stores them. These people are still alive down there—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the kneeling village, "—because their souls are here. Being kept. Being *saved*, like grain against winter."

"Can they be freed?" That was one of the twins, Wren, her voice hushed.

"I don't know. Maybe. If the contract can be voided—" Jude moved along the wall, reading now, really reading. The stone was inscribed, fine lines of contract-script running in bands around every bin, terms and conditions in a language older than the temple's oldest texts but structured exactly like the ledgers she'd spent her life copying. Enforcement clauses. Interest schedules. *Terms of storage.* Her eye found the seams the way it always had, the flaw in the formula, the entry that didn't balance. "There's a master clause. A keystone. If we amend it—"

"No." Sefa's voice cut across the chamber like a blade. "We don't amend anything. We *take*."

Jude turned. Sefa stood at the center of the chamber, before a raised dais she hadn't noticed until now — and on the dais sat a coffer, iron-banded, old, humming with a light that hurt to look at directly.

"The hoard core," Sefa said. "Every shrine feeds its surplus up-chain to the next, all the way to the mountain. But the local principal stays here. Four hundred souls, Jude. Do you know what four hundred trained, bound, obedient souls are worth to us? To the *cause*?" Her voice had gone soft and fervent, and Jude recognized the tone with a sick lurch — she'd heard it in temple elders announcing tithes. "We carry the coffer out. We learn to open it. Those souls fight for the rebellion instead of rotting in a bin."

"That's not freeing them. That's *requisitioning* them."

"It's *using* them! They're being used either way — at least we'd use them for something that matters!" Sefa stepped close, and for a moment Jude saw past the commander to the woman underneath, the god-touched girl who'd watched her own family tithe her brother to a shrine. "You think mercy is leaving them here? You think walking away is kindness? The world doesn't work that way. Power doesn't work that way. Either you hold the ledger or the ledger holds you."

Jude looked at the bins. At the faces in the grain. At the coffer humming on its dais like a heart.

And she thought about a ledger entry she'd falsified once, years ago, in a quiet scriptorium, to save a child from indenture. She hadn't planned it. She hadn't weighed causes. She had seen a name and known it was wrong for that name to be in that column, and her hand had simply moved.

Her hand was moving now.

"Sefa," she said, "the keystone clause. Where the bins meet the floor. If I amend it — just strike the enforcement line — the storage terms void. Everything in these bins goes back to its owner."

"I said *no*—"

"You said take. I'm telling you what I'm going to do." Jude knelt at the wall's base, fingers finding the contract-script, feeling it writhe under her touch like something asleep. "You want to use people. I've spent my whole life watching institutions use people. I'm done being the scribe for it."

She began to rewrite.

The magic came eagerly, hungrily, the way it always did — and she braced for the cost, choosing what to feed it, reaching for a memory—

—and stopped.

Because the nearest kernel of grain had opened its eyes.

It was a child. Even folded a thousand times over, even compressed into something smaller than a fingernail, Jude knew a child when she saw one — knew the particular quality of that attention, the way a child looks at you when they've decided you might be safe. The little light pressed against the side of its bin, against the contract-script, and Jude felt it the way you feel a moth against glass.

*Please.*

Not a word. A pressure. A direction. But Jude had copied prayers for fifteen years, and she knew a petition when she felt one.

The cost came due. She chose fast: her mother's voice, singing. Gone. She struck the enforcement clause and felt the world shudder, felt the shrine groan around them like a ship changing course, and the bin before her split open along its seam and poured its single occupant into her cupped hands.

The child-soul unfolded.

It took the shape of a girl, perhaps seven years old, translucent as breath, standing in the chamber's glow and looking up at Jude with an expression of such naked, astonished hope that Jude's chest cracked open along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"Mara," the girl said. "My name was Mara."

"Hello, Mara." Jude's voice broke on it. "Your contract's void. You're free. Do you know the way home?"

Mara looked toward the village, toward the kneeling man in the doorway who might be her father, and nodded once. Then she walked out through the wall like light through water, and Jude stood in the ringing silence afterward and felt the cost settle into her bones — the lullaby gone, a hole in her memory smooth-edged and total, and worth it, worth it, *worth it*—

Except.

The shrine was screaming.

Not the villagers. The *stone*. The contract-script along every wall flared white, and Jude felt the amendment propagate outward through the web of clauses like a spark down a fuse — because a contract this size was never local, never isolated, it *filed upward*, everything filed upward, to the next shrine and the next and the next, all the way to the court beneath the mountain, and she had just written her signature into their records in letters of fire.

Bren grabbed her arm. "What did you do?"

"I freed one soul," Jude said. Her ears were ringing. Through the shrine walls she could feel it now, distant and approaching, a pressure like a storm front — the court turning its attention, clerks-gods waking, ledgers opening, something vast beginning to move toward Harrow's Bend with the terrible efficiency of a debt-collecting machine. "One. Out of four hundred."

Sefa stood by the coffer, and she was not looking at the shrine's collapse or the coming storm. She was looking at Jude with an expression Jude would remember for a long time: recalculation. Appraisal. The look of a commander discovering that her newest recruit was either the most valuable weapon she'd ever found or the most dangerous liability, and possibly both.

"You've just told the entire court where we are," Sefa said.

"I know."

"Then run."

* * *

They ran.

Out through the crack, across the square, past the kneeling man whose face had begun, at last, to change — confusion spreading across it like dawn, his hands unclasping, his mouth shaping a word that might have been a daughter's name. Behind them the shrine's glow flickered, guttered, and did not go out.

Halfway up the ridge, Jude stopped and turned.

The valley below was lighting up — not with candlelight but with answering light, columns of it rising from the shrine's crown into the darkening sky, beacons, signals, a message written in stolen souls for anyone beneath the mountain to read: *here, here, here.*

Vess appeared at her elbow. She hadn't heard him approach; the witness had a way of arriving like a formality. He followed her gaze down to the burning signal, then looked at her, and his face held something she hadn't seen in him before.

"Not pity," she said. "Don't you dare pity me."

"Testimony," Vess said quietly. "For the record. The witness observed the accused free one soul against orders, and observed that it was the only act committed tonight which the court will consider a crime." He paused. "I am required to note that reinforcements have been dispatched. I estimate three days."

"Three days." Jude watched the light climb. Somewhere under the mountain, a clerk-god was opening a file with her name in it. Somewhere in the rebel camp, Sefa was deciding what Jude Lockridge was worth. And somewhere inside her own chest, where her mother's lullaby used to live, there was a hole shaped exactly like the price of doing one right thing.

"Three days," she said again, and started climbing.
