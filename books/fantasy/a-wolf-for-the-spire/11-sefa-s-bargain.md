## 11. Sefa's Bargain

The rebel camp kept its fires small.

Jude noticed that first, on the morning after the shrine raid — a dozen cookfires banked down to embers, smoke funneled through stone channels so it rose thin and cold and invisible against the grey sky. Everything here was built around the assumption of being hunted. She'd slept four hours in a tent that smelled of tallow and old blood, and she'd dreamed of the child's soul she'd freed, the way it had come apart in her hands like wet paper and then, impossibly, like light.

They hadn't found the rest of the souls yet. Sefa had ordered the shrine's vault sealed until "the council could deliberate," which was a phrase that sounded exactly like what it was: a door closing.

Jude found Sefa at the camp's edge, standing where the trees broke off and the mountain showed itself — a black shoulder of rock forty miles north, wearing a collar of cloud. The spire wasn't visible from here. It didn't need to be. Everyone in this country oriented themselves toward it the way iron filings orient toward a magnet.

"You cost us," Sefa said, without turning.

"I know."

"Do you?" Now she turned, and her eyes had that flat shine they got when the god-fragment behind them leaned forward. "That vault held two hundred souls. Two hundred people who were sold, stored, and forgotten. You opened it for one child and the court now knows exactly where we are, how many we are, and what we can do." She said it without heat, the way a clerk reads figures aloud. That was somehow worse than shouting. "Vess is drafting our apology to posterity as we speak, I imagine."

"He's drafting his testimony," Jude said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

Jude didn't answer. She'd spent three days with these people and she still couldn't tell where Sefa ended and the thing inside her began — whether the god-touched woman spoke with her own voice or was spoken through. Maybe Sefa didn't know either. Maybe that was the price of the power, the same way memory was the price of Jude's.

"The council deliberates at midday," Sefa said. "You'll be there. Walk with me."

* * *

They walked up out of the camp along a goat track toward a ridge where the pines gave way to bare granite. Two guards trailed them at a distance that pretended not to be a distance. Sefa moved like someone conserving herself, each step placed rather than taken, and Jude understood suddenly that the god inside her was heavy — that carrying even a fragment of a waking god was like carrying water in cupped hands, all day, every day, forever.

"You want to ask me something," Sefa said. "You've been circling it since the shrine."

"The souls in the vault. When do they go free?"

"When we win."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one." Sefa stopped at the ridgeline. Below them the land fell away in folds of forest and field, and far off, tiny as a nail-head, a road crawled with traffic — carts, pilgrims, refugees from villages whose shrines had started answering prayers with prices attached. "Freedom isn't a door you open, scribe. It's a system you replace. Open the vaults tomorrow and the court retakes every soul within a season, because the ledger still stands, and the ledger is the law, and the law says those souls belong to whoever holds the paper."

"So we burn the paper."

Sefa smiled. It was a good smile. It was designed to be a good smile, and Jude hated herself a little for feeling its pull anyway.

"Yes," Sefa said. "We burn the paper. Come and see."

She led Jude off the track to a hollow between two boulders, screened by juniper, where a canvas lay stretched over something man-sized. Sefa knelt and pulled the canvas back.

Beneath it, drawn in charcoal and colored chalk on six stitched-together hides, was the mountain. Not the outside — the inside. The court beneath: halls, galleries, stair-shafts, and at the center, rendered in obsessive detail, a vertical structure that made Jude's teeth ache to look at. The spire. Around it, in red chalk, were annotations in a hand too neat to be human.

"This came from a defector," Sefa said. "A filing-clerk, third rank, who walked out eleven years ago with the floor plans in his head and wrote them down before the court's non-disclosure oaths could eat them. He lasted nine days after he finished drawing. What killed him looked like fever." She tapped the spire. "This is where they keep the master ledger. Not a copy. The original. Every contract ever drafted under the mountain, every sale, every enforcement clause, every soul recorded as ink in a bottle. Burn this and there is no law left that says anyone belongs to anyone."

"Then let's burn it," Jude said. "What's the council deliberating?"

Sefa sat back on her heels and looked at her for a long moment, and Jude watched the good smile die by degrees, replaced by something older and wearier and more honest.

"The council is deliberating," Sefa said, "whether to burn it or *keep* it."

* * *

The wind came up the valley below and combed through the juniper, and Jude stood very still while the sentence finished landing.

"Explain that."

"It's simple arithmetic." Sefa folded the canvas back over the map with the tenderness of someone making a bed. "The ledger is the most powerful object in the world. Whoever holds it writes reality. Right now that's the Broker and his clerks, and they use it to own people. If we destroy it, we're free — and so is every other power that's been waiting three hundred years for the old order to fall. The god-touched warlords in the east. The shrine-eaters. Every desperate king with an army and a grudge. Anarchy, scribe. And anarchy has a body count, and I've seen the ledgers of *that* kind of history too."

"So instead you take the ledger yourselves."

"We *hold* it. Temporarily. Responsibly. With oversight." Each word arrived pre-wrapped, the way a merchant wraps something fragile he intends to charge you for. "Someone must keep the contracts until they can be unwound properly. Someone must have the power to enforce the freeing of souls, or the freeing will simply be undone by the next strong hand. Justice without power is a prayer, and we both know how prayers get answered in this world."

Jude thought of the scriptorium. Of three hundred years of copied prayers stacked in the temple archives, beautiful and useless, answered by nothing. She thought of the widow's candle relighting itself. She thought of her own name in a contract, and how it had felt to learn that the whole architecture of her life rested on a piece of paper she'd never been allowed to read.

And she heard herself say, quietly: "That's what the Broker says."

Sefa's face went very still.

"Don't," Jude said. "Don't look like that. I've spent a week listening to him through you. *Someone must hold the power.* *Order must be maintained.* *It's for their own protection.* He says it in better language than you do, because he's had ten thousand years of practice, but it's the same sentence. You're not planning to break the machine, Sefa. You're planning to sit down at the desk."

"You don't know me."

"I know what you did with the shrine vault." Jude's voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "Two hundred souls, and you locked the door. For their protection. While your council deliberates." She gestured at the covered map. "And I know why you showed me this. Alone. Up here. Away from the others."

The wind moved the juniper. Somewhere downslope, a guard coughed and pretended he hadn't.

"Because you need a scribe," Jude said. "Not a soldier. A scribe. The ledger doesn't answer to force — Vess told me that, and the defector's death told me that. It answers to language. To terms. To someone who can read a contract faster than the court can write one and rewrite it before the ink dries. You raided that caravan because of what I did at the boundary line. You've been training me for a week. And now you're showing me the prize." She took a breath. "You're offering me a place beside you. Aren't you."

Sefa didn't deny it. That was the thing about her, Jude was learning — she lied fluently about tactics and never about intentions. It made her trustworthy in the way a knife is trustworthy.

"Yes," Sefa said. "I am."

* * *

They sat on the granite with the map between them like a corpse at a wake, and Sefa laid it out plainly, because plainness was her second-best weapon after the smile.

"One person gets into the spire's archive chamber. Not ten, not fifty — the court counts everything, and fifty hearts beating in a corridor is a number they'll notice. One heart, one mind, one pair of hands that can read and write at speed. The defector proved a single clerk can walk those halls if the paperwork says she belongs there." She tapped the red annotations. "We can forge the paperwork. We cannot forge the hands. The working that opens the master ledger's case is a rewriting — the case is warded against force, against theft, against everything except amendment by authorized signature. You'd have to make the ledger believe you have the right to touch it. That's not strength. That's literacy."

"And when I have it open?"

"Then my people come in behind you and we carry it out in pieces. The ledger is bound in volumes — thirteen of them, one per turn of the true moon. We take all thirteen. We take them somewhere the court can't follow, and we begin the unwinding. Every desperate sale voided. Every enforcement clause struck. Every soul released in an orderly fashion, with records, with restitution—" She caught herself, heard the clerk's cadence in her own mouth, and laughed once, short and bitter. "Listen to me. Ten years ago I was a shepherd's daughter in Karst Vale, and the court took my brother for a debt of four silver marks. Four marks. I joined this rebellion to burn things. Look what they've made me."

"What happened to your brother?"

"He's ink," Sefa said. "A bottle on a shelf in the spire, third gallery, if the defector's maps are right. I'm going to open that bottle myself. Whatever else happens — whatever I become in the process — that part is true, and clean, and mine." She turned to Jude fully, and for a moment the god-fragment receded and it was only a woman looking at her, tired and fierce and forty-something, with crow's feet and ash under her fingernails. "I'm offering you a place beside me, Jude Lockridge. Not under me. Beside. When the ledger is ours, we write the new terms together — you, me, Vess and his damned testimony, everyone the sold. No more contracts signed by people who aren't in the room. Starting with ours. Whatever you want written into it, you write it yourself, in your own hand, and I sign it and abide by it or the bargain's off before it begins."

It was a good offer. That was the horror of it. Jude turned it over and over and could find no flaw in the craftsmanship, only in the metal — because she had read enough contracts now to know that the flaw was never in the terms. The flaw was in the day, ten years from now, when holding the power became more natural than questioning it. The flaw was in the vault door, already locked. The flaw was in *we*, deployed by one voice.

"You'd rule," Jude said. "However gently. However temporarily. You'd be the Broker with a better origin story."

"I might." Sefa said it without flinching, and Jude realized that was the most honest sentence she'd spoken all morning. "So might you. That's what power is — a bet that you'll stay yourself longer than it takes to finish the work. I'm willing to make that bet. Are you telling me you're not?"

Jude looked at the map. At the spire drawn in obsessive detail by a dead man's last nine days of clarity. She thought about her mother's signature on a contract she'd never seen, and about the word *amendment*, and about the fact that she had already, once, falsified a ledger entry to save a child — and how right it had felt, and how easily, and how little it had cost her, that first time.

"I'm telling you," Jude said slowly, "that I need to see the vault with the two hundred souls before I answer anything. Open it, Sefa. All of it. Today. Not after the council deliberates — now, with me standing there. If you do that, I'll believe the temporary part. I'll believe *beside*. And then we can talk about the spire."

Something moved behind Sefa's eyes — the fragment, or the woman, or the negotiation itself recalculating. Jude watched her weigh it: the risk of showing obedience against the value of securing the one scribe in the world who could open the case. Watched her decide.

"All right," Sefa said. "Today. Before midday. You and me and the vault." She stood, and the good smile came back, and this time Jude watched it arrive and knew what it was for. "You drive a hard bargain, scribe. The court taught you well."

"The temple did," Jude said. "The court just collected."

They started down the goat track together, and Jude was three steps into the descent when the first horn sounded from the valley below — not the rebels' horn, low and rough, but something thinner and colder, a sound like a needle finding a vein. A sound she'd heard only once before, at the boundary line, the night the envoy came.

Down in the camp, the banked fires were flaring blue.

"They found us," Sefa said. Not a question. Her hand went to the canvas-covered map as if she could carry the whole mountain under her arm. "The shrine. The child you freed — it talked, or its absence talked, or—"

The second horn cut her off, closer, and this time Jude felt it in her teeth, in the ledger-language part of her mind, and understood it wasn't a horn at all. It was a summons. Written in sound. Addressed, with terrible courtesy, to her by name.

*Jude Lockridge,* the air said. *Your presence is requested.*

The court had stopped hunting.

The court had sent an invitation.
