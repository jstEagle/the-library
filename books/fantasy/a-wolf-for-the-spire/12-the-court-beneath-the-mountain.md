## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first thing Jude noticed, and it frightened her more than irons would have. The court's collectors — tall, gray-robed figures whose faces were smooth as unmarked wax — had simply looked at her when the shrine raid collapsed into screaming and fire, and one of them had said, in a voice like a page turning: *She comes with us. Unrestrained.*

Sefa had not come to save her. Sefa had watched from the ridge with her god-touched eyes burning amber and made no move at all, and Jude understood, even as the gray hands closed around her arms, that this was part of some arithmetic she hadn't been shown. *Everyone wants to own me,* she thought. *The temple sold my passage. Sefa spent me as a distraction. And now the court has come to collect what was always theirs.*

Vess was there too. Her witness, her assigned witness, walking beside the collectors with his ledger clutched to his chest like a shield he no longer believed in.

"Jude." His voice cracked on her name. "I'll record everything. I swear it. Whatever they do down there, it will be written."

"Will that help?" she asked.

He didn't answer, which was its own kind of honesty.

* * *

The mountain swallowed them through a door that had not been there a moment before — a seam of darkness between two boulders, wide enough for one, and the collectors walked into it without slowing, pulling Jude along as though the stone were water and they had done this ten thousand times.

Inside, the dark lasted exactly long enough to be terrifying. Then light bloomed, sourceless and pale, and Jude stopped breathing.

A cathedral.

But no cathedral built by human hands — no vaulted stone, no stained glass, no pews. Instead, the cavern opened upward and outward into a vastness that hurt to look at, and filling that vastness, rank upon rank upon rank, rising higher than any spire she had ever copied prayers about, stood filing-cabinets.

They went up forever. Brass drawers, iron drawers, drawers of black wood and white bone, each one labeled in script that shifted when she tried to read it. Some cabinets were taller than trees. Some were small enough to hold in two hands, and those frightened her most, because she could hear them — faint, muffled sounds from inside, like someone knocking politely.

Between the cabinets moved the clerks.

There were hundreds of them. Thousands. Gray-robed like her escorts but older, stranger, their forms only approximately humanoid, as if they had decided long ago that a body was a useful filing convention and never revisited the decision. They glided between the shelves without touching the floor. Each carried a pen. Each murmured as it worked — and the murmuring was the worst part, because it wasn't random. It was names. Thousands of clerks whispering thousands of names, all day, all night, for three hundred years while the valley above forgot magic ever existed.

One clerk paused beside her as the party passed. Its face was a smooth oval with two ink-dark hollows where eyes should be. It tilted its head, considering her the way she considered a smudged line in a ledger.

"*Unusual provenance*," it said, and drifted on.

"What happens to people here?" Jude asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"You are not people here," said the collector holding her left arm. Not cruelly. Factually, the way you'd tell someone the temperature. "You are an account. Accounts are read, reconciled, and settled. Nothing else is required of you."

Ahead, the ranks of cabinets parted into a great circular hall, and in the center of the hall stood a desk.

It was an ordinary desk. That was the horror of it. Oak, scarred, ink-stained, the kind of desk that might sit in any scriptorium in any temple in the world — except it sat alone beneath a ceiling of stone so far above it might have been sky, and the light pooled on it like a spotlight on an altar, and behind it sat something that wore courtesy the way a wolf wears calm.

"Ah," it said. "The Lockridge account arrives."

* * *

The Broker rose, and Jude's knees nearly buckled, because rising was wrong — it kept rising, unfolding upward past any reasonable height, robes flowing like poured ink, until it chose, deliberately, mercifully, to settle into the shape of a tall man with a scribe's ink-stained fingers and a face of perfect, gentle neutrality. A face designed not to frighten. Which meant somewhere beneath the design was something that needed designing against.

"Please," it said, gesturing to a plain wooden chair across the desk. "Sit. You've had a long journey and an unpleasant afternoon. I apologize for the manner of your arrival. My collectors are thorough but they lack bedside manner. It's why I handle negotiations personally."

Jude didn't sit. She wasn't sure her legs would have held her either way.

"You know my name," she managed.

"I know all the names." The Broker smiled, and the smile was warm, and the warmth was the most frightening thing yet, because it was *practiced*. "Jude Lockridge. Copier of prayers, falsifier of ledgers — yes, we know about the Aldous girl, and no, no one will be punished for it; mercy entered into the record is still mercy, whatever the temple elders believe. Daughter of Maren Lockridge. Debtor. Asset. And—" it tilted its head, and for half a heartbeat the courteous mask slipped and something vast and cold looked out from behind it, something old enough to have drafted the first contract ever signed, "—considerably more than the paperwork suggests. But we'll come to that."

"The paperwork says you own my soul."

"The paperwork says your mother sold it. Ownership is a consequence of sale. I merely enforce consequences." The Broker folded its ink-stained hands. "You've been reading contracts on your journey. Vess has been diligent about showing you the originals — I approved that, incidentally. Transparency is important to me. So you know how this works. Terms, conditions, consideration, signature. I don't lie, Jude. Lying voids contracts. Everything I say to you today will be true. I'd ask you to extend me the same courtesy, but I understand if you're not ready."

"Why am I here? The moon hasn't finished turning. I have weeks left."

"You had weeks left," the Broker agreed. "Then you broke into one of my storage shrines and released a soul that was lawfully held. Do you know what a released soul does, Jude? It goes home. It tries to go home. And a soul that was taken from a dying child in Harrow Fen seventy years ago finds that home is now a sheep pasture, and everyone who loved it is dead, and it begins to *scream*. Audibly. Across three counties. My enforcement division was obliged to respond, and in responding, they found you. Events accelerate. I adapt. Here we are."

Guilt landed in her chest like a stone. The child's soul she'd freed — she'd felt it leave her hands like a bird, bright and grateful, and she had told herself it was mercy. Maybe it had been. Mercy with consequences, Sefa had said, and Sefa had been right, and Sefa had also let her be taken, and Jude was too tired and too frightened to sort out which betrayal stung worse.

"I want to see my mother," she said.

Something flickered across the Broker's face — not surprise, exactly. Recalculation.

"In time," it said. "First, business."

* * *

It opened a drawer.

The drawer was small, knee-height, ordinary brass. From it the Broker drew a single sheet of paper and laid it on the desk before her, squaring its corners with two precise taps.

Jude looked down and saw her own handwriting.

Not similar. *Her own.* The cramped, careful script of a woman who had spent fifteen years copying prayers, every letter formed the same way, the descenders slightly too long because she'd learned from a template with a flaw in it. Except she had never written this document. She had never seen it. It was dated eleven days ago.

*Contract of Employment, Preliminary,* it read. *Party of the first part: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Party of the second part: Jude Lockridge. Consideration offered: continued existence of the physical body, protection from third-party claimants, access to the account holder designated 'Maren Lockridge.' Consideration required: service as scribe, term of one hundred years, during which the soul of the second party remains in court custody.*

"No," Jude said.

"It's a draft," the Broker said mildly. "Nothing is binding until signed. I want to be very clear about that, because I suspect people have told you I'm a monster who traps the desperate. I am not. I am a *clerk*, Jude. The oldest one. I draft, I file, I enforce. Every clause in every contract beneath this mountain exists because someone, somewhere, agreed to it. Your mother agreed. The temple elders agree annually to the tithing schedule. Even your rebel friends — Sefa signs requisitions, did you know that? Freedom fighters still need supply chains, and supply chains still need signatures." It spread its hands. "I don't create the world's desperation. I merely offer it structure."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the draft goes back in the drawer, and we proceed under the original terms. The thirteenth moon completes. What your mother hid wakes up. And the court does what courts do with assets of your particular nature." The Broker's voice stayed gentle. That was the trick of it, Jude realized — the gentleness never wavered, never strained, and somehow that made every sentence feel like a blade being wrapped in cloth. "I would prefer not to proceed that way. Enforcement is expensive and undignified. Negotiation is cheaper. I am offering you negotiation."

"You're offering me a century of slavery."

"I'm offering you a century of *employment*, with visitation rights to your mother, protection from parties — and there are parties, Jude, older and less polite than I am, waking up under other mountains right now — who would take you apart to learn what you are. I'm offering you the only deal on the table that doesn't end with you as ink." It leaned forward, and the lamplight caught its face, and for a moment the courteous shape thinned and she saw the truth underneath: a thing of infinite patience wearing patience like a coat. "You've spent your whole life copying prayers that no one answered. Here, your words would *work*. You'd write, and reality would comply. Isn't that what you've wanted since the candle relit itself under your fingers? Since you felt the words move?"

Jude's breath caught. It knew about the candle. Of course it knew. It probably had a drawer for the candle.

"You're trying to seduce me," she said.

"I'm trying to *hire* you. Seduction would involve flattery, and flattery is a form of lying adjacent enough to make me uncomfortable." The Broker straightened. "Take the night. Read the draft. Ask Vess to verify every clause — he's authorized. In the morning, we discuss terms. There is one more thing."

It opened another drawer. This one was long and shallow, lined in green felt, and inside it lay a single sheet of ancient paper, brown as autumn, sealed with a sigil Jude recognized from her mother's few precious possessions — pressed into the wax of a keepsake locket, worn smooth by a thumb over years of anxious rubbing.

"This is the original," the Broker said. "The contract your mother signed the night before you were born. You've read copies. This is the instrument itself, bearing her signature and my seal. Under court protocol, the account holder may view the original once, in the presence of a witness." It slid the paper across the desk and turned it to face her. "Read it, Jude. All of it. Especially the clauses your mother hoped you'd never reach."

Jude reached for it with shaking hands.

And as her fingertips touched the ancient paper, the words *moved* — the way they had moved under her fingers in the sealed scriptorium, the way they had been moving more and more often since the old gods woke — and deep beneath the cathedral, in ranks of cabinets going up forever, thousands of whispering clerks fell silent at once.

Every drawer in the mountain clicked shut.

The Broker looked up at the dark, and its expression — for the first time — was not courtesy.

"Interesting," it said quietly. "They heard you coming."
