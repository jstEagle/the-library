## 13. Terms and Conditions

They gave her a room with no door.

Not a cell — a cell would have been honest. This was a guest chamber carved into the living rock of the mountain's deep halls, furnished with a bed, a washbasin, a writing desk, and an absence where a door should have been. The archway simply opened onto the corridor, and the corridor was watched by clerks who never blinked.

Jude sat on the bed and counted what she knew.

One: the court beneath the mountain had held her soul in escrow for twenty-six years, and she had walked into its cathedral of filing-cabinets without a fight, which meant they thought she was docile.

Two: the Broker did not make offers he expected to be refused. He made offers shaped like doors, and then he waited for you to notice there was no wall behind you.

Three: the master ledger existed. She had seen it — or the shrine it lived in — from across the cathedral floor: a black cabinet tall as a bell tower, humming like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

She was still counting when the tea arrived.

It came on a tray carried by a clerk whose face was smooth as an egg, features suggested rather than drawn, as if someone had described a person to the stone and the stone had done its best. The clerk set the tray on the desk, bowed at a precise angle, and said, in a voice like paper turning:

"The Broker will receive you at the ninth bell."

"I don't know when the ninth bell is," Jude said.

"You will." The clerk bowed again and left, and Jude understood: time down here wasn't measured. It was filed. She would know the ninth bell because the ninth bell would arrive inside her, the way everything down here intended to arrive eventually — without knocking.

The tea was good. That frightened her more than anything else so far. Someone had considered what a tired scribe would want, warm and slightly bitter, honey at the bottom of the cup, and had provided it. Captivity performed by people who were very, very good at records.

She drank it anyway. You couldn't plan on an empty stomach, and whatever else the mountain wanted from her, it did not want her dead. Dead souls didn't sign.

* * *

The ninth bell rang through her ribs like a swallowed chime, and two clerks appeared to walk her down.

The cathedral of files opened around her as she went — aisle after aisle of cabinets rising toward a ceiling lost in shadow, each drawer whispering as they passed, a sound like ten thousand people murmuring in their sleep. She had heard it yesterday when they brought her in, and it had taken all her discipline not to stop and listen properly. Now she let herself listen while she walked, cataloguing.

Some drawers whispered names. Some whispered numbers. One, near the eastern wall, whispered in her mother's voice — just one word, over and over, too soft to catch. Jude kept walking. If she stopped at every wound this place offered her, she'd never leave.

The Broker's office was not what she expected. She had braced for a throne, something vast and cruel. Instead: a study. Warm lamplight. A desk of dark wood worn pale at the edges by centuries of elbows. Shelves of ledgers in leather gone soft with handling. A fire that burned without smoke.

And the Broker himself, pouring tea.

He looked like a grandfather built by a committee that had only ever heard descriptions of grandfathers. Kind eyes slightly too large. A smile assembled from every comforting smile ever recorded. His hands were remarkable — long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticles, the hands of a man who had written more than any hand should write.

"Jude Lockridge," he said warmly. "Or should I say — " and here his smile deepened by a degree, "— no. Not yet. Sit, please. You'll want your hands free for the cup."

She sat. He poured. The tea was the same blend as hers upstairs, and she noted that too: he was consistent. Consistency was a tool. She'd spent her life among men who used it.

"You have questions," the Broker said, settling into the chair opposite. It creaked under him, which was either a kindness of staging or a genuine concession to weight. "Everyone does. *Why me. Why now. What did my mother do.* I'll answer all of them, but first —" he lifted his cup — "I want to show you something. Because I find that people decide better when they understand what they're deciding about."

He rose, crossed to the far shelf, and drew out a ledger no bigger than a prayer book. He laid it open before her.

It was a prayer record. Valley script — her script, the tight temple hand she'd copied ten thousand times. A widow's plea for rain, dated three hundred years ago. And beside it, in a column she had never once seen in any temple ledger, a price.

Jude read it twice. Her mouth went dry.

"The rain cost the widow her memory of her son's face," the Broker said gently. "She prayed for water; the old gods answered; the answering required material. Souls are the usual currency, but memory will serve, or years, or love, depending on the contract. Your temples copy the prayers and omit the columns. Omission is also a form of record-keeping. I've always found it the most honest kind, actually — it tells you exactly what the omitter fears."

"This is one ledger," Jude said. Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that. "There must be thousands."

"Millions." He said it the way a baker says *loaves*. "Would you like to see?"

* * *

The master ledger stood in its own vault beneath the cathedral, and the Broker walked her down to it personally, no escort, which told her two things: he feared nothing down here, and he wanted her to feel the intimacy of being trusted. Both were true. Neither changed the fact of the bars.

The vault door was iron thick as a coffin lid, and beyond it the black cabinet rose into darkness, humming its unfinished note. Up close, Jude could see that it was not wood and not stone. It was compressed — layers upon layers upon layers, pressed flat, and the layers were pages. The whole tower was made of vellum ground down and bound again, and the vellum had been skin once, and some part of her — the part that had felt words move under her fingers in the sealed scriptorium — knew whose.

"The master ledger," the Broker said, with the tenderness other men reserved for children. "Every contract ever executed. Every prayer answered, and the price extracted. Every soul held, and the terms of its holding." He touched the cabinet's flank, and the humming deepened, almost like leaning into the touch. "Your valley calls itself quiet. But quiet is just debt nobody's collecting."

Jude looked up at it, and she made herself do the thing she had come down here to do.

She memorized.

Not the contents — no one could read the tower at a glance, and pretending otherwise would get her caught. The structure. The access. The vault door had no lock she could see, but it had hinges, and hinges were a direction. The cabinet stood alone, four paces of clearance on every side, no shelving near it, nothing between it and the door but open floor. Two clerks stationed outside the vault, relieved at intervals she would count later. The humming changed pitch when the Broker touched it — responded to him, which meant it responded to *something*, and things that responded could be addressed.

Her mother had taught her that, though she'd never said it aloud. Everything in a ledger answers to a well-formed entry. Even the ledger.

"You're staring," the Broker observed, amused.

"It's very tall," Jude said.

"It is." He watched her a moment longer than comfort allowed, those oversized kind eyes doing their patient work, and then he gestured back toward the stairs. "Come. Let's talk terms."

* * *

Back in the warm study, he laid it out plainly, and she had to give him credit: he was good. No threats. No chains. Just a career.

"A century of service," he said. "As my second scribe. There is already a first — you may meet her, if our arrangement holds — but the work has outgrown her hands. The old gods wake, the contracts multiply, and I require someone who can write the way you write."

"I haven't written anything down here."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But I've read everything you wrote up there. Ten years of prayer-copies. Do you know what I saw in them?" He leaned forward, firelight catching the ink stains on his fingers. "Corrections. Tiny ones. A verb tense smoothed here, an ambiguity resolved there. Nothing that changes meaning — nothing that would flag — but every page you touched came off the desk *cleaner* than it went on. You've been editing prayers since you were sixteen, Jude, and no one ever taught you to. That isn't training. That's nature."

The candle in the scriptorium. The words moving under her fingers. She kept her face still and let him believe the flush in her cheeks was flattery.

"And in exchange for a century of that," she said, "I keep my soul."

"In exchange, I release your claim. Fully. You'd walk out of this mountain a free woman, owed nothing, owned nothing, your name your own." He spread his hands. "I'm not a monster, whatever your rebels say. I'm a custodian. Contracts want honoring, not cruelty. And a century is less than it sounds — ask anyone who's served me. Time moves differently around good work."

*A century.* She thought of the valley, of the temple elders who had paid her passage like a merchant settling a nuisance claim. A century from now, everyone she'd ever known would be dust, and she would still be at this desk, smoothing verbs, while the price column filled and filled.

"And if I refuse?"

The Broker sighed, and for just a moment the grandfather mask slipped enough to show her what was underneath: something old and patient and hungry, wearing kindness the way a clerk wears livery. "Then the thirteenth moon completes, and the contract executes as written, and your soul comes to me anyway — except you won't be holding the pen when it does. You'll be in the column." He refilled her cup, gentle as snowfall. "I'd rather you held the pen. Truly. The ones who hold the pen live better."

Jude looked down at the tea. She thought about Sefa's hand on her shoulder in the rebel camp, offering belonging with one hand and a master ledger's theft with the other. She thought about Vess, somewhere above, writing testimony. She thought about her mother's signature on a contract signed before Jude drew breath.

Everyone wanted her at a desk. The difference was only what got written.

"I'd need to see the work," she said slowly. "Before I commit a century. I'm a scribe, Master Broker. I don't sign blind."

Something flickered across his assembled face — surprise, quickly filed away, and beneath it a flicker of respect. "Of course. Tomorrow, then. I'll set a desk for you in the scriptorium below, and you'll copy under supervision. Consider it an apprenticeship. Consider it a test." His smile returned, warm and terrible. "Consider it, Jude. That's all I ask. Everyone signs, in the end. All I ever do is make sure they understand the terms."

She stood. She bowed — the precise angle the clerks used, and she watched him register that, and enjoy it.

At the doorway — the empty archway where a door should be — she paused.

"One question," she said. "The first scribe. The one whose hands the work has outgrown. What happened to her predecessor?"

The Broker's teacup paused halfway to his mouth.

"There has only ever been one first scribe," he said.

Jude nodded as if that settled something, and walked back up the corridor past the whispering drawers, past the one that murmured in her mother's voice, and did not let her stride break until she was alone in the doorless room with the lamp turned low.

Only ever one. Which meant her mother had been at that desk for decades, and the work had never outgrown her, and the Broker had lied to her tonight — casually, warmly, the way he did everything.

Good, Jude thought, sitting in the dark, running tomorrow's scriptorium through her mind like a proofreader hunting errors. Liars showed you their structure. And she had a century's worth of structure to learn before the moon filled — starting with the location of every hinge in this mountain, and ending with the exact weight of the pen she intended to take out of his hand.
