## 14. The Spire of Names

They came for her at the hour the clerks called *filing*, though nothing in the deep halls had hours. Jude knew it only because Vess told her, standing outside her cell with a lantern that burned blue and gave no heat.

"It's time," he said. "He wants you to see it."

"See what?"

"The spire." Vess's face did something complicated — pity trying to pass itself off as procedure. "Every soul sold beneath this mountain is kept there. He thinks you should understand what you are."

*What I am.* The words had been following her since the waystation archive, since she'd read her mother's signature on a contract older than her own memory. She'd thought she understood then. She'd been wrong, and the Broker had let her be wrong, because a prisoner who believes she knows the worst is easier to walk down long corridors.

She followed him. There was nowhere else to go.

The deep halls of the court were not built; they were *written*. Jude had understood that by now, in the way you understand a language you've been drowning in for days. The corridors were sentences. The vaulted ceilings were clauses. When a clerk-god passed them — robed shapes with faces like the blank first page of a ledger — the walls adjusted themselves around the encounter, the stone reflowing like ink deciding where to go.

"You're afraid," Vess said.

"I'm furious."

"That's the same thing, down here. Fear is just anger that's been notarized." He said it flatly, without humor, and Jude realized he'd been saying things like that all his life — small heresies filed under neutral observation. "When we reach the spire, don't speak until spoken to. Don't touch anything. And whatever you see—"

"Whatever I see, what?"

He didn't answer. Ahead, the corridor opened into a vast dark, and the dark was full of light.

* * *

The spire rose from the center of a cavern so large the lantern couldn't find its walls. It was black glass, or something pretending to be black glass, and it climbed out of sight into a ceiling that might have been sky. Light moved inside it — not flickering, but *flowing*, slow rivers of silver rising through the dark like sap through a tree.

And it was loud. Not with sound. With voices. Thousands of them, layered so fine they blurred into a hum, the way individual raindrops blur into rain. Jude stood at the base and listened, and after a while she stopped hearing a hum and started hearing *words*, and after a while longer she wished she hadn't.

"They're in there," she said. "All of them."

"The sold." Vess kept his eyes forward, formal, witness-shaped. "Not destroyed. Preserved. The court would say honored."

"What would you say?"

"I'm not required to say anything. That's rather the point of me." But his hand had gone tight around the lantern's handle, and she saw the knuckles whiten, and she thought of what he'd almost told her once on the road, before catching himself: a sister, generations back, sold for a winter's grain. Somewhere in that silver flow was a voice that shared his jaw, his stubbornness, his habit of filing heresy under observation.

"How do they keep them?" Jude asked. "Like the shrine? Like grain?"

"Worse. Better. Depends on your mercy." Vess gestured at the flowing light. "Ink. Every soul is rendered down to its truest script — the sentence that soul *is* — and stored in the spire's pages. They can't suffer. They can't want. They can't do anything but remain legible." A pause. "Forever."

Jude looked up the impossible height of the thing. "Who writes them in?"

For the first time since the valley, Vess looked at her directly, and there was apology in it, and dread.

"You'll see," he said. "Come."

* * *

A door opened in the spire's flank where no door had been, and stairs spiraled up inside, and the voice-hum grew louder with every step until Jude could pick out individuals again: an old man reciting the names of his grandchildren over and over; a girl counting to some number she never reached; someone singing a harvest song in a dialect three hundred years dead. Each voice was a line of silver script suspended in the dark, and each line was *alive*, trembling faintly, the way a held note trembles.

Her magic woke without permission. It had been doing that more often since the mountain — rising in her blood like water finding its level, tasting everything. Now it reached toward the nearest line of script the way a starving hand reaches for bread, and Jude felt, for one vertiginous instant, that she could *read* the old man. All of him. His whole self laid open in one sentence, and she could amend him, could strike a clause, could—

She wrenched her hand back and bit her tongue hard enough to taste copper. The line of silver settled. Somewhere far above, something noticed, and decided not to care yet.

"He said you'd try that," said a voice from above. "He said you wouldn't be able to help yourself."

Jude knew the voice.

She had known it every day of her life. It was the voice that had taught her her letters, that had sung the valley's dull hymns off-key on purpose to make her laugh, that had said *goodnight, little copyist* across twenty years of narrow rooms and borrowed time. It was thinner now. Clearer. Younger.

It was coming down the stairs toward her, and it belonged to a woman who looked barely older than Jude herself.

"Mama?"

Her mother stopped three steps up, lantern-light catching a face Jude had last seen lined and gray and bent over a dying prayer. The lines were gone. The gray was gone. Her hair fell black and heavy past shoulders that had never been straight in Jude's lifetime. She wore scribe's robes of deep gray, and in her hand was a pen that gleamed like the spire itself, and she looked at her daughter with an expression Jude had spent her whole life learning to read and had apparently never read correctly at all.

Because it wasn't joy. It wasn't relief. It was the look of a woman caught.

"You're younger than me," Jude said stupidly. "You're *younger than me.*"

"The spire pays its scribes in years," her mother said. "I've been giving mine back for a long time." She descended the last steps slowly, as if the distance between them were something that had to be negotiated. "Jude."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. "Don't say my name like that. Like you have the right. You *sold* my name. I read it. I read the whole contract. You didn't sell my soul, Mama, you sold—" Her voice failed and restarted. "You sold *me*. Before I was born. Before I could be anything to sell."

"Yes."

One word. No excuse attached. Jude had imagined this moment on the road, in cells, in the archive with the contract shaking under her hands — and in every imagining her mother wept, or explained, or begged. She had not imagined calm. Calm was worse. Calm meant practice.

"How long?" Jude asked. "How long have you been down here?"

"Thirty-one years."

Thirty-one. Jude did the arithmetic and felt the floor tilt. "I was seven. You didn't die when I was seven. You *came here*. You left me in the valley and came to work for the god who owns me, and everyone — the temple, the neighbors, *you* — let me believe you were dead."

"You were safer believing it."

"Safer!" The word echoed up the stairwell and a dozen silver lines shivered in sympathy, the sold stirring at the sound of grief like dogs at a fence. "I copied prayers for fifteen years to pay debts you left me! I falsified a ledger to keep a child out of indenture because I know exactly what indenture does, because it *ate my whole life*! And you were here. Writing them in." She pointed at the flowing dark above them, at the voices. "Is that what you do? Is that your work? You take people and you make them *ink*?"

Her mother's composure finally cracked — not into tears but into something harder, wearier, a flinch like an old scar pulled taut.

"Yes," she said again. "That is my work. And I am very good at it, and I have been good at it for thirty-one years, and every day I tell myself the same thing I told myself the day I signed: better my hand than another's. Better a mother's gentleness holding the pen." She lifted it slightly, and the light ran along it like oil. "Do you know what the others were like? The scribes before me? They enjoyed it. They *savored*. I made it painless. I made it quick. I sang to them while I worked, the songs you used to fall asleep to, because it was the only kindness the Broker permitted me and I spent it on strangers."

"On strangers," Jude repeated. "Not on your daughter. Who was also in here. Also ink. Did you sing to me? Did you write me in yourself, or did you make someone else do it, the way you made the temple raise me?"

Something moved behind her mother's eyes — fast, buried, gone. Jude had spent a childhood learning that face and she caught it anyway: guilt, yes, but underneath the guilt, *calculation*. Her mother had always been three thoughts ahead. Even now. Especially now.

"I need you to listen," her mother said quietly. "We don't have much time. He sent you up these stairs for a reason, and it isn't cruelty, and it isn't reunion. It's preparation. Everything in this mountain is preparation for the thirteenth moon, and the moon is nearly full, and when it fills—"

"When it fills, what?"

Her mother glanced upward, at the dark above the dark, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "You are not what the contract says you are. The contract says *sold soul, one, female, infant*. That was the cover. That was the lie I bought with the truth." Her hand found Jude's wrist — cold fingers, impossibly smooth skin, a stranger's grip — "Jude. Listen to me. Your father—"

The stairwell changed.

It happened the way the corridors reflowed around clerk-gods: the steps beneath them rippled, the walls drew inward like a held breath, and every silver line in the spire went silent at once. Ten thousand souls, holding their breath. The silence was worse than the singing.

Then, from above, unhurried footsteps, and a voice like a well-composed letter:

"Sera. You're ahead of schedule."

Her mother released Jude's wrist and turned, and her spine straightened into a posture Jude had never seen her use — deference worn like armor. "Broker."

"I trust the introduction was going well." The Broker descended into the lantern-light, and he looked, as always, courteous and terrible, a gentleman assembled from patience. His gaze touched Jude gently, the way a stamp touches a document. "Daughters and mothers. There is no contract quite so binding. Come, child. Walk with me. Your mother can finish her confession later — she has thirty-one years of it banked, after all. She's very good at saving things for later."

He smiled at Sera as he said it, and Jude saw her mother receive the smile like a sentence being passed.

"One thing first," the Broker added pleasantly, pausing beside her mother on the stairs. "Sera. What were you about to tell her? About the father?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat. Jude watched her mother weigh it — watched her choose, in front of her daughter, with the ease of long practice.

"Nothing she doesn't already suspect," Sera said.

"Liar," Jude breathed.

"Of course she is," the Broker agreed warmly. "It's why I keep her. Come along, Jude Lockridge. Or should I say—" and his smile widened by one precise degree, "—never mind. Names later. The moon is nearly full, and there is so little time, and so very much of you to file."

He extended his hand toward the upper dark, toward whatever waited at the top of the spire, and Jude — with her mother's lie still hanging between them like a blade mid-fall — took a step she did not remember choosing.

Above them, ten thousand souls began, very softly, to sing.
