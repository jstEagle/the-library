## 15. Her Mother's Confession

### Maren

The spire did not have rooms. It had *holdings*.

Maren Iversen had learned that in her first year below, when she'd asked a clerk-god for a place to sleep and it had handed her a drawer with her name inked on the rim. She had slept in that drawer for eleven months, curled around the fact of what she had done, before the Broker promoted her to first scribe and gave her a desk instead — a desk being, in the court beneath the mountain, a kind of cell with better light.

She was at that desk now, copying the terms of a marriage contract between two river gods who hated each other, when the summons came up through the floor like a taste of copper.

*The Lockridge girl has been brought in.*

Not Lockridge. Not anymore, not really — though the girl didn't know that yet, and Maren had spent twenty-two years making sure no one would tell her.

She set down her pen. Her hands were steady. They were always steady; that was the price of the work, steadiness purchased with everything unsteady in her, fed into the spire grain by grain until what remained was a woman who could copy the sale of a child without her pen skipping once.

She had done exactly that, once. Her pen hadn't skipped then either. She still dreamed about it.

The corridor to the guest holdings ran past the memory-vats, and Maren walked it slowly, because she knew what waited at the end of it, and she wanted one more length of corridor in which her daughter was still a stranger she could fail from a distance.

Twenty-two years ago she had stood at the valley line with an infant in her arms and a god's bargain folded into her breast pocket, and she had told herself: *this is protection.* The other gods were waking even then, stirring under their mountains like sleepers turning over, and a child half-divine, half-signed-away, was a thing they would fight over like dogs over a bone. The Broker had offered a hiding place written in law itself. A soul sold is a soul claimed. No other power may touch what the court holds. Even gods keep their contracts — especially gods. Contracts are the only scripture they all obey.

So she had signed. And the valley had closed around her daughter like a hand around a candle flame, and magic could not follow, and Jude grew up copying prayers that no one answered, safe, small, blind.

Safe. Maren said the word to herself the way you press on a bruise to check if it still hurts.

It still hurt.

The door at the end of the corridor was oak, which was a courtesy; most doors down here were clauses. She put her hand flat against it and felt, through the wood, the faint vibration of her daughter's heartbeat, and for a moment she was back in the birthing room in the valley, counting heartbeats while the midwife looked away, counting them because the father had asked her to, before he went back under the mountain to be ground into something useful.

*Count them,* he'd said. *Every beat is a day she isn't theirs.*

He had been wrong about how many days there would be. Or he had known, and hadn't told her, and she had never decided which betrayal was worse.

Maren opened the door.

Her daughter sat on the holding-room's single bench with her spine straight and her hands folded, the way she must have sat through a thousand temple audits, and the sight of it went through Maren like a blade wrapped in cloth — pain delivered politely. Jude looked up. Her eyes were her father's eyes, grey as slate weather, and they widened by a fraction, and that fraction was worth more to Maren than every contract she had ever copied.

"Mother," Jude said. Flat. Careful. A word handled like evidence.

"Jude." And then, because the lie had been so long in her mouth that truth came out shaped wrong: "You've grown."

*You've grown.* Twenty-two years of theft, summarized in two words.

"I need to know," Jude said, "what you signed."

Maren closed the door behind her. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred floors of filed souls, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its fullness, and she could feel it in her teeth, in the ink stains of her fingers, in the old scar of the signature on her palm that the Broker had taken as surety and never returned.

"You should sit," she said.

"I am sitting."

"Yes," Maren said. "You always were good at staying still. I taught you that without meaning to."

* * *

### Jude

Her mother had aged backward.

That was the thing Jude couldn't stop looking at. In the valley, Maren Iversen had gone grey early, bent early, worn down by debt and copying and the slow grind of the Temple's ledgers. The woman standing in front of her now had silver hair but a straight back, unlined hands, eyes clear as a girl's. The spire had paid her in years, apparently. Or taken them back. Down here nothing was given; everything was *adjusted*.

"You look well," Jude said, and heard how it landed — an accusation dressed as an observation.

"The work preserves me." Her mother's mouth twisted. "That's not a kindness. Ask me what the work costs."

"What does the work cost?"

"Ask me what I signed. That's what you came to ask. So ask properly, and I'll answer properly, and we'll both stop performing."

Jude stood. The bench was cold through her clothes, and everything down here was cold in a way that felt administrative rather than natural. "What did you sign?"

Her mother crossed to the room's narrow window — a slit of stone that showed nothing but black rock, a window included purely so the room could be described as having one — and stood with her back straight and her hands clasped, and began to speak in the voice of someone reciting a ledger entry she had memorized against her will.

"On the third night after your birth, a man came to our door. You know this part, or you think you do. The temple story says a moneylender. The village story says a tax man. Both stories are wrong. He was the Broker's herald, and he came because your father sent him."

"Who was my father?" Jude asked, though some part of her had already assembled the answer from fragments — the way prayers moved under her fingers, the way the envoy Vess flinched when he looked too long at her, the way the god-touched rebels had mistaken her for a court agent and then, worse, for something else entirely.

"He was a god," her mother said. "A small one, as gods go. A hearth-god. A keeper of thresholds. His name was—" and here she stopped, and Jude watched her mother's composure crack along a fault line twenty-two years deep. "I can't say his name. That's part of what I sold. I sold his name out of my own mouth. I can walk past his shrine in the valley — you've swept it a hundred times, you thought it was just an old boundary-marker — and I cannot say his name, because I traded it as collateral, and the contract enforces itself."

Jude thought of the squat stone at the valley's edge where she'd left wildflowers as a child because it seemed lonely. Her throat closed.

"Why would a god need to sell anything? Why would *he* need—"

"Because the other gods were waking," her mother said, "and a hearth-god is small enough to be eaten. And you—" she turned from the window, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady, which was somehow the worst combination Jude had ever seen — "you weren't only his. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Divinity doesn't blend like water and wine. It *waits*. It sits in a child like a seed under snow, and every god in the waking world can smell it, the way wolves smell blood under a frozen river. You were born marked. Born *claimable*. Any power that found you first could have made you into whatever it wanted — a vessel, a weapon, a bride, a throne. Your father had seen it happen. He'd seen what the war-god did with a god-touched girl in the east, three hundred years ago. He wasn't going to let it happen to you."

"So he sold me instead."

"He *hid* you." Her mother's voice rose, cracked, dropped. "There is exactly one power in this world that every other power respects, and it is not strength, it is not love, it is *law*. A soul held by contract cannot be touched. Cannot be claimed, courted, corrupted, or stolen. The moment your name entered the Broker's ledger, you became the least interesting object in creation — a settled debt. Gods don't covet settled debts. They walk past them. That signature bought you twenty-two years of being ignored, and being ignored, Jude, is the finest gift anyone ever gave you."

"It didn't feel like a gift." Jude's voice came out low. "It felt like a valley with an invisible wall. It felt like prayers that never got answered. It felt like watching Widow Anthea die while the whole temple shrugged and said *that's the way of things*. You let me grow up believing the world was empty."

"The world *was* empty. For you. That was the point."

"And now it isn't." Jude stepped closer. "The moon's almost full. Vess told me. Thirteen turns, and the contract comes due, and the hiding ends. Was that in the terms too? Did you read that clause? Because I've read the original now, mother. I've seen your signature. I saw what else was on the page."

Her mother flinched — actually flinched, the first uncontrolled movement she'd made — and Jude pressed, because some cold new thing in her, some spire-born thing, wanted to press.

"There was a date. There was always a date. Thirteen moons after the old gods wake, the claim matures. Which means you didn't buy me time. You bought me a *countdown*. You hid me inside an hourglass."

* * *

### Maren

There it was. The sentence she had rehearsed answers to for two decades and never once believed she'd have to say aloud.

"Yes," Maren said. "The hiding was always temporary. Your father warned me. A contract can defer a nature, but it can't erase one. Thirteen moons after the waking, the seed comes up through the snow whether anyone wants it or not. The sale doesn't create the danger, Jude. The sale *postponed* it. We hoped — your father hoped — that by the time it matured, the world would be different. That the gods would settle. That there would be somewhere safe to bring you."

"And instead you stayed in the valley. Copying prayers. Watching me copy prayers. For twenty-two years. You could have told me. You could have prepared me."

"Told you what? That you were a lit wick walking around in a valley full of dry grass? That the only thing keeping the wolves off you was a piece of paper you couldn't see?" Maren heard her own voice sharpening and couldn't stop it. "Children can't keep secrets they don't know are secrets. One prayer said carelessly in the wrong direction — one moment of temper where something *answered* — and every sleeping god within a thousand miles lifts its head. Your safety lived in your ignorance. I chose your ignorance every single morning for twenty-two years. Every morning I woke up and chose it again, and every morning it cost me—"

She stopped. The spire was listening; it always listened; walls down here were witnesses. But maybe that was fitting. Let the record show.

"—it cost me my daughter," she finished quietly. "I paid for your life with the only version of you I was allowed to have. The one who would have known me. Who might have forgiven me. I sold that too. It was in the same contract. Clause nine. *The signatory waives all claim to gratitude.*"

Jude was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone strange — not angry, not soft. Careful. Reading-shaped.

"Clause nine," she repeated. "You memorized the clauses."

"I wrote half of them. Your father dictated; I drafted. I was better with words than he was. Gods speak in thunder; someone has to make it legible." Maren smiled, and the smile hurt her face. "Where do you think you learned to copy?"

Something moved behind her daughter's eyes — recognition, or its refusal.

"The falsified ledger," Jude said suddenly. "In the temple. The child they were going to indenture — Tam's daughter. You taught me the books. You taught me where the columns bend."

"I taught you that a ledger is a weapon that points whichever way the hand holds it. Yes." Maren stepped forward, close enough now to see the fine grey ring around her daughter's irises, her father's exact mark, the threshold-stone color. "And now you're going to hate me for what I say next, so I'll say it fast, the way you pull a splinter."

"Then say it."

"The contract can't be broken. I've spent twenty-two years looking for the flaw. There isn't one. The Broker drafts perfect instruments; it's why he's terrible; it's why he's eternal. But a contract is only as strong as its *enforcement*, and enforcement requires a court, and a court requires a broker, and brokers—" she took a breath — "brokers can be replaced. Amended. Overruled by their own instruments, if someone stands in the right place with the right authority and the right words. Your father believed it was possible. It's the last thing he said to me before he went under: *a signature is just a word that agreed to be trapped. Free the word, and the trap opens.*"

"You want me to fight him." Jude's voice was flat. "The god who owns my soul. With words."

"With *your* words." Maren reached out — slowly, telegraphing it, the way you approach a bird — and touched her daughter's hand. Under the skin, faint as a pulse under ice, she felt the divinity turning over, waking, answering the moon. "You rewrite reality like a ledger. I've watched you do it since you walked in that door — you don't know you're doing it. The holding room is warmer than it was. The window shows starlight now instead of rock. Look at the window, Jude."

Jude looked. The slit of stone had filled with a spill of white stars that did not exist beneath a mountain, and her daughter's face, lit by impossible light, went through wonder and terror in the space of a breath.

"I didn't do that," Jude whispered.

"You did it the way I taught you. You read the room, and you amended it." Maren tightened her grip. "Above us, in less than a day, that moon fills, and every power in this world comes down that corridor to decide what you're for. The court wants to enthrone you. The rebels want to arm you. The Broker wants to *employ* you. Everyone who ever wanted to own a god is about to arrive at your door. So before they come, you need to know the last thing, the true thing, the thing I signed away my right to tell you and am telling you anyway, damn the clause."

"Then tell me."

Maren held her daughter's face in her hands — her daughter, her whole forged, hidden, mortgaged life — and gave her the truth entire.

"Your father didn't die under this mountain. He was *filed*. The Broker keeps him in the deepest vault, in the oldest ink, as surety against your contract — because your father co-signed it, Jude. He bound himself alongside you. Break the contract, and you don't free yourself alone. You free the first god who ever loved you enough to sign himself into a cage."

Far above them, through a hundred floors of filed souls, the mountain shuddered — once, softly, like a throat clearing — and somewhere in the dark of the vaults, something that had been quiet for twenty-two years turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to answer the moon.
