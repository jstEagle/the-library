## 16. Thirteen Turns Complete

They dressed her in the dark.

Not cloth — ink. Two clerks-gods with faces like smoothed wax worked for hours, painting her arms and throat and the backs of her hands with lines of script so fine she could not read them even when she tried, though every other word in this place she could read at a glance now. Her own skin refused her. That seemed fitting.

"The moon completes its turn tonight," the taller clerk said. Its voice was a drawer sliding shut. "The hiding ends. What was written over will surface."

"And then?" Jude asked.

"Then you are what you were always meant to be." The clerk smiled without moving its mouth. "Filed."

* * *

They kept her in a cell that was not a cell. It had a bed, a desk, a window of frosted glass that showed nothing but white light, and no door that she could find, though doors appeared when the clerks wanted in. She had been there — how long? Days. The thirteenth moon had been nearly full when Vess's testimony failed and the court's guards took her from the wreckage of the raid, Sefa screaming her name down a corridor that folded itself closed behind them.

Her mother was somewhere above, in the spire, among the souls kept as ink. Jude had not been allowed to see her. She suspected she never would be, not truly — because whatever surfaced tonight would not be the girl who had copied prayers in a valley where nothing answered.

She sat at the desk and did what she had always done. She wrote.

No pen had been given her, so she used her finger on the desk's surface, and the words sank in like water into sand:

*My name is Jude Lockridge.*

The desk drank it and gave back nothing.

*My name is—*

She stopped. Her hand wouldn't finish it. Not wouldn't. *Couldn't.* There was something underneath the name her mother had given her, something packed down like sediment, and pressing against it felt like pressing on the underside of a frozen lake.

Outside the frosted glass, the light changed. Warmed. Went gold, then copper, then a deep arterial red.

The moon, filling.

Jude stood up. Her heart was doing something strange — beating in a rhythm she recognized from the scriptorium, from the cadence of the oldest prayers, the ones copied in red ink that no one was permitted to read aloud. Her blood had learned liturgy before it learned language. Of course it had. Look at whose blood it was.

"No," she said to the empty room, and heard her voice come out doubled, a chord instead of a note.

The lake under her name cracked.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the worst part. She had braced for fire, for tearing, for the price her rebels' magic always charged — memory for power, power for memory. But this was not borrowed. This had been hers since before she was born, banked and buried and accruing interest for nineteen years, and when it came up through her it came up like spring water, inevitable, sweet, and utterly indifferent to what it drowned.

She saw the room as it actually was: not a cell but a *form*, a document given walls, and herself the field waiting to be filled in. She saw the clerks-gods beyond the walls as columns of intent wearing bodies the way officials wore badges. She saw the whole mountain as a ledger standing on end, millions of entries, and threading through all of it one line written in a hand she knew better than her own —

because it *was* her own. An older hand. A truer one. Written before her mother ever signed anything.

*Jude,* said the line, in the old tongue of the first contracts, the tongue that predated the valley and its forgetting. Except it wasn't Jude. It was the name-before-the-name, the one sold along with her soul, and it rose in her throat and she spoke it aloud and the frosted glass shattered into letters.

Every alarm in the mountain was a bell made of language, and they all began to ring her.

* * *

The door appeared. Through it came the Broker.

He looked exactly as he had in the cathedral of filing-cabinets: courteous, gray-suited, terrible in the way that arithmetic is terrible. But tonight his courtesy had a hairline crack in it, and Jude — who could now read anything — read it easily.

He was afraid.

"Welcome," he said, "to your majority."

"I want my soul," Jude said. Her voice still came doubled. "I want my mother. I want every contract in this mountain burned."

"Yes," the Broker said mildly. "I expected you'd say something like that. It's in your file. Everything you will ever say is in your file; I wrote most of it decades ago." He gestured, and the broken glass reassembled itself into a mirror, and in the mirror Jude saw what the clerks had spent days painting onto her skin: not decoration but *binding*. Lines of enforcement clause wrapped around her wrists like cuffs. Terms of service ran up her spine.

"You're enthroning me," she said.

"We are *installing* you." He corrected her the way he corrected everyone, gently, irreversibly. "A god cannot be controlled. But a god can be given a shape, a schedule, a signature line. You will be the face of this court. Mortals will pray to something they can see. You will answer — according to protocol, according to price. And the old system continues, cleaner than ever, because no one suspects the kindly face." He tilted his head. "You wanted justice for the sold. Here is your chance to administer it. Forever. On our terms."

"That's not justice. That's a desk."

"It's the only desk there is." For a moment something moved behind his face — weariness, maybe, or envy. "I sat where you stand once. Someone installed me too."

The moonlight through the broken window went from red to white. Full. Complete.

And the mountain shook.

* * *

Not an earthquake. An *argument.* Somewhere far above, stone that had been sworn to silence for three hundred years was being unsworn, and the unswearing traveled down through the rock like a shout through a house. Dust fell from seams in the ceiling that had no right to have seams. The Broker turned his head, listening, and Jude read the sound the way she now read everything:

*Sefa.*

The rebel leader had found another way in. Of course she had. While the court prepared its coronation, Sefa had been doing what Sefa always did — treating every wall as a negotiation and every negotiation as a siege. The breach-songs, the god-touched chanting fragments of waking divinity, were punching holes in the deep halls, and through the holes came fire and iron and people who had decided, in their grief, that the only honest prayer was a battering ram.

"They'll kill you," the Broker said. "Rebels don't distinguish between gods. They especially won't distinguish between a god wearing the face of a scribe who betrayed them."

"She didn't betray me," Jude said. "I left. There's a difference, and you of all beings should be able to parse it."

Something detonated two halls away. The door-frame rippled. The clerks-gods poured in, wax faces calm, and formed up around Jude like a filing cabinet closing.

"To the throne room," the Broker said. "Now. Before—"

The ceiling came down.

Not collapsed — *rewritten.* The stone above them converted itself mid-fall into a rain of burning paper, each sheet a page torn from some vast ledger, and through the smoke dropped figures in ash-gray cloaks, and at their head, haloed in stolen light, stood Sefa with a shard of shrine-glass in one fist and murder in her face.

"Jude," she said.

"Sefa." Jude raised her hands, half surrender, half warning. "Whatever you think I am now—"

"I know exactly what you are now." Sefa's eyes were doing something Jude had seen only in the deepest god-touched: glowing from underneath, lit by the fragment she channeled. "I can *see* it. We all can. You're lit up like a struck match, and every soul in this mountain just felt you ignite." She spat. "We came for the master ledger. But we'll take the new god too. Think what the cause becomes with *you* at the front of it. No more hiding in shrines. No more tithing children out of fear. A god who fights *for* us."

"I'm not a weapon."

"Everything's a weapon," Sefa said, "if you hold it right. Ask him." She pointed the shard at the Broker. "That's all he ever was. A tool somebody picked up and forgot to put down."

The clerks-gods moved. The rebels met them. And the deep hall of the court beneath the mountain became a war fought in two languages at once — steel and scripture — while Jude stood in the middle of it, blazing, unclaimed, and more alone than she had ever been in the valley where nothing answered.

* * *

She fought without wanting to. That was the horror of it: her new nature defended itself. When a rebel's blade came at her, the air between them rewrote itself into a clause of non-approach and the blade stopped as if it had hit glass. When a clerk-god seized her arm, the binding-script on her skin flared and the clerk's hand came away as loose calligraphy drifting to the floor. She was a paragraph no one could edit, and both armies were learning it at the same time, and both armies adjusted the same way:

They started trying to *own* her instead of kill her.

"Take her alive!" — Sefa, dragging a wounded god-touched girl behind a pillar.

"Protect the vessel!" — the clerks, reforming into a wedge.

"Jude!" — the Broker, from the doorway, holding out a single sheet of paper that glowed like a coal. "The installation order! Sign it and none of this matters! They can't touch what's filed!"

The hall buckled. Somewhere above, the spire was singing — she could feel it now, feel *everything*, the whole mountain's architecture of debt groaning under assault. Sefa's forces had breached three levels. Court enforcers were counter-writing corridors into mazes. And through it all, the thirteenth moon poured its full light down through a mile of rock as if stone were no obstacle at all, because for light like that, it wasn't.

Jude caught Sefa's eye across the chaos. And Sefa — brilliant, ruthless, grieving Sefa — did the last thing Jude expected.

She hesitated.

Just a moment. Just long enough for Jude to see it: underneath the ambition, underneath the stolen god-light, the leader of the god-touched was afraid of what she was asking for. Afraid of the thing she was trying to put at the front of her army. Afraid because she could see, glowing under Jude's skin, the same truth Jude was only now understanding —

that what had ignited tonight was not a girl who happened to have a god's blood.

It was a god who happened to have a girl's memories. And whichever one won the argument would decide what happened to every soul in this mountain.

The pillar beside Jude exploded. Through the smoke came the clerks in their wedge formation, and behind them the Broker, no longer pretending at courtesy, his gray suit unraveling into something older and vaster, a shape made of terms and conditions, reaching for her with a pen the length of a spear.

"Sign," the Broker said, in the voice that had drafted every desperate bargain for three thousand years, "or be *drafted.*"

Jude looked at the pen. At Sefa's outstretched hand. At the burning hall where everything she loved or owed or feared had converged on the single point of her skin.

Everyone wants to own me, she thought. Fine.

Then let them learn what I own.

She reached past the Broker's pen — past the offered throne, past Sefa's army, past the clerks and their bindings — and put her hand flat against the wall of the deep hall, against the living ledger of the mountain itself, and began, with one finger, to write.
