## 21. The Long Walk Home

The road out of the mountain was littered with paper.

Not ash — paper. Pages of contracts, prayers, promissory notes, all of them blank now, their ink drained back into the world when the ledger amended itself. They lay in drifts against the rocks like snow that had forgotten which way was up. Jude walked through them and they did not stir. Even the wind had stopped collecting.

She had expected to feel triumphant. She had rehearsed it on the long climb from the deep halls, Vess limping beside her with the amended ledger strapped to his back like a relic: *this is what victory feels like, this lightness, this clean air.* But what she felt instead was the absence of something she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for a spine. Her magic was gone. Not sleeping, not sealed — gone, burned away with the spire, spent in a single act of amendment that had cost more than any working before it. When she reached for the language of rewriting, the language answered with silence.

She was, for the first time in her life, only herself.

It should have been enough. Some days, walking, it almost was.

* * *

The first village on the road home was called Harrow's Bend, though no one she asked could say who Harrow had been or where the bend went. It didn't matter. What mattered was the shrine.

Jude had passed shrines like it on the way down — squat stone structures with iron grates over their offering slots, tended by priests who weighed children's hands like butchers weighing cuts. This one stood open. The grate had been pried off and left leaning against the wall, rusted through in places, and inside there was nothing. No coffer of stored prayers. No jars of soul-ink. Just an empty alcove swept clean, and someone had chalked a single word above it:

*FREE.*

A woman sat outside the shrine mending a net, and when Jude asked her about it, the woman laughed — not bitterly, just tired, the laugh of someone who has been surprised too many times to be startled anymore.

"Three weeks ago," she said. "Woke up one morning and every debt in the village was just — paper. The tithe-collector came and read his book and the book said nothing. He read it again. He cried, if you want to know. Sat right there on that step and wept like his own mother had died." She bit her thread. "Maybe she had. Maybe that book was the closest thing he had."

"And the children?" Jude asked. "The ones already given?"

The woman's needle paused. "Some came home," she said. "Walking down from the mountain like sleepers, not knowing how long they'd been gone. Some didn't." She looked at Jude then, really looked, at the road-dust and the hollowed-out look that comes from walking out of a collapsing god-house. "You were up there, weren't you."

"I was up there."

"Did you do it?"

"I amended a contract," Jude said. "I struck the clauses that let desperate people be sold."

The woman nodded slowly, as if this were a perfectly ordinary thing to have done, and went back to her net. "Well," she said. "Someone had to hold the pen."

That night she fed Jude stew and wouldn't take payment, and in the morning Jude found her boots had been oiled and her water skins filled, and chalked beside the shrine's door, small, where only she would see it: *thank you.*

* * *

The second week, the roads filled with the god-touched — or the people who had been god-touched, which was not the same thing.

Jude met the first of them at a crossroads well: a young man sitting on the stones with his hands open in his lap, staring at his palms as though expecting them to explain themselves. When she drew water, he spoke without looking up.

"Mine was a river god," he said. "Small one. A creek, really, if I'm honest. I could taste rain three days before it fell." He flexed his fingers. "Now I can't even tell you if it'll drizzle. I keep waiting to feel him move in me and there's just — me. Only me." He said it like a man describing a phantom limb, grief and relief fighting for the same breath.

"It gets quieter," Jude said. "But it doesn't get worse."

"You'd know."

"I'd know."

He looked up at her then, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod, the way soldiers nod at each other across a field. He offered her his flask. They drank in silence, two people standing in the wreckage of borrowed power, and when she left he was still sitting at the well, but his shoulders had come down from around his ears.

There were others. A woman in a market town who had channeled a hearth goddess and now simply ran a bakery, flour to her elbows, laughing louder than anyone Jude had heard in years. A pair of twins who had spoken prophecy in unison and now argued cheerfully about everything else. An old soldier whose war-god had left him mid-battle, who told Jude he'd dropped his sword and sat down in the mud and laughed until his ribs hurt, because for the first time in forty years no one was shouting through his mouth.

"They're ordinary," Vess said one evening, watching a god-touched girl skip rope with other children, no glow, no whisper, nothing but a girl counting under her breath. He had the ledger open on his knees, making notes in the margins — his testimony, still growing, still being written. "All that power, all that terror, and they're just people."

"That was always the point," Jude said. "They were never meant to be vessels. Someone made them into one because it was cheaper than doing the work yourself."

Vess was quiet for a while. Then: "My sister would have liked this road."

Jude didn't ask. Some debts are paid just by being witnessed, and Vess had spent three hundred miles witnessing hers, reading the court's crimes aloud in his flat clerk's voice until the words lost their power to frighten anyone. She put her hand briefly on his shoulder, and he pretended not to notice, and both of them let it stand.

* * *

She found Sefa in the ruins of a rebel camp in the foothills, three days' walk below the tree line.

Jude knew it was Sefa before she saw her face — knew it by the silence. The camp had been a city once, tents and banners and the crackle of stolen divinity; now it was cold fire rings and trampled grass and maybe forty people packing up the last of their lives. And in the middle of it, sitting alone on a flat stone like a queen on a throne that had been reduced to furniture, Sefa.

Her power was gone. That was the first thing. On the road down, Sefa had burned like a bonfire wearing a woman's shape, fragments of five waking gods crammed into one body, and you could feel her from a mile off, a pressure behind the eyes. Now she was just a woman in a good coat, thin, with grey coming in early at her temples, and her hands — her hands were folded in her lap like anyone's.

Jude stopped ten paces away. Around them, the rebels went still, watching.

"You're alive," Sefa said.

"So are you."

"Barely counts." Sefa's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the kind that lives in the same house as grief. "When the spire came down, everything it held came loose. The god-fragments, the contracts, all of it. We woke up the next morning and couldn't remember why we'd been so angry." She unfolded her hands, studied them. "No. That's a lie. I remember exactly why. I just can't do anything about it anymore."

"The master ledger—"

"Is ash. Or blank paper. Same thing." Sefa looked up, and her eyes were clear in a way Jude had never seen them — no fire behind them, no throne reflected there, just a tired woman who had wanted to save the world and nearly become another thing the world needed saving from. "You knew. Back at the bargain. You looked at me and you knew what I was going to do with it, and you walked away anyway, and you were right, and I hate that you were right."

"I know," Jude said.

"Do you want thanks? Because I don't have much to give. A coat, maybe. Half a camp of people learning to farm."

"I don't want thanks."

"No," Sefa said softly. "You never did. That was the whole problem with you." She stood, and it cost her something — Jude saw the stiffness, the new fragility of a body that had been a weapon and was now just a body. She crossed the distance between them and stopped close, searching Jude's face. "The Broker's dead. The spire's down. Every contract in the world is void. And you're walking home to a valley full of people who sold you before you were born and called it faith." Her voice dropped. "What are you going to do there?"

"Teach children to write their own names," Jude said. "And make sure no one ever signs anything they don't understand."

Sefa studied her a long moment. Then she stepped back, and something in her posture settled — not surrender, not forgiveness, but the end of a war, which is its own strange peace. She gave a short nod, the kind given between equals, or between people who might have been enemies and chose, at the last possible moment, not to be.

Jude walked past her, through the camp, and Sefa watched her go without a word. She was still watching when Jude reached the tree line. She did not turn around to check. Some things you have to trust the silence to hold.

* * *

The valley line appeared on the nineteenth day, the way it always had: a shimmer in the air like heat off summer stone, running along the ridge where the birds used to turn back.

Except the birds didn't turn back anymore.

Jude stood at the boundary and watched a flock of starlings cross it without hesitation, pouring over the ridge like water finding its level, and she understood that the valley's forgetting was over. Whatever had kept magic out — her mother's bargain, the Broker's clause, some old mercy or some old prison — it had been part of the same web of contracts, and she had cut it with all the rest. The valley was open now. Magic could follow her home.

If there were any magic left to follow.

She raised her hand and pressed it against the boundary, half-expecting resistance, the old invisible wall that had defined her whole life. There was nothing. Her fingers passed through air into air, and the only difference between one side and the other was that here, finally, she could name it.

Here, she was home.

* * *

She camped that night at the valley's edge, and in the morning she took out her journal — the cheap one, bought in Harrow's Bend, its pages soft with damp — and she sharpened her pen, and she sat for a long time looking at the blank page.

Because there was one thing left to write.

Her whole life, her name had been a borrowed coat. *Jude Lockridge*, the name her mother gave her after the sale, the name that fit the ledgers and the temple rolls and the prayer-copies, the name that was safe because it belonged to no one — a shell drawn over the name-before-birth, the true name her mother had sold, the name the Broker had kept in his files like a deed to a house. She had learned the true name in the deep halls. She had spoken it once, at the moment of amendment, when the contract required it, and the speaking of it had been like striking a bell inside her own chest.

She had not written it since. Writing makes things real. Writing makes things binding. She had spent her life among people who understood that better than any god.

But the contracts were void now. All of them. Struck through in her own hand, witnessed by a clerk who had turned his testimony against his masters. There was no enforcement left in the world. No one could collect on ink anymore.

Which meant that for the first time in history, a name was just a name. Hers was just hers.

Jude dipped her pen.

She wrote slowly, carefully, the way she'd been taught to copy prayers — every letter deliberate, every stroke honest. And it was a strange name, a name with the mountain in it, a name that sounded like wind through high passes and old cold stars, a name no temple scribe would have given and no ledger could have predicted. It took her most of a page. When she finished, she sat back and read it, and read it again, and waited to feel different — transformed, crowned, remade.

She didn't. She felt like a tired woman on a cold morning with ink on her fingers.

And that, she realized, was the miracle. The name changed nothing because it had never been a chain. It had only ever been a word, and the word was hers now, and she was still just Jude — the girl who falsified a ledger entry to save a child, the copyist who loved the letters more than the prayers, the woman who had held the pen when someone had to.

Below the name, she wrote a second line, smaller:

*Borrowed nothing. Owed nothing. Home.*

Then she closed the journal, shouldered her pack, and started down into the valley as the sun came up behind her and lit the whole basin gold — the temple, the terraces, the river, the little houses with their smoke rising straight into still air.

Halfway down the slope, she stopped.

At the valley line, far behind her and slightly uphill, at the exact place where she had crossed the boundary the evening before, something was sitting. Something grey and large and patient, shaped like a wolf, watching her with eyes that held — for just a heartbeat, in the early light — the glint of a filing cabinet, the gleam of a golden seal, the terrible courtesy of a god who drafts contracts.

Then it blinked, and it was only a wolf. Probably.

It did not follow her. It did not leave. It settled its chin on its paws at the edge of her mother's valley, at the border of the world she had freed, and it watched her walk down toward home — and Jude, who had refused a throne and unraveled a god and rewritten the law of the sold, stood in the morning sun and made the last decision of her long account:

She let it stay.
