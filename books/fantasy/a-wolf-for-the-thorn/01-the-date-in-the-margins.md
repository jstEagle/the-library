## 1. The Date in the Margins

The storm-glass sang to her all night, the way it always did.

Coral Zhou felt it through the layers of oilcloth and straw in the mule's panniers — a thin, restless keening, like wine glasses rubbed by a wet finger, like a choir of dead women humming in their sleep. Most people couldn't hear it. Most people had never stood inside a living storm and been *listened to*. She had, at fourteen, on the salt flats of Vey, and the storm had left something behind in her blood that never quite stopped turning over.

That was why she was here, three days into the high passes of Thornfell with a cargo worth more than her life, listening to glass sing.

"Quiet," she murmured to the mule, or to the glass, or to herself. The distinction had stopped mattering years ago.

The pass narrowed ahead where an old avalanche had chewed the mountainside down to scree and bone-white granite. Above, the sky was doing something she didn't like. Clouds stacking themselves into towers, pressure dropping in a way that made her back teeth ache. A storm coming out of the north with her name half-written in its winds — she could feel it circling her like a dog that remembered being kicked.

She needed shelter before dark. Not for the cold; exile had cured her of delicacy years ago. For the glass. Storm-glass that sat in a lightning-charged atmosphere too long started to *wake*, and waking storm-glass did not stay smuggled for long. It drew the sky down on top of itself.

And it drew wardens.

Coral crested the shoulder of the pass as the first fat drops came down, and there — a miracle of bad architecture clinging to the leeward slope — stood a ruin. A long, low hall roofed in slate gone green with moss, one wall collapsed into a drift of stone, windows like empty eye sockets. Some pious lord's folly from before the Thorn kings unified the passes. She'd sheltered in a dozen such ruins between Vey and the capital and never thought twice about them.

But this one had columns carved with letters.

She saw them properly only when she was close enough to lead the mule under the surviving portico: script chiseled deep into the stone, weathered nearly smooth, in the old high-hand that smugglers used on forged writs because nobody alive could read it well enough to catch the frauds. A library, then. Or whatever passed for one this far above the treeline.

"Fine," she told the building. "But if you fall on my head, I'm haunting you."

Inside, the great room had lost its roof in one long gash down the middle, so rain fell in silver threads across a floor of rotted shelving and drifted leaves. But the eastern corner still held. Coral got the mule settled, saw to the panniers, checked the wards she'd stitched into the oilcloth — small work, a trickle of her gift, nothing that would show on any warden's compass — and then, with the storm booming somewhere over the northern peaks and the light going blue and strange, she let herself look at what remained of the books.

Most were pulp. Rain and frost and a century of mice had reduced them to gray felt. But along the inner wall, in a niche where the collapse had sheltered them, a few volumes survived in leather gone stiff as bark. She pulled one free and cracked it, and dust breathed out at her with a smell like old churches and older secrets.

A ledger, she thought at first. Then, squinting in the failing light: no. Marginalia. Someone had loved this book once, filled its margins with a cramped, slanting hand, arguing with the printed text page by page. She almost put it back. Smugglers didn't read; smugglers counted, weighed, watched horizons. Reading was for people with roofs.

But the rain had settled in for the night, the glass was quiet under its wards, and the blue light through the broken roof was doing odd things to her eyes. She turned pages by touch more than sight until she reached a section near the end — not printed text but the marginalia alone, dense, urgent, the writer pressing so hard the stylus had torn the paper in places.

*The ninth storm-year of Aldric's line,* someone had written in that cramped hand, *and the crown drinks deeper each winter.*

Coral frowned. Aldric. The king's name — though there had been Aldrics in Thornfell the way the sea has waves. She turned the page.

*It names its vessel. It names its debts. And beneath the crown it waits, patient as frost, for the wolf it means to wear.*

She should have closed the book then. Later, in the long ledger of things she would regret, she would mark this as the moment — not when she read the prophecy, but when she chose to keep reading after the first cold finger touched the back of her neck.

Because the next passage wasn't marginalia anymore. The letters had shifted on the page — she would swear they had shifted — the cramped slanting hand gathering itself into a single column of tall, severe script that seemed to sit slightly *above* the paper's surface, the way frost sits on a window without touching the warmth behind it.

*In the hundredth year of the binding, a caller comes out of exile, carrying fire of the sky in forbidden glass.*

Coral laughed. It came out wrong, thin in the big empty room.

"Smuggler's trick," she said aloud, and the rain swallowed her voice. Of course it was a trick. Old ruins on smuggling routes were full of traps for the credulous — fake relics, forged maps, little theatres of ink arranged to spook a carrier into dumping her cargo or paying a toll to some invented brotherhood. She'd seen forged prophecies before. One in Saltreach had named *her*, too, come to think of it, and demanded a donation.

She read on anyway. Her fingers were cold. That was the only reason.

*On the night the north wind breaks the passes —*

Her breath stopped.

Outside, exactly on cue, absurdly, horribly, the north wind hit the ruined hall with a sound like a gate tearing off its hinges. Rain drove sideways through the roof-gash. The mule screamed once, briefly, and went silent.

*— she shall stand beneath a broken roof among dead words.*

Coral looked up at the hole in the roof. Looked at the ruined shelves. At the dead book in her hands.

"No," she said.

*And the date written in the sky is the date written here:* ***the seventeenth night of the Reed Moon, in the year the storm returns to Vey.***

Her hands were shaking. She set the book down on a dry shelf with exaggerated care, the way you set down a snake, and stepped back, and told herself very reasonably that the seventeenth of the Reed Moon was *tonight*, yes, fine, and that storms broke the passes every week this season, and that exiled storm-callers out of Vey were rarer than honest wardens but not rare enough that some clever forger couldn't have guessed —

Guessing wasn't the word that fit. Nothing fit. The passage had known the roof would be broken. Had known the *wind*.

And then the words moved.

Not a trick of the light. Not exhaustion, not the blue storm-light flickering through the gash. The letters gathered themselves, unhurried, deliberate — ink crawling like insects deciding on a new formation — and rearranged into lines she hadn't read yet, lines that had not existed a moment before:

***She will know the wolf beneath the crown. On this night the knife is raised against King Aldric of Thornfell, in his own house, by the hand the prophecy has already chosen. Whether he dies is not yet written.***

The last line hung on the page, trembling faintly, like a plucked string.

Whether he dies is not yet written.

Coral became aware that she had backed against the far wall, that her gift had risen without her permission — static crackling along her forearms, the fine hairs of her arms lifting, the whole ruined hall smelling of ozone. The storm-glass in the panniers had begun to sing again, loud now, harmonizing with the tempest outside, and beneath its song she heard — or thought she heard, and would spend many nights deciding whether she'd truly heard — a voice like the space between lightning and thunder:

*Come and see, storm-bringer. Come and choose.*

"I don't choose anything," she said to the empty hall, and hated how young her voice sounded. "I carry things. That's all I am. I carry things from one place to another and nobody dies."

The ink had stilled. The book sat on its shelf like any dead thing, and by the storm-light she could see the marginalia had returned to their original cramped hand, as if the other script had never worn those letters at all.

But the last line remained. Standing slightly proud of the paper. Slightly *above* it.

Whether he dies is not yet written.

Coral stood in the ruined library for a long time while the storm wore itself out against the mountain. She thought about wardens' compasses and the price of storm-glass and the ten safe-houses between here and the capital, and she told herself she was calculating routes, because calculating was what kept her alive, and calculation had no room for prophecy.

But underneath the arithmetic, another thought was turning over, slow and cold as the glass singing in its panniers: *King Aldric of Thornfell.* The third of that name. Young still, crowned barely nine years. Whatever else he was — and the smugglers' roads said little good of him, said his edicts grew stranger each winter, said his eyes had gone flat as coin — he was a man, and tonight, according to a century-dead hand in a century-dead book, a knife waited for him in his own house.

And she, Coral Zhou, exile, carrier of contraband, woman who wanted nothing from any throne, was apparently standing in the wrong ruin on the wrong night holding the only sentence in the world that could stop it.

She could walk away. That was the thought she clung to as she saddled the mule in the gray hour before dawn, the storm spent, the passes silent. Walk away, deliver the glass, collect her fee, disappear deeper into the lawless margins where names in books couldn't reach. Prophecies were for kings and fools.

But when she swung up into the saddle and turned the mule's head toward the high road — the road that ran, in the end, down out of the mountains, past the Sorrow Bridge, toward the capital where King Aldric slept uneasily beneath his whispering crown — she found she was already pointing that way.

The book lay open on the shelf behind her. She hadn't closed it. She didn't remember leaving it open.

And as she rode out into the pale morning, the last line gleamed faintly in the dark of the ruined hall, patient as frost, waiting to be finished:

*Whether he dies is not yet written.*
