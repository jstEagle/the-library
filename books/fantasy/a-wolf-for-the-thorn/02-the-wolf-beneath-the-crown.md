## 2. The Wolf Beneath the Crown

King Aldric of Thornfell woke with blood on his hands.

Not a great deal of it — that was the worst part. A smear across the heel of his palm, a dark crescent beneath two fingernails, as if he had gripped something living and held on. He lay in the vast dark of the royal bedchamber with the canopy hanging above him like a closed eye, and he did not move, because moving would make it real, and not moving let him pretend, for one more breath, one more, that the blood was a dream.

The candles had burned to stubs. He had no memory of lighting them, no memory of snuffing them, no memory of the hours between his going to bed — he had gone to bed, he was nearly certain, he remembered the weight of the blankets and the taste of the plum wine — and this moment now, with grey light seeping through the shutters and his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

He raised his hands into the light.

The blood had dried to the color of old rust. He turned them over slowly, the way a man turns over a card he already knows will ruin him.

*Debts owed,* said the crown.

It sat on the side table where the chamberlain always placed it at night, iron and black garnet, older than the dynasty that wore it. It did not speak in words, exactly. It spoke in the way a headache speaks, in pressure and certainty, in meanings that arrived already believed. *Debts owed, debts owed, the ledger is long and the ink is not dry.*

"There are no debts," Aldric whispered. His voice came out sanded raw. "I am the king. Kings do not owe."

*All kings owe. This one most of all.*

He got up. His legs were steady, which frightened him more than stumbling would have — his body had been somewhere in the night, and had come home tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking. At the basin he scrubbed his hands until the water went pink, then grey, then clear, and still he scrubbed, because the blood was gone but the feeling of it was not, the tacky memory of it against his skin.

In the mirror, a man looked back at him who might have been a king. Forty-one years old, broad through the shoulder, his father's jaw and his mother's tired eyes. There was a scratch on his throat he did not remember earning. Thin, shallow, already closing. He touched it and his fingertip came away with the faintest shine.

*She fought,* the crown observed, from the table behind him. It was not in the mirror. It was never in the mirror. That was the one mercy of the thing. *She fought, and she lost, and the ledger takes no note of losing.*

"Who?" His voice cracked on it. "Who fought? Who did I—" He stopped. He could not finish it. He turned around and looked at the crown and the crown looked back the way a well looks back, deep and patient and full of dark water. "What did I do last night?"

*You slept,* said the crown. *You slept, and the debt was paid, and the ledger grows shorter, and soon, soon, soon, there will be nothing owed at all.*

* * *

The court of Thornfell hid its fear behind ceremony the way a wounded animal hid its blood beneath its fur.

Aldric had learned to read it over the past two years — since the coronation, since the moment the iron band first touched his brow and something old had poured itself into the space behind his eyes like water finding a crack. He read it now in the way the chamberlain stood too far from him when he took his morning meal. In the way the steward announced the day's petitions with his eyes fixed on the middle distance, as if looking directly at the king might invite the king to look back. In the way the guards at the hall doors had stopped meeting his gaze entirely.

They knew something. Not everything — he was almost sure they did not know everything — but the court had developed a fine instinct for the shape of its king's absences. There were nights he did not come to his bed. There were mornings the servants found doors unlocked that had been barred, candles burned in rooms no one had ordered, mud on the private stair. There were the disappearances, of course. A scullery girl in the autumn. A gate captain at midwinter. A poet, just last month, who had been granted an audience and had walked out of it — or been carried out of it, the story said — and was never seen again.

The court had stopped asking. That was the ceremony's true purpose, Aldric understood now. Not to honor him. To avoid him. To fill the hours with ritual so that no hour was left empty enough for a question.

"Your Majesty." Chancellor Vess bowed at the precise angle that meant *I have something to say that I do not wish to say.* She was a narrow woman, grey as a heron, and she had served his father and his father's father, and she was the only person in the palace who still looked him in the eye. He had never decided whether that was courage or a hunter's steadiness. "The mourning festival preparations proceed. The bells of the lower city will be recast by week's end."

"Mourning for whom?" he asked, and heard, a half-second too late, how the question sounded. Vess's face did not change, but something behind it did, some small door closing.

"For the Queen Mother, Majesty. As decreed. A year ago." A pause, thin as a knife. "By Your Majesty's own hand."

"Of course." He smiled. He had gotten very good at smiling. It was like operating someone else's face. "The bells, then. See it done."

She withdrew. The hall emptied by degrees, courtiers flowing out in their silks and their careful silence, and Aldric remained on the throne with his hands on its arms and the crown on his head — he could not remember putting it on, that was another thing, the crown migrated to him the way cold migrated through a wall — and beneath him, through the stone, he felt the throne's old iron heart beating.

*The bells,* the crown said, amused. *They cast bells to drown me out. As if I lived in sound.*

"Where do you live?" he asked it, silently, the way he had learned to. Speaking aloud drew attention. Attention drew questions. Questions drew the spymaster, and the spymaster drew — he did not know what the spymaster drew. That was the deepest dread of all: that the thing in the crown was not alone in the palace. That it had hands.

*I live in the debt,* the crown said. *I live in the space between what was promised and what was paid. Your great-grandfather's great-grandfather made a bargain, little king, and the bargain is not yet finished, and I am the interest that accrues.*

"I have read the archives. There is no bargain."

*You have read the archives that remain.*

He closed his eyes. Behind his lids, unbidden, came the night again — not as memory, because it was not his memory, but as an afterimage, the way a bright window stays printed on the eye. A corridor. Wet stone. A woman's voice, sharp with fear, saying words he could not keep hold of no matter how he reached for them, words that slid through his grasp like fish. His own hands, reaching.

*The ledger,* the crown murmured, almost tenderly, *is shorter this morning. Be grateful.*

* * *

He found one hour of the day that was his. One hour, in the late afternoon, when the sun came through the west windows of the old scriptorium and the court had no ceremony to perform. He had claimed it under the pretext of reviewing charters. He did not review charters.

He wrote letters.

Not letters anyone would read — he burned them after, always, feeding them to the scriptorium's small fire page by page. Letters to himself, from himself, written in a code he had built from his childhood games with his half-brother, from the nonsense ciphers two boys had invented to pass notes beneath their tutors' noses. Wren. The name was a stone in his chest. Wren, who had been sent to the border garrisons two years ago at the crown's *suggestion*, who wrote him dutiful, empty letters that Aldric could not answer honestly because he did not know which of his own sentences the thing would read.

*The wolf watches the door,* he wrote. *The wolf remembers the games. If the wolf still plays, the wolf should look to the thorn, not the crown. The thorn holds the truth. The thorn bleeds.*

He did not fully know what he meant. That was the terror and the strategy of it both — he wrote in the fog, trusting that some part of him that was still whole understood more than the part that sat on the throne. Sometimes he found sentences in his own hand from previous days that he could not remember writing, and they frightened him until he decided, deliberately, to believe they were his own — the hidden self, the buried king, leaving messages up through the water like a drowning man releasing bubbles.

The fire took the letter. He watched the paper curl and go.

"Brother."

He turned so fast he knocked the inkwell. Wren stood in the doorway — Wren, here, in the capital, in the flesh, tall and dark and wearing a border officer's plain coat as if it were armor — and for one blazing moment Aldric felt joy, pure and uncomplicated, the first in two years.

Then he felt the crown turn its attention, slow as a lighthouse beam, toward the door.

*Ah,* it said. *The wolf comes home.*

"You should not be here," Aldric said, and his voice did what he could not stop it from doing: went cold, went royal, went *other*. He watched his brother's face register it. He watched the hope in Wren's eyes bank itself down to coals. *Run,* he wanted to say. *Turn around. Get out of the light.* "The garrison at Vael Crossing cannot spare its officers."

"The garrison can spare whatever I tell it to spare." Wren came into the room anyway, fool, brave fool, and shut the door behind him. "I've come for the mourning festival. A man should mourn his family."

"A man should stay where he is safe."

Something moved behind Wren's eyes — suspicion, or its beginning. "That's a strange thing for a king to say."

*He is marked,* the crown said, and there was a hunger in it now that made Aldric's stomach turn over, an appetite like the smell of a butcher's shop. *He has always been marked. The ledger remembers the wolf even when the wolf forgets himself. Bring him closer, little king. Let me look at what is owed.*

Aldric gripped the edge of the desk until the wood bit his palms. He thought of the blood under his fingernails. He thought of the woman's voice in the wet corridor. He thought: *it wants him, and it will use me to take him, and I will watch from inside my own skull as it does.*

"Brother," he said, and heard the crown's coldness in his own mouth, fighting it, losing, "welcome home."

* * *

That night the dreams came, and in the dreams he was the corridor again, and the corridor was full of rain that fell upward, and somewhere ahead of him a storm was learning to speak a name.

He woke at midnight with the crown in his hands.

He had not taken it from the table. He had not left his bed. And yet he knelt on the cold flagstones in the dark, holding it, and the garnets were warm as living things, and the whisper had never been so clear, so patient, so certain:

*The date approaches. The storm-bringer walks the high passes. The wolf is in the city. The ledger is nearly full, little king. Soon you may sleep.*

And far below, in the throne room, in the dark, the old iron heart of the throne began — softly, sweetly, like a bell being recast — to ring.
