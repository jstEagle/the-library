## 3. Ambush at the Sorrow Bridge

The prophecy's geography held for three days, and that was the worst of it.

Coral had spent a decade learning which maps lied. Smugglers' maps lied about roads; crown maps lied about borders; the old surveyor's charts in her father's satchel lied about everything except where the bodies were buried. But the words that had rearranged themselves in the ruined library — *the storm-bringer shall stand upon the Sorrow Bridge when the moon is dark* — those had not lied at all. She had walked north out of the high passes with the banned storm-glass still strapped to her mule's back, following a dead woman's sentence like a thread through a labyrinth, and every landmark arrived exactly when the prophecy said it would.

The split oak struck by lightning. The shrine to a god whose name had been chiseled away. The river running red-brown with iron from the old mines.

And then, on the third evening, under a sky the color of a bruise going green: the Sorrow Bridge.

It was a miserable piece of engineering, all things considered. Six arches of black mountain stone spanning a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was only a sound, a long hiss like a snake that never finished speaking. Fog pooled in the gorge and rose halfway up the piers, so the bridge seemed to float on nothing, a road laid across cloud. Someone had hung iron bells along the railings once — pilgrims' offerings, centuries ago — but the bells were long since rusted silent, their clappers fused with corrosion, their mouths stopped with moss. A bridge full of bells that could not ring. Coral thought there was probably a sermon in that, if she'd been the sort of person who wanted sermons.

She was not. She was the sort of person who crouched behind the last lightning-split oak for half an hour before crossing, watching the far end of the bridge with a smuggler's patience, because a sentence that named you and a date did not get to dictate your death without an argument.

Nothing moved. The fog breathed. The mute bells dripped.

"Fine," she told the oak. "But I'm walking in the middle, where the sightlines are bad."

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. The storm-glass in its wrappings on the mule hummed faintly against her hip where she'd taken one shard to carry — a sliver no longer than her finger, wrapped in wool and waxed leather, warm as a sleeping bird. Storm-glass always knew weather before weather did. Tonight it had been humming since noon.

She led the mule onto the stone.

Halfway across — of course halfway, the prophecy would have insisted on poetry — the fog ahead of her opened like a curtain being drawn by careful hands, and four figures stood at the bridge's far end.

They had not been there a moment ago. She was certain of it, and she was rarely wrong about empty ground; noticing what wasn't there was half her trade. These had come up out of the gorge itself, or out of some fold in the air, and they stood in a loose crescent with the ease of men who had done this many times and never once been interrupted. Gray cloaks, gray hoods, faces beneath shadowed by more than darkness — a shimmer, like heat-haze in winter. Crown work. The palace's quiet knives didn't wear livery, but they wore that shimmer; she'd seen it once before, years ago, on a man who had come to collect a debt from her father and left without collecting anything, because her father had been a storm-caller too, before the mountains took him.

"Mistress Zhou." The speaker was the tallest, and his voice was pleasant, almost kind, the voice of a clerk reading a list. "You are expected."

"I'm expected in Vellamark with a cargo I intend to deliver," Coral said. Her hand drifted toward the shard at her hip. "So whoever's expecting me can expect disappointment."

"You are the storm-bringer." Not a question. He said it the way a priest says a name in a litany, and something in the cadence made the hair rise along her arms. "Named in the text. Bound to the date. You understand we cannot let you reach it."

"Reach what?" She kept her voice flat, bored, buying seconds while her mind ran the arithmetic of distance. Forty yards of wet stone between them. Four of them. One of her, one mule, one shard of glass, and a gift she hadn't fully unbound since the pass, since using it felt like opening a door she might not close. "Reach Vellamark? Reach the border? You'll have to be specific. I find palace people are very bad at specifics — it's why they hire smugglers."

"The date," he said simply, and the kindness in his voice curdled into something else. "Tomorrow. The king dies tomorrow, storm-bringer, and your name is written into the hour of it. We are here to see that the writing stays clean."

The world went very quiet and very clear, the way it did before lightning.

They hadn't come to arrest her. They hadn't come to question her. They had come to kill her *before* the prophecy could happen — which meant someone in Thornfell's palace had read the same words she had, believed them, and decided the cheapest solution was a dead smuggler. Which meant someone in the palace had known about the prophecy for longer than she had. Long enough to send riders into the high passes. Long enough to be waiting here, at the exact bridge, on the exact night.

Her fury arrived first as heat in her palms, then as taste — copper and ozone, the flavor of a storm about to break.

"You know," she said, and heard her own voice drop into the register her father had called *the thunder-voice*, low and rolling, "I spent six years in exile for calling one storm over a lord's vineyard. One storm. Hail, mostly. And your master sends four of you to murder me on a bridge, in the open, over a book I barely read." She began to walk forward, leading the mule, unhurried. "That seems disproportionate."

"Stand where you are."

"No, I don't think so."

The tall one sighed — genuinely regretful, she thought, which was somehow worse — and raised two fingers, and the other three came on like water finding a downhill grade. Fast. Silent. Blades already out, and the shimmer around their faces tightening into focus.

Coral let go of the mule's lead.

She had planned, in the half-hour behind the oak, to run. That had been the whole plan: cross fast, or don't cross, keep the glass moving north, stay small, stay alive. Smugglers survived by not being interesting. But they had said *storm-bringer* the way priests say names, and they had said *tomorrow*, and something older and angrier than smuggling sense had uncoiled in her chest and begun, quietly, to make different arrangements.

The first assassin reached her at ten paces. She caught his wrist, turned inside his thrust — sloppy, overconfident, used to victims who begged — and put her bare palm flat against the storm-glass shard through the wool at her hip.

She opened the door.

It is difficult to describe what a storm-caller does, because it is not quite doing. It is permission. The sky above the gorge had been gathering all day, green-black and swollen, looking for an excuse, and Coral gave it one. Lightning came down the length of her arm and out through her hand and into the man's chest, and he was dead before the light finished leaving him, thrown backward off the bridge into the fog with a crack that the mute bells seemed, for one impossible instant, to answer.

The second and third came together, flanking. She dropped to one knee and pushed the storm outward along the wet stone — not a bolt this time but a sheet, a fan of white fire crawling the bridge's surface, and the water that beaded every inch of that ancient rock carried it to their boots. They died dancing. She would remember that later, in the hours when memory chose its own schedule: the way they danced, jerking, their gray cloaks lit from within like paper lanterns, and the smell of it, and the fact that part of her — the part that had watched her father hang for lesser power — felt nothing at all in the moment itself.

The feeling came after. It always came after.

The tall one had not moved. He stood at the bridge's far end with his two fingers still raised, and now he lowered them slowly, and beneath the hood's shimmer Coral saw him smile.

"So it's true," he said. "The text doesn't lie. Kill the vessel, and the storm passes to—" He stopped himself, lips pressing shut, and laughed softly at his own caution. "No. That's not mine to say. You'll learn it soon enough, storm-bringer. Everyone in this story learns their line eventually."

"Then here's mine," Coral said. Her hands were shaking now, the adrenaline arriving late, the cost of the working settling into her bones like cold water. Three dead men. Three. Her father had hanged for hail. "Tell whoever holds your leash that the next messenger they send to a bridge with me on it goes home in the river. Tell them I've read the ending, and I don't care for my part, and I am *coming to argue with the author*."

Something flickered across the shimmered face — surprise, or respect, or fear; at that distance she couldn't tell and didn't try.

"Vellamark will be watched," he said. "Every road south. Every port. You are the most interesting woman in Thornfell tonight, Mistress Zhou, and interesting women drown."

"Then it's fortunate," Coral said, "that I've never much liked roads."

He bowed — actually bowed, a small precise courtier's bow that told her more about the palace than any interrogation could have — and stepped backward off the bridge into the fog, and did not scream, and did not surface, and the river went on hissing below as though nothing had happened at all.

* * *

She did not go to Vellamark.

She stood on the Sorrow Bridge in the aftermath with the rain beginning — real rain now, ordinary rain, the storm spent and sulking off eastward — and she made herself look at what she had done. Three bodies. Two on the stone, one gone into the gorge. She searched the nearest and found what she expected: no papers, no seal, no name. Only a coin, old gold, worn nearly smooth, stamped with a crown whose points had been filed down to rounded nubs. An old coin. Older than the current reign. She turned it in her fingers and felt the weight of a century in it.

*The wolf beneath the crown.* The prophecy's phrase surfaced in her memory unbidden, and she shoved it down, and it surfaced again, patient as tide.

Someone in the palace had known. Had sent killers to keep the prophecy's date clean — which meant they feared the prophecy, which meant they served whatever the prophecy threatened, or they *were* what it threatened. Either way, the trail didn't run south to Vellamark anymore. It ran north and east, over the mountains, down the long road to the capital, to the court of a king she had never seen and a throne she had never wanted to think about.

The mule shifted nervously beside her. The storm-glass in its wrapping had gone quiet at last, its work done, cooling like a banked fire.

"You and me both," Coral murmured to it.

She mounted up, turned away from the border she'd been paid to reach — forfeiting the cargo, the fee, the last thin thread of her respectable exile — and rode north along the river road as the rain thickened, past the split oak, past the nameless shrine, toward the capital and the crown and whatever waited beneath it.

Behind her, in the fog over the gorge, she could have sworn she heard the rusted bells ring once — a single low note, like a door closing, or a door opening.

By morning, she was told later, every smuggler on the mountain roads was telling the same story: a woman had crossed the Sorrow Bridge, and three of the crown's own had tried to stop her, and the storm itself had taken her side.

They were already calling her the storm-bringer. She had not chosen the name.

But names, she was beginning to understand, were how this story caught hold of people — and hers had been written down a hundred years before she was born.
