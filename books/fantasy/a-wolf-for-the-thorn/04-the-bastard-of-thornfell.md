## 4. The Bastard of Thornfell

Coral counted the exits first. It was habit, the way other people counted coins or sins, and the Saltcellar Inn had three: the front door onto the market square, a back door that opened into the tannery yard, and a window in the privy that any child could squeeze through if she were desperate enough to try. She sat with her back to the wall, a cup of sour ale going warm in front of her, and watched the door.

The border town of Marrow's Ford smelled like every border town she'd ever smuggled through — wet wool, woodsmoke, and the particular desperation of people who lived close enough to Thornfell's capital to see its spires and far enough away to pretend they didn't care what happened there. Rain came down in gray sheets outside, drumming on the shutters, and Coral was grateful for it. Rain meant fewer travelers. Rain meant fewer eyes.

Rain also meant her storm-gift sat restless beneath her skin, humming like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

She had crossed the Sorrow Bridge four days ago. She still dreamed about it — the assassins' faces when the lightning found them, the smell of scorched leather, the way one of them had said *storm-bringer* like he'd been expecting her, like she was a line in someone's ledger. She'd taken nothing from the bodies except a coin purse and a name stitched inside a collar: *Vessic*. Someone had paid for those deaths, and that someone knew things about her she hadn't known herself until a week ago.

The prophecy page was folded against her breastbone now, wrapped in oilcloth, carried like a knife. She'd read it so many times the words had worn grooves in her mind. *On the eve of the thirteenth of Emberfall, the wolf beneath the crown shall drink the king's last breath.* And her own name, in ink older than her grandmother's grandmother. *By the hand of Coral Zhou, storm-caller, exile.*

Tonight was the eleventh of Emberfall. Two days.

"You're drinking alone," said a voice behind her, "which is either very wise or very stupid."

Coral didn't turn. She'd heard him come in — she'd heard everyone come in for the past hour, cataloguing them out of habit — but this man had moved quietly for his size, quiet as someone trained to it, and that alone put a cold finger on the back of her neck. "Both," she said. "It depends on the night."

"And which is tonight?"

"The kind where I don't talk to strangers."

He came around anyway, unhurried, and sat across from her without asking. Big through the shoulders, dark-haired, with a face that might have been handsome if it weren't for the expression he wore — something guarded and wry at once, like a man who'd learned to smile as armor. He wore a traveler's cloak over good boots, and under the cloak, when he shifted, the hilt of a sword showed itself deliberately, the way a man shows a weapon when he wants you to know he has one but doesn't want to use it.

"I'm not a stranger," he said. "I've been following you since the Sorrow Bridge."

Coral set down the cup. Her fingers itched; somewhere above the inn, above the rain, there was pressure building in the clouds, answering her mood without permission. She breathed it down. "Then you're either the bravest assassin I've ever met or the worst."

"The worst," he agreed. "If I wanted you dead, I'd have shot you on the bridge while your hands were full of lightning." He leaned forward. "My name is Wren."

"Congratulations."

"Wren Thornfell."

The name landed between them like a dropped glass. Coral looked at him properly then — really looked — and saw it once she knew to see it: the jaw, the dark hair, something in the set of the eyes that she'd seen stamped on coins and carved over courthouse doors. The king's face, worn by a different life.

"They say Aldric has no family left," she said.

"They say a lot of things." His mouth twisted. "They say the king is well. They say the court is loyal. They say the storms this season are natural." He glanced at her hands, folded on the table. "You know better on at least one count."

* * *

Wren had rehearsed this conversation forty times on the road from the capital, and every version had fallen apart within the first minute, because the woman across the table was not what the reports had described. The reports said *smuggler*. They said *dangerous*, *unlicensed*, *exiled from the Storm Colleges at nineteen for burning a customs tower*. They did not say that she would look at him like she was pricing his bones for resale, or that the air in the room would feel charged, faintly metallic, like the moment before a summer storm breaks.

He kept his voice low. "Three weeks ago my brother stopped sleeping. Then he stopped eating, mostly. Then he started writing letters in a hand that isn't his, and signing them with his own name anyway. I've served in his guard since I was sixteen — the only post they'd give a bastard — and I know Aldric's handwriting the way you know your own pulse. This wasn't it. The letters were wrong. The *words* were wrong." He paused. "And two nights ago I watched him stand at a window during a thunderstorm, barefoot, in the snow, smiling up at the lightning like it was singing to him."

Coral said nothing. But the pressure in the room eased slightly, the way a held breath eases, and Wren understood that she was listening with more than her ears.

"There's an old thing in the palace," he went on. "The servants won't go near the crown chamber. The bells in the east tower cracked the same night my brother changed, all seven of them, and no one will recast them because the founder's guild says the metal 'won't take.' There's something wearing my brother, and whatever it is, it's old, and it's hungry, and it sits on the throne of Thornfell and signs death warrants with a steady hand." He spread his hands on the table. "You found a prophecy. Don't bother denying it — the men who died on the Sorrow Bridge were carrying orders that mentioned it. You're named in it. So am I, though I only learned that yesterday, and I'd very much like to know how."

"How do you know what I found?"

"Because I've spent three weeks being ignored by everyone who should have helped me, and so I did the unfashionable thing and helped myself. I bribed an archivist. I read letters a hundred years sealed. And then I asked myself who else was named in the old texts, and where she'd run to ground, and I rode through the rain for six days to sit across from a woman who could kill me with a thought." He smiled, thin and unamused. "So. Here I am."

* * *

Coral turned the cup slowly on the table, watching the ale swirl, and made herself think instead of feel. That was the discipline the Colleges had beaten into her before they'd cast her out: the storm wants everything *now*; the caller decides *when*.

Everything he said fit. That was the problem. A lie built to fit this neatly would cost someone real effort, and Wren Thornfell — bastard, guardsman, ghost of the royal household — didn't strike her as having the resources for elaborate fabrication. He struck her as exactly what he appeared: a man who had run out of options and was gambling on the one person in the world more cursed than himself.

"You want me to break whatever's holding your brother," she said. "With what? Good intentions?"

"With this." He slid something across the table, wrapped in leather. She opened it a finger's width and felt her skin prickle: storm-glass, raw and uncut, humming the same note as her gift. A lot of it. Enough to kill a city block, if she were careless. Enough to do something unprecedented, if she were not. "From the palace vaults. The entity fears it — I've seen it flinch. Whatever binding holds it, it was made of this stuff once. Yara's binding."

The name hit her like cold water. *Yara.* The storm-caller from the prophecy's margins, the one whose curse had passed down the royal line like an inheritance no one wanted. Coral closed the leather carefully, as if the thing inside might bite.

"If I help you," she said, "and you're lying — if this is a trap dressed up as a rescue —"

"Then you'll kill me," Wren said simply. "Everyone assumes I'll die for being a bastard eventually. At least this way it's for something."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she studied his face, looking for the crack, the tell, the thing that would let her write him off. She didn't find one. What she found was worse: exhaustion, and fear, and underneath both, a stubborn, foolish loyalty to a brother the whole court pretended didn't exist.

Two days until the thirteenth of Emberfall. One road to the capital. And the only person offering to walk it beside her was the wolf from the prophecy itself — if the words could be believed, and lately the words insisted on being believed whether she liked it or not.

"Alliance," she said at last. "Not trust. We move tonight, we avoid the king's roads, and if you betray me, the storm keeps what it takes. Understood?"

"Understood." He rose, dropping coins on the table, and for a moment stood silhouetted against the lamplight — the king's face on a smuggler's errand, a bastard carrying a kingdom's hope in a scrap of leather. "There's one more thing," he said, hesitating at the edge of the light. "The archivist I bribed. I went back yesterday to ask him one final question. About the wolf."

Coral went still. "And?"

"He was dead," Wren said. "Three days dead, they told me — but the ledger on his desk was open to a fresh page, in fresh ink, and written across it in a hand I now recognize as my brother's was one sentence." He met her eyes. "*The wolf rides to the crown willingly. Let him.*"

Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere overhead thunder rolled — long, low, and patient, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.
