## 5. What the Smugglers Remember

The Saltwife's Rest sat where the river forgot itself—a drowned tavern on the edge of Marrowfen, half its foundation sunk into black water, the other half propped up on pilings that groaned like old men in a wind. Coral had drunk there before her exile, when she'd still been allowed inside Thornfell's borders, and it had not improved with age. Neither had its keeper.

"Storm-caller." Old Vetch spat into the river through the gap in his teeth. "Thought you were dead."

"Everyone thinks that," Coral said. "It's useful. Keep thinking it."

She set a pouch of storm-glass shards on the table between them—small ones, cracked, worth little to anyone who couldn't wake them, and everything to a man who sold to collectors across the fen. Vetch looked at them the way a starving man looks at bread.

"You want something," he said. "Nobody brings gifts to the Saltwife's Rest unless they want something."

"I want the old story. The one your grandmother told you when you were small enough to still listen. About the first Aldric."

Vetch went very still. Outside, a heron lifted off the water with a sound like tearing cloth. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You found the book."

Coral said nothing. That was answer enough.

* * *

He made her wait three days. She spent them in the loft above the tavern, watching rain crawl down the reed-thatched window, turning the prophecy over in her mind until the edges wore soft. *The wolf beneath the crown shall wear the storm.* Tonight's date—no, not tonight anymore; three nights gone now, and she was still alive, which meant either the prophecy was wrong or she had already changed something by surviving the bridge. She didn't know which possibility frightened her more.

On the third evening, Vetch came up the ladder with a lamp and a bottle of plum brandy older than both of them together, and he did not offer her any.

"My grandmother wasn't fen-born," he said, settling onto the crate across from her. The lamplight turned his face into a map of creases. "She was mountain stock, from before the passes closed. Her mother had been a servant in the old keep at Highmoor, back when the first Aldric still sat the throne. And what her mother saw there, she told her daughter, who told me, because I was the only one of her grandchildren who ever asked twice."

"And?"

"And you'll want to sit down, but you're already sitting, so I'll just say it." He leaned forward. "The first Aldric didn't take the throne. It was given to him. By a woman he put to death."

The rain filled the silence. Coral found her hands had gone cold around the empty cup she was holding.

"Her name was Yara," Vetch said. "Yara of the High Passes. A storm-caller—the last one, they thought, though we know better now, don't we?" His eyes flicked to her, sharp as a fishhook. "She called storms for the rebellion against the old kings. Rain to drown their supply trains. Lightning to split their siege lines. The common folk loved her. Called her the sky's own daughter. And when the war was won, the rebels needed a king, and the man they chose was Aldric Thorne, a general with a good name and a clean face, and Yara stood beside him at his crowning and called a clear sky for it, the only clear sky in a year of rain."

"But he killed her."

"He killed her." Vetch poured himself brandy with a hand that was not quite steady. "A year after. No one knows why—or no one would say. The official story was treason. She'd raised a storm against the crown, they claimed. But my grandmother's mother was in the keep that night, scrubbing floors, and she heard the shouting. Heard Yara screaming—not cursing him, mind, *begging* him. Begging him to listen. And then the thunder came down through the chimney like the sky itself had reached into the room, and when it cleared, Yara was dead and Aldric was standing in the wreckage holding something."

"The crown."

Vetch's head came up slowly. "You know more than you let on, girl."

"I've seen it. Felt it." She wrapped her arms around herself. "It's not empty."

"No," Vetch said quietly. "My grandmother's mother swore the crown screamed that night. Not the king—the *crown*. Like something had been pressed into it, or woken in it. And after that, Aldric changed. Stopped sleeping. Started talking to himself in a voice that wasn't quite his. Started writing edicts in his sleep that his clerks were too frightened to question. And every heir since—" he ticked them off on twisted fingers, "—every Aldric, every Thorne who's worn that circlet, has had the same years. The bad years. The histories call it the Crown Melancholy. Kings who woke with blood under their nails. Queens who ordered executions they couldn't remember signing. One of them, sixty years back, tried to cut the thing off his own head with a carving knife, and they locked him in the high tower until he died, and the moment he was buried, his son was crowned, and the son said afterward—he said this, it's written down—that putting it on felt like *coming home*."

Coral's stomach turned over. She thought of King Aldric in his portrait in the border town's customs house, painted ten years ago: handsome, hollow-eyed, a man smiling the way people smile when someone is standing behind them.

"So it's passed down the bloodline," she said. "Or the throne line. Whatever wears the crown feeds it, or houses it, or—"

"Or is eaten by it," Vetch said. "Depends which end of the meal you're standing at."

* * *

She traded him two more favors before he gave her the last piece, and the last piece cost her most.

"There's a reason you were exiled, isn't there," he said. It wasn't a question. "They cast you out of Thornfell for calling a storm you weren't authorized to call. That's the story."

"It's the true story."

"It's the *surface* story." He tapped the table with one knuckle. "I've moved cargo past the capital walls for forty years, girl. I hear what the clerks whisper when they think no one's listening. Your trial wasn't about the storm. It was about *where* you called it. You called lightning down on the royal procession road, didn't you? Struck the road itself, split it open. And do you know what they found under the flagstones when the work crews repaired it?"

Coral's mouth had gone dry. "I never asked."

"Bones," Vetch said. "Old bones, and scraps of rope, and a stone marker with a name worn nearly smooth. They dug it up, read the name, and burned the records of it within the week. But a clerk told my cousin's boy that the name started with a Y."

The loft seemed to tilt. Coral gripped the edge of the table.

"They executed her on the procession road," she whispered. "Where I—"

"Where you called a storm without knowing why the ground beneath you remembered one." Vetch's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than his suspicion. "You think your exile was an accident, girl? You think the timing was chance? A hundred years to the season, near enough, and a storm-caller rises out of nowhere, calls lightning onto the exact ground where the last one died, and the crown's response is to erase her name and throw her out of the country so fast her boots barely touched the dirt?" He shook his head. "Somebody in that palace knew what you were the moment you did it. Knew, and was afraid."

Coral stood. Her legs held, barely. She walked to the window and looked out at the fen, at the rain stitching silver seams across the black water, and she felt the shape of something vast and terrible assembling itself in her chest.

Yara had been betrayed by the king she crowned. Had died begging. Had been bound—no, not bound; *buried*, her name scraped from stone, her bones hidden under the road—and whatever the crown held, whatever whispered debts into sleeping kings' ears, it had begun the night Yara's storm broke over that chamber. Perhaps it *was* her. Perhaps it was what was done to her. Coral didn't have the words for it yet, but she had the geography of it, and the geography pointed one direction: toward the capital, toward the crown, toward the date that had rearranged itself in a dead woman's margins.

And she understood, with a guilt that settled into her bones like fen-damp, that her whole life had been arranged around a grave. Her gift, her exile, the assassins on the bridge—all of it ripples from a stone dropped a century ago. She had spent five years hating Thornfell for casting her out, and now she learned that Thornfell might have cast her out for the same reason it had killed Yara: fear. Old, inherited, breeding-true fear.

"Vetch," she said. "One more question."

"If it's free."

"The prophecy. The book I found. Who wrote it?"

He was quiet long enough that she turned around. In the lamplight, the old smuggler's face had gone strange—not frightened, exactly. Reverent.

"My grandmother told me one more thing," he said. "Said that Yara had a daughter. A baby, born a month before the execution. The records say the child died with the mother. But my grandmother's mother helped carry a bundle out of the keep that night, down the servants' stair, through the postern gate, into the mountains. And the family that took that bundle in—" he met her eyes, "—the family name was Zhou."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, the river kept moving, indifferent, ancient, carrying everything downstream.

"That's not possible," Coral said. "My family are smugglers. We've always been smugglers. There's nothing else in us."

"Isn't there?" Vetch smiled, and it was a sad, crooked thing. "Girl, why do you think your line can wake storm-glass? Why do you think the sky answers you like an old friend? Blood doesn't forget. It just goes quiet, and waits, and hands things down." He pushed the pouch of storm-glass back across the table toward her. "Keep your payment. Consider it a dowry."

"A dowry for what?"

"For the wedding you're walking into," he said, "between you and that crown."

* * *

She left before dawn, taking the high causeway north, where the mist hung thick enough to drink. Wren would be waiting at the crossroads shrine outside Gildenhollow—they'd agreed on it before the capital, before the festival, before everything—and she rehearsed the telling of it as she walked. *Your brother's crown holds a ghost. The ghost was born the day they killed a storm-caller named Yara. And I think I'm the last of her blood.*

But when she reached the shrine, its candle gutted and cold, Wren was not there. Instead, pinned to the shrine's stone with a knife—a palace knife, she knew the make at once—was a page torn from a court ledger. On it, in a clerk's careful hand, a single line:

*The wolf is known. Bring the vessel home before the moon turns.*

And beneath it, in different ink, hurried, half-smudged, in handwriting she recognized from coded letters she'd only seen once—in handwriting that could only be Aldric's—

*Do not let them crown him. Run, Coral. He is coming for you next.*
