## 6. The Court of Hollow Bells

The bells of Thornfell's capital did not ring for the dead. That was the first thing Wren had to explain to Coral as they waited in the shadow of the Salt Gate, among carts of mourning-wheat and families in grey ash-cloth.

"They're rung hollow," he said, tapping his own throat where a pilgrim's collar sat. "Foundry-cast with a sealed core. No clapper. The sound comes from the metal remembering being struck — an old storm-glass trick, from before the bans." He watched her face do the arithmetic she always did: another thread leading back to Yara, back to the century-old wound they were walking toward. "So when you hear them, you're not hearing now. You're hearing an echo of something that stopped a hundred years ago."

"Charming," Coral said. "Like everything else in this kingdom."

She wore ash-grey like she'd been born to it, hood up, storm-scarred hands hidden in her sleeves. Around them the city poured toward the Processional Way, ten thousand mourners come for the Festival of Hollow Bells, the annual remembrance of every soul lost to the crown's wars. It was, Wren thought bitterly, the one night a year the palace admitted it had killed anyone at all.

It was also the one night a year the gates stood open and the guards were too drunk on ceremony to count faces.

They went through with the wheat carts, heads down, and no one looked twice at the king's bastard brother walking into his own capital like a thief.

* * *

Wren had not been inside the Winter Palace since he was nineteen, when Aldric — newly crowned, still warm-eyed, still calling him *little brother* instead of *the complication* — had given him a room and a title and then quietly never mentioned either again. The corridors remembered him anyway. His boots knew the turns before he did: left at the tapestry of the First Hunt, down the servants' stair that smelled of tallow and old stone, along the gallery where the kings of Thornfell hung in their gilded frames and watched him with painted eyes.

Coral moved behind him like smoke. She'd refused to stay with the smuggler contacts in the Low Quarter. *"You don't leave the only person who can unbind a curse alone in the cursed building,"* she'd said, which was either loyalty or tactics, and Wren had decided not to ask which.

"The king's private study," he murmured. "Third door past the orrery. He keeps his correspondence there — the real letters, not the ones the chancellors draft for him."

"And if he's not Aldric anymore when we open that door?"

Wren's hand paused on the cold iron latch. Below them, faint through three floors of stone, the hollow bells began their first slow toll, and the sound was wrong in a way that raised the hair on his arms — a note struck and struck again by nothing, ringing backward out of the metal.

"Then I'll know," he said, "what I'm fighting."

The study was empty. The fire had burned to embers. And the letters —

Wren crossed the room in four strides. The correspondence lay stacked on the desk in Aldric's own hand, warrants and decrees and diplomatic replies, all of it correct, all of it cold, all of it signed with the firm black signature Wren had known since childhood. But Coral made a small sound and touched the margin of the topmost page.

"Look."

In the border scrollwork, woven into the decorative flourishes where no clerk would ever look, tiny letters hid. Wren bent close, heart hammering. *I am here. I am under. Do not trust the spymaster. The bells are listening.* Then, on the next letter, in the same disguised hand: *He counts my breaths. When I sleep he walks. Wren — if it is you — do not let him mark you.*

"If it is you," Coral repeated softly. "He knew you'd come."

"He hoped." Wren's throat was tight. "That's worse. That's so much worse." Because this was proof — not the whispers of smugglers, not Coral's rearranging prophecy, but his brother's own hand, buried alive inside those flourishes, writing to a future he couldn't be sure would arrive. Aldric was still in there. Still fighting. Still counting his own breaths like a man rationing water in a desert.

Hope lit in Wren's chest like a struck match, and he hated how much it hurt.

"We take the letters," Coral said. "Evidence. For the regent council, for whoever's left to—"

The bells tolled again, closer now, though they hadn't moved. The ember-light guttered sideways, as if something enormous had exhaled through the room. And every candle-stub, every coal, every scrap of light bent — just slightly — toward Wren.

He felt it happen. There was no other word for it. A pressure against his skin like a fingertip testing fruit at market. Something ancient and patient turned its attention across the distance between the throne room and this little study, and *looked* at him, and found him interesting.

"No," Wren whispered.

"What?" Coral was at his shoulder, storm-light flickering warningly in her palms. "What is it?"

"It's looking at me." His voice came out flat and strange. "Coral, it's — it knows me. It's *reading* me." The pressure deepened, slid past his skin, and for one vertiginous instant he felt what the crown felt: a throne grown over with roots, a king wrapped to the eyes in golden binding, and beneath it all a hunger so old it had forgotten what it was hungry *for*. It tasted him the way you taste rain on the wind. And somewhere in that vast dark attention, something shifted — a settling, a recognition, the way a lock recognizes its key.

The words from the prophecy rose in his memory unbidden. *The wolf beneath the crown.*

The pressure withdrew as suddenly as it had come. The flames straightened. The bells fell silent mid-toll, leaving a silence with teeth in it.

"Wren." Coral gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise. Her eyes were wide, storm-grey gone nearly white. "Your eyes. They just — for a second they weren't yours. They were gold."

He put a hand to his face. His fingers were shaking. "It marked me," he said. "Didn't it. That's what that was."

Coral didn't answer, which was answer enough.

* * *

They made it out of the palace by ways Wren barely remembered choosing, his body moving on old habit while his mind circled the same terrible groove. In a disused bell-foundry in the Low Quarter — abandoned since the glass-bans, its great cauldrons rusted into sculpture — Coral built a fire of packing straw and paced in front of it like a caged thing.

"We leave tonight," she said. "Now. Before whatever that was finishes deciding what you are."

"And go where? Run where? It's in the throne, Coral. It's in the *bells*. You heard them — they rang without being struck. It reaches the whole city through—" He stopped. Pressed his palms against his eyes. "My whole life I've been the spare. The bastard. The one nobody planned for. And the one time in history that's an advantage, it's because a monster wants to wear me next."

"That's not going to happen."

"You don't know that."

"I know," she said, and stopped pacing, and looked at him across the fire with an expression he hadn't seen on her before — stripped of irony, stripped of the smuggler's armor, "that I spent six days deciding whether you were worth trusting, and I'm not doing it again. Whatever it saw in you, it doesn't get to have you. That's not prophecy. That's a promise."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, faint and far off, a single hollow bell sounded — one clear, sourceless note, hanging in the dark like a held breath.

Neither of them spoke. Neither of them needed to. It wasn't a random tolling. It was a summons, or a claim, or simply a courtesy: *I know where you are.*

Coral sat down beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and stared into the fire. "There's an old contact," she said at last, quietly. "A ledger-keeper who worked the palace supply routes for thirty years. If anyone knows how the binding passed from Aldric the First into the royal line — how it chooses its vessels, whether it can be broken from outside — it's her. Smugglers remember what courts burn."

"When do we leave?"

"Before dawn. While it sleeps in him." She hesitated. "And Wren — if it starts taking you before we understand it, I need to know now. What you want me to do."

He thought about lying. He thought about *save me*, about *let me die human*, about all the brave answers. What came out was quieter.

"Aldric held on for eleven years," he said. "Alone, in the dark, writing messages no one read. The least I can do is hold on long enough to give him company." He turned to look at her, and the firelight caught the gold flecks in his own eyes — flecks he was suddenly, horribly certain had not been there last month. "But if I start signing things in a hand that isn't mine — stop me. Whatever it costs."

Above the foundry, through the broken skylight, the stars were coming out over Thornfell. And beneath them, in the palace on the hill, a king sat awake at his desk, counting his breaths, while in the margins of his letters the hidden words waited for a reader who had already come and gone — and left, unknowing, a reply written in gold-flecked eyes:

*I am here. I am under. He has seen me now.*

The bells did not ring again that night. They didn't need to.
