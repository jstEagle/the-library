## 7. The Traitor's Ledger

The spymaster's office smelled of tallow and dried ink, and it was the easiest room in the palace to enter, which should have been the first warning.

Coral crouched behind a cabinet of correspondence older than the current king, her knees aching against cold marble, and listened to the silence the way she'd once listened for the pressure-drop before a storm. Above her, through the ceiling, the mourning festival went on — a thousand bells tolling for a queen dead forty years, a tradition so old its grief had worn smooth as river stone. Good. Grief made guards lazy. Grief made everyone look down at their shoes instead of up at the rafters where a storm-caller moved like smoke.

Three nights in the capital. Three nights of hollow bells, and Wren walking the palace corridors in a servant's grey with his brother's eyes on him every time they passed. Three nights of Aldric leaving coded messages in his correspondence — *the debt grows, the debt grows* — and Coral learning to read them in stolen drafts.

And now this room.

The spymaster, Lord Corvane, was at the festival. She'd watched him go, a narrow man in dove-grey with a face like a folded letter. His office was supposed to be locked. It was not. The lock had been picked so often the mechanism barely caught anymore — she'd felt that in the first second, the give of a tumblers worn by years of quiet entries. Someone came and went here who was not Lord Corvane.

She found the ledger at the bottom of a false drawer, beneath a false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth that had been unwrapped and rewrapped so many times the folds were going soft.

It was not Corvane's ledger. Corvane's ledgers, she'd already copied two nights ago, were meticulous, dull, and loyal — records of agents, payments, the usual rot of any court. This one was different. This one was written in a hand that changed.

That was the thing that stopped her breath. She turned pages by the light of a shuttered storm-glass shard, and the handwriting shifted — sometimes cramped and hurried, sometimes flowing, sometimes so alien it looked less written than *grown* across the page, letters like root systems, like frost-ferns. Dates spanning eleven years. And in every entry, the same structure: what was told, to whom, and what was owed.

*The bastard rides north. Told him the roads flood at Sorrel Ford. He will take the coast. Payment: the debt eases.*

*The king sleeps. The king does not sleep. The king writes letters he does not remember writing. Told the household to burn them. Payment: three days of quiet.*

*Storm-caller sighted at the Sorrow Bridge. She lives. This was not the instruction. Payment withheld.*

Coral's hands had gone cold around the ledger. *She lives. This was not the instruction.* The ambush on the bridge — the assassins who had called her *storm-bringer* before she'd ever spoken a word in this kingdom — had been ordered by someone who knew her name before she arrived. And the entity had been *annoyed* that she survived.

She read on, faster now, the bells tolling above her like a countdown.

Eleven years of it. Eleven years of a court spymaster — or whatever wore his office — feeding the thing in the crown everything it needed: where the king's allies slept, which lords could be bought, which letters to intercept, which servants to turn. The entity hadn't seized Thornfell in a night. It had *farmed* it. Patiently. Season by season, debt by debt.

And then she reached the entry dated four days past, in that root-grown hand, and the world tilted.

*The wolf grows restless. He asks questions of the storm-caller. He does not know what he carries. This is good. Let the vessel ripen. When the rite is done, the crown will need a younger throat. The wolf beneath the crown will wear it.*

*Wren.*

Not *the bastard*. Not *the king's brother*. The prophecy's own words, the phrase she'd read in a ruined library with her heart hammering — *the wolf beneath the crown* — and she had assumed, all this road, that it meant Aldric. That the entity *was* the wolf, and the crown its den.

But the prophecy hadn't named the entity. It had named the vessel. The wolf beneath the crown was the man who would carry it next. The thing in Aldric's head had been shopping for a new body for years, and it had chosen Wren — chosen him the way a farmer chooses a field, for youth, for blood, for proximity. Every kindness Wren had shown her, every risk he'd taken, every coded message he'd helped her decode — the entity had watched it all and thought: *good. Let the vessel ripen.*

Coral sat back on her heels in the dark and let the bells wash over her, and for a long moment she could not feel her own hands.

* * *

She found him at the festival's edge, where the bells gave way to the river and the lanterns floated out on the black water like a fleet of small departing souls. He was leaning on the balustrade in his servant's grey, watching the lanterns, and for a moment — just a moment — he looked like what he was: a bastard with no inheritance but his name and no future but the one he stole.

"Wren."

He didn't startle. He'd learned her footsteps somewhere on the road; she tried not to think about what that meant, how closely he'd listened. "You're supposed to be copying Corvane's correspondence. You look like you found something else."

"I found a ledger."

His jaw tightened. "In his office?"

"In his office, in a drawer that opens too easily, in a hand that isn't his." She kept her voice flat, kept her eyes on the lanterns, because looking at him would be a mistake. "Eleven years of entries. The entity's own bookkeeping. It's been running the spymaster's office like a larder."

Wren exhaled slowly. "Corvane?"

"Corvane might be innocent. Corvane might be a skin someone walks around in. I don't know. What I know is that the thing in your brother's head has been fed for a decade, and it's not finished." She turned then, because there was no way to say the next part without seeing his face. "It's not finished because it's leaving."

"Leaving."

"Thrones wear out. Bodies wear out." The words came out of her like stones dropping down a well. "It's chosen its next vessel. Someone young. Someone of the blood. Someone who's been asking questions and getting close to a storm-caller, and the entity thinks that's *ripening*."

The lantern light moved on the water. Wren's face did something complicated — the flicker of a man doing arithmetic with his own death. "Say it plainly, Coral."

"*The wolf beneath the crown.* That's you. That's what the prophecy means. Not Aldric. You."

For a heartbeat he said nothing at all, and she watched him hold himself very still the way people do when the ground has opened under them and movement feels like falling. Then he laughed — one short, ugly note, no humor in it.

"Of course. Of course it's me. Why would it be anyone else." He gripped the balustrade. "I spent my whole life being the spare. The spare son, the spare heir, the spare brother. I finally find a way to be *useful* and it turns out the use is — what? A jar. A throat. A younger set of hands for the same old hunger."

"It doesn't have to be."

"Doesn't it?" He turned on her, and his eyes were bright and furious and afraid. "You've read the ledger. You've read the prophecy. Tell me the ending, Coral. Tell me how this goes where the vessel walks away."

She couldn't. That was the truth she'd carried down from the library and across three hundred miles of smuggler road: the prophecy promised the king's death at the wolf's hands, and prophecies were not suggestions. Every road she could see ended in Wren's hands around his brother's throat — or the entity's hands in Wren's body doing the same, wearing him like a glove.

"I found something else," she said instead. "In the ledger. The entity keeps accounts. Debts and payments. It's *bound* by something — it can't just take a vessel, it has to be *given* one, or earned. Yara's binding, a century ago. It has rules, Wren. Rules can be broken."

"By whom?"

"By a storm-caller." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. "By me. But I need to know which side of you I'm saving."

He stared at her. "What does that mean?"

"It means the ledger calls you *the wolf*." The word came out harder than she intended, and she didn't soften it. "It means for eleven years the entity has been writing about you in a book, planning you, *ripening* you, and I don't know what's already taken root. I don't know if the man who intercepted me at Sorrel Ford and walked me through the palace and held my hand across the Sorrow Bridge is a man or a lure. I don't know if I'm standing next to the victim or the weapon."

The silence between them stretched, and the river carried the lanterns away one by one.

"You think I'd know," Wren said finally, quietly. "If something was riding me. You think Aldric knew, at the start?"

That was the cruelty of it — he was right. Aldric hadn't known. The entity had taken him in his sleep, in small payments, in debts so small they felt like choices. What was it doing to Wren right now, in the space between his heartbeats? What had it already paid into him, the way a farmer pays into a field?

"No," Coral said. "I don't think you'd know. That's the problem."

"So what do we do?"

She looked at him — really looked — at the man who had chosen her over his own safety with no reason to trust her and every reason not to, and she felt the two truths grind against each other in her chest like millstones. *The wolf beneath the crown.* The prophecy had never lied to her. But it had never told her the whole shape of a thing, either.

"We go back inside," she said. "We finish what we came for. And tonight, when the bells stop—" she nodded toward the palace, where the last lanterns were sinking into the river, "—we find the rite chamber. Because the ledger says the entity is preparing a *transfer*, and it names a date."

Wren went very still. "What date?"

She told him. His face went grey.

"That's tomorrow."

* * *

Above them, the last of the festival bells fell silent, all thousand of them, all at once — not fading, not finishing their toll, but *stopped*, mid-note, as if a hand had closed around the sound.

Coral's skin prickled with the pressure-drop she knew better than her own name.

Somewhere in the palace, something had noticed them listening.
