## 8. Storm over the Coronation Road

The summons went out at dusk, sealed with the king's own signet, and every bell in Thornfell rang it down from the citadel to the river gates: *the court will gather at midnight on the Coronation Road.*

Aldric wrote the words himself. He watched his hand do it — a fine, steady hand, his father's hand, ink-black letters marching across vellum in an order he had not chosen — and he screamed inside the small locked room of himself where the thing that wore his crown kept him.

*They must see,* the crown murmured through his jaw, through the silver circlet that had not left his brow in eleven years and could not be lifted by any hand but his own. *The debt comes due before witnesses. That is the old law. That is the only law.*

He had learned, over the long years, to leave messages. A reversed word in a trade decree. A name struck through twice. Once, memorably, a whole paragraph of a treaty written in mirror-script that had taken the chancellery a month to decode and had been dismissed as a scribe's joke. He was still fighting. That was the one thing they could not take from him, though they had taken everything else: his nights, his hands, the memory of what his body did while he slept like a drowned man behind his own eyes.

Tonight there would be no message. Tonight his body belonged to the wolf entirely, and he knew it, because he could feel the road already — feel the old stones of the Coronation Road warming beneath their moss like a hearthstone lit after years of cold. The road his grandfather had ridden in triumph. The road where the first Aldric had put a storm-caller named Yara against a wall and taken her name away with her breath.

*Midnight,* said the crown, sweet as rot. *Bring them all. Let them watch the vessel open.*

Aldric's last free thought, before the dark rose up his throat like water filling a lung, was of Wren. His bastard brother, who had come to him three nights ago under a festival mask and gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise, and whispered, *Hold on. She's coming. The storm-caller is coming.* And Aldric — Aldric had wept, or the body had, tears the entity could not stop because grief ran too deep for even it to dam — and then the dark had closed over his head, and he sank, and sank, and waited.

* * *

Coral felt the storm before she saw it, the way a blind woman feels a doorway: pressure, and the particular hush of air deciding what it wants to become.

She stood on the ridge above the Coronation Road with her arms out and her palms bleeding, because she had opened the veins along her wrists an hour ago with her own knife — the old smuggler's rite, blood for the sky, sky for the blood — and the storm was coming up the valley now like something being born wrong. Green-black light in its belly. Wind that did not blow so much as *arrive*, all at once, flattening the pines below her in ranks like soldiers bowing.

It was too big. She knew it was too big. She had been burning reserves for two days just to reach this ridge ahead of the court's procession, and what she was calling now was not weather; it was everything she had left and a great deal she did not. Every storm-glass shard she'd ever smuggled, every secret she'd ever kept, every mile of exile — she was pouring all of it into the sky above Thornfell's most sacred road, and the sky was drinking it and asking for more.

*Good,* she thought, giddy, terrified. *Drink.*

Below, torchlight. The whole court of Thornfell was coming up the road in procession — lords in mourning grey, priests with hollow bells that made no sound, guards in the king's black-and-silver — and at the center of it, riding a white horse far too calmly, King Aldric, crowned at midnight, which no living soul had seen done since the first Aldric took the throne a century ago.

Wren had gone down among them an hour past. He had kissed her — quick, hard, tasting of rain that hadn't fallen yet — and said, "If I'm the vessel, then I'm the door. Doors open from both sides." And she had wanted to break his beautiful stubborn neck, and instead she had let him go, because the prophecy had named him the wolf and she had spent six chapters of her life learning that prophecy does not negotiate.

"Delay them," he'd said. "However you can. I'll get Aldric to the center of the rite. When the storm breaks over us — that's when you come."

*Delay,* she thought now, watching the procession crawl between the standing stones that flanked the road, each stone carved with a king's face worn smooth to anonymity. Delay was such a small word for what she was doing. She was going to hold the storm back — hold it coiled above the valley like a fist that had already decided — until the moment it mattered, and holding a storm back cost ten times more than loosing it. It was like holding shut a door with a hurricane behind it.

Blood ran off her fingertips and steamed where it hit the rock.

The first crack of thunder came anyway — early, impatient — and she screamed at the sky, actually screamed, words in the old smuggler tongue that had no polite translation, and the thunder swallowed itself, sulking. Below, the procession stopped. Torches guttered. Somewhere down there, a priest began to sing, and the sound carried up the ridge thin and cold as wire.

Coral set her feet wider on the stone, opened her ruined hands, and held.

* * *

Wren rode at the rear of the procession with his hood up and his heart going like a war drum, and he counted the standing stones the way his mother had taught him to count exits in any room: always know how many ways out, always know which ones are lies.

There were nine stones. There should have been eight. He'd ridden this road a hundred times as a boy, racing stable boys, drunk once at seventeen, grieving at nineteen when his mother died and no one at court would let him mourn inside the walls. Eight stones. Now nine, and the ninth stood at the exact center of the road, taller than the rest, and its carved face was not worn smooth at all. Its face was new. Its face was a wolf's.

*Well,* he thought. *That's not subtle.*

"Aldric." He urged his horse forward through the ranks of lords, ignoring the sharp looks, until he drew level with the white horse and the man on it. Up close, in the torchlight, his brother looked terrible — waxen, luminous, like something lit from behind rather than within. But the eyes. For one flickering instant, the eyes were Aldric's own: exhausted, furious, *begging*. Then the crown caught the torchlight and the eyes went flat and ancient again.

"The bastard rides with the court tonight," the king said, and the voice was Aldric's voice pitched slightly wrong, like a song played a half-step low. "How fitting. Family should witness family."

"I wouldn't miss it," Wren said lightly. "You know me. Never missed a coronation."

"There is no coronation."

"No," Wren agreed. "Isn't that interesting."

The entity smiled with his brother's mouth, and Wren felt the ninth stone notice him — actually *feel* it, a pressure against his spine like a finger tracing his name. The marking at the festival had never stopped; he'd been able to sense it for days now, a cold thread running from the crown to somewhere behind his sternum, tightening by degrees. Tonight it pulled taut.

*Come,* said something that was not a voice, in the place where voices don't go. *Vessel. Come home.*

His horse shied. Wren steadied it with his knees and did not let himself look at the ridge, at the green-black darkness gathering there, at the woman holding a hurricane on a leash made of her own blood. If he looked at her, the thing wearing his brother might follow his gaze, and everything would end here on the road instead of at the center of the rite.

So he looked at Aldric instead, and mouthed, silent, the words they'd agreed on: *Hold on. Hold on. Hold on.*

And Aldric — deep down where the drowning man lived — heard, and held.

The procession reached the ninth stone. The hollow bells rang their soundless peal. The priests unrolled a circle of black cloth stitched with a century of dead kings' names, and the entity turned Aldric's face up toward the ridge, toward the storm it could surely feel waiting, and spoke aloud in a voice that carried to every corner of the valley:

"Let the sky attend. Let the debtor be named. Let the vessel open."

The black cloth began, softly, to smoke.

* * *

On the ridge, Coral's knees buckled and she caught herself on raw palms and laughed — a wild, cracked sound torn away by wind — because she understood suddenly, with a smuggler's clarity, exactly what the rite needed. Not the court. Not the priests. The court was decoration. The rite needed a vessel, and the vessel it had chosen was Wren, and the black smoking cloth was a door being cut in the world, and if Wren stepped into that circle the prophecy would close like a snare and there would be no unbinding, no choice, nothing but a dead king and a wolf on a throne forever.

"Now," she breathed. "Now, now, now—"

She let go.

The storm came off the ridge like the wrath of every exiled thing in Thornfell's history. It hit the Coronation Road sideways, tore the black cloth to ribbons, ripped the crowns off startled lords and sent them rolling in the mud, snapped the ninth stone at its base with a crack like the world's spine breaking. Rain fell upward. Lightning walked the road on legs of white fire, and everywhere it touched, the hollow bells finally rang — one vast, shattering note, a century of silence ending all at once.

In the chaos, Wren was moving, dragging his brother's horse around, hauling Aldric bodily from the saddle, shouting words Coral couldn't hear over the roar. And the entity turned its stolen face up to the storm, to *her*, and she saw it understand — saw the ancient patience curdle into something very like fear.

Coral slid down the ridge on a rope of her own lightning, half-falling, half-flying, her gift pouring out of her in a torrent she could not have stopped if she'd tried, and the last of it, the final bright coin of everything she had ever been, she clenched in her fist like a knife.

The wolf beneath the crown looked at her across the ruined road, over the body of the king it wore, beside the bastard it had marked — and smiled.

"You brought the key," it said. "How kind. The lock is *right here*."
