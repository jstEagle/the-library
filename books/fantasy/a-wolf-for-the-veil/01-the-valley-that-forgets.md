## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The forge remembered her mother's hands better than Odile's own.

She knew this the way she knew the valley's weather — not as thought but as fact, settled deep as bedrock. Every morning she banked the coals the way Maren Villalobos had taught her, three breaths on the bellows, no more, and every morning the fire rose in a shape that was almost right and never quite hers. The iron sang under her hammer, but it sang in her mother's key. Eleven years since they'd buried Maren beneath the rowan at the forge's edge, and still Odile worked inside the echo of a dead woman's rhythm, fitting herself into it like a hand into a glove made for someone else.

The glove fit too well. That was the trouble.

She quenched the hinge-pin she'd been finishing — one of forty ordered for the meetinghouse doors, honest work, good work, work that would hold for a hundred years and be forgotten by everyone who leaned on it — and stood a moment with the steam curling around her wrists, watching the light come down through the smoke-hole in the roof. Dust motes turned in it, slow as snow. Outside, the village of Hollowmere was dressing itself for the Founding Festival: garlands going up along the well-house, trestle tables carried out into the square, children already shrieking through the lanes with paper lanterns that would be lit at dusk. Tomorrow the whole valley would gather to remember how their ancestors had crossed the mountains and closed the passes behind them, sealing out the old world and its old magic, building a life so quiet and safe that even memory had gone soft here, worn smooth as river stone.

Safe. The word sat in Odile's chest like a swallowed coin.

She hung the hinge-pin to cool and went, as she did every evening when the forge cooled enough, to the rack beside the back wall where the sword hung.

It was not supposed to exist. Swords were forbidden in the valley — had been since the founding; the elders said steel made for killing called to something in the blood, and the valley had been built to keep the blood quiet. Farming tools, yes. Butchering knives, yes. The ceremonial blade the festival-warden carried was blunted and brass-hilted and had never cut anything but ribbon. But this sword had been her mother's, hidden behind a false panel in the forge wall, wrapped in oilcloth, and Maren had died without ever telling her daughter why she kept it, only gripping Odile's wrist once, hard, and saying: *Keep it. Don't show them. And don't stop practicing.*

So Odile practiced.

She took it down now, unwrapped it, and stepped out into the yard where the grass grew tall between the quenching trough and the woodpile. The evening was turning gold at the mountain-rimmed edges of the sky. She set her feet the way the forms demanded — though who had written the forms, she could not say; they lived in her body the way the valley's songs lived in its children, learned from nowhere, older than any teacher — and began.

First form: the rising cut, slow, the blade tracing an arc from hip to heaven. Second form: the falling answer. Third form turned her whole body, heel grinding the earth, and by the seventh form she had stopped thinking entirely, which was the only way to do them, because thinking broke them.

And then, as always happened when she stopped thinking, the words came.

They came up through her arms like water rising through roots. Not in her ears — in her bones. A humming, a pressure, syllables that shaped themselves against the inside of her skin and dissolved before they could become language. Tonight they were louder than usual. Tonight, with the festival fires being kindled all across the valley below, the sword felt warm in her grip, warmer than the forge-work could explain, and the humming climbed toward her throat until her lips parted around sounds she couldn't make —

A crow went up off the rowan tree, screaming.

Odile stopped. The words sank away like fish into deep water. Her heart was going hard, and her hands, when she looked at them, were steady, which somehow frightened her more than shaking would have.

"Same time tomorrow," she said aloud to the empty yard, and heard how thin the joke rang.

* * *

The festival eve feast filled the square with smoke and fiddle-music and the smell of roasting apples. Odile went because not going would raise questions, and questions were the one thing the valley grew more abundantly than barley. She ate at the end of a table with the cooper's family and let old Bramwell tell his founding story for the four-hundredth time — how the first settlers had fled the sorcery-wars beyond the mountains, how the founders had paid a price she wasn't old enough to hear about, how the valley's borders were blessed and blind and nothing wicked could find its way in.

"And that," Bramwell concluded, thumping the table, "is why we forget what lies outside, and why what lies outside forgets us."

*Why we forget.* Odile turned her cup in her hands. She had grown up inside that sentence and never noticed it had teeth. When she tried to remember her mother's stories of the world beyond the pass — Maren had traveled before Odile was born, everyone said so, though no one could say where or why — she found the memories smooth and featureless, like stones from which the moss had been scrubbed. She remembered that there had been stories. She could not remember a single one.

"You're quiet tonight, Odile." The speaker was Elder Sarelle, silver-braided, kind-eyed, terrible. "Not excited for tomorrow?"

"Always am," Odile said, and smiled the smile the valley required.

Sarelle held her gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then the moment passed, and the fiddles struck up again, and Odile excused herself early, walking home under the first stars with the lantern-light of Hollowmere glowing at her back like something she was already leaving.

She didn't know yet that she was. She only knew that the night felt like the held breath before a bell is struck.

* * *

It was near midnight when she heard the sound from the treeline.

At first she took it for an animal — the valley had wolves enough in the high forest, though they never came down. But the sound came again, and it was not a wolf. It was a man trying very hard not to scream, and failing.

Odile stood in her doorway with the lamp raised. Beyond the forge-yard, the forest began — dark pines climbing the eastern slope, the trees standing shoulder to shoulder like a wall, like a guard. The elders said the treeline was the edge of the safe world. Nothing crossed it. Nothing *could* cross it; the borders were blessed and blind.

Something had crossed it.

He lay half-in, half-out of the fern-brake twenty yards into the trees, and he had crawled, she could see the furrow his body had plowed through the dead leaves, crawled with what must have been the last of his strength toward the lights of the forge. He wore travel-stained clothes of a cut she had never seen — a long coat, buttons of dull grey metal, boots built for roads that Hollowmere had never dreamed of. His left side was soaked black to the ribs.

"Wounds no blade made." She heard her own voice say it flatly, later, in memory. Because that was the truth of what she saw when she knelt and turned him over: five gashes across his chest and belly, deep, weeping, and wrong. No knife made cuts like these — too clean, too straight, each one precisely parallel, as if he had been raked by the fingers of something with a surgeon's geometry. And the edges of the wounds glimmered faintly, the way water glimmers when moonlight touches it, except there was no moon tonight and the glimmer moved, crawling at the rim of each gash like frost given intent.

His eyes found her face. They were grey eyes, fever-bright, and they went wide.

"No," he rasped. "No — you can't be —" A wet cough bent him double. "Villalobos. You have her face. Gods keep me, you have *her* face—"

"I don't know you." Odile's hands were already moving, tearing strips from her apron, pressing cloth to the worst of the bleeding. Warmth soaked through instantly. "Who did this to you?"

"The map—" His hand shot up and clamped her wrist with a strength that had no business surviving in that ruined body. "Listen. There's no time. They'll feel it when I die. They'll know where I fell. You have to burn it, or take it, but you cannot let them—" Another cough, worse. "You're the last. Do you understand? You're the *last*—"

"Last what?"

But his eyes had rolled, and his grip went slack, and somewhere down in the village a dog began to bark, and then another, and Odile looked up at the treeline — at the dark standing dark and patient — and made the choice that would unmake everything.

She got her shoulder under him and dragged him, groaning, across the threshold of her mother's forge, and barred the door against the valley, against the law, against whatever waited in the trees.

Outside, far up the slope, something in the forest shifted its weight, and did not follow.

Not yet.
