## 4. The Hedge-Witch's Bargain

The riders would have caught her at the ford.

Odile knew it the way she knew a blade was about to break — not by seeing, but by hearing. The river ran shallow and loud over its stones, and beneath that noise came another: hooves, many of them, muffled by wet earth but closing. She had crossed out of the valley two days ago with nothing but Merric's map pressed flat against her ribs like a second heart, and she had told herself the fear would feel different once she was moving. It didn't. It felt exactly like standing still.

She waded into the current anyway. The water bit her calves with cold, and her pack — her mother's smallest hammer wrapped in wool, three days of bread gone to two — dragged at her shoulders. Behind her, on the southern bank, the trees stood in their patient ranks, and between them the light broke into shapes that were not deer.

*There.* A horn note, thin as a splinter of ice.

They had been hunting her since the boundary. She understood that now. Not chasing — *herding*, the way you drove a beast toward open ground where it could be taken cleanly. Every road she'd chosen had grown narrower. Every village had watched her pass with eyes that slid away too quickly. And always, somewhere behind or beside, the riders: grey cloaks, grey horses, faces like coins worn smooth.

The far bank rose into bramble and gorse, and beyond it a hillside folded into shadow. If she could reach the fold, she might lose them. She scrambled up through the thorns, feeling them write their small red signatures along her arms, and slipped into the hollow —

— and stopped, because the hollow was already occupied.

A woman sat cross-legged in the dead bracken as though she had been waiting for tea to steep. She was old in the way of river stones: smooth-faced but worn deep, with white hair cropped close to the skull and hands stained green to the wrist. Around her neck hung a cord of knotted feathers and fishbones. Her eyes were the color of pond ice, and they did not widen when they saw Odile. They narrowed, very slightly, the way a merchant's do when he finds the coin is real.

"You're loud," the woman said. "For someone being hunted."

"I don't have time—"

"No," the woman agreed. "You don't." She jerked her chin toward the hillside above. "Six riders. They'll sweep the gorse in a quarter hour, because the gorse is the only place left for you to be. That's how they work. They don't catch you. They make the world small until catching you is just tidying up."

The horn sounded again, closer. Odile's hand went to the sword at her hip — her mother's sword, the one thing she'd carried from the forge besides the hammer — and the woman's eyebrows climbed.

"Oh, you're *that* kind of fool. Good. Fools live longer than the careful; the careful die of caution." She unfolded herself with a series of small cracks, like a house settling, and pushed back the bracken behind her to reveal a low door of woven hazel set into the hillside. "In. Quickly. And if you value your skin, don't touch anything that hums."

Everything in Odile screamed that this was a trap — that the hollow, the old woman, the convenient door were all part of the world being made small. But the horn sounded a third time, and traps at least had walls, and walls could be defended.

She went in.

* * *

Inside was larger than outside, which should have been impossible and which Odile decided, in the exhausted way of the hunted, not to think about. The witch's home was one round room dug into the hill, its ceiling ribbed with roots that dripped a slow, luminous sap. Bundles of herbs hung from every rafter. Jars lined the walls in ranks, each holding something suspended in amber fluid — a moth with too many wings, a key that turned slowly on its own, a human tooth that was, Odile was fairly sure, watching her back.

"Sit," said the witch, pointing at a stool. "Don't touch the stool's cousin."

"The stool has a cousin?"

"Everything has a cousin. Sit."

Above them, muffled by earth and root, came the drum of hooves passing. Then voices — the riders calling to one another in short clipped phrases, the sound of swords parting gorse. One voice passed directly overhead, so close Odile could hear the rider breathing, and under her ribs the map seemed to heat against her skin, and she pressed her palm flat over it and held still and thought of her mother's forge, of the even ring of hammer on steel, until the breathing moved on.

A long while after, the witch spoke. "They'll cast back at dusk. You've bought yourself an evening. Who are you?"

"Nobody."

"Mm. Nobody carries a blood-inked chart against her ribs like a relic and flinches when hounds are named. I smelled the map before I smelled you." The witch crouched, peering up at her with those pond-ice eyes. "Show me."

Every instinct said no. But the instinct had also said *don't trust the hollow in the hill*, and the hollow had saved her life, and Odile was tired of instincts that only ever told her what to refuse. She drew the map from inside her coat and unrolled it across the floor between them.

The witch went very still.

It was the first time Odile had seen anyone look at it who wasn't dying. Merric had looked at it the way a man looks at a debt. The elders of the valley had looked at it the way priests look at heresy. The witch looked at it the way a surgeon looks at a wound she has seen before and failed to close.

"Binding-circles," the witch said at last. Her voice had changed — quieter, rougher, like a file drawn over iron. "The old wards around the crown. Do you know what you're carrying?"

"A warning. A man died giving it to me. He said the king is being eaten from inside."

"Eaten." The witch laughed, and there was no humor in it, only an old bitterness worn smooth as her face. "That's a gentle word for it. I was court witch once, girl. Before. I drew half these circles myself, when they were meant to *protect* the crown-wearer, not cage him with his parasite. Then I saw what the circles were truly for, and I said so, and I was given a day to leave the capital alive. I took eleven hours. I've regretted the other thirteen every day since." She touched the map with one green-stained finger, tracing a line of blood-ink, and where she touched it the ink seemed to darken, as though drinking. "This route isn't a road. It's a diagnosis. Whoever made this walked the binding-circles and marked where each one has rotted."

"Merric," Odile said. "His name was Merric."

"Then Merric was braver than the whole college of physicians and twice as doomed." The witch sat back on her heels. "I'm Sera. Formerly Witch of the Third Circle, formerly trusted, currently a hedge-hag who talks to jars. And you are nobody, which means you're either lying or orphaned by more than parents."

Odile looked at her. At the jars, the watching tooth, the roots bleeding soft light. At the door the woman had opened without asking anything first.

"Odile," she said. "My name is Odile Villalobos."

Something happened then that she would remember all her life: the room *listened*. The hanging herbs stirred without wind. In its jar, the key stopped turning. Sera's eyes went wide, and then narrow again, and she breathed out slowly through her nose.

"Swordspeaker," she whispered. "By the drowned gods. They hid you in the valley, didn't they? The Veiled Valley. Of course. Of *course* they'd put the last oath-heir where the oaths can't hear her." She began, absurdly, to laugh — a dry, cracked sound. "And now she's carrying the map of the king's chains through open country with six riders on her scent and no idea what she is."

"I know what I am," Odile said, and heard how young and stubborn her own voice sounded. "I'm a smith's daughter."

"You're a smith's daughter the way a sword is a piece of iron." Sera rose, joints crackling, and began pulling bundles down from the rafters with brisk, angry purpose. "Eat. Sleep. At dusk we move."

"We?"

"You want to reach the capital. I want to reach the circles before whoever wears the crown finishes eating whoever sits under it. Our roads are the same road." She thrust a bowl of something dark and steaming into Odile's hands. It smelled of mushrooms and rosemary and, faintly, of lightning. "But understand me, girl. This is a bargain, not a rescue. You guide me to the capital alive, and I teach you what the oaths are — what *you* are — before the coronation kills us both. Those are the terms. I don't give gifts. Gifts make slaves of the giver."

"And if I say no?"

Sera smiled, and it was almost kind. "Then eat, sleep, and walk into the dawn alone, and I'll bury whatever the riders leave of you. The offer stands either way. That's what makes it a bargain."

* * *

*Sera had lied about one thing, and she knew it, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone as the girl slept.*

*She had said she wanted to reach the circles. True. She had said the roads were the same road. Also true. What she had not said was why the crown-thing had let her live, all these years, in its kingdom — she, who had seen behind the veil of it, who had spoken its true nature aloud in the Third Circle with the king's own guards at the door.*

*Because it wanted her here. Because exiles make excellent bait, and bait is only useful when the quarry is moving, and the quarry — the last swordspeaker, hidden forty years in a valley that forgets — was finally, finally moving. Sera had felt it the moment the girl crossed the boundary: a tug behind her sternum, delicate as a fisherman feeling the line go taut. The thing in the crown had spent four decades building a road to this child, and Sera's exile had been the first stone laid.*

*She looked at Odile sleeping by the banked fire, the mother's sword across her knees even in dreams, her fingers twitching through forms she'd never been taught. The girl's lips moved sometimes. Shaping words no one had given her.*

*Let it come for us, then,* Sera thought, turning the key-in-its-jar to face the wall so it couldn't watch. *Bait can bite. Bait can choose which snare springs. And when the thing in the crown reaches for this child through me, it will find I have spent thirteen hours a day for thirty years learning exactly how its snares are tied.*

*She banked the fire lower and began, softly, to count her knots.*

* * *

Odile woke at dusk to the smell of ash and rosemary and found Sera already dressed for travel, a hooded cloak the color of wet bark draped over her shoulders, a staff in her hand that had definitely not been in the room before.

"Terms accepted?" the witch asked.

Odile flexed her fingers. Somewhere below her fear, below her grief for Merric, below the guilt that had ridden out of the valley with her, something else had been waiting — the same hum she'd felt in her mother's forge, in the sword-forms, in the words she didn't understand. It had a shape now. It had a *name-shaped hole* in it, and for the first time she wanted to know what fit there.

"Terms accepted," she said. "Teach me on the road."

"Roads are the best classroom. Everything on them is trying to kill you; it focuses the mind." Sera pushed open the hazel door onto the blue-dark hillside. Far off to the south, where the land fell away toward the river and the capital beyond it, a light was burning that was not a star and not a beacon — a smudge of gold on the horizon, like a coal that refused to die.

"What is that?" Odile asked.

Sera was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had the file-over-iron roughness in it again.

"That's the palace, girl. It's burning something tonight. And things that wear crowns burn things when they're getting ready to be crowned." She set her staff to the earth and started downhill. "Walk fast. We've less time than the map says."

Behind them, in the jar on the shelf, the key began to turn again — faster, and faster, and faster.
