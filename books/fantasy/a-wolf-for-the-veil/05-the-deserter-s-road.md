## 5. The Deserter's Road

Tam Vess had learned to sleep in thirds. A third of the night watching the fire, a third watching the dark beyond it, and a third pretending to be a man who could still close his eyes without seeing what he'd seen.

He was in the second third when the horses screamed.

He came up out of his bedroll with his knife already in his hand — old habit, the only inheritance his father had left him that mattered — and found the camp exactly as he'd left it: a low fire banked to embers, two bedrolls, and the witch's mule tied to a birch with the patience of an animal that had survived worse owners than Sera. The scream had come from down the slope, where the road bent around the shoulder of the hill toward the river crossing.

Then it came again, and this time he understood it wasn't horses at all. It was laughter. Wrong laughter, stretched like taffy, like something learning the shape of joy from a description written by someone who'd never felt it.

He knew that sound. He'd heard it in the throne room of Vael Corrin, three winters ago, on the night he stopped being a king's man.

* * *

The court had been rotting for years before anyone said so aloud. Tam had watched it happen the way you watch fruit go soft — slowly, then all at once, then too late to do anything but smell it.

Minister Aldous Crane had been the first to change. Tam had guarded the council chamber for eleven years and knew every minister's walk, every cough, every habit of hand. Crane used to drum his fingers when he thought, quick as rain on a roof. One morning in the spring of the bad harvest, Crane walked past Tam's post with his hands perfectly still at his sides, and he smiled at Tam — smiled, which Crane had never once done in eleven years — and the smile sat on his face like a mask hung slightly crooked.

By midsummer, six ministers had stopped drumming their fingers. By autumn, the court musicians played the same seven songs over and over, because those were the ones the king asked for, and the king asked for nothing else. The kitchens stopped serving anything bitter. The gardeners pulled up all the thorned roses. Little cruelties of sameness, spreading like frost across a window.

And the laughter. That was the worst of it. Courtiers laughed now at nothing — at silences, at griefs, at the funeral of the Lord Chancellor's daughter — and they laughed *together*, in unison, a dozen throats finding the same note at the same instant, and the note was always just a little too high.

Tam told himself it was fashion. Told himself it was fear. Fear made people strange; he'd seen it in sieges and plagues.

Then, on the night of the winter solstice, he'd been posted inside the throne room during the private audience, close enough to see what sat behind the king's eyes when the king turned his head to the shadows and spoke in a voice that used none of the king's throat.

It had looked back at Tam. Just for a moment. Just long enough to *file him away*, the way a clerk files a document — and Tam had understood, with the cold certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that he had been noticed, and that noticed things did not stay long in that court.

He deserted before dawn. He'd been running since, and telling himself the running was a kind of living.

Until tonight, apparently, when instead of running he was walking toward the thing he'd fled, in the company of a witch who smelled of woodsmoke and grudges and a girl from the hidden valley whose sword hummed words when she slept too near it.

The laughter on the road swelled, and Tam went downhill toward it, because apparently he'd also stopped being sensible somewhere along the way.

* * *

They'd picked up the deserter four days ago, at a crossroads shrine where he was arguing with a god that probably wasn't listening.

That was how Odile had put it, anyway, later, to Sera, in the tone she used when she thought Tam couldn't hear: *we picked up a stray.* He hadn't corrected her. Stray was accurate. He'd been sitting at the shrine of the Twin Lanterns with his boots off, feeding dried figs to a stone fox that some pious traveler had left as an offering, when the girl and the witch came up the western road arguing about whether blood-inked maps counted as evidence or as weapons.

He'd known what she was before she'd said ten words. Not the valley — the valley was a rumor even among deserters, a story guardsmen told each other about a place the crown's hounds couldn't scent. But the way she held her shoulders, square and ready, like a drawn blade looking for a reason — he'd seen that posture once before, in the training yards, on the one other swordspeaker the crown had ever found, before the crown found a use for him that no one spoke of afterward.

So Tam had stood up, put his boots on, and said, "You're going to the capital, and you're going to die there, and I'd rather watch it happen than wonder about it," and somehow that had constituted joining.

Sera hadn't trusted him. Good. Sera struck him as a woman who'd survived precisely because she didn't trust anything softer than flint. She'd checked his knife for poison sigils while he was still wearing it, which took a certain professional admiration to attempt.

Odile had simply watched him with those gray eyes that missed less than they should, and said, "Why?"

And Tam, who had rehearsed a dozen lies on the road to the shrine, had heard himself say the truth instead: "Because I ran once already, and it didn't take."

Now he crested the slope above the river crossing and saw why the night was laughing.

There were five of them on the road below — or five shapes wearing the idea of riders, the way a scarecrow wears the idea of a man. They sat their horses wrong, spines curved forward like question marks, and where their faces should have caught the moonlight they caught nothing at all, just a smooth pale blankness that hurt to look at directly. Hounds, Sera called them. Not dogs. Hounds. Things the crown-thing grew, the way a tree grows galls.

They had cornered a peddler's cart against the riverbank. An old woman and a boy, maybe her grandson, pressed against the water with nowhere left to be.

Tam drew his knife and his sword both, and did not let himself think about the last time he'd faced hounds, in the cellars under the palace, when Ser Ollivan had gone into the dark laughing and come out of it in pieces.

"Vess." Sera's voice behind him, low and furious. "Don't—"

"They're going to kill them," he said.

"They're *bait*, you idiot, they're bait for *us*—" But she was already moving, staff in hand, muttering the first syllables of something that made the air taste of iron, and Tam understood that she'd known it was a trap the whole time and was coming anyway, and something in his chest that had been dead for three winters shifted in its sleep.

Below, Odile was already running.

* * *

Later, Tam would try to reconstruct the fight and fail, because fights were like dreams that way — you kept only the pieces that cut.

He remembered the peddler woman screaming at them to run, which marked her as braver than half the ministers he'd ever guarded. He remembered the first hound turning its blank face toward him and the wrongness of it, the sense of being *read*, catalogued, filed — the throne room all over again, condensed into a shape that could gallop.

He remembered Sera's counter-words hitting the lead hound like a hammer through wet paper, unraveling it into a black smoke that screamed in the stretched-taffy voice.

Mostly, though, he remembered Odile.

She fought like nobody he'd ever trained beside, and he'd trained beside the best the crown could buy. She didn't fight like a girl taught forms in a forge-yard. She fought like someone remembering. Her blade sang — actually sang, a thin harmonic that set his teeth aching — and where it passed, the hounds' blankness cracked, and behind the cracks there was something old and hungry and *afraid*, and Tam realized with a lurch that these things feared her sword the way he feared the throne room.

The fourth hound got past her. It came for him — or seemed to; later he suspected it had been aimed past him, at the cart, at the boy, at making them hesitate. Tam put himself between it and the child because his body decided before his mind did, three winters of running undone in half a heartbeat.

The hound hit him like a falling wall. He went down into the shingle, tasted blood and river-stone, and looked up into a face with nothing on it, and the nothing leaned close, and in the blankness he saw — reflected, the way you see yourself in a dead eye — the throne room, the crown, the thing behind the king's eyes, and it *knew him*, it had never stopped knowing him, and a voice that used none of his throat said:

*Little bird flew the cage. Little bird comes back.*

Then fire took the hound's head off.

Not fire — Sera's working, whatever it was, white and silent and smelling of struck flint. The hound came apart across Tam's chest in a spray of cold ash. He lay there gasping while Odile's singing blade finished the last one, and the night went quiet except for the river and the peddler woman pulling her grandson against her and weeping in the ordinary, human, ragged way that Tam hadn't heard in three years and hadn't known he'd been starving for.

* * *

They buried nothing, because the hounds left nothing but ash. They gave the peddler woman coin — Sera grumbled, Odile insisted — and sent her north away from the capital road, and then the three of them sat around a rebuilt fire and did not look at each other for a long time.

It was Odile who finally spoke. "You knew what those things were. Before Sera named them."

"I served the crown," Tam said. "Eleven years. You see things."

"You *fought* like someone who'd fought them before."

He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Once," he said. "In the cellars. With Ser Ollivan, who was the best of us. We were sent to retrieve something the king had lost down there." He almost laughed. "We were sent to retrieve it *for* it, though I didn't understand that until after. Ollivan didn't come back whole. What came back of him was laughing." He looked up. "I ran the next morning. I've been running for three years, and I want you to understand something, girl, because you're going to hear it from worse mouths than mine soon enough: running doesn't work. Those things don't forget. They file you away, and they wait, and eventually the road bends back toward them. Yours bent tonight."

"So why fight?" Odile said quietly. "If running doesn't work and fighting feeds it what it wants — why stand between a hound and a stranger's child?"

Tam considered the question honestly, which was more than he'd given any question in three years.

"Because I was tired," he said at last. "Tired of being a man who watches. And because—" he gestured vaguely at the two of them, the witch sharpening her staff-knife, the girl cleaning ash off a blade that hummed faintly even now, like a struck bell forgetting itself, "—because I'd forgotten what it looked like, people standing next to each other. In the court, near the end, everyone stood alone. Everyone. Even the ones holding hands." He shook his head. "Tonight nobody stood alone. I'd forgotten rooms could be like that."

Sera snorted, but softly, and didn't contradict him.

Odile looked at him across the fire for a long moment, and then she did something that undid him more thoroughly than the hound had: she tossed him the whetstone. Trust, in an object small enough to lose. "Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch."

He caught it. He slept in one piece, for the first time in three years, and dreamed of nothing at all.

* * *

Two days later, on the ridge above the river Vael, with the capital's spires finally visible in the haze — white towers crowned in gold, beautiful the way a well-dressed lie is beautiful — Tam stopped walking.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

He didn't answer at first, because answering meant admitting it. Along the capital road, at every milestone, every shrine, every gallows-post, the crows had gathered. Hundreds of them. Silent. All facing the city, all facing the palace, motionless as painted things.

"They're not birds," Tam said. His voice came out flat and far away. "Or they are, but they're *worn*. Like gloves." He swallowed. "It knows we're coming. It's known since the crossroads. Maybe since the map was inked." He turned to look at Odile, and behind him the crows, all together, all at once, turned their heads too — a thousand small movements in perfect unison, the court's laughter made feather and bone.

"It's not waiting to hunt us," he said. "It's waiting to *welcome* us. There's a difference, and the difference is a trap with our names on the invitation."
