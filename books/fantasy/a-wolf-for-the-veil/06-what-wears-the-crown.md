## 6. What Wears the Crown

The capital announced itself before they saw it — a smell of smoke and river rot, then a sound like the whole world clearing its throat. Odile stood at the crest of the last hill with Sera's grey cloak pulled over her head and Tam's borrowed boots pinching her feet, and she looked down at Vael Amaranth, city of a hundred spires, and felt her teeth ache.

Not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The swordspeech under her skin had been awake since the valley's edge, a second heartbeat that quickened now until she could feel it in her jaw, behind her eyes, in the fillings of her bones.

"Stop staring," Tam muttered. "Staring gets people noticed."

"I'm not staring."

"You're glowing."

She looked down at her hands. She wasn't glowing, not truly — but the air around her knuckles shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers above a forge, and when she breathed out it came silvered. She clenched her fists and willed it still.

"It does that near him?" she asked.

Sera didn't answer at once. The hedge-witch had gone quiet in the way Odile was learning meant *calculating* — her small eyes moving across the city below as though reading a ledger only she could see. "It does that near power," she said finally. "And there is nothing in this kingdom more powerful than what sits on that throne. Come on. We want to be inside the walls before the noon bell, when the guards change and stop looking at faces."

They went down into the noise.

* * *

Vael Amaranth was loud in a way the Veiled Valley could never have prepared her for. In the valley, sound behaved itself; here it piled up in corners, shouted from balconies, wept in alleys. Fishmongers and preachers and children selling stolen apples, cart wheels grinding, a fiddler playing something jaunty with a wrong note buried in it like a splinter. Odile kept her hood up and her eyes down and catalogued everything the way Merric had taught her without meaning to: exits, blades, hands that lingered too long on hilts.

The city was dressed for a coronation. Banners of gold and white hung from every window, and workmen swarmed scaffolds along the processional route, stringing garlands that were already wilting in the heat. Everywhere the same face, painted on cloth and carved in wood and stamped on cheap tin medallions sold by street hawkers: the face of Prince Aurelian, the crown-thing's chosen heir. Young, handsome, smiling a smile that had been painted by someone who had never seen him smile.

"He looks kind," Odile said.

"Paint always does," said Tam.

They found a room above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, where the stink of lye and urine covered their scent from anything that hunted by magic as well as nose. Sera paid the dyer's widow triple and asked no questions and got none. From the narrow window Odile could see, far off across the rooftops, the palace rising pale and many-towered above the city like a jaw full of teeth.

"It'll announce a public blessing tomorrow," Sera said, unpacking her satchel onto the floorboards. "Before a coronation, the old rite requires the sovereign to walk among the people. To be seen. To be *touched*. It's the one day the king comes out of the palace, and the one day the wards around him are thinnest — crowds are messy things, magically speaking." She glanced up. "You wanted to see him. Tomorrow you'll see him."

"And then what?"

"And then we know what we're dealing with." Sera's voice went flat. "I've read about the binding-circles. I've never seen one worn by a living man. I need to look at it with my own eyes before I can tell you how to unmake it — or whether it can be unmade at all."

Tam sat on the bed with his back to the wall, cleaning a knife he did not need to clean. "You're both talking like we're going to walk up to the king in an open square and have a nice long look. There will be guards. There will be *hounds*, maybe. There will be—"

"There will be us," Odile said, "and there will be a crowd, and crowds are messy things."

Sera smiled thinly. "She learns."

* * *

The blessing took place in Coronation Square at midmorning, under a sky so blue and cloudless it felt like a lie told by the weather.

They arrived early and let the crowd close around them like water. Odile wore a dyer's apron over her clothes, her hair bound and stained at the ends with indigo; Tam stood at her shoulder wearing the blank, bored face of a man who carried crates for a living; Sera had smeared herself with age — a stoop, a shawl, trembling hands — until she looked twenty years older and entirely forgettable. They were three faces in ten thousand.

Then the bells began, and the crowd roared, and the king came out.

He rode in an open carriage drawn by six white horses, and the first thing Odile thought was that he looked *small*. A thin man in heavy robes, silver-haired, his hands folded in his lap like two sleeping birds. The crown sat on his head and it was wrong — she knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a dream is wrong even before you can say why. It was old gold, older than any kingdom, worked into shapes that hurt to follow, branches and roots and something like ribs. And it did not sit upon him. It *grew* into him. Where the metal met his brow the skin was smooth and unbroken, as if the crown had been swallowed upward through his skull.

The crowd cheered. The king raised one hand in a small, tired wave.

"Look at his shadow," Sera whispered, not moving her lips.

Odile looked. The sun stood high and the shadows of the horses and the carriage fell crisp and short upon the stones — but the king's shadow stretched long and wrong, crawling up the steps of the reviewing stand ahead of him, pooling at the feet of the ministers who waited there. And the ministers — Odile counted seven of them standing in a row — all of them leaned, very slightly, toward that darkness. Like plants toward light. Like mouths toward food.

Her second heartbeat slammed once, hard, against her ribs.

The carriage stopped. The king descended, slowly, helped by attendants, and began the long walk down the barrier line where the common folk pressed forward to touch the royal hand for luck. He touched a baker's floury fingers. He touched a child's head. He blessed a bundle of wheat. Each time, Odile saw the same thing: the moment of contact, the flicker of something desperate and human crossing his ruined face — and then the crown drinking it, whatever it was, some brightness going out of him like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

*He's still in there,* she thought. *Whatever's eating him, he's still in there.*

She didn't decide to move closer. Her feet decided. The crowd carried her, or she used the crowd — sliding between a fishwife and a soldier, drifting down the barrier line, closer, closer, until she stood three bodies deep with the king perhaps ten paces away and coming toward her, and the swordspeech in her blood rose like water in a flooded cellar.

*Speak,* it seemed to say. *Here is a thing worth saying words to.*

"No," she breathed.

But the gift had never been hers to command, not yet, not fully. As the king drew level with her — as those grey, exhausted eyes swept across the crowd and snagged, for half a heartbeat, on her hooded face — the words came up her throat like a tide.

She didn't speak them aloud. That was the horror of it. Her mouth stayed shut and the words spoke themselves anyway, through her skin, through the air, in a language that had no letters and needed none:

***Crown-of-ribs, root-and-thorn, I see the shape beneath your shape.***

The square went silent.

Not all of it — the fiddler kept playing, a baby kept crying, somewhere a vendor kept calling prices — but everything *near* her, a bubble of dead air ten paces wide, as if the sound had been eaten. The king stopped walking. His hand, extended toward a farmer's wife, hung in the empty space between them.

And the crown turned.

Not the man. The man's face stayed slack, terrified, human. But the crown — the gold branches, the rib-shapes — rotated on his head with a slow grinding motion, like a key turning in a lock, and Odile understood with absolute clarity that she was being *looked at*. Looked at by something that had been waiting a very long time to hear those words, from anyone, from anywhere.

***Little sword,*** said a voice that was not a voice, arriving directly behind her eyes like a nail driven in. ***Little lost sword. Who forged you? Who let you out of your little valley?***

Odile's hand flew to her hip, found nothing there — they'd left the blade hidden in the room, wrapped in oilcloth — and the absence of steel made the words come harder, wilder, bleeding out of her whether she willed them or not:

***I am no one's forging. I am the oath and the oath is mine—***

The pressure clamped down like a fist. Pain flared white behind her eyes. Through it, distantly, she heard the king make a small sound — a cracked, human sound, almost a word, almost *run* —

Then Tam's shoulder hit her, hard, spinning her sideways as a crate-laden porter stumbled between them, and Sera's stick jabbed her in the back, sharp and ordinary, and the bubble of silence burst. Sound rushed back in: cheering, bells, the fiddler's jaunty tune with its splintered note. The king had moved on. He was blessing wheat. His face was a mask of mild, vacant benevolence, and the crown sat still upon his brow as if it had never turned at all.

Only his shadow remained wrong — stretched back across the stones, past the crowd, its far end resting, light as a finger, on the toe of Odile's boot.

"Walk," Sera hissed. "*Walk.*"

They walked. Slowly, in the manner of people with nowhere particular to be, out of the square, through the cheering streets, back through the tanner stink to their narrow room, where Odile sat down on the bed and discovered her hands would not stop shaking.

"It saw me," she said. "It knows what I am."

"Yes," said Sera. She was already pulling herbs from her satchel, crushing them, her movements quick and precise. "Drink this. It won't hide you — nothing hides you now — but it may muffle you. Make you harder to find in a city of forty thousand hearts."

Tam stood by the window, looking down at the street, his knife out again, turning it over and over. "We should leave tonight. While it's deciding what we are. Get out of the city, get ahead of whatever it sends—"

"No."

They both looked at her. Odile heard the word again in her own ears and found, to her surprise, that she meant it completely.

"It's spent years hiding inside a king," she said. "It wears smiles and ministers and a chosen heir, and it moves slow because slow is safe. And today, in front of ten thousand people, it *stopped*. Because of me." She looked down at her shaking hands and made them still. "Merric died getting that map to someone. The king in that square begged me with his eyes. I'm done running. If it wants to know who I am—" she lifted her chin, and the silvered breath came out of her steady now, "—it can ask me to my face."

Sera and Tam exchanged a look over her head, the look of two people deciding whether to argue with a storm.

It was Sera who broke it, with a dry, tired smile. "Well," she said. "At least we're agreed on the direction. Though I'd have preferred a plan."

* * *

The invitation came at dusk.

No knock preceded it — just a soft rush of wings, and Tam whirled from the window with his knife drawn as something pale settled on the sill outside. A bird, or the shape of a bird: white as bone, too large for a dove, too still for anything alive. It held a scroll in its beak, sealed with a disc of gold.

Nobody moved for a long moment. On the sill, the bird-bthing waited with the patience of furniture.

Finally Odile crossed the room and took the scroll. The seal bore the royal arms — but pressed into the wax beside them, so shallow you'd miss it if you weren't looking, was the shape of ribs.

She broke the seal and read aloud, because the alternative was reading it alone in her own head:

*"To the speaker of unbidden words, greeting. The court has long lacked honest conversation. My household requests the pleasure of your company tomorrow, at the ninth bell, in the Hall of Quiet Hours. Come alone. Come unarmed. — Aldric Rex."*

Below the signature, in different ink, in a cramped and hurried hand that shook:

*Come. Please. Before it chooses for us both.*

"That's the king's hand," Tam said quietly. "I watched him sign orders for six years. That's him. That's really him, underneath."

Odile folded the letter along its creases, once, twice, until it was a small hard square in her palm. Outside, the bird-thing unfolded wings that were made of something like paper and something like skin and lifted away into the dark.

Tomorrow, then. The Hall of Quiet Hours. Whatever waited there — the thing that wore the crown, or the man being devoured beneath it, or both, wearing each other — she would walk in and face it.

She thought of her mother's forge. Of the hum in the sword-forms she'd practiced a thousand times without understanding. Of a valley built to hide her, and a life that had fit too well because it had been fitted to her like a cage measured in secret.

"They wanted a weapon they could keep in a box," she said softly, to Sera, to Tam, to herself. "Let's go show them what the box has learned."

She set the letter on the table between them, and the three of them bent over it together, and began — at last — to plan.
