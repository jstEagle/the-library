## 7. Audience with a Hollow King

The invitation smelled of lilies and something underneath them — old meat left too long in a warm room. Odile turned the card over twice before she could make herself touch the seal again: black wax, pressed with a crown whose points curled like talons.

*His Majesty requests the presence of the swordswoman and her companions at the hour of evening bells.*

No name. No threat spoken aloud. The threat was the whole of it, folded small as a moth's wing.

"They know," Tam said flatly. He stood at the window of their rented room with his back to her, watching the street below where lamplighters moved through the blue dusk. "Of course they know. It noticed you yesterday in the square. I told you — when it looks at someone, it doesn't forget."

"It didn't look at me," Odile said. "It looked *through* me."

"Same thing, from where it sits."

Sera had not spoken since the invitation came. She sat on the floor with her back against the bedframe, her counter-rite chalks scattered untouched around her knees, staring at the seal as though it might open its wax eye. Now she said, quietly, "It wants an audience. That means it wants something it can't take."

"Or it wants to take it somewhere private," Tam said.

They went anyway. There was no version of this road that did not pass through the throne room; Odile had known it since the moment her gift flared in the square and the king's head turned toward her like a compass needle finding north. Running would only mean arriving later, and more afraid.

She dressed plainly — no disguise now, there was no point — and wore her mother's sword openly, because hiding it felt like lying to herself. Sera braided a thread of rowan into her collar, muttering words that tasted of smoke. Tam cleaned his boots three times, which was how he prayed.

At the palace gates, guards in silver-and-black took their names without writing them down. That frightened Odile more than any blade could have. Things that needn't be written are things already known.

* * *

The throne room was built to make visitors feel like weather — brief, insignificant, passing over something permanent. Vaulted stone went up and up into gloom, hung with banners so old their colors had gone the gray of drowned things. Torches burned without flickering, which was wrong; fire should breathe. The air tasted of incense laid over rot, the same lily-sweetness as the invitation, thick enough to chew.

And on the dais, beneath a canopy of black velvet stitched with stars that were not quite stars, sat the King of Veymar.

He was smaller than he'd seemed in the square. That was the first cruelty of him up close — a broad man gone hollowed, like a great tree with its heartwood eaten out, bark still standing, still green at the edges, fooling everyone. His crown sat low on his brow, and it did not reflect the torchlight so much as drink it.

But his eyes — his eyes were two wars happening at once.

"Leave us," the king said, and the voice was his own, rough and human, and then something moved behind his face like a fish under ice and the voice came again, layered, harmonized with itself: "*Leave us.*"

The courtiers fled. The guards hesitated, then bowed themselves backward out of the doors, and Tam and Sera were pulled along in the tide of silk and steel until only Odile remained on the long floor of the throne room, alone, her footsteps echoing like heartbeats.

"Come closer," the king said. "Please." And the *please* cracked down the middle, and she knew which one of them had said it.

She climbed the dais steps until she stood three steps below the throne, close enough to see that his hands, gripping the armrests, had worn grooves into the wood. Years of gripping. Years of holding on.

"You're fighting it," she said.

A laugh came out of him, ragged. "Fighting. Yes. That's a kind word for it. Twenty years of fighting." His jaw clenched; the muscles of his face jumped and settled, jumped and settled, like a man wrestling his own shadow in the dark. "It sleeps after the feasts. Not sleep — it *digests*. And in those hours I get to be Aldric again. A few hours. Sometimes less." He looked up at her, and for a moment his eyes were only a tired man's eyes, wet and desperate and terribly ordinary. "You're the swordspeaker. From the valley."

"I don't know what I am."

"You're the last of the oath-line. You're the reason the valley exists." He said it simply, without ceremony, the way a doctor says the name of a disease. "Your grandmothers' grandmothers built those walls to hide a child not yet born. They saw what was coming for the crown, and they hid the only blood that could unmake it, and they let the forgetting magic take even their own memories, because a secret no one knows is a secret no one can be tortured out of." His mouth twisted. "I'm sorry. I imagine you wanted more time to hate that gently."

Odile's hands had gone cold. Somewhere under her ribs, the second heartbeat — the gift — pulsed once, slow, like something turning over in its sleep. "Then you know why I've come."

"I know why you've come." He leaned forward, and the crown shifted, and for one terrible instant the thing inside him looked out — a pressure, a vast attention, like the sky leaning down — and then Aldric wrestled it back under and gasped as if surfacing from deep water. "Listen to me. Listen, because I don't know how many minutes are mine. You want to kill it. Every part of you is singing to do it right now — I can hear your sword wanting it. But if you strike me while it holds me, you don't kill it. You kill the last wall between it and everything else. It feeds on death — that's what it *is*, a hunger wearing a shape. Kill the king and it takes the kingdom. Kill the heir at the coronation and it takes—" he shuddered— "it takes everything it has been starving toward for twenty years. A kingdom's grief, all at once. A coronation's worth of hope, spilled."

"Then what do I do?" Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You called me here. Tell me there's a way or tell me nothing."

"There's a way." His grip tightened on the armrests until the wood creaked. "The binding-circles. Your map shows them — the courier died getting it out, did you know that? Merric. Good boy. I sent him myself, between the hours when I am myself." Something broke open in his face. "The binding was made by the first swordspeakers. Crown and wearer, bound together, each holding the other — it was meant to cage the thing forever. But bindings are oaths, and oaths are speech, and speech can be *unbound* by the right voice. Only the oath-line can speak it. Only at the thinning."

"The coronation."

"The rite thins the veil between crown and wearer — it must, to pass the thing from me into the new vessel. In that moment they are neither separate nor joined. One voice, speaking the unbinding, could cast it out of both of us. Out of the world, maybe. Or at least out of anything with a throat to wear." He laughed again, awful and small. "Do you understand what I'm asking? I'm asking you to stand in front of all the power it will ever have, at the exact moment it's strongest, and speak. Not swing. Speak. And trust that the oaths answer you."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we die, and it wears my successor, and you'll have a very short life full of running." He sagged back. Sweat stood on his brow. "I have watched it eat my ministers and wear their faces. I have watched my wife grow afraid to sleep beside me. I have signed orders I don't remember signing. Whatever dignity I have left, I am spending it begging a stranger with a sword to save my kingdom, because there is no one else." His eyes found hers, human and drowning. "End it. Not me. *It.* Can you?"

For a long moment Odile could not speak. She thought of her mother's forge, the hum in the steel, the words she'd never understood rising off the hot metal like steam. All her life a locked room, and here at last the door, and behind it not glory but a dying man begging.

"Yes," she said. "I think I can."

"*Thank*—" he began.

The torches guttered, every flame in the hall bending sideways as if in wind from a door opened onto winter. The king's spine straightened. His face smoothed. When he spoke again, the voice was layered, harmonized, patient as erosion.

"How touching," it said. "The little sword speaks promises."

Odile's hand went to her hilt. The gift roared awake under her skin, every nerve singing, and the thing in the crown regarded her with something horribly like amusement.

"Do you know," it said conversationally, "how convenient you are? A hedge-witch, exiled twenty years ago — exiled by *my* decree, through *my* sleeping mouth — nursing her grievance in the borderlands, waiting for exactly such a map to fall into such hands. A deserter who knows the guard rotations. And the last oath-heir, raised ignorant, ripe, and angry." It tilted the king's head. "I did not summon you, child. I *cultivated* you. Sera of the fen-roads was given her exile the way a gardener gives water. Every step of your road was planted."

Behind her, at the far doors, she heard Sera's breath catch — heard Tam say, very quietly, "Gods. She's right. The decree had my captain's seal, but the order came down from above, and none of us ever knew why a hedge-witch mattered—"

"No," Sera said. Just that. *No.* Small and fierce, from across the vast cold room.

"The coronation," the crown-thing continued, ignoring her, savoring, "will proceed. Come. Stand in your place. Speak your little name. I have waited twenty years for the last key to walk willingly into the lock." The king's mouth smiled without the king in it. "Bindings cut both ways, oath-speaker. Unbind me — and learn what else comes loose."

The torches straightened. The audience was over.

Odile walked back down the long floor with her spine rigid and her mind roaring, past the doors where Tam gripped his sword-hilt white-knuckled and Sera stood pale as paper, shaking her head, whispering, "It's lying, it has to be lying, Odile, I never—"

"I know," Odile said, and found she meant it. "It lies the way it breathes. But it also just told us the truth by accident." She pushed open the doors into the night air, into the city's noise, and heard her own voice come out hard and clear.

"It's expecting us at the coronation. So that's exactly where we'll be — but on our terms, not its plan. We have one night." She looked at them both, at the family she hadn't chosen yet but already belonged to. "Sera — whatever it planted in you, we dig it out tonight. Tam — find your loyalists. Tomorrow we end this."

Above the palace spires, unseen, the moon was already thinning toward the dark of the rite.
