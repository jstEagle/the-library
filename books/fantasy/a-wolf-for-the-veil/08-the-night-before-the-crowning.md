## 8. The Night Before the Crowning

Tam had spent eleven years learning which doors in the palace opened quietly, and tonight every one of them seemed to creak.

He moved through the undercrofts with a hood drawn low and a guardsman's walk — that particular slouch of a man going somewhere dull and authorized. The loyalists were where he'd left them: in the old armory beneath the east wing, the one the crown-thing's ministers had forgotten when they forgot everything else. He could hear them before he saw them. Not talking. Whetstones. The soft rhythmic whisper of men who had decided something.

Forty-one of them. He counted by habit, counting the way other men prayed.

"You came back," said Captain Ilsa Vane, and there was no surprise in it, only a kind of grim satisfaction, as if she'd bet on him at poor odds and won. She was gray at the temples now, thicker through the shoulders, but her hands still moved over her blade like a lover's.

"I said I would."

"Deserters say many things." She let the silence sit just long enough to hurt. "You saw it. Behind his eyes. You ran."

"I ran." Tam's voice came out flatter than he intended. "I've been running for three years, Captain, and I'm tired of the taste of it. Tomorrow that thing crowns its heir, and after that there will be nothing left to run from because there will be nothing left at all. Just a kingdom wearing a mask shaped like a kingdom."

Around the armory, the whetstones went quiet one by one.

Vane studied him, then the two figures at his back — the witch with her ink-stained fingers and the valley girl who stood like she didn't know yet how much room she took up. "And these?"

"These are the reason I stopped."

* * *

Sera had not slept in two days, and her workshop showed it.

She'd claimed the scriptorium of a defunct monastery three streets from the cathedral, and she had turned it inside out. Chalk circles overlapped chalk circles on the flagstones, some scrubbed away and redrawn so often the floor looked like a map of weather. Bundles of rowan hung from the rafters beside salt-packed herrings — the monks' pantry had been generous and Sera was not a woman to waste salt. The air tasted of iron filings and burnt juniper, and underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the cold mineral smell of the binding-circles themselves, which had gotten into everything, into her hair, into her dreams.

The counter-rite was a sentence being unmade word by word, and she had only tonight to finish it.

The problem was symmetry. A binding laid in ceremony must be unbound in ceremony; the coronation rite was a lock turning, and to turn it back she needed the original key's shape. She had the king's genealogy spread across four tables. She had the liturgy of the crowning copied out in her own hand, each line annotated in red where the words bent wrong — where *may the crown keep him* had been substituted, centuries ago, for *may he keep the crown*. Small change. The kind of small change that ate kings.

She heard Odile come in the way she'd learned to hear her: no hesitation at the threshold anymore, but a pause, always, one breath, as if the girl checked each new room for exits. Valley habit. Hiding-place habit.

"The wards are done?" Sera asked without looking up.

"Sera." Odile's voice was wrong. Careful. "I need you to look at the map again. The margins. Merric wrote in the margins and I never — I couldn't read the script. You can."

Sera set down her pen. Some things you understood before you understood them, the way you felt rain before it fell.

The map was worse than she remembered — blood did not age well, and Merric's route had gone the color of rust and old grief. But the margins, ah, the margins were a clerk's hand, tight and patient, the hand of a man who had copied documents for years and knew how to fit a confession into an inch of space. Sera held it to the lamp, angled the light, and translated slowly, because some things deserved to be said slowly:

*The Veiled Valley was not found empty. It was made empty. Founded by the swordspeaker oaths' last steward, who gathered what remained of the line after the purges and carried them beyond the edge of every map. The magic cannot reach them there because the valley is an oath — the last oath ever spoken, woven shut. It forgets so they may be forgotten.*

Sera stopped. In the silence she could hear the girl breathing.

*There is one heir left,* the margin ended. *I have seen her name. She carries it unknowing, as her mother carried it, as hers before. When the time comes she must speak it herself. No one else can carry it to her. That is why I am carrying the map instead.*

Odile sat down. Not collapsed — sat, deliberately, on Sera's stool, hands flat on her knees, staring at the middle distance where her whole life was rearranging itself.

"She knew," Odile said. Her voice was very level, which was worse than weeping. "My mother. She kept a forge and taught me to shoe horses and she knew I was — the whole valley was a wall built around me. Every festival. Every law about strangers. The elders wouldn't let Merric in because the valley is *supposed* to keep things out, it's supposed to keep *me* stupid and safe and—"

"Odile."

"—and my gift never woke because it was *buried*, it was buried under a hundred years of forgetting, and I practiced sword-forms that hummed and nobody told me—"

"*Odile.*" Sera took her face in both hands, ink and iron filings and all. "Listen to me. Yes. It was a hiding place. Yes. They stole your history and they called it protection and it was, probably, even kindness, once. Grieve it. You should grieve it, and you will, later, when there's time. But hear this too." She waited until the girl's eyes focused. "A hiding place keeps you small. That's all it can do. What happens tomorrow isn't about what they hid. It's about what you do with the only thing they couldn't hide."

"What's that?"

"Yourself. Standing right here. Choosing to be here."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had settled in her face — not peace, nothing so soft, but a decision taking on weight and density, becoming a fact about the world.

"Then teach me the counter-rite," she said. "All of it. If I'm the key, I want to know the shape of the lock."

* * *

Tam found them at dawn-ish, the hour that wasn't night or morning, when the candles had burned to thumbs and the chalk dust lay on everything like the first dirty snow of winter.

He stood in the doorway of the scriptorium and watched them for a moment: the witch asleep sitting up, pen still in her hand, a line of red annotation trailing off the page into nothing; the valley girl reading the liturgy aloud in a low steady voice, practicing, each phrase handled like a blade she was learning to hold without cutting herself. Between them, the map lay open, its margins spent.

Three years ago he'd have laughed at this table. A hedge-witch, a horse-shoer, and a deserter, meaning to unmake a thing that had eaten a kingdom from the inside. Three years of running had burned the laugh out of him and left something cleaner.

"Vane's people are placed," he said, when Odile looked up. "Forty-one. Doors held from the inside, the gallery above the nave, and the crypt stairs. They'll hold the doors as long as holding's possible." He hesitated. "Vane asked me to tell you something. She said: *tell the girl the guard remembers the oaths too. Oaths are older than crowns.*"

Odile was quiet a moment. "It's true, then. What Sera said. The whole world is full of promises waiting for someone to say them properly."

"That's a terrifying thought," Tam agreed. "Tomorrow, try not to say any of them wrong."

But his jest came out crooked, because he had seen behind the king's eyes, and he knew what would be looking at her from the throne when she spoke. He crossed to the window. Below, the city was already waking toward the coronation — bunting going up in colors whose brightness had something rabid in it, bells being tested, a hush under all the noise like the hush before a storm or a lie. And along the boulevard to the cathedral, spaced at intervals too regular for festival, the crown-thing's hounds walked in pairs, heads low, tasting the air.

One of them stopped. Lifted its head. Looked directly at the window, across three streets and a pane of glass, and held there.

Tam stepped back from the light, heart slamming, and turned to the others. Sera was awake after all; she'd seen his face.

"They know," he said simply. "Or it does. There's no more sneaking to be done. It's tomorrow, or it's never."

Odile rose. She picked up her sword — her mother's forge-mark on the scabbard, the blade that hummed — and buckled it on with steady hands, and when she spoke, her voice had the settled weight Sera had seen arrive the night before, a decision grown into bedrock.

"Then we don't sneak," she said. "We walk in the front, with the crowds, and we stand where we're meant to stand. It wanted me found." She touched the scabbard once, lightly, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder before hard news. "Let's not disappoint it."

Outside, the first bell of the coronation began to ring — early, wrong, a note that bent in the ear like heat off iron. Somewhere beneath the cathedral, in circles older than the stones above them, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, with infinite patience, to wake.
