## 9. Coronation of the Devoured

The cathedral had been built to make people feel small, and it succeeded.

Odile stood in the shadow of a pillar near the western door, beneath windows of stained glass so old the saints depicted there had lost their faces, and felt the weight of ten thousand years of stone pressing down like a hand on the crown of her head. Incense thickened the air — myrrh and something sweeter underneath, something wrong, like flowers rotting in a locked room. The congregation murmured in their finery, a sea of silk and fur and candlelight, all of them turned toward the dais where the heir waited in white.

She could feel the wards before she saw them.

They hung over the nave like frost on glass, a lattice of pale geometry visible only when she let her swordspeech rise toward her eyes. Sera's counter-rites sat woven through them, thin bright threads of hedge-magic stitched into the great cold pattern — invisible unless you knew to look, and Sera had spent three sleepless nights teaching Odile to look. *Find the knots,* the witch had said, her fingers stained with ash and chalk. *Every binding is a knot. Every knot can be spoken loose.*

*And if I can't speak it?*

Sera had smiled with all the warmth of a drawn blade. *Then we improvise, girl.*

Now Odile looked, and her stomach turned. The lattice was not one knot but hundreds, and at its center, above the dais, above the heir's bowed white head, hung the crown itself — displayed on a cushion of black velvet, carried in procession an hour ago, and even now it *breathed*. Slowly. Patiently. The way a spider breathes.

Beside her, Tam shifted his weight. He wore the old guard colors under a plain cloak, the blue-and-silver he'd sworn off and never truly shed, and his hand rested on the pommel of his sword with the ease of a man who had decided, finally, what he was for. "Doors first," he murmured. "When it starts, I hold the doors."

"When it starts," Odile said.

"You'll know." His jaw worked. "I've seen it start before. You'll know."

Across the nave, Sera stood among the lesser clergy in borrowed vestments, gray hair hidden beneath a deacon's hood, chalk dust ghosting her knuckles. She did not look at Odile. They had agreed not to look at each other. Looking was a thread, and threads could be followed.

The organ began.

It was a beautiful sound, and that was the horror of it — golden notes rolling down the vaulted dark while the thing in the crown drank the reverence of ten thousand faithful like wine poured into open mouths. The choir sang. The incense swung. The heir rose from his knees, a young man named Aldric whom Odile had seen only from a distance: broad-shouldered, sincere, chosen precisely because he believed. Belief was a door. The crown-thing had always entered through doors people opened willingly.

The archbishop lifted the crown from its velvet.

And the king screamed.

It was not loud. That was the worst part. From the royal enclosure beside the dais, where the devoured king sat propped in ceremonial splendor, came a sound like wind through a keyhole — thin, high, almost inaudible beneath the organ — and only Odile heard it, because only Odile's gift was tuned to the frequency of oaths breaking. The king's body did not move. But behind his eyes something surfaced, drowning, and for one terrible instant his gaze found hers across the whole width of the cathedral, and his mouth shaped two silent words:

*End it.*

Her sword hummed against her hip.

Not steel — she had learned that much. It was her mother's blade, reforged in her mother's forge, and it had never once been used to cut. It was a speaking instrument, a throat for the oaths that slept in her blood, and as the archbishop raised the crown toward Aldric's head, every word Odile had ever half-heard in the valley's forge-fire rose up in her at once, a tide against the inside of her ribs.

The crown descended.

The wards flared white.

"NOW," Sera's voice cracked across the nave like a whip, and the world broke open.

* * *

Tam hit the doors as the first hounds came through them.

They were not dogs. They wore the shapes of dogs the way the crown wore the shape of a king — loosely, hungrily, as a costume worn by something older — and they came down the cathedral steps in a black flood, eyes like coals, breath like grave-cold. Tam planted himself in the doorway with his back to one door and his shoulder to the other and became, for as long as his body would allow it, a wall.

He had held worse doors. He told himself that. He had held the door of the king's bedchamber the night he'd seen what sat behind the king's eyes, and he had run from it, and he had been running for six years, and this — this was the door he owed.

"HOLD!" he roared, though no one needed telling, and met the first hound with steel and fury and the particular gracelessness of a man who has stopped caring whether he survives so long as the thing behind him fails.

Chaos bloomed through the nave. Screaming congregations are a tide; they surge and crush and trample, and Sera moved through them like a needle through cloth, unhurried, precise, her chalk-white hands sketching signs in the air that unraveled the wards one knot at a time. Each knot fought her. Each knot *knew* her — she felt the crown-thing's attention turn toward her like a lighthouse beam, felt her own exile written into its wards, the bait-trap of it closing at last around the trap-maker's ankle instead.

*Clever girl,* the wards seemed to whisper. *Clever witch. We unmade you once.*

"You unmade nothing," Sera snarled, and tore the third knot open with her bare hands, and blood ran freely down her wrists, and she laughed.

Above them all, the crown settled onto Aldric's head.

The scream that followed was not the king's thin wind-through-a-keyhole. It was the building screaming — stone groaning, glass singing, the saints in their faceless windows seeming to lean away. Aldric's body arched. White robes smoked. And the crown-thing poured itself down through the young man's open, believing mouth like ink through water, and Odile understood with sick clarity that in ninety seconds there would be nothing left of Aldric at all, and after him, through him, it would take the kingdom, and after the kingdom—

She ran for the dais.

* * *

The guards should have stopped her. Some of them tried. But her swordspeech woke fully then, for the first time in her life, and the words that lived in her blood went out ahead of her like a herald: *stand aside, stand aside, stand aside.* Not compulsion — invitation. An oath offered. And men who had spent years serving a hollowing king heard in it the voice of every oath they'd ever made and meant, and their swords drooped, and their feet stepped back, and Odile Villalobos ran up the steps of the high altar untouched.

The crown-thing turned Aldric's head toward her.

Its eyes were the eyes from Merric's dying description, the eyes from Tam's haunted telling, the eyes she had dreamed for a hundred nights: patient, ancient, amused. When it spoke, Aldric's voice and a thousand other voices spoke together, layered like pages of a burned book.

*The last swordspeaker. Come home at last. Come to feed me, as your line has always fed me. Do you think the valley hid you? The valley* seasoned *you.*

Odile stopped three paces from the dais. Her heart slammed. Her sword hummed so loudly now that the nearest candles guttered in sympathy.

"I know," she said.

*You know?*

"Merric's map. The valley's founding. My mother's forge, and the forms she taught me that weren't forms at all — they were sentences. I know what I am." Her grip tightened on the hilt. "You want me to draw this blade. You want the strike. Killing the king feeds you — he told me. Killing the heir feeds you faster. Every oath broken in anger, every name spoken as a weapon — that's your food. That's always been your food."

The thing smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was a terrible smile, because it was young and sincere and utterly not his.

*Then what will you do, little heir?*

What she would do. She thought of the valley, warm and small and suffocating. Of Merric's blood cooling on her forge floor. Of Sera's torn hands and Tam holding a door because he owed a debt to his own soul. Of her mother, who had known, who had spent a lifetime teaching her daughter sentences disguised as sword-forms, love disguised as discipline, a hiding place disguised as a home.

The sacrifice was supposed to be the blade. She had walked into this cathedral prepared to give everything — her life, if the unbinding demanded it, her self, her name dissolved into the oaths like salt into water. She had said goodbye to herself on the cathedral steps.

But standing here, with the thing's amusement washing over her like cold water, she understood that sacrifice offered in hatred was just another broken oath. Another meal.

So instead of striking, she knelt.

She laid the flat of her mother's blade on the stone before her, knelt behind it as a knight kneels, as a speaker kneels, and she did the one thing the crown-thing could not have foreseen, because nothing in its long hungry history had ever taught it this:

She gave her name away freely.

"I am Odile," she said, and the word rang through the cathedral like a bell struck in a tomb. "Daughter of Ysolt, of the valley that forgets, of the line that kept the oaths when no one remembered there were oaths to keep. I do not speak my name to bind or to break. I speak it to *give* it — to the king who suffers, to the boy who believes, to the witch and the deserter and the dead courier who carried truth in his blood. Take my name. All of it. Spend it."

The oaths answered.

They came up out of the stone of the cathedral, out of the bones of the mountain beneath it, out of ten thousand years of promises kept and promises betrayed, and they poured through the blade the way dawn pours through a window — not striking, not cutting, *filling*. Odile felt herself emptied and filled in the same heartbeat, hollowed like a flute so that something vast could sound through her, and the sound was her own voice multiplied beyond counting, every swordspeaker who had ever lived speaking through the last one who remained.

The crown-thing shrieked.

It was not amusement anymore. It clawed at Aldric, at the king, at the wards, at Sera's unraveling threads, trying to hold what was being unmade — for a binding cannot survive the free consent of the bound, and Odile had just consented on behalf of every soul in the building, and the oaths, hearing consent at last after centuries of coercion, *loosened*. Everything loosened. The king's binding. The heir's. The crown's own ancient shackles, the ones that had chained it to its hunger, and the thing discovered too late that it had never been the binder at all — only the best-bound creature in the room.

Light took the cathedral. Not fire. Not judgment. Just light, level and clean, the light of a forge at full heat, her mother's light, and in it Odile saw the crown lift from Aldric's head of its own accord, blackening, shrinking, unraveling into smoke that thinned and thinned and — hesitated. Hung. Looked at her with what remained of its attention.

*What* are *you?* it seemed to ask, in a voice suddenly very small.

"Free," Odile said. "Like everyone else."

And the smoke went out like a candle pinched between wise fingers.

Silence. Real silence, the first the cathedral had known in a generation. Somewhere Tam was laughing and crying at once against a door littered with dissolving hounds. Somewhere Sera sat down abruptly on the altar steps, bleeding and grinning. The old king breathed — a whole breath, deep and unwatched, the first in years — and young Aldric knelt dazed and empty-headed and *his own*, blinking at the crown's ashes scattered on the velvet like dead moths.

Odile stayed kneeling a moment longer, her hands still on the cooling blade, her name echoing somewhere far away in the lattice of oaths that now, gently, began to set her back down inside herself.

Mostly set her back down.

Because as the light faded, one thread of it lingered — fine and bright and insistent, running from the blade in her hands out the shattered western doors, north, toward the mountains, toward the valley that forgot.

And in that thread she heard, unmistakable, a voice she had not heard since childhood.

Her mother. Calling her home.
