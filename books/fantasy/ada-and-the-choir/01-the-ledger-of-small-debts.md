## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Casper Thackeray finally allowed himself to do the arithmetic.

He did it the way he did everything — quietly, invisibly, in the margins where no one looked. The scriptorium of Thornfell temple was empty at this hour except for Brother Aldous, who slept sitting up three desks away with his chin on his chest and his spectacles pushed into his hair, and Casper had learned long ago that a man could think dangerous thoughts as long as his hands stayed busy and his face stayed blank. So he copied the evening office — *O Light that kindles the stone, O Voice that wakes the deep* — in his small, even hand, and between each line, while the quill traveled and the ink dried, he counted.

Fourteen winters. That was what remained. Fourteen more years of copying prayers for a debt his father had taken on before Casper was old enough to hold a quill — a debt for seed grain, for a bad winter, for a mule that died in the thaw, for all the ordinary catastrophes that turned a free farmer into an indentured man and his son into temple property. Fourteen winters, if the abbot continued to credit him at the same rate: one copper mark struck from the ledger for every hundred pages copied, less the cost of his bread, his candle, his cell, his boots. Less the fines. There were always fines. Ink spilled was a fine. A page smudged was a fine. Once, when he'd been eleven, falling asleep over the psalter had cost him a full season's earnings, and he had not fallen asleep over a page since.

Fourteen winters. He would be thirty-one. His father would be dead or nearly so, and Casper would walk down the Thornfell road with nothing but the clothes the temple had given him and the skill of writing beautifully for no one's benefit but God's, and he would owe nothing to anyone.

That last part was the part he held onto. He turned it over the way other men turned a coin in their pocket. *Owing nothing.* It was the whole of his ambition, and it fit inside a word so small you could lose it in a sentence.

He dipped the quill. *O Voice that wakes the deep.*

It was a strange prayer, when you thought about it. Most of Thornfell's liturgy was strange, though Casper had stopped noticing years ago, the way a man stops smelling his own house. The mountain temple sang to the mountain itself — that was the doctrine, more or less. The stone had been first, the stone had heard the first song, and the temple's duty was to keep singing so the mountain would keep sleeping, or keep standing, or keep something; the catechism grew vague exactly where a curious boy might want it precise. What mattered practically was that the choir sang four times a day in the vaulted undercroft where the acoustics were uncanny, and that the mountain, as far as anyone could tell, declined to comment.

Casper could not sing. That was the joke his life rested on — a scribe in a singing temple, tone-deaf since birth, or near enough. When the brothers had tested him as a child, lining the foundlings and indentured boys up in the undercroft to hear which voices made the stone hum, Casper had opened his mouth and produced something that made old Brother Fenwick wince and wave him out of the line. *A pity,* someone had said. *Such a still child. Stillness is half the gift.* But there was no gift in him, apparently, and so he had gone to the desks instead of the choir, and the desks had saved him, in their way. Scribes were useful. Scribes were kept.

He finished the line. In the margin, invisible unless you knew to look — a tiny stroke, a tally mark like a shepherd counting sheep — he added one more hour to today's column. Eleven hours worked. At this rate, fourteen winters became thirteen and a half, if he could shave the fines down to nothing, if his eyes held, if the candle allotment didn't shrink again the way it had two years ago when the tallow shipments from the lowlands got thin.

Thirteen and a half winters. He exhaled slowly through his nose, careful not to disturb the flame.

"Casper."

Brother Aldous hadn't been asleep after all, or had woken without moving, which amounted to the same thing in a man of his age. He peered at Casper over the rims of his spectacles with the mild, bottomless patience that made him impossible to dislike and equally impossible to read.

"You've been at that page an hour," Aldous said. "The office should have been done by compline."

"The illumination takes time." Casper kept his voice level. "The initial letter wants gold leaf, and the gilder won't be back until Thursday."

"Mm." Aldous rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and gathered his own stack of copied sheets. He paused at the door. "You're wanted. The abbot has an errand. Archive work."

Casper's stomach performed a small, private drop. Archive work meant the under-crofts below the library, the cold rooms where the temple kept what it did not display — old charters, defunct rites, correspondence with houses that no longer existed. It was dull, dusty, badly lit work, and it was also, in Casper's experience, the only work in Thornfell that ever surprised him. The living parts of the temple ran on rails. The archive did not.

"Tonight?" he said.

"Tonight. He says the tax assessors come at week's end and he wants the debt rolls current." Aldous's mouth twitched, almost a smile. "He trusts your figures. Don't tell him I told you he does."

Casper blotted his page, capped the ink, and followed.

* * *

The stair down to the archive was cut directly into the mountain's bones, and it was always cold enough to feel like a decision you couldn't take back. Casper went down with a lamp in one hand and his keys on the ring at his belt — he was one of only three people in the temple with archive keys, a fact he had never examined too closely because examining it felt like looking down a well. The walls sweated mineral damp. Somewhere above, muffled by forty feet of stone, the compline choir was finishing, and the sound reached him strangely — not fainter, but altered, as though the rock itself were humming along a half-beat behind the human voices, a great slow echo that no throat could make.

He had heard that echo his whole life. Everyone at Thornfell had. They called it the mountain listening, and they treated it the way farmers treat weather: as a fact, unremarkable, not worth investigating.

The archive occupied three chambers. The first held charters and land records. The second held the rite-books, including several so old the script had to be read sideways and the ink had gone the color of dried blood. The third — the deepest, coldest, locked behind a door most brothers assumed was a storeroom — held the ledgers.

The debt rolls lived in the third chamber, and that was where Casper went, because that was his errand. He found them where they always were: on the second shelf, bound in grey calf, one volume per decade going back past the founding of anything else in the temple. He pulled the current roll, set it on the reading table, and began the tedious business of reconciling columns against the abbot's day-book.

His father's name was on the fourth page. *Thackeray, Osric, of Hollowmere farm — principal 40 marks, interest accruing, surety: one son, Casper, age 7 at indenture.* Seven. He had been seven. He could just barely remember the ride up the mountain in the grain cart, his father's hand heavy on his shoulder, both of them silent, because what was there to say? The temple fed you, taught you letters, gave you a trade. It took fourteen — now thirteen and a half — winters. Every family in the valley had some version of the arrangement. It was simply how things were done, the way rain was done and taxes were done, and a boy who resented it was a boy who hadn't understood yet that the world ran on debts the way mills ran on water.

Casper had understood at seven. Understanding early was supposed to be a virtue.

He worked through the roll page by page, checking sums, flagging discrepancies for the abbot's attention. Names, amounts, terms. *Marrowgate tannery, surety: two daughters. Greywater mill, surety: one son. Hollowmere, surety: one son.* The pattern was so constant it stopped registering — the temple lent, the poor pledged children, the children grew up copying prayers or scrubbing floors or, in a few cases, joining the choir, until the debt was paid or the debtor died, whichever came first. It was an economy of small sorrows, administered gently, recorded meticulously. Casper had spent his life inside its machinery and had never once thought to call it anything but fair, because fairness, for people like him, was measured in whether the food arrived and the beatings didn't.

He was halfway through the roll, lamp trimmed low, breath fogging faintly in the cold, when he reached for the next volume and his hand came back wrong.

The book beside the debt roll — shelved flush against it, same height, same grey binding, same plain spine — was not a debt roll. He knew this immediately, the way you know a stranger in a familiar room, before you know why. The weight of it was different. Denser. And the pages, when he opened them, were not ruled in the temple's standard columns.

There were names. That much was the same. But beside each name, where the debt roll recorded marks and winters, this ledger recorded something else entirely — a word Casper had seen perhaps twice in his life, always in old rite-books, always in contexts the brothers glossed quickly past.

*Soul. Received. Sealed. Delivered below.*

He set the lamp closer. His hands, he noticed distantly, were very steady, steadier than they had any right to be, because he had trained them to be steady over twenty years of fine work, and they obeyed their training even now.

The entries were dated. The earliest in this volume went back sixty years. The handwriting changed across the decades — different scribes, different nibs, different inks — but the format never varied, as though the format were older than any of the men who kept it, as though it had been handed down intact like a relic. Each entry: a name. An age. A place of origin, usually a village Casper knew, sometimes a farm he could picture. Then a single word describing the subject — *aptitude: high, aptitude: marked, aptitude: exceptional* — and then a date of delivery, and then a receiving signature.

The receiving signatures were all the same. Every one, across sixty years, across hundreds of entries. A symbol Casper did not recognize — a vertical line crossed by three horizontal strokes, like a ladder lying on its side — pressed into the wax beside each delivery.

He turned pages. The names went on. Children, mostly. Ages ranging from six to sixteen. One entry per page at the front of the volume, growing sparser toward the middle years, thickening again recently — the last five years showed more deliveries than any period since the volume began. The most recent entry was dated eight months ago. A girl. Age nine. From a village in the eastern valleys whose name Casper had copied a dozen times in tax correspondence without once thinking about the children who lived there.

*Aptitude: exceptional. Delivered below, first frost. Received:* — the ladder-symbol, pressed deep.

Casper sat back. The chair creaked in the silence, and the silence was enormous, the way silence underground is enormous, full of stone and pressure and the distant memory of the choir overhead.

He was not a fool. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, sitting alone in the cold with a book he should not have opened. He knew what smuggling was; the mountain roads were threaded with smuggler paths and everyone at Thornfell pretended not to know it. He knew what debt was; he was made of it. He knew that institutions kept two sets of books when the truth was embarrassing — every clerk knew that, it was practically the first law of record-keeping. A second ledger, hidden flush against the first, recording children delivered somewhere called *below*, signed for by a symbol instead of a name —

But *below* was a word from the catechism. *Below* was where the mountain's heart was, where the first song had sounded, where the dead of the temple were said to rest in the stone's keeping. It was a holy direction, like east, like up. You did not deliver children *below*. You buried elders below, ceremonially, in clefts the priests chose, and everyone wept and went home.

Unless the catechism was a story laid over something older. Unless *below* had a literal address.

Casper closed the ledger. Opened it again. Closed it. His breath had gone shallow without his permission. He looked at the shelf and saw, now that he was looking, that the grey bindings ran the length of it — a dozen volumes, one per decade, stretching back further than the debt rolls, further than the temple's charter, back to a first volume whose cover had been re-bound so many times it was more repair than book. Six hundred years of entries, if the volumes matched the dates stamped on their spines. Six hundred years of names.

He did not open the oldest volume. Something in him understood, with the animal clarity that arrives ahead of reasoning, that opening it was a threshold, and that he could not know these things and go on being the man who didn't know them. The safe life — the warm cell, the steady work, the counted winters, the clean arithmetic of debt — all of it stood on the assumption that Thornfell was what it appeared to be: strict, cold, but honest. A hard house, not a rotten one.

He had suspected rot before, of course. Everyone suspects. You smell it in small things — the way the abbot's sermons grew carefully vague whenever the court beneath the mountain was mentioned; the way certain brothers went quiet around certain topics; the way the foundlings with the best voices were sometimes transferred, without explanation, to households down-mountain, and no one asked after them, and asking would have been a fine. Suspecting was survivable. Suspicion was a fog you could live in.

This was not suspicion. This was a ledger. Ledgers did not lie; that was the entire moral architecture of Casper's life. Numbers were the one honest thing in a dishonest world. He had built his whole self on the belief that the written record, whatever else failed, told the truth — and here was the written record telling him, in six hundred years of patient columns, that the temple he served had been measuring children like grain and shipping them into the dark, and that his own careful hands had spent two decades making beautiful copies of prayers for an institution that kept its real accounts elsewhere.

He thought of the echo in the stairwell. *The mountain listening.* He thought of the choir tests, the lined-up children, Brother Fenwick waving him out of the line — *no gift in this one* — and how, at seven, he had been ashamed. He remembered being ashamed for years. He remembered praying, actually praying, the only fervent prayer of his childhood, that the stone would hum for him anyway, that he would be chosen.

Chosen. He put his palm flat on the closed ledger, on the grey calf binding, and felt the cold of it climb into his wrist.

Somewhere far above, the midnight bell rang, and the sound came down the stone to him warped and strange, as if the mountain were ringing it, or swallowing it.

Casper did the only thing his training permitted. He returned the ledger to its place, flush against the debt roll, spine aligned, dust undisturbed. He checked the lock twice. He climbed the stair with his lamp held steady and his face arranged into the blankness that had kept him alive for twenty years, and behind that blankness his mind moved fast and cold, sorting, filing, refusing — *not evidence, not yet, maybe misfiled, maybe allegorical, maybe a rite-record, maybe the dead really are below* — building, brick by desperate brick, the wall of reasonable doubt that every man builds when the alternative is fire.

In his cell, he sat on the narrow bed in the dark and did not sleep. Instead, he counted. It was what he had. Fourteen winters on his father's debt. Twenty years of service already rendered. Two hundred pages a week, every week, without fail. He had been a faithful instrument of this place. He had been *good*. And goodness, it appeared, had been the payment — not for freedom, but for blindness. The temple had not merely bought his labor. It had bought the part of him that would have asked questions, and it had paid for that part in bread and candles and the promise, always receding, of owing nothing.

Near dawn, gray light finding the slit of his window, one thought surfaced through the wreckage of the night and refused to sink again. It was small, and practical, and it frightened him more than the ledger had, because it was not a horror but a plan:

The abbot wanted the debt rolls current by week's end, when the crown's assessors arrived. Which meant the crown's men would stand in the archive. Which meant, for a few days, the third chamber would be opened, and busy, and watched by strangers.

And Casper Thackeray, who noticed everything, who missed nothing, who had survived twenty years by seeing what others overlooked — would be the one holding the keys.

He lay back and watched the light crawl down the wall, and somewhere below his feet, deeper than the archive, deeper than the graves, he imagined — for the first time letting himself imagine it — a great dark hall full of hanging lights, each one a name from a grey book, each one waiting.

Eight months ago, a nine-year-old girl had been carried down there, aptitude exceptional, received and sealed.

Her name, he realized, sitting up abruptly in the cold gray morning, was still in his head. He had read it once and could not unread it.

*Ada.*
