## 2. What the Bell Cannot Say

The bell in the tower of Thornfell had a crack in it that no one could hear but Ada.

She had found it two winters ago, lying in the snow below the bell-rope with her ear pressed to the frozen ground, and she had felt it come down through the stone — a splinter in the sound, a small wrongness riding inside the great bronze voice like a thorn inside a rose. The priests said the bell was perfect. The priests said many things. Ada had learned, in her nine years of being the foundling of Thornfell — the child with no name-day, no family, no last name anyone would write in a ledger — that the things the priests said were mostly walls. You did not argue with a wall. You looked for the door in it, or you dug.

She dug.

Her digging was done with charcoal and chalk and the stubby pencils she hoarded like a dragon's gold, on the backs of discarded practice sheets, on the smooth grey slate the kitchen gave her when she swept it well, on her own skin when there was nothing else — faint lines on the inside of her forearm that she washed carefully every evening so that Brother Anselm would not see and ask questions, because Brother Anselm asked questions the way other people swung hammers.

This morning she was drawing the bell.

She sat in her corner of the foundlings' room — she had a corner, not a bed of her own, but a corner with a straw pallet and a wooden chest that had once held altar wine and now held everything she owned: three drawings, a ribbon the color of river water, a bird's skull, a smooth stone with a hole worn through it, and her pencils — and she drew the bell as she heard it. Not as it looked. As it *sounded*. The round gold body of it, and then, cutting through it, the crack: a thin black line she rendered in a single unbroken stroke, because a crack was a single unbroken thing, a seam where the world had not quite finished saying itself.

Tomas, the smallest foundling, crouched beside her and watched.

"What's that?" he asked, which was what he always asked, because Tomas was four and the world was a question to him.

Ada pointed her pencil at the ceiling, toward the tower, and then touched the paper.

"The bell," Tomas said. "I know it's the bell. Why's it got a line?"

Ada tapped the line. Then she pressed her palm flat against the floorboards and looked at him with her eyebrows raised, the way she said *listen*.

Tomas pressed his palm to the floor too, solemn as a priest at consecration. He waited. The floor said nothing to Tomas. The floor never said anything to Tomas. After a while he lost interest, as four-year-olds do, and went off to see if he could persuade the cook into an early heel of bread, and Ada was alone with her drawing and the hum.

Because there it was again — there it always was, some mornings, when the light came through the frost-furred windows and the temple was still half-asleep. The hum.

It lived in her chest. It was not her heartbeat; she had checked, lying very still in the dark with her hand over her sternum, counting one against the other, and the hum went on underneath her heartbeat the way the river went on underneath the ice. It was not her breathing. It was not hunger, though hunger had a sound too, a thin whine like a wet finger on glass. The hum was older than any of those things. It had been there as long as she could remember, which was a strange way to put it, since her memory only went back to the temple gates — to being set down on the step one October night in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life, with no note, no name, nothing but a small silver chain around her neck with a pendant shaped like a bird in flight, which the priests had taken from her "for safekeeping" and which she had never seen again.

The hum rose when she was happy. That was the worst of it, or the best of it; she had not decided. When the kitchen smelled of cinnamon, when the first snow came, when Tomas fell asleep against her side with his fist closed around her sleeve — on all those occasions the hum rose in her chest like water coming to a boil, and it wanted *out*. It wanted her throat, her mouth, the air. It pressed at the backs of her teeth.

She never let it out.

She had let it out exactly once that she could remember, a year ago, alone in the bell tower where no one went because the stairs were rotten. She had opened her mouth and let the hum have her, and what came out was not a sound she recognized as coming from a child. It was a chord. She was nine years old and she had no words for it, but she knew — the way she knew the crack in the bell — that what had come out of her was many voices braided into one, a sound with *floors* in it, levels, like the temple itself. The tower stones had answered her. She had felt them shiver. Dust had risen from the ledges in a slow silver haze, and for three heartbeats the whole tower had *sung*, low and vast, a note she had not made and yet had made.

She had clapped both hands over her mouth and not made a sound for two days afterward.

Because she had also seen, that same month, what happened to the ones they called *apt*.

* * *

The measuring happened in the spring and again in the autumn, in the chapter house with the good windows, and the foundlings were told it was for their own good — a checking of growth, of health, of *aptitude*. The word was always said the same way, with a small reverence, the way you'd say *blessed*.

Ada had been measured four times. Each time, Brother Anselm had her stand on a marked floor tile while he looked at her over the rims of his spectacles and asked her to hum — a plain note, he said, nothing fancy, just *la*, like the drones they taught the older children. And each time Ada had produced the flattest, dullest, most toneless *la* that a child had ever inflicted on a room. She had practiced it. She practiced in the privy, in the woodshed, walking the cloister with her lips barely moving, this dead little note with no floors in it, no levels, nothing but a flat grey sound like a door shutting. Brother Anselm would sigh, make his small mark in his book — she had seen the marks, a column of little dashes — and wave her away.

The others did not practice. The others hummed as they were, and the ones who hummed *well* — who produced a note with a shine to it, a note that made the measuring brother lean forward in his chair — those ones got a second measuring, and then a third, and then, one morning, they were gone.

Not dead-gone. Ada knew dead. Dead was the sparrow they buried behind the kitchen with a ceremony and a small cross. Dead was final and sad and clean. The apt ones were a different gone. They went down. There was a stair behind the reliquary, a door that was always locked except on the nights when it wasn't, and on those nights Ada would wake in the foundlings' room and feel it — a change in the temple's sound, a hush, the way the air goes quiet before snow. And in the morning there would be one fewer child, and Brother Anselm would say that so-and-so had been *chosen for the choir below*, and the word *chosen* would be said with that reverence again, and the porridge that morning was always sweetened with honey, which Ada had come to understand was the temple's way of apologizing without saying so.

Eight months ago it had been a girl named Maret, who was eleven and could make a wineglass ring with a touch of her finger. Ada had liked Maret. Maret had shown her once, in the laundry yard, how to hold a wet finger to the rim and wait, and the glass had sung a thin clear note, and Maret had grinned and said, *You try.* Ada had tried, and the glass had not rung but had *wept* — a shivering, wobbling tone, three notes at once — and Maret had gone very still and looked at her for a long moment and said, "Don't ever do that where they can hear."

That was the last thing Maret ever said to her. Three weeks later, the honey porridge. The locked stair. The gone.

Ada had drawn Maret afterward — not her face, which was already going soft at the edges in Ada's memory, but the wineglass, over and over, a hundred wineglasses on a hundred scraps, each one with its thin singing line rising out of it like smoke. She kept the drawings in the wine chest. She did not know why. It was not as if the drawings could go anywhere or do anything. But it seemed important that the singing line exist somewhere in the world outside Brother Anselm's book, where Maret was nothing now but a name and a mark and the words *aptitude exceptional, received and sealed.*

Received and sealed. Ada had copied those words out too, from memory, in her best hand, and then crossed them out so hard she tore the paper. She did not know exactly what they meant. But she knew what *sealed* meant when you said it about a jar or a tomb, and she was nine, not stupid.

* * *

On the morning the riders came — though she did not know yet that they were coming, that was still days away — Ada was in the kitchen, doing her morning work, which was scrubbing the great hearthstone and laying the fires for the day. The cook, a fat, kind, half-deaf woman called Sorrel, let Ada work alone because Ada's fires were better than anyone's and because Ada did not chatter, and Sorrel had had a lifetime of chatter.

Ada had just coaxed the first flame up when she felt it.

The hum in her chest turned over. Not rose — *turned*, like a sleeper disturbed, like a dog lifting its head before the knock comes. She stood with the poker in her hand and the firelight on her face and listened with the part of her that was not ears.

Down. It was always down. The temple's sounds came down to her through the stone the way the bell's did — footsteps, doors, the scrape of the great bolts — but this morning, underneath all of it, there was something else. A rhythm. A slow, deep, patient pulse, far below the deepest cellar, far below anything the temple admitted to having, and it was not a machine, because it breathed. It rose and it fell. And this morning, for the first time in her nine years, it was *faster*.

Ada set down the poker. She crouched and put her palm flat against the warm hearthstone, and she listened until her knees ached, and the pulse went on beneath her hand, quick and hungry, like something that had caught a scent.

She drew it that evening, by the last of the light, on the back of a practice sheet: the temple drawn as she drew all buildings, as a simple box with its tower, and then — in a color she mixed herself from charcoal and a stolen pinch of red ochre — a shape beneath it. A second temple, upside down, its tower pointing into the earth. And inside the upside-down tower, a small red mark, beating.

She looked at the drawing for a long time. Then she folded it small and put it in the wine chest, under Maret's wineglasses, and lay on her pallet with Tomas breathing against her side, and did not sleep.

Because here was the thing she had been circling for months the way a moth circles a flame, never quite touching: the measuring, the honey porridge, the locked stair, the pulse below — all of it fit together, if you let yourself fit it. The temple did not send the apt children down to sing in some blessed choir. Nothing blessed had a pulse like that. The temple *fed* them down, one by one, the way you fed a fire, and the fire below was burning quicker now, and somewhere in Brother Anselm's book there was a column of little dashes, and one of those dashes was hers, and the only reason she was still above ground was that she had spent two years teaching herself to be a closed door.

Doors did not hold forever.

* * *

Three days later, the riders came, and Ada knew it before the horn sounded, because the dogs in the yard went silent all at once, and because the pulse below her feet stuttered — one skipped beat, like a heart that has been caught doing something.

She was in the yard, carrying water, when they rode in: six of them in the crown's grey and silver, with a crier, and a proclamation nailed to a board, and the abbot coming down the steps with his face arranged into welcome the way you arrange a room for company you fear. She could not read the proclamation from where she stood. She did not need to. She watched Brother Anselm come out of the chapter house carrying the grey book hugged to his chest like a child, and she watched his eyes go down the foundlings gathered in the yard — Tomas, and the twins, and the older ones — and she watched his eyes *skip* her, the smallest skip, a stone off water, and come back.

And then he looked at her properly, and Ada saw the thing she had been dreading and expecting for two years arrive in his face like weather.

He looked at her the way you look at a jar you have finally decided to open.

That night the temple was full of a new sound — the sound of men who had signed something and were telling themselves a story about it. Ada lay awake in the dark and felt the pulse below the mountain quicken, quicken, and the hum in her chest rose to meet it, unbidden, terrified, *ready*, and she pressed her hands flat over her sternum and held it down, held it, held it, the way you hold a bird that will break its own wings against your grip if you do not.

She got up in the dark. She took the wine chest out from under her corner. She took the three best drawings — the bell with its crack, the upside-down temple with its beating red mark, and Maret's wineglass with its line of song — and she folded them small and sewed them into the hem of her skirt with thread she'd been saving, working by touch, by the snow-light through the window. She took the ribbon the color of river water and the stone with the hole in it. She left the bird's skull. It had been hers, but the past was heavy, and she was about to become very light.

She looked at Tomas, asleep, his fist curled around the blanket she'd tucked him under. She memorized him: the cowlick, the chapped lips, the small snore. She could not take him. She was nine and he was four and the mountain roads would kill them both, and a door that opens for one lets the cold in on everyone. She touched his hair once, very lightly, the way you touch a page you must not smudge.

*Tomorrow*, she said, in the only language she had, in the pressure of her hand and the ache behind her eyes. *Tomorrow I go first. And one day I will come back for the rest of you, or I will die trying, and either way, Brother Anselm will never open this jar.*

She lay back down. She did not sleep. Below her, the pulse beat on, quicker now, and above her the cracked bell hung silent in the tower, holding its one flaw where no one could hear it but her — and in the grey hour before dawn, Ada closed her eyes and practiced, one last time, in the dark behind her teeth, the flat dead note of a closed door.

She had three days at most. She could feel it in the stone.

She would need help. She would need someone who knew the way the mountain breathed — and there was only one person in Thornfell who was always, always in the archive, copying, counting, watching the ledgers.

The scribe with the tired eyes, who had once, a year ago, found her crying in the stairwell and had said nothing at all, which was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for her.

*Him*, she thought, in the pressure and the ache. *It has to be him.*
