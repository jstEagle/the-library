## 3. A Hunt Proclaimed

The bells woke him before dawn — not the usual sequence for matins, but the great bell, the one they rang only for coronations and deaths of abbots and fires that threatened the granary. Casper sat up in his cell with his heart already pounding, because the great bell had a third meaning that no one spoke aloud but everyone knew: *visitors from below the mountain.* And visitors from below the mountain never meant anything good for a man whose ledger of debts was kept in someone else's hand.

He dressed in the dark, fingers finding the familiar buttons of his scribe's coat by touch, and stepped into the corridor where cold moved like water. From the high window at the corridor's end he could see the switchback road climbing toward Thornfell's gate, white with rime, and on it — small as ink-mites at this distance — a column of riders. Eight of them, maybe ten. Their banners were furled against the wind, but even unfurled he would have known them: the crowned mountain sigil of House Aldermere, the king's own mark, carried up these roads perhaps once in a decade.

Casper had been at the temple eleven winters. He had seen the crown riders twice before. The first time, they had brought a writ confirming the abbot's authority over the parish tithes. The second time, they had taken a boy.

He did not let himself think about the second time. He went down to the scriptorium instead, because ink was honest and parchment never lied about what had been written on it, and because when his hands were full of work his mind could not wander to places like the archive and its second ledger, its columns of names without prices, its little notation in the margin beside every entry — *aptitude: high* — that had been keeping him awake for six nights now.

But the scriptorium was not empty. Brother Anselm was there, an old man bent over his copying like a question mark, and he looked up when Casper entered with an expression Casper had never seen on him before. Fear, yes — everyone was afraid, fear was the weather of the temple this morning — but something else beneath it. A kind of shame that had been waiting years for company.

"They've come for the proclamation," Anselm said.

"What proclamation?"

The old brother turned back to his page. His pen hovered and did not descend. "You'll hear it soon enough. We all will." He hesitated. "Casper. When you hear it — don't be brave. Bravery is how they find you."

*How they find you.* As if it were a thing that could be found in a man. As if Anselm knew something about Casper that Casper himself did not yet know he carried.

Casper took his seat at his desk, uncapped the ink, and stared at the half-finished psalm from yesterday without seeing a word of it. Outside, the riders' horses clattered into the outer court, and the great bell fell silent, and in that silence he could hear everything: boots on frozen gravel, the creak of saddle leather, the murmur of brothers gathering in the cloister, and — faintly, from the foundlings' dormitory where she should have been asleep — Ada's footsteps, quick and light, moving toward the stair.

* * *

They assembled in the temple's great hall because there was nowhere else large enough. The hall had been carved directly into the mountainside by hands older than any record, and its pillars were living stone, veined with quartz that caught candlelight and held it like trapped breath. The brothers filed in by seniority, then the lay servants, then the foundlings — a line of children in grey wool, Ada among them, her dark hair braided wrong because she could never see the back of her own head and would not ask anyone's help. She caught Casper's eye across the crowd and touched two fingers to her chest, their old signal: *I am here. I am whole.*

He wished, absurdly, that he could answer honestly.

The riders stood at the hall's head in the king's colors, snow still melting off their cloaks, and their captain was a woman — tall, grey-eyed, with a scar that split her chin and jaw in a thin white seam. Later he would learn her name was Serah Vail, and that name would come to mean more things than he could bear; but that morning she was simply the crown's voice, and the crown's voice did not care for ceremony.

"By decree of King Aldermere the Fourth," she read, unrolling a document heavy with seals, "and by counsel of his court, let it be known throughout his realm and all holdings thereof—" her eyes lifted, scanning the assembly with the flat thoroughness of a woman counting sheep "—that the gift called soul-song, being an old and dangerous inheritance, is hereby claimed for the crown. All persons bearing this gift, of whatever age or station, shall surrender themselves for sanctification within forty days of this proclamation. Those who comply shall be received with honor. Those who conceal themselves," and here her voice did not change at all, which somehow made it worse, "shall be hunted as thieves of the royal patrimony. Sanctuary is denied them. Every temple, every household, every road is bound to give account."

She lowered the parchment. "The abbot will sign."

Abbot Colm came forward, and Casper watched him the way a man watches a structure begin to fail — looking for the first crack. The abbot was sixty, broad, famous for the steadiness of his hands; he had once set a broken finger on a screaming novice so calmly that the novice stopped screaming out of sheer confusion. Now he took the proffered quill and his hand shook so badly that the first stroke of his name came out as a stutter of blotted ink, and he had to begin again, and Serah Vail watched him do it with an expression that was not quite a smile.

*He isn't afraid of her,* Casper realized, and the understanding arrived cold and complete, like ice water finding a seam in a wall. *Or rather — he is, but not most. He is afraid of what the signing means. Compliance. Inspection. The crown's men walking through his temple with open eyes.* Because a temple that had spent generations selling souls down the mountain could not afford scrutiny, and so it would comply — eagerly, thoroughly, gratefully — because eager compliance was the best camouflage rot ever found. Who would hunt a gift through a house that hunted it hardest? The temple would turn itself inside out searching its own corridors, and in doing so would bury its own crimes under the noise of its own virtue.

The abbot finished his name. His hand kept shaking after the quill left it.

Serah Vail rolled the parchment, handed it to a lieutenant, and addressed the hall. "Your temple has a history of cooperation with the court. My lord the Pale Cantor's stewards speak well of Thornfell. I expect that history to continue." Her gaze crossed the room again, unhurried. "You know your children better than I do. Look at them. Listen to them. If a child hums when others are silent, if a child's grief carries farther than it should, if birds land near a child's window in winter — bring that child to me. There is no shame in it. The gift is a debt to the crown, nothing more. And we always collect our debts."

*We always collect our debts.* The words went through Casper like a blade finding the gap between ribs, because he had lived his entire adult life inside another man's debt, measuring winters against coin, and now the crown had just told him — casually, publicly — that debts were the only language it understood.

Around him, brothers nodded. That was the part he had not prepared for. They nodded. Brother Marcus, who taught the foundlings their letters and wept when the flu took two of them in Casper's first year — nodded. Sister Ede, who had held Ada through the fevered night three winters past when the child shook and drew ladders and locked doors on every scrap of paper she could reach — nodded, her face composed into the expression of a woman doing her duty, and Casper understood that she believed it, that compliance had already put on the costume of virtue inside her, that she would walk Ada to the riders herself and call it sanctification and sleep afterward.

Ada did not nod. Ada was watching the captain with her whole body gone still, the stillness of prey that has decided running is death and calculation is life, and her hand had drifted, unconsciously, to rest against her own chest — over the hum, over the thing she thought no one knew she carried.

Casper began, very quietly and very uselessly, to pray.

* * *

The hall emptied slowly. Brothers lingered in knots, speaking in the low urgent voices of people deciding what to report and what to bury, and the foundlings were shepherded out by novices whose faces were carefully blank, and the riders remained at the head of the room like a stone in a stream that the whole current must break around.

Casper stayed. It was not courage; he told himself that honestly, later, when honesty was a luxury he could afford. It was the scribe in him. Where documents gathered, information gathered, and information was the only currency he had ever been able to earn honestly.

"Brother." Serah Vail had noticed him. Of course she had; he was the only man in the hall who had neither fled nor flocked. "You're the archivist's man. Thackeray."

"You know my name, Captain?"

"I know every indenture registered above the snowline. Your father owed the temple four hundred crowns at his death. You're paying it in ink." She said it without contempt, which was almost worse — the way a merchant reads a price tag. "Good. Indentured men are obedient men. Tell me, does the archive hold records of aptitude? Old ones? The Cantor's stewards kept assessments of the gifted in certain houses. If Thornfell is one of them, I'll want them copied for the court."

And there it was. Laid out between them on the flagstones like a dropped knife: the second ledger, the names without prices, the margins marked *high*, and the crown asking him — *him*, the man who had found it, who had lain awake six nights with it — to fetch it into the light.

"The archive holds many things," Casper said. His voice was steady. Eleven years of practice, holding his voice steady while calculating how many winters remained. "I'd need the abbot's warrant to release anything."

"Then get the abbot's warrant." She turned away, dismissing him, and then paused. "Thackeray. One more thing. In my experience, archives keep two ledgers everywhere — one for what a house wants known, one for what it needs forgotten. If you find the second one—" she looked at him fully for the first time, and her grey eyes were tired, tired the way a blade is tired, honed to exhaustion "—bring it to me before anyone knows you have it. Not out of loyalty. Out of sense. Men who carry secrets in houses like this one have short lives unless the secrets belong to someone powerful."

He bowed, because bowing cost nothing, and walked out of the hall with his skin crawling, because she had just described his exact situation — the secret, the danger, the choice of masters — with the accuracy of a woman reading his palm, and because somewhere behind her tired eyes she had meant it as kindness.

That frightened him more than the threat would have.

* * *

He found the abbot in the chapel of the Nine Voices, kneeling before the altar where no god had answered in living memory. The candles were lit, though it was long past the hour for them, and Abbot Colm's shadow lay across the floor like something spilled.

"You signed quickly," Casper said.

The abbot did not turn. "I signed what had to be signed."

"There are children in this house."

"There have always been children in this house." Colm rose, slowly, knees cracking, and when he faced Casper his face had aged ten years since morning. "And some of those children have gone below the mountain, Thackeray, and some of them have thrived there, and none of them starved, and none of them froze on a smuggler's road in January. Do you imagine the world outside these walls would have been kinder to them? Foundlings. Mutes. Bastards of debtors." His mouth twisted. "The court pays. The court feeds them. Whatever the Cantor does with—"

He stopped. The sentence hung unfinished between them, and in the unfinished part of it lived everything: the ledger, the margins, the boy taken when Casper was nineteen, the humming he had felt in his own chest since childhood and had spent thirty years refusing to hear.

"Do with," Casper said softly. "Say it, Father. Say the word. For once in this house, say the true word."

The abbot's hand came up — Casper thought, for one strange suspended moment, that the man was going to strike him — but instead it pressed against his own mouth, briefly, like a man staunching a wound. "Get out of my chapel."

Casper went. But at the door he paused, because the candles guttered all at once in a draft that no open door explained, and the flame-light shivered along the quartz veins in the pillars, and for half a heartbeat the whole chapel seemed to hold a note — low, subterranean, patient — as if the mountain itself had leaned in to listen to what nearly got said.

His own chest hummed in answer. It always did, near the deep stone. He had told himself all his life it was the wind in the passages.

There was no wind today.

* * *

The temple changed overnight, the way a house changes when illness enters it. Brothers stopped speaking in corridors. Doors acquired the habit of closing softly. In the refectory at supper, conversation happened in the safe registers — harvest, weather, the price of tallow — while underneath ran the true subject, unspoken and enormous: *who among us is one of them, and who will be first to say a name aloud.*

Casper ate mechanically and watched the room do arithmetic. He saw Brother Marcus glance twice at the foundlings' table. He saw Sister Ede not look at Ada at all, which was its own kind of telling. And he saw Prior Gerren — the abbot's right hand, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and ambitions folded neatly as letters — rise from the high table and go out into the falling dark, and return an hour later with snow in his hair and a look of settled purpose, and Casper knew, with the certainty of a man who has spent his life reading other men's accounts, that Gerren had gone to make a list.

The temple would comply eagerly. Compliance hid its own crimes. And the first name on Gerren's list, if Gerren was even half as clever as he dressed, would not be a child's.

It would be the archivist's man's. The one who knew what slept in the archive. The one whose father's debt made him deniable, expendable, a name that could be written into any column without troubling the sums.

Casper pushed his bowl away and sat in the noise of safe conversations and understood, finally and completely, that his safe life had ended the night he opened the second ledger. Everything since had been the sound of the trap closing — slow, well-oiled, courteous. Forty days, the crown had given the gifted to come forward. But the temple would not wait forty days. The temple needed to fill the crown's quota faster than any rival house, needed its zeal witnessed and stamped, and zeal has never once in history paused to check whether its chosen sacrifice deserved the name.

He climbed to his cell and barred the door, though a bar would stop nothing that was coming, and sat on his cot with his head in his hands.

Eleven winters. He had measured his whole life in winters owed, and he had four remaining — he had counted, God help him, he had counted them like a miser counts coins — and now he discovered that the counting had been the trap all along. A man watching a ledger does not watch the door.

A soft scratch at the wall. Two scratches, a pause, two more. Then, from the gap beneath his door, a piece of paper slid into his cell, folded tight and small.

He picked it up. Unfolded it. Ada's drawing, done in stolen charcoal, quick and sure as always: the great hall, the riders, the captain with the seamed face — she had caught the scar perfectly in three strokes — and above the captain's head, floating like a thought made visible, a bird in a cage drawn with a bar across its song. And below, in the corner, in the space where Ada always put the truth too dangerous for pictures:

a ladder, leaning against a wall. A door, drawn open. And two small figures — one tall, one small — already climbing through.

She had scratched at his wall to tell him she was ready.

She was waiting for his answer.

* * *

Casper sat a long time with the paper in his hands. Below, muffled through floors of stone, the temple sang compline — the full choir, all the voices of the house rising together into the vaulted dark — and he listened to it the way a condemned man listens to weather, knowing he would never hear it innocently again. Somewhere in that choir were voices the crown wanted. Somewhere below the mountain hung lanterns that had once been children from this house. And somewhere down the corridor Prior Gerren was writing his list, and the ink was drying, and the forty days were already burning down like a candle in a draught.

Casper folded Ada's drawing along its crease and slid it inside his coat, over his heart, where a man keeps the things he cannot afford to lose and cannot afford to be found carrying.

Then he snuffed his candle, waited for the midnight bell, and went down to the salt cellars to find her.
