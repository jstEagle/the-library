## 4. The Smuggler's Road

The proclamation went up at dawn, nailed to the temple doors with nails that were too long for the wood, as though whoever had hammered it there wanted it to hurt the building. Casper read it twice while the ink was still tacky enough to smell — iron-gall and something else beneath it, something that reminded him of the archive's deepest shelf, of the second ledger and its columns of small, patient horrors.

*By order of the Crown: all bearers of the old gift shall present themselves for sanctification within thirty days.*

Sanctification. He turned the word over the way he turned over unfamiliar words in old manuscripts, looking for the root, the true meaning underneath the polish. He found it quickly enough. The second ledger had taught him the temple's vocabulary. Sanctification was what happened to children who were measured and found apt. It was what happened to the ones who went down the mountain and did not come back.

By midmorning the temple had rearranged itself around the decree the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. Brothers hurried through the cloisters with their eyes down. The abbot's door stayed shut. And somewhere in the lower reaches of the building, Casper knew, the measuring would begin again — the tuning forks and the silk threads and the bowls of water that trembled, they said, near a gifted throat.

He should have felt nothing. He was not a child. He was twenty-six years old, a scribe with callused fingers and a debt of eleven winters still standing between him and freedom, and no one measured scribes. His gift, if it was a gift, had never been more than a hum in his chest, a sense that certain sounds were *true* the way certain stones were true, a trick he'd used to quiet a restless horse or lull a feverish brother to sleep. Nothing the crown would want. Nothing worth sanctifying.

But Ada was a child. And Ada had been measured once already — he'd seen the entry himself, in the second ledger, in the abbot's own cramped hand: *Foundling. Female, age approx. six. Aptitude: extreme. Held for the spring delivery.*

Held. Like a parcel. Like something already owned.

Casper stood in the scriptorium with a half-copied psalm drying under his hand and realized he had stopped breathing. He made himself start again, slowly, the way he'd been taught to steady a shaking nib. Then he put down his pen, capped the ink, and left the psalm unfinished — the first work he had ever abandoned in nine years of indenture — and went down toward the salt cellars.

---

The cellars were the coldest part of the temple, cut into the mountain's root where the stone stayed dark and dry, and they smelled of rock salt and old tallow and the particular mineral silence of places the sun had never touched. The brothers stored the temple's winter salt there in great clay jars, and the salt was precious — Thornfell salt kept meat for months and fetched a good price in the lowlands — so the cellars were supposed to be locked.

The lock hung open.

Casper stood in the doorway and listened. Below, in the dark, something shifted. Not a rat. Rats moved in small quick scratches. This was a slower sound, the sound of a body trying very hard to be nothing.

He should have called for a brother. That was the correct action, the safe action, the action of a man who intended to survive his eleven remaining winters with his head down and his ledger balanced. He stood there in the doorway and weighed it, and found — with a kind of sick, vertiginous clarity — that he had already weighed it, days ago, weeks ago, maybe years, in the part of himself that had copied that second ledger and could not uncopy it.

He went down the stairs.

"Ada," he said quietly. "It's Casper. From the scriptorium. I bring you ink sometimes. The good black, for your drawings."

The shifting stopped. He counted to thirty. Then, from behind the row of salt jars at the cellar's far end, a small face emerged — thin, sharp-chinned, with the enormous grey eyes of a creature that had learned to watch everything because it could not ask about anything. Ada did not speak. Ada had never spoken, in all her four years at the temple; the brothers called her simple, or cursed, or sometimes, in the older brothers' private shorthand, *already hollowed* — though Casper had looked in the ledger and knew the truth was worse. She hadn't been hollowed yet. She was *scheduled*.

She was clutching a bundle to her chest: a crust of bread gone hard, a stub of charcoal, and the rag of cloth she drew on when no one would give her paper. Her feet were bare and blue with cold.

"They're going to measure you," Casper said. There was no point pretending otherwise. She'd seen the other children taken; she'd watched the spring delivery leave with her own eyes, standing at the kitchen window with her charcoal still in her fist. "You know what today is. You know why the bell rang twice."

Ada's chin lifted. It was not a child's defiance, or not only that. It was the look of someone who had made a decision days ago and had simply been waiting for the world to catch up. She pointed one small finger at him. Then at the cellar door. Then down — the gesture every soul in the temple used for *below*, for the mountain's depths, for the direction the taken children went.

"No," Casper said. "Not below. Never below."

She pointed at the door again, harder, and her eyes said what her hands could not: *then get me out of it.*

And here was the moment Casper would think about for the rest of his life — though he did not know it yet, standing in the salt-smelling dark with a psalm unfinished upstairs and his father's debt still coiled around his name like a snake asleep. Here was the fork in the road, and both paths led somewhere he had never been.

He could walk back up the stairs. He could report a foundling hiding in the cellars, and the brothers would fetch her, and she would be measured, and in the spring she would go below, and Casper would go on copying psalms for eleven more winters until his father's debt burned away and he walked out of Thornfell a free man with clean hands and a hole in him shaped exactly like a small grey-eyed girl.

Or.

Casper closed his eyes. He saw the map — not the official one in the archive, the pilgrim's map with its honest roads and its honest gates, but the other one, the one that lived behind his eyes, assembled over years from the fragments the smugglers paid him to record and the routes he'd walked himself on errands no one questioned. The salt road, west from the cellars' loading door, through the rock-cut passage the temple pretended it didn't have because the temple had built it, generations ago, for purposes the temple no longer admitted to. The goat track above the falls. The way down through the Thornfell passes that the crown's riders would not find, because the crown's maps were the temple's maps, and the temple's maps were lies.

He had spent years making himself useful to smugglers so that he could survive quietly. He had never once imagined what else that knowledge might be *for*.

"Right," Casper said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the only kind of steadiness he'd ever had. "Right. Come on, then. Quickly, and don't make a sound, and if I say run, you run."

Ada was already moving.

---

The loading door opened onto the mountain's grey shoulder, and the morning was the kind of thin, bright cold that made every sound carry. Somewhere above them, the temple bell began to ring — not the twice-rung alarm of the night before but the deep, measured tolling that called the brothers to the abbot's morning address, and Casper knew what the address would be. *Compliance. Cooperation. The crown's riders are our guests. Any foundling or brother who conceals himself conceals a sin.* The temple would comply eagerly. The temple had been complying for generations.

The salt road ran west along the mountainside, a shelf of packed earth and old stone no wider than a cart, with the drop falling away on their left in a long grey tumble of scree and pine. Casper kept Ada ahead of him, one hand on her shoulder, both of them bent low where the road passed below the temple's upper windows. The riders — he'd counted six horses in the yard at dawn — would be in the chapel listening to the abbot. They would have an hour, maybe two, before someone thought to check the cellars, before someone remembered the foundling who hadn't appeared at morning prayer, before the search began in earnest.

Two hours. The salt passage would take them twenty minutes. The goat track above the falls another forty, if the ice hadn't taken it. After that, the forest, and after the forest—

After that, he didn't know. That was the exhilarating and terrifying truth of it. In nine years at Thornfell he had mapped every road out of the mountains for other people's use. He had never once walked one himself.

The rock-cut passage was exactly as the old survey had described it and exactly as the temple wanted to forget: a corridor of dressed stone behind a door that looked like cliff face, running half a mile through the mountain's shoulder and emerging in a fold of the hillside a full mile west of the gates. Casper's fingers found the counterweight mechanism in the dark — a wheel, a chain, a groove worn smooth by hands a century dead — and the door ground open on air that smelled of deep stone and, faintly, of salt.

Ada's hand found his in the blackness. Small, cold, certain. She did not pull away, and neither did he.

They came out into daylight in a fold of the mountain where a stream had cut a green slot through the rock, and Casper allowed himself one breath of something that was almost laughter. They were out. They were actually out. The temple was a grey smudge behind and above them, and the road ahead wound down through pine shadow toward the lowlands, toward Marrowgate, toward — he didn't know. Toward.

Then the dog began to bark.

It came from above and behind — the temple's mastiff, the big brindled brute they kept at the gate, and it had their scent on the wind. Casper's stomach dropped. A dog meant handlers, and handlers meant the search had begun early, and the salt road was a shelf in open sight of anyone standing on the temple's wall. He grabbed Ada's hand and ran, the loose stones rolling under their feet, the barking swelling behind them, and then — rounding a shoulder of rock — he saw the second problem, the one he should have foreseen.

One of the riders had not gone to the address. A young brother-handler in temple grey stood at the bend of the salt road ahead, the mastiff straining at his rope, and the man turned at the sound of their feet with his mouth already opening to shout.

Casper did the arithmetic of desperation: forty feet of open road between them, Ada's bare feet, his own bad knee, no cover, no second route. The handler's chest swelled with the shout. The mastiff's bark climbed to a frenzy.

And the hum in Casper's chest — the hum he had carried all his life like a stone in a shoe, the thing he had never named, never fed, never *used* — rose up on its own.

He did not decide to do it. That was the part he would struggle to explain later, to Wren, to anyone. It was less a decision than a sneeze, less a choice than a reflex: the handler's shout gathering in his chest, and something in Casper reaching toward that gathering sound the way a hand reaches to steady a falling cup. He breathed in, and he let out a note — barely a note, more a whispered *ah* pitched low, aimed, the way you'd aim a thrown stone — and he *bent* the sound in the air between them.

The shout never landed. It left the handler's mouth and came apart, unraveled into nothing, a shout heard by no one. And the mastiff — the mastiff simply stopped. Sat down. Its ears, pricked and furious a heartbeat before, folded soft, and it yawned enormously, and lay its brindled head on its paws as though the whole morning had been too much excitement and it intended now to sleep for a week.

The handler stared at his dog. Then at Casper. His mouth was still open, but nothing came out of it — not because Casper held it shut, but because the man had seen what he'd seen, and what he'd seen had emptied him of words.

Casper grabbed Ada's hand and ran.

---

They ran the goat track above the falls with the water roaring loud enough to drown any pursuit, and the spray soaked them both to the skin, and Ada — Casper glanced back once and would remember this forever — Ada was *laughing*. Not soundlessly, for once, or rather soundlessly still, but laughing with her whole body, her mouth wide open, her grey eyes streaming with spray and something else, her bare feet finding the wet stone with the sureness of a creature that had been born to this mountain and had never once been allowed to climb it.

The exhilaration carried him the first mile. It carried him through the pine forest and down the switchbacks and along the old charcoal-burners' path that the crown's maps showed as a river. His blood sang. His bad knee didn't hurt. For one bright, impossible hour he was not a debtor, not a scribe, not a man whose life had been measured out in winters owed — he was a fox with the hunt's horn fading behind him, quick and free and *alive*.

Then, at the forest's edge, where the trees opened onto the first long view of the lowlands — brown fields, a grey thread of river, the smoke of Marrowgate far off — Casper stopped, and put his hands on his knees, and the exhilaration drained out of him like water through scree, and what came in behind it was cold.

He had shouted without a voice. He had stopped a dog's heart with a note. He had done, with no training and no warning, in a moment of panic, the very thing the crown's proclamation described — the old gift, the soul-song, the sound that the temple measured in children with tuning forks and trembling bowls of water. He was not a man who had rescued a child from the temple's crimes. He was a man who had *confirmed himself*, in front of a witness, as exactly what the hunt was for.

The handler had seen. The handler would tell the abbot, and the abbot would tell the riders, and by nightfall there would be a description on the road behind them: a scribe, tall, brown-haired, bad left knee, traveling with a mute foundling girl. And the foundling girl, Casper thought with a fresh wave of cold, was *already in the ledger*. Already owned. Her recovery would be worth a purse to someone.

Ada tugged his sleeve. He looked down. She held up her rag of cloth, and on it, in charcoal, in the swift sure strokes that the brothers called idle scribbling and Casper now understood had never been idle at all, she had drawn three figures on a road: one tall, one small, and behind them, small and sharp, a dog, asleep.

Below the figures she had drawn a fourth shape — a shape that made the cold in Casper's chest turn over. A woman, standing in the road ahead of them, with a broken thing at her throat that might have been a larynx or might have been a lyre, and an expression the charcoal somehow made both weary and knowing.

Ada pointed down the road, toward Marrowgate, toward the smoke. Then at the drawing. Then at him, with those enormous grey eyes, and Casper understood that the child had known — had *known*, somehow, the way she knew things — that the road ahead had someone on it they needed. Someone who could teach him what he had just, in one terrified whispered note, revealed himself to be.

The wind came down off the Thornfell passes behind them, carrying the faint, far sound of the temple bell, still ringing, still ringing, and Casper took Ada's hand and walked down out of the mountains into the lowlands, toward a woman he had never met, in a town he'd only ever seen on a smuggler's map.
