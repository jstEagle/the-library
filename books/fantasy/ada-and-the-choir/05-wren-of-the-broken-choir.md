## 5. Wren of the Broken Choir

The Drowned Bell was the kind of tavern that kept its secrets in the cellar, along with the sour wine and the rats. Wren had been keeping company with all three for the better part of a season, and she could tell you honestly that the rats were the best listeners.

She sat in her usual corner — the one by the hearth where the smoke gathered and stung the eyes, so that no one looked too closely at her face — and watched the door the way she always did. Not hoping. Hoping was a coin she'd stopped carrying years ago. But watching. A woman who had once stood before the choir of the Chapel Royal with two hundred voices under her hands did not simply stop watching doors. Somewhere in her ruined chest, that habit still beat like a second heart.

The rain came in sideways whenever the door swung open, and with it the smell of Marrowgate: fish rot and tallow smoke and the river's grey breath. The town huddled at the foot of the north road like something the mountains had coughed up and couldn't be bothered to swallow. Everyone here was going somewhere else, eventually, or had given up on going anywhere at all. Wren belonged firmly to the second sort. She'd made her peace with it, or something shaped enough like peace to fool a drunk.

Three cups in, the world had gone soft at the edges, which was the only way she liked it. Then the door opened again, and the softness sharpened all at once.

Two figures came in out of the rain. A man, tall and hollow-cheeked, with the careful stoop of someone who had spent years making himself smaller than his debts. And a child — a girl of perhaps eight winters, wrapped in a man's coat, with dark eyes that took in the whole room in one slow sweep, cataloguing exits the way Wren herself might have done.

Fugitives, then. Wren knew the breed. She'd been one, once, before she'd stopped running and started drinking instead, which was slower but arrived at the same place.

She would have gone back to her cup and forgotten them by morning — truly, she would have — except that the man held his hands over the hearth fire, shivering, and without seeming to notice what he was doing, he began to hum.

Wren's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

It was nothing. That was the terrible thing about it. It was *nothing* — three notes, low and wandering, the kind of sound a man makes to fill the silence in his own skull. Any fool could hum. But Wren had spent thirty years listening to voices the way a jeweler listens to stones, and she heard what was underneath: a resonance that didn't stop at the hum. It went *through* the fire, through the hearthstone, through the floorboards under her feet, and the flames leaned toward him. Just slightly. Just the way flowers lean at the sun. No one else in the room noticed. The fire was ordinary fire doing ordinary things, except that it burned a little brighter where his hands were, and the cold that had lived in Wren's bones since the burning of the chapel Royal pulled itself upright and paid attention.

*Gifted.* Here. In the Drowned Bell, in drunk old Marrowgate, walking straight into the lion's mouth with a stolen child on his arm.

She set down her cup. Her hand wasn't steady, but it hadn't been steady in years, and she'd learned to work with what remained.

* * *

He ordered bread and broth, counting coins with the anxious precision of a man who knew exactly how many he had. The girl ate like a small bird, quick and watchful, and when a drover at the next table laughed too loudly she flinched and moved closer to the man's side. He put his arm around her without looking, the way you shield something you've already decided to die for.

Wren waited until the drovers' talk had swelled again, then rose, crossed the room, and sat down across from them as if she'd been invited.

"Bad night to be traveling north," she said.

The man's eyes came up, grey and guarded. "We're not traveling."

"Mm." She nodded at his boots. Mud from the high road, not the river flats. "And I'm the Duchess of Candlemere. You're heading for Thornfell, or you're a dead man walking, and either way you're sitting in the one tavern between here and the ford where the crown's riders drink on Thursdays."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the quick arithmetic of a man deciding whether to run. She watched him weigh it and discard it, because running would mean leaving the girl, and leaving the girl was not on any table he owned.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Wren." She let the name sit there. Most people in Marrowgate knew it, or a version of it — *the mad choir woman*, *the drunk who sings at gulls*. "Once of the Chapel Royal. Before it burned."

His expression didn't change, but the girl went very still, and her eyes — those dark, old eyes — fixed on Wren with an intensity that made the skin of her arms prickle. As if the name meant something to her. As if she'd been waiting for it.

"You don't look like a choir mistress," the man said carefully.

"No," Wren agreed. "I look like what's left when they take everything else." She leaned forward, lowered her voice until it was barely more than breath, and said the words she hadn't said aloud in six years: "You're humming soul-song into a public hearth, scribe. Do you know what the court beneath pays for a voice like yours? Do you know what the temple does to people who carry it?"

The color left his face in stages, like a tide going out. He glanced at the girl; she was watching Wren with her head tilted, birdlike, unblinking.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Of course you don't. They never do, at first. That's how they catch you — the gift comes up through your chest like water through chalk, and nobody ever taught you the walls of it." She tapped her own sternum. "I had the finest training in the kingdom. Two hundred voices under my baton, and every one of them gifted, and every one of them taught to hold their song behind their teeth where it belongs. And now?" She spread her hands, showing him the tremor she couldn't hide. "Now I sing at gulls."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because in about four days, the hunters will reach Marrowgate. Because there's a bounty on gifted souls that would keep me in wine until my liver gives out, and I'm telling you anyway, which should tell you something about how far I've fallen." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile. "And because you're going to get yourself caught, and worse, you're going to get *her* caught, stumbling north with a gift you can't control and a child who—" She stopped. Looked at the girl properly. Looked, and listened, the way she'd been trained to listen since she was younger than this child was now.

There was no song in the girl. That was the first thing. Every living person carried some small music in them, even the deaf, even the tone-deaf — a flicker, a candleflame of resonance. This child had a *silence*, and the silence was not empty. It was shaped, deliberate, like a rest written into a score by a composer who understood that the pause could be louder than the note.

Wren had heard of such things. Old stories, from before the chapel burned. Songs sung without sound, gifts that cost nothing because they were never *spent* — only offered. She'd thought them fables, the way priests think miracles are fables right up until one happens in front of them.

The girl met her stare and did not look away. Then, slowly, deliberately, she raised one small hand and touched her own throat, and shook her head.

*Mute,* Wren thought. *Or pretending to be. Or—* and the thought arrived whole, fully formed, terrifying — *or she speaks in a language you haven't learned yet.*

"She can't talk," the man said quietly. "Her name is Ada. I'm Casper."

"Casper," Wren repeated, tasting it. "Well, Casper. Here is what I know. I know the smuggler roads end at Greywater, and the toll-keeper there doesn't take coin. I know the gates below Thornfell seal at midwinter, and after that, whatever you're carrying that child toward will have to wait until spring — if spring finds you alive. And I know that in four days, Serah Vail rides into this town with a warrant and a memory of every gifted face she's ever seen." She watched him absorb that last one. "You need passage, and I need passage, and unlike you, I happen to know the roads."

"What do you want for it?"

"Teaching." The word came out of her rougher than she intended. She covered it with a cough. "You'll want control before Greywater, or the mountain itself will hear you coming. Every note you spend costs you something — warmth, memory, years, depending on how careless you are. I can teach you to pay less. I can teach you to choose what you pay. Without me, you'll learn the same lessons anyway, but you'll learn them the way I did: by losing things you can't get back." She looked at her own hands again, at the fine tremor that six years of wine had woven into them. "I've paid enough for both of us. Let me spend the rest of it teaching."

Casper was silent for a long moment. The fire cracked. Outside, the rain kept up its grey arithmetic against the windows.

"Why?" he asked finally. "Why help us? You said yourself there's a bounty."

And there it was — the question she'd been dreading, the one with the honest answer buried under six years of sediment. Wren turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the wine cling to the sides.

"Because I had a choir once," she said. "Forty children, all gifted, all mine. The crown sent word that they were wanted below the mountain. Sanctification, they called it. I said no." She laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know what happens to a choir mistress who says no to the crown? Nothing, at first. That's the elegant part. They wait. They let you believe you've won. And then one night the chapel burns, and forty beds are empty by morning, and the ones who ran are hunted through three counties, and the ones who didn't run—" Her voice caught. She let it. Some wounds deserved fresh air. "One of mine survived. Sold anyway. A boy called Tam. He'd have been fourteen now." She looked up, and found the girl — Ada — watching her with an expression far too old for her face, and something in that look undid the last of Wren's caution entirely. "So no, scribe. I'm not helping you. I'm finishing a job I failed at. There's a difference, and it matters to no one but me, but it matters."

Ada reached across the table then — quick as a wren herself, quick as her namesake — and touched the back of Wren's hand. Just touched it. Just laid one small finger against the knuckle, the way you'd test whether a bird was still alive.

Wren looked at that finger. Then at the child. Then at the man who had gone quiet in the way men go quiet when they realize the woman across the table has more to give them than they'd guessed and more to lose than they'd feared.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out steadier than it had any right to. "That's settled, then."

* * *

They took a room above the tavern because the roads were flooded and even fools don't cross the Greywater ford in a night-rain. Wren paid with the last of her honest coin and told herself the sacrifice was practical, not sentimental.

The room was narrow and smelled of lamp oil and old smoke. Casper slept sitting up against the wall with Ada curled against his side, and Wren lay on the pallet by the window, listening to the rain and to the breathing of two strangers she had somehow, in the space of an evening, decided to keep.

She must have drifted, because when she opened her eyes the candle had burned to a stub and the girl was awake, sitting cross-legged by the window, watching the storm. Watching Wren, really — the window was just courtesy.

"You should sleep," Wren murmured.

Ada didn't answer, of course. Instead she lifted one hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart. Then she pointed at Wren. Then she pressed her hand to her chest again, harder, and her face did something complicated — grief and fury and a longing so naked it hurt to witness — before smoothing itself back into the careful blankness of a child who has learned that her face is safer empty.

*I had something there,* the gesture said. *It was taken.*

Wren sat up slowly. Six years of wine sat up with her, and the cold that lived in her bones, and the memory of forty empty beds.

"I know," she said softly. "I know exactly what that feels like."

Ada studied her for a long moment. Then, with the gravity of a queen conferring a honor, she reached out and took Wren's trembling hand in both of hers, and held it still. Held it until the shaking gentled. Held it the way you hold a wounded bird — not to keep it, but to remind it that wings exist.

Wren did not cry. She was proud of that, later. She had used up all her crying in front of a burned chapel, and what remained was something harder and more useful.

When dawn came, grey and rinsed, she woke Casper with a boot to the sole of his boot — gently, but a boot nonetheless.

"Up," she said. "Lessons start on the road. First rule of soul-song: never practice indoors. Walls remember."

He blinked at her, hair wild, the girl already stirring. "Walls remember?"

"Everything resonates, scribe. Stone most of all. Sing carelessly in a walled room and the stone keeps the shape of your note long after you've gone silent — and there are ears in this kingdom trained to read stone the way priests read scripture." She slung her pack over her shoulder, wincing at the weight of it, at the weight of everything. "Second rule: we leave before the tavern wakes. Third rule—" She paused at the door, looking back at the two of them in the grey light: the fugitive and the foundling, the debt-ridden scribe and the sold child, her strange new congregation of two.

"Third rule?"

Wren smiled, and for the first time in six years it almost reached her eyes.

"Nobody gets left behind. Not again. Now come on — Greywater's five days hard walking, midwinter's closer than that, and I promised you control." She pushed open the door onto the wet bright morning, onto the road north, onto whatever was coming for them. "Consider this your first lesson in spending wisely: everything costs. Starting with time."

Behind them, the Drowned Bell's sign creaked in the wind. Ahead, the mountains waited, patient as ledgers.

And somewhere on the road behind them — though none of them knew it yet — a rider in crown black had already begun asking questions in Marrowgate, and the first of them was: *has anyone seen a woman who used to sing?*
