## 6. Lessons in Borrowed Voices

Wren had taught a hundred voices to sing, and she had never once been afraid of one until she heard Casper hum.

It happened on the third morning out of Marrowgate, in the grey hour before the sun cleared the eastern ridges. He crouched at the edge of their cold camp, cupping his hands around a fire that refused to catch — wet wood, wet kindling, the whole world damp from two days of drizzle — and he made a sound without seeming to know he made it. A low note, barely more than breath, pitched somewhere below speech. The fire leaned toward him like a dog hearing its name.

The kindling caught.

Wren stood very still with her back against an alder trunk and watched the flames climb, and felt something old and cold turn over in her chest. She knew that sound. She had spent eleven years training choristers for the Chapel Royal, and in all that time she had heard true soul-song exactly twice: once from a girl named Merrin Hale, whose voice could make grown men weep or sleep or confess, and once from herself, on the worst night of her life, when grief tore something open in her that had never properly closed again.

Casper Thackeray didn't even know what he was doing. That was the frightening part. He was warming his hands like a man whistling at his work, and he was spending himself without counting the cost.

"Stop," she said.

He looked up, startled. Ada, curled in her blanket beside the dead coals of last night's fire, opened her eyes.

"You're bleeding," Wren said, crossing the camp. "Not with your body. With everything else." She knelt and took his hands — they were warm now, warmer than they should have been, warm as fresh bread — and turned them over as though she might see the wound there. "What did you just remember? Tell me. Quickly, before it fades."

"I don't—" His brow furrowed. "My mother's kitchen. There was a song she used to—"

He stopped. Wren watched him reach for it and find the edges gone soft, like bread left out in rain. She watched the exact moment he understood.

"It was longer than that," he said quietly. "The memory. It had a whole middle to it. Flour on the table, and her humming, and my sister—" He blinked rapidly. "There was a sister in it. I can't see her face anymore."

* * *

They walked while she talked, because Wren had learned long ago that lessons went down easier with motion, the way medicine went down easier with honey. The road north from Marrowgate climbed through sheep country, stone walls running beside them like the ribs of some buried giant, and the mist sat low in the valleys so that the three of them walked through cloud.

"The gift has a hundred names," Wren said. "Soul-song is the temple's word. The court beneath calls it currency. My grandmother called it the second voice, and said everyone has one, but most people's sleeps so deep it never wakes." She kicked a stone off the path. "Whatever you call it, the rule is the same. Every note you give comes out of you. Not your throat — your throat is just the door. What comes through the door is *you*. Memory first. Then warmth — the feeling of things, the color of them. And if you spend past that—" She paused. "If you spend past that, you start giving away pieces of who you are. Names go. Faces go. Eventually the wanting goes, and after that, nothing much matters."

"And you know this how?" Casper asked. There was no cruelty in it. He was a scribe; he took notes even with his eyes.

"Because I sang a chapel full of people to sleep once," Wren said, "and forgot my own teacher's name for a year afterward." She kept walking. "Her name came back eventually. Most small payments do, if you rest and don't spend again. But the big ones—" She thought of Merrin Hale, sold below before her seventeenth birthday, and did not finish the thought.

Ada tugged her sleeve. The girl held up her slate, on which she had chalked, in her careful round hand: *How do you get it back?*

"You mostly don't," Wren said honestly. "You learn not to spend. You learn control." She looked at Casper. "Which is why you're going to do everything I say for the next however-many-days-it-takes-us-to-reach-the-mountain, scribe, because right now you're a man with a purse full of gold walking through a crowd with his bag open."

"I've survived this long."

"You've survived by accident. Accident runs out." She stopped at the crest of the hill, where the road bent and the Thornfell massif showed itself for the first time since Marrowgate — a vast blue-grey shoulder of rock shouldering into the clouds, snow already lying in its high folds like linen in a chest. Somewhere under that stone was a court full of stolen souls, and somewhere in it was a ledger entry with Ada's name. "We have maybe twenty days before midwinter seals the gates. In twenty days I have to teach you enough discipline that you don't hollow yourself out before we ever get there. So. Lesson one."

She planted herself in front of him. "Sing me the note you used on the fire. Exactly that note."

He frowned, gathered himself, and produced it — and Wren flinched, because he did it wrong in the worst possible way. He reached deep, grabbed hold of the memory of his mother's kitchen, and *poured*. She felt the heat of it pass through the air between them like wind off an oven. The frost on the grass around his feet melted in a hand's breadth circle.

"Stop! Stop." She gripped his shoulder. "That was a shilling where a farthing would do. You gave away half a memory to melt frost. Do you understand? The gift doesn't take what you offer. It takes what you *attach*. Every feeling you wrap around the note travels with it. Your mother's kitchen isn't fuel, boy. It's yours. Guard it."

"Then what do I use?"

"Nothing. At first, nothing." She made him sit on a wall in the cold and try to produce the note empty — pure technique, breath and pitch, no cargo. It was like watching a man try to lift a sack that wasn't there; he kept reaching for something to pour in, because pouring was all he knew. Three times he caught himself reaching for memories. Twice he caught himself reaching for the warmth of the fire, the taste of breakfast, the simple animal comfort of being warm.

"That's the hunger," Wren told him. "Every gifted person feels it. The note wants ballast. It feels thin and dead without something inside it, and the feeling of spending is sweet — I won't lie to you. It's the sweetest thing there is. That's how the court buys people. Not with chains. With the pleasure of paying."

By late afternoon he could produce a bare note that moved a leaf, no more. It cost him nothing. He looked absurdly proud of it, and Wren, despite herself, felt the old muscle of teaching flex in her chest — the thing she had sworn never to feel again, because every student she had loved had either been taken or burned.

* * *

It was Ada who ended the argument, four days later, on the banks of the Greywater's upper reaches.

They had been fighting — not loudly; Wren and Casper fought the way tired people fight, in flat voices with long silences in between — about whether he should practice spending at all. Wren said no, never, not unless death left no alternative. Casper said a blade you never draw is a blade you don't know how to hold. And Ada, who had been sitting apart on a fallen log, drawing in the dirt with a stick, got up and walked to the middle of their camp and stood there until they both shut up.

She pointed at Casper. Then at the dead fire pit. Then she raised one finger: *watch*.

Wren watched. They both did.

The girl closed her eyes. Her lips parted slightly — and nothing came out. No breath, no tone, no sound at all. But Wren felt it anyway, the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. Something moved through the camp, silent as light through glass. The charred sticks in the fire pit glowed. Embers that had been grey since morning woke to orange, then to flame, and a kettle of water began, gently, to steam.

And Ada stood there with her eyes shut and her mouth closed, singing a song with no sound in it, and when she opened her eyes and looked at the fire she had lit, her face was exactly as it had been before. Nothing spent. Nothing paid. Nothing taken.

Wren sat down on the ground. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their arrangement with her.

"That's not possible," she said.

Ada tilted her head. *I just did it*, said the tilt.

"No — listen to me, child." Wren's voice came out strange, high, half a laugh and half something else. "Every singer spends. Every one, always, in the whole history of the gift. The teachers of the Chapel Royal had a word for a perfect zero-cost note, and the word was *myth*. It was a story we told novices. Like telling children the moon is made of cheese." She pressed her palms against her own cheeks. "Unless—"

Unless the door worked differently in her. Unless being mute hadn't broken her instrument but rebuilt it, so that the sound never passed through the flesh at all — so that the song went from soul to world directly, with no toll taken at the gate of the throat. Wren thought of the theology texts she'd been made to burn, the heretical commentaries on the mountain's first song, the ones that claimed the original singers had needed no voices at all. She had always assumed that was metaphor.

She looked at Ada — small, silent, sold before she could speak, carrying nothing in the world but a slate and a stick of chalk — and understood that the temple hadn't measured this child for aptitude. They had measured her for *danger*.

"Do it again," Wren whispered. "Slowly. I want to feel where it comes from."

Ada obliged. This time Wren closed her own eyes and listened with the other sense, the one she trusted more than her ears, and what she felt nearly undid her. The girl's song had no cost because it had no *taking* in it. Casper's notes were withdrawals; Ada's was a current, like a stream that flows because it is a stream. She wasn't spending herself. She was letting something pass through her that had been passing through the world since before the temple, before the court, before anyone thought to put a price on a soul.

The fire burned merrily. The kettle steamed. Ada picked up her slate and wrote, in her round, patient letters:

*It doesn't hurt if you don't push.*

Wren laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound — and then, to her own horror, found that her eyes were full. She turned away and busied herself with the kettle, and let the steam hide her face, and thought about a chapel full of children's voices, thirty-two of them, and how she had stood in front of them every morning for eleven years and taught them to spend themselves beautifully, note by note, because that was what the crown required and she had not known — had chosen not to know — what the spending was for.

Merrin Hale had sung like Ada. Wren remembered it now, suddenly, unbearably: the girl's zero-cost notes, which the masters had called a flaw, an instability, *the Hale irregularity*. They had sold her below the mountain within the season.

They hadn't wanted to fix the flaw. They had wanted the flaw for themselves.

"Wren." Casper's voice, gentle. "Are you all right?"

"No," she said, and found she meant it in the strangest way — no, and also more whole than she had been in nine years. She wiped her face with her sleeve and turned around. "No. But I'm going to be. Listen to me, both of you. If the court finds out what she can do — if the Pale Cantor learns there's a singer alive who costs him nothing to use—" She shook her head. "We don't bring Ada to that place. We bring her *near* it, we take back what's hers, and we leave. And if it comes to a choice between my plan and my pride, pride loses. Understood?"

Casper nodded slowly. Ada wrote on her slate: *Understood.* Then, beneath it, smaller: *Teach me more.*

Wren looked at the child — at the impossible, quiet, blazing child — and felt the old choir mistress rise in her like a tide, the one she had thought drowned in wine and ash. There were four days of road behind them and perhaps sixteen ahead, and for the first time since the Chapel Royal burned, Wren Fairweather had something worth teaching and someone worth surviving for.

"All right," she said. "Lesson two."

But that night, when the others slept, she sat awake by the fire Ada had lit without a sound, and in the flames she saw the faces of thirty-two children, and one of them — the one with the irregular voice, the one she had failed — seemed to look back at her from the heart of the blaze. Far to the north, the peaks of Thornfell had come out from under the clouds, white and vast and waiting, and Wren found herself wondering, with a coldness that would not warm, whether the court beneath already knew what was walking toward it.

Two days later, pinned to the door of a waystation shrine, they found the first bounty notice. The sketch was crude, but the face was unmistakably Casper's — and beneath it, in the crown's formal hand, the words: *Also sought: a mute child. Reward doubled.*
