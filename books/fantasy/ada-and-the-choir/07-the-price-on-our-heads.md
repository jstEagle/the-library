## 7. The Price on Our Heads

The notice was nailed to the market post in Marrowgate's lower square, and it was wrong in every particular except the eyes.

Casper saw it from across the square, between the shoulders of a fishwife and a boy selling roasted chestnuts. He had learned, in his years at Thornfell, to read documents at a distance — a scribe's habit, squinting across the scriptorium to check another man's work without leaving his own desk. So he read it before he understood what he was reading, and by the time understanding arrived, cold and complete as well-water in winter, he had already taken in the words: *By order of the Crown. The bearer of an unlawful gift.* And beneath them, rendered in charcoal and bad proportions, a face he knew.

His own face.

The sketch had been made from memory — that much was obvious, almost insultingly so. The nose was too broad, the jaw too heavy, the hair drawn as a shapeless dark cap rather than the fine, unruly thing it actually was. A temple informant had sat with a crown artist and described him, and the description had been filtered through someone who had seen him a hundred times and never once looked. That should have been comforting. It was not. Because the eyes were right. Whoever had described him had remembered the eyes — grey, too pale, watchful — and the artist had given them a stillness that made the whole crude drawing feel like a window. Like something looking back out of the paper at him.

*Fifty crowns for information leading to capture,* said the notice. *One hundred for the gift-bearer alive.*

Alive. Of course. Dead men could not be sanctified.

"Handsome fellow," said the chestnut boy beside him, studying the same paper with the frank appetite of the young for other people's danger. "Shame about the eyes. Makes him look like a corpse."

"He does look ill," Casper agreed. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Probably why they want him alive."

He bought chestnuts he did not want, because a man fleeing a notice does not walk away from a notice empty-handed, and because the heat of the little paper cone gave his hands something honest to do. Then he walked — not quickly, never quickly — out of the square, past the tanner's reeking yard, down the lane where Wren had rented them a room above a cooperage whose smell of oak shavings almost managed to cover the river stink.

Ada was sitting on the floor of the room when he came in, cross-legged, drawing on the back of a scrap of Wren's old sheet music. She looked up at his face and set down her chalk immediately. She had become fluent, these last weeks, in the language of his expressions. He wondered sometimes what she saw when she looked at him — whether his face was as legible to her as her hands were to him, or whether she simply read the weather coming off him the way sailors read the sky.

He held up the notice. She took it, studied the face, then looked up at his actual face, comparing, and something moved through her expression — not fear exactly. Recognition. The particular grief of a child who has learned that papers can hunt people.

She pointed at the drawing's eyes. Then at his. Then she drew, quickly, on her scrap: two circles, and inside them, small flames.

"I know," Casper said quietly. "I know."

Wren came up the stairs two at a time half an hour later, smelling of the cheap gin she had sworn off three times since Marrowgate and carrying bread under one arm like a shield. She was fifty if she was a day, built like a door, with a voice that had once filled the Chapel Royal and now mostly filled taverns. When she saw the notice she went very still, in the way Casper had begun to recognize as her version of screaming.

"Temple hand," she said, tapping the charcoal face with one blunt finger. "See the shading? They teach it in the cloister schools. Cross-hatching for shadow, never smudge. Your informant is a churchman, love, or was one." She turned the paper over, as if the answer might be written on the back. "Brother Aldous, I'd wager. He always did have a memory like a flypaper. Never forgot a face. Never forgave one either."

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows everyone in God's house. That's the trouble with it." Wren dropped onto the room's single stool, which groaned. "They'll have copies in every market town within a week. Riders will carry them along the north road. Which means, my dear fugitive, that we are done with roads."

"We're not done with anything," Casper said. "The court seals at midwinter. Six weeks. Maybe five. We go through Greywater or we don't go at all."

"And every soul on the Greywater road will have your face in their pocket." Wren rubbed her eyes. "You understand what this is? This isn't a hunt anymore. This is arithmetic. Fifty crowns is a year's wages to a farmhand. A hundred is a new life. You've put a price on yourself that turns every hungry man between here and the mountain into a hunter. There's no outwalking that. There's only outlasting it, or—" she stopped herself, but Ada finished the thought aloud in the only language she had, dragging one finger across her throat.

"No," Casper said. "Not that."

Ada looked at him, and there was no fear in her face at all, which frightened him more than fear would have. She was nine years old and she had already priced her own death and found it reasonable. Whatever the temple had done to her — whatever the foundling ward did to all of them — it had taught her that first.

"We keep moving," he said. "We go tomorrow, before more notices come. North road as far as the old drover's track, then west to Greywater. Wren — how far is the ford?"

"A day and a half, if the weather holds. Three days if it doesn't." She glanced at the window, where the light had gone the color of dirty wool. "It won't hold."

Casper folded the notice into quarters and put it in his coat, over his heart, where he could feel it. He told himself he kept it as evidence. He knew, in the honest part of himself that he tried not to visit often, that he kept it the way a man keeps a knife wound: to remember what he owed, and to whom.

* * *

The rain began at dusk the second day and by morning had turned to snow, fat and wet and blinding, the kind that fell straight down and erased the world in layers. The drover's track had become a brown river of mud, and the Greywater, when they finally heard it, announced itself as a roar through the white — high, fast, swollen with melt and rain, the color of strong tea.

The ford was marked by two leaning posts and a rope that had been strung between them sometime in the previous century. On the far bank stood a toll-house, shuttered, its keeper evidently having decided that no one sane would cross in this. On the near bank, warming themselves around a fire under a lean-to, were four men with crown badges on their oilskins and horses stamping in the shelter of a broken barn.

Casper saw them first, because seeing things first was the one skill his years of servitude had sharpened to an edge. He pulled Wren and Ada back behind a drystone wall, snow soaking through his knees, and they crouched there breathing steam while he counted.

Four riders. One was walking the bank, testing the water with a pole. The others were watching the road behind them — the road Casper had just come down.

"They're not here for the ford," he whispered. "They're waiting. Someone told them which way we'd come."

"The innkeeper at Harrow's Rest," Wren breathed. "I knew I didn't like his face."

"Can we go around? Another crossing?"

"Not for twenty miles, and the water's worse everywhere else. This is the shallow stretch." Wren's jaw worked. "We could wait for dark."

"They'll have fires and fresh horses. We'd be crossing black water in the dark with a child." He shook his head. "And every hour we wait, more notices, more riders. No. We cross now, while they're warm and lazy and sure we're a day behind them."

"Casper—"

"Wren." He heard his own voice come out flat and certain, and was briefly startled by it. Somewhere in the last weeks, without noticing, he had stopped being the man who waited for instructions. "Get her ready. Rope around her waist, tied to you. If the water takes her, you hold. Promise me you hold."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and began uncoiling the rope.

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do, each piece arriving before the last one has finished being understood.

They were a third of the way across — Ada between them, the rope taut, the water shoving at their thighs with the indifferent muscle of a living thing — when the shout went up from the bank. One of the riders had turned, maybe catching movement through the snow, maybe just lucky, and now his arm was out and pointing and the other three were moving, snatching up weapons, kicking snow over their fire.

"Hold there! In the king's name, hold!"

Casper held. It was the correct instinct — obedience buys seconds — and he used those seconds to turn, to look, to measure. Thirty yards of open water between them and the bank. Four men. Two were running for the posts; one was fumbling with a crossbow, cursing the snow getting into the mechanism; the fourth had mounted and was riding along the bank to cut them off downstream.

The crossbowman raised his weapon.

And Casper felt it rise in him — the hum, the thing in his chest that he had spent thirty-one years pretending was indigestion, or nerves, or nothing. Wren had spent the road teaching him its grammar: *every note costs, boy, every note is paid for out of your own flesh, your own past, so you spend small and you spend rarely.* He had practiced whispering notes at campfires. He had stilled a guard dog with one, weeks ago, and paid for it with the taste of his father's stew, gone from his memory like a coin from a purse.

A note could not stop a crossbow bolt. He understood that. But a note could do something else.

He opened his mouth and sang.

There was no melody in it, nothing a listener would have called music. It was a single tone, low, pitched below hearing as much as within it, and he poured into it everything the moment demanded: *you see nothing, you see nothing, there is nothing on this river but water.* He felt the note leave him and he felt what it tore loose on its way out, and he had just enough time to understand — with the strange clarity of a man falling — what it was taking.

Snow. A kitchen. His mother standing at the basin with her sleeves rolled, humming, her hands red with wash-water, turning to smile at him over her shoulder. *Sing it again, Casper, the middle part, you always rush the middle part—*

The memory went out of him like breath into cold air. He felt it leave — felt the specific, irreplaceable warmth of it pass through his throat and into the world — and where it had been there remained only a smooth, polished absence, the shape of a memory with nothing inside it. He knew he had loved his mother. He knew there had been snow, and a kitchen, and a song. He knew it the way you know a fact in a ledger. The knowing cost nothing. That was the horror of it. The knowing was free, and the having was gone forever.

On the bank, the world went soft.

The crossbowman lowered his weapon, blinked, and stared out at the river with the mild, vacant interest of a man watching weather. The two runners stopped at the posts and stood, shoulder to shoulder, watching the brown water slide past as though it were the most absorbing thing they had ever seen. The mounted rider reined in and simply sat, snow gathering on his shoulders, his horse blowing steam, both of them gazing at the flood with perfect, empty contentment.

Casper sang the note until his lungs burned, and then he turned and hauled on the rope, and they crossed.

The water fought them the last ten yards — Ada slipped once, went under to her chin, and came up silent and furious, clawing at the rope — and then the far bank's shingle was under his boots and they were stumbling up into the trees beyond the toll-house, and Casper let the note die in his throat.

Behind them, across the roar of the river, the four hunters stood on the bank and watched the water. One of them waved. A slow, pleasant, absent wave, at nothing.

Then the trees closed, and the snow swallowed everything.

* * *

They walked until the light failed, because stopping meant shaking, and shaking meant thinking, and thinking meant the hole in his chest. Wren made camp in the hollow beneath a fallen fir, a fire small enough to hide, and fed Ada dried apples and heated wine, and did not ask Casper questions, because she had seen his face when he came out of the water and she knew better than anyone alive what that face meant. She had paid that price herself, once, note by note, until the Chapel Royal burned and there was nothing left worth singing.

Casper sat with his back against the log and watched the fire and conducted an inventory.

He remembered that his mother had existed. He remembered her name — Maren — the way he remembered the names of kings. He remembered that she had died in the fever winter, when he was eleven, and that his father had never recovered, and that the debts had begun the following spring. All facts. All clean. All weightless.

But when he reached for the kitchen — for the smell of lye and wet wool, for the sound of her humming, for the exact way her chapped hands had looked wringing out linen — he found a room swept bare. Not dark. Bare. Lit and orderly and utterly empty, like a chapel after the congregation has gone home, and he sat in the doorway of it and understood that he would sit in that doorway for the rest of his life.

"It was the good one," he said, to the fire. His voice sounded foreign to him. "Out of everything I had left of her, it was the good one. The best one. I'd have given you the debt ledgers. I'd have given you my father's face, gladly, take all of him, take every winter of it. But the gift doesn't ask what you'd rather lose, does it, Wren? It just takes what's dearest and calls it fair."

Wren said nothing for a long time. Across the fire, Ada had stopped eating and was watching him with her whole body, the way she did, her chalk forgotten in her fist.

"It's not fair," Wren said at last. "No one ever said it was. That's the first lesson and the last one: the song spends what you love, because that's what gives it power. Cheap notes are wind. Only what costs you anything can move the world." She poked the fire, and sparks climbed. "I watched choristers spend themselves for applause. Little notes, little memories — the taste of a sweet, a game in a courtyard. They thought it was nothing. Ten years on, they couldn't remember why they'd loved singing at all. You spent big, Casper. You spent like a fool and like a hero in the same breath, and I'm not going to insult you by telling you it was worth it. It wasn't worth it. Nothing is worth it. But it worked, and she's alive—" he nodded at Ada, "—and so are you, and the men on that bank will wake tomorrow remembering a nice long look at a river."

"I resent it," Casper said. The words came out of him with a force that surprised them all. "I want you to know that. Everyone talks about the gift like it's holy. The mountain's own music, the old blood, all of it. Nobody says the truth, which is that it's a tax collector. It waits until you're desperate and then it takes the best thing you own and leaves you a receipt." He pressed his fist against his sternum, against the smooth hollow where the memory had been. "I hate it. I hate that I used it. I hate that I'll use it again, because we both know I will, because there's a court under that mountain full of souls hanging in the dark and a child sitting next to you who's missing hers, and I will pay every—"

He stopped. Because Ada had moved.

She had come around the fire on her knees, and she knelt in front of him now, close, and took his fist in both her small hands, and unfolded it, finger by finger, until his palm lay open between them. Then she pressed her own palm against it, and looked up at him, and did the thing she had been learning to do, the impossible thing, the thing Wren called a blasphemy and a miracle in the same sentence.

She sang to him. Without sound. Without spending anything at all.

He felt it as warmth — a warmth that rose through his palm and up his arm and settled precisely, deliberately, into the swept-bare room where his mother's kitchen had been. It did not fill it. Nothing could fill it. But it warmed the walls of it, the way a coal warms a cold hearthstone, and in the warmth there was something wordless and stubborn and fierce that he understood perfectly, because it was the same thing he had felt the day he'd found her hiding in the salt cellars and decided, in one breath, to damn them both:

*I see the hole. I will sit in it with you.*

Casper bowed his head over their joined hands, and the resentment did not leave him — he suspected it never would, and some loyal part of him hoped it wouldn't, because the resentment was proof the payment had been real — but it loosened its grip, just enough to breathe around.

When he looked up, Wren was staring past them, past the fire, northward, into the dark, and her face had changed.

"What?" he said.

"That rider," she said slowly. "The one on the bank. The one who waved." She was quiet a moment. "Gifted hunters carry a mark, Casper. A badge struck in lead, worn under the collar. Serah Vail's company wears them — Vail herself, if the songs are true. And gifted folk feel each other's notes, even spent ones. Even across a river." Her eyes came back to his, and they were grim as slate. "That wasn't a man waving at the water, love. That was a woman acknowledging a colleague. She felt you sing. She knows exactly what crossed her ford tonight, and she knows it's heading for the mountain — and Serah Vail does not stop, Casper. Not for snow, not for rivers, not for midwinter gates. They say she was gifted once herself, and bought her pardon with a warrant."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, the Greywater roared on in the dark, carrying the melting snow down toward the sea.

"How long?" Casper asked.

Wren looked at the sky, at the snow still sifting down through the branches, and did the arithmetic she clearly wished she didn't have to do.

"If she rides hard and sleeps little? She's behind us now. By Greywater bridge—" she met his eyes, "—she won't be."
