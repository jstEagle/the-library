## 8. Ashes of the Chapel Royal

The road to Marrowgate's northern gate ran past the chapel, and Wren had spent three days pretending it wouldn't.

She had told herself a hundred small lies since leaving the inn — that the old royal road curved east before it reached the hill, that the snow would close the pass and turn them back, that Casper would take one look at the blackened skeleton on the ridge and choose the longer way around out of simple courtesy. But roads are honest things. They go where they have always gone, and the Chapel Royal of Saint Audra had stood beside this road for four hundred years, and so the road carried them toward it with the indifferent patience of all old roads, and Wren walked at the back of their little procession and hated every step.

Casper led, his breath smoking in the grey morning air. Ada rode on his shoulders, her small mittened hands laced under his chin, her eyes — those enormous, patient eyes — cataloguing everything they passed with the diligence of a scribe. The girl missed nothing. Wren had learned that in the weeks since Marrowgate. She watched the child watch the world: the way a crow's shadow made her tilt her head, the way she'd traced the shape of frost-flowers on a windowpane and then looked up at Wren as if to say *you see it too, don't you, the way cold makes its own music*. Ada spoke no words. She didn't need them. Her silence was more articulate than most people's chatter.

And now the road bent, and the trees thinned, and there it was.

The chapel stood on its knoll above the frozen river like a burned ribcage picked clean by winter. The roof was gone — had been gone for six years — but the stone walls still rose, black-streaked, windowless, the great arched frames empty as eye sockets. Snow filled the nave. Through the shattered west front you could see straight through to the apse, where the altar stone remained, furred white, and above it the empty niche where the statue of Saint Audra had once held her golden songbird. Someone had toppled the saint. The crown's men, probably, or the weather, or time doing what crowns pay it to do.

Wren stopped walking.

Casper went three paces before he noticed, then turned, Ada swaying gently on his shoulders. He said nothing. He had learned that about him in their weeks together — the man knew when silence was a kindness. He simply stood there in the road, breath rising from him in slow plumes, and waited.

"I used to warm my hands on those stones," Wren said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Every morning before matins. November through March. The south wall held the sun and I'd stand there like a cat and think, *this is mine, this wall, this warmth, this life.*" She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle like river ice. "Sixty choristers, Casper. Sixty children with voices like cut glass. We sang for the king's grandmother's funeral and the sound was so fine the old woman's ladies swore they saw her sit up in the coffin."

Ada slid down from Casper's shoulders without being asked. She came and stood beside Wren and did not touch her, which was its own kind of tact, and looked up at the ruin with an expression Wren couldn't read — or could read too well.

"We don't have to stop," Casper said quietly. "There's a track along the river. Adds half a day."

"Half a day we don't have." Wren lifted her chin at the mountains, far off now but visible, blue-white and implacable against the sky. "Midwinter's coming whether I face this place or not." She started forward before her legs could refuse. "Come on, then. If I'm going to bleed, I'd rather do it standing in my own nave."

* * *

Inside, the snow lay unbroken except for fox prints, a lacework of small dark stitches across the white. Their boots broke the crust with sounds that echoed off the walls and came back wrong — flattened, deadened, as if the chapel itself had forgotten how to carry a voice. Wren knew better. Stone remembers. It was singing that had gotten this place killed.

"The choir loft was there." She pointed to the broken stair against the north wall. "I stood on that landing the night they came. You could see the whole nave from it. I used to conduct from there — sixty children below me, and I'd lift my hands and it was like raising a flock of birds." She turned a slow circle, naming the dead geography. "The rehearsal room behind the apse. The dormitory wing — over there, that low wall, that was where the little ones slept. They put the youngest nearest the kitchen so they could steal warm bread at night. I pretended not to know. A choir mistress who doesn't notice stolen bread is a blind woman, and blind women keep their posts."

"Wren," Casper said, "you don't—"

"I refused." The words came out of her like something expelled, not spoken. "That's the whole of it. That's why there's ash in your boot-prints instead of gold leaf. In the spring of the year the old king died, a delegation came from below the mountain. Fine grey cloaks, soft voices, papers sealed with the Pale Cantor's mark — though we weren't told the name then, only that they served the court beneath Thornfell. They came to the chapel with a royal warrant and a list. Names. Ages. Voice ranges." Her hand shook as she gestured at the apse. "They wanted twelve of my children. Twelve. Measured like hams. And the warrant said the crown required their 'sanctified service,' and the master of the delegation smiled at me with such patience, such *reasonableness*, and said the chapel should be honored."

Casper had gone very still. Beside him, Ada had stopped moving entirely, the way small animals stop when a hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"And you?" he asked, though Wren could see he already knew.

"I said no." She walked to the altar stone and laid her palm flat on the snow covering it, as if greeting an old horse. "I stood in this nave with sixty children hiding in the crypt beneath my feet — I'd put them down there myself, given them candles and told them it was a game — and I told the grey-cloaked men that the Chapel Royal did not sell its singers. That voices were not the crown's to requisition. That they could take the matter up with God, whose property the children arguably were, and let Him negotiate." She smiled, and it was an ugly smile, full of old broken glass. "They wrote it down. Everything gets written down; that's how you know a thing is real. And six weeks later the king's soldiers came at night, and by morning the finest choir in the kingdom was a smell of smoke and a story mothers tell to frighten children."

"They said it was lightning," Casper said slowly. "In Marrowgate, everyone says lightning."

"Lightning in winter. Lightning that burns only the chapel and spares the tithe barn full of the king's own grain ten feet away." Wren wiped her palm on her coat. "It was an example, Casper. That's all I ever was afterward — an example. *This is what happens to women who say no.* They let me live because a dead martyr is dangerous and a disgraced drunk is useful. Every town I crawled into, someone recognized me and spat, and the story got retold, and the crown never had to burn another building. I did their work for them, drinking myself stupid in gutter taverns. I was the cautionary tale that walked."

The wind moved through the empty windows, a low moan over the stones, and Wren thought: even now, even gutted, the acoustics hold. Some part of her wanted to sing just to hear what the walls gave back. Some larger part of her knew she would shatter if she tried.

* * *

Ada had wandered — no, not wandered; Ada never wandered, she moved with intent always — to the base of the choir loft stair. She was crouched in the snow, brushing it aside with her mitten, and when Wren came near she saw the child had uncovered a scrap of scorched wood with gilded paint still clinging to one edge. Ada turned it over in her fingers. Then she looked up at Wren and held it out, offering it, her face grave as a judge's.

Wren took it. Her throat closed.

"You want to know the worst of it," she said, and found she was going to say it regardless, the confession hauling itself up out of her like a drowned thing finally surfacing. "You want to know why I can't forgive myself, and it isn't the fire. The fire, I can live with. I chose the fire. I'd choose it again tomorrow, God help me, because sixty children lived — scattered, hidden, smuggled out by friends in the night while the soldiers drank the cellar dry — but they *lived*, most of them. No." She crouched down, bringing her face level with Ada's, because some things you cannot say from above. "One of them I failed. One name on their list I couldn't save, because I wasn't fast enough, because I counted heads in the dark like a fool counting coins and came up one short."

Ada's eyes were steady on hers. Waiting. Children like Ada — children who lived inside silence — had a way of waiting that pulled truth out of adults like a splinter.

"Her name was Merrin," Wren said. "Eleven years old. Alto already, with a bottom note that made grown men cry, and I mean that literally — the king's chamberlain wept at the midwinter concert, big bearded man, sobbed into his sleeve. When the soldiers came, I got fifty-nine children out the crypt passage and Merrin wasn't among them, and I went back in, I swear to you I went back in, the roof was coming down and I went back in—" Her voice broke and she let it. "They took her. The grey cloaks found her in the bell tower, hiding in the frame of the great bell, and they took her below the mountain, and I have spent six years not knowing whether she is alive or dead or worse than dead, and every drunk night in Marrowgate I told myself she was dead because dead is clean, dead means she isn't *there*—"

She stopped. The word *there* hung in the freezing air of the ruined nave, pointing downward, the way it always pointed, the way it had pointed in every conversation they'd had since Brannoc's fire, since the ledger, since the hunt began.

Below. Beneath Thornfell. Where they were going. Where souls hung like lanterns in the dark, if Brannoc's stories were true — and Wren had begun, terribly, to believe they were.

"Oh," Casper said softly. Not *I'm sorry*. She was grateful to him for that. Sorry was a coin everyone spent and no one honored anymore.

"She might be alive," Wren said. The words felt blasphemous and holy at once. "Down there. Six years in the Cantor's vaults. If the soul-ledgers are real, if they keep records the way they kept records of everything else, then somewhere in that mountain there is a line in a book that says *Merrin, eleven years, alto,* and it will say what became of her, and I am going to read it if I have to burn my way into the vaults with my bare hands." She looked at her own hands, chapped and scarred and trembling, and laughed again, that same cracked laugh. "Listen to me. An old drunk with a broken voice, talking about storming the court beneath the world."

Ada reached out and closed Wren's fingers around the scrap of gilded wood, folding the child's small hands over the woman's knotted ones. Then she did something Wren had seen her do only twice before. She hummed — no, not hummed; Ada made no sound, had never made a sound in all the weeks Wren had known her, and yet—

Yet Wren *felt* it. Felt it in her breastbone, in the hollows of her teeth, a note with no air behind it, pure intention shaped into pitch, a song sung in the medium where souls themselves were supposedly woven. The silent song. The impossible technique that had made Wren, a trained mistress of the royal chapel, feel like a novice scraping a bow across strings for the first time. And the note Ada sang now was warm, and it was steady, and it said — there was no other way to describe it — *I am here. You are not alone in this nave. Whatever you did and whatever you failed to do, I am here, and I am not afraid of you.*

Wren bowed her head. Tears froze on her lashes before they fell.

"You shouldn't comfort me," she whispered. "You of all people. Your soul was sold before you could walk, girl. Sold by the same machine I fed children into for fifteen years — because don't think I didn't know, don't think the choir mistresses didn't *suspect*, the lists we were asked for, the measurements, the 'aptitude assessments' — we suspected and we signed anyway because signing kept the chapel open and the bread in the pantry and the music alive, and I told myself the ones taken were few and the ones taught were many, and that arithmetic is the exact arithmetic of hell—"

Casper's hand landed on her shoulder. Heavy, steadying. "And now?" he asked. "What's the arithmetic now?"

Wren looked up at him — this tired scribe with his smuggler's maps and his terrified, stubborn decency — and then at Ada, small and silent and burning like a candle in a draft.

"Now the arithmetic is three," she said. "Three against a mountain. And I'll tell you what I learned in fifteen years of conducting, Casper Thackeray: it was never about how many voices you had. It was about whether they sang together." She stood, joints cracking, and pressed the scrap of gilded wood into her coat pocket over her heart, next to where Merrin's name lived. "We get to the court. We find the ledger. We find your soul, Ada, and we find my Merrin if there's anything left to find, and we walk out of that mountain owing the Pale Cantor nothing but our contempt. That's the concert program. That's the whole program."

Ada smiled. It transformed her whole face, that smile — rare as midsummer, bright as the gilding on the scrap of wood — and she nodded once, sharply, a general accepting terms.

They turned to go. At the broken west front Wren paused, one hand on the frozen stone, and looked back down the nave at the place where the choir loft had been, and the place where the dormitory had been, and the place where she had lifted her hands and raised birds.

"Sing loud wherever you are, Merrin," she murmured. "However they've got you. Sing loud, and mind the high C, and I'm coming."

Then the three of them crunched out through the snow into the grey light, and took the north road, and behind them the chapel stood in its ashes keeping its silence — until, just at the edge of hearing, as if the stone itself had remembered an old kindness, the wind through the empty windows carried something that was almost, almost, a chord.

They made good distance that afternoon, the road climbing gently toward the distant blue wall of Thornfell. Toward evening the land flattened into moorland, and it was there, at the crossing of two ancient tracks marked on none of Casper's precious maps, that they saw the rider.

He sat his horse on the rise ahead, motionless, black against the last red light — too far off to make out his face, too deliberate in his stillness to be anything accidental. As the light died he did not approach and did not leave. He simply watched them, the way a hawk watches a field it has decided contains something worth taking.

Wren felt the cold slide down her spine and settle, familiar as an old debt.

"That's no bounty scout," she said quietly. "Bounty men travel in packs and drink on the road. That's a *herald*." She glanced at Casper, at Ada, and her hand found the child's shoulder and gripped it. "Someone knows exactly where we are. And I'd wager my last memory they've sent word ahead to the mountain."

The rider turned his horse, unhurried, and was gone into the dusk — leaving three travelers on a darkening moor, and a long cold night between them and the passes, and the certain knowledge that the road ahead was no longer merely hard.

It was waiting for them.
