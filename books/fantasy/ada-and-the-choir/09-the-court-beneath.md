## 9. The Court Beneath

The smuggler's hut sat where the map said it would, though Casper had half expected the mountain to have swallowed it in the years since his father's ink had marked its position. It crouched at the treeline above a frozen stream, built of grey stone and turf, with smoke leaking from a chimney so crooked it seemed to be leaning away from its own fire. A rack of split fish hung under the eaves, stiff as parchment. Somewhere behind the hut, a goat complained to no one.

"You knock," Wren said. "You're the one who knows him."

"I know *of* him," Casper said. "My father mapped his roads. I copied his maps." He looked down at Ada, who stood pressed against his side with her mittened hand fisted in the hem of his coat. She was watching the chimney smoke with an expression he had learned to read over the weeks of their flight: not fear exactly, but a kind of listening. As if the smoke were saying something.

"He'll want payment," Wren said. "Smugglers always do."

"We don't have anything."

"We have you," Wren said, and there was no comfort in the way she said it. "A man with a voice that can still a guard dog at forty paces. That's worth more than coin up here."

Casper didn't answer. He climbed the two stone steps and knocked — three raps, then two, then one, the old pattern from the margins of his father's maps, the rhythm smugglers used to tell each other *friend, not crown*. The door opened on a chain, and through the gap came one eye, pale as river ice, set in a face like weathered bark.

"Thackeray's boy," the eye said.

"Brannoc?"

"Who else would live in a place like this?" The chain rattled off. The door swung wide onto warmth and gloom and the smell of woodsmoke, tallow, and something else beneath it all — a mineral smell, cold and deep, like the air that rises out of a well in summer. "Get in before your heat draws every crow on the ridge. And bring the child inside, she's blue to the wrists."

Inside, the hut was one room, low-ceilinged, crowded with the archaeology of a long life lived sideways to the law: coils of rope, bundles of dried herbs, a wall of small drawers labeled in a crabbed hand, a stove blacker than sin, and maps — dozens of them, layered on a great table like fallen leaves, some drawn on vellum, some on hide, one, Casper noticed with a lurch, on what appeared to be human skin, though he decided firmly not to think about that.

Brannoc was older than his voice suggested. His beard was white and untrimmed, his hands enormous, knuckles swollen like the roots of an old tree. He poured three cups of something dark and steaming without asking what they wanted, and set a fourth cup of warmed milk before Ada, who took it in both hands and watched him over its rim with the unblinking attention she gave everyone.

"Thackeray's boy," Brannoc said again, settling into a chair that groaned under him. "Last I saw your father, he owed me for a crossing at Greywater and paid me in flattery instead. Said I was the only honest man on the mountain. Which tells you what his flattery was worth." He drank. "Now. You're running from the temple, which is stupid but common. You're running north, which is stupider and less common. And you've got the girl with you, who has no soul-sound when I look at her, which means either she's dead or her soul's been sold below." He set down the cup. "So. Ask me what you came to ask me, and let's have done with circling."

Casper felt Wren go very still beside him. He realized his own hands were shaking around the cup, and made them stop.

"The court beneath Thornfell," he said. "Is it real?"

Brannoc laughed. It was not a pleasant sound; it was the sound of rockfall, distant and indifferent. "Real? Boy, I've carried cargo to its gates. I've taken payment in its coin, which spends nowhere else in the world because it isn't minted anywhere in the world. Real as the toothache." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved in the hollows of his face. "But you're asking the wrong question. The right question is: what is it? And the answer to that depends on how much of your night you want to ruin."

"All of it," Casper said.

"Good. Honest answer. Most people want just enough ruin to feel brave and not enough to change their plans." Brannoc pushed back his chair, crossed to the wall of little drawers, and rummaged until he found what he wanted: a lump of something waxy and pale, which he set on the table between the maps. Candle-wax, Casper thought — and then saw that it wasn't. It was too dense, too smooth, and faintly warm, though nothing near the stove had touched it.

"Soulsong wax," Brannoc said. "Comes up from below, once a year, in trade. Burns without flame. Sings while it burns, if you've ears for it. The temple buys most of it — did you ever wonder what your abbots did with their quiet evenings?" He tapped it once with a thick finger. "This is what a soul looks like after it's been rendered down. This one was a child. You can tell by the pitch."

Ada made a sound. It wasn't a word — Ada didn't make words — but it came out of her like a crack in ice, and both adults turned. She was staring at the wax with an expression Casper had never seen on her face before, and he found he couldn't look at it long. He put his hand over hers on the table. Her fingers were rigid as iron.

"Put it away," Wren said quietly. There was steel under the quiet. "Or I'll melt it in your stove."

"It's already been melted once, choir mistress," Brannoc said, but he swept the wax back into its drawer, and Ada's breathing slowly evened out under Casper's hand. "Sit. Both of you. Girl, you too. What I'm going to tell you, I've told maybe six living people, and four of them are dead and the fifth wishes he was."

They sat. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind walked along the ridgeline, testing doors.

"Before Thornfell had temples," Brannoc began, "it had singers. Not priests — singers. The first folk who came to this mountain found it hollow, and the hollow sang back when they sang into it. That's the truth of the gift, if you've been wondering. It's not in your blood. It's in the mountain's blood, and some few are born tuned to it, the way a string is tuned to a note. The old singers knew this. They'd descend into the deep places and sing with the stone, and the stone would hold their songs, and their dead, and their winters. A bargain. Mountain keeps you warm, you keep the mountain company."

"And the court?" Casper asked.

"The court is what happened when men decided the bargain could be improved." Brannoc's mouth twisted. "Somewhere in the deep generations, someone worked out that the mountain didn't just *hold* song. It held *souls*. That a soul, sung rightly, could be pulled loose of its body like a thread pulled loose of cloth — and stored. Kept. Spent. The first Cantor learned it, or stole it, depending whose account you believe, and he went down into the hollow with a choir of volunteers who never came up, and he built himself a court in the dark. The Pale Cantor, they call him now. Pale because he hasn't seen sun in longer than your temple's been standing. Pale because he's given so much of himself away to keep the rest of himself going that there's hardly anything left but the shape of a man and the hunger."

"Hunger," Wren repeated. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the way it went flat when she was keeping herself level. "Hunger for what?"

"What do you think, woman? For what he trades in." Brannoc spread his hands over the layered maps. "Down there, souls are currency. Coin, candle, bread, and wine. The court runs on them. The lanterns in its halls are souls. The warmth in its hearths is souls. The Cantor himself —" he paused, and for the first time the old man looked, briefly, genuinely tired — "the Cantor eats. Slowly. Carefully. The way a miser spends. But he eats. And everything in that court that glitters or glows or sings is somebody who used to walk under this sky."

The silence that followed had weight. Casper became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, and of Ada beside him, who had gone very still in the way she did — not frozen, he corrected himself, not anymore, but *listening*, her whole body turned toward the story like a flower toward light.

"The temple sells to him," Casper said. It wasn't a question. He had known since the second ledger, since the columns of names in abbot's handwriting, each name followed by a sum and a date and a small, neat notation of *aptitude*. But saying it aloud, in front of a man who nodded as if Casper had remarked on the weather — that was different. That made it geography instead of suspicion.

"Temple's been his factor for two hundred years," Brannoc said. "Gifted children measure high. Aptitude, your abbots call it. Down below they call it *ripeness*. Twice a year the temple sends a consignment down the Deep Stair, and twice a year the payment comes up — soulgoods, wax, coin that buys silence in high places. Your abbot's robes are paid for in children, boy. So is the roof over his head. So is the crown's tolerance of his little kingdom." He shrugged, vast and slow. "I don't judge. I carried for them both. A man's got to eat, and better I carry than some fool who talks."

"But the gates," Wren said. "We've heard the court seals itself. When. How long."

"Ah." Brannoc looked at her, and then at Casper, and last at Ada, and something in his weathered face shifted — regret, maybe, or the particular pity of a man about to take away the last thing a person is holding onto. "There it is. There's the question that matters, and I'm sorry in advance for the answer.

"The gates open twice a year. Midsummer and midwinter. Midsummer's gone. Midwinter opens the Deep Stair for nine days — nine days when the court trades, receives, settles accounts. After that, the gates shut, and they don't open again till spring thaw, and I mean *shut*. Not locked — sealed. The mountain itself closes. Stone grows over stone. I've seen the stair-mouth the morning after closing. Smooth as an egg. You could stand in front of it with a hundred miners and ten years and never get through, because it isn't a door anymore, it's just mountain." He held up one gnarled finger. "And here's the part you won't like. Once you're down when the gates close, you're down till spring. No messages out. No rescue. Nothing crosses that stone, going either direction — nothing except song, and precious little of that."

"When is midwinter?" Casper asked, though his stomach already knew.

"Eighteen days," Brannoc said. "Nineteen, counting today."

Casper did the arithmetic without wanting to. The distance they'd come. The distance remaining — the high pass, Greywater, the climb to the stair-mouth on the mountain's northern flank. In summer, with clear roads and luck, eight days. In winter, with snow coming and hunters behind them and a mute child's legs half Ada's size —

"We'll never make it," he said. The words came out smaller than he intended.

"You might," Brannoc said. "If you don't sleep much and the weather lies to you kindly. Or you might make it in twenty days and find an egg of stone where the door used to be, and then you'll spend a winter on this mountain waiting for spring, with Vail's hunters picking through the passes for you, and the girl's soul hanging in a lantern a mile under your feet getting dimmer." He said it without cruelty, the way a surgeon says what the knife must do. "I'm not telling you this to break your heart, Thackeray's boy. I'm telling you so you know the shape of the race you're running."

Wren had gone to the window. Her back was straight, her hands folded, and when she spoke her voice was the choir-mistress voice, the one that had once commanded forty children into harmony. "Is there another way in? Any way. Old ways, smuggler ways, ways the temple doesn't use."

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Ada drank her milk in small, deliberate sips, her eyes moving between the adults' faces, reading them the way she read everything — completely, silently, missing nothing.

"There's the Chimneys," Brannoc said at last, reluctantly, as if the words cost him. "Fissures in the deep rock where the mountain's original song still leaks through. The old singers used them. They're not stairs — they're cracks, and some of them breathe, and some of them swallow. I know where three of them open. I don't know where any of them *lead*, because nobody who's gone in asking questions has come back out to draw me a map. Except—" he stopped.

"Except?" Casper prompted.

"Except the ones who sing," Brannoc said. He turned and looked at Ada, directly, for the first time since the wax had gone back in its drawer. "The fissures answer song. That's the old teaching. If you could sing to the stone — truly sing, the way the first folk sang, giving nothing, taking nothing, just *sounding* — the mountain might let you through where it lets no one else. Might." He raised a warning finger. "Might. And I've never met anyone alive who could do it. The gift costs, everyone knows the gift costs. Memory, warmth, years. Everyone who sings pays, and the mountain knows a paid note from a free one."

Casper felt Wren's eyes come to him across the room. He knew what she was thinking, because he'd been thinking it since the salt cellars, since the dog had lain down and slept at the sound of his whisper: *what did my note cost me, and what will the next one cost, and the one after that.* Every note a coin spent from a purse he couldn't see into. Every song a little poorer.

But Ada —

Ada had set down her cup. She was looking at the great table, at the layered maps, and her hand rose, hesitant, and touched the oldest one — the vellum, brown with age, marked with lines that weren't roads. They spiraled. They branched. They descended, in slow patient coils, toward a space at the center of the vellum where someone, centuries ago, had drawn a small circle and written a single word in a dead alphabet.

She looked up at Brannoc and pointed at the spiral lines. Then she pointed at herself. Then she pointed at the circle at the center, and her finger did not shake.

"That's the Deep Stair map," Brannoc said slowly. "Older than the temple. Where'd a child learn to read—"

"She doesn't read it," Casper said quietly. Understanding arrived in him the way dawn arrives — not all at once, but inevitably, from the east of things. "Look at her. She's not reading. She's remembering."

Because it was true. He could see it now, had perhaps seen it for weeks without letting himself know: the way Ada studied unfamiliar country and then walked it as if she'd walked it before. The way she'd hummed — silent, airless, a song with no sound in it that he'd felt more than heard — on the nights the road grew bad, and the road had grown less bad. The way she listened to smoke, to wind, to the tick of stoves. She wasn't broken, this child the temple had declared empty. Something in her had been *taken*, and what remained was tuned to what remained of the mountain, and somewhere in the coils of that old vellum was a route her feet remembered.

Brannoc stared at her for a long time. Then he laughed again — that rockfall laugh — but softer this time, and there was something underneath it that hadn't been there before. Wonder, maybe. Or dread.

"Well," he said. "Well, then. Nineteen days, the Deep Stair, and a child who reads the mountain like her mother's face." He hauled himself up from his chair, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the wall of drawers. From the bottom one he took a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table: a knife, a coil of silk cord, a pouch that clinked, and a strip of dried meat enough for three. "You'll need all of it and more. Rest tonight — a real night, walls around you, because after this hut there's no shelter I'd trust between here and Greywater. Leave before dawn; the riders sweep the valley road at midmorning and I'd rather they found my goat than your tracks."

He paused at the stove, his back to them, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its performance, its smuggler's showmanship, and was simply an old man's voice, tired and honest.

"One thing more, and then I'll let you sleep. Whatever you find down there — whatever the Cantor offers you, and he *will* offer, he's reasonable, that's the horror of him, he'll offer you fair trade with a smile like a priest's — remember what the wax was. Remember the pitch. Everything in that court will seem civilized. Warm. Orderly. That's the trap of it. Hell with good manners is still hell." He turned. His pale eyes found Casper's, then Wren's, then rested longest on Ada. "Keep the child's name in your mouth. Names hold. When the mountain tries to make you forget why you came, say her name out loud, and remember."

"Ada," Casper said. Just that. Testing it, the way Brannoc had said.

The girl looked up at the sound of her own name, and smiled — small, crooked, entirely hers — and pointed at the old vellum map, and then at the door, and then north.

* * *

He could not sleep.

The others had — Wren curled in Brannoc's spare blanket by the stove with the practiced efficiency of a woman who had learned to seize sleep wherever it offered itself, and Ada beside her, breathing slow and even, one hand still closed around the warmed milk cup as if even in dreams she kept hold of her possessions. But Casper lay on the pallet Brannoc had grudgingly surrendered, staring at the rafters, and the arithmetic would not stop.

*Nineteen days.* Eighteen now, past midnight. The high pass in winter. Greywater's bridge with its toll-keeper of whom Brannoc had refused, flatly, to speak. Hunters behind them with his own face sketched on paper — he'd seen the notice in Marrowgate, the temple informant's memory given ink, *THACKERAY, CASPER, SCRIBE, GIFTED — SANCTIFY ON SIGHT*. And ahead, at the end of all of it, a door that would become an egg of stone.

He got up quietly, wrapped his coat around him, and sat at the great table where the maps lay in their layers. By the banked firelight he lifted the old vellum — carefully, it was stiff as autumn leaves — and traced the coiling descent with one finger. Down and down, through strata marked in the dead alphabet, past symbols he half recognized from forbidden pages in the temple archive, to the circle at the center. The court. Someone had drawn it small, almost shyly, as if afraid to give it too much presence on the page. Around the circle, in a ring, ran a line of text he could not read — but the shape of it, the repetition, the rise and fall of the letterforms, looked like nothing so much as musical notation.

*A song around a door,* he thought. *Or a lock shaped like a verse.*

Behind him, a floorboard creaked. He turned, guiltily, and found Ada standing in the dark, blanket around her shoulders, watching him with eyes that caught the ember-light.

"I couldn't sleep either," he whispered, though whispering to Ada was a habit rather than a necessity. "Come look at this. Tell me if I'm wrong."

She padded over and climbed onto the bench beside him, and together they bent over the map like two scholars over a psalter. She studied it a long time. Then she raised her hand and, with one finger, drew in the dust on the table beside the vellum: a spiral, quick and sure, matching the descent. Below it she drew a circle. Then she paused, her finger hovering, and drew something else — a small figure, stick-limbed, standing outside the circle. Beside it, a second figure, smaller. Then, after a moment's thought, a third: tall, angular, unmistakably Wren's sharp silhouette.

She tapped each figure in turn. Herself. Him. Wren. Then she tapped the circle — the court — and looked up at him, and her expression was the one that had carried them out of the salt cellars, out of Marrowgate, over the pass: not hope, exactly. Hope was soft. This was harder. This was a decision wearing patience as a disguise.

*All three of us go down,* she was saying. *Together. All three of us come back.*

"Ada," Casper said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and he didn't care. "What they took from you. We're going to get it back. I don't know how yet. I don't know what it costs. But we're going to get it back, and you're going to walk out of that mountain whole, and I swear it on —" he stopped. On what? His father's debt? His mother's memory? On nothing, on no god, on no saint, on nothing but himself, a scribe with a stolen gift and eighteen days. "On me," he finished. "I swear it on me. It's all I've got."

Ada considered this with the gravity she brought to everything. Then she reached out, took his hand — the one that had traced the map — and pressed it flat against the vellum, over the small shy circle at the center. Her other hand she laid against her own chest, over the place where, weeks ago on a frozen road, he had first felt it: the hum. The silent note that cost nothing and feared nothing and sounded, when the world went quiet enough to hear it, exactly like the mountain.

Outside, the wind rose along the ridge, and beneath the wind — faint, deep, patient, rising out of the stone under the hut's foundations like a pulse out of a sleeping throat — Casper heard, or imagined he heard, the mountain singing to itself in the dark.

Eighteen days.

He memorized the map until the candles died.
