## 10. What Ada Remembers

The fire in the ruined shepherd's hut burned low and orange, and Ada sat as close to it as she dared without singeing her skirt, because the fire was the only thing that talked back to her.

Not with words. Fires never used words. But it popped and settled and breathed the way a sleeping animal breathes, and if she held her hands out over it she could feel its warmth climb up her arms like something small and friendly climbing a fence. Wren said fire had no soul to speak of — *no pun intended, little one* — but Ada thought that was wrong. Everything hummed. The fire hummed. The stones of the hut hummed, low and patient, holding all the heat of the day down inside themselves like secrets. Even the snow outside the broken door hummed, though its song was so thin and high that only someone who had spent six years learning to listen with her whole body would ever catch it.

Ada caught it. She caught everything.

That was the trouble.

She glanced across the fire at Casper, who sat with his back against the wall and his ledger open on his knees — not the temple's ledger, his own now, a cheap thing of grey paper he'd bought in Marrowgate with coins Wren called *blood money* and Casper called *wages*. He wrote in it every night. Routes, mostly. Distances. The names of passes and which ones the riders watched. His pen scratched like a beetle in a wall, scratch-scratch-scratch, and beneath the scratching Ada could hear the other sound, the one he didn't know he made: the low, warm note of him, steady as a hearthstone. When he worried, the note went sharp. Tonight it was a little sharp. He kept looking at the door.

Wren was asleep sitting up, which she did more often now, her chin on her chest, her scarred hands folded in her lap like two old birds roosting. Her song was quieter than Casper's, frayed at the edges, with holes in it — places where notes should have been and weren't, like a sweater with moths in it. Ada had learned not to stare at the holes. Staring at them made Wren's sleeping face flinch.

Three of them. That was what they were now. Three songs around one fire, and the mountain ahead of them humming its enormous slow hum under miles of stone.

Ada pulled her slate onto her knees.

Wren had given it to her in Marrowgate, along with three sticks of chalk worn down to stubs. *A picture's worth a hundred shouted words,* Wren had said, and then she'd laughed at her own joke in the way she did when the joke wasn't funny, so Ada had known not to smile. But the slate had changed everything. Before the slate, people guessed at her. They looked at her hands and saw only waving. With the slate, she could build things — whole rooms, whole days, whole storms — and set them down where others could walk into them.

Tonight she was going to build the worst room of all.

She picked up the white chalk first, because the memory began in white. White stone. White light that came from nowhere and everywhere, the way light does underground. She drew the archway first — tall, taller than a man, taller than two men — and then the stairs going down beyond it, and then, because perspective was hard and she was impatient, she simply turned the slate sideways and let the stairs run off the edge into nothing.

Casper's pen had stopped scratching.

She felt him notice before she saw him look up. His note went sharper still, a needle of attention. She didn't raise her head. She drew.

The stairs ended in a hall. She knew the hall the way you know a dream you've had a hundred times — not perfectly, but with terrible certainty about the parts that mattered. Pillars like the ribs of some enormous pale animal. A floor so polished it showed you your own face looking up at you from underneath, which had frightened her badly when she was very small, until she'd understood that the girl below the floor was only herself. And hanging above it all —

Here Ada hesitated, chalk hovering.

Then she drew the lanterns.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. Little flames in glass, strung on chains from the unseen ceiling, each one burning a slightly different color — gold, blue, green, one furious violet near the far wall. Except they weren't lanterns. She drew faces inside the glass. Small, simple faces, closed-eyed, peaceful-looking, which was the most horrible part, because they were *peaceful*, they had been made peaceful, and even at four years old, even wordless, Ada had understood that peace like that was stolen peace, the kind that comes after crying so long you forget why you started.

She heard Wren wake. Not the waking of a body — the waking of a song. The frayed old melody snapped taut, alert, and Wren's voice came low across the fire. "What is it, child? What are you drawing?"

Ada turned the slate around and held it up.

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on her ears the way deep water presses. Casper had gone very still, stiller than still, and his note dropped so low she felt it in the slate rather than heard it, a tremor through the wood frame into her fingers.

"That's the court," he said quietly. "Beneath Thornfell." He looked at her as if seeing her for the first time, or perhaps as if finally believing something he'd only suspected. "Ada. Have you *been* there?"

She nodded once.

And then, because a nod was not enough — because a nod was a pebble thrown at a fortress — she took up the chalk again and began to tell them everything.

* * *

She drew herself first. A small figure, stick-thin, with the dark scribble of hair she'd had before Wren cut it short with a knife in Marrowgate. She drew the figure standing in the hall among the lanterns, holding an adult hand.

Then she drew the adult. A woman. Tall in the way children draw tall — head nearly touching the top of the frame. Long hair. A dress with a torn hem, because Ada remembered the torn hem with unreasonable vividness; it had dragged when the woman walked, whispering on the white floor, *whish, whish*, and Ada had gripped it like a rope.

"Your mother," Wren said. Not a question. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they step onto ice.

Ada nodded. Then she shook her head. Then she made the gesture she'd invented long ago for *I don't know* — both palms up, tipping, like scales that couldn't settle. The woman had been her mother. The woman had also been someone else by then, someone smaller than she'd been before, and Ada hadn't understood that until much later, until Wren taught her what it meant when a person came back from somewhere with holes in their song.

She drew the next part fast, before her courage could fail.

The woman kneeling. The woman pressing something into another pair of hands — hands she drew coming out of shadow, because she couldn't remember the face they belonged to, only that they'd been cold. And between the woman's hands and the cold hands, she drew a small flame. A single lantern-light, cupped like a bird.

She held the slate up again.

Casper read it the way he read everything, slowly, his lips moving slightly, and Ada watched the understanding arrive in him like weather crossing a valley. "She paid," he said. "For passage out. For *your* passage out." His voice cracked on the last word, and his note went strange — grief and anger braided together. "She gave them your soul, and they let her carry you up the stairs."

Ada put the slate down. Her chest hurt. It always hurt in this part, in the place behind her breastbone where the hum lived, because the hum was the hole wearing a coat. She had spent six years telling herself the story the gentle way: Mama left me at the temple because she had no choice, Mama will come back. But the slate didn't allow gentle stories. The slate made you draw the cold hands. The slate made you draw the flame leaving.

She picked up the red chalk — the stub Wren had traded two eggs for — and drew the last panel.

The woman again, but alone now, walking away down the hall, and above her, among all the hundreds of lanterns, one new light burning where there had been none before. Ada drew the woman's face turned back over her shoulder. She drew tears coming off it in the way children draw tears, big and round and falling free. And then, because anger is honest even when longing isn't, she drew the woman's feet pointing toward the stairs that went *up*, away, while the little flame stayed behind in the dark.

She thrust the slate at them and crossed her arms, and her chin went up, and she stared at the fire because she would not stare at their faces while they read it.

*You left me there,* the drawing said. *You climbed out and you left my light burning in the dark.*

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The fire popped. Snow ticked against the broken shutter. Somewhere out on the mountainside a fox sang its thin ragged song, and Ada hated it a little, because it got to sing and she didn't.

It was Wren who moved first. Wren always moved first; it was the thing about her that Ada trusted most, that under all the drink-roughened weariness was a woman who moved toward trouble instead of away from it. She came around the fire and sat down beside Ada on the bench, close enough that their shoulders touched, and she didn't take the slate and she didn't ask questions. She just sat, solid and warm and smelling of woodsmoke and the rosemary oil she rubbed into her hands, and she let the silence be a place to stand instead of a place to drown.

When she finally spoke, her voice was rougher than usual. "My choristers," she said. "Eleven of them. I stood in a doorway very like that one, and I told them to run, and I stayed behind to hold the door." Her thumb traced the old burn scar along the edge of her palm, back and forth, back and forth. "Ten ran clear. One didn't. I have done the arithmetic of that night every day for nine years, child, and it never comes out any kinder."

Ada leaned into her shoulder. Just a little. Enough.

Because here was the thing she couldn't draw, the thing the chalk had no color for: she wasn't only angry. Anger was the loud part, the part that banged pots in her chest. Underneath the anger was the other thing, the shameful thing, the thing she poked at in the dark of her bunk at the temple when sleep wouldn't come. *Longing.* She wanted her mother. Not the mother of the drawing — the mother with the torn hem and the turned-back face — but the mother from before, the one from the earliest memories, the ones so old they might have been dreams: being carried high enough to see over a crowd. Warm bread held where she could smell it. A lullaby hummed against her hair, low and off-key and *hers*, the first music Ada had ever loved.

Was that woman still down there? Was she one of the lanterns now? Or was she walking those white halls with holes in her song, half-hollowed, paying piece by piece for something Ada couldn't imagine?

Ada wanted to find her. That was the truth under all the truths. She wanted it so badly it frightened her, because wanting it meant walking back into the hall with the lanterns, past the cold hands, down into the dark — and what if finding her mother meant finding out that the lullaby had been sold too? What if the woman who came up the stairs to meet her had gaps where the love used to be?

She took up the chalk one more time.

This time she drew slowly, deliberately, making each line count. She drew the court beneath the mountain — the arch, the stairs, the lanterns, all of it smaller now, pushed to the bottom corner of the slate. Above it she drew the mountain itself, and the road, and three small figures climbing it: one tall and bent (Wren), one lean and burdened (Casper), and one small figure between them, drawn with her chin up.

Then she drew the small figure's chest, and inside the chest, where everyone else carried a flame, she drew a knot. A tight, tangled scribble of lines, dense as a bird's nest, and from the nest a single thread running down — down through the drawing, down through the mountain, down to the smallest lantern of all, the one she marked with a tiny star.

She turned the slate to face them both.

"My soul," Casper said softly. "You're showing me where yours is." He looked at the tangled nest she'd drawn in her own chest, and then at her, and his eyes were wet in the firelight. "That's not emptiness, Ada. What you drew there. That's not a hole."

Ada frowned. She pointed at the nest. She made the gesture for *broken* — the one the priests had used, the one the foundling-mistress had used, the one she'd seen in a hundred pairs of eyes: fists pressed together, then pulled apart.

"No," Casper said, and his voice came out with a note in it she'd never heard from him before, a ringing note, a bell-note. He tapped the nest of lines with one finger. "This is a *grip*. You've been holding on. Six years, and you've held on so hard you learned to do without, and I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "On the road, at the ford. When I spent the memory to blind the hunters. I know what it costs to give a piece of yourself away. And you—" His voice did break then, properly. "You've been carrying the absence of the biggest piece there is, and you *walk*, and you *draw*, and you *love*, and I don't think you're broken at all. I think you're the strongest person I've ever met."

Wren made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half sob. "He's right, little one. Gods help me, he's right."

Ada looked down at the slate. At the nest she'd drawn. At the thread running down to the starred lantern.

Something in her chest shifted — not the hum, something deeper, something structural, like a beam settling in an old house. She had spent six years believing she was a room with a missing wall, wind blowing through her. But maybe. Maybe she was a fist. Maybe she was a grip that had never once let go.

She picked up the chalk and, beneath the three figures on the road, she wrote — in the laborious, beautiful letters Wren had taught her, chalk grinding, tongue between her teeth — the first full sentence she had ever written for anyone:

**WE GO DOWN. WE GO ALL THE WAY DOWN.**

And beneath it, after a moment's fierce deliberation, smaller:

**AND I TAKE IT BACK.**

Casper read it aloud, voice unsteady, and Wren read it, and then Ada held the slate up to the firelight so the flames could read it too, because the fire was her friend and friends should know your plans.

It was Wren who noticed it first — Wren, whose ear for song had trained royal choristers, whose frayed old melody suddenly went taut as a bowstring. She rose slowly from the bench, head cocked, listening to something beyond the walls, beyond the snow, beyond the ordinary world.

"Child," she said quietly. "When you drew that lantern. When you drew *your* lantern." Her eyes were wide and fixed on the dark doorway, on the mountain looming black against the stars beyond it. "Did you feel anything?"

Ada had. She hadn't wanted to say. All through the drawing, from the first white arch to the final starred flame, she had felt it: a pulling. Faint as a fisherman's line felt through a mile of water. Something down in the deep places had felt her attention land on it like a fingertip, and it had *turned over*.

And now, as she nodded, the pulling came again — stronger, unmistakable, a thread of resonance rising through sixty miles of stone and snow and silence, threading through the hut, through the fire, through the nest of lines behind her ribs.

Far below them, muffled by the whole sleeping weight of the mountain, something vast began — very softly — to sing back.

Casper was on his feet. "They know we're coming," he breathed.

Ada hugged the slate to her chest and looked at the dark doorway, at the road that led down into the white halls and the lanterns and the cold hands, and she was afraid — truly afraid, down in the marrow — but the fear had changed shape. It was no longer the fear of a foundling hiding in a salt cellar. It was the fear of a fist about to open.

She picked up the chalk and added one final mark to the slate: a tiny star, drawn beside the smallest lantern.

*Found you,* it said.
