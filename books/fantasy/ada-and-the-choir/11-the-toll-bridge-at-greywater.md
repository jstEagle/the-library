## 11. The Toll Bridge at Greywater

The river announced itself long before they saw it — a low, grinding roar beneath the snow, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Greywater did not freeze. Casper had heard that from three different smugglers in three different voices, and all of them had said it the same way men speak of things they'd rather not explain: the water ran too fast, too deep, too angry with the mountain's melt to ever close its eye. Which meant there was no ice road, no ford, no frozen crossing. There was only the bridge.

And whatever kept it.

They came down out of the high pines at dusk, when the light went the color of old pewter and the cold stopped biting and simply settled into the bones like a tenant. Ada rode on Wren's back, wrapped in the good wool blanket they'd bought in Marrowgate with the last of Brannoc's coin, her chin tucked against the choir mistress's shoulder. She had not slept properly in two days. None of them had. The whiteout was behind them now, but so was Wren's duel with Serah Vail — no, Casper corrected himself, forcing his thoughts down a safer road. Serah had let them go. Whatever else could be said of the crown's chief hunter, she had stood in the blinding snow with her song coiled and ready, looked at Ada's face, and stepped aside. What that meant — mercy, or a trap with a longer fuse — he could not yet say.

The bridge appeared all at once, the way things do when you've been dreading them: one moment grey trees, the next a black span of timber and iron reaching across the roaring water to the far bank, where the Thornfell road began its long climb in earnest. It was older than any bridge had a right to be. The wood was dark as bog-oak, silvered at the edges, and the iron fittings were worked into shapes Casper didn't recognize — spirals, mostly, and small open mouths.

At the near end stood a toll-house. It was less a building than an afterthought, a hut of stacked stone with a single window glowing amber against the dusk. Smoke rose from its chimney in a thin, obedient line.

"There'll be a fee," Wren said quietly. She set Ada down and rubbed feeling back into the girl's arms without seeming to think about it — a gesture so practiced, so automatic, that Casper felt something twist behind his ribs. "There's always a fee."

"Coin," Casper said. "Brannoc gave us—"

"Brannoc told you what the coin's for." Wren's face was half-shadow, but her voice carried the flat certainty of someone who had already done this arithmetic and hated the answer. "The ferryman on the Marrow takes coin. The gate-wardens at Vell take coin. This one doesn't." She nodded at the toll-house window, where a shape moved — slow, unhurried, patient as the river below. "Greywater trades in memories. Everyone north of the lowlands knows it, and everyone pretends they don't."

Casper looked at the bridge. He thought about the ledger in the temple archive, the neat columns of souls sold south, each entry in the abbot's careful hand. He thought about how easy it was to build a whole life on top of a thing like that, how many prayers you could copy by candlelight while the rot spread underneath. *Everyone pretends they don't.* Of course they did. Pretending was the mortar that held every comfortable wall together.

"Then we find another way across," he said.

"The river says no." Wren crouched to Ada's level, and Casper watched her take the girl's hands — both of them, warming them between her own. "We could go east to the falls, try the stepping stones. In summer, maybe. In midwinter, with the melt running—" She shook her head. "We don't have time for maybe. The gates close at midwinter, love. We have eleven days."

Ada pulled her hands free. Then, deliberately, she reached up and touched Wren's cheek — one finger, tracing the deep lines that exhaustion and grief and years of cheap wine had carved there — and shook her head.

"I know," Wren said softly. "I know you don't want me to. But you don't get a vote, little bird. Not on this."

*Little bird.* Casper had heard her use it a dozen times since Marrowgate, and every time, something in Ada straightened, the way a plant straightens toward light. He understood then — not fully, not yet, but well enough — that Wren had lost a choir once. That somewhere in the ashes of the Chapel Royal there were names she still said to herself at night, and that Ada had walked into that empty space inside her and simply taken up residence, uninvited and unrepentant, the way children do.

He also understood that Wren had already decided. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, in the way she was folding the blanket with unnecessary care, smoothing corners, making order — the things she did instead of weeping.

"Wren," he started.

"Don't." She stood. "Don't waste your breath arguing, scribe. Save it for the mountain." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "Besides. You don't even know what I'm going to give them yet."

She did, though. He could tell she did. And so, he suspected, could Ada.

* * *

The keeper of the toll was a woman, or had been once. She sat in the doorway of her stone hut in a chair made of antler and driftwood, wrapped in furs gone grey as her hair, and she watched them approach with eyes the exact color of the river — that restless, depthless grey-green that never quite finished moving. She was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years. Casper had seen it before, in the oldest monks at Thornfell: a person worn smooth, like a stone in a current, until nothing remained but the shape the water preferred.

"You'll want to cross," she said. Her voice was surprisingly young. That was worse, somehow.

"We would," Wren said.

"Then you'll want to know my prices." The keeper smiled, and her teeth were very white and very even, like piano keys. "Not coin. Coin I have no use for — you can't eat it, can't burn it warm, and it doesn't keep the dreams away." She gestured, and Casper saw, for the first time, the interior of the hut through the open door. Every surface was covered in small objects: river stones, buttons, birds' nests, keys, a child's shoe, a lock of hair tied with ribbon. Thousands of them, arranged with obsessive care on shelves of driftwood. "I keep what people give me. That's the trade. A memory, freely given, freely kept. I never take. Remember that, if anyone asks. I never take."

"What are your prices?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. Too steady.

"That depends on what you're carrying." The keeper's gaze slid from Wren to Casper, lingered, and moved on to Ada with something that might have been tenderness, or hunger, or both. "The big ones cost more. First loves. Mothers' faces. The day your children were born. The last time you were happy, if you were ever truly happy at all." She folded her hands in her lap. "Small memories are cheap. The taste of a particular soup. A stranger's name. The color of a door you walked past once. But those won't get you across my bridge in winter, I'm afraid. Winter crossings need winter prices."

"How much?" Casper asked.

"For the three of you?" The keeper tilted her head, listening to some sound beneath the river's roar — or perhaps she was the river's roar, wearing a body the way other people wore coats. "One memory. One true one, given whole, held back nothing. From one of you. The others cross free." Her eyes came back to Wren, and stayed there, and Casper realized with a lurch that the keeper had known from the moment they walked out of the trees exactly who would pay. "You may choose who. I'm told that's a kindness. I've never been sure."

Silence, except for the water.

Casper stepped forward. "Take mine."

"No," Wren said.

"You don't know what she'll ask for—"

"She'll ask for what hurts most. They always do." Wren didn't look at him. She was looking at the shelves through the doorway — at the child's shoe, the lock of hair, the thousand small surrendered things. "That's the trick of it, isn't it? Freely given means you choose the wound yourself. That's why it costs. Anyone can bleed when they're cut. Choosing where to cut — that's the price."

"Then choose something small," Casper said desperately. "Choose a soup, a door, a—"

"And the bridge will know." For the first time, the keeper sounded tired. "The bridge always knows. I've had clever ones try to pay me in trifles and call it sacrifice. The span drops them in the river. Greywater keeps what it keeps." She shrugged, fur rippling. "I don't make the rules. I only keep the accounts."

Wren laughed then — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all. "Accounts," she said. "Of course. Everyone keeps accounts." She turned to Casper, and her expression gentled into something he hadn't seen on her before: not pity, not gratitude, but recognition — the look of one survivor acknowledging another. "Scribe. Listen to me. I've spent twenty years drinking myself toward forgetting that concert anyway. At least this way, the forgetting buys something."

"What concert?" Ada asked — no. Ada couldn't ask. Ada signed it, quick and sharp, her small hands cutting the air: *What?*

Wren knelt. She took Ada's face in her weathered hands, and Casper looked away, because some griefs are performed in private even when they happen in public.

"When I was young," Wren said, "I conducted the Chapel Royal. Forty voices. Children, mostly — foundlings and choristers, gifted ones, the ones who could sing the way you sing. On the last night — the real last night, before the fire, before everything — we performed the Lamentation of the White Vale. All forty of us, and the chapel acoustics were built for it, stone throat and stone lung, and for one hour the music was so beautiful that the king wept, and I remember thinking: *this is the most beautiful thing anyone has ever made, and I am standing inside it.*" Her voice caught. Held. "That memory is the last beautiful thing I own, little bird. Everything after is ash and wine. If I have to lose something, better it be that than you."

Ada's hands moved, fast and furious: *Keep it. Keep it, keep it.*

"Can't." Wren caught the flying fingers and pressed them still. "Listen. Memories aren't the person. I thought they were, for years — that's why I drank, trying to drown the ones that hurt. But they're not. They're just... rooms the person lived in. Burn a room down, the house still stands." She smiled, and it was terrible and brave and entirely genuine. "And besides. I'll write it down first. Scribe here can copy it out fair, can't you? Every detail. Forty voices, the king crying into his beard, the smell of the incense. So it's not gone. Just... moved."

"It won't be the same," Casper said quietly.

"No," Wren agreed. "It won't. Nothing ever is. That's rather the point of paying, isn't it?" She stood, and squared her shoulders, and Casper saw her become, in that moment, the woman she must have been at thirty — the choir mistress, the one who stood in front of forty children and refused, absolutely refused, to send them below the mountain. "Right. Keeper. Let's do it before I lose my nerve, which I assure you is a distinct possibility."

* * *

It was quick, in the end. That was the worst part — how quick.

The keeper rose from her chair with a grace that didn't match her body, and led Wren to a flat stone before the toll-house door, worn smooth as a table. "Sit," she said, "and give it to me whole. Don't ration it. Don't hold back the corners. A memory given in pieces rots, and rots in *you*, not me. Whole, or not at all."

Wren sat. She closed her eyes. And Casper, watching, saw something extraordinary happen: her face filled with light. Not actual light — the dusk was thickening around them, the first fat flakes of evening snow beginning to fall — but an inner radiance, the look of a woman hearing forty children breathe in together before the downbeat. Her lips parted. Her hands rose slightly, unconsciously, into the shape of a conductor's gesture.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it's starting. The candles. Four hundred of them, and the king in the third pew, and Tomas is flat on the opening phrase, sweet boy, always flat, and nobody minds, nobody has ever minded, because the chord underneath him is so wide, so wide, it holds even his mistakes like a river holds a leaf—"

"Give it," the keeper whispered. "All of it. Now, while it's whole."

Wren exhaled — a long, slow breath, the kind singers are taught to take, the kind that empties the lungs completely — and Casper watched the light go out of her face the way a tide goes out of a pool. It didn't hurt to watch, exactly. It was worse than hurting. It was watching someone set down something precious on a high shelf, turn away, and immediately begin to forget which shelf it was.

When Wren opened her eyes, they were wet, and puzzled, and empty in a specific place.

"Well," she said. Her voice sounded fine. That was somehow the most horrifying part. "Cold, isn't it? Shall we cross?"

In the keeper's hand was a small object: a candle stub, no longer than a thumb, wax gone soft and yellow with age. She placed it on her shelf of ten thousand treasures, between a wedding ring and a sparrow's egg, and adjusted it twice until it sat exactly right.

"Cross," the keeper said. "The bridge is paid."

* * *

The span took them single file, the planks groaning underfoot, the river screaming below through gaps wide enough to swallow a child. Halfway across, Wren stopped walking.

"I've forgotten something," she said. Not frightened — puzzled, the way you puzzle over a word on the tip of your tongue. "Something important. I can feel the hole where it was. Like a tooth." She frowned at the churning water. "Did I have a choir? I know I did. I know the shape of it. But when I reach for the middle of it — the good part, the part that mattered — there's just..." She made a vague gesture. "Snow. Just snow."

Casper's hand found hers in the dark. "You had a choir," he said. "Forty voices. And you were magnificent, and you saved them as long as you could, and then you saved us. That's the middle of it. That's the part that mattered."

Wren looked at their joined hands for a long moment. Then she squeezed, hard, and laughed — a real laugh this time, rough and startled out of her.

"A family," she said wonderingly. "Look at that. Three nobodies on a bridge, holding hands in a snowstorm. My mother would be appalled." She wiped her eyes with her free hand, briskly, businesslike. "Come on, then. Mountain's waiting, and I find I'm impatient to hear this silent song of hers. If the girl can sing the way I suspect she can, we may yet give the Pale Cantor a concert he won't forget."

Ada, walking ahead of them both with her hood up and her spine straight, turned briefly and signed one-handed, without breaking stride:

*Family.*

Then the far bank rose to meet them, and the Thornfell road began its climb, and behind them the toll-house window glowed amber over its ten thousand surrendered things — among them, on the highest shelf, a candle stub that no one would ever burn again.

They made camp that night in the lee of the first true crags, and it was there, watching Ada sketch the bridge by firelight — adding, with great care, a fourth figure to the crossing, small and hooded, holding both their hands — that Casper saw the light of another fire far above them on the mountain road.

Two fires. Moving. Descending toward them through the snow.

Riders.
