## 12. Hunters in the Snow

The snow came down like a verdict.

Wren had weathered storms before — she had sung through a gale in the chapel Royal's bell tower once, back when her voice could still bend weather instead of merely surviving it — but this was different. This was not weather. This was a whiteness with intent, a blindness rolled down off the peaks of Thornfell to swallow the road whole. She could not see Casper three paces ahead. She could not see Ada at all, only the small dark shape of the girl's hand gripping the back of Casper's coat, and even that kept flickering out of existence like a candle guttering.

"Keep the rope taut," she called, and heard her own voice come back at her flattened and strange, as if the snow were eating the edges off every sound. "If it goes slack, stop. If it pulls hard, run."

Casper turned his head just enough for her to catch the pale smudge of his face. "Run where?"

"That," said Wren, "is an excellent question I'll answer when we're not all about to die."

They had made the mistake of trusting the road. The smuggler paths Casper knew so well in fair weather had become treacheries under a foot of fresh powder; twice already they had stepped where the mountain fell away and been saved only by the rope and by Casper's uncanny memory of the ground beneath them. The third time there would be no memory to save them. Wren could feel it in her knees, in the ache of old frostbite scars from a winter she did not like to remember, in the way the wind had begun to moan against the rock face above them — a long, hollow note, almost musical, almost like a warning.

Or like a summons.

She stopped walking. The rope went slack behind her, then taut, then slack again as Casper felt his way back toward her voice.

"Wren?"

"Hush." She tilted her head. Beneath the wind's moaning there was something else now, something deliberate: a thread of sound woven through the storm, high and clean and cold as a needle. She knew that thread. She had taught that thread, years ago, to a girl with grey eyes who learned faster than anyone Wren had ever trained and who threw it all away — no. Not threw away. Sold it. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of what remained of Wren's heart.

"She's found us," Wren said.

"Who?" But Casper knew. She saw it land on him even through the whiteout, saw his shoulders draw up around his ears like a man bracing for a blow. "Vail? Here? How—"

"Because she's gifted, boy, and the storm is half hers. You don't hunt soul-song in a blizzard unless you can sing one." Wren unwound the rope from her waist with fingers gone clumsy inside their mittens. "Listen to me now, because we will not have time for this later. The path forks ahead — you told me so yourself, two days past. Left goes up along the ridge to Brannoc's cave. Right goes down into the valley and the hunters' camp."

"I know the fork."

"Take the left. Take Ada. Do not wait for me, do not call for me, do not be noble, because nobility is how gifted people die." She pressed the rope's end into his hands and folded his fingers over it, holding on a moment longer than she needed to, feeling the tremor in him and hating that she could not spare him this. "You are the map, Casper Thackeray. Maps are worth more than choir mistresses. Get her to the court."

"And you?"

Wren smiled, and hoped the snow hid how much effort the smile cost. "I'm going to have a word with an old student."

* * *

Serah Vail came out of the whiteout the way a blade comes out of a sheath — sudden, silent, inevitable.

One moment the slope above Wren was nothing but swirling death; the next, a figure stood upon it, dark-cloaked, hood rimed with frost, and the storm bent around her in a slow spiral, as though the wind itself had been ordered to make room. Wren's breath caught. Not from fear. From recognition. That stance — weight low, chin lifted, hands loose at the sides — she had drilled it into forty children over eleven years. She had drilled it into this woman most of all.

"You always did stand too wide," Wren called. Her voice carried strangely well through the storm, and she understood why a heartbeat later: Vail was carrying it, lending the wind her own gift so that the words would arrive intact. A courtesy. Or a taunt. With Serah it had never been possible to tell the two apart.

"Mistress Fairweather." The name came back across the snow without warmth. "Eleven years, and you still haven't learned to run quietly."

"Eleven years, and you still haven't learned to walk away." Wren moved as she spoke, angling left, putting a shelf of rock between them. It bought her seconds, nothing more. "I taught you better than this, Serah. Chasing children through mountains for men who eat songs."

"They don't eat songs." Vail descended the slope with terrible economy, each step placed as if the mountain had agreed in advance to hold her. "That's temple gossip. Court gossip. They keep them. They keep them safe, and warm, and the ones who surrender willingly live comfortable lives. I've seen the vaults."

"You've seen the lanterns." Wren let the word hang. She had heard Brannoc say it, had heard the old smuggler spit into the fire as he described the hanging lights of the court beneath, each one flickering with somebody's stolen life. "You looked at a child's soul hanging on a hook and called it comfortable."

Something moved behind Vail's eyes — quick, quickly buried. Good, thought Wren. There's still a person under all that crown-issue wool. A person can be reached. A person can hesitate. And hesitation, in a duel of song, is a wound.

"You're under arrest," Vail said, and her hand came up, and she began to hum.

Wren felt it strike her like cold water poured down the spine. Of course. Of course Vail opened with that — the nulling note, the dampener, the first thing the crown taught its gifted hunters: how to silence another singer before the fight begins. Wren's own gift stuttered in her chest like a lamp in a draft. For one horrible instant she was fifteen again, standing in the chapel Royal's audition room while some grey functionary wrote *insufficient discipline* beside her name.

No. She was fifty-one, and she was angry, and anger was fuel if you knew how to burn it.

She did not fight the dampening note. She joined it. She matched Vail's pitch exactly, folded her own voice beneath it, and held there — two singers producing one flat, dead tone between them, canceling each other out, the storm roaring back in to fill the silence. And in that roar, Wren moved. Three steps right, boots whispering on powder, circling toward the rock shelf.

Vail's head turned. Tracking by ear. Clever girl. But Wren had spent eleven years teaching clever girls, and she knew every trick she'd ever taught, plus four she'd invented since and never written down.

She sang the stone.

It was an old technique, older than the chapel Royal, older than the crown — a deep fundamental pitched to the grain of the rock itself, the kind of note that made masonry shiver and snowfields think about becoming avalanches. She didn't push it far. She didn't need far. She needed the shelf of rock between them to groan, and shed its burden of snow, and it did: a soft white slither, a curtain of powder collapsing between hunter and hunted, and in the blind second it bought, Wren ran.

Not away. Up. Toward the ridge, toward the fork, toward the place where she could hear — faint, frantic, human — the sound of Casper shouting Ada's name against the wind.

They hadn't gone. Of course they hadn't gone. She crested the shelf and there they were at the fork, roped together, Casper pointing desperately up-trail while Ada stood planted like a fencepost, shaking her head, pointing back down the slope toward Wren. The girl's face was furious and terrified and absolutely immovable, and Wren thought, with a love that hurt worse than the cold: *oh, you magnificent stubborn creature, you'd have made a fine chorister.*

"GO!" she screamed, and put everything into it — not volume but command, the old choir-mistress note that made forty children sit up straighter in an instant, that had once made a visiting bishop apologize for being late. "UP! NOW!"

And because the gift was in it, they went. Casper grabbed Ada's hand and hauled her onto the left-hand trail, and the last Wren saw of them was two dark shapes swallowed by the climbing white, and Ada looking back, always looking back, until the snow took even that.

Then a hand closed on Wren's collar, and the world spun, and she was on her knees in the drift with Serah Vail's knee between her shoulder blades and the dampening note humming directly against the back of her skull.

"Foolish," Vail breathed. "Foolish, wasteful old woman. You could have walked away in Marrowgate. You could have stayed drunk and anonymous and alive."

"Yes," Wren gasped, into the snow. "But then who'd have taught you humility?"

She got one arm free. She got one note free — a small one, cheap, costing her the memory of her mother's kitchen, the smell of bread, gone, spent, worth it — and she drove it upward through the point of contact, a spike of pure resonance that made Vail flinch and lose her grip for exactly one heartbeat.

In that heartbeat, Wren did the last thing she had strength for. She did not attack. She sang *downward*, into the mountain, into the deep grain of Thornfell itself, a message more than a weapon, a thread of sound aimed at the two people scrambling up the ridge: *the cave, Brannoc's cave, the resonant stone, go where the mountain listens.* She had no idea if they'd hear it. She had no idea if the gift carried meaning or only feeling. She only knew that mothers and mistresses send their children ahead into the dark with whatever light remains.

Then Vail recovered, and bound her wrists with frost-stiff cord, and the storm closed over everything.

* * *

They sat her on a boulder at the treeline while the hunters' camp assembled itself out of the whiteness — six riders, seven, faces raw with cold, horses steaming like kettles. Someone built a fire with hands that shook. Someone else brought Vail a flask, which she accepted and did not drink.

Wren flexed her bound fingers and counted her losses the way she counted everything: like a ledger. One dampening hold, survived. One memory of bread and her mother's voice saying grace, spent. One pair of students, escaped upward, unaccounted for. Net position: acceptable. She had lost choirs before. She had lost a chapel, a career, a country's respect, and the use of her own name in polite society. Losing her freedom was, by the arithmetic of her life, nearly a rest day.

What was not acceptable was the taste the victory left in her mouth. Because Vail had hesitated. Twice. Once at the mention of the lanterns, and once — Wren had felt it through the contact of the dampening note, singer to singer, skin of sound to skin of sound — once when Wren had sung the message downward, when the shape of it, the *maternal* shape of it, had passed between them unbidden. Serah Vail had felt what Wren was doing and why, and for the length of a single held tone she had not wanted to stop her.

That was the crack in the ice. Wren had spent a lifetime finding cracks in voices. She could work with a crack.

"So," she said pleasantly, as Vail crouched in front of her to check the bindings. "The vaults. Tell me about the vaults again. The part where you looked at them and decided they were kindness."

"Don't."

"Because I've been thinking about it, sitting here freezing, and I can't make the sums work. You were the best ear I ever trained. Better than me, God help me. You could hear a lie in a hymn from the back pew. So either you genuinely believe souls hang comfortably on hooks—" she watched the flinch, catalogued it, pressed on— "or you've spent eleven years not listening. Which is it, Serah? Deafness or dishonesty?"

Vail stood. For a moment she said nothing, and the fire crackled, and somewhere above them the storm went on grinding the mountain's bones.

"There's a thing below the mountain," she said at last, very quietly, as if the words cost her. "Older than the court. Hungry. The Cantor holds it back. Every soul he takes buys the rest of us time. That's what they told me when I swore. That's what I've told myself every night since." Her grey eyes came down to meet Wren's, and for one unguarded instant they were nineteen years old again and terrified of disappointing her. "Tell me it isn't true, Mistress. Tell me what you know. And I'll speak for the boy and the girl. Let them run. I'll report them lost in the whiteout."

Wren stared up at her, and felt the trap close around her own heart, because she did not know. She knew the Cantor was a thief and a monster; she knew the temple sold children like Ada; but whether the theft fed a god or merely a tyrant's hunger — that she could not swear to. And Vail would read the uncertainty in her face as easily as Wren had once read it in forty young voices.

"You want absolution," Wren said slowly. "From me. After everything."

"I want it to have meant something."

"It never does," Wren said. "That's the lesson, Serah. It never means anything until someone makes it mean something. And you—" she nodded at the ropes, at the fire, at the whole frozen machinery of the hunt— "you've just made your choice, and it wasn't the making-something kind."

Vail's face closed like a door. She turned away and gave the order in a voice scraped flat: break camp, move at dawn, prisoners bound, the ridge trail swept for tracks. Two hunters dragged Wren to a tent and left her alone with a blanket that smelled of horse and a headache where the dampening note had bruised her gift.

She lay in the dark and did the only thing left to do. She listened. Past the wind, past the snores of hired men, down into the deep grain of Thornfell where she had sent her last thread of song — listening for an answer, for any sign that the mountain had heard her, that two small figures had reached the cave of resonant stone, that somewhere above her the map and the mute girl were still moving north.

And there — faint, so faint she might have dreamed it — an answer rose. Not Casper's voice. Not any voice at all. A harmony with no sound in it, a chord she felt in her sternum rather than heard, threading up through the rock beneath her prison, through the frozen ground, through the floor of the tent: pure, patient, and utterly unbought.

Ada was singing. Silently. And the mountain was singing back.

Wren pressed her bound hands to her breastbone and laughed until the tears froze on her face — grief and pride and hope in one ugly, glorious chord — because whatever waited for them below the mountain, whatever the Pale Cantor believed about the price of souls, he had made one catastrophic error in all his centuries of theft.

He had let this child live long enough to learn that some songs cannot be taken.

Dawn would bring interrogation, and shackles, and the long road down. Wren closed her eyes and began, silently, in the dark, to practice.
