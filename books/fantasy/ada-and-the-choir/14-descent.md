## 14. Descent

The crack in the rock was barely wide enough for a child. Ada went first, folding herself into the dark the way water folds into stone, and Casper followed because stopping was worse than going.

For a long time there was only the passage — a throat of granite that swallowed their footsteps and gave nothing back. Wren had gone silent an hour ago, or a day; time moved strangely in the Cave of First Notes, and stranger still beyond it, where the mountain's hum no longer harmonized with them but *contained* them, note by note, as though each of them had been written into the stone long before they were born and were only now catching up to the music.

Casper kept one hand on the wall and one on Ada's shoulder. She did not flinch from his touch. That small fact steadied him more than any prayer he had ever copied: the child who could not speak let him speak for her in touch, and answered in pressure, her fingers tightening twice for *yes*, once for *no*, a rhythm they had built between them out of necessity and long roads.

The passage narrowed, then opened without warning, like a held breath released.

He had expected darkness. What he found was light.

The court beneath the mountain opened beneath and around them, a cathedral hollowed from the world's roots, so vast that his eyes failed before his courage did. The ceiling was lost in a haze of glimmering distance. Pillars rose from a floor of polished black stone — not carved, he understood after a moment, but *sung*; the rock itself had been shaped into flutes and arches by voices that had died centuries before anyone now living drew breath. And hanging in the air between those pillars, suspended at every height, at every distance, filling the whole impossible vault of the cavern —

Souls.

They hung like lanterns. There was no other word his mind would permit, though every other word it offered was worse. Each one was a soft radiance the color of a life: warm amber, pale gold, a green like spring water, one far off that burned a deep and wounded violet. They swayed slightly, though there was no wind. They flickered. And when Casper looked closely at the nearest — close enough to see that within the glow something moved, something turned over in its sleep like a sleeper dreaming — he felt the flicker answer him.

It knew he was looking. It *leaned* toward him, the way a candle leans toward a window left open.

"Merciful gods," Wren breathed behind him. Her voice came out stripped of everything she usually wrapped it in — the irony, the gravel, the drink-roughened defiance. What remained was very old and very young at once. "I sang in a chapel my whole life. I thought I knew what worship was." She swallowed. "This is a granary."

Ada made a sound — not a sound; her hands flew up to her own chest and pressed there, flat, hard, as if holding something in or keeping something out. Her eyes were enormous. In the light of ten thousand stolen souls her face was silver and her silence louder than the cavern itself.

*I know some of them,* her hands said, though she had no formal sign for it yet, only the urgent grammar of fingers and face that Casper had learned to read the way he once read ledgers. *Some of these lights. I know them.*

He took her hand. "Then we'll find yours," he said, and heard his own voice shake, and hated it, and went on anyway. "We find the ledger first. Everything here will be written down. Men who do this kind of work—" he gestured at the hanging lights, the pillars, the vast patient dark— "always write it down."

*Why?*

"Because writing makes it seem permitted."

* * *

They descended along a gallery that followed the cavern wall, a road of black stone worn smooth in its center by generations of feet that had come this way carrying things that could not walk themselves. Casper counted pillars to keep himself from counting souls, and failed at both. Three hundred. Six hundred. He stopped when the numbers stopped meaning anything — when the count grew so large that it ceased to be arithmetic and became geography, became weather, became simply *the world down here*, the way stars are simply the sky.

Each lantern had a name. He was certain of it. Not marked anywhere, not labeled, but named nonetheless, in the way that fire is named by what it burns. As he passed, the nearest lights stirred faintly toward him, and he understood with a horror that sat in his stomach like swallowed stone that they were not sleeping. They were waiting. They had been waiting, some of them, since before the temple above was built. Since before Thornfell had a name.

His father's debt had bought candles. That was the ledger Casper had copied for eleven years — tallow, wax, ink, salt, the small economies of a mountain priory, each entry signed in his own careful hand. He had thought himself a man who kept accounts of harmless things.

Now he walked through the true account, and every entry hung in the air above his head, glowing.

Wren touched his elbow. Ahead, the gallery ended at a terrace overlooking a lower floor: an archive. Of course it was an archive. It looked, absurdly, horribly, like the scriptorium he had left behind — long tables, lamps of cold blue flame that needed no oil, shelves rising into shadow, and scribes. A dozen of them, perhaps more, moving between the shelves with the unhurried diligence of men doing honest work, quills whispering, pages turning. The sound carried up to the terrace: the soft rustle of record-keeping, the most ordinary sound in the world, transposed here into the key of nightmare.

"They're clerks," Casper said. His voice sounded foreign to him. "They're just — clerks."

"Most atrocities are staffed," Wren said grimly.

Ada tugged his sleeve. She pointed not at the archive but past it, to where the floor of the great cavern dropped away entirely and the soul-lanterns gathered thickest — a grove of light around a dais of white stone, empty at present, ringed with instruments he could not name and did not want to. Above the dais, suspended alone at a height no ladder could reach and no chain could explain, hung a single lantern larger than all the others, burning a white so pure it hurt to look at, pulsing slowly, like a heart at rest.

Even from this distance, Casper felt it notice them. Felt it *want*.

"That one," Wren whispered. "That's recent."

"How can you tell?"

"Because it hasn't learned to stop hoping yet." Her jaw tightened. "The old ones sway. That one reaches."

Ada was already moving. She had seen something on the terrace below — a lectern apart from the scribes' tables, set against the near wall, holding a single volume. Even at this distance Casper recognized the binding: vellum over boards, corners capped in dull silver, exactly like the second ledger he had found in the temple archive, the one that had started everything. The mother book. Every corrupt ledger has an original, and originals are always kept where the money is.

They went down. There was no other direction that mattered anymore.

* * *

Getting past the scribes was absurdly easy, and that ease frightened Casper more than resistance would have. They were absorbed. Two of them glanced up as the three strangers crossed the archive floor, registered nothing worth recording, and returned to their work. One yawned. Somewhere a lamp hissed. The whole machinery of theft ran on the greased wheels of tedium.

The lectern stood in an alcove cut deep into the rock, and the alcove was cold — a cold with intention to it, the cold of a strongroom, of a thing guarded less by walls than by the certainty that no one would dare. The book lay open. It had always been open, Casper understood; it was never meant to be closed, because closing it would be an admission that it could end.

The hand was beautiful. That was the first obscenity. Whoever kept this ledger wrote a fine, disciplined secretary hand, flourished capitals, rubricated entries in red that matched the red of the souls' deepest flicker. Casper's eye — trained by eleven years of copying prayers, trained to read fast and read true — ran down the columns before his conscience could object.

*Fenwick, Marek, of Marrowgate. Taken age nine. Purchased by the House of Ashen, price: two winters of memory, paid forward.*

*Tull, the twins, of Greywater. Taken together. Sold separately.*

*Vail, Serah. Surrendered freely, conditionally. See covenant, folio 300.*

He stopped at that one. Read it again. Filed it away in the part of his mind that still worked like a clerk, because that clerk was the only part of him not screaming.

Ada's hand closed on his wrist. Not pulling. Holding. She was watching him read, and her face was composed with an effort that broke his heart — the composure of someone bracing for a door to open onto either rescue or ruin.

He turned back a page. Then another. The entries marched backward through years, decades. He found the temple's mark — the little spiral that stood for Thornfell priory — appearing again and again, regular as harvest. Found his abbot's predecessor's mark. His predecessor's predecessor's. The trail ran back past the edge of the readable, past the founding of the kingdom, into entries kept in a script older than any he had been taught, and still, page after page, the same trade, the same calm prices, the same beautiful hand or its ancestors.

"Here." Wren's voice, very low. She was pointing at a column header he had almost missed: *Held in reserve. Personal acquisitions of the Cantor.*

The personal acquisitions ran to four entries in forty years. Four souls too precious to sell, kept the way a miser keeps one coin unworn.

The third entry was Ada.

Not *Ada*. Of course not Ada. Ledger names are the names of things priced, and things priced are stripped to their essentials:

*Foundling, female, approx. age two, recovered at the Marrowgate ford. Gift present — unusual form, see annotation. Soul extracted and held. Vessel released above per custom.*

*Annotation:* the hand here changed, became larger, slower, a hand used to being obeyed. *The silent song. First confirmed instance in three generations. Do not sell. Do not spend. The Cantor claims this one personally.*

Below it, in the same commanding script, a single line that Casper read four times because his mind refused to accept it on three:

*Note the bloodline. The vessel is collateral. So is the scribe.*

"The scribe," Casper said. His voice came out flat, toneless, the voice of a man reading his own death warrant in someone else's beautiful handwriting. "It says the scribe."

Ada looked up at him. Then at the entry. Then, slowly, her hand rose and touched his cheek — not a question this time, just touch, just *here, I am here, whatever this means* — and Casper found that he could not answer her, because he did not know.

Collateral for what? Against whom? His father's debts were candle-debts, salt-debts, the small coin of a small life. Unless they had never been. Unless the temple had held his indenture all these years not for what he owed but for what he *was* — for the gift he had only just learned he carried, bred quietly in his blood like a fuse laid through generations, waiting for someone to want it lit.

The Cantor claimed Ada personally. The ledger bound Casper to her entry. Which meant that everything — the hunt, the proclamation, the temple's eager compliance, the road that had seemed like escape — had perhaps never been pursuit at all, but *herding*.

"We weren't running," he said. The words tasted of ash. "We were being delivered."

Wren's face had gone the color of the cold flame. "Then we stop walking where they push us."

Above the archive floor, far across the cavern, the great white lantern pulsed — once, twice — and every lesser soul in that vast cathedral of theft turned toward it like flowers toward a sun, and dimmed in deference. The light reached the alcove where they stood, washed over the open ledger, over Ada's entry, over the words *the Cantor claims this one personally*, and in that light Casper heard — not with his ears, but with the place behind his sternum where his gift lived — a voice begin, somewhere deep in the court, to sing.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard. It was coming for them.
