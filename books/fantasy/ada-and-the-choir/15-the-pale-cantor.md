## 15. The Pale Cantor

He felt them before the doors opened.

The Pale Cantor had been listening to his hoard — as he did every hour of every day, though he no longer remembered when the habit began, or whether there had ever been a first time. The vaults beneath Thornfell held four thousand and some souls, each one hung in its niche like a lantern of blown glass, each one flickering with the shape of the life it had once been. He knew them all by their light. A farmer's soul burned steady and amber, patient as a field under snow. A child's flickered quick and silver, always on the edge of laughter or tears. A soldier's guttered even in stillness, dreaming of smoke.

And tonight, three small lights were climbing toward him through the mountain's throat, and one of them sang without singing.

The Cantor set down the tuning fork he no longer needed — his own voice had long since become the only instrument in the court — and rose from the obsidian bench where he conducted his endless, silent inventory. Around him the great cathedral hall breathed with the sound of hanging souls: a shimmer, a susurrus, like wind through a forest of glass leaves. It was the most beautiful thing in the world. He had made it so. Or it had made him so; after this many centuries, the distinction had worn away like an inscription on a stair.

"Open the doors," he said, and his voice moved through the stone the way water moves through roots.

* * *

Casper Thackeray came through the gates with a child's hand gripped in his and a woman's silence at his back, and the Cantor read him in a single glance the way a moneylender reads a ledger. Younger than his weariness. A scribe's posture — shoulders braced for correction. Hands that had copied ten thousand prayers and believed, until very recently, that they meant something. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable now that the man stood inside the court's resonance: the gift. Raw, untrained, spending itself carelessly with every heartbeat, a purse full of coin leaking through a torn seam.

The child was more interesting.

Ada. The Cantor had her entry by heart — Entry 3,912, purchased at birth from a mother who had wept exactly as long as the transaction required and not a moment longer, because grief beyond the terms of sale was a luxury the poor learn early to forgo. Her soul hung in the eastern vaults, third row, a small fierce light the color of dawn through ice. He had listened to it often. Children's souls were the sweetest in the hoard, and hers was sweetest of all the children's — not because she was innocent, innocence being common, but because she had been *taken* rather than given, and taken things burned brighter. Resentment was a fine preservative.

And yet here she walked, soulless and unbroken, humming through her chest a note that made the nearest lanterns lean toward her like flowers toward sun. Singing without spending. The Cantor had seen perhaps five such beings in all his long stewardship, and he had eaten two of them himself, and the memory of those meals was the closest thing he had left to regret.

"Welcome," he said, and let courtesy pour through the words like honey through a cracked jar. "You have come far, and cold, and frightened. None of that need continue."

Wren — he knew her too, of course; Wren of the Broken Choir, whose refusal had cost her everything except the one thing she'd have traded gladly — Wren stepped slightly in front of the child, an old habit wearing new clothes. The Cantor inclined his head to her with genuine respect. Enemies who chose their ruin deserved that much.

"You are the Pale Cantor," Casper said. His voice held steady. Good. Steady men bargained better, and the Cantor liked a bargain that took effort.

"I am the steward of this court," the Cantor said. "I keep what is kept here. I feed what must be fed." He gestured upward, and the whole vast dark answered — thousands of lights swaying gently on their invisible stems, casting a glow that had no source and no shadow. "You have seen my collection. I imagine you found it terrible."

"It is theft," Wren said flatly.

"It is *keeping*," the Cantor corrected, gently, the way one corrects a beloved student. "There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of my office. Come. Walk with me. I will show you why the theft, if theft it is, is also mercy — and then we will discuss what you came for, because you did not crawl up my mountain in midwinter out of moral outrage. Outrage does not climb."

* * *

He led them along the gallery of souls, and he watched them look, and he let them look.

This was part of the ritual, and the ritual mattered. Terror closed minds; beauty opened them, and a bargain struck in beauty held better than one struck in fear. So he walked them slowly past the niches while the hoard murmured its glass-leaf music overhead, and he named what they saw with the tenderness of a curator.

"The miller of Hollowbrook. Sold by his sons, who could not afford his coughing. He burns warm. They visit him sometimes — the living can hear their dead here, if they stand close. Few do. Grief is heavy luggage." He paused before a lantern the color of deep water. "A queen's fool. Bought by the queen herself, who could not bear that anyone made her laugh harder than she laughed alone. He is still funny, in there. I listen to him on bad nights."

"Stop," Wren said quietly.

"No," the Cantor said, with real gentleness. "You need to see it whole. That has always been your failure, Wren. You saw the taking and never the keeping. You called my work butchery from the safety of a chapel built on tithes extracted at spearpoint, and when the crown asked you to send children down to me, you refused — which was brave, and futile, and changed nothing except the color of your reputation. One of your choristers came below anyway. Do you remember? Little Tam, with the cracked voice. He hangs in the western vaults. He sings still. Would you like to hear him?"

Wren's face did something complicated and terrible. The Cantor noted it, filed it, and did not press. Cruelty was a tool for amateurs; he preferred leverage, and leverage worked best polished bright.

They walked on. And it was Casper he stopped for, at last, before a niche in the older rows — one of the deep ones, where the light had gone soft and brownish with age, like paper left too long in sun.

"This one," the Cantor said, "has waited a long time for a visitor."

Casper looked up at the dim lantern, and the Cantor watched the recognition move through him slowly, the way frost moves across a window — not all at once, but cell by cell, from the edges inward.

"She would be thirty-one now," the Cantor said. "Sold forty years ago — no. Forgive me. Twenty-six years ago, when she was five. The transaction is recorded in your temple's second ledger, page one hundred and twelve. The seller's name is familiar to you. Thackeray. Your father sold his daughter's soul to pay a debt of eleven crowns and a cart-horse."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the hoard seemed to hush around it, the lanterns swaying closer, drawn — as all light is drawn — to grief.

"That's not true," Casper said. His voice had gone thin.

"It is entirely true, and you have always known it was possible. Why else do you think a man like your father indentured his son to a temple rather than sell him outright?" The Cantor let the question hang, delicate as a hook baited with truth. "He kept you because you were collateral that appreciated. He sold her because she was surplus. This is not monstrosity, scribe. This is arithmetic. Every debt in your world is paid eventually by someone who didn't borrow."

Casper's hand had found Ada's shoulder without his seeming to notice. The child stood very still, looking up at the dim lantern with an expression the Cantor could not read — and he had read every expression the living wore, so the failure unsettled him more than he admitted.

"What do you want," Casper said at last. Not a question. A brace against a fall.

"Ah," said the Cantor. "Yes. The bargain."

* * *

He received them properly then — in the heart of the hall, beneath the oldest lanterns, where the resonance of the mountain ran strongest and every word spoken was half song whether the speaker willed it or no. He seated them at a table of black stone that rose from the floor at his gesture, and set before them bread and honey and hot wine, because hospitality disarmed where threats hardened, and because he genuinely enjoyed feeding people. It was one of the few appetites he had left that eating did not serve.

"Here is the truth of my office, offered freely, so that whatever you decide, you decide knowing it." He folded his hands — pale, long-fingered, translucent at the tips if the light caught them right, and it always caught them right; he arranged the light. "The mountain is hungry. It was sung into being ages ago by singers greater than any now living, and a song, once begun, must be sustained or the thing it sustains begins to devour itself. I am the sustain. Four thousand souls, burning in rotation, feed the note that holds the mountain up. If the note fails, Thornfell does not crumble — crumbling would be kind. It *wakes*. And a waking mountain eats everything within a hundred miles, beginning with the lowland towns, beginning with Marrowgate, beginning, I should think, with the temple whose abbot has grown fat selling me children and calling it sanctification."

"Lies," Wren whispered. But her whisper shook.

"Believe what comforts you. Now — the bargain." He turned to Casper, and let his voice drop into the register he saved for closing: low, resonant, almost tender. "You want the child's soul returned. Entry 3,912. I am prepared to release it. Freely. Tonight, if you wish — the gates do not seal for three days yet."

"And the price?"

"Your voice." The Cantor smiled, and made the smile gentle, and hated how easily gentle came to him after all these centuries, the way rust comes to iron. "Not your speech — you may talk until the end of your days, gossip, argue, recite your tedious prayers. I mean the gift. The soul-song. The thing in you that spends memory and warmth to bend the world's hearing. I will take it entire, root and branch, and add it to the hoard, and it will burn in my vaults beside your sister's, and the mountain will hold another winter, and another, and Ada will grow up whole."

"You're lying," Wren said. "Souls don't come out. You told us yourself—"

"Hers can. Because it was never fully mine." The Cantor spread his hands. "A soul sold at five years old, never eaten, never spent — it retains its anchor in the body that grew without it. That is why the child hums, mistress Wren. She is a bell with no clapper, ringing anyway. Cut the ledger-line between her and the vault, and the light returns to her like water finding its old bed. It is a simple surgery. I have performed it twice in four hundred years, and both times the subject thrived." He paused, delicately. "Both times, someone else paid."

"And me?" Casper asked. "If I give you my voice — what happens to it? To me?"

"You will be quieter," the Cantor said honestly, because honesty in the final hour was the cruellest courtesy and the most effective. "You will lose memories faster, at first, as the gift dies back — the way a felled tree bleeds. Warmth goes next. You will find fire less satisfying, food blander, other people faintly further away. In ten years you will be a competent, forgettable man who copies prayers and cannot say why his eyes sting at certain chords." He tilted his head. "In exchange: a child keeps her soul. A mountain keeps its silence. And somewhere in my vaults, a light the color of yours will burn beside your sister's, and the two of you will finally keep company. Think of it, scribe. Your father separated you. I will reunite you. There is a poetry in it that even your abbot could not corrupt."

He watched the bargain settle over Casper like snowfall — soft, inevitable, burying. He watched the man look at Ada, and he knew that look; he had priced it for centuries. It was the look of someone measuring their own worth against a child's future and finding the arithmetic, for once, merciful.

"Do not answer yet," the Cantor said, rising. "Midwinter gives you three days. Rest. Eat. The court is open to you — the vaults, the galleries, all of it. Look at what I keep. Decide what you can live with." At the doorway he paused, and could not help adding, softly, the one cruelty he permitted himself:

"For what it is worth, scribe — your sister's light has dimmed these past months. They all do, near the end of their rotation. If you mean to bargain, bargain soon. Sentiment is a currency, and mine is running low."

* * *

Alone again, the Cantor climbed to the highest gallery and stood among his lanterns, listening.

Below, in the guest chambers, three heartbeats argued with themselves. The scribe's beat steady and slowing, decision gathering in him like weather. The choir mistress's ragged with old guilt and new terror. And the child's —

The child's was strange. It had been strange since she entered the court, and it was stranger now. Steady. Unafraid. And underneath it, so faint that only the mountain itself could have heard it, that impossible note again — the silent song, the spending-nothing song — threading through the stone like a root through a wall, touching the lanterns one by one as she lay in the dark, learning them. Learning *him*. Learning the shape of the lock before anyone had shown her it was a lock.

The Cantor had ruled this court for longer than kingdoms had names. He had heard every voice the world could produce, bought or stolen or freely given, and nothing in all that time had frightened him.

He was not frightened now, precisely.

But he found, as he stood among his hoard in the breathing dark, that he had begun — very quietly, very carefully, the way one moves near a sleeping animal — to count the days until midwinter, and to wonder, for the first time in four hundred years, whether the gates would close in time.
