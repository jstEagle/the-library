## 16. Serah's Choice

The descent into the vaults took an hour by the Cantor's counting-stones, and Serah Vail counted every one of them.

She had been a hunter for eleven years. She knew how to keep her face still while a man wept, how to hold a lantern steady while a mother begged, how to walk away from a burning chapel with her spine straight and her hands clean — or as clean as they would ever be again. She had learned all of it in the first year, when she was still young enough to believe that what she did was mercy: that the gifted were safer in the crown's keeping than in the crown's fear, that surrender was kinder than the pyre, that the word *sanctification* meant something other than what the silence beneath it meant.

She no longer believed any of it. But she had stopped believing years ago, the way a woman stops believing in the god she still prays to — quietly, without changing anything about her life, because the alternative was to change everything, and she had already sold everything once.

The stair wound down through living rock, and the rock sang.

That was the thing no one told you about the court beneath Thornfell. The mountain hummed. Not wind, not water — a note so low and patient it seemed less like sound than like intention, as if the stone itself were waiting to be sung back to sleep. Serah felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the old scar of her gift, the place behind her sternum where her own song had gone quiet twenty years ago, when she had knelt before a royal magistrate and given up her voice's power in exchange for a badge and a pardon. The magistrates had called it *consecration*. They had taken her ability to spend herself in song and left her only the ability to hear — which was, she understood now, exactly what a hunter needed. A hound does not need to sing. A hound needs only to listen for the ones who can.

"Mind the threshold," said the Cantor's steward, a grey man named Ossen whose voice never varied from the pitch of courtesy. He lifted a lantern of green glass, and the light fell across a doorway carved with a frieze of singers — hundreds of them, spiraling inward, their mouths open, their faces rapt. "The vaults remember who enters. Best not to give them anything to remember you by."

Serah stepped through, and the humming changed key.

She had been below twice before. Both times she had been escorted directly to the Cantor's audience hall, that cathedral of pale stone where he received hunters and abbots and the occasional trembling noble come to purchase longevity or luck or simply the absence of a rival. She had seen the hoard from a distance — the great slow constellation of lights that hung above his throne, drifting like lanterns on a windless sea. Everyone below knew what those lights were. Everyone above pretended not to. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

But today the Cantor had sent her down alone, past the audience hall, past the choir galleries where veiled figures swayed in silence, down a stair that had not been on any map she'd memorized, to a door of black iron banded in silver.

"The thief," Ossen had said, handing her the key. "And the child's keeper. The Cantor wishes them kept comfortable until midwinter. You will watch them yourself. He trusts your ear."

*He trusts your ear.* It should have been an honor. It sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

The lock turned with a sound like a held breath released. Beyond the door, the vaults opened out into a cavern so vast that her lantern-light found nothing — no wall, no ceiling, only a darkness that drank the flame and gave back a faint grey shimmer, as though the air itself were faintly luminous. And hanging in that shimmer, receding into distances her eyes refused to measure, were the souls.

She had seen them from afar. She had never stood among them.

They hung at every height, at every depth, thousands upon thousands, each one a soft point of light wrapped in a skin of something like glass, something like membrane, something like the last moment of a person's life made visible. Some burned white and quick. Some pulsed slow and amber, like coals remembering fire. Some had dimmed almost to nothing — little grey husks turning gently in currents she could not feel — and it was these that made her stop walking, because she recognized the rhythm of their fading. She had heard that rhythm before. In hospital wards. In deathbed rooms. The rhythm of something alive running out.

"They fade if they're not sung to."

The voice came from her left, out of the dark, and Serah's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up with the accent — lowland, Marrowgate-bred, roughened by road-dust and something harder. A woman stepped into the edge of her lantern-light with her wrists bound in silver cord and her chin lifted as though the cords were a fashion she had chosen.

Wren. The disgraced choir mistress. The one the temple records called dead eleven years, and the bounty notices called very much otherwise.

"You're supposed to be locked in the holding gallery," Serah said.

"I was." Wren smiled, and there was no humor in it, only a terrible steadiness. "Your master's locks are made for thieves and children. I spent eleven years learning what songs do to silver. It sings along, if you ask it nicely." She tilted her head toward the hanging lights. "You came to gloat? Or to count?"

"I came to guard you."

"Then guard." Wren turned back to the vault, and began, softly, to hum.

Serah's whole body flinched. It was not a loud sound — barely more than breath shaped into pitch — but the vault answered. The nearest lights brightened. Dozens of them, then hundreds, a tide of gentle kindling rolling outward into the dark wherever the sound reached, each soul leaning toward Wren's hum the way frost-flowers lean toward a window's warmth. And in the light they cast, Serah saw their faces.

Not faces. But something like faces — impressions, suggestions, the way a pressed flower holds the shape of the living thing. She saw a girl of perhaps eight, her light flickering in a pattern that could only have been laughter. She saw an old man whose glow steadied, just slightly, as though comforted. She saw — and her heart stopped, and restarted wrong —

She saw a boy. Small. Dark-haired. His soul wore the shape of a lullaby she knew, because she had sung it herself, in another life, in a chapel that no longer existed except as ash. One of hers. One of the Chapel Royal choristers, taken in the purges after her fall, listed in the ledgers as *sanctified*, and here he was, hanging in the dark under a mountain, brightening for a stranger's hum because no one had sung to him in eleven years.

"What is this place," Serah said. Her voice came out flat. She made it flat on purpose. Flatness was armor, and she had worn the armor so long she had forgotten there was anything underneath.

"You know what it is." Wren let the hum die. The lights dimmed reluctantly, reluctantly, like children being told bedtime has come. "You've known since the first bounty you carried. You just never let yourself do the sum."

"The souls are harvested. The Cantor keeps them. That's the arrangement—"

"The arrangement." Wren laughed, short and bitter, and the sound skittered off into the dark and came back changed. "Eleven years hunting gifted children, and you never asked where they go after the ledger closes? Never followed one down? Never wondered why the Cantor pays the temple in gold it doesn't need, and the crown in favors it can't refuse?" She stepped closer, and the silver cords caught the soul-light, and her eyes were terrible and pitying all at once. "Look at the dim ones, hunter. Look at the grey ones. Count them."

"I don't take orders from—"

"Count them."

And Serah, who had spent eleven years giving orders and taking none, who had cornered fugitives in snowfields and talked mothers out of their own daughters with a voice like warm oil — Serah counted.

It was the oldest discipline of her training, the first thing they taught you at the hunter's academy: *when the mind wants to flee, give it arithmetic.* She fixed her gaze on the nearest cluster of faded lights and counted. Thirty. Fifty. A hundred. The numbers climbed and the cavern did not end and the grey husks did not end, and somewhere around three hundred her discipline betrayed her, because arithmetic done honestly becomes a sum, and the sum became a rate, and the rate became years, decades, centuries, and the answer arrived in her chest like a blade slipped between ribs:

There were too many. Far too many. More than the hunt could ever have supplied. More than the temples could have sold. This hoard went back further than the crown, further than the decree, further than any ledger she had ever audited. Which meant the taking had not begun with the hunt. The hunt was only the newest tributary of a river far older and far wider, and she — Serah Vail, chief hunter of the gift, keeper of the king's peace — was standing in its delta.

"Why are some of them dying?" she asked, and hated how small her voice sounded in all that dark.

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her bitterness had drained away and left something worse: gentleness.

"Because he eats them."

The words did not make sense. Serah stood in the vast grey shimmer and waited for them to make sense, and they refused, and Wren watched her wait with the patience of a woman who had once stood in this same dark and waited for the same words to make sense.

"The mountain is hungry," Wren said. "You've felt it — everyone below feels it, that hum in the rock. It isn't music, Serah. It's appetite. The mountain was sung into being, ages ago, by singers who poured themselves into the stone until there was nothing left of them but the song — and the song wants more. It always wants more. Left alone, it would hollow the world. So the first Cantor found a way to feed it something else. Souls. Given slowly. Sung into the stone a piece at a time, enough to keep the hunger drowsy." She lifted her bound wrists. "And when the offerings grew scarce — when the gifted hid, or died out, or the temples got squeamish — he started eating the hoard himself. Taking the stored souls into his own body and singing them out to the stone in his own voice. Spending them the way you spend a memory. Except he doesn't get memories back. He gets nothing back. He burns them entire, and the hunger sleeps a little longer, and wakes a little bigger."

"That's—" Serah stopped. Started again. "That's a story. That's what the Cantor tells thieves to frighten them."

"Is it?" Wren nodded upward, and Serah followed the nod, and high above — impossibly high, near the vault's unseen ceiling — she saw the throne-gallery's distant glow, and against it, a silhouette. The Pale Cantor, alone at his rail, his arms raised, his mouth open. And as she watched, one of the hanging lights — a small one, a child-sized one, a light that had been amber and quick — drifted up from the hoard like a bubble rising through honey, and reached him, and went out.

The hum in the rock deepened. Satisfied. Briefly satisfied.

Serah Vail had not vomited since the academy's endurance trials, twenty-two years ago. She vomited now, into the dark at the vault's edge, silently, with the discipline of a professional, and when she straightened she wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and found Wren watching her without triumph, without judgment — with only that awful gentleness, the gentleness of someone who had already wept this exact weeping and come out the other side with her spine intact.

"Now do the rest of the sum," Wren said quietly. "Every child you brought in. Every mother you talked out of her daughter. Every 'sanctification' you witnessed and signed for. Where did you think they were going?"

"Safety," Serah whispered. "I thought — the pyres were real, in the early years. The burnings. I told myself the vaults were better than the fire. I told myself I was choosing the kinder death."

"And now?"

And now the kinder death was a small amber light rising through the dark to be devoured by a starving god, and she had been the shepherd dog driving the flock to his table for eleven years, and the badge on her chest weighed what a mountain weighed.

"I bought my life with my gift," Serah said. The words came out of her like splinters, one at a time, each one leaving a hole. "Twenty years ago. I gave up my song, and they gave me this." She touched the hunter's sigil at her throat — a silver bell, closed, mute. "I told myself it was trade. Fair trade. Everyone pays something. That's what I believed. That's what I *made* myself believe, because the alternative was—" She stopped. Her hand had closed around the silver bell hard enough to hurt. "The alternative was that I fed children to a starving god to save my own skin. And that I kept doing it. Year after year. Because stopping would mean admitting what I'd already done."

"Yes," Wren said. Simply. Without cruelty. "That's the shape of it. I know the shape. I built the same shape out of drink." She looked up at the vault, at the thousand thousand lights, and her voice cracked at last, just once, and mended. "The question isn't what you've done, Serah Vail. The question is what you do in the next hour. Because the Cantor didn't send you down here to guard us. Think. Why would a thing like that trust a hunter with its larder?"

The cold that went through Serah then had nothing to do with the mountain's chill.

"He doesn't trust me," she said slowly. "He's testing me. He wants to see whether I'll—" She stopped. Whether she would what? Report the escape attempt? Put down the prisoner? Or something else — something the Cantor had been circling for years, some suspicion that the chief hunter's obedience had gone soft at the center, that the mute bell at her throat rang truer than she let anyone hear?

Or worse. Much worse. The thought assembled itself with the horrible clarity of a key fitting a lock: *he sent me down here because the thief and the child are going to reach him anyway, and he wanted a witness he owns to see it happen.*

Above them, faint through leagues of stone, a bell began to toll. Midwinter's eve was coming. The gates would begin their closing within the day.

Wren heard it too. She turned to Serah, and held out her bound wrists, and her eyes held no plea at all — only an offer, level as a drawn blade.

"Well, hunter," she said. "You've done your sum. Time to choose what side of the ledger you stand on."

Serah looked down at the silver bell in her fist. Then she drew her knife, and cut the cords.

* * *

They climbed fast. Wren knew the vaults' geometry better than she should have — eleven days in captivity, she admitted, spent listening to the guards' boots and mapping the echoes, because a choir mistress hears a corridor the way a sailor reads water. Serah followed, knife sheathed, hunter's sigil torn from her throat and left lying in the dark among the roots of the hanging lights, where some dimmed soul's glow caught it and made it shine like a small dead moon.

At the junction above the black iron door, Wren paused.

"There's a girl," she said. "Ada. She sings without spending anything — silent song, real silent song, the kind the old stories talk about. And Casper — the scribe — he traded his voice to the Cantor for her soul. Gave it away whole." Her jaw tightened. "We have to reach them before the bargain finishes. Before the gate takes his song."

"How?" Serah said. "The gates close at midwinter. Nothing leaves."

"Nothing leaves by the gates." Wren's smile was thin and fierce and lit from somewhere the Cantor had never touched. "But I've heard the mountain hum, Serah. All these years, I've heard it. And a hum is just a song that's forgotten its words." She started up the stair, toward the tolling, toward the closing dark. "Come on. Let's go remind it."

Behind them, far below, the hoard glimmered on — thousands of small lights in the enormous dark, waiting, the way the saved always wait, for someone to come back with a song worth following.

Serah Vail, hunter no longer, went up after her into the ringing stone.
