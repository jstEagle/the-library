## 17. The Bargain Struck

The gates of the court beneath the mountain were closing.

Casper felt it before he saw it — a slow tightening in the air of the great cathedral-vault, as though the whole cavernous space were drawing a long breath and preparing to hold it until spring. The stolen souls hanging in their thousands from the vaulted dark flickered in answer, lanterns guttering in a wind that did not blow. Somewhere far above, snow was falling on Thornfell. Somewhere far above, midwinter was arriving with its white arithmetic, sealing every road, every crack, every hope of passage until the thaw.

And here, at the center of everything, the Pale Cantor waited for his answer.

* * *

They had been given an hour. The Cantor had said it the way a host announces dinner — *take what time you need; the mountain is patient* — and then withdrawn into the shadows behind his throne of veined black stone, leaving Casper alone with Ada in a side chapel carved from living rock. Wren was somewhere below, in the soul-vaults, under guard. Serah Vail had not been seen since the whiteout. And the gates were closing.

Ada sat on the chapel's stone bench, her legs swinging because they did not reach the floor, her hands folded in her lap with a composure that frightened him more than any weeping would have. She was seven years old. She had crossed half a kingdom on silent feet, survived hunters and toll-keepers and the whiteout that took Wren, and now she sat waiting for an adult to decide her life, because that was what children learned to do when adults kept deciding it badly.

"I'm going to do it," Casper said. His voice sounded strange in the small chapel — too loud, too human, against all that ancient quiet. "You understand? I'm going to trade."

Ada looked up at him. Her eyes were dark and steady, and she did not nod, which he had learned to read as her way of saying *I hear you, and I am not finished thinking.*

"It's the only way." He heard how thin the words were, how much they wanted to be true. "The Cantor holds your soul. He won't give it back for coin or argument or anything else we have. But he wants my voice. He said so himself — a voice like mine comes along once in a generation, he said. A voice that can still the blood of a guard dog with one note. He'll trade. He *wants* to trade. That's the worst part, Ada. He's not even going to make me fight for it."

Ada reached out and took his hand. Her fingers were cold — the mountain cold, the deep cold — but her grip was certain. She turned his palm upward and traced something in it with one finger: a line, then a curve, then another line.

He knew her shorthand by now. *A road.* Then: *a door.*

"You're asking if there's another way out," he said. "There isn't. Believe me, I've spent three days looking. Brannoc's maps, the smuggler routes, the crack you opened in the rock — it's sealed again. The gate needs a song to open, and after midwinter it needs more than a song. It needs a key, and the only key in this mountain is sitting on that throne wearing a face like winter water."

Ada's mouth pressed into a line. She pulled her hand back and began to draw in the frost-dust on the bench beside her — quick, sure strokes, the way she always drew when words failed everyone around her. She drew a bird. She drew the bird's throat, and inside the throat she drew a small bright circle.

Then she pointed at him.

"No," Casper said quietly.

She pointed again, insistent, and drew the circle moving — out of the bird's throat, into the air, and then back in again. *Given. Returned.*

"He can't give it back. That's not how it works. Wren told me — everything spent below stays below. Every memory, every warmth, every note. The toll-keeper at Greywater doesn't return what he takes. The Cantor certainly doesn't. If I give him my voice, Ada, I will never sing again. Never hum. Never—" He stopped, because his throat had closed around the word *never*, and it seemed suddenly enormous, a word the size of a lifetime.

Ada took his hand again. She held it between both of hers, the way you hold something wounded, and she looked at him with an expression no seven-year-old should have had to learn: the expression of someone who has already lost everything once and knows exactly what it costs.

Then, slowly, deliberately, she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against his chest, over his heart, and stayed there.

Casper put his arms around her. Above them the stolen souls flickered and dimmed, and somewhere in the deeps of the mountain, stone ground against stone as the gates drew narrower, and he understood that whatever he decided, he had already decided it — had decided it in the salt cellars of Thornfell when he'd taken her hand instead of sounding the alarm, on the smuggler's road when he'd sung the dog to sleep, at the river ford when he'd paid his mother's smile to blind the hunters. Every step of the road north had been a step toward this bench, this child, this bargain. There had never been another way. There had only ever been the price, and whether he would pay it.

"All right," he whispered into her hair. "All right. Stay here. Whatever happens — stay here, and don't watch if you don't want to."

She pulled back and shook her head once, firmly. *I will watch. I owe you that.*

* * *

The throne room of the Pale Cantor was not a room so much as a wound — a vast hollow where the mountain's heart should have been, its walls ribbed with stone that might once have been organ pipes, its ceiling lost in darkness hung with souls. They burned softly, those stolen lights, each one carrying the color of the life it had been: gold for joy, blue for grief, green for the particular shade of a summer remembered. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Generations of theft, hanging in the dark like fruit no one dared pick.

The Cantor stood beneath them, and up close he was worse than he had been at a distance. He had chosen a shape that was almost human — tall, spare, robed in grey that moved like smoke — but the almost was the horror. His skin had the translucence of candle wax. His eyes were the pale grey of a frozen lake, and when he looked at Casper, Casper felt looked *through*, catalogued, appraised as one appraises timber or ore.

"You've considered my offer," the Cantor said. It was not a question. His voice filled the vault without effort, resonating in the ribbed stone until the whole chamber seemed to speak with him.

"I have."

"And?"

Casper's mouth was dry. He made himself stand straight, made himself meet that lake-grey gaze. "I want terms. Spoken plainly, before witnesses." He gestured to the handful of hooded figures who attended the throne — the Cantor's household, such as it was, faces Casper could not read. "Your ledger shows an entry. Ada, daughter of Maren of the lowlands, sold by her own mother's hand nine years past, purchased by the Pale Cantor personally. You will release that soul. Whole. Unspent. Not one memory eaten from it, not one warmth drawn off. You will restore it to her body, here, tonight, before the gates close. In exchange—"

"In exchange you give me your voice," the Cantor said. "Freely given, fully surrendered, forever. Yes. Those are the terms. I am not a merchant of fine print, scribe. I deal in simpler currencies." Something moved across his waxy face that might, on a human, have been a smile. "You are wondering why I want it. A voice is a small thing, next to a soul."

"Is it?"

"To you, perhaps. To me—" The Cantor lifted one translucent hand, and the nearest souls flickered toward it like moths. "Do you know what I am, Casper Thackeray? Do you know what this court is?"

"Brannoc says you're a god."

"Brannoc says many things." The Cantor's laugh was a low chord, felt in the teeth. "I am the echo. This mountain was sung into being — did you learn that in your cave of first notes? A song so vast it pulled stone out of chaos and taught it to stand. But songs end, scribe. Songs must end, or they become something else. When the singers fell silent, the mountain was left with the last note still ringing in its bones, and the ringing became hunger, and the hunger became me. I am what remains when a song refuses to stop." He spread his hands, and the light of ten thousand souls washed over him, and for one terrible moment Casper saw through the almost-human shape to the thing beneath: a hollow vast as the vault itself, empty, aching, *starving*. "I eat what I can catch. Souls keep best. They burn slowly. But they are thin fare, and the mountain grows, and the hunger grows faster. Your voice — a living voice, a voice that creates rather than remembers — that is a meal I have not tasted in four hundred years."

"So I'm food."

"You're a bargain freely struck between two honest parties." The Cantor inclined his head. "Which in this age makes us nearly unique. Do you accept?"

Casper thought of his father. It came unbidden, the way it always did — the broad stooped back bent over debt-ledgers in the lamplight, the smell of ink and cheap tallow, the heavy sigh that had been the soundtrack of Casper's childhood. *We owe what we owe, boy. The temple keeps its books, and the books keep us.* His father had signed him over to Thornfell at eleven years old to clear a debt Casper had never incurred, and had died two winters later owing more than when he started, and Casper had spent every year since measuring his life in installments. How many winters remained. How much was left to pay. Who owned the hours of his days.

His father had sold a soul here too. The Cantor had told him that, in this same vault, with terrible gentleness — a sister Casper had never known he had, traded away before Casper was born, her entry in the ledger of souls written in his father's own careful hand. The debt that defined him went deeper than coin. It went into his blood. It had been written into the family before he existed.

Well. Tonight the account would be settled.

"I accept," Casper said.

* * *

The Cantor did not use instruments. He did not need them. He simply opened — there was no other word for it — opened like a door opening in the world, and the hunger came out.

Casper felt it take hold of his song the way a tide takes hold of a swimmer. His voice rose without his willing it, and it was *his*, every note of it, the hum that had stilled the guard dog, the whisper that had blinded the hunters, the low warm tones he used to soothe Ada's nightmares on cold nights — all of it pouring out of him and into the dark, and the dark drank. With each note, something went with it. That was the law below, Wren's law, the discipline she had beaten into him on the road: *every note given costs*. He felt the costs being taken now, felt them torn out rather than paid —

No. He caught himself on that word. *Torn.* He made himself open his hands at his sides, palms forward, the posture of giving. He would not be robbed. He would pay.

The memories went in order, whether the Cantor chose the order or the hunger did, Casper never knew. First the small ones: his father teaching him to hold a pen, the big warm hand closed over his small fist. His father's laugh, rare as summer snow, when Casper made a joke about the abbot's hat. The smell of his father's coat, wool and pipe-smoke and lamp oil. Each one flared bright as it left him — he *saw* them, one last time, whole and vivid, and then they were gone, and the space where they had been was smooth and clean as unwritten paper.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange thing. Wren had warned him it would hurt, that spending was agony, that the toll-keeper's customers wept. But paying willingly was different from being robbed. Each memory departed like a guest taking leave — bowed to, thanked, released. What remained was not pain. It was *lightness*, terrifying and clean.

More went. His father's anger, the nights of shouting about debts. His father's fear, hunched and whispering in the dark of their rented room. The day his father signed the indenture papers, the scratch of the quill, the way he wouldn't meet Casper's eyes. Casper had carried that day like a stone in his chest for fifteen years, turning it over, asking it questions — *did he love me, did he grieve, was I worth less than the debt* — and now it simply left, and the questions left with it, unanswered and finally, mercifully, unaskable.

He was singing still. He could feel the song moving through him like water through an emptying channel, and he understood with a distant clarity that the Cantor was taking everything, that *voice* meant not just the instrument but the whole music of a life, and he found he did not care. Let it go. Let all of it go. The ledgers, the debts, the winters counted, the father whose weight he had carried so long he had forgotten it was not part of his body —

The last memory rose. He knew it would be this one. It always was, in the order of a life: the earliest thing.

Snow. He was very small. His father stood in the doorway of their house with a bundle in his arms — no. No, that wasn't right, the bundle was *him*, Casper himself, small and red-faced in the cold, and his father was holding him up to see the mountains, Thornfell white under the first snowfall, vast and silent and impossibly beautiful. *Look,* his father said, and his voice was young, younger than Casper had ever known it, hopeful, a man not yet broken by what he would become. *Look at it, Casper. Isn't it something? One day you'll climb it, maybe. One day you'll be free of all this.*

The memory hung in him, glowing. The Cantor's hunger reached for it.

Casper gave it freely. *Take it. Take all of it. I was never free because of him. I'll be free without him.*

And it went — and the song ended.

* * *

Silence.

Not the silence of the vault, which was never truly silent — the souls murmured, the stone hummed, the mountain's old hunger breathed. But from Casper: nothing. He stood in the center of the wound-room with his hands still open at his sides, and he reached for his voice the way a man reaches for a limb that is no longer there, and found nothing. No hum. No note. No name rising to be spoken.

He tried to say Ada's name. His lips shaped it. No sound came.

*It's done,* he thought, and the thought itself seemed to echo strangely in the emptied rooms of his head, off walls that no longer held pictures. *It's done, and I am light. Gods forgive me, I am so light.*

The Cantor stood before him, and the change in the creature was visible, dreadful, magnificent. Color had come into the wax-pale flesh — a faint flush, like dawn on ice. The lake-grey eyes had deepened. For the first time, the almost-human shape looked almost *alive*, and Casper understood what a four-hundred-year hunger satisfied looked like, and pitied the thing even as he feared it.

"A fair trade," the Cantor said, and his voice was richer now, warmer, horribly — it had notes in it now, Casper's notes, borrowed and worn. He raised one hand, and from the darkness above, a single soul descended, drifting down through the flickering thousands like a leaf falling through a burning forest.

It was small. It was bright. It burned the color of morning.

Ada's soul came to rest in the Cantor's cupped hands, pulsing gently, and even across the vault Casper felt it — felt *her*, the hum she had hidden in her chest all those years at Thornfell, the silent song she carried, the whole unspent brightness of a child who had never yet had to pay for anything.

The Cantor turned. "Come and receive what you purchased, scribe. Though I warn you—" and there, in the new-warm voice, was something almost like regret, "—the restoration is not gentle."

Ada stepped out of the shadow of the chapel arch.

She had not stayed on the bench. Of course she hadn't. She walked across the vault floor with her chin up and her fists at her sides, small and resolute, and did not look at Casper — or rather, she looked at him only once, quickly, the way you look at something you're afraid will break your resolve — and stopped before the Cantor and held out her arms.

The Cantor hesitated. Casper saw it — a flicker across the transfigured face, some ancient hesitation, the echo remembering what it had been before it was hunger. For a moment the whole vault seemed to hold its breath, ten thousand souls flickering in unison.

Then the Cantor lowered the small bright soul into Ada's open arms.

What happened next, Casper's emptied mind recorded in fragments, in images without sequence: light. Ada's body arching, her mouth opening in a soundless cry. The hum — *her* hum, the one she had hidden all her life — swelling up out of her chest and filling the vault, harmonizing with the stone, with the souls, with the mountain's buried first note. The Cantor staggering back as though struck. Cracks of light racing up the ribbed walls. The gates, far away, groaning — not closing now, but *shuddering*, as if something on the other side of them had begun, very softly, to sing.

And Ada, standing in the center of it with her soul restored and blazing, turning to look at Casper with tears running down her face and her hands pressed over her heart — and mouthing, silently, clearly, two words:

*My turn.*

She raised her hands toward the hoard above — toward the ten thousand stolen lights, toward the vaults below where Wren was held, toward the whole vast architecture of theft — and began, without sound, without cost, without spending anything at all, to sing.
