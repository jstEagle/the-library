## 18. The Silent Song

Ada had never heard her own voice.

She knew it the way you know a room you've never seen lit — by its shape in the dark, by the way other things echoed against it. The priests at Thornfell had called her broken. The foundling ledger had called her mute. Wren, on the cold road north, had called her *quiet as snowfall*, and said it like it was a kind of weather worth having.

But Ada knew what she was now. She had known since the cave of first notes, where the mountain hummed through the soles of her feet and she had hummed back without opening anything — not her mouth, not her throat, not the small locked door inside her where a voice might have lived if anyone had ever sold her one whole.

She was not broken. She was *unspent*.

And Casper — Casper who had given everything, who stood before her now with his mouth open around a silence that used to be his song — Casper was empty in the way that frightened her more than any hunter ever had.

The gate of the court beneath Thornfell was closing.

She could feel it the way she felt everything down here: as pressure, as a slow tightening behind her sternum, as though the whole mountain were drawing a long breath and meant to hold it until spring. The gates were two slabs of black stone taller than the temple's bell tower, carved over every inch with lines of script that glowed faintly, the color of embers seen through closed eyelids. Between them ran a seam of light, thin as a wire, and along that seam the Cantor's servants had written their oldest law in letters older than the crown:

*A gate opens to a song. A song is paid for. Nothing given freely; nothing taken back.*

Casper had already paid. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. He had traded his voice for Ada's soul, and the Pale Cantor had honored the bargain with the precision of a moneylender counting coins — because that was all he was, in the end. A keeper of accounts. Ada's soul hung now inside her chest like a lamp relit after years of dark, warm and strange and *hers*, and she could feel every edge of it, the way you feel a tooth that has stopped aching.

It should have been enough. It should have been the end of the story: the child saved, the debt settled, everyone walking out into the snow.

But the gate did not care about endings. The gate wanted its song.

Wren knelt beside Casper on the vaulted floor, her hands cupping his face the way she used to cup the faces of her choristers before performances, thumbs resting just below his ears. Her lips were moving. Ada couldn't hear the words — the mountain ate sound here, swallowed it into stone — but she could read them, because she had spent her whole life reading mouths.

*Sing something. Anything. Even broken.*

Casper shook his head. His throat worked. What came out was air, shaped and reshaped, a flute with no breath in it. Ada watched him try three times, four, and each failure landed on his face like a small physical blow. He had spent his father's memories willingly — she had felt the release of them from across the vault, a lightness like a held breath finally let go — but the voice had gone with them, poured out into the Cantor's hoard, and now there was nothing left in him to open doors with. Nothing left but the man himself. Which was, Ada thought with a fierce clarity that surprised her, rather a lot.

Serah Vail was shouting somewhere behind them, holding the narrowing gap with her hunter's stance and her hunter's song, buying seconds the way she'd once bought her own safety. Freed souls streamed past them toward the light — hundreds of lantern-flames walking upright now, flickering, bewildered, beautiful — and none of them could leave either, not really, not while the gate demanded its toll from each one. She could see it happening at the threshold: souls pressing against the seam and faltering, dimming, turning back into the dark because they had nothing left to give. They had already been given. That was the whole horror of this place. Everyone here had already paid everything.

Ada looked at the gate.

Then she looked at her own hands.

They were small hands, chapped at the knuckles, ink-stained from years of drawings pressed into wax and ash and frost. They had never done anything the world considered important. But they had learned things on this road. In Wren's lessons by the fire, when Casper practiced spending notes and winced at what each one cost, Ada had sat apart and listened with her chest instead of her ears, and she had noticed something no one else seemed to see:

Every voice that sang *outward* lost something. But a song didn't have to go outward.

Her whole life, people had treated her silence as an absence. A missing thing. A flaw to be measured for, catalogued, eventually sold. No one had ever asked what the silence *held*. And the answer, which she had carried secretly under her ribs since the cave, was: everything. A song sung inward cost nothing, because nothing left. It only turned, the way water turns in a deep well, going down and down and coming up sweeter. The mountain itself had been sung into being that way once — she'd understood it in the cavern, in the resonance, in the old joy still sleeping in the stone. The court below wasn't the mountain's song. It was the mountain's song *stopped*, dammed, hoarded by a starving thing that had forgotten music was supposed to move.

Ada stepped forward.

Wren saw her first. The choir mistress's face went through surprise and then fear and then — slowly, like dawn coming up over Marrowgate's rooftops — understanding. Her hand shot out and caught Ada's wrist, gentle even now, even with the gate grinding closed above them.

*No,* her mouth said. *You don't owe them anything. You hear me? You don't—*

Ada smiled at her. Then she took Wren's hand off her wrist, folded the fingers closed around nothing, and pressed the fist briefly against her own heart — the gesture she'd invented on the road, the one that meant *I am keeping this*, the one that meant *safe*, the one that meant, in the private language the three of them had built out of necessity and firelight and love: *family*.

Then she walked to the gate.

Up close, the script on the stones was singing. She could feel it against her skin like heat off a forge — the old law, the hungry law, the law that said all songs must be bought. It reached for her the way the toll-keeper at Greywater had reached, the way the Cantor had reached, the way every adult with ledgers in their eyes had always reached for her. Expecting payment. Expecting her to be small and spendable.

Ada put both palms flat against the black stone.

And she sang.

Not with her mouth. Not with her throat. She sang the way she had been singing her entire silent life — inward, downward, around — but this time she let the song *touch* the gate, the way you let a wave touch the shore without giving the sea away. The note moved through her chest, through her arms, through her palms, into the stone, and the stone *recognized* it.

Because it was the first note. The one the mountain had been sung with, before the court, before the Cantor, before anyone thought a soul was a thing you could write in a ledger. Ada had found it in the Cave of First Notes, curled up inside the resonance like a seed in winter soil, and she had kept it, unspent, the way she kept everything. Hoarded it, if you wanted to use the Cantor's word for it. Saved it, if you used hers.

The seam of light between the gates shivered.

The law on the stones flared bright, furious, demanding — *a song must be paid* — and Ada answered it the only way she knew how, the way she'd answered every priest and scribe and hunter who had ever tried to price her: she gave nothing, and she did not stop. The note turned in her like the well-water, deeper and deeper, and where it touched the gate the old script began to unravel, letter by letter, the way frost unravels off a window when a hand lies warm against the glass. The law hadn't been broken in a thousand years. It had simply never been met by someone who owed it nothing.

Behind her, she felt Wren begin to weep — she knew the shape of it without looking, the hitch in the breathing, the way the whole body went still around grief. And she felt something else too, rising to meet her note: Casper. Voiceless, emptied, ruined Casper, pressing his hand flat against her back between her shoulder blades, adding what he had left. Not a sound. Not a memory. Just *presence* — the steady warmth of him, the stubbornness, the man who had walked into hell on a smuggler's road to buy back a child the world had already written off. He couldn't sing anymore. So he became part of hers.

The gates opened.

Not violently. The way a hand opens. The two black slabs swung apart onto a corridor of grey light and falling snow, and the freed souls at the threshold hesitated, flickering, waiting for the toll they were sure would come.

Ada pulled her hands from the stone, turned, and gestured — wide, sweeping, imperative, the gesture Wren used at choir practice when the timid ones hung back at the chapel door. *Go. All of you. It's free. Go.*

They went. Lantern-faces streaming past her into the snow, some of them touching her hair as they passed, some of them weeping soundlessly the way she had always wept, and Ada stood in the middle of it with her heart hammering and her soul blazing and thought: *this is what I was for.* Not sold. Not spent. This.

* * *

But it wasn't finished.

She felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in her chest, a seam that didn't sit flush. Her soul was back in her, yes, warm and burning. But it came back the way a coat comes back from a pawnshop: hers, and yet carrying the smell of someone else's house. Somewhere in the weave of it was a thread that belonged to the Cantor's hoard, a claim-stitch, a lien. And threaded through that same stitch, she realized, with a jolt like stepping into cold water —

Was Casper's voice.

The trade had been made in the vault, soul for voice, and the exchange had passed through the Cantor's hands, and the Cantor's hands left marks. His hoard was collapsing around them — she could feel it, the great dim cavern where stolen songs hung like lanterns guttering one by one as their owners walked free — but the voice itself hadn't returned to Casper. It was caught. Hooked on the same stitch that hooked her, snagged in the dying net of the Cantor's claiming, and every moment it hung there it faded a little more, fraying like a rope over a fire.

Casper didn't know yet. He was helping Wren up, steadying Serah at the gate, doing the ordinary brave work of getting people out, and he would never know, because he couldn't feel his own absence anymore. You couldn't miss a voice you no longer remembered owning.

Ada could feel it for him.

She looked at the corridor to the vaults. The Cantor was dissolving somewhere in the deep places — she'd felt him start to come apart when the mountain woke, starved of his hoard, unraveling back into the stone he'd crawled out of a thousand years ago. But a hoard outlives its hoarder. That was the whole point of hoarding. The lanterns still hung in the dark, and among them, tangled in the last threads of his claim, hung one voice that belonged to a scribe with ink-stained fingers and a laugh he didn't know he'd lost.

Wren caught her arm again. *Where are you going?* her eyes said. *We're free. We're leaving. Ada—*

Ada pointed at Casper. Then at her own mouth. Then she drew, quick and fierce on the frost-dusted floor with one fingertip: a circle for a mouth, and lines coming out of it — and then she crossed the lines out, and pointed at Casper again.

Wren went white. "Oh," she breathed, and the word actually reached Ada's ears, small and cracked in the ringing air. "*Oh, child, no.*"

Casper looked between them, frowning, hands half-raised, asking the question he didn't know the shape of.

Ada didn't explain. There wasn't time, and explaining had never been her language anyway. She hugged him — hard, quick, her cheek against his chest where his heartbeat lived where his voice used to — and then she turned and ran back down into the dark, toward the vaults, toward the hoard, toward the last thread of the Pale Cantor's thousand-year theft.

The vaults were colder than the cold outside. The lanterns of souls still hung in their thousands, most of them dark now, emptied, released — but the hooks remained, rows and rows of them, iron curlicues driven into living rock, and from the deepest hook, the one shaped like a hand mid-gesture, dangled a single fading thread of gold.

His voice. She knew it instantly, the way she knew everything that mattered: by its shape in the dark. It sounded — it *felt* — like candlelight on copied prayers. Like a whispered note that stilled a guard dog. Like a man humming to warm his hands in a lowland tavern, not knowing a disgraced choir mistress was listening.

Ada reached up. She was small; the hook was high; she climbed the ironwork the way she'd climbed the salt cellars' shelving a lifetime ago, and she wrapped both hands around the thread of gold.

The Cantor's claim bit her. She felt it — cold, accounting, *mine, paid for, entered in the ledger* — and she almost let go. Almost. Because the claim was strong, and she was small, and every authority in her short life had taught her that small things get taken.

But she had opened a gate that hadn't opened in a thousand years. And she had done it by owing nothing.

So she did the only thing she knew how to do. She held the voice against her chest, next to her own returned soul, and she sang to it — silently, inwardly, the well-song, the first note, the one that costs nothing because it never leaves — and she sang *him* loose. Not taking. Giving. A voice can't be stolen back, she understood suddenly; it can only be *returned*, and return requires a giver, and she was the giver, she had always been the giver, the mute girl with the full heart.

The claim-stitch snapped like a harp string breaking.

The gold poured through her fingers, through her ribs, into the place where she kept things safe — and then out again, outward, for the first time in her life sending something away from herself on purpose, following the thread of love back up the stairs, back through the corridors, back to the man waiting in the snow.

* * *

Casper's gasp was the first sound Ada ever heard him make.

She came up out of the vaults into the falling snow and found him on his knees at the threshold, both hands pressed to his throat, and the noise coming out of him wasn't words — it was just sound, raw and cracked and astonished, a chord breaking over him like daylight. Wren had her fists pressed to her mouth. Serah stood in the gate with snow gathering on her shoulders, and even her hunter's face had come undone.

He saw Ada and he made a sound that was trying to be her name and failing, and then he crossed the snow and picked her up, all the way up, spinning her once like she weighed nothing, laughing and sobbing into her hair, and the sound of it — *his sound*, restored, unhooked, given — rang off the mountainside and woke echoes in the stone that had been sleeping since the world was sung.

"You gave it back," he kept saying, into her shoulder, wrecked and wondering. "You — the trade was final, the ledger was closed, how did you—" He pulled back and looked at her, really looked, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop asking. Some things you didn't need a voice to say. He set her down and signed it instead, clumsily, with cold-stiff fingers, using her language, their language: the fist to the heart. *Kept. Safe. Family.*

Above them, the mountain groaned — a vast, tectonic sound, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep — and far below, in the deep places where the Pale Cantor had been, the last of the hoard-lanterns went out, all together, like a city going dark at dawn.

Wren's head snapped up. "The gates," she said. "Look at the gates."

The black slabs were trembling. And beyond them, down the long corridor of the court, the walls themselves had begun to glow — ember-script flaring line by line, the whole subterranean realm lighting from beneath like a banked fire remembering it was ever meant to burn — and Ada felt, through the soles of her feet, through her chest, through the unspent song still turning sweetly in her like well-water, the mountain beginning to remember its original note.

All of it. At once.

"Run," said Serah Vail, and for once, no one argued.
