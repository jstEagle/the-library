## 19. The Mountain Wakes

### Serah Vail

The gates were closing when Serah turned her back on everything she had been.

She felt them closing the way a drowning woman feels the surface recede — a pressure in the chest, a cold certainty that the way out was narrowing to a seam, then a thread, then nothing. Behind her, in the vaults, Wren was shouting something that was not words. Ahead of her, up the long throat of the stair, Casper Thackeray carried a child and a stolen voice toward a door that would not stay open for grief or hesitation or anything less than a song.

So Serah Vail did the only thing left to her. She planted her boots on the lowest step of the gate-stair, spread her arms across the width of the passage like a bar across a door, and sang.

It was not a beautiful sound. She had spent twenty years making sure it could not be — she had buried her gift under duty and discipline until what came out of her was a soldier's note, flat and hard and stubborn as iron. But the gate did not want beauty. The gate wanted *song*, and the mountain beneath her feet knew the difference between a voice offered and a voice withheld. She offered hers the way you offer blood: all of it, at once, without bargaining.

The stone shuddered. The closing seam slowed.

*Hold,* she told herself. *Just hold.*

And then, from far below — from the vaults where the lanterns hung — something answered.

* * *

### Wren

Wren had led choirs for thirty years. She had conducted coronations and funerals, had coaxed a note out of a frightened eight-year-old with nothing but a lifted eyebrow, had stood before the Chapel Royal's thousand pipes and made them sound like weather. She knew what a choir could do. She had thought she knew everything a choir could do.

She had never heard one sing itself free.

It began with the lanterns. That was the only word for them — the stolen souls hanging in their hundreds from the vaulted dark, each one flickering with the shape of the life it once held, each one dimmer than the last, fed upon year by year by the pale thing that called itself their keeper. When Ada's silent song tore through the hoard — when Casper's returned voice rang off the stone like a bell struck after centuries of silence — the lanterns *woke*.

Not all at once. One first, near the vault's eastern wall: a small gold flicker that pulsed once, twice, and then began — Wren felt it before she heard it — to hum. A woman's hum. A lullaby-fragment, half-remembered, the kind of thing a mother sings without knowing she is singing it. Then another lantern caught it, a deeper tone, a man's voice joining below the melody. Then three more. Then ten. Then the whole vault was a constellation catching fire, each stolen soul remembering its own note, and the notes did not clash — they *fit*, the way stones fit in an arch, the way voices fit in a choir that has been waiting a very long time to sing together again.

Wren stood in the middle of it with tears freezing on her cheeks and her hands raised as if she were conducting, though no conducting was needed. This was not her choir. It had never needed her. It needed only permission, and Ada had given it, and now the music was doing what music has always done, which is refuse to be imprisoned.

"Up," she said, and her voice cracked, and she said it again, louder. "Up! All of you — upward! Follow the child's song!"

They could not walk, most of them. They were light and memory, not flesh. But they could *move* — they poured along the vault ceiling like dawn pouring through a window, a river of gold rising through the cathedral vastness of the court beneath, and where they passed the black stone brightened, and where it brightened the mountain *remembered*.

That was the thing Wren would never be able to explain afterward, not to Serah, not to the crown's investigators, not to herself on sleepless nights. The mountain remembered. It had been sung into being once — she understood that now, understood it in her bones the way you understand a chord — and every stolen soul that rose through its passages was a note returning home, and the mountain took each one back into itself like a body taking back warmth, and the whole massif above them began, very slowly, to resonate.

The floor hummed under her boots. The walls hummed. Her own ribs hummed, sympathetic strings, and Wren of the Broken Choir — drunk, disgraced, stripped of her chapel and her title and the memory of her finest concert — opened her mouth and gave the rising river of souls the one thing she still owned outright: her training. She found the key. She held the tempo. She shaped the chaos of a hundred freed voices into something with an architecture, an ascent, a *direction* — up, always up, toward the gates where a woman in a hunter's coat stood holding a mountain open by main force.

"Serah!" she screamed into the roar. "*Now!*"

* * *

### The Pale Cantor

He had eaten kingdoms' worth of song and he was starving anyway. He had known this for a long time, in the way a dying man knows it — not as news but as weather, a condition of existence. The mountain hungered. It had hungered since the first singers fell silent, and he had fed it, and himself, on the only food that quieted it: souls. Whole ones. Fresh ones. Children best of all, because children's souls burned bright and slow and sweet.

The hoard had kept him alive for four hundred years. The hoard was leaving him in the space of a single chord.

He stood at the center of his court — his cathedral, his larder, his tomb — and watched the lanterns rise past him like sparks ascending a chimney, and he reached for them, because reaching was all he had ever been, and his hands closed on nothing. They were not his anymore. They had never been his; that was the joke, the terrible ancient joke, that a thief who steals songs discovers too late that a song cannot be owned, only borrowed, and every borrower must someday return what was lent.

The Pale Cantor opened his mouth to command them back, and produced silence.

His voice was gone. His voice had been the first coin paid, long ago, in a bargain he no longer fully remembered, struck with a mountain that had wanted a keeper and gotten instead a hunger wearing a keeper's face. Everything since — the court, the vaults, the ledgers, the trade routes through Thornfell, the temple above with its tidy double books — all of it had been built on that first theft, and now the theft was being undone in reverse, note by rising note, and there was nothing underneath it. There had never been anything underneath it.

He looked down at his hands. They were becoming translucent. Through them he could see the stone of the floor, and the stone was singing, and the song was warm, and he understood suddenly — with the clarity that comes only at the end — that the stone had never hated him. The stone had pitied him. The mountain had taken him in when he was nothing but an appetite, had let him hollow out a kingdom in its belly, had waited four hundred years for someone to come and open the doors.

A child had done it. A mute child with a silent song, spending nothing, owing nothing. Of course. Of *course*. Only someone who had never paid could teach the mountain that the debt was never real.

The Pale Cantor sat down on the floor of his dissolving court, which was also the floor of the mountain, which had always been the same floor, and he let go.

He did not scream. He had no voice to scream with. What he did instead, in his final moment, was the closest thing to grace available to a creature made entirely of taking: he stopped resisting. And the stone received him the way stone receives rain — without ceremony, without malice, without memory. Where he had sat there was afterward only a paler patch in the rock, faintly shaped like a kneeling man, and even that faded within the hour, worn smooth by the vibration of a thousand returning voices.

* * *

### Casper

Casper Thackeray had his voice back and did not know how to use it yet. It sat in his throat like a bird returned to a cage whose door had been broken open — familiar, strange, trembling. Ada had pulled it out of the Cantor's hoard with both small hands, had pressed it back into his chest with her forehead against his sternum, and he had felt it settle into him like heat into cold stone, and he had wept, and there had been no time for anything but running.

Now they ran. Up the great stair, through corridors that had been black for centuries and were filling with light, the freed souls streaming past them in a golden flood that parted around the living the way a stream parts around stones. Ada rode on his back with her arms around his neck, and against his ear he could feel it rather than hear it — her silent song, a vibration with no sound attached, a note made of pure intention — and everywhere her song touched the stone, the stone woke faster.

"It knows us," Ada had written to him once, in the cave of first notes, her charcoal letters careful and small. "The mountain. It remembers being sung."

He believed it now with his whole body. The mountain was not waking *around* them; it was waking *with* them. Each corridor they climbed lit ahead of their footsteps. Each archway they passed hummed a fragment of some original melody, and Casper — scribe, debtor, fugitive, thief of his own sister's memory of a sister — found that he knew the fragments. Not from study. From *inheritance*. The gift in his blood was old, older than the temple, older than the debt, and it recognized this place the way a salmon recognizes the river of its birth.

At the top of the stair, the gates.

They were nearly shut — two slabs of living rock grinding together like millstones, and between them a gap no wider than a hand, and in the gap, arms braced against both slabs, boots skidding on frost-slick stone, stood Serah Vail, singing.

Her voice was wrecked. It cracked and frayed and bled at the edges, and it was the bravest sound Casper had ever heard. Frost had crept up her coat from the threshold; her lips were blue; her eyes, when they found him, were furious with effort.

"*Sing,*" she ground out. "You've got your voice back, scribe — *sing*, or I swear I'll haunt you—"

So Casper Thackeray, who had spent his life copying other people's prayers, sang one of his own.

It was not much. It was barely a melody — three notes, rising, the shape of the mountain's own first song as he had learned it in the resonant dark of the cave. But behind him the freed souls caught it, and Wren caught it and gave it spine, and Ada's silent harmony threaded through it like silver wire through cloth, and the three-note phrase became a chord, and the chord became a rising thing, a stairway of sound built in the space of a breath, and the gates of the court beneath the mountain — gates that had sealed against entry and escape for four hundred years — *opened*.

Not broke. Opened. The way a fist opens. The way winter opens, finally, into spring.

Serah Vail collapsed forward onto the threshold, and Casper caught her by the collar, and then they were all of them through — the living and the light together, pouring out onto the mountainside under a sky full of midwinter stars — and behind them the mountain sang.

* * *

### Ada

Ada stood in the snow and watched the souls come out.

They rose from the opened gates like embers from a bonfire kicked apart by wind — hundreds of them, gold and white and every color between, spiraling upward into the cold air, and the snow around them melted and refroze into patterns no one had ever seen, ferns and feathers and spirals, the mountain writing itself in frost. Some of the souls were small. Some were very small. Those ones hovered lowest, close to the ground, close to the people, as if they were not sure yet whether they were allowed to leave.

Ada raised her hand to them. She had no words — she had never had words — but she had her silent song, and she gave it to them freely, the way it had been given to her, and the smallest lights drifted toward her like moths, and settled in the air around her, and *rested*.

One of them, near her shoulder, hummed a fragment of a lullaby.

Ada went very still.

She knew that lullaby. Four notes, descending, with a catch in the third one where the singer's breath had always caught — she knew it from a drawing she had made once by firelight, from a memory that was not quite hers, from a mother who had traded a soul to buy a daughter's passage out of the dark. The soul beside her was not her mother's. She knew that too, with the strange clear sight the silent song gave her; her mother was alive somewhere, breathing, walking, unowned. This was only an echo. A woman who had once sung the same song to a different child, in a different kitchen, in a world that had stolen them both.

But she hummed it anyway. And Ada, who could not speak, sang it back to her — silently, perfectly, the catch in the third note included — and the little soul flared bright as a struck match, and rose, and rose, and was gone into the stars, and Ada stood in the falling snow with her hand still raised and felt something in her chest that was not grief and was not joy but was somehow both at once, braided together, true.

Below her, the mountain's song swelled. Above her, the sky filled with returning light. Around her, battered and frostbitten and alive, stood her family: the scribe with his borrowed-back voice, the choir mistress with her ruined hands full of snow, the hunter who had chosen, at the last, to hold the door.

* * *

### Wren

By dawn, the chorus reached the lowlands.

Farmers in the valleys around Thornfell would tell it for the rest of their lives: how they had woken before sunrise to a sound in the mountains, vast and layered and unmistakably *choral*, rising through the passes like weather made of music; how their animals had gone quiet and then, strangely, had seemed to listen; how grown men had stood in their doorways and wept without knowing why. In Marrowgate, the bells of the town chapel rang on their own, unpulled, harmonizing. In the temple of Thornfell, high on its crag, the abbot woke to find every candle in the sanctuary burning blue and every prayer-book open to the same page, and knew — before any rider came, before any ledger was produced — that it was over.

Wren led them. She could not have said how she ended up at the front; the souls simply arranged themselves around her the way water arranges itself around a keel, and so she walked at the head of a river of light going up the mountain road, and she taught as she walked, the way she had always taught, calling parts — *"you low ones, hold the drone; you children, take the descant; don't push, don't rush, let the mountain carry you"* — and the freed souls obeyed her as if she had been their mistress all their lives, and perhaps, in some way that had nothing to do with the Chapel Royal, she had.

Behind her, Serah Vail walked in silence, carrying in her saddlebag the ledger of souls, copied page by page in Casper's precise scribe-hand during the hours when no one could sleep. Evidence. Testimony. The end of the hunt, if the crown had any honor left, and if it didn't, evidence enough to shame it into compliance anyway. Serah had looked at the ledger for a long time before she agreed to carry it. "I put some of these in," she had said. Casper had answered: "Then you can carry some of them out."

Ahead of her, Casper walked with Ada asleep against his back, and Wren watched them and thought about choirs. About what they were for. She had spent her life believing a choir was a building, an institution, a rank and a title and a seat in a royal gallery. She had been wrong. A choir was this: a debt-ridden scribe singing three notes he learned from a cave; a mute child harmonizing with stone; a hunter holding a gate with her bare voice; a drunk leading the dead home by teaching them, gently, to breathe in time. A choir was anyone who refused to sing alone.

The sun came up over Thornfell, and hit the snow, and the whole mountainside blazed.

And the mountain, still singing, sang the sun up the rest of the way.

* * *

They camped that night at the tree line, in the lee of a granite shoulder, with fires burning high and bright because no one among them feared pursuit anymore. The freed souls had gone — most of them, drifting east and south and west through the starlight, each toward whatever waited for a soul set loose — but the air still shimmered faintly where they had passed, the way a room holds the shape of music after the singers have gone.

Wren sat sharpening a pencil stub with a knife, because old habits keep a body together, and found Ada at her elbow with the charcoal and the roll of bark-paper she had carried the whole journey.

Ada drew. Wren watched over her shoulder.

A road. A small figure on it — stick-limbed, unmistakably Ada, drawn with the fierce economy of a child who wastes no lines. Behind the figure, a mountain, and inside the mountain, drawn in cross-section like a cutaway diagram, a tiny pale man-shaped absence fading into stone. Ahead of the figure, the road forked: one branch toward a walled town with a chapel and a bell, the other branch winding away toward — Wren's breath caught — a small house with smoke from its chimney, and beside the house, a woman. Just a woman. Standing in a doorway. Waiting, or not waiting. Drawn with a question mark where her face should be.

Ada tapped the woman. Then she looked up at Wren, and her eyes were dry and steady and unbearably old.

"She's alive," Wren said quietly. It was not a question; the drawing had already answered it. "Your mother. Somewhere down in the lowlands. Alive."

Ada nodded once.

"And you're going to find her."

Ada looked back at the drawing for a long moment. Then she picked up the charcoal and wrote, beneath the house, in her small careful letters:

*When I'm ready. Not before.*

Wren laughed — a rough, wet-edged laugh, the first real one in years — and pulled the girl against her side. "Good," she said. "That's the right order. Ready first. Then find." She looked up at the dark bulk of the mountain, still humming faintly to itself like a hive gone quiet, and thought of the road ahead: down to Marrowgate, to debts unpaid and a hunt unwound and a chapel with no choir in it.

A chapel with no choir in it.

"Well," Wren said to the fire, to the girl, to the mountain, to whatever gods had survived the night. "I suppose I know where to start."

In the morning they would walk down out of the snow, three survivors and a ledger, toward a town that had no idea yet what was coming — toward reckoning, and testimony, and a debt-record scheduled to burn. But that night, high on Thornfell under the washed and glittering stars, they slept the sleep of the unowned, and the mountain sang them down into dreams, softly, softly, like a mother who has finally set down her burden and can sing for joy alone.
