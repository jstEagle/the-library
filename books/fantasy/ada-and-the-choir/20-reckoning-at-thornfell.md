## 20. Reckoning at Thornfell

The mountain let them go gently, which Casper had not expected. After everything beneath it — the vaults, the lantern-souls, the Cantor dissolving into stone with a sound like a chord finally resolving — he had braced for the Thornfell road to fight them at every switchback, to make them earn the sky. Instead the snow had softened into slush, and the pines stood dripping, and somewhere above them a bird was singing a phrase so ordinary that Ada stopped dead on the path and stared up into the branches as though she had never heard anything so strange.

She hadn't, he realized. Not with her whole self. Not until now.

Wren walked ahead of them both, moving like a woman who had left her bones somewhere in the whiteout and was still negotiating their return. Serah Vail's chains were gone — Wren had struck them from her wrists below the mountain with a note that unmade iron's memory of itself — but she walked apart from the three of them, half a dozen paces back, the way a dog walks when it is no longer sure whose dog it is. She carried the soul-ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and every time Casper glanced back he saw her hands pressed flat against it, as if checking that it still breathed.

They came down out of the treeline at dusk on the third day, and there it was: the temple of Thornfell, squatting on its shoulder of rock, candles already lit in the high windows, the bell tower black against a bruised violet sky.

Casper had spent nine years looking at that silhouette and calling it home.

He found now that he could not summon the word. It was simply a building, and inside it were men who had signed their names in a ledger of children.

* * *

They did not go through the gate. They went through the wall of the scriptorium, because Casper knew — had always known, in the way a scribe knows a building the way other men know their own hands — that the mortar on the eastern side had crumbled behind the ivy, and that a man of average size could shoulder through if he did not mind the noise.

He minded nothing anymore. That was the strangest gift the mountain had given him. He had emptied himself of his father's memories willingly, every one, down to the last cold morning when his father had signed him over to the temple's care with a handshake and averted eyes. There was a space where those memories had been, and into that space had poured something harder and cleaner than grief. He suspected it would not last. Grief always came back for its own. But tonight, tonight it held.

The ivy gave. The mortar gave. They came through into the scriptorium smelling of dust and candle tallow, and Casper stood among the desks where he had copied ten thousand prayers, and felt nothing at all for any of them.

Ada touched his sleeve. She pointed at the ledger desk — *his* desk, the one by the window with the good light — and then made a small gesture he had learned on the road: fingers opening like a bird released. *Let it go.*

"Not yet," he said, and heard how rough his voice sounded, this voice that had been taken and given back, that belonged to no temple and no debt and no father. "First we burn."

* * *

Brother Anselm found them in the archive.

Casper had planned it differently — had imagined, in the long climb down, confronting the abbot in the great hall before the assembled brothers, proof held high, some speech rehearsed and righteous. But the abbot was not in the great hall. The abbot was in the archive, kneeling before the second ledger — the true ledger, the one Casper had found by candlelight all those months ago, the one recording souls instead of coin — and he was feeding its pages into a brazier one at a time, with the careful, reverent movements of a man performing last rites.

He looked up when they entered, and Casper watched nine years of authority collapse in the old man's face in the space of a single breath.

"Casper." The abbot rose. His knees cracked. He had always seemed tall; kneeling, rising, he seemed only old. "You're alive. Praise be. We were told you'd fled with the Vail woman and the—"

"The foundling." Casper crossed the room and took the ledger out of the brazier's reach. Half of it was ash already. The remaining pages were warm and smelled of smoke, and the names on them were still legible, row upon row upon row, going back further than the temple itself went back. "Her name is Ada."

"Child, you don't understand what you're—"

"No." Casper heard his own voice come out flat and quiet, and was distantly surprised by it. He had expected shouting. What came instead was the voice he used for reading aloud, the voice trained into him for nine years, precise and unhurried. "No, I understand perfectly. I've read your ledger, Father Abbot. I've been *below*. I've seen what 'sanctification' means. I've seen the lanterns." He turned a page toward the firelight, toward a name halfway down: *Girl, aged six, mother's trade accepted, debt discharged.* "I know what my father bought with me. And I know what you sold."

Behind the abbot, in the doorway, brothers were gathering — drawn by the noise, by the smoke smell, by whatever instinct brings the faithful running when the foundation cracks. Casper saw Brother Marcus, who had taught him his letters. Saw old Brother Tomas, who dozed through compline and had once slipped Ada an extra heel of bread. Their faces were doing different things. Some were afraid. Some were only confused.

And Serah Vail stepped forward out of the shadow of the doorway, and the room went silent the way rooms go silent around soldiers.

"Serah Vail," she said, to no one and everyone. "Chief hunter, by crown warrant. This house stands accused." She set the soul-ledger on the reading table and opened it with a hand that did not shake, though Casper saw the cost of that steadiness in her jaw. "Not by me. I served this rot too long to be the one who accuses. By its own records. Read them. Every name is a child. Every entry has a signature. Count the signatures."

Nobody moved. The brazier popped.

It was Brother Tomas who came forward first, slowly, wiping his hands on his robe as though the ledger might stain him at a distance. He bent over the open page. His lips moved, reading. Then he straightened, and looked at the abbot, and his face was the worst thing in the room — worse than anger, worse than fear. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic he could not stop doing.

"How many?" Tomas asked quietly. "Father. How many of ours?"

The abbot's mouth opened. Closed. When he spoke, his voice had the wheedling quality of a man who has just discovered the size of the hole he is standing in. "It was necessity. The crown demanded tithe. The court beneath demanded— we were *preserving* the temple, we were keeping the hunt from falling on all of us, you cannot judge nine hundred years of—"

"I can count," said Tomas.

That was all. That was the abbot's fall — not a trial, not a sentence, not even an accusation in the end. Just an old brother counting, and the counting spreading through the gathered faces like frost across a window, and the abbot understanding, in the lengthening silence, that he had become a small man in a room full of people who could read.

He sat down on the floor beside his brazier, among the ashes of the pages he had tried to save himself with, and put his head in his hands, and no one helped him up.

* * *

They burned the debt-records at dawn.

Not the soul-ledger — Serah was adamant about that, wrapping it again in oilcloth, swearing she would carry it to the capital herself and lay it before whoever at court still had a conscience or the power to act like one. The hunt for the gifted would end or it would not, but it would end or fail on evidence, not on rumor. "I spent eight years hunting children for that throne," she said, cinching the saddle of the horse she'd taken from the temple stable without asking anyone's leave. "I'll spend whatever years I have left undoing it. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances. But it's what's in my hands."

Casper understood what was in her hands. He carried his own version of it: the memory of leaving Wren in the whiteout, the memory of the bargain he had struck believing it was the only way, the memory of Ada's face when she pulled his voice out of the Cantor's hoard and gave it back — freely, impossibly, spending nothing. Those things didn't balance either. You just carried them.

But the debt-records — the great ledgers of Thackeray and Osgood and Marsh and forty other family names, the columns of coin and winters and interest that had bound children to candlelight for generations — those they burned in the temple yard, in the same iron brazier, while the brothers stood watching and the smoke went up straight into a windless grey morning.

Brother Tomas brought the torch himself. Before he lit it, he turned to Casper.

"Your father's account," he said. "It's in there?"

"Page four hundred and eleven."

Tomas found the page. He read it for a long moment — longer than arithmetic required — and then he fed it to the flame first, alone, and held it until the fire took Casper's father's name entirely, and only then lit the rest.

"You owe nothing," Tomas said. It was not absolution; Casper saw that. It was a statement of fact, delivered by a man who kept accounts, from one keeper of records to another. "You never owed what they said you owed. But the record says it now, and the record is ash, and ash is the only honest ledger there is."

Casper watched the rest of it burn. Nine years of copying prayers by candlelight, measured out in winters, priced and discounted and deferred. He waited to feel triumphant.

What he felt instead was the weight of Ada's hand slipping into his, and the knowledge that page after page in the soul-ledger under Serah's arm had no such clean ending — that somewhere below, in the dark the mountain was still learning to sing over, some of those souls had burned with the Cantor and some had risen with Wren's chorus and some, perhaps, were lost in ways no reckoning would ever reach. Justice, it turned out, was not a chord resolving. It was a list of names, and you read it aloud, and you kept reading, and you did not pretend the reading was the same thing as the restoring.

Ada tugged his sleeve. She pointed at the burning ledgers, then at her own chest, then opened her fingers — the bird gesture, the letting-go — and then, carefully, she pointed at him and shook her head.

*Not you. You don't let this go.*

He knelt down to her level in the ash-smelling morning. "No," he agreed. "I won't."

* * *

Serah rode south an hour later, alone, the soul-ledger strapped behind her saddle like a wounded thing being carried to a surgeon. At the gate she reined in and looked down at the three of them — the scribe, the choir mistress, the child — and for a moment the hunter's mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was just a tired woman who had chosen, late, the harder side.

"The girl I took in the whiteout," she said to Wren. Her voice was low. "I told myself it was duty. It wasn't. It was fear wearing duty's coat. If the crown asks you — when the crown asks you — tell them Serah Vail said the hunt was wrong from the first warrant. Let them take my name off the rolls. It's the only payment I have that's worth anything."

"It's more than most offer," Wren said. "Keep the rest of yourself. You'll need it for the road."

Serah looked at Ada last. The child looked back, unblinking, and something passed between them that Casper did not try to read — some accounting of the whiteout, of the duel of songs, of a capture undone. Then Serah Vail turned her horse south toward the capital and the crown and whatever mercy or ruin waited there, and did not look back, because she was the kind of woman who understood that looking back was a luxury paid for in memory, and she had spent enough.

* * *

They walked out through the front gate, the proper way, in full view of the brothers gathered in the yard. Casper had thought he would feel smaller, walking past them. He felt instead exactly his own size, which was a thing he had never once felt in this place.

At the bend in the road, where the temple dropped out of sight behind the shoulder of the mountain, Wren stopped and looked north, toward Marrowgate and the lowlands and the chapel that was not yet built except in the fierce architecture of her face.

"There'll be letters," she said. "From the crown, when Serah's evidence lands. Summonses, probably. Inquisitions dressed as invitations." She smiled, and it was the crooked smile Casper had first seen in a Marrowgate tavern, over a mug, before he knew what she had lost. "Let them write. We'll be busy."

"Busy with what?" Casper asked, though he already knew.

Wren nodded at Ada, who had climbed onto the low stone wall at the roadside and was standing balanced on it, arms out, facing the wind coming down off Thornfell — and humming, silently, that song with no sound that cost nothing and broke everything open. The mountain hummed it back.

"That," said Wren.

And far to the south, on the road Serah Vail was riding, a rider's horn sounded — once, twice — carrying on the wind a phrase none of them could hear, but all of them, somehow, already knew.
