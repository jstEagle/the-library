## 21. A Choir of the Unowned

The chapel on Tanner's Row had been a tannery first, then a granary, then nothing at all — a long stone shell with a sagging roof and windows of horn instead of glass. Wren had chosen it precisely because it was ugly. Beautiful buildings made people behave beautifully, and beautiful behavior was a kind of lie she had no more patience for. Let them come to sing in a room that smelled faintly of old brine and sheep. Let the music be the only lovely thing in it.

She had hung her old tuning fork from a nail above the door. It was the one possession she'd carried out of the Chapel Royal's ashes, fished from the rubble with burned fingers eleven years ago, and for eleven years it had hung in her pack like an accusation. Now it hung where anyone entering had to pass beneath it, and if that wasn't a blessing, Wren thought, it was at least a dare.

They came slowly at first.

A salt-widow named Maren who hadn't spoken above a whisper since the flood took her sons, because loud sounds reminded her of the water coming through the door. A tanner's boy with a voice like a cracked bell who'd been told all his life to stop singing before someone noticed what his songs did to the people hearing them. Two sisters out of the Thornfell foothills who flinched at bells and would not say why, though everyone by now had heard the stories coming down off the mountain — the temple, the ledger, the lanterns in the dark. An old man called Doss who claimed he couldn't carry a tune in a bucket but whose humming, when he forgot himself, made bread rise faster and bruises fade.

And Ada.

Ada came every morning when the frost still silvered the sill, took her place in the second row — always the second row, never the front, never quite hiding — and sang without sound while thirty voices around her found their way into harmony. She sat with her slate on her knees in case words were needed, though she needed them less and less. Her silence had stopped being a wound in the room. It had become, Wren thought, the room's keel: the thing beneath everything that kept the whole vessel upright.

Wren taught them the way she wished someone had taught her.

"Again," she said, on the third morning, when Maren's voice broke apart on the high phrase. "But this time don't give it anything. Don't pay. The old choirs taught you to spend — a memory here, a warmth there, pour yourself into the note until the note is worth more than you are. That's how they kept us hungry. That's how they kept us *cheap*." She walked the row, her boots loud on the tannery floor. "The silent song costs nothing. It isn't smaller for costing nothing. Say it back to me."

"It isn't smaller," the choir said, raggedly, then again, firmer, "for costing nothing."

Maren tried once more. This time when her voice climbed, she did not flinch away from the grief underneath it; she let the grief be part of the pitch, and the note came out whole, and two rows over the tanner's boy began to cry without knowing why, and Wren felt the old ache in her chest that was not grief anymore but something adjacent to it, something she supposed she would have to learn to call hope.

She had lost a choir once. She knew the arithmetic of that loss by heart — nine children sent below the mountain because she'd said no too loudly and too late, eight names carved into her memory like letters into stone, and the ninth, Tam, sold anyway after she fell, after the chapel burned, after her refusal accomplished nothing except making an example of her. For years she had drunk to make the arithmetic go quiet. Now she stood sober in an ugly chapel every morning and made the numbers mean something different. Not penance. Penance was still a payment, and she was done teaching payments.

This was repair. Repair was slower. Repair did not absolve. Repair just built the thing again, better, in a uglier room.

* * *

On the sixth morning, Casper came.

He arrived at the door as the light was turning gold, and Wren saw immediately what midwinter beneath the mountain had taken from him. He moved carefully, like a man carrying water across ice. When Maren greeted him he opened his mouth and produced — nothing. His throat worked. His hands shaped a word. The air stayed empty.

He had given his voice to buy Ada's soul back, and Ada had stolen it home again through the Cantor's hoard, and yet some residue of the transaction clung to him still — Wren could see it, could almost hear it, a faint wrongness in the silence around him, like a room where a clock has recently stopped. The gift was in him. The instrument was scarred.

He wrote on the slate Ada pressed into his hands: *I can hum. Barely. It hurts.*

"Then you'll sit in the back," Wren said aloud, "and you'll listen, and you'll do nothing else, and you'll hate every minute of it, and in a year perhaps we'll try a single note." She softened. "You gave your voice away once. You don't get to spend the return of it carelessly. That's not a punishment, Casper Thackeray. That's the whole lesson."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he nodded, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, as though permission had been the thing he'd climbed the mountain twice to find.

He took the back bench beside Doss. And when the choir began — Maren's low anchor, the sisters' braided line, the boy's cracked-bell brightness, and beneath and through it all Ada's soundless song holding the shape of the music like a hand holds water — Casper closed his eyes and listened the way only a man who has paid for sound can listen.

Wren watched him and thought: *this is what I should have built the first time.* Not a choir for kings. A choir of the unowned.

* * *

It was on the ninth morning that the woman came.

Wren noticed her before she entered — a figure standing outside in the grey drizzle, looking up at the tuning fork on its nail as though it were a sign in a language she'd almost forgotten. She was thin in the way of people who had been hungry a long time and then fed badly for longer. Her coat had been good once; now it was patched with three different cloths, none matching. Her hair was going grey at the temples though her face was young enough that the grey looked earned rather than aged.

When she finally stepped inside, she hovered at the back, near Casper, and did not sing. She mouthed along with the phrases. Her lips knew them. That was the first thing Wren noticed — that this stranger's mouth moved in perfect time with a hymn from the Chapel Royal's repertory, a hymn Wren herself had arranged, a hymn that had not been sung publicly in eleven years.

The second thing she noticed was Ada.

The child had gone rigid on her bench. Her slate slipped from her knees and clattered against the floor, and she didn't pick it up. She was staring at the woman in the back with an expression Wren had no name for — terror and hunger braided so tightly together they were indistinguishable, the face of someone seeing a door they had mourned as walled-over suddenly standing open a crack, with light behind it, and being afraid of both.

Because Wren had spent a season drawing with Ada by firelight. Because she had seen the pictures — the woman with the crooked smile, the woman who signed *little bird* against her own collarbone, the woman whose eyes were drawn always, always, with a particular downward fold at the outer corners, like a deer's, like a leaf about to fall.

The stranger had those eyes.

Ada rose without picking up her slate. She crossed the floor between one verse and the next, past the sisters, past Maren, and stopped three feet from the woman, close enough to touch, and did not touch. Her hands came up — hesitated — and then made the sign. *Little bird,* against her own collarbone, small and fierce and shaking.

The woman went white.

Her hand flew to her mouth. She stared at Ada — at the child's face, at the signs, at whatever ghost of resemblance lived in the space between them — and her knees buckled, and Casper caught her arm, and she sagged onto the back bench with her chest heaving.

"I sold—" she got out, and stopped, and tried again, her voice a ruin of whisper. "I didn't sell her. I traded. There's a difference, they told me there was a difference, passage north, one soul for two lives, and I told myself—every day I told myself—" She looked up at Ada and the deer-folded eyes spilled over. "You were so small. You had a sound in you even then. I could feel it when I held you, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. They said the court would keep it safe. They said—"

Ada had not moved. She stood in the aisle with her fists at her sides, and Wren saw the war in her plainly: the child who had drawn her mother's smile by firelight wanting with her whole body to cross those last three feet, and the survivor who had hidden a hum in her chest for years because love, in her short experience, was the thing that got you sold.

The choir had gone silent. Thirty people held their breath.

Slowly — slowly, giving her all the time in the world — Ada knelt. She picked up the fallen slate from the aisle floor. She wrote on it in her careful, round hand, and turned it toward the woman.

*I'm not ready. Are you?*

The woman read it twice. Something moved through her face — shame, and then, astonishingly, a flicker of humor, bleak and real, the humor of a person recognizing her own wreckage described accurately. She shook her head. Then she stopped, and shook it differently, and reached into her coat and drew out a scrap of cloth, a child's cap, far too small, worn soft as water, which she must have carried for years against her heart the way Wren had carried a tuning fork.

She set it on the bench between them. An offering. Not a claim.

"I'm not," she whispered. "Not yet. But I'm here. Every morning. If you'll let me be here."

Ada looked at the cap for a long time. Then she took it — took it, folded it into her pocket, next to her chalk — and returned to the second row. She did not sit beside her mother. She sat where she always sat. But before the next verse began, she turned around, once, and looked at the woman with the deer-folded eyes, and held up one hand, flat, at shoulder height.

*Soon,* the gesture said. Or perhaps: *perhaps.* In Ada's language the two had always been neighbors.

Wren lifted her baton — a plain stick, tannery oak, worth nothing — and found her own eyes stinging and did not care who saw.

"From the top," she said, and her voice only broke a little. "All of us. Nobody sings alone, and nobody pays. From the top."

And the choir of the unowned began — the freed and the gifted and the grieving, the salt-widow and the cracked bell, the silent girl in the second row and the ruined woman in the back finding her voice one thread at a time, and beneath it all, from the rear bench, so faint it might have been the building itself settling, so faint that only Wren heard it and understood what it meant — Casper Thackeray, testing his scarred instrument, joined them with a single trembling note.

It cost him nothing. It was not small.

Outside, the last of the winter ice went dripping from the eaves of Tanner's Row, and somewhere north, in a temple scrubbed clean of its abbot, a new ledger lay open on a scribe's desk, blank, waiting to record something other than debts.
