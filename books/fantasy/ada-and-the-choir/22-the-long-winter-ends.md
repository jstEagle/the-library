## 22. The Long Winter Ends

The snow on the Thornfell road had gone soft overnight, the way it always did in the last week of winter — holding its shape but not its pride, slumping at the edges where the sun found it. Casper walked through it with his boots making that particular wet sound he had hated all his life, the sound of a scribe trudging to morning prayers, of a debtor trudging to another day of someone else's arithmetic. Except this morning the sound meant nothing. This morning it was only boots on snow, and no one owned them but him.

He had checked. Twice, before leaving Marrowgate, he had gone back to the ashes of the debt-records — Serah had carried copies south for the crown's magistrates, but the originals they had burned themselves, Wren striking flint while Ada stood watch with her chin lifted like a small queen presiding over an execution. He had watched twenty years of his father's borrowing curl and blacken, the ink lifting off the pages in ghosts before the fire took the paper too. *Thackeray, indentured, sum outstanding.* He had read those words so many times he could have recited them in his sleep, and now they were smoke, and the smoke was going up into a sky that belonged to no one.

His father had been dead eleven years. The debt had outlived him the way a curse does, feeding on a son who had never signed anything. Casper had spent most of his life believing that was simply what the world was: a ledger, and you were either the hand that held the pen or the line written in it.

He knew better now. He had seen what happened to people who believed the ledger was the world. He had seen the vaults beneath the mountain, where souls hung in their thousands like lanterns in a cathedral of theft, each one flickering with somebody's mother, somebody's summer, somebody's first song. And he had watched Ada reach into that hoard with her silent voice and pull his own song back out of it, the way you'd pull a drowning man from a river, without hesitation, without price, because love does not do arithmetic.

That was the lesson, if there was one. That was what he intended to carry up the mountain this morning.

* * *

The road climbed past the shrine at the first switchback, the little stone alcove where travelers used to leave offerings — a button, a crust, a carved bird — asking the mountain for safe passage. During the hunt years the alcove had filled with different offerings: locks of hair, name-tags cut from coats, the small desperate tokens of people who wanted to be remembered as more than entries in a soul-ledger if the riders took them. Most of those were gone now, gathered up by Wren's choir in the autumn and buried properly beneath a rowan tree in Marrowgate, each one sung over. But one remained, wedged deep in a crack where the gatherers hadn't reached: a child's wooden whistle, weathered silver-grey.

Casper stopped. He always stopped. He touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch a gravestone or a sleeping child's head, and walked on.

Behind him, Ada matched his pace. She had grown over the winter — not taller, exactly, though she had done some of that too, but *filled out*, the way a house fills when someone finally lives in it. Her cheeks had color. Her eyes, which had been flat as coins the day he'd found her hiding in the salt cellars with her knees drawn up and her whole self folded away behind them, had become expressive in a language entirely her own: a tilt of the head for curiosity, a wrinkle of the nose for Wren's cooking, a slow deliberate blink that Casper had learned, after some months, meant *I love you* — because she had decided, in her wordless way, that blinking was how she said it, and she said it often, and she never let him pretend he hadn't noticed.

She was carrying her drawing book under one arm, the new one, the fourth since Marrowgate. The first three lived on a shelf in Wren's schoolroom, and strangers sometimes asked to see them, and Wren let them, and Casper watched their faces change as they turned the pages — drawings of the court below, the lantern-souls, the Pale Cantor's terrible courteous face; drawings of the road, the ford, the whiteout, Wren dueling Serah Vail with a chord that cracked the air like ice; and then, page after page after page, drawings of ordinary things. Bread. Boots. A window with rain on it. A dog. The world, catalogued by a girl who had nearly lost it, hoarding the ordinary the way misers hoard gold.

This morning she had drawn something new. She showed him at the second switchback, holding the book open against her chest so he had to stop and look.

It was the mountain. Thornfell itself, from below, its peak still wearing snow like a crown it had forgotten to take off. But she had drawn lines coming out of it — fine radiating lines, like the rays around a saint's head in an old illumination, or like the rings a bell makes in water. And at the summit, drawn so small you might miss it, a single figure with its mouth open.

"Is that me?" Casper asked.

Ada nodded. Then she pointed at the figure again, and tapped her own throat, and shook her head, and pointed at the figure's throat, and nodded, and looked at him with such fierce patience that he understood: *You will sing. I will listen. That is how we do it.*

"That's how we've always done it," he said.

She smiled — her whole face doing it, unguarded, extravagant — and closed the book, and took his hand, which she had started doing sometime in midwinter without ever asking permission, and which he had never once thought to refuse.

They climbed.

* * *

The temple came into view near noon, and Casper felt almost nothing, which was itself a kind of miracle.

It sat in its saddle of rock the way it always had, grey walls, blue roof, the bell-tower standing up against the sky like a finger raised in permanent admonition. For twenty years that building had been the shape of his life. He had copied prayers in it by candlelight, ten thousand pages of them, beautiful useless script, while somewhere below his feet a second ledger recorded the true business of the house: names, prices, souls. He had thought himself safe inside it. He had thought safety was something you could earn by being useful and quiet. The temple had taught him that lesson carefully, the way you train an animal, and it had taken a mute child and a disgraced choir mistress and a burned chapel and a river ford and a toll paid in memories to teach him that quiet usefulness is just another word for complicity when the house is rotten.

The abbot was gone. Not dead — Casper had not wanted that, had discovered to his own surprise that he did not want it even after everything — but gone, sent south with Serah Vail to answer for the ledger before magistrates who had, for the first time in living memory, been given evidence they could not burn. Whether he would hang or merely rot in some respectable prison, Casper did not know and had stopped checking. Justice, he had learned, is not a thing you get to watch being finished. You plant it and walk away and trust the season.

The new keeper of Thornfell was a woman named Odile, formerly of Wren's choir, one of the freed souls who had come up out of the mountain singing and discovered, blinking in the daylight, that she had nowhere to go and every right to go anywhere. She had chosen the temple. *Someone has to keep the light and change the meaning of it,* she'd said, and Wren had kissed her forehead like a bishop, and that had been that.

Odile was in the yard as they passed, splitting kindling, sleeves rolled despite the cold. She straightened when she saw them and raised the axe in salute.

"Going up?" she called.

"Going up," Casper said.

"Take your time." She glanced at Ada, and softened, the way everyone did, the way Ada permitted. "There's bread in the chapel if the wind turns."

The wind would not turn. Casper could feel it in his teeth, in the old scar on his knuckles, in the place behind his sternum where his voice lived: a high bright pressure, the sky scrubbed clean, the particular stillness that comes on the first day of spring when the world is holding its breath between one season and the next. It was the same stillness he had felt in the Cave of First Notes, the day the mountain had shown him what it was — not a pile of stone with a corrupted echo underneath, but a *song*, sung into being before there were ledgers or temples or crowns, before anyone thought to write down what a soul cost.

Today he was going to give the first note back.

* * *

The last stretch of road left the trees behind and became only rock and sky, the switchbacks narrowing until the temple was a toy far below and the world opened out in every direction — the lowlands hazed blue to the south, the pass eastward still white, and above it all the peak itself, close enough now that he could see the individual seams in the stone, the places where the mountain's old melody ran visible through it like grain through wood.

Casper had not been up here since midwinter. None of them had. After the gates, after the rising chord, after the freed souls poured out into the snow like a river uncorked — after all of it, the mountain had needed to rest, and so had they, and so he had stayed away, half in reverence and half in fear, wondering whether the gift would still be in him when he returned, whether the voice Ada had pulled back out of the Cantor's hoard would still be *his*, or whether it would come out changed, spent, secondhand.

He needn't have worried. His voice had come back whole — warmer, if anything, the way a house is warmer after a fire has been lit in every room. But there was a difference, and he had only identified it in the past month, humming to himself over chores while Wren's students practiced scales in the yard: the difference was that none of it was owed anymore.

Every note he sang now was free.

He chose the spot the way Brannoc had shown him, that shelf of bare rock below the summit where the wind made no sound at all, as if the mountain cupped its hands around the place to listen. Ada settled beside him cross-legged, her drawing book on her knees, her face turned up. Snowmelt ticked somewhere under the stones. Far below, a bird was arguing with another bird about territory, and the sound of it rose up thin and clear across all that empty air.

Casper closed his eyes.

He thought about where to begin, and then he laughed at himself, quietly, because there was only one place to begin. There had only ever been one place. In the Cave of First Notes the mountain had shown him its origin — the first tone, the note out of which all the rest of it had been built, the foundation-stone of the world sung rather than laid. He had heard it once, resonating up through the bedrock, and he had spent the whole winter trying to remember it exactly, the way you try to hold a dream, and failing, and trying again.

But this morning, sitting in the windless hollow with a child's hand resting against his sleeve, he stopped trying to remember and simply listened — down through the rock, down through his own bones, down to that place Wren had taught him to find, the floor of the breath, where a note begins before you decide to sing it.

And the mountain helped him. That was the truth of it, the truth he would carry down and tell Wren tonight by the fire: the mountain *helped*. It leaned toward him the way a leaning thing does, the way a starving thing does not — generously, patiently, the way a grandmother leans toward a child fumbling through a tune she knows by heart. The first note rose in him like water finding its level, and he gave it shape, and gave it air, and sang it out.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. It went out across the snowfields and down the switchbacks and into the grey walls of the temple and the rowan tree in Marrowgate forty miles south, and everywhere it went, things answered. The bird stopped arguing. The snowmelt quickened. Somewhere far below, in the deep places where the Cantor had starved for centuries, stone shifted against stone with a sound like a long exhale — not hunger, not anymore, just the settling of an old animal turning over in its sleep, dreaming of the time before it was hungry.

Casper sang the first note, and then the second, and then the melody that grew out of them, the mountain's own melody, the one it had been waiting all winter to hear again. He sang it with everything he had, and everything he had was enough, because everything he had was *his* — bought with memories, yes, with his mother's face and his father's debts and Wren's last concert and a hundred small warmths spent along the road like coins flung to pursuers. The price had been real. He did not pretend otherwise, even now, even in joy. Some mornings he still reached for his mother's laugh and found the shape of the hole where it had been, and grieved, and would grieve all his life.

But grief, he had learned, is not the opposite of joy. Debt is. And he was out of debt — every kind, every ledger, every hidden column. What remained was only what remained: this voice, this morning, this child beside him with her face lifted and her eyes shining, this mountain remembering its own name.

* * *

Halfway through the melody, Ada began to sing.

She did not open her mouth. She never did. The silent song came out of her the way heat comes off a stone in summer — invisible, undeniable, arriving not in the ear but somewhere deeper, in the chest, in the blood, in whatever part of a person is older than hearing. Casper felt it wrap around his melody the way a hand wraps around another hand, and lift, and carry. Where his voice reached, hers *arrived*. Where his notes fell like light, hers moved like warmth. It was the technique that had opened an unopenable gate, that had stolen a voice back from a god, that had broken a claim two bloodlines deep — and here, on a spring morning, with nothing to break and no one to save, she used it for no reason at all except that the music was better shared.

That, Casper thought, his throat tightening around the melody without breaking it, that is the whole of it. That is everything the ledgers never learned. A song kept is a song sold. A song given is the only wealth that grows.

The melody crested. Below them, impossibly, faintly, the temple bell rang — noon, only noon, Odile pulling the rope the way she did every day — but today the bell sounded less like an admonition and more like an amen, one bronze note joining the chord and falling away satisfied.

Ada blinked at him. Slowly. Deliberately.

*I love you,* said the blink.

"I know," Casper said, and his voice did not shake, or if it did, the mountain kindly covered for him. "I know, little sister. Me too."

*Sister.* He had not planned the word. It had simply arrived, the way the first note had arrived, rising from the floor of the breath where the true things live. Sister — not by blood, though perhaps by blood too, in the way that mattered, both of them children of people who had been sold and sellers both, inheritors of a rotten ledger they had spent a winter tearing up page by page. Family was not what you were born into. Family was what you refused to sell.

Ada beamed, and flipped to a fresh page in her drawing book, and began, right there on the windy shelf below the summit, to draw the two of them — the singing brother and the silent sister, the mountain around them drawn with its rays of light, and below, tiny but unmistakable, the road going down: to the temple, to Marrowgate, to the schoolroom where Wren was surely at this moment losing patience with a room full of gifted children who could not be made to practice their scales in any orderly fashion, to the choir of the unowned, to the woman with Ada's mother's eyes who had begun, cautiously, tremulously, to attend the Tuesday gatherings, sitting at the back, not yet ready, coming anyway.

Spring had come to Thornfell. The long winter was over — the real one, the one that had lasted generations, the winter of the ledger and the lantern-vaults and the children measured for aptitude. It would take years to thaw completely. Some corners of it might never thaw. But the first note had been sung back, and the mountain had answered, and the road down was a road home, and home was full of people who owed each other nothing except breakfast, and lessons, and love freely given, which is to say everything worth owing.

Ada finished the drawing and showed it to him, and Casper laughed — a real laugh, from the floor of the breath — and took her hand, and together they started down the mountain, singing all the way, one voice aloud and one voice silent, into the long bright beginning of the year.
