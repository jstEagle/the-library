## 1. The Valley That Refuses

The Stillvale did not welcome Casper Coldharbour. It merely tolerated her, the way a scar tolerates the skin that grew over it.

She had learned its rules in her first winter, nine years ago, when she was nineteen and so hollowed out by flight that she could not remember which road she'd taken or why. She had stumbled past the border stones at the valley's mouth—two leaning monoliths furred with grey lichen, carved with symbols worn to suggestion—and felt something peel away from her. Not painfully. More like a coat lifted from her shoulders by a courteous stranger. She had stood between those stones for an hour, waiting for the loss to hurt, and it never quite had. That should have been her first warning. Losses that don't hurt are usually losses of things you've already stopped believing you deserve.

Inside the vale, magic simply died. A charm spoken at the border stones would unravel mid-word, syllables fraying into ordinary air. Enchanted objects went quiet within a day, their glow guttering like candles in a draft. Once, in her early years, a traveling peddler had come through selling luck-knots guaranteed against shipwreck and heartbreak alike; by the time he reached the far side of the valley, his cart's wheels had come off and his prices had doubled, and every knot on his racks hung as dumbly decorative as any fisherman's tangle. The peddler had laughed about it. Casper had not.

Because she had known, even then, what the valley was taking from her—and some treacherous part of her had been grateful.

Now she lived in a cottage at the vale's northern edge, where the hills folded close like cupped hands and the stream ran cold and opinionated over stones the color of old teeth. She kept goats. She kept bees. She kept, above all, a careful and deliberate smallness to her life, the way other women kept cats: something tame, something contained, something that would not turn on her in the night.

The village of Marrow's End lay two miles downslope, and it knew her as the mender. Not a witch—the word was never used, not here, not anywhere in the Stillvale, because there was nothing here for a witch to be—but a mender, which was a respectable trade. People brought her broken things. A loom with a warped treadle. A clock that had sulked for a generation. And charms, always charms: the little woven protections that grandmothers made for cradles and door lintels, the knotted cords meant to keep fevers from children and wolves from sheep.

Here was the joke the valley played on her, though only she was in on it: she could mend a charm but not cast one. Her fingers remembered the weaves. Her hands knew how a protection should sit in its knots, how the tension ought to run, where the flaw in a failing ward would hide itself like a splinter under skin. She could take a grandmother's fever-charm, sagging and threadbare, and restore it until it hummed again with something almost like purpose. But if she spoke the words that were supposed to go with the weaving—if she so much as whispered the intention behind them—the charm stayed exactly what it was: string. Dead string, in a valley that refused everything else.

So she wove silence into her work instead, and the villagers called it skill, and paid her in eggs and firewood and the particular wary courtesy that mountain folk extend to anyone whose usefulness they cannot quite explain. It was enough. She told herself it was enough, the way you tell yourself a room is warm when you have forgotten what sunlight feels like.

On the evening it all began to crack, she was mending a birth-ward for Tilda Marsh, whose daughter was swollen with her first child and whose nerves had grown correspondingly enormous. The charm was an old one, river-willow and red thread, and someone had tied it badly decades ago—a crossed knot at the third binding that would let bad dreams leak through like water through a roof. Casper sat at her table with the thing spread before her, lamplight pooling yellow around her hands, picking the crossed knot apart stitch by stitch.

Outside, dusk was coming down the valley the way it always did, pouring down the slopes like ink into water, gathering blue in the hollows first. The border stones would be invisible now, just two darker shapes against the dark. Somewhere beyond them, the world went on being full of the things she had renounced. She tried not to think about them. She had gotten very good at not thinking about things.

The knock came at full dark.

Three knocks, spaced wrong—not the quick rattle of a neighbor, not the measured patience of the miller collecting his debt. Three slow knocks, each one placed like a footstep, and then silence, and then, after long enough that Casper had begun to convince herself she'd imagined it, three more.

Her hands went still over the birth-ward. Nine years is a long time to practice stillness, and she had become a master of it; her heartbeat didn't even quicken, though her mind began moving very fast indeed, running through the short list of people who might knock at her door after dark, and finding the list empty, and disliking what that emptiness implied.

She took up the poker from beside the hearth—it was iron, and heavy, and in the Stillvale that was the whole of anyone's arsenal—and opened the door on its leather hinges.

A child stood on her step.

It could not have been more than seven or eight years old, small for even that, dressed in mud to a depth that suggested days rather than hours of travel. Mud in its hair, mud crusted in the creases of its eyelids, mud dried in pale cracks across cheeks that must once have been washed. The clothes beneath the mud had been fine once—she could see the ghost of embroidery at the collar, silver thread gone grey—and that detail snagged in her like a fishhook. Fine clothes, ruined. A child from somewhere, walking to nowhere, arriving at the edge of the world.

Its eyes were the worst part. Not wild, the way a lost child's eyes should be. *Attentive.* They fixed on her face with a gravity no eight-year-old should carry, the way an old dog watches a door it knows will open.

"Are you lost?" Casper asked. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Where are your parents?"

The child said nothing. Its lips parted, closed. It looked at her mouth as though her words were objects it could see but not touch.

"Come inside," she heard herself say, and hated the softness in it, and opened the door wider anyway. "You're frozen half to death."

The child stepped over her threshold with the strange formality of a cat entering a room it has decided to own. It stood dripping on her clean floor, tracking grey across the boards, and looked around the cottage with that same grave attention—at the herbs hanging from the rafters, at the mending basket, at the banked hearth—as though checking each item off some inventory only it could see.

Casper moved on instinct then, the instinct she had spent nine years pretending was mere habit. She heated water. She found bread and the last of the honey. She worked the mud from the child's hair with a comb and her fingers, and the child endured it without flinching, without speaking, watching her in the small brass mirror the whole time as though memorizing her.

Under the filth, the child's skin was pale as paper birch. Under the ruined finery, at the base of its throat, there was a mark: a small scar, precise as a signature, in the shape of a circle broken by a single vertical line.

Casper's hands stopped moving.

She knew that shape. She had never seen it before in her life, and she knew it the way you know a word you've only ever read in a dead language—known, and forbidden, and buried so deep she hadn't known it was buried until this moment it clawed its way up. Aldous's voice surfaced in her memory, unbidden, nine years distant and yet suddenly close enough to touch: *Never draw the open ring, girl. Never speak of it. Some doors are locked from both sides.*

She had not thought of Aldous in months. She had trained herself not to. Now his name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"You can't stay," she said quietly, to the child, to herself, to the valley. "People will talk. I can't—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a family in Marrow's End. The Marches. They lost a daughter to fever two winters back. They'd take you in."

The child turned from the mirror and looked at her directly, and for the first time, it spoke.

The sound was wrong. That was Casper's first coherent thought—that the *sound* was wrong, too deep for such a small body, too resonant, each syllable landing with the weight of a bell struck underwater. The words themselves were worse. They were built of sounds she had no names for: a rising tone that bent in the middle, a click deep in the throat, a long vowel that seemed to continue after the child's mouth had closed. No tongue of any kingdom she knew. No tongue, some cold and rising part of her insisted, of any kingdom *living*.

The child finished, and waited, and its expression held something terrible in its patience. Expectation. As though it had asked a perfectly reasonable question and was prepared to wait years for the answer.

"I don't understand," Casper whispered.

The child frowned—a small, precise frown, more disappointed than distressed—and reached out and took her hand. Its fingers were ice-cold despite the fire, despite the soup, despite everything. It pressed her palm flat against its own small chest, over the heart, over the broken-circle scar, and spoke again, slower this time, enunciating each impossible syllable like a lesson.

And this time, Casper felt the words.

Not heard—*felt*. Each syllable landed in her breastbone like a pebble dropped into a well, sinking, sinking, and where they settled they left something behind: a faint warmth, a pressure, a sense of meaning pressing against the inside of her skull like a face pressed against glass. Close. So close. A summons, something in her knew, with a certainty that had nothing to do with her mind. This child had walked out of the world's ruin to the edge of a valley that refused all magic, carrying words older than any kingdom, and it had walked them straight to *her* door.

The child released her hand. It picked up the bowl of soup she'd set out, and ate, neatly and completely, and then it looked at her again and said one more phrase—short this time, two syllables, gentle almost—and pointed one muddy finger at her chest.

At the place where the words had been landing.

Outside, the wind came down off the hills and moved through the valley, and the border stones at the vale's mouth stood in the dark doing what they had always done, refusing everything, keeping everything out—or, Casper thought with a dread that arrived slowly and then all at once, keeping something *in*.
