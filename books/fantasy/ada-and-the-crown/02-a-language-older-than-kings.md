## 2. A Language Older Than Kings

The child slept in Casper's bed, and Casper sat awake in the chair by the cold hearth, watching the small chest rise and fall as though breath were the only honest thing left in her house.

She had done what she could. Stripped off the mud-caked clothes and washed them in the basin, fed the child bread softened in goat's milk, examined bare feet for cuts and found them unmarked—strange, that, when the border stones lay a full day's walk from anywhere, and the Stillvale's paths were all stones and bramble. The child had simply appeared at her door at dusk, as if the valley itself had carried her in on its refusal.

And she had not spoken. Not a word. She had eaten, drunk, let herself be washed and bundled into Casper's spare nightshirt without protest or thanks, and then slept with the deep, boneless surrender of someone who had walked a very long way from something worse than distance.

Casper told herself she would take the child to the reeve in the morning. Tell him a foundling had come out of the hills, no name, no tongue, let the village sort it out the way villages sorted everything—by absorbing it, assigning it chores, forgetting it had ever been strange. That was the Stillvale way. That was *her* way: nine years of being unremarkable, of mending other people's charms because she could not cast them, of keeping her hands busy so her mind would stay quiet.

But the child had spoken. Once. At the threshold, before sleep took her, looking up at Casper with eyes the color of rain over slate, she had said something—three syllables, four, soft as ash settling—and the sound of it had gone through Casper like a needle finding an old scar.

She did not know what the words meant. But some part of her, some part she kept locked in a room at the back of herself, had understood them perfectly, and that part was still trembling.

* * *

Morning came gray and unhurried. The child woke watchful, ate more bread, watched Casper churn butter and mend a neighbor's luck-knot—a simple thing, a braid of red thread meant to hold good fortune near a doorway, which Casper could repair but never renew, because renewal required a spark of working and the Stillvale permitted none. The child watched her fingers move through the knot's pattern with an attention that felt less like curiosity and more like recognition.

"You'll have to have a name," Casper said, mostly to fill the silence. "Even a made-up one. The reeve will want something for his ledger."

The child looked at her. Opened her mouth.

"No," Casper said quickly, holding up both hands. "Don't—not yet. Eat your bread."

Because she had decided something in the night, in the chair, in the dark. Before anyone else heard that voice again, she needed to know what it was saying. And there was exactly one place in the world where that answer might live: a book she had sworn never to open, in a box she had sworn never to unlock, buried beneath the floorboards under her bed like a sin she couldn't bring herself to confess or commit.

Aldous's lexicon. Her master's forbidden book.

She waited until the child wandered out to the garden to stare at the bees—which she did with an odd solemnity, as if greeting each one—and Casper knelt, pried up the board, and lifted out the box.

It was smaller than memory insisted. Wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone stiff with age. Inside: a bundle of Aldous's letters she'd never read, a dried sprig of something that might once have been rowan, and the lexicon itself—slim, bound in leather gone dark as old blood, its title stamped in letters she had never been able to read until this moment.

Until this moment, the letters had been blank to her. She had opened the book once, as a girl of fourteen, curious and careless, and seen nothing but empty pages, and Aldous had taken it from her hands gently and said, *Not yet, little wren. It isn't hiding from you. You're hiding from it.*

Now the cover read, in silvered script that seemed to shift if she looked at it directly:

**LEXICON OF THE COVENANT TONGUE.**

Her mouth went dry. She knew that word the way you know a word from a dream—*Covenant*—without knowing where the knowledge came from. It sat in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without landing.

The child came back inside. Stood in the doorway, dripping garden damp, and looked at the book in Casper's hands with an expression far too old for her face.

"Say it again," Casper whispered. "What you said last night. Say it again."

The child considered her for a long moment. Then, in that voice like ash and honey, she spoke:

*"Sethari vael. Omma keth-rin. Sethari vael, anna Corvenn."*

The sounds went through the room and the room *leaned toward them*. Casper would swear to that later—the candle flames bent, the dust hung suspended, the whole house held its breath. And in her own chest, the stone hit bottom.

She opened the lexicon with shaking hands.

* * *

The pages were dense with script in two columns: the old tongue on the left, glosses in a flowing common hand on the right. And here was the terrible thing, the thing that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world: she could read the glosses. Of course she could—they were plain words, *water*, *bind*, *witness*, *name*. But between the columns, faint as breath on glass, ran annotations in a different hand entirely, cramped and slantwise, and she knew that handwriting the way she knew her own reflection.

Aldous. He had annotated the forbidden book. He had owned it, studied it, filled its margins—and he had given it to her to bury, not to burn. Masters burned what they wanted dead. They buried what they wanted *kept*.

*Sethari*, said the lexicon. *Heir. Lit. "the one who comes after."*

Casper read the word four times. The child had said it twice, pointing—not at herself.

Pointing, last night, at Casper.

*Omma keth-rin.* She found it further down the page. *The binding wakes. Lit. "the knot remembers its rope."*

Her skin prickled. She thought, absurdly, of the luck-knots she mended, of thread and tension and the way a knot holds only while both ends are held.

*Anna Corvenn.* This one took longer; the phrase appeared in no entry she could find, but the marginal note beside a related root read, in Aldous's slanted hand: *Corvenn — the anchor-name. See: the Stillvale working. See: what I have done and why. Do not look for this page again.*

She looked at the page again. Of course she looked at the page again.

And then she noticed her hands.

There was a mark on the back of her left one, fine as a vein of gold in quartz, tracing the exact path her finger had followed along the line for *heir*. She turned her hand in the light. It didn't smudge. It didn't hurt. It simply *was*, a thin bright filament lying just under the skin, as though the word had soaked through her fingertip and set somewhere beneath.

"No," she said aloud. "No, no—"

The child watched her with those slate-colored eyes and said, patiently, as if explaining weather to a fool:

*"Sethari vael."*

And the second mark bloomed across Casper's wrist, cool as water, spelling itself along her pulse.

She threw the book onto the table. Backed away from it. Pressed her marked hand against her apron as if the word could be blotted out like spilled milk. But she could feel it sitting there, patient, luminous, *legible*—and worse, she could feel that it fit. That the marks were not wounding her but *completing* her, the way a key's teeth complete a lock.

Three centuries, the frontispiece claimed. Three centuries since the Covenant-tongue had passed out of living use, since the last speakers died or were silenced, since the great Concord of binding fell and the realms drew their borders and forgot each other. A dead language. Dead things did not knock on doors at dusk. Dead things did not bloom under the skin of hedge-witches who had spent nine years being nobody.

"It's a summons," she said, and her voice sounded far away to herself. "You're—you're summoning me."

The child tilted her head. Then she pointed, deliberately, at the marks on Casper's hand and wrist, and spoke the longest sentence yet, slow and clear, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

*"Vael omma rin. Vael omma rin, anna Corvenn. Keth-alla. Keth-alla, o serath."*

Casper made herself pick the book back up. Made herself translate, word by word, her finger moving down the columns even as new lines of light rose to meet it—one across her knuckle for *vael*, heir; one curling at her elbow for *keth-alla*, which the lexicon gave as *the fraying, the unraveling of the great bind*; and the last phrase, *o serath*, which stopped her breath, because the gloss read simply: *come home.*

By the time she reached the end of the sentence, her forearm bore a delicate lattice of golden script from wrist to elbow, and she understood—with the calm that arrives at the center of terror—that the marks were not random. They were accumulating. Phrase by phrase, translation by translation, they were writing something on her skin, and the something had a shape she almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face across a crowded market before the crowd closes.

*Aldous,* she thought. *What did you do to me? What am I?*

The child crossed the room. Took Casper's marked hand in both of hers—small, warm, mud still in the creases of her knuckles—and turned it over, studying the golden script with the gravity of a scholar reading her own thesis. Then she looked up.

"Heir," the child said—in the common tongue, plain and clear and heartbreakingly ordinary, her first word in any language Casper knew. "Anchor. Come home."

Then her eyes rolled back, and she folded like laundry off a line, and Casper caught her an instant before the floor did.

* * *

She laid the child on the bed and sat with two fingers pressed to that small throat, counting a heartbeat that was slow and strong and utterly untroubled, as though speaking a dead language awake and then collapsing were simply the day's expected labor. Whatever the child was, she wasn't dying. She was *spent*. A lamp burned down to the rim of its oil.

Casper looked at her arm.

In the flat gray light the lattice of marks glowed faintly, and now that she forced herself to truly look—to stop flinching and *read*—she saw what the accumulated phrases composed. Not a spell. Not a warning. A name.

Written in a dead tongue, in light under her skin, letter by letter across nine years of hidden life: a name she had never been called, longer than Casper, older than Casper, arranged in the grammar of the Covenant-tongue the way a keystone is arranged in an arch. *Anna Corvenn.* The anchor-name. The name the margins warned her not to look for.

It was her. She knew it the way she knew her own hands, her own grief, the specific loneliness of the Stillvale's silence. Somewhere, three centuries ago or nine years ago or both, someone had written this name into the world, and the world had been waiting ever since for someone to say it back.

The knocking, when it came, nearly stopped her heart—but it was only the neighbor's boy with a basket of late plums, calling through the door that the reeve wanted word about the foundling, that folk were asking, that strangers had been seen on the high road asking after children.

Strangers. On the high road. Asking after children.

Casper stood very still in the middle of her small, careful house, with a sleeping oracle in her bed and a dead language written on her arm and a forbidden book open on her table, and understood that the life she had built here—nine years of knots and butter and deliberate smallness—was already over. The valley had kept magic out. Nothing, she saw now, had ever promised to keep *her* safe.

She looked north, toward the hills, toward the cottage where Aldous had taught her everything except the truth.

"Aldous," she said quietly, to the empty room, to the marks, to whatever was listening. "You and I are going to have a conversation."

She packed the lexicon, wrapped the child in her warmest cloak, and stepped out into a morning that felt, for the first time in nine years, like the first page of something.
