## 3. What the Master Buried

The road to Aldous's cottage had not changed in nine years, and that was the first wrong thing.

Roads change. Ruts deepen, branches fall, brambles creep or are cut back by passing hands. But the track that wound up through the birches toward the master's door was exactly as Casper remembered it—every stone in its place, every root worn smooth in precisely the shape her boots had made as a girl of sixteen walking them twice a day for water. It was as if the road itself had been holding its breath, waiting for her to come back and finish whatever she had started.

She walked it now with the child's hand in hers, small fingers cold even inside her own glove. The girl had not spoken since the night she arrived at Casper's door, but she had not needed to. When Casper had said *I know someone who can help*, the child had simply looked at her with those old, old eyes and nodded once, like a queen granting permission.

The cottage came into view through the trees at midmorning, and Casper stopped so abruptly that the child bumped against her hip.

It was ransacked.

Not burned, not broken—the way of truth-binders, she would learn later, was never to break what they could unmake—but emptied. The shutters hung at angles. The herb racks along the south wall were bare, their hooks glinting nakedly. The garden gate lay flat in its own trampled rosemary, and the door—

The door was sealed with wax.

A disc of grey-green wax the size of a dinner plate, pressed into the wood where handle met plank, stamped with a sigil she knew only from stories mothers told naughty children: an open eye crossed by a quill. A truth-binder's seal. The Crown's answer to a locked door. Nothing passed through it—not air, not light, not rumor—and nothing ever would again until the inquisitor who set it chose otherwise.

Casper stood in the ruined rosemary and felt something in her chest go very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before weather.

"They've been here," she said aloud, though the child could not understand her words. Perhaps she understood more than words. Her hand tightened in Casper's.

*You hid from magic for nine years,* Casper thought, staring at the seal. *And the Crown found you anyway. Or found him. Or found the thing he left behind when he carved you out of yourself.*

Because that was what she had begun to suspect in the small hours of the last three nights, sitting up with Aldous's forbidden lexicon open on her knees while the child slept: that the Stillvale was no accident of geography. That silence did not simply *happen* to a valley. Someone had made it. And someone had paid for it.

She had a terrible suspicion about whose hands had held the knife.

* * *

Nine years ago, on the last night, there had been snow.

Casper remembered it in fragments, the way one remembers anything from the worst night of one's life—in shards too sharp to hold together. She remembered Aldous standing at the hearth with his sleeves rolled up, his forearms inked to the elbow in script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it. She remembered being afraid, and being ashamed of being afraid, because this was Aldous, who had taught her to read, who had saved her from the workhouse, who had never once raised his voice in eleven years.

"Sit down, child," he had said gently. "This will only hurt if you fight it."

That should have been the warning. Kind men do not say such things.

There had been a circle chalked on the floor, and within it smaller circles nested like rings of a tree, and in the innermost ring a single word written in a language she did not yet know was called Covenant-tongue. He had asked her to stand in it. She had asked why. He had smiled—that warm, crinkled smile she had trusted more than her own heartbeat—and said:

"Because the world is full of teeth, Casper, and I mean to build you a place without any."

She had stepped into the circle because she loved him. That was the whole of it. That was the whole ugly simple truth she had spent nine years refusing to look at: she had not fled power. She had stepped into a circle because a kind man asked her to, and everything after followed from that.

What happened next lived in her memory as sensation rather than image. Cold, first—a cold that began in the floor beneath her feet and climbed her bones like frost climbing a window. Then the sense of being *read*, thoroughly and without shame, every page of her turned by patient fingers. And then the pulling. Something leaving her—not painfully, worse than painfully, *surgically*, with care, the way a surgeon lifts out the thing that must not be touched roughly. She had screamed. She thought she had screamed. Snow had come through the walls somehow, whirling around them both inside the cottage, and Aldous had been carving—not with a knife, with his finger, tracing lines in the air that hung there glowing, lines that ran outward through the walls and away into the dark, over the hills, down into the valley, miles of them, a web of light settling over the whole Stillvale like a net over a pond.

And at the center of the net, a knot. A knot shaped like a girl.

When it was done she had woken on the hearthstones with snow melting in her hair and Aldous weeping—actually weeping, she remembered that with strange clarity, his tears hissing where they struck the chalked circle—and he had gathered her up and said, "Forgive me. There was no other anchor. Forgive me."

She had not answered. She had walked out into the snow that same hour, past the border stones, and kept walking until she reached the town of Harrow's Ford and a room above a chandler's shop, and she had not cast so much as a cantrip since. Not because she couldn't.

Because she had believed, all these years, that she was fleeing what Aldous had *made* her.

Now, standing before his sealed door with a mute child tugging at her hand, she understood at last that she had been fleeing what he had *unmade*. There was a difference, and the difference was the size of a life.

* * *

The seal would not let her pass, of course. She pressed her palm to the door and felt the wax hum against her skin like a trapped wasp, and beyond it the cottage breathed its closed, stale breath. Through the gap where a shutter had torn loose she could see the wreckage of the main room: shelves stripped, the great desk split open along its seams, drawers pulled out and left hanging like tongues. They had taken everything written. Everything *true*, a truth-binder would say. Grimoires, letters, ledgers, the lexicon's twin—whatever existed here in ink had gone into a Crown satchel and away.

But Aldous had not kept everything in ink. Some things he had kept in her.

Casper circled the cottage slowly, the child trailing her like a small shadow, and at the north wall—where the plaster had cracked in a long diagonal seam, old damage never repaired—she stopped. Because the crack was not a crack. Up close, in the flat grey light, she could see that the line was too regular, too deliberate, and that it was not damage at all but script, hair-fine letters running the length of the wall and continuing, she realized with a lurch, onto the next wall, and the next, circling the entire cottage in a band of Covenant-tongue written directly into the stone and plaster.

Aldous had built his wards into the house itself. And the truth-binders had taken his books but they had not taken his *house*, because a house is not evidence; a house is just a house, unless you know how to read it.

Her fingers found the first letter. She knew perhaps forty words of Covenant-tongue now, learned in three feverish nights from the lexicon, and the first line gave her four of them: *bound*, *anchor*, *kept*, *willing*.

*Willing.* Her stomach turned over. The word sat there in the wall like a lie set in stone, and she understood suddenly that it was not a lie—it was a legal term, a covenantal term, the word that made everything else permissible. The old Covenants, the lexicon said, required consent. Not enthusiastic consent, not informed consent. Just the word, spoken or signed or tricked out of someone, and then the binding held forever.

He had made her willing. Eleven years of kindness, of warmth, of *child*—he had grown her consent like a crop and harvested it in one snowy night.

Casper's hand was shaking. She traced onward anyway, word by word, and the wall gave up its secrets grudgingly, in fragments: *the valley shall be silent... the heir shall sleep... until the last of the line wakes the tongue...*

She stopped.

*The heir.*

She read it again. Read the line before it, and the line after, fitting the forty words she knew around the gaps like stones around a missing keystone. *The anchor shall be drawn from the blood of the wardens... the last of the line... the heir shall sleep until—*

Until what? The script ran on into a section too eroded to read, decades of rain having eaten the letters, and Casper stood there with her heart slamming against her ribs, looking at the word *heir* and thinking not of herself.

Thinking of a mud-caked child who had appeared at her door speaking a language dead three hundred years, whose arrival had cracked the Stillvale's silence like ice cracking on a spring pond—she had heard it, hadn't she, that first morning? A bird singing a note too complex, a wind carrying a whisper of grammar?

The child tugged her sleeve. Casper looked down. Those ancient eyes were fixed not on her face but on the wall, on the word *heir*, and the child's mouth opened, and what came out was not the broken syllables of their first night but a full sentence, smooth and fluent, in the tongue older than kings—and this time, for the first time, Casper understood it without the lexicon, the meaning arriving in her mind the way meanings arrive in dreams, whole and undeniable:

*"You are not the last. You are the door."*

Behind them, down in the valley, a bell began to ring—the alarm bell at Harrow's Ford, the one that meant riders on the north road.

Casper took the child's hand and ran.
