## 4. The Queen of Ashes

The near realm had no sun, and Vessandra had long ago stopped missing it.

What it had instead was the Ashfall—a slow, perpetual snowing of grey flakes that drifted down through a sky the color of an old bruise, settling on the terraces of her ruined court, on the shoulders of her hollow-eyed courtiers, on the thrones where lesser lords still sat pretending their kingdoms had not crumbled to dust around them. The ash did not burn. That was the cruelty of it. It simply accumulated, patient as grief, until everything it touched took on the soft, blurred outlines of something remembered rather than something lived.

Vessandra stood at the high window of her tower and watched it fall.

Below her, in the great hall that had once held ten thousand voices and now held perhaps three hundred, her court moved through the motions of eternity. A baron whose name she had forgotten fed wine to a shadow. Two duchesses who had been beautiful for so long that beauty had curdled into habit played a game with pieces carved from the knucklebones of a god. Somewhere a musician drew sound from an instrument with no strings, and the music was lovely and meant nothing, as all music here meant nothing, because meaning required consequence and consequence required time and time, in the near realm, was a river dammed at both ends.

Three hundred years since the Covenant had sealed. Three hundred years since Aldous Coldharbour's clever hands and his cleverer apprentice had wound the worlds shut like a fist, and Vessandra had gone from queen of all the twilight countries to a whisper pressed flat against a windowpane.

She had learned to be patient. Patience was the one luxury exile granted in abundance.

* * *

She felt the child before anyone told her.

It came as a tremor in the ash—not the falling of it but the falling-through, a single flake drifting down past the boundaries of the near realm into some crack between worlds, and Vessandra turned from the window so quickly that her reflection in the dark glass lagged half a heartbeat behind her, as reflections in this place sometimes did, out of respect or fear.

"Tam," she said.

He was there before the echo of her voice finished, because he was always there before echoes finished; it was his single talent and he wore it the way other men wore fine coats. He was slight and quick, with a face designed by someone who understood that charm is more useful than beauty—arranged to be likable rather than admired, warm rather than striking. His eyes were the exception. They were pale as watered milk and held nothing whatsoever, which Vessandra had found, over the centuries, made people trust him more. People trusted what they could not read. They mistook emptiness for honesty.

"My queen."

"The seal has cracked." She crossed the hall, her skirts whispering over ash, and the courtiers nearest her found urgent business elsewhere, because even after three hundred years they knew the weather of her moods. "A child has come through. Mute, mud-caked, dropped into the mortal world like a coin through a torn pocket. Do you know what that means?"

"That your patience is rewarded," Tam said.

"It means," Vessandra said, "that the last heir is awake. It means the Covenant is failing on its own, without any help from me, which I confess wounds my pride—I spent two centuries picking at those seams and the thing finally unravels itself." She smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes, which suited them; they had grown accustomed to solitude. "And it means that somewhere in the mortal world, a woman named Casper Coldharbour has just heard words in a dead language and felt them crawl across her skin."

"You want the child brought to you."

"I want *her* brought to me. The child is the key. The woman is the lock." Vessandra stopped before him, close enough that the ash settled on both their shoulders alike, indifferent as ever to rank. "Aldous made her, Tam. Carved her out of covenant-stuff and mortal flesh and hid her in the one valley in creation where her own nature couldn't find her. He built a prison and called it a refuge, and she has lived in it for nine years thanking him for the walls." She let that sit. She was very good at letting things sit. "I am going to offer her the truth. And then I am going to offer her something better than truth."

"Which is?"

"A story she'll want to live inside." Vessandra reached up and straightened Tam's collar, a gesture almost maternal, almost tender, performed with the same care she gave to everything: precisely, and for effect. "Truth is cold. Truth says: you were made as a tool, your whole life was shaped by another's hand, your refuge is your cage. No one can bear that naked. But wrap it right—tell her she was chosen, tell her she belongs to something older than kings, tell her that the only person who ever lied to her loved her enough to do it—and she will walk to you herself. Belonging, Tam. It is the oldest currency there is, and mortals spend it like drunkards."

"And if she refuses?"

Vessandra looked at him for a long moment. Ash drifted between them.

"Then we are all of us, eventually, dust," she said lightly. "Bring her to me anyway."

* * *

Tam left at dusk, though dusk in the near realm was a formality—the sky merely deepened its bruise and the ash kept falling—and Vessandra returned to the window to watch him go, if watching could be said to apply to a departure that left no door opened and no door closed, only a Tam-shaped absence where a Tam had been.

She did not think about the child. Children were instruments, and instruments did not interest her until played.

She thought about Aldous.

Three hundred years, and she could still see him as he'd been on the last day—young then, or young-seeming, hedge-witch cleverness sharpened into something terrible, standing on the broken rim of the first Covenant-stone with blood on his cuffs and his apprentice's small hand gripped in his own. He had spoken the binding in the old tongue, the true tongue, the tongue the child now babbled in a mortal valley, and every word had been a nail, and Vessandra had been nailed.

But here was the thing she had never told her court, never told Tam, never told the knucklebone-playing duchesses or the stringless musician: she had heard the second voice. Underneath Aldous's ringing incantation there had been another voice, smaller, cracking with terror and grief, saying words that were not the binding's words at all. A girl's voice. Saying *I'm sorry*, saying *I didn't want this*, saying—though Vessandra had not understood it then, and had three hundred years to turn it over—something that sounded terribly like a name being spoken and unspoken in the same breath.

The heir had helped bind the Covenant. The heir had also, perhaps, tried to break it, in the only way a terrified child could: by hiding part of herself inside the working, a splinter under the world's skin.

And now the splinter had walked out of the Stillvale wearing a woman's body and a mender's calluses, and Vessandra intended to be there when it remembered what it was.

"She will come to me," Vessandra said to the empty room, to the falling ash, to no one. "They always come. All I have ever had to offer is the one thing no one else will give them: permission to stop pretending."

The ash fell. In the hall below, the music without strings played on, lovely and meaningless, and somewhere beyond the sealed edge of the world, a woman in a quiet valley was lighting lamps against the dusk, unaware that she had already been claimed twice—once by the man who made her, and once by the queen who was coming to unmake her.

* * *

Tam traveled light, because Tam was mostly traveling-light-shaped to begin with.

He crossed the crack in the Covenant the way he crossed everything—sideways, smiling, apologizing to the seam itself for the intrusion—and stepped out into the mortal world on a hillside smelling of cut grass and woodsmoke, under a sky so aggressively blue it seemed to be showing off. He sneezed. Mortal air was always so *insisted-upon*.

The Stillvale announced itself before he saw it: a hush, a wrongness, a place where the world held its breath. At the border stones he paused, feeling the magiclessness press against him like deep water, and marveled, as he always marveled, at the audacity of it. Someone had carved silence into the shape of a valley. Someone had used a living girl as the keystone and called it mercy.

He walked in. His charms died at the stones—one by one, little deaths, like candles pinched out—and what remained walking down the lane toward the cottage with the crooked chimney was only a man, charming and hollow-eyed, carrying nothing but an offer.

He knocked at dusk, because dusk was honest. Doors answered differently at dusk.

Inside, he heard the small sounds of a life being lived carefully: a kettle, a chair, footsteps pausing. Then the door opened a hand's width, on its chain, and a woman's eye appeared in the gap—wary, sharp, lit by lamplight.

"Good evening," Tam said, and smiled his smile, the one that opened doors. "Forgive the hour. I've come a long way to tell you a story about yourself, and I'm afraid it doesn't keep."

Behind the woman, in the shadows of the cottage, a small figure went rigid—and in a tongue dead three centuries, in a voice like a bell rung once, the child spoke a single word.

Tam's smile did not waver, but behind his empty eyes something sat up and paid attention, because he recognized the word, and the word was a name, and the name was not the woman's.

It was the child's own—and it meant, in the old tongue, *the one who chooses*.
