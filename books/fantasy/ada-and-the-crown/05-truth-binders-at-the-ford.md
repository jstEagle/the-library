## 5. Truth-Binders at the Ford

They had been walking for three days when the river appeared through the trees like a promise.

Casper heard it before she saw it—the low, constant mutter of water over stones—and something in her chest loosened for the first time since the child had arrived at her door. The Stillvale had no rivers that sang. It had streams, yes, obedient ones, but the water there moved as though it had been told to, and Casper had never realized how much she missed the sound of water that did not know it was being listened to.

The child walked beside her with the tireless, eerie steadiness of someone who had never learned to complain. She had stopped trying to name the child in her head; the marks on her own forearm had settled into a pattern she could almost read now, a thin silver script that surfaced whenever the girl spoke and faded by morning. *Wren*, the script seemed to say, though whether that was the child's name or simply the word for *small bird that survives winter* in a language dead three centuries, Casper could not say. Wren suited her. Small bird that survives winter.

"We'll cross at the ford," Casper said, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the wind's. "There's a road on the far side. Roads mean towns, and towns mean I can buy us bread instead of stealing it."

Wren looked up at her. The girl's eyes were the color of deep water over old stone, and they held an attention no child should possess—as if she were listening to more than words, to the spaces between them, to whatever Casper was not saying. Which, lately, was a great deal.

Casper had not said aloud what she'd found at Aldous's cottage: the ransacked shelves, the missing grimoire, the truth-binder's seal of black wax cooling on the door like a dead thing. She had not said that she remembered now—remembered the night Aldous had carried her, nine years old and feverish, to the border stones of the Stillvale, remembered his hands shaking as he pressed them to her sternum, remembered him whispering *forgive me* while the valley went silent around them like a held breath. She had not said that her refuge was a wound, and the wound was shaped exactly like her.

The ford was wide and shallow, the water braided silver around dark stones. On the near bank stood three of the old waymarkers—waist-high pillars of granite, their runes worn to shadows. Border stones. Casper knew them on sight the way you know a scar in a mirror. Beyond them, the world where magic lived. On this side, the mute country of the Stillvale's long silence, which she had made without knowing she was making anything at all.

She was halfway across, Wren's small hand cold in hers, the water tugging at her ankles like a curious animal, when she felt it.

Not heard. Felt—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like copper coins and candle smoke. The air on the south bank changed texture, the way silk changes when someone runs a hand along it wrong-ways.

"Hold still," she whispered, and hated how her voice shook.

They came out of the treeline one by one, unhurried, the way wolves are unhurried. Six figures in grey travel cloaks, hoods back, faces bare because truth-binders had nothing to hide and wanted you to know it. Their badges caught the afternoon light: a scale crossed with an open eye, worked in dull iron. No gilding. The Crown did not decorate its truths.

At their center walked a woman with grey shot through her black braid and a face like a verdict delivered kindly. She stopped at the water's edge and did not draw a weapon. Truth-binders rarely needed to.

"Casper Coldharbour," the woman said, and her voice carried across the water without effort, flat and clear as a struck bell. "My name is Serah Dane. Inquisitor of the Third Circle. I would like very much not to hurt you."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. The water pushed gently at her legs, patient, indifferent. Nine years. Nine years of mending charms she couldn't cast, of salves and stitches and careful ordinary days, of teaching herself to flinch away from every flicker of the uncanny—and here it was, standing on the bank in iron-trimmed grey, calling her name like it had been waiting in a ledger somewhere all along.

"You have the seal on your door," Serah Dane continued. "You've read it, I assume. You understand what it means. You are named in a writ of inquiry concerning the practice of unlicensed craft, the concealment of a bound artifact—" her eyes flicked, briefly, to Wren, and Casper felt the look like a hand on the back of her neck, "—and the harboring of persons designated Crown property under the terms of the Old Accord."

"She's a child," Casper said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "She's a starving, silent child who showed up on my doorstep covered in mud."

"She is a key," Dane said, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Keys do not starve. Keys do not suffer. Keys open things. What opens when she turns, Inquisitor Dane does not care to imagine—but the Crown cares, and the Crown has sent me." She crouched, slow and deliberate, and set one hand flat on the wet stone of the bank. "Here is my offer, Coldharbour, and I will make it only once, because my oaths require me to speak true and I find I grow tired of repeating myself. Give me the child. Walk away. Your crimes—every one of them, the craft, the concealment, the years of it—will be struck from the record. Forgotten. There are arts for forgetting, and we are very good at them."

*Forgotten.* The word went through Casper like a splinter finding its depth. She thought of nine years of quiet mornings, of bread rising, of Wren asleep by the fire with mud still drying in her hair. All of it could be unwritten. That was the horror of the offer—not the threat but the mercy, the clean erasure, the life she'd built dismissed as a clerical error.

"And if I refuse?"

Dane rose. Behind her, five hands moved to five belts, unhurried, the way wolves are unhurried.

"Then I bind you where you stand," she said, "and I take the child, and the record keeps everything. Including what your master wrote about you in his grimoire, which we recovered from the men who ransacked his cottage." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "He wrote a great deal about you, Casper Coldharbour. Would you like to know what you are? Or would you prefer to keep pretending you're a hedge-witch who mends kettles?"

The river roared softly. Wren's hand tightened around hers—small fingers, surprising strength—and the girl stepped forward, putting herself half in front of Casper, facing six armed riders of the Crown with the calm of someone reciting something memorized long ago. And when she spoke, the Covenant-tongue fell out of her like water out of a broken jar, and on Casper's forearm the silver script bloomed hot:

*The heir crosses. The heir chooses. Let any who bar the way answer to the wording.*

Dane's composure cracked—one heartbeat, two—before the bell-flat calm reasserted itself. But Casper had seen it. Even the inquisitor's oaths had not prepared her to hear a dead language argue back.

"Last chance," Dane said quietly.

Casper looked at the water, at the far bank, at the worn waymarker stones with their shadow-runes. She thought about running back into the Stillvale, back into the silence, back into the prison she had mistaken for a sanctuary. She thought about Aldous's shaking hands and his whispered *forgive me*. She thought about the fact that she had spent nine years believing herself empty, and that emptiness, it turned out, had been carved into her by someone who loved her, which meant it could be carved out again—or filled.

"Come on, then," Casper said, and turned, and pulled Wren toward the far bank.

The first arrow—no, not an arrow; a binding, a whipcord of pale light—cracked across the water and wrapped her ankle, and it burned like frostbite, and she went down into the shallows with Wren screaming soundlessly beside her. Grey cloaks waded in. Dane's voice rang out words in a cadence Casper knew in her bones, the grammar of compulsion, and the river itself began to slow around them, thickening, obeying—

And Casper reached for the only thing left.

She reached past the fear, past the nine years, down to the place Aldous had hollowed out and hidden himself inside, and she *knocked*.

The world broke open.

Magic came back the way blood comes back to a limb gone numb—first a terrible prickling, then heat, then a flood so fierce it was indistinguishable from pain. Every spell she had swallowed unsaid for nine years rose in her at once. The binding on her ankle unraveled like rotten thread. The river remembered how to run. And Casper Coldharbour stood up in the middle of the ford with light running off her skin like rain off a lit lantern, and she *pushed*—not a spell she knew, nothing from any grimoire, just raw refusal given shape—and the six truth-binders staggered back as though the river had stood up with her.

It was terror. It was exhilaration. It was both at once, wound together so tightly she could not tell which end was which, and she was laughing, or sobbing, or both, dragging Wren up the far bank while behind her Serah Dane shouted orders in a voice that had finally, finally lost its calm.

Casper's foot came down on the far side of the waymarker stones, onto soil that hummed faintly, eagerly, under her boot like a dog recognizing its master.

The power did not stop. It kept flooding in, warm and vast and hungry, filling channels dug nine years ago and left dry, and she understood—with the last clear part of her mind—that power this long dammed does not return for free. Somewhere in the flood, something of hers was already being carried away downstream.

She looked down at Wren, whose eyes were wide and wet and fixed on her with something like grief.

"What?" Casper breathed. "What is it? What's happening to me?"

The child opened her mouth, and answered in words Casper could understand—in plain, living, mortal speech, her first sentence in any tongue Casper knew:

"You paid for it. Now come quickly, before you forget why we ran."

* * *

They ran until the light went amber and then blue, until the road became a track and the track became a deer path, and Casper cast ward after ward behind them with hands that shook—not from fear anymore, but from the strange, spreading hollowness that followed each working like a shadow follows a flame.

By full dark she could no longer remember her mother's face.

She sat down hard against a fallen oak, Wren curled watchful beside her, and tried to hold on to the Stillvale—to the smell of bread, to Aldous's rough kindness, to the reasons she had run—all of it slipping through her fingers like river water, smooth and warm and gone.

In the darkness ahead, someone began, softly, to whistle a tune she almost knew.
