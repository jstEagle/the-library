## 6. The Cost of the First Spell

The road out of the Stillvale did not exist on any map, which was precisely how Casper had come to trust it. It was a drover's path mostly, worn into the hills by sheep and the men who followed them, threading between hawthorn hedges gone feral with the long summer. The child walked beside her without complaint, small boots squelching in the mud of the recent rain, and said nothing—but the silence had changed since the ford. Before, it had been an empty silence, the silence of a locked room. Now it was a held breath.

Casper felt the magic the way she imagined a man might feel a splint removed from a broken arm: everything ached, and everything could move. Nine years of stillness were running off her skin like rain. Every hedgerow whispered its green arithmetic to her; every stone remembered being molten. The world past the border stones was loud with power, and she was newly deaf no longer, and she did not know whether to weep or sing.

She did neither. She watched the road behind them instead, and kept the child walking, and thought about Serah Dane's face—patient, reasonable, the face of a woman who had never once needed to raise her voice because the truth always did her shouting for her.

They made camp at dusk in the hollow of a fallen oak, its root-plate reared up like a wall against the wind. Casper gathered deadwood and, before her mind could argue with her hands, spoke the word.

It was not a word so much as a permission. *Burn.* She felt the syllable leave her the way blood leaves a wound—warm, immediate, hers—and the wood caught alight without flint or tinder, flames rising clean and gold and eager, curling toward her palm like cats toward a known hand.

The child stared at the fire. Then at Casper. And then, in a voice rusty from disuse but steady as a plumb line, the child spoke.

"The Covenant is failing. Every day it frays, a realm dies."

Not in the common tongue. In Covenant-tongue, the language that had marked Casper's skin word by word back in the Stillvale—but this time there was no lexicon between them, no slow translation. This time Casper simply *understood*, the meaning arriving whole in her skull the way meaning arrives for one's own name. Three centuries dead, and the child spoke it as if waking each morning to it. As if it were the only language worth learning, because it was the only one that told the truth.

"That's a full sentence," Casper said stupidly. "That's your first full—" She stopped. The rest of it caught up with her. "A realm dies."

The child nodded once, gravely, the way children nod when they have been patient with a slow adult for a very long time.

"How many?" Casper asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

The child held up both hands, fingers spread, then folded three of them down.

Seven realms left. Casper looked at the fire and tried to make her mind hold the shape of that arithmetic: seven realms, ticking down like a clock with no one winding it. And somewhere behind her in the dark, truth-binders who wanted the child like a warrant wants a name, and ahead of her—she did not know what was ahead of her. Only that the child had crossed a dying country to find her specifically. Only that the marks on her skin spelled a name Aldous had carved away.

"All right," she said, to the fire, to the child, to herself. "All right."

* * *

The trouble began an hour later, when she reached for the memory of her mother's face and found a hole where it should have been.

Not a blurred memory. Not a faded one. A hole, clean-edged, the way a pane of glass leaves a window frame intact around its absence. She knew she had a mother. She knew the smell of her—the rosemary-and-lye smell of washing day, that came back to her now with horrible vividness. She knew her hands, strong and cracked at the knuckles. But the face above those hands was smooth blankness, a coin worn featureless, and when Casper groped for it in the dark of the hollow oak she found herself breathing too fast, too shallow.

"Mother," she whispered, testing the word like a loose tooth. *I have a mother. Her name is—*

Nothing. Even the name was gone.

The fire popped. The child watched her across the flames with those old, old eyes, and Casper understood, all at once and completely, what the spell at the ford had cost her. When she had torn herself across the border stones with magic flooding back into her like blood into a numb limb, she had cast—she hadn't counted it as casting, but it had been casting—a working of concealment, of misdirection, enough to put mist between them and Serah Dane's riders. And magic, real magic, hedge-witch magic, Covenant magic, did not run on goodwill.

It ran on payment.

Aldous had told her once, early, laughing at some village cunning-man's pretensions: *Everything works, girl. The question is what it costs. Charms cost sweat. Cantrips cost sleep. True spells cost you something you can't make more of.* She had thought he meant blood. She had been such a fool.

"Take my memories," she said aloud, testing the shape of the horror. "It takes memories."

The child tilted their head. Then they reached into the collar of their shirt and drew out a small thing on a cord: a flat river stone, grey, unremarkable, except that when it swung into the firelight, Casper saw faint script crawling across its surface—Covenant-tongue, tiny as lice, line upon line upon line.

"What is that?"

The child pressed it into her hand. The stone was warm, warmer than body-warm, warm like the ford-magic had been warm, and as her fingers closed around it the script shifted, rearranged, and resolved—for her alone, she understood—into the common tongue:

*What is given is given. Choose carefully what you give.*

"It knows," Casper said. "You know. You've seen this before." She looked up. "Who are you? What are you?"

The child took a stick and drew in the dirt between them: a circle, and inside the circle, a smaller figure with arms raised. Then lines radiating outward from the figure like rays of a crude sun. Then the child tapped the figure, and tapped Casper's chest, and looked at her with an expression of terrible gentle patience.

"No," Casper said. "No, I'm not—I'm nobody. I'm a charm-mender from a valley where nothing works." But even as she said it, the marks on her forearm stirred beneath her sleeve, the translated phrases of the summons itching like healing skin, and she remembered the flash of the flashback that had ambushed her in Aldous's ransacked cottage: his hands, steady and terrible, carving silence into the world while she stood at the center of it screaming without sound. The Stillvale had not grown quiet on its own. She had quieted it. He had used her to quiet it.

Her refuge. Her prison. Her work.

And now every spell she cast to protect the child took another piece of the life she'd built inside that prison, and she could not even remember why the pieces had mattered.

* * *

They walked for three days, and Casper learned the price of survival by heart.

On the second evening, riders on the far ridge—truth-binders or worse, she couldn't tell—forced her to blur the road behind them, a working of misdirection that cost her the taste of bread fresh from the oven. Just the taste. She could still smell bread, could still remember that she had loved it, but the pleasure itself was gone, scooped out, a socket where a sweetness used to live. She sat by the fire moving her tongue around her dry mouth, mourning something she could no longer properly miss.

On the third morning, wading a flooded stream with the child on her back, she cast strength into her own legs and lost Aldous's kindness. That was how she would have described it afterward, if she had been able to describe it at all: not the memory of any single act, but the whole warm category of it, the felt sense that the old man had ever once been good to her. She remembered saving him. She remembered the cottage. She remembered the carving, the anchor, the betrayal. But the kindness—the afternoon he'd taught her to whistle through a blade of grass, the winter he'd sat up all night with her fever—healed over like water closing after a thrown stone, and when she tried to be angry at him she found her anger had nothing soft to push against anymore, and it frightened her how much easier hating him became.

"Grief as tuition," she muttered, fording the shallows, the child's arms around her neck. "That's what this is. Someone's charging me tuition."

The child, hearing the tone if not the sense, patted her shoulder twice, the way you'd soothe a horse.

By the fourth night she had catalogued the losses like a merchant taking inventory: her mother's face. Bread's taste. Aldous's kindness. The reason she had run—not the fact of running, which remained, but the *reason*, the burning certainty that had carried her out of her master's orbit nine years ago, gone now, leaving only the dumb habit of hiding. She sat poking the fire and realized she no longer knew why she had ever feared her own power. She only knew that she had, because the fear had left grooves in her, the way cartwheels leave ruts in a road long after the cart has passed.

"I'm erasing myself," she told the child. "One spell at a time. I'm spending myself."

The child considered this for a long moment. Then they crawled close, took the river stone from Casper's pack, and set it deliberately in her lap. The script had changed again. New lines had grown along its edge, small and urgent:

*There is another way. There is a vault that does not spend. Ask the ash queen's servant. Ask him what he carries.*

Casper read it three times. The fire crackled. Somewhere behind them, days away now, Serah Dane was surely picking up their trail again, and ahead lay the near realm, and Vessandra, and Tam with his hollow eyes and his easy smile.

"She sent him to me," Casper said slowly. "He offered sanctuary. He offered—" belonging, she almost said, and stopped, because the wanting of it still rose in her like water finding a level, shameless and immediate. "Why would her envoy carry the answer to the cost of spells? Why would she give me that?"

The child looked at her with an expression that was almost pity, and answered in Covenant-tongue, four short words that needed no translation, because Casper already understood them in her bones:

*Because she wants you.*

And far down the darkening road, where the hedges gave way to open moor, a lantern was approaching—one lantern, unhurried, swinging in a familiar jaunty rhythm, and Casper knew before she could see the face whose smile it was.
