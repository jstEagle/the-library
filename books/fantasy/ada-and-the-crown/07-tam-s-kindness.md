## 7. Tam's Kindness

Tam had been hollow for a long time before he learned to fill the hollowness with charm.

It was a serviceable trade. Vessandra's court ran on it—the way a mill runs on water, the way a body runs on blood. You smiled, you bowed, you made the frightened feel chosen and the proud feel seen, and in exchange you were permitted to continue existing in the warm amber light of the Queen of Ashes' favor. Tam had been very good at it for a very long time. Long enough that he no longer remembered what he had been before she found him, only that there had been a boy once, and that the boy had wanted something so badly he had traded everything else to get it.

He could not remember what the something was.

That was the price of her gifts, and he had paid it gladly, or at least he had told himself he'd paid it gladly, which amounted to nearly the same thing after three hundred years of practice.

So when Vessandra sent him through the crack between realms with two words—*find them, bring them*—he went the way rain goes downhill: without deliberation, without resistance, because going was simply what he was shaped to do.

He expected a hedge-witch. He expected fear, bargaining, maybe fire if the woman had any spine left under all that Stillvale softness. He expected to be done in a week.

He did not expect the child to fall asleep against his shoulder on the second night, mud-streaked and feverish, gripping his coat lapel with one small fist as though he were furniture. Furniture did not mind. Furniture did not lie awake afterward listening to the small wet sound of the child's breathing and feel something move in the hollow places, like water finding an old channel.

He did not expect Casper Coldharbour to share her last apple with him, cut into three uneven pieces, when she had every reason to believe he was a knife sent by someone worse than the Crown.

* * *

They were three days out from the ford, camped in the ruin of a shepherd's cot with its roof half caved in, when Tam understood that his orders had become a problem.

The child sat by the fire turning a smooth grey stone over and over in her hands, and every few minutes she would look up at Casper with an expression Tam recognized from mirrors: the look of someone checking whether the person they loved was still there. Casper, meanwhile, sat with her sleeves pushed back, staring at her own forearms in the firelight, where the marks rose and faded like writing on water. Her true name, surfacing and sinking. She had stopped flinching at them sometime around noon, which worried Tam more than screaming would have.

"You're staring," Casper said, without looking up.

"I'm observing. It's my profession."

"Your profession is lying."

"My profession is *persuasion*. Lying is merely the most efficient dialect." He turned the spit he'd rigged over the coals—a rabbit, skinned badly, because Casper had refused to let him use a glamour to make it cook itself. "You've gone quiet about the memories."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her reach, deliberately, for something—a name, a face—and watched her hand close on nothing. It happened four, five times a day now. She would start a sentence about the Stillvale and stall mid-word, like a clock with a bent tooth.

"The spell I cast at the ford," she said finally. "To get us across. It took—" She stopped. Started again. "There was a woman who raised me. Before Aldous. I know this because I wrote it down. I don't remember her face anymore. I remember that I used to remember it."

Tam looked at the fire. The rabbit dripped. Somewhere in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"I can show you something," he said, before he could stop himself. "If you want to see what you're walking toward. What we're all walking toward. What the Covenant actually *was*."

Casper's eyes came up, wary and green and tired. "Why would you do that?"

Because the hollow places were filling up with things he didn't have orders for, he thought. Because the child had gripped his lapel and he had not pulled away. Because somewhere between the ford and here he had begun to dread delivering them, and dread was not an emotion he had budgeted for.

"Consider it a professional courtesy," he said aloud. "And consider that you'll make better decisions about Vessandra if you know what she's actually offering to save you from."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "But it's the only kind I give for free."

* * *

He gave her the memory the way his queen had taught him to give all gifts: held out flat on open palms, so the receiver believed they could refuse it.

"Take my hands," he said. "Both of you. It doesn't hurt. That's not flattery—it's simply easier to carry someone gently than roughly, and I've never seen the point in roughness."

The child took his hand first, immediately, trustingly, and something in Tam's chest went sideways. Casper hesitated longer. Then she took his other hand, and he let the memory rise.

He showed her the old days—not the pretty version, not the one in the ballads where the Covenant was a marriage between worlds, a handshake across the veil. He showed her the truth.

He showed her the great stone, black and veined with gold, and the kings of the mortal realm kneeling before it—not in reverence but in *registration*, the way one registers livestock. He showed her the collar-rites, where a mortal child with a drop of old blood was brought forward and bound, named as a hinge, a door, an anchor—*a thing*, in the legal language of both realms, and the language had been very careful about the word *thing*. He showed her the tithe-trains crossing the border stones in autumn, wagons of grain and wool and, once a generation, a person, smiling and waving because they had been glamoured to smile, because consent had been deemed unnecessary when obedience was cheaper.

He showed her the leash. Not as metaphor. As object—an actual working of the old magic, a cord of woven oath-light running from the Covenant-stone down into the mortal world, and at its end, always, a human throat.

"They called it peace," Tam said quietly, inside the memory, his voice coming from everywhere and nowhere. "And it was peace. No wars between the realms for nine hundred years. No blight, no wandering horrors, no children stolen from cradles—except the ones the Covenant itself required, and those were called sacrifices, and everyone agreed not to call them murders. Your master grew up under that wording. So did I. So did she." The memory rippled; far away, a figure of ash and gold stood at the top of a hill of years. "Vessandra was there when the first clause was written. She has spent three centuries trying to tear it out. And she will tell you—truthfully, because she cannot lie about this—that she wants to burn the whole rotten thing down."

The memory began to fold closed around them, gentle as a book shutting.

"But ask yourself," Tam said, "what she wants to build on the ashes. Ask who holds the new leash. And ask why she needs *you*—why she needs the last heir of the anchor-line—to hold the door open while she does it."

* * *

Casper pulled her hands back and sat very still for a long time.

The child, unbothered, had already returned to her stone. The fire had burned down to a red heart. Outside, the night pressed against the broken roof like a listener.

"Aldous," Casper said at last. Her voice was strange, scraped thin. "In the memory. The binding-rite. There was a man holding the child's shoulders. Keeping her still while they carved the name into her." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "That was him. Wasn't it. He didn't just find me. He *made* me. He's been doing it for centuries."

Tam said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

"And your queen knew. All along. She's known since before I was born what I am, and she waited until I was desperate and hunted and forgetting my own mother's face, and then she sent *you*—" Casper laughed, a short ugly sound. "You're good, Tam. I almost believe you care."

The worst part, the part that sat in him like a swallowed stone, was that he did not have to perform the next words at all.

"I do care," he said. "That's the problem. My orders are to bring you to her. I have carried out orders for three hundred years without once asking whether they were right, because asking is how you start remembering what you traded away, and I—" His voice caught on something unexpected. He smoothed it over. Almost. "I don't want to remember. Remembering hurts. But I find that I would rather hurt than hand you over feeling nothing."

Casper stared at him. The child looked up too, and said something soft in the dead tongue—two syllables, questioning—and though Tam could not translate it, he understood it anyway. They always understand, in the end. It was the oldest language there was.

"She's asking if you're going to betray us," Casper translated anyway, her voice steadying. "She says people who say they care usually mean they want something."

"Tell her she's right," Tam said. "I do want something. I want to be the sort of person who wouldn't have shown you that memory. And I'm not yet. But I'm standing in the doorway between who I was ordered to be and who I might be, and I am telling you—" he spread his empty hands, "—that tomorrow, when Vessandra's riders catch our trail, I am supposed to deliver you to her throne of whispered names. And tonight, sitting in this ruined shepherd's hut, I genuinely do not know which way I'll go."

The fire cracked. The child reached over and placed her smooth grey stone in Tam's palm, closing his fingers around it with grave ceremony, as though paying him for the truth.

He held it like it was worth more than his soul, which, given recent accounting, it probably was.

And then, far off in the dark to the east—too far to see, near enough to feel—the air shivered, and every mark on Casper's arms lit at once like a struck match, and Tam felt, with cold certainty, the pull of his queen's attention swing toward them like a compass needle finding north.

"She knows where we are," he said, rising. "We have until dawn. Maybe less."
