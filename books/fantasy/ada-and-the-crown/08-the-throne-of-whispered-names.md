## 8. The Throne of Whispered Names

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first cruelty, though Casper did not understand it as one until later. The truth-binders had bound her wrists with cold iron at the ford—Serah Dane's own hands doing the work, gentle as a mother dressing a child for winter—and then, somewhere on the long road into the near realm, the irons had simply been removed. No explanation. No order given. The guards rode on either side of her cart as if her freedom were a formality they had already settled, and the child slept against her hip with the terrible trust of the very young, and Casper understood: there was nowhere to run that Vessandra had not already made room for.

The road climbed through country that remembered being beautiful. Ash lay on the branches like late snow, and beneath it the trees were black but not dead—she could feel them now, since the ford, since her magic had come flooding back like blood into a limb gone numb, she could feel *everything*—straining toward some warmth that never came. The nearer realm was a hearth swept clean of its fire. She caught herself reaching for it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

The child woke once, at dusk, and spoke.

*"Máthair na n-ainmneacha,"* the small voice said—the mother of names—and pointed up the road to where a light burned that was not firelight.

Casper had learned enough Covenant-tongue in nine years of stolen evenings with Aldous's lexicon to know the word. She wished she didn't. In the old grammar, *mother of names* meant a queen who owned what you were called, and what you were called was what you were.

The throne room had no roof.

That was the second thing she noticed, after the throne itself—a thing of fused glass and bone-white stone that looked less built than *grown*, rising from the floor in a spiral like smoke given patience. Above it, where a ceiling should have been, the sky hung close and bruised-purple, and stars moved in it that Casper was almost certain were not stars. They drifted like fish under ice. Watching.

Vessandra sat the throne as if it were a chair by a fire, one ankle crossed over her knee, chin propped on her knuckles. She was not beautiful in the way queens are painted. She was beautiful the way a fever is warm. Her hair was the grey of old ash, her eyes the amber of resin with something trapped inside, and when she smiled at Casper it was with such unguarded delight that Casper's whole body leaned toward it before her mind could object.

"There she is," Vessandra said, and her voice filled the roofless hall the way water fills a cupped hand. "The girl who broke my valley."

"I didn't—" Casper stopped. Nine years of careful silence fought the words. "I didn't know I'd done anything."

"No." The queen rose, descended the dais without hurry. "You never do, you anchors. That's rather the point of you."

The word landed in Casper's chest like a dropped stone. *Anchors.* Plural. As if there had been others. As if there had been a shelf of them, used up and set aside.

She looked down at the child instead, who stood pressed against her leg, staring up at Vessandra with an expression no child should wear: recognition, and grief, and something older than both.

"You sent Tam," Casper said. "You sent him with kindness. Real kindness—I know the difference now. And then you let the Crown take us anyway."

"Tam is fond of you. I permitted it." Vessandra stopped three paces away, close enough that Casper could smell her—woodsmoke and something floral gone to rot. "Fondness is a fine seasoning. But Casper Coldharbour, I did not bring you here to bargain for the child. I brought you here to give you something no one has ever given you."

"And what's that?"

"The truth." The queen's smile faded into something quieter, and somehow more dangerous. "All of it. Freely. Because unlike your master, unlike your Crown, I have never needed to lie to get what I want. Lies are for people with weak hands."

* * *

They walked while she told it. Through galleries where the ash-fall drifted slow as snow through open windows, past tapestries woven with scenes that moved when Casper wasn't looking directly at them. The child rode on Vessandra's arm now—had gone to her willingly, which frightened Casper more than anything else that day—and watched the queen with those old, old eyes.

"Three hundred years ago," Vessandra said, "the Covenant was forged. You've read the mortal version, I expect. Peace between realms, sworn at the great stone, all very solemn. What Aldous's lexicon won't tell you is *how* it was anchored. A covenant is a promise, and a promise needs somewhere to live. So the first binders grew one."

"Grew."

"A person. Shaped from the Covenant's own root-logic and laid into a mortal life so thoroughly that even she believed herself ordinary." Vessandra glanced sideways. "It takes nine years for an anchor to ripen, you see. Nine years sealed away from magic, so the binding sets like mortar. Your Stillvale wasn't a refuge, dear. It was a curing shed."

The floor tilted. Or Casper did. She put a hand against the wall and the wall was warm, faintly pulsing, and she snatched her fingers back.

"Aldous found me," she said. "When I was— he took me as an apprentice. He taught me. He was kind."

"He was kind," Vessandra agreed, gently, horribly. "He was also the last binder alive who knew the shaping-work, and he was dying, and the Covenant was failing, and he needed his anchor cured before he went. So yes. He loved you. Binders always do. It makes the meat more willing."

Casper thought of the border stones. Of the way spells died at the valley's edge—not naturally, not like frost killing a flower, but *refused*, pushed back, the way a body rejects a splinter. She thought of nine years of mending charms she could not cast, of the hollow ache she had always carried and named homesickness, though she had been home.

She thought of Aldous carving, in the flashback-memory that had surfaced at his ransacked cottage: his hands in the earth of the valley, her name in his mouth, and herself—younger, dreaming, trusting—standing at the center of the working like a nail driven into the world.

"You're lying," she whispered.

"Am I?" Vessandra turned, and the trapped things in her amber eyes swam closer to the surface. "Then look. I'll show you the way Tam showed you the old Covenant. I keep memories, Casper. Centuries of them. It's the one wealth I never spent."

She touched two fingers to Casper's brow.

* * *

There was a room underground, in a cottage that smelled of ink and dried yarrow, and in the room there was a cradle.

Aldous stood over it. He was younger than Casper had ever known him—dark-haired, upright, his face still carrying the architecture of the man she'd fled rather than the ruin she'd left behind. In the cradle lay an infant, and the infant was not breathing right; its breath came in the rhythm of tides, of seasons, of something far too large for its small chest.

"It won't hold," said a voice Casper did not know, thin and urgent. "The vessel keeps failing. We've tried six."

"Because we keep making vessels," Aldous said. His voice cracked with exhaustion. "We need to make a *person*. Root to crown. A life, not a jar."

"That's forbidden even among us—"

"Everything is forbidden. The realms are dying." He lifted the infant—it weighed nothing, or everything—and Casper felt the memory's edges fray with the horror of watching, because she knew, she *knew* whose small body that was, she knew the birthmark on the ankle, the same one she washed in the Stillvale river every morning—

The memory folded forward. Years compressed. A cottage in the Stillvale, and Aldous kneeling in the garden dirt with a girl of perhaps seven, teaching her to press seeds into soil, and beneath the lesson, beneath the words, a working turning like a wheel beneath a millstream: *grow, root, take hold, become, believe.* Every kindness a layer of the shaping. Every story a stitch. The girl laughed at something and the working tightened, and Aldous flinched, and kissed the top of her head, and kept carving.

The last image was the night Casper ran. Aldous standing at the border stones, watching her go, and saying—to no one, to the valley, to the working—"*Nine years. Forgive me. Nine years and then she'll be ready, and the realms will live.*"

Vessandra withdrew her fingers.

Casper was on her knees. She did not remember falling. The ash-snow drifted through the high windows and settled on her shoulders like a shroud being fitted, and somewhere nearby the child was humming a Covenant-tongue lullaby, soft and tuneless, and Casper could not stop seeing the cradle. Could not stop doing the arithmetic. *Root to crown. A life, not a jar.*

Every memory she owned of love was load-bearing masonry in her prison.

"Now," Vessandra said, crouching to her level, and her voice was warm as a hand on a cold neck, "here is what I offer. Not the child. Not yet. First: yourself. I can unmake the shaping. All of it. Reach into the root of you and pull out every thread Aldous wove, until what remains is only Casper—the woman, not the anchor. Free. Ordinary. Able to love without wondering if the love was installed."

The words went through Casper like a blade finding the gap in armor, because it was the exact wound. Nine years she had wondered. Was her fear of power hers, or placed? Was her stubbornness hers? When she had run from Aldous, had she chosen, or had the choosing been carved into her like everything else?

"You can't do that," she said hoarsely. "No one can."

"I am the mother of names. I unmake what has been named." Vessandra tilted her head. "And in exchange, I ask one small thing. The child comes to me. Not harmed—cherished. She is the first soul born outside the failing Covenant, and I need her to seed the new one. Yours would be a world of consent, Casper. Mine will be a world of *order*. Someone must hold the leash, or the realms tear each other apart again. I watched the last age burn. I would rather rule than watch."

"She's a child."

"She is a foundation. As you were." The queen smiled, and it was almost tender. "The difference is that I'm asking."

*And Aldous didn't,* said the small cold voice in Casper. *He never asked. He just carved.*

The temptation was not a clawed thing. That was the horror of it. It was warm. It was a fire after nine years of the Stillvale's chill, it was the promise of a self with no hidden rooms, no buried cradle, no question mark at the center of every feeling she'd ever had. She could be free. She could be *simple*. She could hand over the child and walk into some sunlit mortal country and grow herbs and never again wonder which parts of her were real.

Her mouth opened. The word *yes* was already shaped behind her teeth, weightless, easy, the easiest word she had ever nearly said.

The child stopped humming.

Small fingers found Casper's chin and turned her face, and the child looked at her with eyes that held the purple star-drifted sky, and said—in plain, accented, imperfect Common, her first sentence in any tongue Casper could speak:

"If she unmakes his work, there will be nothing left to save."

The hall went silent. Even the ash seemed to hang suspended.

Vessandra's smile did not falter, but the trapped things in her eyes went very still, and above the throneless roof, the false stars began, one by one, to turn and look downward.

"Ah," the queen said softly. "So you've guessed what else I keep in this palace. Come along, then, Casper Coldharbour. Before you answer me—there is someone I think you should meet. He's been waiting nine years to apologize."

And from the deep of the palace, behind a door of fused glass, came a sound Casper knew better than her own heartbeat: a cough, dry and old, and a voice worn thin as parchment, saying her name.

*Aldous.*
