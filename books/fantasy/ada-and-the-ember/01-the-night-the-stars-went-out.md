## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The temple of Hollowmere had forty-one windows, and Silas Eastwick had counted every one of them so many times she could have drawn them blindfolded. Forty-one panes of green valley glass, each one a small square promise that the world outside was exactly as it had always been: orchards, terraced fields, the slow brown river, and above it all the mountain, wearing its snow like a crown it never took off.

She sat in the scriptorium with her sleeves pushed to the elbows, copying the tithe ledgers for the ninth year running. The quill scratched. The tallow candle guttered. Outside, the first true cold of autumn pressed its face against the glass.

*Debtor: Eastwick, S. Balance: carried forward.*

Her own name, in her own hand, for the ninth year. She wrote it the way she did everything — neatly, precisely, without feeling — because feeling was a luxury the ledger had no column for.

"Your margins are crooked," said Brother Aldous from the doorway, though they weren't. He liked to say it. It was the closest thing either of them had to conversation.

"Yes, Brother."

"You'll want to finish before the frost comes up the valley. Cold makes the ink go strange." He lingered, which he rarely did, and his eyes went to the window, to the sky, with an expression she couldn't read. "Strange weather coming. The elders say the wards are humming."

"The wards are always humming."

"Not like this." He left before she could ask what *like this* meant. That was the way of Hollowmere: everyone knew things, and no one said them, and the silence between what was known and what was spoken was where Silas had lived her whole life.

She looked at her hands. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained, chapped at the knuckles, unremarkable in every way except one — the one no one in the valley ever spoke about directly, the way you don't speak about a missing tooth in someone's smile.

Magic could not follow her.

It wasn't that she had no gift. Every soul in Hollowmere had a gift; the lamplighter's daughter coaxed flames to dance, old Widow Marsh could still the rain with a word, even Brother Aldous made the temple bells ring sweet on windless days. But when magic touched Silas, it slid away, like water off oiled cloth, like a thought you couldn't hold onto when waking. The hedge-witch had tried once, years ago, pressing a charm into her palm. Silas still remembered the witch's face — the confusion, then the fear, then the careful blankness as she'd snatched the charm back and said, *perhaps next year, child,* and never tried again.

They were kind about it, mostly. Kind the way people are kind to the lame and the simple. And Silas had learned to be kind back, to smile her neat, precise smile, and to want things only in secret, in the dark, in the space between copying one ledger line and the next.

She wanted to see the sea. She wanted to see a city with towers. She wanted to stand on the mountain's shoulder and look down at the whole world at once. Small wants. Impossible wants. A debtor did not travel. A debtor copied ledgers until the balance was paid, and the balance was always carried forward, and no one — not once in nine years — had ever told her what the debt was *for*.

*Your mother's debts,* they said, when they said anything at all. *Be grateful the temple took you in.*

She was twenty-three years old and she had never seen anything but the valley, and tonight, for no reason she could name, the walls of it felt less like safety and more like the inside of a jar.

Silas set down her quill and blew out the candle, though it was hours yet before the bell rang for sleep. She wanted darkness. She wanted to sit in the dark and feel the shape of her own wanting, sharp and private, like a stone under her tongue.

That was when the stars began to go out.

* * *

She saw it through the window over her narrow bed, the one she'd climbed through a hundred times as a girl to watch the valley breathe. First one star winked out — high in the north, above the mountain's crown. Then another. Then three together, like candles pinched by an unseen hand.

Silas pressed her forehead to the cold glass. Stars did not go out. Stars burned whether you watched them or not; that was the whole nature of stars. But these were going out in order, methodically, a line of small deaths marching across the sky toward the mountain, and beneath them the snowfields had begun to glow — faintly, wrongly, a red light seeping up from the rock itself, as if the mountain were a banked coal remembering fire.

Below in the village, dogs began to howl. Not the ragged chorus of a fox in the henhouses, but all together, all at once, one long note of animal terror.

Then the wards fell.

She didn't see it so much as feel it — a sound below sound, a hum she'd heard every day of her life without knowing she heard it, and then silence where the hum had been. Like a tooth pulled. Like a bell rung so long you forget it's ringing until it stops, and the stopping is deafening. The air over the valley shimmered once, the faint gold veil that hung between Hollowmere and the rest of the world, and tore — and beyond the tear there was nothing. No hills. No distant roads. Just blackness, absolute and hungry, pressing against the edge of everything she knew.

Every star was gone now. All of them. The sky was a lid.

Silas's hands were shaking. She noticed distantly, as if they belonged to someone else. Nine years of neat, precise living, and it took the sky breaking open to teach her that fear had a taste — copper and smoke and something older, something like the smell of a doused fire.

*Get the brothers,* she thought. *Ring the bell. Do something useful.* Her feet wouldn't move. She stood at the window with her breath fogging the glass, watching the blackness lean closer, and some traitorous part of her — the part that dreamed of seas and cities — whispered: *so this is what was on the other side of the wards. This is what they kept out. Or kept in.*

The window exploded inward.

Glass rained across her bed in a glittering hail, and through the frame — through the hole where forty-one windows became forty — poured something burning. Not fire exactly. Fire was destructive, fire was mindless. This was *alive*, a small whirlwind of ember-light and ash-white fur, four-legged and quick, landing on her floorboards in a spray of sparks that hissed and died without scorching the wood. It had the shape of a fox and eyes like two coals blown to full flame, and when it turned those eyes on her, Silas forgot how to breathe.

"You," the fox said. Its voice was a woman's voice, roughened, urgent, crackling at the edges like paper catching. "You're the one. I've crossed half the dark to find you, and you're *here*, in this — " it glanced around the cell, the cot, the ledger lines still drying on the desk — "this *box*."

Silas backed against the wall. "You're talking."

"I'm doing considerably more than talking." The fox paced a tight circle, shedding embers, and Silas saw that its light was guttering — flaring and dimming like a candle in wind, like something burning down. "Listen to me. Listen. Tonight the wards fell, and I felt you through the ruin of them like a shout in a silent room. You cast *nothing*. Do you understand? Everyone in this valley casts shadows — little ones, big ones, the way everyone breathes. Shadow-magic, hearth-magic, the small warm craft of sheltered folk. But you—" the ember eyes fixed on her, blazing, bewildered, almost angry— "you cast *nothing at all*. Magic doesn't slide off a person, girl. Magic doesn't avoid a person. Magic is *taken* from a person. Something has your shadow. Something is holding the piece of you that burns."

The words landed in Silas like stones dropped into a well she'd thought was bottomless. She thought of the hedge-witch's snatched-back charm. Of nine years of sliding water. Of *carried forward*, written in her own hand, again and again and again.

"What are you?" she whispered.

The fox sat, and for a moment its flames steadied, and behind the fire Silas saw — or thought she saw — a woman's face, young and tired and fierce.

"My name is Ada. I keep embers. Or I did, before your mountain's court grew greedy." The light in her dimmed another degree, and she winced as if struck. "And you, Silas Eastwick, are going to tell me who sold you — because someone did. Souls aren't lost. They're *sold*."

Outside, the first snow of the long winter began to fall — fat, slow flakes, falling upward.
