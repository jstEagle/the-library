## 2. What the Ledger Owes

Ada did not walk so much as pour herself along the road, a ribbon of low orange flame that left the frost unmelting in her wake. Silas followed at a stumbling run, her copyist's cloak snapping behind her, and the valley that had been safe and dull for twenty-three years opened around her like a book with its spine cracked.

The stars were gone. Not clouded — *gone*, as if a hand had reached up and pinched them out one by one. The moon hung thin and wrong, a nail-paring of light, and beneath it the road to the border shrine lay white with a frost that had no business falling in autumn.

"Keep up," Ada said, over her shoulder. Her voice was smoke and struck flint. "The wards fell at moonrise. What comes after falls faster."

"What comes after what?" Silas's breath tore out of her in rags. "You burst through my window, you set my inkwell on fire, you called me a — a hollow thing — and now we're running toward the *border*? Nobody goes to the border. The border is where the valley stops being the valley."

Ada stopped so abruptly that Silas nearly ran through her. The fox turned, and her eyes were two coals banked down to embers, and for a moment she looked old beyond any counting.

"You copy ledgers," Ada said. "Debt-ledgers. Tithes. Contracts. You have copied ten thousand names into ten thousand books, and never once wondered why your own name is written nowhere but in your mother's hand."

"I'm in the temple rolls. I'm in the—"

"The rolls record what the valley owes the sky." Ada's muzzle lifted toward the starless dark. "I am asking who owes what for *you*."

Silas had no answer. She had never had an answer, not really — not for why magic slid off her like rain off oiled canvas while every other child in the valley learned to coax green from stone and warmth from words. Not for why her mother had gone pale and silent whenever Silas asked about the years before the temple took her in. Not for why, when she dreamed, she dreamed of a world she had never seen: a court lit like a furnace, a mountain with a door in its root, a voice saying *the account is settled* in tones like cooling iron.

She had told herself it was longing. A copyist's hunger for a life larger than ink and lamplight.

The shrine appeared out of the frost-fog all at once, the way grief appears — suddenly, and already long ruined.

* * *

It had been a beautiful place once. Silas could see the bones of it: seven standing stones in a ring, each carved with ward-script older than the valley's oldest ledger. Now four of the stones were toppled, and the script on the remaining three was scorched black, burned away from the inside, as though the words themselves had caught fire and died.

At the ring's center stood an altar of black slate, and on the altar lay a contract.

Silas knew contracts. She had spent six years copying them, learning their grammar the way priests learn liturgy — the invocation clauses, the consideration clauses, the surrender clauses written in small careful letters at the bottom where tired eyes would not linger. This one was different. It was written on vellum that had been half-consumed by fire that had not destroyed it, and it was signed in a hand she knew better than her own.

Her mother's.

"No," Silas said. It came out very small. She stepped forward anyway, because her hands were already reaching, because some part of her had always known there was a page with her name on it somewhere and she had spent her whole life walking toward it.

The vellum was cold as riverbed stone. She read it by the light of Ada's burning, and the words went into her like splinters:

*In settlement of all debts accrued by Maren Eastwick, late of Hollowmere, to the Court Beneath the Mountain — being debts of borrowing, of sanctuary, of survival through the famine winter — the undersigned does convey and surrender entire: one soul, undivided, belonging to her issue, SILAS EASTWICK, aged six years.*

*Consideration received: remission of said debts. The child shall live free in the valley, unclaimed, unmolested, whole in body —*

Silas stopped reading. The word *whole* sat on the page like a joke told by someone cruel.

"— whole in body," she whispered. "*Whole in body.* Not whole in soul. You sold the soul and kept the body, and everyone called me lucky."

"Hollow enough that magic cannot find purchase," Ada said quietly. "A soul is the hook everything hangs on. Power, wonder, shadow-magic, starlight — it all needs something in you to catch on. You are smooth as glass inside, Silas Eastwick. That is why the valley could keep you safe. That is why the valley could keep you *empty*."

"I'm not empty." Her voice cracked on it. "I feel things. I want things. I've wanted — I've lain awake wanting things my whole life—"

"Wants are yours. They grew back around the wound, like grass over a grave." Ada padded closer, and her heat touched Silas's frozen fingers like a kindness. "But the deep part, the part that answers when the world calls — that part is under the mountain. In a ledger. In a vault. Burning in a lamp in the Ember Court, keeping some magistrate's hall warm."

Silas looked down at the contract again. At her mother's signature, steady and deliberate, the signature of a woman who had known exactly what she was doing. And beneath it, in script that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at it, the seal of the court: a mountain with a door in its root, drawn in a line of ember-red that still glowed faintly after all these years.

*The account is settled.* There it was. The phrase from her dreams. She hadn't been dreaming of a stranger's bargain. She had been dreaming of her own sale, remembered by the only part of her that had been present for it.

The soul remembers what the body is made to forget.

Something in Silas went very quiet and very cold, colder than the frost, colder than the starless sky. It was not despair. Despair would have been easier. This was the other thing, the thing that comes after despair has finished with you and finds nothing left to break.

"They let me copy ledgers," she said. "For six years. I sat in that temple and copied other people's surrenders in my best hand, and the whole time there was a page with *my* name on it under a mountain, and nobody — not the priests, not my mother, not one single person — thought I should know."

"Now you know." Ada's flames had gone low, guttering, and Silas realized with a lurch that the fox was dimmer than she'd been at the window. Dimmer than she'd been an hour ago. "And now you must choose quickly, because knowing changes nothing unless you act, and the season will not wait for your grief."

* * *

They heard the travelers before they saw them — or rather, they heard the absence of them. The north road, which should have been loud with the last caravans fleeing the cold, had gone silent.

Three wagons stood in the middle of the road a half-mile on, laden with goods, harnesses still buckled. The oxen stood motionless, and Silas thought at first they were dead, until she saw the frost climbing their flanks in slow, deliberate spirals, like the mountain writing its signature on them.

The people were worse. A woman sat at the lead wagon's bench with the reins loose in her hands, and frost had sealed her eyelashes, her lips, the tears that had frozen halfway down her cheeks. A boy — no older than eight — lay in the wagon bed among the bolts of cloth, curled small, his hands tucked under his chin as if he were only sleeping and the sleep had simply decided to keep him.

Silas knelt beside him. She pressed her bare fingers to his throat and felt nothing, and then felt the wrongness of the nothing — not death, which has a texture, a finished quality, but a *pause*. A held breath. As if the boy had been filed away somewhere, pending.

"The long winter," Ada said. "It doesn't kill. It *collects*. Everything the starless dark touches goes into storage — bodies, roads, rivers, time itself. By midwinter the whole valley will be a warehouse. And when the last star goes out, the mountain seals, and everything inside stays collected forever."

"How do you know all this?"

The fox was silent a moment. Snow began to fall — real snow now, fat and slow, the first snow of a winter that intended to be the only season left in the world.

"Because I carried the ember that lit those stars," Ada said at last. "Because I remember what was taken, same as you. Because I have been looking for you for seventeen years, Silas Eastwick, and I am burning down to nothing to find you before the mountain closed." Her eyes lifted to the black peak looming over the valley's northern edge, where a faint red glow pulsed at its root, patient as a heartbeat. "There is a door. There is always a door. And there is a ledger in that court with your name in it, and as long as that page burns, you are theirs."

Silas rose. Her legs were shaking. Her hands, remarkably, were not.

Every lesson of her life had taught her to be careful, to be small, to be grateful for safety bought with someone else's coin. She had been a good copyist. She had kept her head down and her lines neat.

But she had also, for six years, read the fine print. She knew how contracts worked. She knew that every surrender clause had a mirror clause somewhere — a reclamation clause, a breach clause, a way back — if you were willing to go where it was written and tear it out with your own hands.

"Then we're going down," she said. "Into the court. To the ledger. I'm taking my name back."

Ada's flames leapt — briefly, gloriously — and just as quickly banked again, and something in the fox's expression that might have been fear.

"It will cost you," Ada said. "The court does not give. It only trades. Whatever they offer you, whatever they show you — remember the boy in the wagon. Remember what they think a soul is worth."

The red glow at the mountain's root pulsed once, twice, like something below turning over in its sleep.

Silas pulled her mother's contract from the altar, folded it into her coat against her heart, and turned her face to the mountain.

"Then let them make me an offer," she said. "I've read enough fine print to know a trap when I sign one."

And together — the hollow girl and the dying flame — they started up the road that led beneath the world.
