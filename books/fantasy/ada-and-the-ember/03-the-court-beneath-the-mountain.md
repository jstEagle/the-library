## 3. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not open. It *swallowed*.

Silas felt the threshold pass through her like cold water down the throat — a moment of pressure behind her ribs, then nothing, and then they were inside, and she understood why no one who entered ever described the way in. There was no way in. There was only before and after.

The Ember Court spread beneath them like a city built from a single held breath.

Caverns vast as cathedral naves rose overhead, their ceilings lost in a haze of gold, and every light in that endless dark was alive. Souls — or what remained of them — hung suspended in glass lanterns along streets of black stone, each one burning its own color: amber, rose, a green like deep water, a white so pure it hurt to look at. They drifted on slow currents of warm air, rising and falling like lantern-fish in an ocean trench, and where their light pooled, the court's inhabitants basked.

They were beautiful. That was the first obscenity of it. The debtors' souls had been rendered into chandeliers, and the court wore them like jewelry.

"Keep your eyes forward," Ada murmured at her shoulder. The fox-spirit walked on four paws now, her ember-coat banked low, a dull orange glow beneath fur gone gray at the edges. "And keep walking. The court likes to be admired. Admiration is how it feeds."

"I thought it fed on souls."

"It feeds on *wanting*." Ada's ears flattened. "Souls are just the fuel wanting leaves behind."

Silas kept her eyes forward. It did not help. Wanting came for her anyway.

She wanted warmth — real warmth, not the grudging heat of temple braziers but the generous warmth of a place that had never known winter. She wanted color; the valley above was all slate and ash-gray, and this place burned with every shade fire had ever invented. And beneath all of it, patient as water wearing stone, she wanted *herself*. A whole self. A self that magic could find, could cling to, could call its own. She had spent twenty-three years being a hole in the world shaped like a woman, and here, in this gilded dark, she could almost see the shape of what had been taken from her — flickering in a thousand lanterns, any one of which might have been hers once, might have been hers still—

"Silas." Ada's muzzle pressed against her hand, hot as a coal. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're drowning. There's a difference."

* * *

Magistrate Veyne received them in a hall of mirrored obsidian, seated upon a throne that was not a throne but a *scale* — two great pans of black iron balanced on a pillar, and upon those pans, objects instead of seats: on one side, a heap of contracts bound in vellum and sinew; on the other, a single lantern holding a soul the color of dawn.

Veyne himself was difficult to look at directly, the way a flame is difficult to look at directly. He had a face — silver-haired, dignified, the face of a grandfather in a portrait — but the face sat wrong on him, like a mask worn slightly askew, and behind his eyes there was only ledger-arithmetic, endless and patient.

"The copyist," he said, and his voice was warm. That was the second obscenity: it was genuinely warm, rich as mulled wine. "I confess I wondered when you'd come. Most of them don't. Most of them live out their little hollow lives above and never think to ask why the world slides off them." He smiled. "You asked. I do admire curiosity in the property."

"I'm not property."

"No?" Veyne lifted a sheet of paper from the pan beside him, and Silas knew it without seeing the signature — knew it the way you know your own handwriting, because it *was* her own handwriting, aged fourteen, pressed hard enough to tear the page. Her mother's debts, itemized. Her mother's remedy, itemized. One daughter's soul, sold whole. "The law is very clear, child. The law is the only thing in either world that is never unclear." He set the contract down with something like tenderness. "But I am not without mercy. In fact, I've been looking forward to offering it."

He descended from the scale-throne. Up close he smelled of ink and struck matches.

"Here is my bargain. Walk out of my court tonight with your soul restored — every stolen year, every stolen spark, returned to you whole. Magic will know your name again. You will feel sunlight the way the whole-feeling do. And in exchange—" he gestured, and a door opened in the mirrored wall, "—you sign over the soul of another debtor. One. A fair trade, soul for soul, debt discharged in full. The law asks nothing more."

Through the doorway, in a small room lit by a single guttering lamp, sat a child.

A girl, perhaps nine years old, thin as a winter branch, copying lines into a ledger with a stub of pencil. Her hair was braided badly, the way children braid their own hair. She did not look up. She had the particular stillness of someone who learned long ago that stillness was safest — and Silas knew that stillness. Had lived inside it. Had been that girl at that desk, pressing too hard on the page, making herself small enough that the world would forget to collect on her.

"She owes eleven marks," Veyne said gently. "Trivial. But the law is the law, and the law requires a soul, and souls are not minted — only traded." He produced a fresh contract and a pen whose nib was a sliver of obsidian. "Sign, and walk free. Refuse, and remain as you are: hollow, magicless, and mine until the long winter seals us both in together. Choose."

* * *

Silas looked at the pen.

That was the shame of it, later — that she looked. That some traitorous chamber of her heart, the one that had counted every cold night and every meal skipped and every year of being a hole in the world, leapt toward the pen like a starving dog toward bread.

One signature. Eleven marks against twenty-three years. A child she'd never met against a self she'd never had. The arithmetic was so *clean*. Veyne had built his whole court on clean arithmetic, and standing there in the mirror-hall with the pen's shadow falling across her wrist, Silas understood that this was the true engine beneath the mountain — not cruelty, cruelty was crude — but *reasonableness*. Every soul in every lantern had been traded reasonably. Every debtor had signed. The court never stole; it only offered, and let want do the rest.

Ada lay at her feet, and Silas saw, in the mirrors, what she had been refusing to see directly: the fox had dimmed. Her coat was ash to the elbows, her ember-eyes grown small and far apart, like coals going out in a grate. Outside the court's reach, away from whatever ancient pact kept her burning, she was spending herself just by existing — an hour at a time, a pawprint of heat at a time. If Silas refused and they fled empty-handed, Ada would gutter out somewhere on the mountain's knees, and the valley would stay dark forever, and Silas would go on being hollow until the winter took her too.

*Sign*, said the warm hall. *Sign, and be whole. She's nobody. You're somebody. That's just arithmetic.*

Silas stepped through the doorway.

The girl looked up. And it was the eyes that did it — not fear, worse than fear. Recognition. The child saw a tall stranger enter the room and her shoulders dropped half an inch in relief, because a grown-up meant a decision made above her head, and decisions made above her head were the weather she had learned to endure. She didn't beg. She simply waited, pencil poised over her ledger, for the adult world to finish deciding what she was worth.

Silas had waited like that once. Pressing too hard on the page. Making herself small.

And no one had come through the door to decide for her — someone had come through the door to *sell* her, and the difference between those two things was the width of a signature, and she was standing on the wrong side of it with a pen in reach.

"No," Silas said.

It came out quiet. She said it again, louder, turning back into the mirror-hall where Veyne waited with his grandfather's smile.

"No. I won't trade. Not her, not anyone. Your law can keep its clean arithmetic — I've seen what it rounds down."

Veyne's smile did not falter, but something behind his eyes recalculated, rapid as an abacus. "Then you leave as you came. Hollow. And the fox—" he glanced down, almost kindly, "—the fox has perhaps three hours of burning left. Sentiment is expensive, copyist. You are about to learn its price."

"Maybe." Silas's hands had closed into fists, and in the ten thousand mirrors of the hall, ten thousand Silases closed their fists with her. "But while I'm learning it — tell me one thing, Magistrate. All these souls in all these lanterns. Where do you keep the *ledger*?"

For the first time, the warmth in Veyne's voice went out.

Behind him, atop the scale-throne, the great iron pans shifted — and Silas saw that beneath the heap of contracts, beneath the vellum and sinew, one book lay at the bottom of everything, older than the rest, its cover stitched shut with wire.

The master ledger. Every debt in the court. Every name.

Including, she suddenly knew with a certainty that felt like memory, her own.

"You should not have asked that," Veyne said.

"Yes," said Silas, and began to walk toward the throne. "I really shouldn't have."
