## 2. Prayers Answered

The rumor reached the guildhall before the sun did.

Lena heard it first as a change in the quality of the noise outside — the morning market on Vellum Street had a rhythm she knew the way she knew her own pulse, and that morning the rhythm was wrong. Too many voices speaking at once. Too many of them saying the same word.

*Gods.*

She set down her pen. The entry before her — six years of a fishmonger's life, traded to a widow in the Salt Quarter for a house and a grudge — sat half-finished, the ink still wet enough to shine. She would come back to it. Some part of her already knew she would not come back to anything ordinary again for a long time.

Through the tall window of the counting room, the city of Vessany-that-was-not-Vessany spread out in its gray morning light: slate roofs, chimney smoke, the canal cutting its slow silver line toward the harbor. A country erased from every map but still standing, still smoking, still arguing about the price of bread. The guild had kept it that way for three hundred years — unwritten, unrecorded, safe inside the garden walls where the old gods slept under the roses.

Safe, Lena had always assumed, the way a grave is safe.

Now the voices in the street were saying *gods*, and saying it the way people say a name they'd been taught never to speak.

* * *

Tomas found her at the second bell, breathless, his runner's coat still buttoned wrong from hurrying.

"You've heard," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I've heard shouting."

"It's true." He dropped into the chair across from her desk, and up close she could see he was frightened — Tomas, who had once delivered a summons to a debt-collector's den on a dare and come back laughing. "The beggar by the Fountain of Small Mercies. Old Ferren, the one with the crooked back who's begged there since I was a boy. His daughter — the sick one, the one with the lung-rot —" He stopped, swallowed. "He prayed. Last night, at the fountain. Just knelt down in the wet and prayed like his grandmother taught him, before the guild told everyone that was finished. And this morning she got up and made breakfast. The physician swears the rot is gone. Not better. *Gone.*"

Lena felt something move through her that had no proper name — awe with a splinter in it.

"That's impossible," she said, and heard how thin the words sounded. "The gods don't answer. That was the whole settlement. That was the entire reason the guild exists. They were buried, and buried things stay buried, or we'd have no trade at all."

"Well." Tomas turned his hands palm-up, helpless. "Someone forgot to tell them."

Around them the counting room had gone quiet in that particular way offices go quiet — everyone listening without admitting it. Old Mistress Havelle two desks over had stopped pretending to copy. Even the ink seemed to wait.

"And that's not all," Tomas said, lowering his voice. "There are others. A woman on Cordwainer Lane whose well came back sweet after thirty years of brackish water. A child in the Weavers' Row who'd been born mute — spoke his mother's name at dawn, clear as a bell. And every single one of them prayed first. To the old names. The ones in the founding ledgers."

"The ones nobody's supposed to know are in the founding ledgers," Lena said quietly.

"Yeah." Tomas grimaced. "Funny thing, that."

It wasn't funny. Lena looked down at her desk, at the ledger open beneath her hands, and felt for the first time in nine years of service that the book was looking back at her. Every page held trades in impossible things — years, names, luck, grief — and every one of those trades rested on a single foundation: that the old powers were asleep, their debts frozen, their accounts closed. The guild didn't deal in miracles. The guild dealt in the *absence* of miracles, sold at retail.

If the absence was ending —

"We should tell Corvane," she said.

"He knows." Tomas stood, smoothing his coat. "Summons went out an hour ago. All senior scribes to the great hall, noon bell. And Lena—" he paused at the door, and for a moment he looked very young, "—they're saying the fountain's been crowded since sunrise. Hundreds of people. Praying."

* * *

The great hall of the Guildhouse had been built to hold ceremonies, and in Lena's nine years she had seen it used for exactly four: two investitures, one funeral, and the annual audit-feast where the master scribes drank too much and recited the Nine Principles of Account until the apprentices wept with boredom. It was a room designed for the performance of order — vaulted ceiling painted with the guild's arms (a closed book, crossed keys), floor of pale stone worn into a path by centuries of processional feet.

At noon it held perhaps sixty scribes, clerks, and runners, and Corvane Ash walked down that worn path like a man entering his own lungs.

Lena had served under Corvane for five years, ever since he'd risen to guildmaster on the strength of reforms nobody could argue with and a smile nobody could read. He was not a large man. He did not need to be. He had the particular gravity of someone who has never once doubted that history will cooperate with him. When he reached the front of the hall, he did not call for silence; he simply waited, and the silence assembled itself around him like staff taking positions.

"By now," he said, "you have all heard the stories. I will not insult this room by pretending they are only stories."

A ripple went through the benches. Beside her, Mistress Havelle's pen had gone still above her notebook.

"This morning I sent runners to verify four claims. Four healings, four restorations, four answered prayers — all witnessed, all documented, all real." He let the word sit in the hall. "Real. I want you to understand what I am about to say next, because your lives may depend on grasping it quickly."

He folded his hands.

"For three centuries, this guild has maintained the Ledger Peace. The old gods sleep because their debts are recorded, catalogued, and *held*. Nothing they are owed has come due, because nothing they are owed has been allowed to exist as a claim. We did not kill them. We did not banish them. We *bookkept* them. And so long as our books remain authoritative — so long as every prayer, every offering, every spiritual obligation in this country passes through our columns — they cannot act except through us."

His gaze moved across the room, unhurried, and Lena felt it pass over her like a cold draft.

"What happened last night is a breach. Someone prayed directly. Someone was answered directly. Which means somewhere in our records there is a gap — an unrecorded claim, an unflagged debt, a column left open — and through that gap, the old powers have remembered they have hands." He unfolded his hands, laid them flat on the lectern. "So here is what happens now, effective immediately."

"One. Every ledger in this hall is to be audited for god-related entries. Any contract touching a divine name, an old rite, a shrine tithe, a votive account — flag it with red thread. I want those pages found before the week is out."

"Two. No scribe copies, quotes, or removes any flagged page without my personal seal. The penalty is forfeiture of contract." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, which somehow made it worse. Forfeiture of contract meant being sold against your own debts. Everyone in the hall knew what that meant. Nobody wanted to look at it directly.

"Three." Here his voice softened, and that softness was the most frightening thing in the room. "We are going to be generous. The city will hear that the Guild of Vessany has interceded with the old powers — that the healing of Ferren's daughter was accomplished through our mediation, our ancient rites, our careful stewardship. The guild takes credit. The guild takes *control*. Because if the people believe the answers come through us, then the prayers will route through us, and if the prayers route through us—"

"—then the debts route through us," Lena said.

She hadn't meant to speak. The words simply left her, the way a pen slips. Sixty faces turned. Corvane's gaze settled on her with the mild, weighing attention of a man appraising a piece of furniture he might yet find useful.

"Precisely, Scribe Nakamura," he said. "You've understood the shape of it. If we own the routing, we own the collection. And whoever owns the collection owns—" he smiled, small and precise, "—everything the gods are owed. Which, given three hundred years of accumulated human hope, is rather a lot."

He turned back to the hall. "Red thread by week's end. Dismissed."

* * *

The counting room that afternoon had the atmosphere of a hive that has smelled smoke. Scribes worked fast and spoke low. Red thread appeared on desks like a rash.

Lena pulled the ledgers she was responsible for — the Salt Quarter accounts, the harbor tithes, the personal contracts — and began the audit with the mechanical competence of nine years' practice. Shrine tithes. Votive candles, accounted. Funeral offerings, accounted. An old contract from 211, in which a miller had traded his grandfather's blessing for relief from drought — *flagged*. Red thread. Another from 178: a midwife who'd promised her firstborn's gratitude to a river god whose name was written only as a smudge, as though even the ink had been afraid of it. *Flagged.*

Page after page. Thread after thread. And with each one, Lena felt the ground shift a little further beneath her feet, because she was reading, for the first time in her career, the *whole* of what the guild actually was. Not a trading house. A prison warden's logbook. Every entry she'd ever made in good faith was a bar in a cage, and the cage had just been found to have a door left unlatched.

Near dusk, she came to the last ledger of her stack — the oldest one assigned to her, its spine cracked, its pages the color of weak tea. And there, near the back, an entry she had seen a hundred times and never truly read:

*Received in trust: one name, female line, Nakamura. Collateral against obligations outstanding. Do not collect. Do not release. Held in perpetuity.*

Her own surname. Her own family's line.

She stared at it. The candle guttered. Somewhere across the room, red thread whispered as another scribe tied off another flag, and Lena sat very still with her hand flat on the page, feeling — distinctly, undeniably — that the entry was warm.

Names were collateral. She knew that; everyone in the guild knew that; half the city had borrowed against their names at one desperate hour or another. But collateral belonged to *someone*. There should have been a creditor listed. A counterparty. A date of origin.

There was nothing. Only the trust, and the instruction, and the warmth of the paper under her palm, like a hand held too long over a candle.

"Lena." Tomas's voice, low, at her shoulder. "You've gone white. What is it?"

She looked at the entry again. *Do not collect. Do not release.* As if someone had fought to write those words. As if someone had wanted her — or the idea of her, decades before she existed — kept safe and kept *unclaimed*, in a building full of predators, forever.

"An entry," she said. "In my family's name. Older than me. Older than my mother." She swallowed. "Tomas, I need to know who wrote this."

He glanced at the page, and she watched him arrive at the same place she had — watched the color leave his face by degrees.

"Lena," he said carefully, "the guildmaster wants god-debts flagged by week's end. You're holding one that flags itself." He hesitated. "And if Corvane finds out your name is sitting in a divine contract—"

"Then I need to move faster than he does." She closed the ledger, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing. Outside, the bells rang the evening hour, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, she could hear the crowd at the Fountain of Small Mercies still praying.

They weren't stopping. Word was spreading. And every prayer spoken outside the guild's books was a debt incurred on paper that didn't exist yet — paper that someone, someday soon, would have to write.

Lena picked up her pen. Her hand, she noticed, was not entirely steady.

Tomorrow she would find who had written her name. Tonight, she had ledgers to balance — and for the first time in her life, she understood that the most dangerous documents in Vessany were the ones in her own keeping.

The ink on the oldest page caught the candlelight and gleamed, wet-looking, though it had dried sixty years before she was born.
