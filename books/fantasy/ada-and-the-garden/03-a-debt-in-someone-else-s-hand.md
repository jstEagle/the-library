## 3. A Debt in Someone Else's Hand

The page would not stay written.

Lena had discovered this the way she discovered most things in the guildhall: by accident, at an hour when no one else was awake to see it, and with ink still wet on her fingers. It was a little past three in the morning, the deep hour of the night shift, when the candles burned low and the building settled around her like an old animal folding its limbs. She had copied the entry four times now. Four times she had set it down exactly as it appeared in the old contract — *one name held in trust against the debt of Vessany* — and four times, by the time she reached for the blotting sand, the words on her fresh page had shifted under her eyes like fish turning in dark water.

She sat back and looked at what the fifth attempt had become.

*One name held in trust,* it read now, in handwriting that was not hers — that was, in fact, unmistakably hers, the same slanted hand she'd used since she was nine years old, but pressed into the paper with a confidence she did not feel at three in the morning. The ink gleamed as though freshly laid, though she had written those words nearly an hour ago.

Lena Nakamura had balanced ledgers for eleven years. She knew every trick ink could play: fading, bleeding, forgery, the way cheap tallow smoke could yellow a page until yesterday's entry looked like last century's. She knew how a scribe's eye could tire and read what it expected rather than what was there. She told herself all of this, methodically, the way she reconciled a column of figures — and then she turned back to the original contract and read it again, slowly, aloud, because saying things aloud made them harder to deny.

"*One name held in trust against the debt of Vessany.*"

The original said something slightly different tonight than it had said last week. Last week it had been a routine line in a routine instrument of collateral — one of hundreds in the guild's deep archive, contracts so old they predated the current binding style, the vellum gone the color of weak tea. She had flagged it during the general audit Corvane had ordered after the prayers started being answered, one more god-adjacent debt to be inventoried before the creditors came calling. She remembered thinking nothing of it. She remembered the date, though. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the splinter in her mind.

The contract was dated sixty-one years ago.

Lena Nakamura was twenty-seven years old.

She pulled the lamp closer. The archive room was cold; the guildhall's heat never reached this far into the stone bowels of the place, where the oldest records lived in iron cabinets and the air tasted of dust and tannin and something faintly sweet, like flowers rotting in a closed room. Her breath fogged faintly in the lamplight. She wrapped her shawl tighter and unrolled the contract fully across the reading table, weighting its corners with the brass plummets, and forced herself to read it the way she would read any debtor's instrument: from the top, without flinching, letting the terms tell their own story.

The parties were named in the old style, before the guild standardized its forms. On one side, *the Undersigned Creditor, being a power of Vessany in its final season, hereinafter the Lender.* On the other, *the Undersigned Debtor, being a free woman of the city, hereinafter the Borrower.*

The borrower's name was Sana Nakamura.

Lena stared at it for a long time. Nakamura was not a common name in Vessany — there had been, as far as she knew, only ever one family of them, and that family was her father, dead eleven years, and his brother, dead longer, and no one else. No grandmother had ever been spoken of. Her father had been a quiet man who mended awnings and said little about his childhood, and when Lena as a girl had asked about his parents he had given her the look he gave all questions about the past — not anger, exactly, but a kind of careful blankness, like a man closing shutters before a storm — and she had learned not to ask again.

The loan itself was strange even by the standards of the guild's strangest holdings. There was no sum of money anywhere in it. The consideration, as far as she could parse the archaic phrasing, was *passage* — passage for one soul out of a country that was dying, out of Vessany in its final season, when the gods were falling and the maps were already beginning to forget. In exchange for that passage, the creditor took security. Not land. Not years of life, though the guild traded those freely enough these days. Not a borrowed name, though heaven knew the vaults were full of those.

Security: *one name, yet unwritten, of the Borrower's line, to be held in trust until such season as the Lender should rise again.*

A name yet unwritten. A child not yet born.

Lena's hands were very steady on the table, which surprised her, because inside she felt the particular vertigo of standing on a stair that isn't where your foot expected it to be. She had spent her whole life believing her name was the one solid thing about her. Everything else had been taken or lost early — mother, country if you counted the half-country her father muttered about, any inheritance beyond a box of letters she couldn't read and a talent for numbers. But her name had been hers. Lena. Two syllables her father had chosen, she'd always assumed, out of nothing, out of love, out of the ordinary human business of naming a child.

Except apparently he hadn't chosen it. Apparently it had been chosen sixty-one years before her birth, by a woman she had never heard of, and signed over to a dying god as surety on a debt.

And the god — she looked again at the creditor's line, and felt the cold of the archive reach all the way through her — the god had risen again. That was the whole reason Corvane had ordered the audit. Three weeks ago the beggar on Candle Street had prayed over his daughter's fever and the fever had broken, and since then the old names had been answering all over the city, small mercies first and then larger ones, each one a transaction, each one a collection coming due. The gods of Vessany were awake. Which meant, by the plain terms of this contract, that the season named in the security clause had arrived.

Which meant someone was about to come collect her name.

* * *

She worked until dawn because working was the only thing she knew how to do with fear. She traced the contract through the cross-reference ledgers, following the chain of custody the way she'd follow any asset: entered into the archive in the year of the fall, transferred twice between custodians in the century after, flagged once — only once — for review, ninety years ago, by a senior scribe whose marginal note read simply, *Dormant. Do not wake.* And then nothing. Sixty years of silence, the contract sitting in its cabinet like a seed in frozen ground, while above it the guild traded years and names and doors and forgot, gradually and then completely, that it had ever been anything but a counting house.

At some point she realized she was looking for one specific thing, and she let herself admit it: she was looking for her own name elsewhere in the record. If the contract held her name in trust, there should be a corresponding entry somewhere — a registration, a notice, something. Ledgers answered to ledgers. That was the whole architecture of the guild's truth: nothing existed unless two books agreed on it.

She found the second book an hour before the morning shift came down. It was a thin volume, unlisted in the catalogue index, shelved among the founding records that no one touched because no one understood them anymore. Its cover bore no title, only a stamped image worn nearly smooth: a gate, and above the gate a flower.

Inside, the entries were few. A dozen pages, perhaps, in a mixture of scripts spanning centuries. Most of it was liturgical — invocations, dedications, the kind of language the guild had scrubbed from its official forms generations ago. But near the end, in a hand much steadier than the rest, was a single recent-looking entry, dated the year of Lena's birth.

*Sana Nakamura presents for registration the name of her granddaughter, per covenant. Entered not as debt nor as collateral but as trust. Let no power claim this child entire. Let her name remain hers to spend, hers to keep, hers to sign away or sign away never. Witnessed under the Door of Ada, which opens for the named and closes for the greedy.*

Below it, a signature: *S. Nakamura.*

Lena sat with both books open in front of her — the contract that called her name collateral, the register that called it trust — and understood that she was looking at a war conducted entirely in paperwork. Two documents, sixty years apart in intent, describing the same two syllables. One said: *this belongs to us.* The other said: *no, it belongs to her.* And between the two claims, like a coin left on a counter, she had walked through her whole life unaware, spending a name that had never once been unencumbered.

Her eyes stung. She told herself it was the candle smoke.

There was a knock at the archive door — two soft raps, the runner's pattern — and Tomas's voice, pitched low: "Lena? You down there? Corvane's asking for the god-debt inventory by midmorning and half the third floor is pretending they don't know where the transfer ledgers went."

"I have them," she called back, and heard how strange her voice sounded, and hated it.

A pause. "You all right?"

She looked at the open books. At the signature that was almost certainly her grandmother's — *S. Nakamura*, a woman who had bargained with a dying god to buy a child passage out of a falling country, and then, in the ruins, had done something stranger still: she had gone to the Door of Ada and registered her unborn granddaughter's name not as payment but as protection. *Let no power claim this child entire.* Someone had loved Lena before Lena existed, fiercely enough to fight a god with a pen.

"I'm fine," Lena said. "I found something. I need — give me an hour, and then I need to know everything you've ever heard about the garden behind the guildhall."

"The garden?" Tomas sounded genuinely baffled. "It's just a wall, Lena. Sealed since before either of us was born. There's nothing in there."

"That," said Lena Nakamura, closing the register gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping person, "is what worries me."

Because she had read the witness line again, the last line, the one that had made her blood run cold and warm at once: *witnessed under the Door of Ada.* Everyone knew Ada was a story — the last goddess, the one the histories said died holding the gate shut while Vessany fell around her. Buried, the stories said, in the garden behind the guildhall, under the wall no one had opened in two hundred years.

The stories said she was dead. But the ledger in Lena's hands said *which opens for the named* — present tense, a door described as opening, not as having opened. And the contract on her table said the lender would rise again.

Two books, agreeing at last. The gods were waking. The debt was due. And somewhere behind a sealed wall in the heart of the guildhall, a door was waiting for a name — her name — to decide whose it truly was.
