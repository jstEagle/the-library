## 4. The Guildmaster's Garden

The key to the garden was not a key.

It was a word, and Corvane Ash had carried it in his mouth for thirty years without once speaking it aloud — not to his wives, both dead now; not to his auditors, who would have sold their mothers for a glimpse of the inner ledger; not even to himself, in the privacy of his own skull, where a man might be forgiven for talking. He held it the way a miser holds a coin too worn to spend: by the habit of holding it alone.

Tonight he spoke it.

He stood before the iron door at the end of the guildhall's eastern corridor, past the vaults, past the cold room where the oldest contracts slept in lead-lined drawers, past the point where even the guild's own maps of its own building grew vague and apologetic. The door was black with age, and the hinges had not been oiled in living memory because oil was a courtesy and the door deserved none. Corvane put his lips close to the rusted seam and said the word — *Vessany* — softly, the way one says a name over a grave, and the lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Beyond it: stairs going down, then up, which was wrong, and he had stopped noticing that they were wrong sometime around his fortieth year. The passage smelled of wet stone and, faintly, of roses out of season. He carried no lamp. The dark ahead of him had learned, over the centuries, to make way for whoever held the word, and it parted now in a slow gray tide, revealing steps worn into shallow crescents by feet that had stopped climbing them two hundred years before Corvane was born.

At the top, the garden waited behind a second door, this one wooden, painted green so long ago that the paint had become less a color than a memory of one. He did not need a word for this one. It opened for him the way a mouth opens for a familiar tongue.

Cold air rolled out and broke against his face like water.

Corvane Ash stood on the threshold of the guild's oldest secret and let the cold have him for a moment — the whole of him, from the silver at his temples to the ache in his knees that he refused to call old age because old age was a debt and he did not intend to pay it. Then he stepped through, and pulled the green door shut behind him, and heard the night seal itself around the sound.

* * *

The garden did not look like much. That was the first thing every new guildmaster learned, and the last thing most of them believed.

It was perhaps four acres, walled in stone gone soft and furred with moss, laid out in rows like an orchard or a cemetery — and it was both, though the headstones were low and unmarked and the trees between them were nothing you would plant on purpose. Thorn scrub. Blackthorn. One enormous yew near the center whose roots had heaved the ground into a slow green wave, as if the earth there had flinched and never quite relaxed. Weeds everywhere. In high summer the whole plot stank of hot dust and something sweeter underneath, something like rotting fruit and candlewax, which the gardeners' ledgers — the few pages that survived about the garden at all — called *the smell of sleeping*.

Four acres of weeds behind the richest building in Vessany. Corvane had inherited it at thirty-one, along with the word and the ledgers and the particular loneliness of the office, and for the first year he had come down here weekly, certain there must be more. There was always more. That was the guild's founding principle: everything has a price, and everything priced can be owned.

He had found the more eventually. It had simply taken him twenty-nine years to learn how to look.

He walked the rows now, hands clasped behind his back, boots quiet on the packed earth. The moon was three days shy of full and gave the garden a washed, underwater light. Frost had come early this year — the first frost in Vessany's recorded history to arrive before the Feast of Ledgers — and it lay on the weeds in a thin silver skin, and here and there it had melted, in small perfect circles, around the bases of certain graves.

Melted frost. In circles. As if something under the ground were warm.

Corvane crouched beside one of these and pressed two fingers to the soil. Not warm, exactly. But not cold either. A soil at body temperature, at *someone's* temperature, holding heat the way a bed holds the shape of the sleeper who has just left it.

"Ah," he said quietly. "There you are."

For six hundred years the guild had told the city that the old gods of Vessany were dead. This was even true, in the way that all the guild's best truths were true: technically, provably, and beside the point. The gods had died in the fall — when the country's borders were unwritten and its name struck from the maps of everyone who mattered — and the guild, which had been their priesthood before it was their creditors, had done what any sensible institution does with valuable assets in a market collapse. It had buried them. Deep, in consecrated ground it controlled absolutely, in a garden walled off from the world and from history both, and it had written them into the ledgers as *dormant*, which is the word accountants use for *dead but still appreciating*.

Dormant assets. Corvane had spent his whole career on dormant assets. Names nobody remembered owning. Years of life sitting in escrow. Doors to countries that no longer existed, kept oiled and waiting in case the countries came back. The guild's genius — *his* genius — was patience. You did not sell a thing the moment you got it. You waited for the world to forget it had ever wanted it, and then you sold it back at ten times the price, and the buyer wept with gratitude because he thought he was buying a miracle.

And now the world had started praying again.

He rose, joints complaining, and walked on. Word had reached him at dusk, carried up from the shrine district by three separate runners who did not know they were carrying the same news: a beggar named Oskar Vey had prayed at the cracked altar of Saint Ilse-the-Forgotten — a saint so minor she didn't even rate a god's title, a small local divinity, a parish miracle at best — and his daughter's fever had broken before he finished the amen. Broken. Not eased. *Broken*, the way a fever breaks when something takes hold of it and snaps it in half, and the girl had sat up asking for bread, and the bread had been in her hand.

Three runners. Three witnesses. And this morning, Corvane knew, there would be thirty, and by week's end three hundred, and the city would go mad with hope, and hope was the one commodity the guild had never managed to corner.

Until now.

Because here was the thing the beggars and the bishops and the praying crowds did not understand, the thing Corvane had understood since he was a young auditor tracing a debt backward through forty years of entries until it terminated, impossibly, in a prayer: *the gods keep accounts too.* Every miracle was a transaction. Every answered prayer drew on a balance, and the balances had been empty for six hundred years — which was why the prayers had gone unanswered, why Vessany's altars had gathered dust and its priests had turned to lending, quietly, shamefully, profitably, trading in years and names because heaven's credit had run dry.

But the gods were stirring now. Something had changed. Something had woken them early, or hungry, or desperate — and a creditor waking desperate was the most dangerous creature alive, and also, handled correctly, the most profitable.

Corvane intended to handle it correctly.

* * *

Near the center of the garden, the yew threw its long shadow across a grave larger than the rest — a slab of black stone, unmarked, older than the wall around it. He did not approach it. No guildmaster approached it. The founding entries were explicit on this single point, in a hand so furious the ink had scarred the vellum: *the central grave is not an asset. Do not audit the central grave.*

Corvane had spent a satisfying decade determining that the entry was written by Ada herself, last of the gods of Vessany, the one they had never managed to bury properly — or had buried twice, depending which fragmentary page you believed, and the guild's archivists had killed each other over less. He filed the knowledge away the way he filed everything: carefully, in the dark, where it could earn interest.

Instead he turned down a row of smaller graves and stopped at the third, where the frost had melted widest. This one hummed. Not a sound — a vibration, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the fillings of his back molars, a note below hearing like the growl of a distant engine. He had felt it faintly for years and told himself it was the aquifer. Tonight it was strong enough to make the loose skin of his hands tremble.

He knelt. Put his palm flat on the earth.

Beneath the soil, beneath six hundred years of silence and guild bookkeeping, something turned over in its sleep. He felt it the way you feel a great ship change course while standing far aft — not movement exactly, but intention, mass deciding. The hum deepened. Somewhere down the row, another grave answered it, a half-tone lower, and then a third, and for a moment the whole garden was a chord, vast and groaning and barely awake, and Corvane Ash knelt in the frost with his hand on the dirt and his heart going like a boy's.

They were waking. All of them. The buried pantheon of a murdered country, lying in rows like coins in a hoard, and every one of them dreaming of being owed.

He took his hand back slowly. His palm was printed with soil, dark and fine, and the print did not brush off. He looked at it for a long moment — his own hand, marked — and then he laughed, once, softly, a sound with no humor in it and a great deal of satisfaction.

"Do you know what I see," he said to the graves, to the rows, to the chord still dying slowly in the frozen air, "when I look at you?"

The garden, naturally, said nothing. It had been saying nothing for six hundred years. But the frost had melted further now, he noticed. The circles were widening. Merging.

"I see collateral," Corvane said. "I see the greatest portfolio ever assembled, sitting unmanaged under four acres of weeds, and I am the only man alive with the word, the ledgers, and the will." He straightened, brushing his knees, precise even in his gestures, especially alone. "Your faithful servants sold your miracles for six centuries and called it stewardship. They prayed to you and lent in your name and buried you like embarrassments. I am not your servant. I have never been anyone's servant. What I offer is better than worship."

He began walking toward the green door, unhurried, and the garden's attention — he could feel it now, unmistakably, a pressure like weather — followed him along the rows.

"What I offer," he said, "is management."

The plan assembled itself in his mind as he walked, the way plans always did for him, clean and interlocking, each piece a document waiting to be drafted. The gods were answering prayers again, but blindly, chaotically, bleeding power to any beggar with a sick child, drawing on reserves they did not understand they had. Unmanaged. The guild would fix that. Flag every god-related debt in the books — he would order it tomorrow, first thing, before the other masters woke. Trace every prayer answered back to its account. And then, when the panic came, when the aging collectors came, when the city tore itself apart wanting miracles it could not afford — the guild would step forward with the only thing it had ever truly sold.

Terms. Structure. A contract.

Let the gods wake. Waking things needed someone to hold their pen. And the man who held the pen for a pantheon did not serve the pantheon; the pantheon served him. Six hundred years of dormant interest, compounding in the dark. Enough power to do more than buy back Vessany's name from the maps. Enough to write the maps himself.

Enough, said a voice in him that sounded suspiciously like his father, poor and pious and dead at fifty-three, to finally pay for everything.

"I don't pay debts," Corvane said aloud, to the garden, to the dark, to the dead. "I collect them."

At the green door he paused, hand on the wood, and looked back once at the rows of sleeping graves, the melting frost, the black slab at the center that he did not look at directly. The hum had not stopped. If anything it had organized itself, settled into something almost like breathing — slow, deep, patient, the respiration of something very large deciding whether to go on pretending to sleep.

Something in the garden, he thought, had listened to him tonight. And something in the garden, he thought, with the first flicker of unease he had permitted himself in thirty years, had taken notes.

He shut the door. Spoke the word. Descended the stairs that went down and then up, which were wrong, and which he had stopped noticing, and which he did not notice now either.

Behind him, in the sealed and freezing dark, every circle of bare earth in the garden widened by another finger's width, and the frost retreated from the graves like a crowd giving way, and under the yew's long shadow the black slab at the center — the one that was not an asset, the one no guildmaster audits — lay bare to the moon for the first time in six hundred years.

Empty. It had always been empty. That was in the founding entries too, if you knew where to look.

By morning, three of Corvane's senior scribes would report the same impossible thing: a page in the general ledger that resisted the pen. An entry that rewrote itself overnight, in a hand none of them recognized, dated sixty years in the past.

Corvane would read the report at breakfast, and set down his cup, and smile — because the entry concerned a debt of collateral, and the collateral's name was Nakamura.
