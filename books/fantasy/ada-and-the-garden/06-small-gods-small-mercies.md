## 6. Small Gods, Small Mercies

The petition was three pages long and smelled of tallow smoke and desperation, which Tomas Quill had learned to read the way other men read ink.

He'd been a runner for the Guild of Recorded Exchange since he was nine years old — seven years of carrying documents through Vessany's crooked streets, up staircases that leaned like drunkards, across bridges that hummed when the wind came off the canals. He knew every shortcut in the city that no longer appeared on any map anyone would admit to owning. He knew which doors opened for a guild seal and which ones required a bribe, a sob story, or a well-aimed boot. And he knew, better than most, that the guild's business had always been strange. You didn't spend your childhood ferrying contracts for traded years and borrowed names without developing a certain tolerance for the impossible.

But this morning, the impossible had developed a taste for the streets.

It started at the Tallowmarket, where a fishwife stood on an overturned crate telling anyone who'd listen that her brother's withered arm had straightened overnight. *Straight as a mast,* she said. *He prayed at the old shrine on Candle Lane — the one nobody's swept since my grandmother's day — and come morning, straight as a mast.* She said it the way people say things they don't quite believe themselves, testing each word against the air to see if it holds.

Tomas shouldered past her with the petition tucked inside his coat, because runners did not stop for street prophets, and guildmaster Ash docked wages for lateness, not for curiosity.

Except that by the time he reached Candle Lane itself, there were forty people outside the shrine. By the time he fought his way to the door, there were eighty, and a woman was weeping into her hands while her neighbors touched her shoulders like she was something holy, and someone else was shouting that the god inside had taken her stutter, *taken it clean out of her mouth*, and she could speak plain as rain now, plain as rain.

The shrine was small and ugly and old, a squat stone box wedged between a chandler's shop and a house that had burned down twice and given up. Its god was a minor one — Tomas didn't even know its name, only that the niche above the altar held a statue of something round-shouldered and patient, its face worn smooth by centuries of weather and neglect. There was no incense. No offerings. Cobwebs hung from the statue's elbows like lace.

And the cobwebs were burning.

Not with fire. With light. A soft gold that moved through them like breath, and beneath it the statue's smooth blank face seemed — Tomas would swear this later, swearing it to Lena over cheap wine, swearing it until she believed him — seemed to be *listening*.

The woman who'd lost her stutter knelt at the altar, laughing and crying at once, saying thank you, thank you, thank you.

"Thank you," the statue said back.

Its voice was dry as old paper. It came from everywhere in the little stone room and nowhere in particular, and every hair on Tomas's arms stood up under his coat.

"You are welcome," the god said. "Now we settle accounts."

The laughter stopped.

Tomas had delivered petitions to shrines before. Dead shrines, mostly — the whole city was littered with them, little stone boxes where the old gods of Vessany had once been worshipped before the country fell and the guild took over the business of keeping accounts. The gods had gone silent generations ago. Everyone knew that. Everyone knew the prayers went unanswered and the offerings rotted and the priests became scribes and the temples became warehouses, and that was simply how the world worked. The guild traded in years and names and doors, but never in miracles. Miracles were out of stock.

Apparently the stock had come in.

"What accounts?" the stutterless woman asked. Her voice was still clear as rain. It shook anyway.

"Your speech," the god said. "Restored this morning, upon request. The price of restored speech is—" and here the statue's blank face tilted, the way a man tilts his head when consulting a ledger, "—one memory of being understood."

"I don't— what does that mean?"

"It means you will keep the words. You will lose the times someone truly heard you." The voice was not cruel. That was the worst part, Tomas thought later. It was not cruel at all. It was *procedural*. "This is fair. This is the rate. Do you consent?"

"No," the woman said. "Wait. I didn't know there was a price. Nobody said—"

"You prayed. I answered. The account stands open until paid." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "You may refuse. But the debt does not vanish, child. It waits. Debts are patient. It is the one thing we taught your grandmothers' grandmothers, and they taught it to the scribes, and the scribes built a guild on it. We have been asleep a long time. Our books have not."

The woman looked around at the crowd, at the eighty faces pressed against the doorway, and Tomas watched her understand — the way you understand a wave is coming, not by seeing it but by seeing everyone else see it — that she was not going to be allowed to leave this room the same person she'd entered it.

"I consent," she whispered.

The gold light in the cobwebs brightened. The woman swayed. And something left her — Tomas couldn't see it, but he felt it pass him like a draft from a door opening in winter, felt it the way you feel someone walk over your grave — and when the woman straightened, she was smiling, and she was crying, and she said, "I can't remember my wedding day. I can remember that I was married. I can't remember being *happy* about it. Why can't I remember being happy about it?"

Nobody answered her. The statue's face was smooth and blank again, its account settled, its attention already drifting toward the next petitioner in line.

Tomas got out of there. He told himself it was because of the petition, because Corvane Ash would flay him if the delivery was late, and that was even true. But his hands were shaking so badly he had to hold the document against his chest with both arms like a shield, and when a beggar on the shrine steps grabbed his sleeve and asked, eyes shining, *boy, did you see, did you see it answer*, Tomas lied and said he hadn't seen anything at all.

* * *

The petition was addressed to the Shrine of the Ninefold Tide, down in the harbor district, and it was exactly the kind of errand Tomas hated: a debt-collection notice dressed up as a prayer request. He'd read enough of the guild's correspondence to know the shape of these things. Some harbormaster's widow had prayed — before the gods woke, back when praying was free — for her son's safe return from a voyage that had already sunk him. The guild had sold her a *consolation*, one of their nastier products: a memory, implanted, of the son coming home. Now that the gods were answering again, the widow had gone to a real shrine to ask whether her boy might actually be alive somewhere, and the real answer threatened to expose the fake one, and the guild wanted the matter handled quietly before the widow started asking questions in public.

Corvane Ash's covering note, in his small cold hand, read: *Deliver to the shrine keeper. If the shrine is active, do not engage. Observe and report.*

*If the shrine is active.* Two weeks ago that phrase would have been a joke. Now it was a warning label.

The Ninefold Tide shrine sat at the end of a rotting pier, half-swallowed by salt rot, its nine-faced god staring out over gray water. Tomas had delivered to it a dozen times over the years and it had never once been anything but empty and cold and smelling of dead fish. Today there was a keeper — a stooped old man sweeping gull droppings off the steps — and today the nine faces were all faintly, horribly *damp*, as though the statue had been sweating.

The old man took the petition without a word. Then, as Tomas turned to go, he said: "You're guild."

"Not by choice," Tomas said, which was true, and also the kind of thing you said, and also — he realized as the words left his mouth — possibly a mistake.

"Everything is by choice," the keeper said mildly. "That's what the accounts are *for*." He broke the guild's seal, read three lines, and snorted. "Consolation product. We remember those. My lady of the tide remembers everything sold against her waters." He looked up, and his eyes were the flat gray of the harbor in November. "Boy. Do you know what happens to a consolation when the real thing comes home?"

"No," Tomas said honestly.

"Neither do we. That's rather the point." The old man tucked the petition into his robe. "Tell your masters the tide keeps its own books. Tell them the interest has been accruing since before their grandfathers were ink." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Tell them to check their ledgers. Carefully. Every page."

Tomas walked back up the pier very fast and did not run, because running would have meant admitting what his legs wanted to do.

* * *

He found Lena at the guildhall near dusk, in the scribal hall, surrounded by towers of ledgers and looking like a woman losing a war against paperwork. Her sleeves were pushed up, there was ink on her cheekbone, and she was staring at a single page with an expression Tomas had never seen on her before. Lena Nakamura did not startle. Lena Nakamura did not gape. Lena Nakamura balanced entries for traded years the way other people balanced their supper, and she did it flawlessly, and she was the only person in Vessany whom Tomas trusted completely, which is why he crossed the hall, dropped into the chair beside her, and said:

"The gods are charging for miracles."

She looked up. "Hello to you too."

"Lena. I'm serious. I saw one. Little shrine on Candle Lane, the round-shouldered one, the one with the cobwebs. A woman asked for her speech back and it gave it to her and then it took—" he groped for it, "—it took the *feeling* of being understood. Every time anyone ever really listened to her. Gone. Like clipping coins." He lowered his voice. "It called it a rate. It called it *fair*. And the whole street's lining up to pay."

Lena set down her pen slowly. "The guildmaster flagged every god-related entry this morning. All of them. Six hundred years of records." Her voice was carefully level, which Tomas knew meant it wasn't. "Tomas, some of those contracts name collateral. Real collateral. People."

"People like us?"

"People like anyone." She glanced toward the guildmaster's door at the end of the hall, shut and dark. "There's a page on my desk that keeps rewriting itself. I haven't told anyone but you."

"That's flattering and terrifying."

"You're welcome." But she didn't smile, and neither did he, and for a moment they just sat there in the lamplight among the ledger towers, two small people in a building full of debts, listening to the city murmur beyond the windows with a new note in its voice — hope, raw and unpriced, pouring into the streets.

"They'll all go," Tomas said quietly. "To the shrines. They're already going. Half of Vessany is on its knees tonight and nobody's told them what it costs."

"Somebody will figure it out."

"And till then?"

Lena looked at the self-rewriting page on her desk, and then at him, and something in her face made his stomach drop. "Then we'd better find out what's in these books before the books find out what's in us."

* * *

He dreamed about the shrine that night.

In the dream he was delivering a petition to the round-shouldered god, and the statue's blank face turned to him, and it said, in its dry paper voice: *Ah. The runner. We have an entry for you too.*

*I don't have an account,* he said.

*Everyone has an account.* The cobwebs glowed. *Yours was opened the day you first carried a guild seal. Every letter you delivered was a prayer routed through our house. Every door you knocked on. We kept count, little runner. We keep count of everything.* The statue leaned close, and its breath smelled of grave-soil and roses. *Would you like to know your balance?*

And Tomas, being Tomas — being a boy who'd grown up hungry and quick and unable to leave a closed box alone — said yes.

The god smiled, or the smooth stone did something that was almost a smile, and it began to read aloud. His mother's laugh, itemized. The taste of the plums he'd stolen at nine, itemized. The exact weight of Lena's hand on his shoulder the day his father died, itemized, priced, *payable*. Line after line after line, and with each one something went dim behind his eyes, some small warm room in him going dark, and he understood with perfect dream-clarity that the god was not stealing anything. He was *withdrawing*. These were his own memories, held in trust, and the trust had terms, and he had never read the terms, and—

He woke gasping in his cot in the runners' dormitory, heart slamming, and lay in the dark doing inventory. Mother's laugh: there. Plums: there. Lena's hand: there. All of it, intact. Just a dream. Just a stupid dream.

But downstairs, through the floorboards, he could hear the guildhall already awake — voices, hurried footsteps, the ring of the great front bell being pulled over and over — and when he stumbled to the window he saw why.

The sky over the harbor district was glowing. Not dawn-glow. Gold-glow, pulsing softly, shrine by shrine by shrine, like embers catching along a fuse laid across the whole sleeping city.

Every god in Vessany was awake. And every god, Tomas thought, pulling on his boots with shaking hands, was open for business.

He needed to find Lena. Whatever was written in those ledgers — whatever was written about *him* — they needed to read it before the collectors came calling.

Because the gods had made one thing perfectly clear, in that dry procedural voice, in the little stone room on Candle Lane:

Debts are patient.

And theirs had just finished waiting.
