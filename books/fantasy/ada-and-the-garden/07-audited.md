## 7. Audited

The summons came at the hour when the guildhall's lamps burned lowest and the ink in every well had gone cold and sluggish, the way ink did when it was thinking about drying up.

A runner Lena didn't know — one of Corvane's own, gray-liveried where the ordinary runners wore brown — stood at the end of her aisle without speaking. He simply held out a card of black paper with a single line of silver script that was not written so much as *deposited*, each letter sitting on the surface like a coin laid on a dead man's eye.

*The Guildmaster will see you. Now. Vault level.*

Lena set down her pen. Around her, forty other scribes bent over their ledgers in the great counting room, and none of them looked up, which meant all of them had heard, which meant by morning the whole floor would know that Nakamura had been called down. There were only two reasons a scribe went to the vault level. One was promotion.

She thought of the page that rewrote itself. She thought of her name in someone else's hand, sixty years old, patient as a seed. She thought of Aunt Iyo's voice: *They were prayer-books first, girl. Everything else came after.*

"Tell him I'm coming," she said, though the runner was already gone.

She took the stairs rather than the lift. It bought her ninety seconds and she used every one of them, pressing her palm flat against the cold stone of the stairwell wall, breathing, arranging her face into the particular expression she'd worn through every audit since she was fourteen: attentive, competent, slightly tired, entirely unremarkable. A scribe who was remarkable got noticed. A scribe who got noticed got sold. That had been the first lesson of the ledger house, taught to her before she could write a full sentence, and she had built her whole life inside its shadow like a plant growing away from a wall.

But the wall had moved. The wall had her name on it now.

At the bottom of the stairs, two doorwards flanked the iron gate, and they did not ask for her token. They simply looked at her, and something in their looking made her skin prickle — the way you feel watched not by men but by the building itself, by the ledger-house that had swallowed her grandmother's signature and her mother's silence and her own thirty years of careful, invisible work.

The gate opened without being touched.

* * *

Corvane Ash received her in the Long Room behind the vault, a chamber Lena had never seen and had not known existed, which was itself an education. The guildhall above was all arithmetic — rows, columns, candlelight, the shuffling patience of accounts. This room was nothing like it. The ceiling arched up into darkness the eye couldn't price. The walls were shelved floor to ceiling with books, but they were not ledgers; they were older, fatter, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, and their spines carried no titles. Some of them breathed. She was almost certain some of them breathed.

Between the shelves stood a single table of black wood, and on it lay three things: a decanter of amber liquor, two glasses, and a folder of loose pages tied with red string.

Corvane stood at the window at the far end, hands clasped behind his back, watching the garden.

She had only ever glimpsed the garden through slits and keyholes — the sealed square behind the guildhall, walled higher than any roof, its gate locked with seven locks that no keyring in the building opened. From the vault window it lay below like a held breath: dark soil, bare trees, frost silvering the graves.

Graves. She hadn't known until this moment that they were graves. She had always assumed the shapes beneath the snow were planting beds.

"You've been busy," Corvane said, without turning. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man reading figures aloud. "Iyo Ferren. Retired, senior class, pensioned eleven years. You visited her twice last week. Paid her in scrip and a bottle of plum wine." He turned then, and his face was exactly as she remembered it from the few times she'd stood in his presence: long, dry, courteous, the face of a man who found everything interesting and nothing surprising. "You asked her about Vessany."

"I asked her about archaic notation," Lena said. "Some of the older contracts use—"

"Lena." He said her name the way he said everything, gently, precisely, and it landed on her like a hand on the back of her neck. "Sit down. Have a drink. Let's not spend the evening pretending either of us is stupid. It insults us both, and I have very little evening left."

She sat. She did not take the drink. He poured one anyway, for himself, and settled into the chair across from her with the ease of a host who had never once been refused anything.

"Do you know," he said, "what I admire most about you? Everyone downstairs thinks you're dull. Diligent, accurate, forgettable. You have spent fifteen years making yourself the least interesting object in that building, and it is genuinely masterful work. I've watched scribes try to be invisible before. They usually overdo it. You didn't. You became furniture." He smiled faintly. "Furniture that reads."

"The guildmaster honors me."

"He does not." Corvane lifted the red-stringed folder and laid it on the table between them, but did not untie it. "He is about to ruin her, and he wants her to understand why, because he finds waste distasteful. So." He untied the string.

The pages inside were copies. Lena knew them instantly, the way you know your own handwriting on a stranger's letter — her own fair-copy script, meticulous, the work of three nights spent hunched over the self-rewriting page, transcribing what it became each time it changed. She had hidden the originals. She had burned her rough notes. But she had kept the fair copies, because a scribe who destroyed records stopped being a scribe, and she had thought — she had *thought* —

"There is a room," Corvane said, "on the third floor of the west wing. Old records storage. It has a lock that opens to my seal and four other seals, and one of those four belongs to a woman named Maret Doss, who has served this house faithfully for twenty-two years and whose daughter needs a surgery that honest wages cannot buy. I don't tell you this to threaten her. I tell you because you'll want to hate someone other than me tonight, and I'd rather you hated accurately." He turned a page with one finger. "Night of the ninth. Night of the eleventh. Night of the thirteenth. Three visits. You copied a contract that predates the guild charter, that references a country erased from every map we keep, and that names — let me find it — ah. Here."

He read aloud, in the flat tone of a man reciting inventory:

"*Collateral: one (1) soul, female line, to be born of the house of Nakamura, held in trust against the reopening of the Door.*" He looked up. "Held in trust. Interesting word, trust. Most collateral isn't trusted with anything. Someone wanted you protected, Lena. Someone with access to founding-era script and a sentimentality problem." He folded his hands. "Your grandmother, I'd wager. The dates fit. Iyo would know. Did she?"

Lena kept her face still. Fifteen years of practice. Inside, her heart was going like a runner's feet on cobbles.

"She talked about notation," Lena said.

"Mm." Corvane rose, walked to the shelf, and drew down one of the breathing books. Up close she could see that its cover was stitched with thread that might have been hair. He did not open it. He simply held it, the way a man holds a sleeping animal, and spoke to her over the top of it.

"Three days ago, a beggar on Cordwainer Street prayed to a god that has been dead for two hundred years, and his daughter's fever broke. Two days ago, a fisherman prayed for a catch and pulled up enough silver-belly to feed the harbor. Yesterday, a minor shrine god collected a debt of memory from a boy carrying our mail — you may know him. Tomas. Red hair, bad knee, talks too much." Something flickered across Corvane's face at the boy's name, too quick to read. "The gods are awake, Lena. All of them. And every debt this guild ever recorded in their names is coming due at once — including, I suspect, the oldest one in the building. The one with your name in it."

He set the book back on its shelf. The shelf sighed.

"So here is what happens now. Option one." He returned to the table, refilled his glass, and did not offer her one. "I sell you. Tonight. Your contract is valid, your collateral is documented, and there are parties in this city — old families, older churches — who would pay a fortune for a soul named in a founding covenant. You would live out a short, expensive life as someone's talisman, and I would have removed from my house the one scribe who knows too much and can be bought by no one, because everyone can be bought by no one except by fear, and fear I am using right now."

"And option two," Lena said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect.

"Option two." Corvane sat down again, leaned forward, and for the first time his mildness dropped away — not into anger, but into something worse: sincerity. "Option two is that you are the most talented notational analyst this guild has produced in a century, and I am a man standing in front of a vault full of sleeping gods with a ledger system built to trade in years and names, and I need someone who can do what no one alive can do. Bind them. Write the returning power into the books. Make it *accountable*. Do you understand what I'm offering? Not survival. Partnership. When the gods finish collecting — and they are collecting, Lena, half my senior partners aged thirty years in a single night last week, you've seen the funerals — whatever remains of their power will flow through documents. Through ink. Through *us*. And the person holding the pen when that happens will hold more than any guildmaster has ever held. More than any king. I'm offering you the pen."

He slid the folder of her confiscated copies across the table.

"Keep these. Consider them a signing bonus. Your originals are already in my safe, along with your contract, and they will stay there — untouched, unexecuted — for exactly as long as you are useful and loyal. Which, knowing you, will be a very long time." He smiled again, warm as a lamp. "You cannot win against me, Lena. I own the ledger house. Every record of who you are lives under my roof. But you don't have to win. You only have to write."

* * *

Later — much later, after the doorwards had walked her back up through the sleeping floors, after the great counting room had received her like a tomb receiving its occupant — Lena sat at her desk with her lamp trimmed low and her hands flat on the blotter, and made herself think.

That was the thing Corvane had miscalculated, or perhaps hadn't miscalculated at all and simply didn't fear: he had given her time alone with the shape of the trap, and traps look different once you stop struggling in them.

He owned the records. True. He owned the contract with her name in it, the covenant page, the vault, the seals, the doorwards, the city's courts and probably half its clergy. If she ran, he would sell her by morning. If she fought, he would bury her in paperwork so thorough her own mother wouldn't recognize the corpse. If she refused him, option one. If she accepted him, she became the pen in the hand of a man who intended to chain gods into a filing system.

And yet.

She drew a fresh sheet toward her. Dipped her pen. And wrote, in her small, exact, forgettable hand, a single line:

*Audited by C.A., this date. Findings: satisfactory. Subject retained.*

Then she sat back and looked at it, and felt the resolve settle into her the way sediment settles in water — slowly, inevitably, until the water runs clear.

Because here was what Corvane Ash, with all his seals and shelves of breathing books, had never understood about ink: authority doesn't make writing true. Writing makes things true. He thought he owned the ledger house. But the ledgers had been prayer-books first, and prayers had never obeyed the man who paid the priest — they answered whoever spoke them honestly. Aunt Iyo had told her that much. And the grave-soil ink, if Iyo's hints were half right, answered intent, not office.

Corvane wanted a pen. Very well. She would be a pen.

Pens get close to the hand. Pens see everything the hand writes. And pens, if they are patient, if they are diligent, if they are furniture — pens can be dipped in anything.

She blotted the line, folded the sheet, and slipped it into the inner pocket of her coat, next to the scrap of grave-dirt-stained cloth she'd taken from Aunt Iyo's sewing basket, next to the copied coordinates of the garden wall's weakest stone that Tomas had pressed into her palm that afternoon with a grin and a whispered *Thursday?*

"Thursday," she murmured to the empty counting room, to the forty sleeping ledgers, to the country that existed only in writing and the door that waited inside it.

Above her, faint through the vaulted ceiling, she heard the garden exhale — or imagined she did — a sound like a thousand flowers opening underground, all at once, in the dark.

Two nights, then. She had two nights to learn everything the ledgers knew about binding, before she walked into the guildmaster's garden and began unbinding the world.
