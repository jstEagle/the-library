## 8. The Founding Page

The vault did not have a lock so much as a hunger.

Lena had heard the stories since her first day as an apprentice: that the door to the deep archive tasted the blood of whoever touched it, that it opened for guildmasters and closed on thieves, that old Scribe Pelloran had lost three fingers to it and gained nothing but a story nobody believed. The truth, as with most things in the guildhall, was smaller and stranger. The door was iron banded with lead, and it opened only when you spoke your name aloud — not to a guard, not to a mechanism, but to the door itself, as if introducing yourself to something that had been waiting.

"Lena Nakamura," she said, and her voice came out thinner than she intended. "Scribe of the third bench."

The bolts turned like knuckles cracking.

Inside, the air was cold and dry and smelled of tallow, dust, and something older underneath — a mineral smell, like rain about to fall on stone that hadn't seen weather in centuries. Lamps burned in niches without anyone to trim them. She had been down here twice before, both times under Corvane's eye, both times to fetch ledgers he named by number. This was the first time she had come alone, with a stolen hour between the midnight audit and the dawn shift, and a key she had copied from wax pressed against Aunt Iyo's ring while the old woman slept in her chair.

She should have felt like a thief. Instead she felt like a woman walking into a room where an answer had been sitting, patient as furniture, all her life.

* * *

The vault was deeper than the guildhall above it. That was the first impossibility, and Lena made herself look at it directly rather than explain it away. The hall above occupied a city block. The vault's central aisle ran straight back farther than any block in Vessany — farther than Vessany itself, if Vessany still had edges, which everyone agreed it didn't, though no one agreed on why. Shelves rose out of the lamplight into darkness. Ledgers stood on them in their thousands, spines lettered in hands dead two hundred years, three hundred, more. Some of the oldest were not books at all but tablets, bundles of reed, slats of wood lacquered black and scratched with characters no living scribe could read.

*Prayer-books first,* Aunt Iyo had said, two nights ago, in her room above the dye-shop with its smell of mordant and old wool. *You think we invented the trade, girl? We inherited it. Before the fall, every entry was an offering. You wrote what you wanted to give the god, and the god wrote back what it would cost. The ledger was the conversation. Then Vessany fell, and the gods went quiet, and the conversation kept going with no one answering — and men like Corvane learned they could hold both sides of the book.*

Lena walked the aisle with her lamp held high, counting shelf-marks the way Iyo had taught her, following the sequence backward through time. Recent ledgers: leather, brass corners, neat. Older: vellum gone soft as cloth. Older still: skins she chose not to identify. At the very end of the aisle, past a shelf whose contents had been chained shut since before the guild kept records of its own keeping, stood a lectern of black wood, and on the lectern lay a single book.

It was small. That was the thing that undid her composure, standing there in the cold with her heart going like a runner's feet. She had imagined the founding ledger as a massive thing, a tome, a monument. It was barely larger than her two hands. Its cover was grey leather worn smooth as riverstone, and it had no title, no guild seal, no lock. Just a faint impression on the front cover, so shallow she might have imagined it: the shape of a door.

Her hand shook. She noticed it the way you notice rain starting — one drop, then the whole sky.

*Careful,* said the sensible voice in her head, the voice trained by ten years at the third bench. *Anything written here is older than the rules that govern writing. Anything you read may read you back.*

She opened it anyway. She had been opening other people's accounts since she was fourteen. This was only one more, she told herself, and then stopped telling herself anything at all, because the first page took the words out of her mouth and left them somewhere behind her.

* * *

The handwriting was beautiful. That was her first coherent thought, and it shamed her — a scribe's vanity surfacing even here, even now — but it was true. The script flowed across the page with the confidence of someone who had never once needed to cross out a line. And beneath the beauty, in the way the letters leaned, in the spacing of the words, Lena recognized a rhythm she knew intimately, because she saw it every morning in her own hand when she was tired and honest: the handwriting of someone who wrote the way other people prayed.

The first entries were not trades. They were vows.

*We who remain of Vessany, being few, write this so that what was may be owed forward rather than lost. Let it be entered that we do not surrender our country. We lend it. To the earth. To the garden. To the last of our gods, who carries it where conquerors cannot follow.*

Page after page. Names — hundreds of names, each one followed by a line of terms, and the terms were not coin or years or borrowed faces but things like *one lullaby, to be returned to my daughter's daughter* and *the taste of plums from the tree by the east gate, holder unknown* and *my anger, until such time as it can be set down safely.* A whole country filing itself away in the only vault its conquerors would never think to search: a book of debts owed to the future.

And then, near the middle, a page whose margins had been repaired with newer paper, sewn in with thread gone amber — a page that had been torn out and put back.

Lena's lamp trembled. In the light, the entry swam up at her like a face rising through water.

*Let it be entered that Mirei Nakamura, scribe of the second bench, being the last keeper of the Door-covenant, does hereby deposit the following: one name, unborn, to be held in trust by the Door of Ada until such time as the bearer claims it freely. Terms: the name shall belong to no god, no guild, no master, no mother, and no ledger but the bearer's own hand. Collateral: none. Price: everything I have, which is this book, this garden, and my silence.*

Below it, in the same flawless hand, a signature. *Mirei Nakamura.*

And below the signature, added later — the ink a different shade, the pressure lighter, as if written by someone exhausted past fear — a single line:

*She will be born in the spring. Let her be born free.*

* * *

Lena sat down on the floor. There was no decision in it; her legs simply ended their argument with her.

The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere far back in the vault, a shelf settled with a sound like a sigh. She sat with the open book in her lap and read the entry again, and again, and each time the same two sentences collided inside her and refused to resolve:

*Sold before her birth by an ancestor who bargained with a dying god.* That was what the contract in the active files said — the contract Corvane's creditors had been circling for weeks, the debt that had brought her to Aunt Iyo, to the vault, to this floor. Sixty years old. Her name listed as collateral. She had read it a dozen times, and every reading had landed in her chest like a stone: *you were property before you were a person; your life began as a line item.*

But the founding ledger said otherwise. The founding ledger said the collateral clause was a mask over a covenant. Someone — Mirei, her grandmother, a name she had never heard spoken aloud in her life — had taken the cruelest document in the archive and hidden a sanctuary inside it. Anyone reading the contract would see a child sold. Only someone who traced the paper back, who pulled the thread all the way to the founding book, would find the truth stitched underneath: *the name shall belong to no god, no guild, no master.*

No god. Lena thought of the beggar's daughter in the shrine square, healed overnight, and of Tomas's white face three days ago when he came off the shrine steps having traded half his childhood for a minor god's mercy. The gods were awake again, and they were collecting, and every name in every ledger was suddenly currency in an economy that had reopened without warning. Except hers. Except, if this covenant held, the one name in Vessany that no collector could touch — because a woman sixty years dead had paid the price in advance.

*Everything I have, which is this book, this garden, and my silence.*

The garden. Lena looked up the dark aisle toward the world above, where behind the guildhall's sealed wall the buried gods slept under rows of impossible flowers. Mirei had been there. Mirei had carried the covenant to Ada herself — or Ada had come to Mirei; the ledger didn't say, and its silences felt deliberate, the silences of a writer who knew exactly which questions she wanted her reader to ask.

Grief arrived first, and it surprised her with its direction. She had expected grief for herself — for the stolen childhood of not knowing, for every year she'd spent believing her name was a debt instead of a gift. But what flooded her now was grief for Mirei. A scribe of the second bench, in the year the country fell, holding a newborn granddaughter in one arm and a collapsing world in the other, and choosing — with nothing left, with everything already taken — to spend her last holdings on a girl who wouldn't be born for decades. Who would never know her face. Who would grow up an orphan in the guild that had swallowed her country, balancing other people's debts, never once told that the most important line in the archive was written in her own bloodline.

*You never told me,* Lena said aloud, and her voice cracked on the vault's cold air. *You could have left a letter. One letter. My name on the outside of it.*

But even as she said it, she understood. A letter could be found. A letter could be seized — by Corvane, by creditors, by any god waking hungry. The only safe place for the truth was inside the machinery of the lie, buried so deep in the ledgers' architecture that only a scribe stubborn enough to audit her own damnation would ever dig it out. Mirei hadn't hidden the truth from her. Mirei had hidden it *for* her, the way you bury a seed: under dirt, in darkness, trusting the growing thing to find its own way up.

Gratitude came then, and it broke her open. She cried the way she hadn't since she was small — ugly, silent, doubled over the founding ledger with her tears spotting the margin of the covenant page, and some distant professional corner of her mind wincing at the damage to the paper while the rest of her let the wince go hang. She cried for the grandmother she would never meet. She cried for the spring she'd been promised freedom in, and the sixty years the promise had waited in the dark. She cried because *let her be born free* was the kindest sentence anyone had ever written, and it had been written about her, and she was the only person alive who knew.

When it was done, she wiped her face on her sleeve, the way you do when the work isn't finished, and looked down at the page again with clear eyes.

And saw what she had missed.

* * *

Beneath the final line, in ink so faint it might have been breath given substance, one more passage — not part of the covenant entry but tucked against its foot like a postscript whispered too quietly for strangers:

*If you are reading this, then you dug, little one. Good. Now listen, because I have strength for only one instruction. The covenant holds while the Door holds. The Door holds while the garden sleeps. But the gods are stirring — I feel them under the soil even as I write — and when they wake fully, every covenant in this book will be tested. Yours most of all, because yours is the newest and the boldest. If that day comes, do not run from the garden. Go in. Find the grave at the center. What waits there is not what the guild believes. Bring nothing you are not willing to lose.*

*Your name is yours. Spend it wisely.*

*M.*

Lena read it three times. On the third reading, the lamp beside her dimmed — not guttered, not failed, but *dimmed*, deliberately, the way a person lowers their voice — and in the reduced light the faint ink seemed to darken, just slightly, as if the words were drawing closer to the surface of the page to make sure she'd gotten them.

The cold in the vault changed quality. It had been the cold of storage, of preservation. Now it was the cold of attention. Far down the aisle, past the chained shelf, something shifted — not a sound exactly, more a redistribution of silence, the way a room changes when someone enters it. The lamps in the far niches burned steady. Nothing moved. Everything listened.

Lena closed the founding ledger with the gentleness she reserved for the oldest documents, and held it against her chest, and stood.

*Bring nothing you are not willing to lose.*

Above her, through forty feet of stone and earth, the sealed garden waited behind its wall — the wall Corvane inspected every dusk with his hands in his pockets and his ambition showing through his face like a lamp through a shutter. The wall the guild pretended was a boundary and the ledgers implied was a lid. The wall that, according to a dead woman's postscript, was not a wall at all but a threshold, and the only direction left to walk.

She had one night's stolen hour left, a copied key, a covenant with her name woven into it, and a grandmother's single instruction pointing at the center of the buried gods' graveyard.

Lena Nakamura — hers, finally, irreversibly, *hers* — turned toward the stairs and began to climb, and behind her in the dark the founding ledger lay closed on its lectern, humming almost inaudibly, like soil remembering rain.
