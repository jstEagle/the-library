## 9. Rust and Roses

**Lena**

The wall behind the guildhall was older than the guildhall itself.

Lena had walked past it every working day for eleven years — a stretch of mortared stone, shoulder-high, furred with ivy that never quite died in winter. She had never once thought about what was on the other side. That was the trick of it, she understood now. The wall was not built to keep people out. It was built to be *uninteresting*, and it had done its work so well that even the guild's own scribes stepped around it without seeing it, the way you step around a stain you've stopped noticing.

Tonight she saw it. Tonight she could not stop seeing it.

"Here." Tomas crouched at the base of the wall where a drain grate had rusted half through, its iron bars flaking like burnt paper. He'd brought a pry bar from the delivery yard, tucked under his coat like something shameful. "Been loose since I was twelve. Used to hide in there when Corvane's predecessor wanted someone flogged."

"You hid inside the garden?"

"I hid *in the drain*. Never went past the second bend." He worked the bar into the gap between grate and stone. "There's a reason for that, probably. There's a reason for everything about this place, and none of them are good."

The grate came free with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw. Cold air breathed out of the dark beyond it — not cellar-cold, not sewer-cold. Something else. It smelled of turned earth and rain that hadn't fallen yet, and underneath both, faint as a memory of a memory, something sweet. Flowers, Lena thought. But no flowers grew in Vessany in the dead month of Vorst, when even the hothouses slept.

She looked at Tomas. He looked at her. Neither of them said the thing they were both thinking: that they could still turn back, that the vault was locked behind them now anyway, that Aunt Iyo's words were still ringing in both their ears — *the ledgers were prayer-books first* — and that a woman who has learned her whole life is a lie does not turn back at the first true thing she finds.

Lena went first. She always did. It was one of the few things about herself she had never doubted.

The drain ran eight feet, then opened into darkness that should have been the narrow strip of ground between the garden wall and the guildhall's rear kitchens — ten paces wide, maybe twelve, packed dirt and weeds. Instead the darkness went on. Her boots found grass. Wet, deep, real grass, heavy with dew that gleamed even without moonlight, because the blades themselves gave off a light like the last grey minute before dawn.

"Tomas," she said quietly.

"Yeah."

"How far apart are the kitchen walls?"

He came up beside her. She felt him go still. Behind them, the drain hole glowed faintly, a coin of city lamplight — and it was already far away. Too far. They had crawled eight feet.

"It's bigger inside," he said, in the flat voice he used for deliveries to houses he didn't want to enter. "It's bigger *inside*, Lena."

"Yes."

"That's not possible."

"No," she agreed, and started walking.

* * *

**Tomas**

Tomas had grown up in the Warrens, where the alleys were so tight you shook hands across them from opposite windows, where a courtyard was a luxury and a tree was a rumor from the river districts. He knew small places the way fish know water. He knew exactly how much world fit into how much space, and everything his body told him now was wrong.

The garden unrolled ahead of them like a bolt of black silk. Rows — hundreds of rows — stretching out under a sky that wasn't the sky over Vessany. No smoke-haze, no gaslight glow, no stars he recognized. Just a high soft dark, and hanging in it a moon that was full when the real moon tonight was a nail-paring. He kept checking. He couldn't stop checking. Every time he looked up, some animal part of him expected the sky to have noticed its mistake and corrected itself, and every time it hadn't.

"Lena." His voice came out smaller than he intended. "How long do you think we've been walking?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty."

"And how far would you say we've come?"

She didn't answer right away. Ahead of her, the first row of graves rose out of the grass like teeth.

They had seen them coming for a while — pale shapes in the pre-dawn glow, regular as fence posts. Now, close up, Tomas saw what they were, and his mouth went dry. Headstones. Simple ones, unhewn-looking, as if the earth had pushed them up itself rather than letting anyone carve them. Each one stood at the head of a long low mound, and each mound was covered — no, *crowned* — by a flowering plant in full bloom.

In Vorst. Under a false moon. In a country that had been dead for two hundred years.

He knelt beside the nearest grave because his legs decided it before he did. The flower growing there was a lily, or had been once; its petals were the white of old paper, veined with faint brown lines that might have been writing. He leaned closer without meaning to, the way you lean toward a whisper.

And heard it hum.

Not a sound, exactly. More like standing near a struck bell after the strike — a vibration in the air, in the soil, in his own breastbone. A note too low to hear and too present to ignore. The lily hummed, and the hum had a shape to it, and the shape was *someone*. Tomas had delivered petitions to shrines all his life; he knew the feel of a god's attention the way you know the feel of sun through a window. This was that, but folded up small, folded down into roots and petals, sleeping.

"They're buried here," Lena said behind him. Her voice had gone strange — reverent and furious at once, which was, he was learning, simply her voice lately. "Aunt Iyo said the gods fell with Vessany. She never said anyone *buried* them. Someone dug graves. Someone planted these."

"Someone tended them." Tomas touched the soil at the lily's stem. It was warm. In a night garden under a lying moon, the earth was warm as a sleeping animal. "Look at the next row. And the next. Someone's been keeping these beds for centuries."

They walked the rows. Tomas counted without wanting to — forty graves in the first row, forty in the second, and row after row beyond, diminishing into the glowing distance until he lost count somewhere past a thousand and stopped counting because the number had begun to feel like an accusation. A thousand gods. More. Every one asleep under its flower, humming its single held note, and the notes wove together overhead into something that was almost music, almost weather, almost a word being said very slowly by a very large throat.

Halfway down the fourth row, he found a grave with no flower. Just bare turned earth, freshly dark, and a headstone whose surface was smooth as an unwritten page.

He did not tell Lena about that one. Some things you keep in your pocket like a stone.

* * *

**Lena**

Lena had balanced entries for traded years. She had recorded the sale of a man's shadow, a woman's first love, a child's talent for whistling — line items, debits, credits, the clean arithmetic of things that should never have been priced. She had believed herself past surprise. The ledger had taught her that every horror, properly entered, becomes manageable.

The garden refused to be entered.

She tried, walking the rows, to do what she always did: categorize. Flower by flower, god by god, grave by grave. A rosebush whose blooms were the exact red of arterial blood, humming a note that made her think of her mother's hands, though her mother had been dead nine years and had never gardened. A bed of crocuses opening only at midnight-black, each petal threaded with silver. Something tall and pale like a foxglove that hummed so deeply she felt it in her fillings, in the bones of her skull. She catalogued four rows this way before she understood what she was doing — that she was trying to *file* the sacred, that the guild had trained her so thoroughly that even here, knee-deep in the burial ground of gods, her first instinct was bookkeeping.

She stopped. She took her hand off the imaginary pen. She let herself simply stand among the graves and feel it.

The hum was everywhere. It was in the ground and the air and the backs of her teeth. And it was not one hum. Each flower sang its sleeper's note, and the notes were different, and they were not discordant — they were *waiting*, the way an orchestra waits, every instrument tuned and silent and ready, and the silence between them was the most frightening part. Because silence, in an orchestra, is not emptiness. It is intention.

"They're not dead," she said aloud. Her voice sounded profane and tiny. "Everyone says the gods died with Vessany. They didn't. They were *planted*."

"Is there a difference?" Tomas asked. He had drifted a few graves down; he was looking at a patch of blue flowers she didn't have a name for, and his face wore an expression she'd never seen on him — wonder with the skin flayed off it, wonder that hurt.

"When a thing dies, it stops." Lena knelt and pressed her palm flat to the warm earth of the blood-red rose's mound. The hum climbed her arm like water climbing a wick. "This hasn't stopped. It's *held*. Someone put them here and held them, the way you hold your breath, the way you hold a door—" She stopped. The words arrived all at once, the way the important ones always did. "The way you hold a door shut. Or hold one open."

"The Door of Ada."

"Aunt Iyo said it opens only for someone named in the founding ledger." Lena stood, wiping earth from her palm, and looked down the endless rows, toward whatever lay at the center of a garden that was larger than the building that contained it. "Two thousand graves, Tomas. Maybe more. If Ada is buried here, she'll be at the heart of it. Things like this always have a heart."

"Things like this usually have teeth, too."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

He huffed — almost a laugh, the first sound either of them had made in an hour that wasn't a whisper — and fell into step beside her. They walked the rows together toward the center, and the hum thickened around them like honey, like incense, like the moment before a name is spoken.

* * *

**Tomas**

Somewhere around the two hundredth row — Tomas had given up counting precisely and now estimated in the way sailors estimate distance, by ache and stubbornness — the flowers began to change.

The outer rows had been wild things: roses, lilies, crocuses, blooms he knew from market stalls and funeral wreaths, however wrong their colors and seasons. These were different. These were flowers he had no words for. One grew in a perfect spiral, its petals arranged like the chambers of a shell, and when Tomas passed it, he briefly remembered a summer that had never happened to him — a lake, a wooden boat, a girl laughing — and then the memory slid out of him again like water through fingers, leaving only the certainty that he had just been lent something and it had been called back.

"Don't touch anything," Lena said, without turning around. She had felt it too. He could tell by the set of her shoulders.

"I didn't touch it. It touched *me*."

"Then walk faster."

But it was hard to walk fast. The garden pulled at him gently, the way sleep pulls, the way a warm bed pulls on a cold morning. Every flower offered something. Not demanded — *offered*, which was worse. A nodding white bloom shaped like a bell whispered a name he'd been called as a boy by a grandmother who'd sold her voice to pay rent. A cluster of dark purple globes hummed the smell of bread baking, actual smell, rising off the petals, and his stomach clenched with a hunger that had nothing to do with food. The whole garden was a marketplace of the impossible, and every stall was open, and nothing had a price tag, and Tomas had spent his whole life in a city where nothing was ever free, and he knew — he *knew* — what that meant.

"They're dreaming," he said. "That's what the hum is. They're asleep and dreaming, and the dreams leak."

Lena glanced back at him. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. You never listen." But his joke came out thin, because ahead of them the rows were converging, the way streets converge on a plaza, and the hum was gathering itself toward a single point, and at the center of the garden — visible now, a pale shape against the false moon — stood something that was not a grave marker.

It was a frame. Tall as a gallows, wrought of black metal gone green with age, standing alone on a circle of bare silvered earth. Rust wept from its joints in long orange streaks, and where the rust met the grass, roses grew — dozens of them, climbing the iron legs, blooming straight out of the corrosion, red blooms on rusted arms like a thing being born and buried at once.

No gate hung in the frame. No door. Only empty air, and the strongest hum of all, and — Tomas squinted, refusing to believe his eyes, believing them anyway — a faint reflection in that empty air, as if the space inside the frame were polished glass waiting for someone to breathe on it.

"The Door of Ada," Lena breathed.

"Yeah," Tomas said. "And Lena? Look at the roses."

She looked. So did he. And the roses on the rusted frame, all of them, every bloom, had turned — slowly, silently, the way sunflowers follow the sun — to face them.

* * *

**Lena**

The roses watched them come.

Lena told herself it was wind. There was no wind. She told herself it was the hum moving the stems. The hum did not move; it gathered. By the time they reached the circle of silvered earth, every flower in the last hundred rows had gone silent — not faded, not wilted, but *hushed*, the way a congregation hushes — and the only note left in the whole vast garden was the one rising from the empty door, deep and patient, a held breath two centuries long.

At the foot of the frame lay a grave.

It was the only grave they'd seen with a proper stone — cut marble, old, its inscription worn to shallow ghosts of letters. Lena knelt and brushed away the moss with her sleeve, and her heart performed a slow, terrible recognition, because she could read the worn letters the way she read everything, the way she had been trained to since childhood, tracing grooves with her fingertip the way she traced ledger lines:

*ADA — WHO WAS THE COUNTRY AND WENT AHEAD OF IT*

Below, in smaller letters mostly eaten by time, a final line:

*SHE IS NOT UNDER THIS STONE.*

"No," Lena whispered. "She isn't."

Because the grave was empty. Not robbed-empty, not collapsed-empty. Empty the way a bed is empty when the sleeper has risen. The earth inside the low border was smooth and undisturbed and bore, impossibly, a single object: a mirror, oval, its glass clouded like breath on a window, lying face-up in the soil as if it had grown there.

Lena reached for it and stopped, her hand trembling an inch above the glass. Every lesson of the guild said *do not sign what you have not read*. Every instinct of the last three days said the same.

Behind her, Tomas made a small sound. "Lena. The roses."

She looked up. The climbing roses on the doorframe had finished turning. All their blooms faced her now, and in the center of each red flower, where the heart of a rose should be, there was a small dark opening — not a defect, not a rot, but a deliberate shape, an aperture, like a pupil. Like an eye. And they were not hostile. That was the worst of it. They regarded her with the drowsy, ancient patience of something that has waited a very long time and can wait longer, and the hum of the empty door bent around her like a sentence awaiting its verb.

"She became the door," Lena said. She hardly recognized her own voice. "Iyo said it opens for someone named in the founding ledger. She never said who the door was."

Tomas's hand closed on her shoulder — solid, warm, terrified, loyal. "You don't have to."

"I know." She looked at the mirror in the empty grave, at the clouded glass, at the faint shape gathering in it that might have been her own reflection and might have been something wearing her reflection like a borrowed coat. "That's what makes it a choice."

The mirror cleared, one slow circle at a time, the way frost leaves a pane.

And in the glass, the reflection smiled before she did.

* * *

**Tomas**

Tomas had one job, self-appointed, non-negotiable: stand between Lena and the door if anything moved wrong.

Something was moving wrong. Everything was moving wrong. The roses had eyes. The mirror had a smile that didn't match the woman above it. The hum had become a voice-shaped pressure, and though no words had formed yet, Tomas understood with total clarity that the garden had noticed them — had perhaps *only now* noticed them, after two hundred years of tending its sleeping thousands — and that everything that followed would happen quickly.

"Lena," he said. "Whatever it offers — and it will offer, everything in this place offers — remember the shrine. Remember what the little god wanted from me."

"I remember." Her hand hovered over the mirror. "But Tomas — the little god was collecting. Look at this place." She swept her arm at the silent rows, the thousand-fold graves, the patient watching blooms. "Nobody's collected here. Somebody *paid*. For centuries. Tended every bed, kept every root warm, held the door." Her voice cracked on the last word, and he saw it plainly: she wasn't afraid of the mirror. She was afraid of how much she wanted what it was offering.

The reflection raised its hand. Lena's hand, above the glass, stayed still.

The reflection spoke, and its voice was the hum given words at last, and it used Lena's mouth-shapes, Lena's timbre, and it said:

"*You're late, little scribe. But you're here. Show me your name.*"

Tomas's blood went cold, because he knew — everyone in the guild knew — that in the old trade, in the oldest trade, the one written about in the sealed ledgers, *showing your name* was the first step of surrendering it.

Lena's fingers curled, hovering over the glass. And Tomas, runner, friend, keeper of the one job he'd given himself, stepped forward onto the silvered earth and said, loud enough to shake the roses:

"Her name's not for showing. It's hers. Ask for something else."

Every eye in every rose turned to him at once.

And the reflection in the mirror laughed — softly, warmly, terribly — and said, "*Good. Bring me the honest one instead.*"
