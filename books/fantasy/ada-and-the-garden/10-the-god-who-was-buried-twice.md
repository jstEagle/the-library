## 10. The God Who Was Buried Twice

The paths through the garden did not behave like paths.

Lena had noticed it first an hour after the wall, when the row of graves they'd been following should have ended at the guildhall's rear foundation and instead curved gently inward, toward a center that seemed to recede as they approached. Tomas had marked their route with chalk on the low stones, the way runners marked delivery routes through unfamiliar districts, and twice now they had passed his own chalk marks — fresh, unsmudged, pointing in a direction they had already walked.

"We're going in circles," he said for the third time. He had stopped pretending to be calm somewhere around the second circle. His voice had the thin, bright quality of a man whistling past something enormous.

"No." Lena crouched at the next marker and studied it. The chalk was hers — she'd taken to carrying a stub of it since the vault, since she'd learned what ink could do when you meant it — and beneath her mark, half worn away, was another mark in a different hand. Older. A spiral, small as a thumbnail, drawn in something that had once been white and was now the color of old bone. "We're being read."

"That's worse."

"It might be better." She stood, brushing dirt from her knees. "Circles are how a book holds its place. Whoever tends this garden wants us to find the middle. It just doesn't want us to arrive before we understand why."

Tomas looked at her the way he always looked at her when she said things like this — the look of a boy who had grown up memorizing street names and delivery windows and had married, somehow, into a world where a woman could talk about books holding places. "And do we understand?"

She thought of Aunt Iyo's hands shaking around the teacup. *The ledgers were prayer-books first.* She thought of the founding page, her grandmother's cramped, fierce script: *Let no one claim her outright.* She thought of the mirror she hadn't seen yet but had been dreaming about for three nights, ever since the soil from Ada's grave had first whispered against her fingers.

"No," Lena said. "But we're close enough that it doesn't matter."

They walked on. The graves thinned as they went — not in number but in density, each stone given more room, more silence, until the flowers growing from them were no longer flowers you could name. One grave grew a stem of glass that chimed in a wind Lena couldn't feel. Another grew a slow coil of smoke that smelled, impossibly, of bread baking in a house that had been gone for six hundred years. At each one, Lena felt the same faint pressure behind her eyes that she felt when reading a very old document — the sense of a voice waiting to be transcribed.

Tomas kept his hands in his pockets and his eyes forward. He had touched nothing since the rose that hummed. She didn't blame him.

The last stretch of path opened without warning into a clearing, and the clearing held a single grave.

It was larger than the others — not grand, exactly, but *settled*, the way a chair is settled by someone who has sat in it for a long time. The headstone was plain granite, unmarked by any chisel, and yet words moved across its surface like fish under ice, surfacing and diving before Lena could read them. No flower grew from it. Instead, set into the earth where the grave's chest would be, level with the ground, framed by a border of black soil so dark it looked cut rather than grown—

A mirror.

Full-length, though there was no frame and no glassmaker's seam. It simply lay in the earth like water that had decided to stand still, reflecting the grey morning sky and the two of them standing over it, and reflecting them *wrong* — or right, Lena couldn't decide — because in the reflection the garden behind them was not the garden around them. In the mirror, the sky was gold. The graves bloomed. And the two figures standing at the edge were dressed in clothes she almost recognized: scribe's robes, deep green, embroidered at the hem with a spiral.

"Ada," Lena said. Not a question. The name came out of her the way names came out of ledgers — because the entry demanded it.

Her reflection smiled.

Not Lena's smile. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that made her step back and feel Tomas's hand close hard on her arm: the smile belonged to the face wearing it, but it did not belong to *her*. It was older. It was patient in a way no living face is patient. It was the smile of someone who has waited so long that waiting has become a kind of architecture, and you have just walked into one of its rooms.

"You're late," the reflection said. Its voice came not from the mirror but from everywhere — from the soil, from the moving words on the granite, from the space just behind Lena's own ears. "Sixty years, give or take. Your grandmother was punctual. I expected the blood to tell."

Lena's mouth was dry. "You knew my grandmother."

"I buried her." The reflection tilted its head, and the gesture was wrong too, graceful in a way that made Lena's teeth ache, like watching a door swing on a hinge that shouldn't exist. "Well. I wrote her burial. There's a difference. Ask me sometime about the difference between writing a thing and doing a thing; it's the whole history of my country."

"Your country doesn't exist," Tomas said. His voice cracked on *exist*, but he said it.

The reflection turned its attention to him, and Tomas went pale and sat down abruptly in the grass, which Lena suspected was the correct response. "No," Ada agreed, gently. "That was rather the point. Sit, runner. You've carried enough today. Both of you have carried more than anyone should carry, and you've carried it honestly, which is rarer than you know."

Lena forced herself to step closer to the mirror. Her reflection — Ada's reflection, Ada wearing her — stepped closer too, and this time, up close, she could see that the gold-lit garden in the glass was not a memory. It was *current*. Flowers swayed in a wind that was blowing *now*, somewhere, in some Vessany that had never finished existing.

"They told me you died," Lena said. "The fall of Vessany. The gods were killed or buried. Corvane's records—"

"Corvane's records." Ada laughed, and the sound made every grave-flower in the clearing turn toward the mirror at once, a thousand stems bending like a congregation. "Yes. Corvane reads his ledgers the way a thief reads a house: looking for what can be taken. Let me tell you what actually happened, scribe, and then let me tell you what I want, because I have been lying in this ground for six hundred years composing the request, and I would like finally to make it."

The moving words on the granite stilled. For one moment they resolved into legibility — and Lena read them, and her breath left her.

*Here lies Ada, who was not buried here.*

"The gods of Vessany were dying," Ada said. "Not killed — dying, the way a language dies when its last speaker falls asleep mid-word. We were made of belief, and the belief was leaving, draining south into countries with better armies and worse memories. So we gathered in this garden, the ones of us who remained, and we lay down in the soil like coins in a miser's hand, and we waited for the belief to come back." A pause. "It hasn't come back. But something else has. Something that prays *to us* now instead of *through* us, and that is a different food entirely, and it is making my brothers and sisters wake up hungry. You've seen it. The beggar's daughter. The shrine that took your friend's childhood as toll. They are waking up as creditors, Lena Nakamura, because the only model of divinity this city has left is a ledger house."

Lena thought of the senior guild members aging overnight. Of Corvane in his tower, planning rites. "And you? Did you wake up hungry?"

"I never slept." The reflection raised a hand — Lena's hand, with her ink-stained calluses — and pressed it flat against the inside of the glass. Lena, before she knew she was doing it, pressed hers against the outside. Between their palms: coolness, like a window in winter. "I was the youngest of the gods and the least believed in. My domain was doors. Thresholds. The moment of passing between. Do you understand what that means, when your country is falling? Everyone was praying to the war gods to stop the armies and the harvest gods to save the fields, and no one — not one soul in all of Vessany — thought to pray to the god of *leaving well*."

Her voice did not break. Gods' voices don't break. But something in it bent, the way light bends through water.

"So while my siblings lay down to sleep, I stood up and walked to the border of the country, and I did the only prayer anyone ever offered me. I became the door. The last gate out of Vessany. I folded myself into the shape of a threshold and I hid myself here, in the one place the guild would guard forever without knowing what it guarded, and I have been open and shut at once, waiting, for six hundred years. Waiting for the country to need leaving again." The golden eyes — Lena's eyes, lit from within — fixed on her. "Waiting for a scribe honest enough to open me."

The word landed in Lena's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it echo all the way down. *Honest.*

"I'm not honest," she said. "I falsified an entry when I was nineteen. I've skimmed from the petty cash of traded years. I helped breach your wall with a bribed gatekeeper and a stolen key. I am currently conspiring against my own guildmaster, which I'd do again, but let's not pretend it's honesty. It's survival with better posture."

The reflection smiled again — and this time it was, unmistakably, Lena's own smile, turned back on her like a lesson.

"Yes," Ada said. "All of that. I know. I've read your ledger."

"My—" Cold walked up Lena's spine. "My ledger isn't yours to read."

"Every ledger in Vessany is mine to read. I am the door every entry passes through, in or out. And I will tell you what your ledger says, since you seem determined to disqualify yourself: it says a woman who found her own name sold before her birth, and did not burn the books. It says a woman who learned her grandmother hid her rather than sold her, and grieved *for the grandmother* instead of raging at the inheritance. It says a woman standing in front of a god offering her everything she wants, listing her sins aloud rather than letting me flatter her past them." Ada's palm stayed pressed to the glass. "Honesty isn't cleanliness, child. Honesty is accuracy. You are the most accurate person I have met in six hundred years. That is the whole qualification. That is the entire door."

Tomas, from the grass, in a small voice: "What does she get? If she opens you. What does it cost?"

"There it is," Ada said softly. "The runner asks the real question. Yes. There is a cost, and I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not tell it to you secondhand, Lena Nakamura. You will learn it when you're ready to weigh it — not before, because if I name it now you'll spend every hour between now and the choosing rehearsing your refusal or your sacrifice, and both would ruin the arithmetic. Some entries can only be read once."

"That's monstrous," Lena said.

"That's a contract," Ada said. "You work for a ledger house. You knew that when you walked in."

Lena laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her, that seemed to surprise even the garden; the glass-flower grave chimed once, like applause from a cautious audience. She took her hand off the mirror. The absence of the cold was strange, like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"Corvane is coming," she said. "He has the covenant page, he has my contract, and he's begun a rite to bind your sleeping brothers and sisters into his books and pour what's left into himself. When he takes their power, the debts come due all at once — every unpaid prayer, every traded name, six centuries of interest. It will eat him alive. But it will also wake every god in this garden at the moment of his coronation, hungry, in the middle of a city full of people whose names are collateral." She swallowed. "If I'm going to open you, it has to be before he finishes. Possibly during. Possibly in the middle of a riot."

"Riots open doors beautifully," Ada said. "Doors are mostly riots anyway, if you think about it — one side pressing in, one side pressing out, and the hinge deciding the future." The golden light in the reflection dimmed slightly, banking itself like a fire settling in for work. "Go back through the garden, scribe. The paths will let you leave quickly now; you've been read, and you've been found accurate. Gather what you'll need. And Lena —"

The reflection leaned close, and for a moment it was only Lena's face in the glass, her own grey morning sky above it, her own frightened eyes.

"—when you come back to me, bring your own pen. Not the guild's. Not your grandmother's. Yours. The door opens for the hand that signs, and I would very much like to be opened by *you*."

Lena stepped back. The words on the granite resumed their swimming. Behind her, Tomas scrambled up, and together they turned toward the winding paths — and the paths, true to the promise, unspooled ahead of them like ribbon, straight and eager, drawing them back toward the wall and the city and the tower where a man was preparing to become a god.

At the edge of the clearing, Lena paused. She didn't look back at the mirror. She looked at her own right hand, at the ink stains worked so deep into the skin they looked less like marks than like grain in wood.

"Honesty is accuracy," she murmured.

Behind her, faint as a flower turning, the garden hummed agreement.

Then the paths closed behind them like a book whose place had been kept, and somewhere across the city, in a tower above a hall full of ledgers, Corvane Ash began to sing.
