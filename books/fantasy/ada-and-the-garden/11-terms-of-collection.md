## 11. Terms of Collection

Corvane Ash woke to the sound of screaming and knew, before he opened his eyes, that it had begun.

He had slept in his office again — a habit he despised in other men and permitted himself only because the guildhall's upper floors were the safest rooms in Vessany, warded by contracts older than the city's stones. The scream came up through the floorboards, through the marble, through three hundred years of accumulated paper, and it was a sound he recognized from his grandfather's accounts: the sound of an account being settled.

He dressed without hurry. His hands were steady. He noted this with professional satisfaction, the way he might note a favorable exchange rate. Fear was a currency too, and he did not intend to spend any of it.

The corridor outside his office smelled of lilies.

That was wrong. That was *deeply* wrong. There were no flowers in the guildhall; Corvane had them banned decades ago, along with birdsong and mirrors, on the principle that anything which grew or sang or reflected belonged to the old country, and the old country was dead. He had signed the ban himself. He remembered the ink drying.

But the corridor smelled of lilies anyway, thick and funereal, and beneath it ran something sweeter — roses, perhaps, or the memory of roses — and Corvane walked through it with his chin lifted as though the scent were a tribute being paid to him personally.

They were gathered at the top of the grand staircase. Scribes in their grey coats, runners still in their nightclothes, a cluster of junior clerks pressed against the balustrade. They parted for him the way water parts for a keel, and he saw what they were looking at.

Master of Coin Aldous Vey lay on the landing.

Corvane had last seen Vey two days ago: sixty-one years old, silver at the temples, a man whose face carried its age the way a well-cut coat carries its price. The thing on the landing wore Vey's nightshirt and Vey's signet ring, but the face was a riverbed after drought — skin gone to parchment, drawn down over the skull, hair white as spilled salt. Ninety years had been added to him between one midnight and the next. Perhaps more. It was difficult to count the rings on a tree that had been felled.

"He was coming up to your office," whispered a clerk Corvane did not know by name. "He said — he said he heard his name being called. From under the ground."

"Has anyone sent for a physician?" Corvane asked mildly.

"Three," said the clerk. "None will come. They say—" The boy swallowed. "They say the debt-houses are full. Half the moneylenders in the Salt Quarter aged forty years in the night. A bishop. Two judges. Anyone whose family ever took guild coin against a blessing."

*Anyone who owed,* Corvane thought. The gods had come back into their inheritance, and they were doing what gods had always done best: calling in what they were owed.

"Carry Master Vey to the infirmary," he said. "Gently. And no one speaks of this outside these walls. Not to family, not to priests, not to each other. Am I understood?"

They understood. They always understood. But as he descended past them, stepping carefully around the ruin of his Master of Coin, Corvane felt their eyes on his back like small cold coins pressed to his skin, and he understood something in return: fear spreads faster than any ledger can record it. By noon, all of Vessany would know. By evening, all of Vessany would be asking the same question the clerk had not dared ask aloud.

*If the gods are collecting from them — what do I owe?*

Corvane smiled at the thought, privately, the way a man smiles at a storm he has built a roof against.

*Nothing,* he intended to answer. *You owe nothing. Because I am going to pay it for you.*

* * *

The vault below the guildhall was the coldest room in the country, and Corvane liked it precisely for that reason. Cold kept ink honest. Heat made men sentimental, and sentiment, in his long experience, was simply bad bookkeeping wearing a kinder face.

He had the founding ledgers brought out onto the reading table — all nine of them, chained at the corners, each one heavy enough to crack a man's foot if dropped. Around them he arranged the working documents: the debt registries, the covenant abstracts, the genealogies of every family that had ever traded with the guild. And at the center, alone on a stand of black oak, the page he had taken from Lena Nakamura's desk.

Her grandmother's page. The covenant page.

It resisted him even now. He could feel it the way one feels a stone in a shoe — a small persistent wrongness. Ink should sit where it was put. This ink moved. He had watched it twice in the night, in the lamplight, letters rearranging themselves with the slow patience of insects. Whatever the old woman had written here, she had written it with something more than skill. She had written it with authority.

*No matter.* Authority could be seized. That was practically the guild's founding principle.

His senior staff assembled around the table at dawn: Undermaster Pell, dry as August; Mistress Ondine from Records, her hands trembling so badly she clasped them behind her back; young Harrow from Enforcement, who kept glancing at the door as if expecting the ground itself to come collect him. Good. Let them be afraid. Fear made subordinates attentive.

"The situation," Corvane began, "is an opportunity."

Pell made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a prayer. Corvane continued.

"For six centuries, this guild has held the spiritual debts of Vessany — every vow sworn at our altars when we were priests, every blessing sold, every name borrowed against future payment. We recorded those debts. We did not pay them. We assumed we never would have to, because the creditor was dead." He laid one hand flat on the oldest ledger. "The creditor is not dead. The creditor has woken up, reviewed its accounts, and begun collections. Vey is merely the first notice. There will be others. There may be many others."

"And you call this an opportunity?" Ondine's voice cracked on the word.

"I call it the greatest transfer of power in the history of this country." Corvane let the words hang, let them work on the room the way yeast works on dough. "Think. Every god in that garden holds a claim against thousands of souls — claims measured in years, in memories, in names. Those claims are assets. Enormous ones. Currently they are being collected piecemeal, at random, by whatever god stumbles across whichever debtor. Wasteful. Chaotic. Beneath us." He turned the covenant page toward them. "But a debt can be transferred. Any scribe worth her salt knows that. If the claims against ten thousand souls were consolidated — bound into a single instrument, assigned to a single obligor—"

"You," said Harrow.

"The guild," Corvane corrected smoothly. "And the guild has a head. Yes. Me."

Ondine stepped back from the table. "You're talking about taking the debts of the entire city onto yourself. No one could survive that. The collection would—"

"Kill me? If I took the debts as they are, yes. Naked obligation, unsecured, payable on demand." He smiled. "But debts can also be *discharged*, Mistress Ondine. Paid down. Settled. And there is one form of payment the old gods value above all others, one asset in this building that appreciates rather than depreciates." He tapped the covenant page. "Names. True names, freely given, entered in a founding hand. The garden wants its country back. The Door of Ada is the way. Whoever controls the door controls the terms of settlement — and whoever settles the gods' accounts becomes, by the oldest law of Vessany, their steward."

He had read that law himself, years ago, in a marginal note in the third founding ledger, in handwriting so faint he had needed a lens to find it. *Whoso pays the gods' arrears shall hold their trust.* He had thought it a curiosity then. Now it was a ladder, and he intended to climb it rung by rung until he stood above the garden itself.

"You would become a god," Pell said. Not accusing. Almost reverent.

"I would become *solvent*," Corvane said. "Divinity is merely solvency at scale."

* * *

The problem — and there was always a problem; problems were the interest paid on ambition — was the girl.

Lena Nakamura sat in the scriptorium when he came up from the vault, exactly where he had left her, exactly where he wanted her: copying forbidden pages under supervision, her pen moving with that infuriating precision, her face arranged into the blankness of a woman biding her time. She had accepted his bargain too easily. He had told himself it was terror. He was beginning to suspect it was arithmetic.

She had found her own name in the collateral registries. Of course she had; he had made sure the trail was warm enough to follow. What he had not anticipated — what his informants had failed to report until it was nearly too late — was that she had then gone looking for Aunt Iyo, and that Aunt Iyo, that treacherous dried-up relic, had talked.

Corvane stood in the doorway of the scriptorium and watched her work for a long moment. She was good. That was the trouble with her. In thirty years of running this guild he had employed hundreds of scribes, and he could count on one hand the ones whose pen he had actually feared. Her grandmother had been one. The girl was another. Give her ten years and she would be dangerous. Give her the founding ledgers and she would be fatal.

She looked up. Met his eyes. Did not rise.

"Master Ash."

"Nakamura. How goes the copying?"

"As ordered." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Though some pages resist me. The covenant page especially. It doesn't seem to want to be copied."

"It doesn't want to be *understood*," Corvane said. "There's a difference. Continue."

He crossed to her desk and set down a single sheet — a summons, freshly sealed, listing a hearing before the full guild council: charges of unauthorized access to restricted archives, theft of documents, conspiracy with a retired scribe. He watched her read it. Watched the pulse jump once in her throat and go still. Good control. Better than most.

"This is dated tomorrow," she said.

"Yes."

"I haven't done anything yet."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But you will. You've been to see Iyo. You know about the Door. You know about the founding ledger. And tomorrow morning you were going to go back into my vault and take what isn't yours." He leaned down, close enough to smell the iron-and-oak scent of the grave-ink she'd been using. "I'm not angry, Nakamura. Ambition in a scribe is like moisture in ink — necessary. I'm simply informing you of the weather."

"If you report me, you lose me. You need a scribe who can read the founding hand. You said so yourself."

"I need a scribe who can read the founding hand *and nothing else*." He straightened. "So here is the amendment to our arrangement. You will transcribe the covenant page for me — all of it, including whatever parts rewrite themselves while you watch. You will identify every name bound into the Door's covenant. And when the time comes, you will stand beside me in the garden and write what I tell you to write. In exchange, your hearing is cancelled, your contract is honored, and your name stays exactly where it is: safely mine."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow's hearing proceeds, you're convicted, and I sell your contract to settle the guild's most urgent arrears." He smiled at her, warmly, the smile he used for clients signing ruinous terms. "There are gods waking up in that garden with very large appetites, Nakamura, and your grandmother left you pre-packaged. Do you understand how rare that is? A soul already bound, already documented, already *delivered*? You're not a person to them. You're a convenience."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear, he realized. Calculation. She was pricing him, the way she priced everything, and he found, to his irritation, that he could not read her total.

"All right," she said quietly. "I'll transcribe the page."

"Wise."

"But I'll need the original. Copies lose the living ink. Even you must know that."

He considered refusing. Then he considered the alternative — her fumbling at second-hand text, missing the nuances that might matter when ten thousand debts hung in the balance — and nodded once. "Tomorrow. After the hearing is cancelled. You'll work in the vault, under guard."

"Of course," she said, and bent back over her copying, and Corvane walked out of the scriptorium feeling the pleasant hum of a transaction closed.

It was only much later — standing at his window as dusk came down over the city, watching smoke rise from districts where more collectors had visited in the night — that he replayed the conversation and noticed what she had not asked.

She had not asked what would happen to the debtors' names once he took them.

A careful scribe would have asked. A frightened scribe would have begged. She had simply agreed, quickly, cleanly, and requested access to the one document in the world that could open the Door of Ada.

Corvane stood in the darkening glass, his reflection ghosted over the burning city, and for the first time in thirty years he felt the specific chill of a man who suspects he has been outbid.

Below, in the garden, the humming had grown louder. He could feel it through the floor, through his boots, through the soles of his ambition: the old gods turning in their sleep like creditors rolling over toward the alarm clock.

Let them wake, he thought. Let them come with their ledgers and their appetites. Tomorrow he would have the covenant page in his hands, and the day after, the names of every debtor in Vessany bound into a single instrument with a single signature at the bottom.

He dipped his pen. He began to draft the consolidation writ, and the ink went down black and obedient, and if the page trembled slightly under his hand — if somewhere far beneath the guildhall something vast rolled over and *smiled* — Corvane Ash chose not to record it.

Some entries, he told himself, are better left unwritten.

In the scriptorium below, by the light of a single lamp, Lena Nakamura was writing too.
