## 13. The Runners' Riot

Tomas had carried messages through plague quarantines, through the Night of Broken Bells, through a tax season so vicious that three scribes in the Salt Ward faked their own deaths to escape it. He knew what a city sounded like when it was about to turn. It was not loud. That was the thing nobody understood. A city on the edge of riot went quiet first — a held-breath quiet, a listening quiet — and then it made one sound, all at once, like a bone setting.

He heard that sound from the roof of the guildhall's courier annex, where he'd gone to escape the smell of the counting room and eat his lunch in peace.

Below him, the Processional was full of people who were not walking anywhere. They were standing still, in clusters, all facing the same direction: toward the shrine of the Small Mother, where an hour ago a woman named Odile Ferrant had knelt down with her hands pressed to the cobbles and begun to weep. Not ordinary weeping. Tomas had seen her come out of the shrine himself, seen her face, and the face was wrong — young. Odile Ferrant was sixty-one years old and had hands like tree bark, and when she stood up from that shrine her hands were a girl's hands, smooth and pink, and she could not stop looking at them, and neither could anyone else.

The god inside the shrine had answered her prayer. Everyone knew it by now. The gods were answering again, after two hundred years of silence, and for three days the city had behaved like a drunk given back his wages — giddy, reckless, spending prayers it couldn't afford.

Tomas bit into his bread and watched the clusters below begin to move toward each other, the way water finds water.

* * *

It started going wrong at the fourth bell.

He was back in the annex, sorting the afternoon runs, when Pell came through the door gray-faced and moving too fast, the way people move when they've been running longer than their pride will admit.

"Tomas." Pell caught the doorframe. "They're collecting."

"Collecting what?"

"Debts." Pell's voice cracked on the word. "The gods. They're not just answering anymore. They're calling things in. You know old Master Ellery, third floor, contracts? He went to the shrine on Copper Lane this morning to pray for his gout. He came back—" Pell swallowed. "He came back ninety years old, Tomas. Older than ninety. His hair went white as chalk between the shrine and the corner. He's in the infirmary now and they say his heart is beating like a boy's but his body is done, it's just *done*, and he keeps saying he never signed anything, he never signed—"

"He didn't have to," said a voice from the sorting benches.

Everyone turned. It was Maret, the oldest runner in the annex, sixty if she was a day, who'd been carrying guild mail since before Tomas was born. She hadn't looked up from the letter she was folding. Her hands were steady. That frightened Tomas more than anything else that day.

"The ledgers," Maret said. "You think the guild trades in names and years and nobody pays? Somebody pays. We pay. Every runner who ever took a guild oath, every scribe who ever wet a pen — our names are in those books. Our grandmothers' names. Our children's names, probably, if any of us were fool enough to have children." She folded the letter with a small, precise crease. "I always wondered why the guild paid us in scrip instead of coin. Scrip you can only spend at the guildhouse. Coin you could leave with."

The room had gone very quiet. Outside, through the shutters, Tomas could hear the Processional again — that held-breath sound, deeper now, wider.

Pell grabbed his sleeve. "What do we do?"

And here was the thing about Tomas Vey, the thing Lena had once described as *either your best quality or the death of you*: he did not think about what to do. He thought about what Lena would do, and then he did the opposite of nothing.

"We go look," he said.

* * *

The ledger hall was open.

That should have been the first alarm. In eleven years of running for the Guild of Recorded Exchange, Tomas had never once seen the ledger hall doors stand open to anyone below the rank of senior scribe. Now they were flung wide, and the hall beyond was chaos — clerks hauling armloads of volumes off shelves, Corvane Ash's black-liveried guards directing them with flat voices, and everywhere, everywhere, the smell of ink, sharp enough to taste.

A crowd had gathered at the doors anyway. Runners, scribes, a few tally-clerks from the counting floor, pressing against an invisible line the guards were holding with crossed halberds and expressions of bored contempt.

"What's happening?" someone called. "Why are they moving the books?"

Nobody answered. Nobody official, anyway. But a junior clerk near the door — a girl Tomas half-recognized from the copying room, ink-stained to the elbow — turned around with a volume still clutched to her chest, and her face was the face of someone reading something she wished she hadn't.

"It's true," she said, not loudly, but in the silence it carried like a shout. "The personnel rolls. I file them every quarter. I never read them, I just — today I read them." She held up the book. "Every name has a second entry. Under *service*. There's a column called *encumbrance*. Do you know what encumbrance means?"

Silence.

"It means collateral," she said. "We're collateral. All of us. Our names are listed as assets against debts the guild took on before most of us were born. My name is worth—" her voice wobbled, steadied, "—my name is valued at four hundred crowns against a contract signed in the Year of the Drowned Bell. I wasn't born until six years ago. They sold my name before I existed."

The sound the crowd made then was not the bone-setting sound. It was quieter than that, and worse: a long collective exhale, like a building settling, like something deciding.

Tomas felt it in his chest. He thought of Lena — where was Lena, why wasn't she here, she'd know what page, what column, what to burn first — and he pushed forward through the crowd until he was beside the girl with the book.

"Show me mine," he said. "Tomas Vey. Runner, second class."

She flipped pages with shaking fingers, found the index, found his name, and read aloud: "*Encumbered. Value assessed at maturity.*" She frowned. "At maturity. That's odd phrasing. Most entries say *at signing* or *at collection*."

"At maturity," Maret repeated behind them, and her steady voice finally shook. "Like fruit. Like they're waiting for us to ripen."

The guards shifted. One of them rapped a halberd butt against the marble. "Clear the hall. By order of the guildmaster."

Nobody cleared the hall.

Instead, someone — Tomas never learned who, and he'd have bought them a drink for the rest of his life if he could have — began to laugh. Not merry laughter. The other kind. And then the crowd did the thing crowds do when fear gets too heavy to carry individually: they set it down together, and it became fury, and fury is light enough for anyone to lift.

The first guard went down under six bodies before his halberd finished falling.

* * *

Later — much later, when people asked him how a mob forms, because people always asked him, because he'd been at the front of this one — Tomas would try to explain that no one decided anything. That was the truth of it. There was no moment where the runners and scribes of the Guild of Recorded Exchange voted to riot. There was only a series of small, individual surrenders: a man picking up a stool; a woman tearing the guild badge off her shoulder and dropping it on the marble; the girl with the personnel rolls holding her ledger over her head like a torch she hadn't lit yet.

They poured out of the ledger hall into the Processional, and the Processional received them the way a river receives rain.

By dusk, half the city knew. Not because anyone announced it — because the gods were telling people. That was the part that made Tomas's skin crawl even in the middle of everything. He saw it happen three times in the space of an hour: some terrified citizen would grab a stranger and start reciting their own debt terms, word for word, in a voice not entirely their own, the way you recite something you memorized long ago and forgot you'd memorized. *Encumbered. Valued at signing. Payable upon the return of the creditors.* The gods weren't just collecting. They were publishing. Every secret in the guild's books was being read aloud in the streets by the mouths of the people it was written about.

A tanner on Cooper's Row tore his own shop apart looking for the contract he'd signed twenty years ago. A widow stood on her balcony reading her dead husband's name-value to the whole street while her neighbors listened with their hats in their hands. Someone set fire to the scrip office, and the burning money smelled, Tomas swore afterward, like incense.

And through all of it, the guild guards kept forming lines and the lines kept breaking, because half the guards were encumbered too, and they were reading their own names off the backs of their own hands.

* * *

Tomas found Maret sitting on the steps of the fountain in Sable Square, which was where the crowd had pooled after burning itself out of the Processional. She had a cloth pressed to a cut above her eye. Around her, maybe two hundred people sat or stood or paced — runners, scribes, tally-clerks, a handful of guards who'd taken off their livery, and more arriving every minute, drawn by the simple gravity of *people who know what happened to you*.

"They'll send soldiers next," Maret said when he sat down beside her. "Real ones. City watch, if Ash can buy them. Maybe worse."

"Then we shouldn't be sitting here."

"Where would you rather be, boy? Home?" She laughed, winced, touched the cut. "Our homes are in the books too. Everything we love has a line item next to it. There's nowhere to run *from* a ledger. You have to run *at* it."

Tomas looked out at the square. Torches were being lit — someone had organized that, someone always organizes torches — and in the flickering light he could see faces he knew: Pell, still gray; the girl from the copying room, whose name he'd finally learned was Sefa; old runners he'd shared bread with for a decade, young scribes he'd never spoken to. Two hundred people, and every one of them waiting for someone to tell them what they were for.

Fury without direction curdles. He'd seen it. He'd grown up in the Salt Ward watching mobs eat themselves.

"Lena," he said.

Maret looked at him. "The Nakamura girl? The head balancer?"

"She's been onto this for weeks. Longer than weeks. She knows the books better than Ash does — better than anyone. If there's a way through this, a real way, not just burning things, she'll have found it. Or she'll be the reason there is one." He stood up. "And I know where she'll be heading. The garden."

"The garden's sealed," Sefa said. "Has been for a century. Guards on the wall, wards on the gate—"

"The wards are failing," Tomas said. "The gods are coming up through the ground. You want proof? Look at what's happening to the senior guild members. Look at Master Ellery. Something under that garden is awake and hungry and it's eating the guild from the top down. Ash is in there right now trying to bottle it for himself — I'd bet my encumbered name on it." He raised his voice, not shouting, just letting it carry the way you learn to in the trade, the way that makes people lean in instead of cover their ears. "Listen! Listen, all of you!"

Two hundred faces turned. For a moment the square was silent except for the fountain and the far-off crackle of the scrip office.

"I'm a runner. Second class. Eleven years. I've carried every kind of message this city has, and tonight the message is this: they wrote us down like we were theirs. Before we were born, they priced us. But here's what I know about writing, because I've delivered enough of it — paper doesn't make a thing true. Paper just *keeps* it. And whatever's written can be rewritten." He pointed south, toward the guildhall, toward the wall behind it, toward the dark mass of the garden rising over the rooftops like a held breath. "There's a door in that garden. The Door of Ada. Last gate out of Vessany — yes, I know, Vessany isn't supposed to exist, well, neither are we, apparently, except on paper. Lena Nakamura can open it. I don't know how. I don't need to know how. I know *her*, and if anyone in this rotten counting house can turn their ledger against them, it's the woman who balances it."

Someone jeered — "One scribe against the guildmaster?" — and Tomas let it land, let the silence hold it, and answered quietly, which was somehow louder:

"One scribe with two hundred of us between her and Ash's guards. That's not a mob. That's a wall that walks."

Maret stood up slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and pulled the cloth from her brow. "I'm sixty-two years old," she announced to the square, "and I have been ripening in their books since I was nine. I am done maturing on schedule." She picked up her runner's satchel and slung it across her chest like a bandolier. "Who's coming to the garden?"

The square answered the way cities answer — not all at once, not unanimously, but with a sound like a tide changing its mind. Some stayed. Some slipped away into the dark, and Tomas let them go without hatred; fear was honest. But more rose than sat, and more followed than rose, and by the time the column moved out of Sable Square it was four hundred strong and growing at every cross street, runners at the front who knew every alley and shortcut in Vessany-that-wasn't, scribes behind them carrying whatever ledgers they'd managed to seize — evidence, ammunition, hostages of ink — and behind them everyone else, everyone whose name had a price tag on it, which was eventually, Tomas understood, going to be everyone.

* * *

They reached the guildhall district at moonrise and found the gates barred and the walls manned, and found, too, that the walls were manned badly — because the guards could hear the crowd chanting now, and the chant was not *burn it down* or *death to Ash*. The chant was names. Their own names, thousands of them, recited street by street, person by person, the way the gods had taught the city to recite them. *I am not an entry. I am not an entry. I am not an entry.*

Tomas stood at the head of the column in the wide empty avenue before the guild gates, and his legs were shaking, and he let them shake, because hiding it would have cost more than showing it.

Then a side door opened in the wall — a runner's door, postern-sized, the kind Tomas had slipped through a thousand times on late deliveries — and a figure stepped out into the torchlight with ink on her hands and exhaustion carved into her face and her chin set at the exact angle Tomas had followed into trouble perhaps a dozen times before.

Lena Nakamura looked at the crowd, and the crowd looked at Lena, and the chanting faltered into silence, because everyone could feel it: this was the hinge. This was the moment the fury became either a weapon or a fire that ate its holders.

Tomas walked to her through the hush, took her ink-stained hand, and raised it high.

"She's going to open the door," he told the city. "All we have to do is hold the wall."

Behind the guildhall, beneath the earth, something vast rolled over in its sleep — and every flower in the buried garden opened at once, though no one living saw it yet.
