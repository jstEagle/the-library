## 14. A Bargain With Teeth

Lena had rehearsed the offer forty times on the walk from Aunt Iyo's garret to the guildhall, and by the fortieth telling it sounded almost like something a rational person would say to Corvane Ash and live.

*You want the covenant page. I want my contract released. One page for one name.*

Simple. Clean. Balanced, as her ledgers would say. She had even written it out in her head in the guild's formal hand, columns aligned, debits against credits, so that when she spoke it would come out with the weight of an entry rather than the tremor of a plea.

The trouble was that Corvane Ash did not read entries. He wrote them.

She was announced at his study door at the ninth bell of morning, which she took as a good sign — he received petitioners at dawn when he meant to devour them, but mid-morning was for business. The study smelled of cedar oil and old vellum, and beyond its tall windows the garden wall rose gray and seamless, higher than she remembered it, though she supposed walls grew when you fed them secrets.

Corvane sat behind a desk bare except for a single sheet of paper, face down. He looked rested. That frightened her more than anything else could have, because everyone else in Vessany — everyone who still existed inside the country's shrinking borders — looked like they had been aging in their sleep.

"Master Nakamura." He gestured to the chair across from him without touching anything on the desk. "You've been busy."

"I balance books," Lena said. "It's what I'm for."

"It's what you were *for*." He let that sit between them like a coin on a table. "Sit."

She sat. The chair was lower than his, which she noted, catalogued, and refused to be moved by. In the guildhall's hierarchy of furniture she had spent eleven years learning exactly how much power lived in the height of a seat, and she made herself comfortable anyway, folding her hands, meeting his eyes.

"I'll come to the point," she said.

"Please. It saves us both the pretense that you came here for any reason other than the one you're about to name."

"The founding ledger has a page missing. A covenant page, hidden sixty years ago by a scribe named Mirei Nakamura. You've been looking for it since the gods woke. I know where it is."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not surprise; she'd expected anger or hunger, and got instead the terrible patience of a man confirming arithmetic. "Go on."

"In exchange, my contract is released. All of it. The debt my grandmother's signature carries, the collateral clause, every entry that names me. Struck from the book. Witnessed, sealed, done before I hand over the page."

"And where is this page?"

"Where I put it. Somewhere only I can reach. If anything happens to me, instructions go to three separate people, none of whom like you." This was true. Tomas was one of them. The other two were Aunt Iyo and a debt-collector named Sefa who owed Lena a favor older than either of their careers and hated Corvane with the clean heat of a man who had once been audited into ruin.

Corvane turned the paper on his desk over. It was a copy of her contract — her own handwriting staring back at her from the collateral line, because of course he'd had her copy it during the audit, because of course everything she had ever touched in that building had been quietly filed away as evidence.

"You understand my position," he said. "I have a country's worth of gods waking up under my floorboards, senior partners aging thirty years in a single night, and runners rioting at my gates because someone — not you, I assume, since you're here — told them what we've been trading in for six generations. And you come to me offering to trade a page of paper for a line in a book."

"Not a line. My name."

"Your name." He said it the way a butcher says *cut*. "Lena Nakamura. Do you know what your grandmother actually did, girl? Everyone tells you she hid you. Protected you. Wrote you into the Door's covenant like some saint tucking a child under a blanket." He leaned forward. "She *encumbered* you. She took the most valuable asset this guild ever held — a name written into the founding covenant of the last door out of Vessany — and she buried it in a page no one could find, so that no one could collect. Not the gods. Not the guild. Sixty years our books have carried a liability we couldn't touch, and now the debts are coming due and you walk in here asking me to forgive the one asset that might balance them."

"She wasn't an asset. Neither am I."

"Everything is an asset. That's the first lesson of the ledger, and you learned it better than anyone I've ever trained." He smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the most dangerous thing in the room. "That's why I offered you partnership. That's why I'm listening now, instead of sending for the vault-keepers. You're the only person in this building who understands that a bargain is just a story two people agree to stop arguing about."

Lena kept her hands still. Her heart was doing the work of a runner on a stair, but her hands were still, and that was the discipline that mattered.

"Then agree to the story," she said. "My name off the books. The page in your hands. You get your covenant, I get out of Vessany's arithmetic entirely. After today, whatever happens to the gods, to the guild, to you — none of it touches me. Isn't that worth one page?"

He considered her for a long moment. Outside, faintly, through the glass, she heard the garden humming. She had heard it for three days now whenever she passed the wall — a sound like a struck bowl that never quite finished ringing — and she saw Corvane hear it too, saw his jaw tighten around it the way a man tightens around a toothache he refuses to name.

"Struck from the book," he said at last. "Witnessed and sealed. Bring me the page."

"Here? Now? No. We do it properly. Your seal, a witness of my choosing, the release executed first, then I send for the page. It arrives within the hour."

"You think I'd release you on a promise of paper?"

"You think I'd hand you the covenant and trust your memory?" Lena stood. "We're both ledgermen, Master Ash. Don't insult the craft by pretending you'd honor an unwitnessed deal either."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. Then he laughed — short, genuine, worse than any threat — and pulled a fresh sheet toward himself.

"Very well. Sit down. You'll draft it; you write faster than my clerks and you won't dare make an error."

They drafted the instrument together across his desk, and it was the strangest hour of Lena's life: bargaining for her own freedom in the guild's formal hand, Corvane dictating clauses in a voice gone smooth and reasonable, both of them watching each other's pens the way duelists watch wrists. She wrote the release in full — *all obligations, encumbrances, and claims arising from the contract of Mirei Nakamura, dated [sixty years past], including all collateral interests in the person and name of Lena Nakamura, are hereby discharged, extinguished, and struck from the record of the Guild of Accounts, irrevocably, in perpetuity* — and Corvane read it twice and signed it, and pressed his seal into the wax, and the wax held the shape of the guild's sigil, scales over a closed door.

A witness was summoned: old Mistress Hale from the second-floor archive, half-deaf, incurious, perfect. She signed. Lena signed. The document went into a folio, and the folio went into Corvane's desk, and Lena felt something in her chest loosen that had been clenched since the night she found her own name rewriting itself under her pen.

"Now," Corvane said pleasantly, "the page."

"I'll send for it. As agreed — within the hour."

"Of course." He rose, crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, looking out at the garden wall. "You know, I've been thinking about your grandmother. Mirei Nakamura. I never met her — she left the guild before my time — but I've read everything she wrote. Beautiful hand. Precise. Economical. Never a wasted stroke." He turned his head slightly, so Lena caught his profile against the gray stone. "Do you know what I admire most about her? She understood that ink doesn't care who holds the pen. She hid her page, yes. But a hidden page is just a page that hasn't been found yet. Everything she did — everything *you're* doing right now — only works if the other side agrees to play fair."

Lena's stomach went cold, slowly, like water filling a boot.

"Which brings me to my confession," Corvane continued, still pleasant, still watching the wall. "I signed your release. Witnessed, sealed, executed. Every word true." He tapped the desk once. "But you drafted it, Master Nakamura. And you made one small error. Clause four. *Discharged, extinguished, and struck from the record of the Guild of Accounts.*" He finally turned to face her fully, and his smile was the smile of a man presenting a balanced column. "You said *struck from the record*. You didn't say *struck from the books*. The record is what we show auditors. The books are what we keep. I have struck you from the record, Lena. The book remembers you perfectly."

"That's not—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "That's bad faith. That's—"

"That's ownership of the ledger house." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the cruelest thing she had ever heard. "You cannot out-ledger a man who owns the ledger house, child. I tried to tell you with the partnership offer. I genuinely wanted you beside me. But you went to Aunt Iyo. You went digging in the founding ledger. You've been in my vault twice this month — don't look surprised, the locks report to me — and this morning a little bird told me you'd already copied the covenant page. So there's really no need to send for it at all."

The door opened behind her. Two vault-keepers, broad as wardrobes, faces like furniture.

"Search her," Corvane said.

They did, efficiently, impersonally, and Lena stood rigid while a stranger's hands found the folded page sewn into the lining of her coat — her grandmother's hand, sixty years patient, the covenant that had kept her unclaimed her whole life — and drew it out and carried it to the desk as if carrying a relic.

Corvane read it standing. His expression didn't change, which somehow made it worse; she wanted him to wince, to hunger, to feel *something*, and he gave her nothing but the flat attention of a man verifying a count.

"Yes," he said softly. "Yes, this will do beautifully." He looked up. "Take her down. The long vault — the one with the reading lamp. She likes ledgers. Let her keep company with them while I prepare the rite."

"Lena—" started Mistress Hale, half-rising, and Corvane glanced at her, and the old woman sat back down and became very interested in her own hands.

"Your release is real," Corvane told Lena as the vault-keepers took her arms. He held up the folio, almost tenderly. "On the record, you are free. Remember that. When the gods finish collecting and this country finishes burning, somewhere in an archive that will no longer exist, there is a document saying Lena Nakamura owes nothing to anyone." He smiled. "I keep my word, Master Nakamura. I simply decide which words count."

They marched her down the back stairs, past the sealed doors, past the humming that grew louder the deeper they went, until the long vault swallowed her — shelves of ledgers rising into darkness on every side, the smell of dust and grave-earth ink, a single lamp burning on a reading table as promised.

The lock turned behind her with a sound like a full stop.

Lena stood in the lamplight and let herself shake for exactly ten breaths — she counted them, because counting was the discipline that held — and then she stopped shaking and began, as she had always done, to take inventory.

Her coat lining: empty. Her sleeves: nothing but lint and a stub of pencil. Her hairpins, her boots, the false seam in her belt — stripped, stripped, stripped. They had taken everything writable.

Almost everything.

Because as she turned slowly in the lamplight, her eye caught the shelf nearest the table — the working shelf, the one where vault clerks left out the day's ledgers and the day's ink — and there sat an open pot of ink, black and faintly red at the meniscus, and she knew that color, knew that smell, earth and iron and something underneath it like a flower remembered rather than smelled.

Grave-soil ink. Ada's soil. The ink the oldest ledgers were written in, the ink that answered intent instead of authority.

Corvane had locked her in a room full of books and given her a pen.

Lena sat down at the reading table, drew the pot toward her, and picked up the nearest ledger — a vault inventory, decades old, listing in some dead clerk's careful hand: *Long Vault, outer door, iron, triple-locked, key held by the Guildmaster.*

She dipped the pen.

"Let's see whose words count," she whispered, and began to edit.
