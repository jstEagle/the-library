## 17. Coronation of Ash

The rite began at the hour when night is thinnest, and Corvane Ash stood at the center of the garden wearing the guild's oldest robe, which had been cut for a priest and never for a merchant, and felt — for the first time in forty years — that he was dressed correctly.

The graves had opened. Not all at once; that would have been vulgar. They opened the way ledgers open, one page after another, each row of flowers lifting its heads as though called by name. The hum he had heard beneath the soil all his life, so faint he'd once convinced himself it was water in the pipes, now rose into something with structure, with *syntax*. The gods were speaking. They had been speaking for sixty years, and no one had listened, and Corvane intended to make up for the oversight by taking everything they said.

Around him, the senior guild members knelt in a crescent, or what remained of them. Half had aged decades in a single night when the collections began — Master Ellery's hands now spotted and trembling, Mistress Vane's hair gone white as ledger-paper between one dawn and the next. They had come anyway. Fear made them obedient, and obedience was the only currency Corvane had ever needed from anyone.

"The names," he said.

Vane lifted the tray with both hands, and her arms shook, and Corvane did not help her because helping was a debt and he was finished with debts. On the tray lay the covenant pages — every debtor's name the guild had ever recorded against the old gods' favor, three centuries of borrowed luck and healed children and safe passages, written down in fair hands by scribes who believed they were keeping accounts. Including, folded separately, the girl's grandmother's page. Including, folded inside that, the girl's own contract.

Lena Nakamura was locked in the vault two floors below the garden, behind a door whose lock he had personally entered into the master ledger, in ink, in his own hand. A name on a page could not walk through walls. He had built his life on that principle. He would build his death on it too, if it came to that, but he did not intend for it to come to that.

He took up the pen — Ada's grave-soil ground into ink, black as the space between stars — and he spoke the words Aunt Iyo's predecessor had died rather than teach him, words pried out of a drowning man's memory fifty years ago, and he wrote the first name into the circle of ash he'd burned into the turf.

The garden exhaled.

* * *

It is difficult to describe what it is to receive a god, Corvane thought afterward, in whatever afterward turned out to be, and the difficulty was that there was no afterward in which thought remained shaped like a man's.

The first god came up through the soles of his feet. He felt it the way a building feels a foundation settling — a vast adjustment, a redistribution of weight, and then he was taller than his body, wider than the garden, older than the guild, older than the country that no longer existed except under this soil. He was the god of thresholds, the small one, the one who had watched doors for four thousand years before men learned to write *door*, and he poured himself into Corvane like water finding a channel, and Corvane — Corvane held him.

That was the part no priest had managed. That was the part the guild had spent three hundred years learning to avoid: you did not *become* a vessel, you became a *ledger*, and a ledger does not resist what is written in it. It only records. He let the god pour through him and recorded nothing, gave back nothing, kept everything, and felt the god realize, dimly and too late, that it had not ascended into a worshipper but descended into a vault.

*Yes,* Corvane thought, in the voice he imagined his soul must have, if souls had voices. *Yes. Stay. I have room.*

The second god came as heat. The third as a sound like a struck bell that never finished ringing. The fourth, fifth, sixth — he stopped counting, because counting was a kind of bookkeeping and bookkeeping was for the man he had been an hour ago, the man with the ledgers and the locked vault and the kneeling, aging subordinates. That man had been preparing for this since he was nine years old and watched a priest of Vessany burn a debt-note and weep with relief. Corvane had understood, even then, with a child's terrible clarity: the priests had power and gave it away. He would take power and keep it. Everything else in his life had been arithmetic leading to this sum.

He rose above the garden. He was aware of his body still standing in the ring of ash, knees buckled, mouth open, blood running freely from both nostrils — a small, distant inconvenience, a house he was no longer living in. From this height the city of Vessany-that-was spread below him like an open book seen from a great distance, its streets legible, its ten thousand sleeping citizens each carrying their little debts and grudges and prayers, and he could read them. All of them. Every wish ever whispered in this country was written somewhere in him now, cross-referenced, indexed, *his*.

A woman in the Salt Quarter was praying for her son's fever to break. He broke it. It cost him nothing; it cost less than nothing; it was a gesture, a signature, a demonstration. Somewhere below, a woman gasped awake beside a cooling child and began to weep with gratitude toward a heaven that had just changed management.

*They will pray to me,* he thought, and the thought rang through the six gods inside him like a chord. *Not to the soil. Not to the graves. To me. And I will answer, and every answer will be a debt, and every debt will be mine to collect, forever.*

Power, he discovered, tasted like the moment before signing. Like the pen hovering over the line. Like the whole world waiting, blank, for your hand.

Then the seventh god arrived, and with it, the collections.

* * *

Here is what Aunt Iyo had tried to tell him, thirty years ago, drunk and sentimental in the archive after the winter festival, when he'd been young enough to listen without hearing: *the ledgers were prayer-books first, boy. You don't understand what that means. A prayer-book isn't a record of what people owed. It's a record of what was promised to them. We didn't write down sins. We wrote down vows. Every entry is a promise someone made to a god, and the god keeps copies.*

Corvane had smiled and poured her more wine and filed the information away as sentiment.

Now, holding seven divinities in a body built for one bad-tempered merchant, he understood.

The gods he had swallowed were not the whole of what slept in the garden. They were the *creditors*. And a creditor, once it has a debtor's full attention, presents its accounts.

They arrived all at once, because gods do not queue.

Three centuries of unpaid vows came home through him like water through a burst dam. Every prayer the guild had answered and billed for. Every blessing sold at interest. Every time a guildsman had traded a year of someone's life, a name, a door, a country — the original promise had been made in the gods' names, sealed with the gods' authority, and the gods had been buried before the invoices came due. Now they were awake, and they were inside him, and they used him the way he had planned to use them: as a ledger.

He felt the first collection land as cold. Then as weight. Then as *entry* — actual entries, being written into him, lines of script appearing on the inside of his skin, he could see them when he closed his eyes, columns of figures in a hand older than writing:

*Borrowed: the healing of Maren Ostrey's daughter. Interest accrued: 61 years.*
*Borrowed: safe passage through the Ashford ford, 214 souls. Unpaid.*
*Borrowed: the name "Vessany" itself, spoken 40 million times without license—*

"Stop," Corvane said aloud, and his voice came out layered, seven voices deep, and none of them obeyed him.

He tried to do what he had done with the first god — hold, record, refuse to give back. But these were not gifts flowing in. These were *debts flowing out*, and a ledger cannot refuse its own red ink. The entries wrote themselves into his marrow. He felt his left hand age forty years in a heartbeat, the skin going slack and spotted, and he looked at it with detached horror, the way a man looks at a fire starting in a neighbor's house.

Master Ellery was screaming. Corvane registered it distantly — the old man had crept forward during the rite, greedy even now, reaching for the circle of ash, and the moment his fingers crossed the boundary the collections had found a second address. Vane was dragging him back, white-haired, weeping, shouting something about stopping, about undoing it, and Corvane wanted to laugh at her. Undo it. As if power were a contract with a cancellation clause.

*You misunderstand,* the gods said, in the voice that was also his voice, also the bell that never stopped ringing. *We are not punishing you. Punishment implies judgment. This is simply arithmetic. You took us in. Therefore you assumed our accounts. Read your own doctrine, guildmaster. Liability follows possession.*

"I know the doctrine," Corvane snarled. "I *wrote* the doctrine."

*Yes,* they said, and there was something horribly like amusement in it. *We read it. It's very good. It's binding.*

More entries. His spine curved; his teeth loosened; somewhere below, in the body, blood was pooling in his mouth. He reached for the tray, for the covenant pages, for the desperate gambit he had prepared — sacrifice the debtors' names, transfer the obligations, buy himself clean with other people's collateral. His right arm would not lift. It had become the arm of a man of ninety, and then the arm of a man of one hundred and twenty, and then it was simply old beyond sense, a relic, a thing from a museum of hands.

He understood, then, with the same child's clarity that had started all of this, exactly what he had built.

He had spent his life believing that whoever held the ledger owned the debt. It had never occurred to him — it had genuinely never occurred to him, and the omission was so vast he almost admired it — that the ledger might be held *against* him. That every page he'd ever balanced was a page on which *he* appeared, in the creditor's column, in a book kept by things that had been patient for three hundred years because patience costs creditors nothing.

The girl had known. He saw it now, absurdly, clearly, the way the dying see the obvious. Lena Nakamura, locked in his vault, had grown up among these pages and somehow understood what he never had: that the ledger was not a weapon. It was a *witness*. And witnesses, unlike weapons, cannot be pointed — only answered.

Somewhere far away, at the edge of the garden, a wall was shaking. Voices — hundreds of them, runners and scribes and clerks, the people whose names he had spent generations trading like coin. Tomas was out there, the runner boy, holding them at the stones. And smaller than all of it, fainter than the ringing of the seventh god, he heard footsteps on the gravel path behind him, quick and deliberate, coming toward the ring of ash where a man who had briefly been a coronation was folding inward like a burning page.

Corvane Ash turned — slowly, terribly, his neck grinding like an old hinge — and looked at the girl walking toward him with grave-ink on her fingers, and the last thought he had as a free man was the most ledger-minded thought of his long career:

*She's here to collect.*
