## 20. Blank Pages

She woke to the smell of rain on warm stone.

That was the first thing — not a name, not a place, but a smell, arriving before language did, the way light arrives before you open your eyes. Lena lay still and let it come: wet stone, cut grass, woodsmoke somewhere far off, and under all of it a green sweetness she could not name and did not want to, because naming it felt like pressing a bruise.

She opened her eyes.

Sky. A pale, washed-out morning sky with clouds moving in long slow ranks, the way clouds move when they have nowhere particular to be. She was lying on her back on a hillside of close-cropped grass, and the grass was not Vessany-grass — she knew that without knowing how she knew it, the way you know a stranger's handwriting is not your mother's. The blades were broader. The soil beneath them was darker, richer, smelling of things that had never been traded or taxed or written down.

She sat up slowly, and her body reported itself to her in pieces. Hands: ink-stained at the cuticles, calloused along the right middle finger where a pen had rested for years. That was hers. That she recognized. Knees: stiff from sleeping on ground. Shoulders: carrying an ache like she'd been holding something heavy for a very long time and had only just set it down.

Her name. She reached for it first, the way a drowning woman reaches for a rope, and found it waiting — *Lena* — whole and solid and unmistakably hers. Not borrowed. Not collateral. Not a line in someone else's book. Just *Lena*, sitting inside her chest like a coal that would not go out.

Everything else was fog.

Not gone, exactly. Fog. Shapes moved in it. She knew there had been a country, because the word *country* meant something, and the word *Vessany* meant something too — a shape, a weight, a grief with no face attached. But when she reached for the streets of it, the markets, the sound of the guildhall bells, the color of the door of the house she must have grown up in — the fog closed over her reaching hand like water.

There had been a grandmother. She was almost sure there had been a grandmother. Someone who had — done something. Something enormous. An act whose shape remained even as its content drained away, the way a footprint remains in snow after the foot has moved on. Lena pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars and tried to make herself cry, because she understood dimly that she ought to be crying, that this was the kind of loss that deserved tears, and the tears would not come. Grief needs details. Hers had been confiscated at the border.

What remained, when she took inventory, was strange and specific:

She could read. Three scripts, maybe four — she tested them silently against the sky, against the bark of a nearby tree, and the letters resolved into meaning without friction. She could keep accounts; the discipline of it lived in her hands like a scar. She could write a fair hand at speed. She knew what tallow smelled like when it burned too long, knew the weight of a ledger bound in grey leather, knew — with a certainty that made her laugh, once, a small cracked sound — exactly how many pages a standard guild folio held.

The skills had survived. The life they'd belonged to had not.

"Careful," said a voice above her. "Sit up slow. You've been out since yesterday."

Tomas.

He was sitting three feet away on a flat stone, wrapped in someone else's coat, and he looked terrible and wonderful and entirely himself. His face was scraped along one cheekbone. His hands were blistered. He had the look of a man who had not slept and had decided, sometime in the night, that sleep was a luxury for people with fewer responsibilities. When he saw her eyes open and focus, something went out of his shoulders — a tension he'd apparently been holding for hours, maybe days.

"Lena." He said it carefully, the way you say a word you're afraid might break. "Do you know me?"

She looked at him. The fog stirred. There was a shape in it — a runner's grin, a boy who carried petitions through dangerous streets, someone who had stood beside her at a wall while the sky filled with names — but no name attached to the shape except the one he'd just given her.

"Tomas," she said, testing it. It fit his mouth. It fit the shape. "You're Tomas."

Something flickered across his face — a flinch, quickly mastered, the pain of a man hearing himself described back to himself by someone who used to know better. Then he smiled, and it was mostly real.

"That's right," he said. "I'm Tomas. And you're Lena Nakamura. And we're—" He stopped. Looked around at the hillside, the strange broad grass, the smoke rising from a cluster of low buildings in the valley below. "We're here. Wherever here is. It doesn't have a name yet. I asked."

"You asked who?"

"The locals. Down in the valley. They don't have a name for the country because—" he shrugged— "it turns out most countries don't need one. They just have roads and bread and arguments about fences. Nobody's ever had to *be* a country before. It's optional, apparently." He said it lightly, but his eyes were doing the work of two men, watching her face for every small sign of what was missing.

"How long was I asleep?"

"A day. Maybe more. Time's funny here — or maybe it was just funny last week and everything's settling down now." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "There are others. From the garden. Scattered down the valley and along the river — maybe two hundred, maybe more, still coming through in ones and twos. Some of them remember everything. Some of them remember nothing. Most are like you — in between. You kept your hands and lost your houses."

"My hands," Lena said, and looked at them again, ink-stained and steady, and felt the truth of it settle over her like the coat Tomas wore: ill-fitting, borrowed, warm.

"There was a door," she said slowly. "I opened it. I paid for it. I don't remember what I paid, but I can feel the shape of the payment, like a tooth socket." She flexed her fingers. "And there was a man. Corvane. He—"

"He burned," Tomas said quietly. "Or unburned. Or whatever the opposite of being is. The gods collected, and he'd taken out loans against divinity, and the interest came due all at once." He paused. "You don't remember that either?"

"I remember fire that wasn't fire. And I remember a mirror. And a voice asking me a question." Her throat tightened. "I remember answering. I just can't remember what I answered with."

"Your name," Tomas said. "You chose your name. You told me after — you were laughing and crying at the same time, and you said, *I kept the one thing that was actually mine.*" He swallowed. "Then you slept for a day, and when you woke up you didn't know my face, so I've been sitting here rehearsing this conversation about forty times, and I have to tell you, it's going better than rehearsal."

Lena laughed. It hurt, and it helped, the way laughter does when it's the only tool left in the kit.

They sat together on the hillside while the morning warmed. Below, the settlement was waking — smoke from cookfires, the distant complaint of a cart, children's voices with the particular pitch of children who had recently been somewhere terrible and were determined, with the ruthless resilience of the young, to be loud again. Refugees. People who had walked out of a country as it dissolved into light, carrying whatever they could hold in their arms and their heads.

"I lost things," Lena said at last. "I know I lost things. I can feel the holes." She turned to him, and the question came out smaller than she wanted it to. "Was there anyone? Family? Did I leave anyone behind?"

Tomas was quiet for a moment. She watched him weigh something — the mercy of invention against the cruelty of honesty — and choose honesty, because he was Tomas, and that was apparently the kind of person he was, the one fact about him the fog had not managed to take.

"There was a grandmother," he said. "You talked about her sometimes. Late nights, balancing entries. You never said her name — I think maybe you never knew it, or it was one of those things you kept private. But you said she'd saved you. Before you were born. That everything good in your life came from something she did in a vault, alone, sixty years ago." He met her eyes. "You said you were going to find out if it was true. And then you did find out. And it was true. And then you gave up knowing it so a whole country could stop being a debt."

Lena pressed her hand flat against the grass, against the dark rich soil, and felt the hole where the grandmother should be — a grandmother-shaped absence, filled now only with secondhand knowledge, a story told by a friend in place of a memory. It was like being handed a photograph of a room you'd lived in your whole life. Every detail correct. Nothing warm in it.

"She sounds like someone I should have known," Lena said.

"You did know her. For about four days. Intensively." Tomas's mouth twitched. "You cried on a grave. Then you signed a book with dirt-ink and kicked a god's door open. Very you, from what I could tell in the time I had."

"What else was very me?"

He considered. "You worked too much. You trusted ledgers more than people, until you learned they were the same thing. You were stubborn in ways that looked like cowardice from the outside and turned out to be load-bearing walls." He counted these off on blistered fingers, and she realized he was giving her back herself piece by piece, carefully, the way you hand someone their belongings after a fire — sorting what's salvageable from what's ash.

"Keep going," she said.

"You liked honey in your tea but pretended not to, because guild scribes weren't supposed to have weaknesses. You hummed when you balanced a column correctly — badly, tunelessly, but you couldn't help it. You once threatened to audit a debt-collector." He grinned. "That one I'll remember till I die. He apologized."

"I did not threaten to audit a god."

"You absolutely did. Small god. Shrine of the Weeping Spring. It had taken a deposit of someone's wedding day and you informed it, in writing, that misrepresentation of assets was grounds for excommunication from the ledger." He shook his head. "It gave the memory back. Full refund. I think it was scared of you."

Lena found she was smiling — really smiling, the muscles remembering their old work even if the reasons behind them were new-minted, secondhand, his. It was an odd way to learn yourself: through another man's eyes, in anecdotes, like reading a biography of a stranger who happened to have your hands.

"Why do you remember so much?" she asked. "Of me, I mean. Of us."

The grin faded into something quieter. He picked up a pebble and turned it over in his fingers.

"When the door opened, everyone got to carry one thing," he said. "One thing, chosen in the heart, before the mind even knew the choice was happening. Most people grabbed their children's names. Their mother's recipes. The layout of their street. One old man carried the smell of his wife's hair and won't stop talking about it, which is fair." He rolled the pebble between finger and thumb. "I tried to grab Vessany. All of it. The whole country. Greedy, I know. It wouldn't fit — it started slipping through my fingers like water, and I panicked, and at the last second I grabbed the nearest solid thing instead."

"Which was?"

"You," he said simply. "Standing in front of the mirror-grave with ink on your hands, deciding to give away everything you loved. I figured somebody should remember that. Somebody should remember *you*, since you couldn't afford to." He looked out at the valley, ears going faintly red. "So I've been keeping you. In here." He tapped his temple. "External storage. It's what runners are for — carrying things that matter fast and safe. You taught me that, probably. I don't remember who taught me, but I'm choosing to believe it was you."

Lena looked at him for a long moment — this scraped, blistered, sleepless boy who had walked through the end of a world and come out the other side holding nothing but her.

"Tomas," she said. "Will you tell me everything? All of it, whenever I ask, as many times as I ask? Even when it's boring. Even when I ask the same question twice in an hour."

"As many times as you ask," he said. "For both of us. That's the deal. You keep the accounts—" he nodded at her ink-stained hands— "and I'll keep the stories. Between us we might make one whole person. Two, counting me. I'm missing things too — I can feel the holes on my side. My mother's face is gone. I know I had a mother. I know she was angry about something the week before the end, and I will never know what about, and that's—" his voice caught, steadied— "that's a hole I'm going to have to live around. So we'll live around them together. The holes. Like neighbors sharing a wall."

"Like neighbors sharing a wall," Lena agreed, and found that her eyes were wet at last — not for the grandmother she couldn't see, but for this: the two of them on a hillside in an unnamed country, pooling what little they had, making a household of fragments.

Below them, someone was ringing a bell — not a guild bell, something smaller, brighter, calling people to a meal or a meeting. Figures were converging on the largest building in the valley, a long hall with a turf roof. Among them Lena could see faces she almost knew: shapes in the fog, gestures that rang faint bells of their own. A woman with silver braids who walked like a soldier. A pair of twins who kept finishing each other's sentences. An old man clutching a bundle to his chest and talking, endlessly, joyfully, about the smell of someone's hair.

"They're asking for you," Tomas said. "Down there. The ones who came through. Word's gotten around that you opened the door, and—" he hesitated— "there's a problem. A ledger-shaped problem."

Lena felt her spine straighten, some old professional reflex firing in the dark.

"What kind of problem?"

"People brought things through. What they could carry. And some of them carried debts — old ones, guild ones, contracts that were half-burned when Vessany went. And the contracts came through anyway, because paper remembers." He pulled a folded, scorched-edged document from inside the borrowed coat and laid it on the stone between them. "Two hundred people, Lena. Maybe more. Standing in a country with no laws and no banks and no guild, holding contracts on their own lives, and every one of them looking at the only person they've heard of who ever beat the books."

Lena looked at the scorched contract, at the columns of numbers marching across it like ants escaping a fire, and felt her hands itch — genuinely itch — to pick up a pen.

She had no homeland. No grandmother's face. No memories worth the name, only other people's stories of her. But she had her name, and her hands, and a friend who remembered enough for both of them, and two hundred people down in the valley who needed someone who could read the fine print of miracles.

"Well," she said, standing, brushing the strange rich soil from her skirts, "then I suppose we'd better open a new ledger."

Tomas grinned, and offered her his arm, and together they started down the hill toward the smoke and the bell and the blank pages waiting below.
