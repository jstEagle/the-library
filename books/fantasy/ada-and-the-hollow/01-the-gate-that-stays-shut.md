## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

The gate had not opened in eleven years, and Ada Orsen Crane intended to keep it that way.

She walked its length at dawn the way her father had taught her, boots crunching on frost-rimed stone, one hand trailing along the iron-banded timber as if she could read it by touch. Forty feet of black oak and older iron, set into a throat of granite so narrow that two carts could not have passed abreast. Above it, the pass walls rose sheer for six hundred feet, and above those, the peaks of Thornfell wore their snow like a crown no king had earned.

"Good morning," she said to the gate. She always said it. Her father had said it before her, and his mother before him, back through a line of keepers so long that the ledger in the tollhouse recorded names in a hand nobody living could decipher. The Cranes kept the gate. The gate stayed shut. These were not rules so much as facts of the world, like the color of the sky or the direction water ran.

The inspection took an hour and she did not hurry it. Hinges first — she climbed the scaffold her grandfather had built and worked oil into the great pins, though the hinges would never swing again if she had any say. Then the bands, checking for rust bloom, tapping each one with the little hammer on her belt and listening. Sound iron answered with a flat, dense note. Rotten iron rang hollow. This morning they all sang true.

Then the mortar joints in the flanking masonry, then the counterweight channel behind the wall — a crawl-space most keepers never bothered with, because what was the point of checking machinery you'd sworn never to use? But Ada crawled it anyway, on her elbows, lamp held in her teeth, feeling along the greased chain links for wear. Her father's voice lived in that dark space still. *A keeper who trusts the gate more than her own eyes is a keeper the mountain will bury.* He'd been dead nine years and he still inspected the gate with her every morning.

She came out dusty and cold-blooded and satisfied, and stood a moment in the shadow of the arch, looking up at the pass beyond. The road climbed away from her in switchbacks, white and empty, toward the high col where the border stones stood buried to their shoulders in drift. Somewhere past that col were the lowland kingdoms, their wars, their kings, their hungers. None of it concerned her. The Treaty of Ash had closed this pass a generation ago, after the last war, and the world on both sides had agreed — without ever quite saying it aloud — that some doors are better left shut.

Ada agreed too. Mostly.

* * *

The tollhouse sat a quarter mile down from the gate, squat and stone-roofed, with a stable lean-to and a smokehouse and a garden plot she fought the altitude for every summer. It was the only building for eight miles in any direction, and it was hers, and she loved it the way you love a thing that has never once lied to you.

Her morning ritual continued indoors: kettle on, ledger open, yesterday's weather logged, today's forecast guessed. She wrote small and neat, in columns. *Wind NW, hard. Frost. No traffic.* Most entries looked like that. Some months the ledger held nothing but wind and frost and the occasional line — *Eagle sighted over the col. Old ewe lost near the scree. Repaired south shutter.*

Once, when she was nineteen and restless in a way she hadn't let herself be since, she'd written an entire page about a dream she'd had — a city under the mountain, lit from below, full of voices. Her father had found the entry, read it without expression, and told her that the ledger was for the gate, not for dreams. She'd burned the page in the stove and never written another. But she still remembered the city sometimes, in the half-hour before waking. In the lullabies, too — the ones the clan women sang at the autumn markets, words worn smooth as river stones: *down below, down below, where the hollow waters flow.* Nobody remembered what they meant. They were just songs. Songs were allowed to mean nothing.

She was grinding barley for porridge when she heard the bell.

Not the gate bell — that hadn't rung since before her time. The trade bell, bolted beside the tollhouse door, rung by anyone coming up the smugglers' road from the valley, because Ada shot first and asked questions second and everyone below knew it. Three rings, spaced. The signal of someone who wanted to be known.

She set down the pestle, took her bow off its pegs, and went out onto the step.

He was maybe two hundred yards down the slope, leading a mule, and even at that distance she knew the shape of him: broad through the shoulder, narrow through the luck, walking with the rolling gait of a man whose left knee had been broken badly once and set worse. Bren. The only person in the world who visited her on purpose.

"You're early," she called, not lowering the bow.

"I'm punctual." His voice carried easily up the slope; he'd spent thirty years making himself heard across distances he wasn't supposed to cross. "You're the one who lives at the top of the world. Time runs slow up here."

"Time runs exactly the same everywhere. That's what makes it time."

"That," Bren said, arriving at last in the yard and looping the mule's lead over the rail, "is the kind of thing a woman says who hasn't argued with anyone but a goat in four months."

"Six weeks. And the goat started it."

He grinned. He was somewhere north of forty, grey coming into his beard like frost spreading across a window, with a face that had been handsome once and had decided, around thirty, to stop pretending. Smugglers weren't supposed to be likable. That was rather the point of them. But Bren had been trading up the forbidden road since before the Treaty was dry ink — salt up, wool and pelts down, and certain other goods whose names he never spoke in daylight — and in all those years he had never cheated her, never brought trouble to her door, and never asked a single question about why a woman alone would choose to keep a dead gate at the top of a dead pass.

That last part was the friendship, really. Not the conversation, though the conversation was good. The not-asking.

"Salt," he said, slapping the mule's packsaddle. "Lamp oil. A bolt of blue cloth I suspect you'll pretend you don't want and then take anyway. And—" he produced a paper-wrapped parcel from inside his coat with the flourish of a man revealing a winning card, "—honey cakes. From the Lowmarket baker. Don't ask what I paid."

"You paid too much."

"I paid exactly enough, which is different." He handed it over and watched her break the seal and smell it, and something in his face went briefly soft and strange, there and gone, like a fish turning under dark water.

They ate porridge and honey cakes at her table while the morning strengthened outside. He told her valley news — the ferry at Coldbridge was out again, the Ashen Order had posted new men at the toll shrines, a child in the village below had fallen down a well and lived, which everyone agreed proved the gods had a sense of humor after all. She told him about the ewe, the eagle, the shutter. It was, by any reasonable measure, almost nothing. It was also, by any reasonable measure, the best conversation either of them would have all week, and they both knew it, and neither of them minded.

"The Order's thick on the roads lately," Bren said, into his third cake. "More grey cloaks than I've seen since the war scare. Asking questions at the crossings. Nothing to do with us," he added quickly, which was how she knew it might be something to do with them. "Mountain folk. We're beneath their interest."

"Everyone is beneath their interest until they aren't."

He looked at her then, a beat too long, and she caught it — that fish-under-water flicker again, there and gone. "Something like that," he said, and changed the subject to the price of lamp oil.

Later, when he'd unloaded and was watering the mule, she walked him to the edge of the yard, the way she always did, and he paused with his hand on the animal's neck, the way he always did, and said, the way he always did:

"You could come down sometime, you know. Off this rock. See people."

"People," Ada said. "I've met them."

"One or two. You might like the rest."

"I like my ledger. My ledger doesn't talk."

"No," Bren said quietly. "It just records." And then, before she could decide whether that had been a joke: "Storm's coming. Feel that air? Out of the northwest, heavy. You'll want your shutters checked."

"My shutters are always checked."

"Course they are." He swung up onto the mule, gathered the lead rope of the packhorse behind it, and looked down at her from that height with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — something between fondness and apology, aimed at her but perhaps not about her. "Keep well, Keeper Crane."

"Walk safe, Bren."

He went down the road, and she watched him until the switchbacks took him, because that was the ritual too, and rituals were the bones of a life. Then the wind arrived ahead of the clouds, smelling of far-off snow, and she went inside to bar her door against a storm that hadn't broken yet.

* * *

It broke at dusk.

Not gradually — the mountains didn't do gradual. One hour the sky was bruised and silent; the next the wind hit the tollhouse like something with hands, and the snow came sideways, and the world beyond her windows ceased to exist. Ada made her rounds twice, lantern swinging, checking the shutters she already knew were sound, and then settled in with the ledger and a fire built against the worst.

She liked storms. She'd never admitted that to anyone, because it sounded like the sort of thing that got a solitary woman pitied, but it was true. A storm was the world acknowledging her work. Every traveler safe below the pass instead of frozen on it, every shutter holding, every joint of the old house creaking and surviving — a storm was proof that her careful life held. On clear days the world ignored her. On nights like this, it tested her, and she passed.

She was logging the barometer drop — *wind NW gale, snow heavy, visibility nil* — when she heard the sound.

At first she took it for the wind. It had that same long, rising quality, a note pulled thin. But the wind didn't repeat. The wind didn't come in phrases. This did: three cries, spaced apart, ragged, human, torn to shreds by distance and weather but unmistakably coming from upslope. From the road. From the direction of the gate.

Ada stood very still.

No one traveled the pass in a storm. No one traveled the pass at all — that was the entire architecture of her life. The smugglers' road stopped at her tollhouse; the gate road beyond it was closed by treaty, by law, by eleven years of her own stubborn maintenance, and the only souls who ever came up it were her own inspections and, once a year, the Order's survey riders, who came in summer and hated her for living there.

But the cry came again, closer now, and it was not a survey rider's voice. It was thinner. It was a woman's voice, and it was saying something she couldn't make out, over and over, like a word being worn away with each repetition.

Ada looked at the fire. At the ledger. At the barred door.

Every rule she had said stay inside. The rules had kept her alive through nine winters alone. Her father's voice, patient in the dark of the counterweight channel: *the gate stays shut, and so does the door.*

But the voice was getting weaker. And underneath the fear, underneath even the thought of her father, some older thing stirred — the half-remembered city from the dream, the lullaby verse about the hollow waters, the sense she'd carried all her life without examining that the mountain was not empty but merely quiet, holding its breath, waiting for something it had been promised a long time ago.

"Oh, hells," she said aloud, to the empty room, and took down her lantern and her bow, and unbarred the door.

The storm took her breath on the threshold. She leaned into the wind and shouted, "This way! Keep to the road — follow the light!" — knowing the words would die three feet from her lips, knowing the woman couldn't possibly hear, knowing she'd have to go out and find her.

She found her forty yards up the gate road, half-crawling, one hand pressed to her side, snow crusted red-black on her coat. She was older than Ada had expected — fifty, maybe, silver-streaked hair plastered to her face — and when Ada got an arm under her and hauled her upright, the woman's eyes found hers with a sudden terrible focus, as if the storm and the dark and the dying had all been waiting for precisely this moment to release her.

She grabbed the front of Ada's coat with a strength that had no business remaining in her.

"You're hers," the woman rasped. "I knew it. The moment I saw the light — you stand the way she stood."

"Who—"

"You already know why." The woman's free hand fumbled inside her coat and came out with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and leather, bound with a cord, and pressed it against Ada's chest with the insistence of someone handing over a live coal. "Keeper's daughter. Gatekeeper's blood. You already know *why*, Ada Crane. Don't let them burn it. Don't let them—"

The word broke. Whatever came after it went out with the rest of her, like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, and her weight came all at once into Ada's arms, and the storm closed over them both.

Ada knelt in the snow on the closed road below her sealed gate, holding a stranger who was not a stranger — she understood that much already, in the place where understanding arrives ahead of proof — and the bundle lay against her heart, warm as a second heartbeat, heavier than anything she had ever carried.

Behind her, the gate stood shut. Ahead of her, the storm erased the road in both directions.

Somewhere below, impossibly far, she thought she heard a bell ring three times.

But that was madness. No one could ride up through this.

She gathered the woman in her arms and carried her home.
