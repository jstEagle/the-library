## 2. A Stranger in the Snow

The storm came down the pass like something with intent.

Ada had watched weather her whole life — it was half of a gatekeeper's work, reading the sky the way a reader reads a page — and she had never seen a storm move like this one. It didn't build. It arrived. One hour the peaks above Thornfell were clean and blue-shadowed, and the next the whole western wall of the mountains simply vanished behind a grey curtain that swallowed the light.

She stood at the gatehouse window and watched it come and felt, without any reason she could name, that it was coming for her.

"Ridiculous," she said aloud, because the gatehouse was empty and no one was there to hear her be ridiculous. Talking to herself was another habit of the solitary. She'd stopped apologizing for it years ago. The stones didn't mind. The stones had heard every thought she'd ever had, more or less, given how many of them she said out loud.

The gate itself was shut. Of course it was shut. The Thornfell gate had been shut for eleven years, since the Order closed the high passes after the last of the border troubles, and Ada's entire office consisted of keeping it that way. She inspected it twice daily: the great iron-banded leaves, the counterweight chains, the lockwork that three generations of Cranes had oiled and adjusted until it moved like a living thing. She checked the seal-wax impressions on the locking bar, verified them against the Order's registry, initialed the ledger. Twice a day, every day, for six years since her father died and left her the post nobody else wanted.

*The gate that stays shut,* the lowland folk called it, with a kind of grim humor. *And the girl who stays with it.*

She pulled on her storm cloak, checked the oilskin over the ledger one more time out of pure ritual, and went out into the first spit of the storm to walk the inspection circuit before the weather made it impossible.

The wind hit her like a hand. Snow came not falling but flying, horizontal, stinging. By the time she'd crossed the courtyard to the gate her eyebrows were crusted white and her fingers ached inside two layers of wool. She ran her gloved hand along the locking bar anyway, found the wax seals intact, found the bar seated true, and stood there a moment with her palm flat against the cold iron as if she could take its pulse.

"All quiet," she told it. "Another night."

The gate did not answer. Gates never did. But standing there with her hand on it, in the teeth of a storm that had arrived like an accusation, Ada felt the first cold thread of something that wasn't quite dread and wasn't quite hope slide under her ribs.

Something was going to change. She'd learned to trust that feeling the way other people trusted their eyes. The mountain spoke first in weather, then in animals, then in dreams, and only last in words — and all three of its lower languages were saying the same thing tonight.

She finished the circuit fast, fed the brazier, ate standing up, and was reaching for the ledger to record the evening inspection when she heard the bell.

Not the gate bell. The road bell — a small iron thing hung on a post a quarter mile down the smugglers' switchbacks, put there by some long-dead Crane so travelers in trouble could ring for shelter. In six years it had rung twice. Once for Bren, who could have knocked. Once for a mountain goat.

It rang now, three times, ragged, weak. And then — she strained through the wind's howl — a fourth ring that trailed off into nothing, like the hand on the rope had let go.

Ada stood with her hand on the ledger and let herself have exactly five seconds of the argument she was already losing. *Storm of the decade. Road's a deathtrap. Whoever it is will freeze or they won't; either way it's not your problem. Your problem is a sealed gate and a ledger and staying alive and unnoticed until you're old.*

Then she took down the rescue sledge, the coil, the lantern, and went out into the white roaring dark, because her father had raised her with one commandment above all the others, carved somewhere under his skin where she couldn't help inheriting it:

*A keeper keeps.*

* * *

The smugglers' road below Thornfell wasn't really a smugglers' road anymore — the smuggling had mostly died when the passes closed — but the name stuck, and so did the road: a ledge cut into the mountainside, wide enough for a cart in the old days, now just a scar of stone zigzagging down toward the valley. In clear weather it was a hard walk. Tonight it was a place where the world ended two feet from your boots and began again wherever the wind decided.

Ada went down it roped to the gatehouse anchor, bent nearly double, one hand on the rock, the lantern a useless coin of light in the murk. Snow filled her hood, her collar, her seams. Three times the wind picked her cloak like a sail and tried to peel her off the mountain, and three times she wrapped herself around the rock and waited it out, counting her breaths, thinking about nothing at all because thinking was a luxury for later.

She found the bell post first, its rope whipping loose — someone had rung it and been blown or dragged away from it. She found the sledge marks second: a thin line scored in the snow crust, running off the road's edge.

Her stomach dropped.

"No," she said, to the storm, to herself, to whoever had gone over. "No, no—"

She got down on her knees at the edge. Below the road the slope fell away in a steep tumble of scree and snow, forty feet down to the tree line. Her lantern caught movement there — or imagined it. A shape. Dark against white. Not moving.

Ada looked back up the road toward the warm gatehouse, and the distance between here and there suddenly seemed enormous, uncrossable, a different country. Then she looked down at the shape again.

"A keeper keeps," she said, and went over the edge.

The descent was ugly. She slid more than climbed, using the rope, using her heels, using her elbows, the lantern banging against her hip. Scree shifted under her and once carried her ten feet in a small avalanche of her own making. She fetched up against a wind-twisted pine with her lungs full of ice and her heart slamming, and looked around, and saw the shape again — closer now, unmistakably human, curled against the base of a boulder like something the mountain had spat out.

A woman. Grey-cloaked — no, the cloak had been grey once, was now the color of wet ash, torn along one side, crusted with blood and ice. Middle-aged or older, though it was hard to tell; the face was gaunt, frost-nipped, the lips cracked and dark. One arm was bent wrong at the elbow. There was blood on the snow beneath her, black in the lantern light, too much of it, still spreading faintly at the edges, which meant —

Which meant she was still alive.

Ada dropped beside her and got her hands under the woman's shoulders, shouting into the wind — "I've got you! I've got you, stay with me!" — knowing even as she shouted that the woman couldn't hear her over the storm, and possibly couldn't hear her at all.

The woman's eyes opened.

They were dark, sharp, shockingly present — eyes that looked at Ada with total, deliberate focus, the way no dying person should be able to look at anything. They moved over Ada's face once, quick as a scan, and something flickered in them. Recognition? Relief? Grief? In the bad light, in the screaming wind, Ada couldn't read it. It passed too fast.

The woman's good hand came up and seized the front of Ada's coat with shocking strength.

"You're—" she started, and coughed, wet and terrible. "You're the keeper."

"I'm the keeper," Ada said. "Hold on. I'm getting you up to the gatehouse. There's fire, there's—"

"No." The word came out cracked but absolute. The woman's grip tightened. "No time. Listen. Listen to me."

"We can't talk here, we'll both—"

"*Listen.*"

There was something in the voice — authority, desperation, the particular flat calm of someone who has already done all their panicking in advance — that stopped Ada cold. She knelt in the snow with a stranger's blood soaking through her knee and listened.

The woman's hand left Ada's coat and went to her own breast, fumbling beneath the ruined cloak. For one mad instant Ada thought: *weapon* — and then the hand came out holding a bundle, leather-wrapped, tied with cord, small enough to fit in two palms. The woman pressed it against Ada's chest and pushed it into her arms with the same strength that had gripped her coat, folding Ada's own hands around it, closing her fingers over the leather one by one.

"Take it," the woman said. "Keep it. Don't show it. Don't sell it. Don't burn it." A shudder went through her, and her face spasmed with pain she refused to make a sound about. "They'll come for it. Grey cloaks. They'll ask you questions and every question will be a knife. Don't answer."

"I don't understand what this is—"

"You do." The woman's dark eyes fixed on hers again, and this time there was no mistaking what was in them. It was grief. Old grief, worn smooth, the kind that has been carried so long it's become part of the body carrying it. "You already know why."

The words went through Ada like a blade finding a gap in armor.

Because — and this was the thing she would turn over a thousand times in the nights afterward, the thing that would keep her staring at ceilings — she *did*. Not in her head. Somewhere deeper than her head. The weight of the bundle in her hands felt familiar the way a word feels familiar on the tip of the tongue. The lullabies her father used to sing, the ones he'd stop singing when she asked what they meant. The way he'd gone silent whenever the Hollow was mentioned, which was rarely, because everyone knew better than to mention it. The hollow-eyed look he'd get sometimes at dusk, looking west into the mountains, as if waiting for someone to come walking down out of them.

*You already know why.*

"I don't," Ada said. "I don't know anything. Who are you?"

The woman smiled. It transformed her face — made her, for a heartbeat, decades younger, made her someone Ada had seen before, somewhere, in some context she couldn't seize hold of, a face from the edge of a dream—

"My name—" the woman began.

The storm took the rest. Wind screamed down the slope and filled the world with white noise, and by the time it eased the woman's lips had stopped moving. Her grip on Ada's hands had loosened. Her head had tipped back against the boulder. The dark eyes were still open, still fixed on Ada's face, and they were still, and the spreading blood had stopped spreading.

"No," Ada said. "Hey. No. Stay with me. I can get you up there, I can—"

But she was a gatekeeper's daughter, born and raised on a mountain that killed careless travelers every winter, and she knew death when she held it. She checked anyway — throat, breath, the works — with cold professional hands while some other part of her screamed. Nothing. The woman was gone, gone between one gust and the next, gone with her name unspoken, gone in the space of a sentence.

Ada knelt in the snow holding a dead stranger and a leather-wrapped bundle, and the storm howled around her like something celebrating.

* * *

Getting the body up the slope alone was beyond her and she knew it, so she did what could be done: she weighted the corpse with stones against the scavenging birds, marked the spot with her spare lantern hook driven into the pine's roots, and climbed back to the road with the bundle strapped tight inside her coat, against her skin, where she could feel it with every heartbeat.

Back in the gatehouse, she built the fire up high, stripped off her frozen outer layers with shaking hands, and sat on the floor in front of the hearth with the bundle in her lap for a long time before she touched the cord.

The fire cracked. Wind battered the shutters. Somewhere in the walls the old timbers ticked and settled, the sounds the gatehouse made every night of her life, sounds that had always meant safety and tonight meant nothing at all.

The bundle was wrapped in oiled leather, old but well-kept, tied with waxed cord in a knot Ada didn't recognize — some clan knotting, maybe, the kind the lowland traders used. Inside, she could feel stiff shapes: boards, paper, something flat and rectangular. A book? A case?

*Don't show it. Don't sell it. Don't burn it.*

Grey cloaks. The Ashen Order kept agents in every tollhouse and market town in the highlands — everyone knew that, everyone arranged their lives around it the way they arranged their lives around weather. Grey cloaks asking questions. About what? About whom?

About a dead woman on the smugglers' road, Ada thought. That's what they'd ask about. And the only person who'd seen her was the keeper of the Thornfell gate, who had never lied to anyone in her life, whose ledgers were famous for their accuracy three valleys over, whose entire existence was built on the principle that things are recorded and records are kept and truth is the whole of the law.

*Don't answer,* the woman had said.

Ada untied the cord.

Inside the leather was a map. She knew that much immediately — the heavy paper, the fold-lines worn soft with handling, years of handling, hundreds of openings and closings. She unfolded it halfway across her knees and held it to the firelight.

Mountains. Inked in a fine, sure hand, ranges she almost recognized and ranges she didn't, drawn with elevations and passes marked in a script she couldn't read. Rivers like veins. And at the center, rendered in careful cross-section, as if the artist had peeled back the skin of the earth itself: a city. Underground. Tiered galleries descending into darkness, vaulted halls, stairways spiraling down past the bottom edge of the paper into blankness. Around the city's heart, someone had inked a single word in letters small and precise and somehow afraid:

*the Hollow.*

Ada's mouth went dry.

Every child in the highlands knew the Hollow — knew it the way children know things they're not supposed to, through the cracks adults leave unguarded. The buried city. The forbidden rhyme. *Down, down, under the stone, where the names went and didn't come home.* A story to frighten children straight, a superstition, a song with no meaning. Her father had sung it to her. Her father, who had gone silent as a grave whenever anyone real mentioned it.

This was not a song. This was a survey. Someone had measured the Hollow, walked it, mapped it, and folded the proof of it into oiled leather and carried it over the mountains in a storm.

Someone had died carrying it.

Ada looked at the map for a long time. Then she looked at the door, bolted twice, with the storm hammering it. Then at the window, black and blind. Then at the fire.

*They'll come for it.*

She should burn it. That was the sane thought, the safe thought, the thought any sensible person would have. Burn it, scatter the ashes, tell the grey cloaks whatever they wanted to hear, go back to her ledgers and her rituals and her comfortable loneliness. The woman was dead; the secret owed Ada nothing; the smart move was written in fire.

Instead, Ada refolded the map along its soft old creases, wrapped it in its leather, and retied the cord. Then she sat holding it, feeling its weight, feeling the shape of her life quietly breaking apart around her like ice before a thaw.

"You already know why," she whispered to the empty room.

Outside, far below in the valley, so faint she might have imagined it, a dog began to bark.
