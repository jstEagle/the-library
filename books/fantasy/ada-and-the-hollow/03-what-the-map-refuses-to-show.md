## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

The map would not lie flat.

Ada had weighted its corners with river stones, smoothed it twice against the tollhouse table, and still the leather curled at the edges like something trying to close itself. She'd thought, at first, that this was age — that whatever hide it was made from had spent so many years rolled tight around a stranger's secrets that it had forgotten how to be anything but furled. But by the third morning she began to suspect the truth: the map did not want to be read. It wanted to be carried.

She had not slept properly since the woman died in her arms. Sleep came in shallow pulls, and each time she surfaced she found herself back on the smugglers' road, snow in her mouth, the weight of a dying body going heavier against her chest. *You already know why.* Four words. She turned them over like stones in a creek bed, looking for what lived underneath.

She knew why strangers died on Thornfell road — cold, wolves, bad luck, the Order's long reach. She did not know why one of them should look at Ada Orsen Crane, gatekeeper of a pass nobody important crossed anymore, and say *you already know why* as though handing her something she'd been waiting for all her life.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick and bent over the map again.

It was drawn on leather, dyed a grey-brown that might have been the animal's own color or might have been deliberate — the color of ash, of old snow, of things deliberately made hard to see. The cartographer's hand was fine and sure. Mountain ranges marched across it in strokes so precise they seemed less drawn than remembered. Rivers ran as single unbroken threads. And there, in the lower third of the skin, beneath the roots of the mountains themselves, lay a city.

The Hollow.

Ada's finger hovered over it without touching. Even now, alone, with the door barred and the storm two days gone and no one for thirty miles but herself and the crows, she hesitated to name it. That was what the lullabies had done to her. They had taught her, before she could read, before she could count past twenty, that some names were doors and you did not knock on them without meaning to walk through.

Her mother used to sing them. That was the strange part — the part Ada had never examined until now, because examining it meant asking why a keeper's wife, a woman who mended nets and salted fish and complained about the damp in the tollhouse walls, would know songs the clans had agreed to forget. Songs the Order had paid good silver to see unwritten. *Down, down, where the lamps remember,* her mother would hum over the wash basin, low enough that Ada was never certain she'd heard it at all. *Down, down, where the names grow warm.*

She had asked once, aged seven or so, what the song was called.

Her mother had gone very still, hands buried in wet wool, and said, "It's nothing. It's just for getting children to sleep." And then she had sung it again that same night, softer, and Ada had understood even then that it was not nothing, and that it was not for sleeping.

Now she looked at the city on the map and the lullaby assembled itself in her memory line by line, verse by verse, matching itself to the inked streets like a key finding its wards. The map showed the Hollow as a set of concentric rings descending into the mountain's root — ring above ring above ring, roads spiraling inward and down toward a small marked circle at the center that bore no label at all. Just an empty circle, drawn with more care than anything else on the skin. Drawn, Ada thought, the way you'd draw a wound.

*Where the lamps remember.* The outer rings. *Where the names grow warm.* The center. The unlabeled circle.

She sat back. Her tea had gone cold hours ago; she drank it anyway, because ritual was the spine of her days and she had lost every other one since the woman on the road. Inspect the gate. Walk the wall. Count the stores. Log the weather. For eleven years she had kept Thornfell the way you keep a promise nobody checks on — faithfully, invisibly, because the keeping mattered and not the checking. Her father had done it before her, and his father, and somewhere back along that line the Cranes had stopped being people who guarded a pass and become the pass itself: stone with a heartbeat.

And now a dead woman had put a map of the forbidden city into her hands and told her she already knew why, and every ritual Ada owned felt suddenly like a child's game played in the shadow of something enormous.

There were other markings. She'd catalogued them methodically, because method was how she held fear at arm's length. A seal stamped near the edge — wax, once, though only the ghost of it remained, pressed under a second layer of leather that had been stitched over the map's backing. A trail of tiny symbols running from the mountain's southern face down into the Hollow's rings, too regular to be decoration. And in the corner, in a hand smaller than the cartographer's main script, a single line of text she could not read — not because it was faded, but because it was written in characters that predated any alphabet the lowland schools taught. Old clan-script, maybe. Older.

The fire popped. Ada jumped, cursed herself for jumping, and was still cursing when someone knocked at the tollhouse door.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. Bren's rhythm — he'd invented it years ago, joking that if the Order ever learned to knock in threes-and-twos, he'd simply invent a new one, and wouldn't that be a fine life, always one pattern ahead of the hangman.

She wrapped the map in its oilcloth and slid it under the floorboard by the hearth before she opened the door. She didn't decide to do it. Her hands decided, and she let them, and only afterward did she notice what that meant: she was hiding things from Bren now. Keeping things from the one man who brought wood and salt and news up a mountain nobody climbed.

That was new. That was yesterday-new.

"Storm's passed," Bren said, stamping snow off his boots on her threshold without waiting to be invited, because fourteen years of trading below the pass had dissolved whatever boundary a threshold was supposed to hold. He carried a sack of flour over one shoulder and a look on his face she couldn't read, which was unusual, because Bren's face was usually a public document. "Brought you flour. Brought you gossip. Brought you—" he paused, setting the sack down, "—a question I've been chewing on for two days."

"Ask it."

"The woman." He didn't look at her. He looked at the fire, at the kettle, at the wall of pegged tools — everywhere but at her, which was unlike him, and therefore information. "The one you buried. What did she give you?"

The silence stretched. Ada heard her own pulse in it.

"Nothing," she said.

Bren nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he's been told a lie he expected and is disappointed anyway. "Nothing," he repeated. "She crawled half-dead up a smugglers' road in the worst storm in six years, died holding your hands, and gave you nothing."

"People don't always die carrying things, Bren. Sometimes they just die."

"They do." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were steady and unhappy. "But this one was carrying something. I saw the shape of it under her coat when I helped drag her in. Flat. Long. Wrapped." He shrugged, and the shrug was terrible in its casualness. "Maybe the crows took it. Crows are thieves. Maybe the snow ate it. Snow eats everything up here eventually."

"You went through her things?" Ada kept her voice level. Keeper's voice. Gate voice. "You searched a dead woman's coat?"

"I checked her pockets for identification, which is what you do when someone dies on your road, so you can write to their people." His jaw tightened. "I didn't take anything. I'm telling you what I saw, Ada, because I've been chewing on it for two days, and because—" he stopped himself. Started again, quieter. "Because there are men in grey cloaks asking questions at the tolls below, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from them."

The room tilted slightly. Ada gripped the back of her chair. "Grey cloaks. Here?"

"Not here. Not yet. Kestrel's Ford, two days south. Asking after a woman traveling alone. Offering silver for information about what she carried." Bren's mouth twisted. "I told them I hadn't seen anyone. Which is true, technically, because she was dead by the time I saw her, and dead women aren't anyone."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're lying to me right now, and you're bad at it, and bad liars end up in graves with grey cloaks standing over them looking satisfied." He picked up his hat, put it down again. "I've known you fourteen years. You've never lied to me before. Not once. Not even about small things. It's one of the reasons I come up this mountain, if you want the truth — talking to you is like drinking clean water. So whatever she gave you, whatever you're hiding under that floorboard by the hearth—" Ada's breath caught, and he saw it catch, and something in his face closed like a shutter, "—you should decide soon whether I'm the kind of person you tell, or the kind of person you survive."

He left the flour. He took his hat. At the door he paused, back to her, and said, almost gently:

"For what it's worth — the shape under her coat. I know what maps look like. I've moved enough of them through worse places than this." A beat. "Not that it matters. Since she gave you nothing."

Then he was gone, boots crunching down the frozen path, and Ada stood in the middle of her tidy tollhouse feeling as though the ground beneath it had developed a slow tilt.

* * *

She did not sleep that night either. She took the map out and spread it under the lamp and made herself look at the thing she'd been circling for three days: the Hollow itself, rendered in ink older than her grandmother's grandmother.

The lullabies had never named it directly. Of course they hadn't — that was the whole architecture of forgetting, the cleverness of it. You didn't ban a song; you let it decay. You let mothers hum it half-remembered, syllables slurring, verses dropping away generation by generation until what remained was melody without meaning, a tune children fell asleep to that contained, folded inside it like a seed, the entire shape of a city. *Down, down, where the lamps remember* — and the map showed the outer ring lined with small marks, dozens of them, evenly spaced, and Ada understood with a chill that walked her spine that those marks were lamps. Lamps that had burned, once, in a city under the mountain. Lamps that remembered.

*Where the names grow warm.* The center circle. Unlabeled. She'd assumed it was a vault, a treasury, a throne. Now, tracing it with one fingertip, she thought: no. It's smaller than a treasury. It's the size of a heart. Whatever the Hollow had been — capital, refuge, grave — its true center was something you'd draw as an empty circle because naming it would be worse than leaving it blank.

And the war. The lullabies touched the war the way a tongue touches a broken tooth: briefly, carefully, around the edges. *The kings went under and the under won,* one verse went, which everyone sang without hearing, because it was nonsense, because wars weren't won by the people underneath them. Except the map, with its patient spiral descent, suggested that once upon a time somebody had indeed gone under — and stayed there. And won there. Or lost there. Or been erased there, which was a fourth thing entirely, and the one the Order burned books to prevent anyone considering.

Ada thought about the Ashen Order's actual work, stripped of its grey dignity. Everyone knew they kept the peace, settled disputes, maintained the archives at Greyharrow. Everyone knew they collected dangerous texts. Nobody asked what made a text dangerous, or noticed that the answer was always the same: a text was dangerous if it remembered. Not if it lied — lies were fine, lies were load-bearing. Only if it remembered.

A map of the Hollow was not contraband because it led to treasure. It was contraband because it was proof. Proof that the city existed, that the descent had happened, that the war's ending had a location — and locations could be dug up, and dug-up things could testify.

She pressed her palms flat against the leather, over the empty circle, and spoke aloud to the empty room, because eleven years of solitude make a woman honest in ways company would not.

"Who were you?" she asked the dead woman. "And why me?"

The map, curled stubbornly at its corners even under river stones, declined to answer. But as she leaned closer, adjusting the lamp, she noticed something she'd missed in three days of study: along the southern approach, where the tiny regular symbols marked the route down into the rings, one symbol differed from the others. Slightly larger. Inked over, once, by a hand other than the cartographer's — a later hand, a correcting hand. And beside it, in that same small later script, a word in trade-tongue she could read:

*Blood.*

Just the one word. No explanation. It sat beside the symbol the way a name sits beside a door.

Ada sat back, heart hammering, and told herself it meant a blood-price, a toll, a danger — anything except the thing it suddenly, horribly, seemed to mean. Because her mother had sung those songs. Because her mother had gone still as stone when asked. Because a dying stranger had looked at her — at plain Ada Crane, keeper of an unimportant gate — and said *you already know why*, and Ada had been too grief-struck and too frightened to ask the obvious follow-up: *know why what? Know why you? Know why me?*

The lamp flickered. Outside, far down the valley, so faint it might have been wind, a dog began barking — the way dogs bark when strangers come up a road at night.

Ada blew out the lamp, slid the map beneath the floorboard, and stood in the dark listening.

The dog kept barking. And it was not stopping.
