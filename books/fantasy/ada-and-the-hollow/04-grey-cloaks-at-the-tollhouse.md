## 4. Grey Cloaks at the Tollhouse

The storm passed in the night, and morning came up white and silent, the kind of silence that felt like a held breath. Ada stood at her window with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands and watched the light come over the pass, gilding the snowfields, touching the black iron of the gate two hundred paces below the tollhouse where she had lived for eleven years.

She had not slept. The map was under the third floorboard in her bedroom, wrapped in its leather, wrapped again in an old flour sack, weighted down with the spare hinge-pins she kept for the gate mechanism. She had checked on it four times between midnight and dawn, which was three times more than made any sense, because no one was coming to take it. No one knew she had it.

Except the dead woman had known. *You already know why.* The words kept surfacing like bodies in thawing water.

Ada drank the tea, dressed, braided her hair back the way she always did — tight, practical, nothing loose to catch on machinery — and went down to open the tollhouse for the day.

Her rituals were the same as they had been every morning for eleven years. She lit the stove. She counted the coin box from the night before — four coppers and a bent silver bit, a slow day even by Thornfell standards. She took the inspection ledger from its hook and walked the path down to the gate itself, boots crunching on ice-glazed stone, breath smoking.

The Thornfell gate was older than any kingdom that claimed it. A wall of grey mountain granite forty feet high, set into a notch in the pass, with doors of black iron banded in bronze, and above them, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, a carving no one could read anymore: a spiral, broken in the middle. The clans below said it was a snake. The clerks in the lowland capitals called it decorative. Ada's predecessor, old Master Halloway, had told her once, drunk on festival wine, that it was neither — that it was a seal, and the gate wasn't built to keep people out.

*It's built to keep something in,* he'd said, and then laughed at himself and never mentioned it again.

She ran her inspection the way she always did. Hinges oiled. Lock-ward turning smooth. Bolt seats clean of grit. Drainage channels clear of ice. She wrote each item in the ledger in her small, exact hand, dated it, signed it. *A. O. Crane, Keeper.* Eleven years of identical entries. Eleven years of a life so regular you could have set clocks by it, and she had loved it, or told herself she had, the way you love a room you've never left.

Today her hand hesitated over the signature. Just a fraction. Just long enough for her to notice herself noticing.

She finished the entry and walked back up to the tollhouse, and that was when she saw the riders coming up the smugglers' road.

Three of them, spaced wide, moving at the deliberate pace of men who wanted to be seen coming. They wore travelling cloaks with the hoods back despite the cold, and under the cloaks, when the light caught them right, she saw the grey — tunics dyed the flat, ash-grey that didn't reflect so much as absorb, the color of the Order the way the sky was the color of weather. Ashen Order. She'd seen them twice before in eleven years, both times passing through with sealed writs, both times polite in the way a drawn blade is polite.

They stopped at the bottom of the tollhouse steps. The one in front dismounted and handed his reins to another. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with a face like a folded map — all creases, nothing wasted. His eyes were pale grey too, or maybe that was just what she expected them to be.

"Keeper Crane." He didn't ask. He'd read the ledger postings, or someone had told him. "My name is Serel. This is Warrant Officer Vess and Officer Dunmore." He gestured at the other two without looking at them. "We're traveling under Order authority. May we come up?"

"You may," Ada said. Her voice came out level. She was surprised by that. "Mind the second step. Ice under the snow."

He minded it. They came up into the tollhouse, filling it — the room had always seemed adequate-sized until three grey cloaks stood inside it, and now it seemed like a cupboard. Vess stayed near the door. Dunmore drifted, casual as a cat, toward the window that looked down at the gate.

"Tea?" Ada asked, because offering tea was what keepers did, and doing what keepers did was armor.

"No, thank you." Serel removed his gloves finger by finger, unhurried. "We're looking for someone. A woman. Travelling alone, on foot, likely ahead of anyone else on this road. We believe she may have come through the pass in the last few days."

"The pass has been closed since yesterday noon," Ada said. "Storm off the high fells. Nobody's crossed since."

"We're aware of the storm." Serel smiled slightly, and it was a good smile, warm even, and Ada understood immediately that every part of it was manufactured, the way the bronze bands on the gate were manufactured — to look like ornament and function as restraint. "We're asking about before the storm. Two days ago. Perhaps three."

"I keep the toll log." She turned to the shelf, took down the book, and laid it open on the counter between them, because showing him the log was also what keepers did, and it cost her nothing, because the log was true. "Every traveller, in and out, name and business. You're welcome to read it."

He did. He read slowly, running one finger down the columns, and while he read, Dunmore drifted along the wall, looking at things. The rack of gate-keys, each on its numbered hook. The inspection ledgers, eleven years of them, spines cracked. The map of the pass routes that hung behind the counter — official issue, lowland cartography, showing the smugglers' road as a dotted line marked *impassable, unmaintained.*

Ada watched Dunmore look at that map and thought about the leather-wrapped thing under her floorboard, which showed the same mountains but none of the same roads, and whose dotted lines ran places no lowland cartographer had ever dreamed of, down and down, past the roots of Thornfell, to a city that existed only in lullabies.

*You already know why.*

Serel reached the last page. "No single women in the last week," he said.

"No."

"No one unusual at all."

"There was a tinker with a lame mule. There was a clan family going down to a wedding. There was Bren Colm, who's through here twice a month and always will be." She heard herself say Bren's name easily, naturally, and some cold, new part of her mind noted how easy it was, how the lie hadn't even needed building — she simply hadn't mentioned the woman, and everything else she said around that absence was technically true. *I keep the toll log. Every traveller is in it.* The woman wasn't in it. Therefore, by the logic of the book, the woman had never existed.

Serel closed the log gently, the way you'd close a hymnal. "Keeper Crane, I'll be direct with you, because I think you're a direct person. The woman we're seeking is a courier. She carries material that belongs to the Order — material taken from us. She is elderly and she is ill and we are genuinely concerned for her welfare in this weather." The concern in his voice was flawless. It was the best acting Ada had ever seen, and she had seen mummers' troupes at the autumn fair every year of her childhood. "If she passed this way — if she collapsed on the road, if anyone found her — the Order would want to know. For her sake."

For her sake. Ada looked at the creased face and the pale eyes and thought: *she died forty paces from my door with my mother's voice coming out of her mouth, and you want to know if I'll hand you the reason she died.*

"She didn't come through," Ada said.

Something shifted behind Serel's eyes. Not disappointment exactly. Recalculation. "May I ask how long you've held this post?"

"Eleven years."

"And in eleven years, has anyone ever lied to you at this counter?"

The question landed like a dropped anvil. Ada kept her face still. "People try. Smugglers understating their cargo. That's why there's a log, and weights, and a warden's oath. I don't rely on people's honesty. I rely on the record."

"A sensible philosophy." Serel put his gloves back on, one finger at a time. "Then you won't mind if we examine the record more thoroughly. Vess will want to see your correspondence file. Dunmore will walk the gate with you — standard procedure when the Order inspects a crossing. And I'll look at your quarters, if you'll show me up. All quite routine. The Order does an audit of every major pass every seven years; Thornfell is overdue."

It was not routine. She knew it was not routine the way she knew weather coming — in her teeth, in the ache of old breaks. But refusing would be worse than anything the audit could find, because refusal was a confession wearing different clothes.

"Of course," Ada said. "Mind the third stair. It talks."

* * *

Dunmore walked the gate with her and asked cheerful, stupid questions — how old was the mechanism, did the winch still work, had she ever had to close it against anyone — and Ada answered them all truthfully, because they cost nothing, and all the while she catalogued what he looked at. He looked at the hinges longer than a curious man would. He looked at the broken spiral carving and touched it, and when his fingers came away he rubbed them together like a man testing for dust. He looked down the cliff-face side of the gate, where the smugglers' road switchbacked away into the fog below, and he looked at it the way you look at a door you intend to come back to.

Upstairs, Serel spent twenty minutes in her quarters. She stood in her own doorway watching him do it, because leaving him alone was unthinkable and hovering was suspicious, so she hovered openly, arms crossed, and let suspicion and transparency cancel each other out. He opened her wardrobe. He looked under the bed. He lifted the mattress. He examined the floorboards — she watched his boot heel press each one in turn, methodical as rain — and when his heel came down over the third board from the window, the one with the hinge-pins on top of the flour sack, Ada discovered something about herself.

Her heartbeat didn't change.

That was the discovery. Not fear — fear was there, humming under everything like the wire in a fence. But on top of the fear, cool and separate, sat a small clear-eyed creature she had never met before, and it was watching Serel press the boards and thinking, with perfect calm: *he checks left to right. He'll find nothing today. Next time he'll start from the right. Move it tonight.*

Eleven years of telling the truth, and it turned out the machinery for lying had been installed in her all along, waiting, the way the gate's old mechanisms waited under their grease. One turn of the ward and everything moved.

Serel straightened, dusted his palms, and gave her the warm manufactured smile again. "Thank you for your cooperation, Keeper. You've been very helpful."

"I'm glad the Order thinks so."

He paused at the top of the stairs. "One more thing, and then we'll be out of your hair. The woman — our courier. If she contacted anyone in these mountains, anyone at all, we'd want to know. She had... associates. People who've helped her before." He let that hang. "And she had family, once. In this region. Long ago. It's possible she came looking for them."

The floor did not move. The walls did not move. Ada stood in her own doorway with her heart going like a slow drum and said, "This region's full of families. Half the lowland names started as clan names before the war."

"Before the war," Serel repeated softly. "Yes. Everyone's history starts there, doesn't it? Or stops." He descended the stairs. At the bottom he paused again. "You've been keeper here eleven years. Appointed young. Was it family before you? A mother or father in the post?"

"My predecessor was Master Halloway. No relation. He trained me six years and retired to the coast." All true. All safe. All beside the point, and she understood suddenly that this was the whole art of it — burying the one true dangerous thing under a fall of harmless truths, like hiding a blade in a drawer of spoons.

"Master Halloway." Serel nodded as though filing it somewhere permanent. "Well. We'll be at the Wayfarer's Rest in the valley for some days yet. Audits take time." He settled his cloak. "If you remember anything, Keeper — anything at all — you know where we are."

"I know where everyone is," Ada said. "There's a log."

For just a moment — half a heartbeat — the warmth fell off Serel's face entirely, and what was underneath was not anger but appetite, patient and vast, and then the smile came back up like a shutter and he tipped his head and went down the steps to his horse.

* * *

She watched them ride down the switchbacks until the fog took them. Then she went inside, bolted the tollhouse door, and stood in the middle of her own kitchen shaking — not much, just her hands, just enough — while the kettle she'd forgotten about sang itself dry on the stove.

They'd be back. Not tomorrow, maybe not this week, but back, and next time starting from the right-hand floorboard. *Move it tonight.* Where? Not the tollhouse. Nowhere in the tollhouse. Down to the gate? No — Dunmore had looked at the gate like a man memorizing it. Somewhere outside the whole grammar of her careful life. Somewhere only she —

*Bren.*

The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome. Twice a month, reliable as tides, and he knew every hollow and cache on this mountain better than she knew her own ledgers. He'd carried stranger cargo than paper. He owed her — she'd looked past the misdeclared cargo often enough, and they both knew it, and neither of them had ever named it.

But Serel had asked about associates. About people who help her. And Ada had heard herself give Bren's name freely at that counter, easy as breathing, *Bren Colm, twice a month, always will be* — had handed the Order the one thread that connected her to the outside world, and only now, standing in the dry-singing silence, did she feel the shape of what she'd done.

Below the tollhouse, far down through the thinning fog, the black iron gate stood shut against the pass, and beyond it the smugglers' road wound down into the valleys where Bren Colm was due in three days with the spring freight.

Three days. She looked at the floorboards, at the third one from the window, and began, very quietly, to plan how to move a dead woman's inheritance before the grey cloaks came back — and whether the only man she trusted was a lifeline or a leash.
