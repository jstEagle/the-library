## 5. Bren's Price

The storm had three voices, and Bren knew all of them.

There was the low one, the groan of wind shouldering against the Thornfell gate like a drunk trying a locked door. There was the high one, the whistle through the pass that sounded almost human if you'd been alone too long. And there was the middle voice — the hiss of snow filling every track on the smugglers' road, erasing the night's work before it was even finished.

He sat in the lean-to below the tollhouse with a cup of pine tea going cold in his hands and watched Ada's lamp move behind the gatehouse window. Back and forth. Back and forth. She was doing her rounds, the same rounds she did every night, the same order every time: the winch first, then the counterweights, then the seal-stone, then the little iron door at the base where nobody had knocked in eleven years.

Bren had watched her do it for four winters now. He could have done it blindfolded. He sometimes thought he could do it better than she did — faster, anyway — and then he thought about what it would mean to be the kind of man who took a lonely woman's only ritual away from her, and he drank his cold tea instead.

The coin was in his left boot. It had been there for twenty-six days.

It wasn't much to look at. Grey metal, unmarked, too heavy for its size, the kind of thing you'd find in a dead man's purse and think twice about spending. The man who'd given it to him hadn't looked like an Order man — no grey cloak, no ash-sigil burned into the collar — but he hadn't needed to. He'd had the voice. The flat, patient voice of someone reading from a list they'd memorized so long ago the words had stopped meaning anything.

*Watch the road below Thornfell. A courier will come down out of the pass or up from the south. She'll carry something wrapped in leather. You send word to the tollhouse relay within a day. This is the first payment.*

Bren had asked the obvious question. *What's she carrying?*

The man had looked at him the way you look at a mule that's learned to open gates. *You're not paid to know.*

And Bren — because he had debts in three towns and a name that opened doors he mostly wanted closed, and because a grey unmarked coin spends the same as any other — had said yes.

That was twenty-six days ago. The courier had come last night. Dying. In the snow. And the something wrapped in leather was now sitting in the pocket of a woman who made him tea when his hands were too frozen to tie his own laces, and who had lied to two grey cloaks this morning with a steadiness that had scared him more than the cloaks did.

He turned the coin over in his fingers. It caught no light at all. That was the trick of it — he'd never figured out how it did that.

*Send word within a day.* The relay was the hollow oak at the second switchback. He'd passed it twice since the stranger died. Both times he'd kept walking. Both times he'd told himself he'd stop on the way back.

The way back was now.

* * *

Ada opened the gatehouse door before he finished knocking, which meant she'd seen him coming, which meant she'd been watching the road herself. Guilt did that to people. Made them watch.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Storm's coming in off the north face. I came to tell you."

"I can smell it." She stepped back to let him in. "Tea?"

"You have any of the black leaf left? The stuff from the Kessrin trader?"

"That was two months ago, Bren."

"Worth asking." He hung his coat on the peg by the door — third hook, always the third hook, she'd never once commented on it and he'd never once used another — and sat at her table while she put water on. The map was what he was looking for, and he hated himself for how quickly he found it: the corner of oiled leather showing from under the loose floorboard by her bed, tucked the way she tucked everything, neat as a hymnal. She'd moved it. Last night it had been under the mattress. She was already learning to hide things.

That was his fault. That was the coin in his boot doing its slow work.

"Two riders came through yesterday," he said, casual as weather talk. "Grey cloaks. Asking after a dead woman."

"I know. They stopped here."

"And?"

"And I told them what I saw. A woman on the road, frozen, nothing on her but wet wool." Ada set two cups down. Her hands were steady. Her eyes weren't quite. "They didn't believe me."

"They don't have to believe you. They just have to leave." He wrapped his hands around the cup. "They went down toward Kessrin?"

"Toward the split stone. Bren." She sat across from him, and the lamplight did something honest to her face, stripped the keeper's calm off it. "Why are you really here?"

There it was. Four winters of trading small kindnesses across a toll table, and she could read him like a shipping manifest. He'd known women who couldn't do that after twenty years of marriage.

He had a speech prepared. He'd been composing it since dawn, walking up through the snow, turning phrases over like river stones. *I've got to tell you something, and you're not going to like it, and I need you to hear the whole of it before you decide anything.* Something like that. Something that let him feel like a man delivering information rather than a man confessing.

What came out instead was: "I was hired to watch for her."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Wind pressed at the shutters. Ada didn't move.

"Three weeks ago," Bren said. "A man met me at the ferry crossing below Hollowmere. Never gave a name. Paid me in these." He dug the coin out of his boot and set it on the table between them, where it sat like a dead thing. "Watch the road. Watch for a courier with a leather-wrapped bundle. Report to the relay at the second switchback. First payment up front, more on delivery."

Ada looked at the coin. Then at him. He watched her do the arithmetic — every shared meal, every winter he'd helped her haul seal-oil up the gate stairs, every time she'd stood at her window and watched his lamp come up the road and maybe, maybe, felt less alone because of it — and he watched the sum come out wrong.

"How long," she said quietly, "have you been reporting?"

"Never." His voice cracked on it, which was fine, because cracking was honest and he needed every scrap of honest he could get tonight. "I never sent a single message. I've walked past that relay forty times in three weeks. I kept telling myself I'd decide later. That's the truth, Ada, and I know how it sounds. I took their money and I told myself taking money isn't the same as working, and that's a lie men like me tell until it kills somebody."

"The woman on the road."

"She died fifty yards from where I was supposed to be watching." He made himself hold her eyes. "If I'd been at my post, maybe I'd have flagged the cloaks sooner. Maybe she'd have died anyway — she was half-dead when she reached you, anyone can see that. But maybe not. Maybe there's a version of this month where I'm not a coward and she gets further down the mountain."

Ada picked up the coin. Turned it. It refused the lamplight, same as it always did.

"This is Order make," she said. "Or close enough. Where would a ferry-crossing man get Order coin?"

"I wondered that too. He wasn't wearing grey. But he talked like them. Flat. Like he was reciting." Bren leaned forward. "Here's the part I keep circling. They hired me three weeks ago, Ada. Before your stranger ever set foot on this mountain. Which means they knew she was coming. They knew the route. They were waiting for her at the one place she had to pass."

"So they didn't follow her here."

"No. They were here first. Laid out like a snare." He tapped the table. "And a snare means somebody knew exactly which road she'd take, and when, and carrying what. That's not a hunt. That's an ambush with paperwork."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the first gusts of the real storm arrived, rattling the shutters like fists. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — lower, harder, the voice of the woman who held a mountain pass shut against the world.

"Why are you telling me this now? Why not just walk down to the relay tonight and collect your payment? One message, Bren. One message and your debts are gone and you never have to see this pass again."

Because you make tea for a man whose hands shake. Because you use the third hook. Because I have spent four years being the closest thing you have to a friend and I am about to find out whether that was ever worth anything.

"Because I chose," he said. "Twenty-six days of not choosing, and then yesterday I watched you lie to those cloaks to protect a dead woman's bundle, and I realized you'd already chosen for both of us without knowing it. So." He spread his hands. "I'm choosing out loud. Burn me for it if you want. I'd deserve it."

She set the coin down and slid it back across the table to him.

"Keep it," she said. "You may need to buy something."

"That's forgiveness?"

"That's practicality. We'll get to forgiveness." But the corner of her mouth moved, barely, the way it did when she was winning a fight against her own face. "Who else knows? About the hire?"

"Nobody. The man at the ferry, me, and now you."

"Then we have until the next payment is expected. How long?"

"He said thirty days from the hire. Day twenty-seven is tomorrow."

Ada nodded slowly, and Bren watched her mind go somewhere ahead of him, the way it always did — she was a keeper, she thought in walls and approaches and lines of sight. "Then whoever hired you expects silence by tomorrow night. And when they get it, they won't assume you're loyal. They'll assume you're dead, or bought by someone else, or—" She stopped.

"Or that I found the courier myself," Bren finished. "And decided she was worth more than their coin."

"They'll come up the mountain."

"They'll come up the mountain," he agreed. "And they won't send two riders asking polite questions this time."

The kettle boiled. Neither of them moved to pour. The storm found its full voice outside, all three of it, and the lamp guttered once in the draft under the door.

Ada rose, crossed to the loose floorboard, and lifted out the leather-wrapped map. She didn't unwrap it. She just held it against her chest the way you hold something that's either a treasure or a wound, and Bren honestly couldn't tell which she thought it was.

"I need to go down the mountain," she said. "Tonight, before the passes close. Whatever this is, it doesn't stay in Thornfell."

"The north roads will be white by midnight. Even I wouldn't—"

"Then show me the roads you *would* take." She looked at him, and there it was, the thing he'd been dreading and hoping for in equal measure: trust, offered plain, to a man holding a traitor's coin. "You know the smugglers' grammar of these mountains, Bren. Teach it to me. Fast."

He looked at the coin on the table. Twenty-six days of cowardice, weighed against one sentence of a woman asking him for help.

"Well," he said, standing, reaching for his coat. "First lesson. Never take the road they're watching. Second lesson — there's always a road they're not watching. Third lesson costs extra."

"We'll settle accounts after the storm."

"See, that's what worries me," Bren said, and followed her out into the white.

Behind them, on the table, the grey coin sat in the lamplight, refusing to shine — and down at the second switchback, under the hollow oak, the relay waited for a message that was now never coming, and whatever waited beyond *that* was already climbing.
