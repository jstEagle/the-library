## 6. Down the Smuggler's Road

**Ada**

They left the tollhouse before dawn, in that grey hour when even the crows hadn't decided to be awake yet.

Ada carried a pack she'd packed three times. The first time it held everything she owned; the second time it held half of it; by the third, she'd learned what Bren meant when he said *"weight is a debt you pay every step"* — so she took bread, oilskin, flint, her father's old climbing iron, and the map wrapped tight against her ribs where no strap could press it. Everything else stayed on the shelf like a life she was lending out.

She paused at the gate on her way down.

It was habit more than anything. She put her palm to the cold iron of the Thornfell gate, felt the mountain's breath come through the seams, and said the words she'd said every morning for eleven years: *"Shut and staying shut."* Except this morning her voice cracked on *staying*, and she hated herself for it.

"You're not locking it," Bren said behind her. Not a question.

"I'm leaving it shut. There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed, and didn't push it. That was one thing about Bren Corrigan: he knew which silences were load-bearing.

They went down the smuggler's road, which wasn't a road at all. It was a suggestion the mountain made sometimes — a goat track here, a dry streambed there, a stretch where the pines grew crooked because some old slide had broken their bones and they'd healed wrong. Ada had lived above this terrain her whole life and had never once seen it as passable. It was like discovering her own face had a second profile.

"Keep off the skyline," Bren murmured, angling them into a fold of shadow between two ridges. "Riders read ridgelines the way priests read scripture. Anything that moves up there gets interpreted."

"Interpreted as what?"

"As a target."

By midmorning they heard the first horn from the high pass behind them — a long, flat note that rolled down the valley and died against the rock. Then another, farther east, answering. The Ashen Order sweeping the passes, calling to each other across the stone like grey geese.

Ada's stomach tightened. "They're close."

"They're loud," Bren corrected. "Loud means they haven't found anything. Quiet's when you worry." He glanced back at her, and there was something almost gentle in the set of his mouth. "Rule one of my road, keeper: the mountain tells you everything if you stop expecting it to speak plain."

He said *my road* without irony. She was beginning to understand he meant it. This whole broken country — the slides and deadfalls and streams that vanished underground and came back somewhere else wearing different names — it was his scripture, and he read it fluently.

She followed him down into it.

* * *

**Bren**

The girl walked like a mule carrying glass.

Bren had spent two days telling himself that was unfair, and two days failing to believe it. Ada Orsen Crane had spent eleven years standing in one place, guarding one gate, performing the same rituals in the same order, and it showed in every step she took. She planted her feet like the ground owed her an argument. She looked at his secret paths the way a priest looks at a tavern.

But she kept up. That was the thing. When the trail dropped through the scree field below Widow's Tooth — loose stone to the knee, the kind of slope that ate ankles for breakfast — she didn't hesitate or complain. She watched how he placed his feet, and then she did the same, exactly, like a woman copying letters from a page. By the bottom of the field she was doing it without watching.

Fast learner. Stubborn as a frozen hinge. He'd known both facts about her for years, from across the tollhouse counter, but knowing a thing at a distance and knowing it at arm's length were different animals.

"Hold up." He caught her sleeve and pulled her into the lee of a boulder split clean down the middle, like the mountain had taken an axe to it. Ahead, maybe four hundred yards down-valley, a thread of smoke rose from the treeline. Grey. Thin. Deliberate.

"Cookfire?" she whispered.

"Cookfires are messy. That's a signal fire. They're marking the valley floor." He chewed on the map of the country in his head. "Which means they've given up on the high passes for now and decided we're coming down. Which means someone told them we'd come down."

Ada went very still. "Who would—"

"Later." He said it harder than he meant to. Because the truth was he already had a list of whos, and his own name was on it, and he wasn't ready to say that out loud. "There's a way around. It adds half a day and costs us a wet crossing. You game?"

"I'm not going back to stand at a gate while they burn whatever's on this map," she said. Her hand had gone, without her noticing, to her ribs. To the leather-wrapped shape under her coat. She did that constantly, he'd realized. Touched it like a bruise. Like proof.

"Half a day and a wet crossing it is."

They climbed instead of descended, cutting east along a ledge so narrow Bren had named it years ago — the Liar's Walk, because it looked impossible and wasn't, or possibly looked safe and wasn't; he'd never settled on which lie it told. Below them the signal smoke thickened. Somewhere a horn sounded again, closer, and Ada's jaw set in that way he was starting to catalog: not fear exactly. Fear with its sleeves rolled up.

"You've done this before," she said as they edged along. "Run."

"Once or twice."

"From whom?"

"The kind of people who don't stop at tollhouses." He let a few steps go by. "Debts, mostly. Debts and a man I used to be. The road's good for losing both."

"And did you lose them?"

"No," he said honestly. "I just got far enough ahead that they stopped mattering daily."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh, strangled at birth. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in eleven years."

"That's depressing, keeper."

"Don't call me keeper. I'm not — I left the gate."

"You left the gate," he agreed. "You didn't stop being its keeper. People like us don't get to resign. We just change what we're keeping."

She looked at him sideways then, really looked, wind whipping a strand of dark hair across her face, and Bren discovered he'd said too much true things in a row and should probably shut up for a mile or two.

He didn't. That was the trouble with mountains. They made a man talk.

* * *

**Ada**

The wet crossing nearly killed them both.

The river came down from a glacier somewhere above, and it ran the color of skimmed milk, fast and vicious with cold. Bren found a place where two fallen pines had wedged themselves across a gorge, forming something that technically qualified as a bridge the way a rumor technically qualifies as news.

"One at a time," he said. "Weight in the middle. Don't look down at the water — look at me."

"Why wouldn't I look at—"

"Because everyone looks at the water, and the water wins."

He went first, easy as a man crossing his own kitchen, and stood on the far bank with his hand out. Ada stepped onto the pine trunk and immediately understood the rule about the water. It pulled at the eye like a wound pulls a thumb. The whole white roar of it, ten feet down, hungry and indifferent.

*Look at me.*

She looked at Bren. His hand, steady in the grey air. His eyes, which were lighter than she'd ever bothered to notice from across a tollhouse counter — river-stone green, unremarkable until they were the only fixed point in a tilting world. She crossed the whole span looking at nothing else, and when her boots hit stone on the far side her knees buckled and his grip closed on her forearm and held until they remembered their job.

"Water wins," he said, letting go.

"Water wins," she agreed, teeth chattering.

They walked on soaked to the thigh and shivering, and something strange happened in the next hour, which was that Ada stopped being afraid of the Order for a little while and started being afraid of how much she didn't hate any of this. The cold, the climb, the constant low-grade terror of pursuit — it should have been misery. Instead there was a clarity to it. Eleven years of ritual had been a kind of sleep, she realized. Same walls, same words, same weather seen through the same arrow-slit. Two days on Bren's road and her senses had come back online like lamps lit room by room. She could smell pine resin and wet stone and, faintly, woodsmoke from a valley away. She could hear which birdsong was alarm and which was gossip.

"Teach me," she said.

Bren glanced back. "Teach you what?"

"This. The grammar. You talk about the mountain like it's saying things. I want to understand."

Something moved across his face — surprise, then a pleasure he tried to smother and failed. "All right. Lesson one." He pointed at a slope of scree ahead, grey and innocent. "See that fan of loose stone? Fresh. See how the stones are pale, unworn? Means it slid recently. Means either rain or something shook it loose. Rain, fine. Something shook it—" he tapped two fingers against his palm, *riders, riders* — "we go quiet and wide."

"Lesson two?"

He crouched and turned over a flat stone near the trail. Beneath it, pressed into the mud, a hoofprint, days old, filling slowly with seep-water. "Old print fills with water. New print's dry inside. Count the water, count the days." He stood, dusting his hands. "The mountain keeps records better than any clerk. It just doesn't file them where you'd expect."

"You're enjoying this," she accused.

"Immensely."

And there it was again — that warmth in her chest that had no business existing, given the circumstances. Given the dead woman whose face she couldn't stop seeing. Given the horns sounding behind them. Given the fact that Bren had lied to her, badly, about recognizing the Hollow, and she hadn't forgotten it, only shelved it, the way you shelve a knife within reach.

But when he smiled like that — quick, crooked, gone again — she understood why smugglers survived. Not because they were hard. Because they were pleasant company in terrible places, and terrible places were most places.

* * *

**Bren**

They made the hollow under Raven Crag as the light went copper, a shallow cave behind a curtain of hanging roots, and Bren allowed himself the luxury of a small fire because the alternative was Ada freezing to death and him having to explain that to nobody.

She sat across the flames turning her hands, and he watched her do the thing she did — touch her ribs, check the map was there, touch her ribs again. A rosary with one bead.

"Ask me," he said.

Her head came up. "What?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for two days. Ask it. It'll keep better outside your head than in."

The fire popped. She fed it a twig, buying time, a habit he recognized from himself.

"You knew the map," she said finally. "When you saw it. In the tollhouse. You said you didn't, and you're a bad liar, Bren Corrigan, so don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. What did you recognize?"

He'd known this was coming since the moment he'd lied. Lies between people traveling together were like splinters — ignored, they worked deeper.

"The seal," he said. "On the wax. The broken tower with the star over it. I've seen it before, on papers that passed through my hands years ago. Papers I shouldn't have touched."

"What papers?"

"Order requisitions. Burn orders." He kept his voice level. "There was a purge, maybe fifteen years back. Villages, archives, a monastery up past the Sorrow Lakes. Everything with that seal on it was supposed to burn. I was young and greedy and I carried some of the cargo that did the burning, and I told myself it was just books." He looked into the fire. "So yes, keeper. I recognized it. And I lied, because the last time I saw that seal, people died, and part of me hoped you'd never ask."

Ada was quiet a long time. The roots at the cave mouth swayed in the wind like slow thoughts.

"Fifteen years ago," she said at last, carefully. "My mother disappeared fifteen years ago."

The words landed in the fire and burned there.

"I know," Bren said quietly.

"How do you know?"

"Because I did the arithmetic the night you showed me that map, and I haven't slept properly since." He met her eyes across the flames. "I don't know what your mother has to do with the Hollow, Ada. But I know the Order doesn't send riders and horns and signal fires after a dead woman's knitting. Whatever she was carrying, they've wanted it buried for fifteen years, and now it's strapped to your ribs, and I'm—" he exhaled, "—I'm glad it is. God help me. I'm glad it's you and not anyone else."

She stared at him. The firelight did something complicated to her face — grief and fury and something warmer fighting for the same ground.

"You could still sell me," she said. "One word to those horns down the valley, and you're out of debt. Out of all of it."

"Yeah," Bren said. "I could."

"Why won't you?"

He thought about giving her a clever answer. He'd always been able to find clever answers the way other men found coins in couches. But the fire was small and the cave was close and she'd asked plainly, and somewhere in the last two days plain had started to feel like the only currency worth carrying.

"Because I watched them burn a monastery once," he said, "and I drove the cart, and I've been driving away from that cart for fifteen years, and I'm tired, Ada. I'm tired of being the man who drives the cart." He shrugged, helpless. "You're the first thing I've ever been on the right side of. I'd like to stay there a while."

The wind moved the root-curtain. Down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, a horn sounded — then another, closer than the last, and this time neither of them pretended it was far away.

Ada reached across the fire and squeezed his wrist, once, hard, and let go.

"Then we walk tonight," she said. "While they're reading the wrong valley."

Bren grinned despite everything, kicked dirt over the fire, and thought: *the cart stops here.*

Above them, on the crag's shoulder, something shifted — a shadow detaching from shadow, brief and deliberate, gone before either of them turned. A watcher, or a trick of the wind in the roots.

Only the mountain knew. And the mountain, as Bren liked to say, keeps records.
