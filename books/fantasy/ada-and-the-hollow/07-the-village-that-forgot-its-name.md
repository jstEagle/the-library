## 7. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The village had no name on any map Bren carried, and when I asked him what it was called, he shrugged and said, "Depends who's asking."

We came down into it at dusk, three days after the Split Stone road, following a goat track that narrowed until the mountainside itself seemed to be trying to shrug us off. My legs had stopped arguing somewhere around noon and settled into a dull, obedient ache. Bren walked ahead with the easy, rolling gait of a man who knew every stone in this range personally and had borrowed money from half of them.

"Rules," he said, without turning around. "Before we go in."

"I'm listening."

"You're not. You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're about to ask questions." He stopped and faced me. The last light caught the stubble on his jaw and the bruise-yellow remains of the cut along his temple — my fault, that, from the ambush, though he'd never once said so. "This is a clan village. Old clan. Older than Thornfell, older than the Order, older than whatever story they tell down in the lowlands about how the war ended. And they have survived being old by doing exactly one thing very well."

"Forgetting?"

He blinked. "You've been here before."

"No. But I grew up on lullabies about places like this." I didn't say more than that. The map was wrapped against my ribs under my coat, warm from my body, and I had taken to touching it the way you touch a bruise — not because it helps, but to check whether it still hurts.

"Then you know the rules better than most," Bren said. "Don't say the word *war*. Don't say *Hollow*. Don't say *Order* — they'll hear it even if no grey cloak has come through here in twenty years. Don't ask anyone's age. Don't ask why the shrine by the well has no idol in it. And for the love of everything, don't ask them their name for this place."

"Because they don't have one?"

"Because they do," Bren said, "and they'll lie about it, and lying makes people unfriendly." He started walking again. "We need a roof, food, and maybe a look at your shoulder. That's all. In and out before dawn."

I touched the map through my coat. "And if someone recognizes it?"

He was quiet for three steps. "Then we leave faster than we came."

* * *

The village sat in a bowl of terraced hillside like something the mountain had cupped in its palm and then tried to forget it was holding. Stone houses with turf roofs. Smoke rising thin and straight. A well at the center with a low stone shrine beside it — and yes, an empty alcove where an idol should have stood, swept clean, deliberately clean, the way a scar is deliberate.

People looked at us the way I imagined fish look at birds: briefly, warily, and with a private certainty that we would soon be gone.

An old woman feeding chickens spoke first. "Travelers." Not a question. Not quite a greeting either.

"Two," Bren agreed. "One of us is limping."

"Both of you are limping," she said. "It's the road." She studied me a moment longer than she studied him. "You have gatekeeper hands."

I looked down at my own hands as though they might explain themselves. Calluses from the winch. Ink stains from the ledgers. The particular wear of a person who turns the same iron key ten thousand times.

"I keep a pass," I said carefully.

"Someone keeps every pass," she said, which was not agreement, and turned back to her chickens, and that was apparently the whole conversation.

Bren got us a room in the longhouse by trading salt and a coil of good rope, and by not asking a single question, which I began to understand was the local currency, rarer and more valuable than coin. We ate barley stew with a family who did not introduce themselves and did not ask our names, and the silence around the table was not hostile. It was practiced. It had the smoothness of a thing handled daily, worn soft at the edges like a tool grip.

It was the children who broke it.

There were four of them, ranging from a toddler to a girl of perhaps nine, and after the adults withdrew to their evening work, the girl drifted over to where I sat mending a strap on my pack. She watched my hands for a while.

"You're from outside," she said.

"Far outside."

"My grandmother says outsiders remember things wrong." She said it without accusation, the way you'd report weather. "She says remembering wrong is safer than remembering right, but she won't say right about what."

My needle stopped. "What does she say happens to people who remember right?"

The girl considered this with great seriousness. "They go pale," she said. "And then they go away. Like Great-Uncle Tam. He remembered something right and now there's just a stone for him." She pointed out the window toward the shrine by the well, and my stomach dropped, because the empty alcove wasn't empty of meaning at all. It was full of it. Every uncarved space was a name.

"Do you know what Great-Uncle Tam remembered?" I asked, and heard myself breaking Bren's rules, unable to stop.

The girl shook her head. "Nobody does. That's the point." Then, brightening: "Do you know any songs? From outside? We only get the same ones."

I sang her the first verse of a threshing song from the lowlands, harmless as bread, and she sang back two verses of something else — a counting rhyme, the kind children chant while skipping rope, and I froze halfway through the second verse because I knew it. Not the words. The *shape* of it. The cadence was the cadence of the forbidden verses my nurse used to hum with her lips pressed shut, the ones that got her dismissed from service when the Order's inspectors found her humming them to herself while kneading dough.

"*One for the door and one for the deep,*" the girl chanted, rope-less, skipping anyway, bare feet slapping stone. "*One for the ones who fell asleep. One for the name that isn't said, and one for the hand that—*"

"That's enough, Sela."

Her grandmother stood in the doorway with a basket on her hip, and her voice wasn't sharp. It was worse than sharp. It was flat and final, the voice of someone closing a shutter against a storm that has come before.

The girl went. The grandmother stayed, looking at me, and I saw her decide something — decide *not* to ask what I'd been about to say, or what the girl had been about to finish. Her jaw worked once.

"You should sleep," she said. "Travelers sleep early here."

* * *

But I couldn't sleep. The longhouse loft was warm and smelled of wool and smoke, and Bren was already breathing slow below me, and I lay staring at the underside of the turf roof with the map's weight against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Finally I unwrapped it. Just a corner. Just enough to see the seal.

I'd been turning it over in my mind for days: the wax impression pressed into the leather backing, a design I couldn't place — a circle broken by a vertical line, with three small marks descending beneath it like drops falling from a wound. I'd assumed it was a merchant's mark, a smuggler's guild stamp, something Bren would recognize and pretend not to.

In the dark, holding it up to the strip of moonlight through the smoke-hole, I noticed for the first time that the seal wasn't stamped into the leather at all.

It was stitched. Thread, fine as hair, dyed the same color as the backing so that it vanished unless the light came at it sideways. Someone had sewn the seal into the map itself, hidden the way the girl's grandmother hid names, the way the whole village hid its memory — not erased, but folded inward, kept alive by the act of concealing it.

And the light coming sideways through the smoke-hole shifted, and the stitched circle resolved into something I suddenly understood, and my breath went out of me.

A circle broken by a line. A gate, seen from above. The three marks beneath it were not drops.

They were steps. Three steps down into the earth.

It was a map-mark. The kind cartographers use for a descent. For a way *in*.

"Put it away."

Bren's voice, low, from below. I hadn't heard him wake. I hadn't heard him move. He was standing at the foot of the ladder with his hand resting on the rungs, and his face in the darkness was unreadable, which from Bren was unusual enough to frighten me more than any expression could have.

"You recognized it," I said. "Back at the tollhouse. You lied."

"I didn't lie. I said I didn't recognize it." He climbed up and sat across from me, knees nearly touching mine, and lowered his voice further. "Which was true. I recognized the *style*. There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I'm still traveling with you instead of selling you."

"That's comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be honest, which around here passes for comfort." He nodded at the map. "That stitching. That's clan-work. Mountain-work. Nobody else threads a mark that fine. Which means whoever made your map learned it here, or somewhere like here, and everything like here does the same thing with what it knows."

"Folds it in."

"Folds it in and prays nobody asks." He rubbed his eyes. "Ada. Listen to me. These villages aren't ignorant. They're *amputated*. Somebody cut the memory out of them so cleanly they don't even feel the phantom limb anymore — they just know that reaching for it hurts, so they teach their children not to reach. That took generations. That took *work*. And the Order calls that work peace."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I've sold things to villages like this for eleven years," he said, "and I finally asked one old man, six years ago, why his people wouldn't take cloth dyed grey. Just grey. Any other color. He looked at me the way that grandmother looked at you tonight, and he said, 'Grey is a uniform,' and then he never spoke to me again, and I lost the account, and I decided I didn't want to know." His laugh was short and had no humor in it. "Deciding not to know is a luxury, Ada. I've been living on it for years. You're the reason I'm going broke."

Below us, the household slept. Somewhere a dog turned over. The mountain wind moved across the turf roof with a sound like a long exhale.

"Bren," I said. "The seal. If it's clan-work — if it marks a way down — then someone from the clans made this map. Someone who knew the Hollow. Someone who wanted it found badly enough to hide the finding inside a hiding."

"Yes."

"And the woman who gave it to me—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "She said *you already know why*. To me. A stranger. On a road in the middle of nowhere."

Bren was silent for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had gone gentle in a way I hadn't heard from him before, and gentleness from Bren was somehow the most alarming thing yet.

"Then maybe she wasn't a stranger," he said.

Before I could answer, there was a creak on the ladder, and the grandmother was there, lantern unlit in her hand, standing at the edge of the loft. How long she'd been listening I couldn't tell. Her eyes went to the map in my lap — to the corner of it, to the stitched seal catching the moonlight sideways — and I watched the color leave her face the way water leaves sand.

She went pale.

Not the ordinary pallor of an old woman woken in the night. It was the pallor the little girl had described, the pallor of remembering-right, of a wound opened after decades of careful scarring. She put one hand against the ladder's post to steady herself, and her mouth shaped a word — a single word in the old clan tongue, soft as a prayer, too soft for me to catch —

— and then she looked at me, really looked, at my face, my gatekeeper's hands, the map in my lap, and whispered in the common tongue:

"Where did you get your mother's seal?"
