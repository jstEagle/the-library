## 8. Lullabies With Teeth

The elder's name was Maren, and he did not sleep that night.

Ada knew because she didn't either. She lay on a pallet in the guest hut — the same hut Bren had been carried into, still feverish, his bandaged shoulder seeping through the linen every few hours — and listened to the old man's footsteps crossing the frozen yard again and again. Out to the well. Back. Out to the shrine at the village's edge, where the fire never went out. Back.

A village with no name had no reason for a man like Maren to be awake. But he was.

Near dawn, the door flap lifted and his face appeared, silvered by snowlight, creased with something between apology and dread.

"You're up," he said, though it wasn't a question. He'd heard her not sleeping too, then. "Come. Bring the map."

* * *

The shrine was smaller than it looked from outside — a low stone room sunk half into the hillside, its ceiling black with decades of hearth smoke. The eternal fire burned in a ring of river stones, and around the walls hung things Ada couldn't immediately parse: strips of bark scored with symbols, bundles of dried herbs tied in red thread, and one long shelf of small clay figures, each one faceless.

Maren saw her looking at them.

"We make them without faces," he said, "because faces are what we're not allowed to keep."

"Whose faces?"

He crouched by the fire and held his palms over it, though the room wasn't cold. "Sit, keeper."

"I'm not—"

"You carry a gate-seal and a Hollow chart. Sit."

She sat. The stone floor bit through her trousers. She unwrapped the map from her coat and laid it between them, and Maren looked at it the way a man looks at a snake he knows is venomous but can't remember which end bites.

"My grandmother taught me rhymes," he said at last. "The way all grandmothers here do. You know them?"

"Some." The words came out strange in her mouth. "My mother sang them. Or — someone sang them. I was very small."

"Everyone says *someone*. That's how they survive, these songs. No one owns them, so no one can be punished for them." He turned the map over, studying the seal pressed into the wax at its corner — the coiled serpent biting its own tail, the mark Ada had seen a hundred times now without understanding. "But there's one verse we don't sing anymore. Not even badly. Not even drunk. My grandmother made me swear I'd let it die with her."

"And you swore."

"I swore." He fed a twist of bark to the fire. "And then you walked into my village wearing that woman's face on your chart, and I've been walking circles all night trying to decide which promise matters more."

"The one to the living," Ada said. "Always the one to the living."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Something in his expression shifted — not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of a man finding another person standing in the same prison cell.

"Then listen," he said. "And understand that when I'm done, you'll wish I hadn't."

* * *

He sang the first rhymes the way children sing them — quick, rhythmic, almost cheerful, the melody doing most of the work while the words slipped past like stones under fast water. Ada knew them. Of course she knew them. Her body knew them before her mind caught up; her foot tapped the rhythm before she could stop it.

*Down where the mountain keeps its dead,*
*the doors are shut and the words unsaid,*
*and what was won was never won,*
*and what was done was never done.*

"That one," Maren said, "every child in every clan village knows. They think it's about ghosts. It's a good song for ghosts. Ghosts don't ask questions."

"What does it actually mean?"

"It means the war didn't end the way the books say. It means the ending was *written*, the way you write anything — by whoever held the pen." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report weather. "The victors didn't just defeat the Hollow. They unmade it. Burned the records, killed the keepers, and then — this is the part the Order will kill you for knowing — they went into the villages and took the memory itself. Not with swords. With songs. They gave us new rhymes to replace the old ones, and the new ones were sweet enough that mothers taught them gladly, and within three generations nobody remembered that remembering had ever been possible."

Ada thought of the village below: the blankness in people's eyes when Bren asked about the war, the elder who'd gone pale at the map's seal. A whole community built on an amputation, calling the scar tradition.

"The verse you don't sing," she said. "The one you swore would die with you."

Maren didn't answer right away. He reached up to the shelf of faceless clay figures and took one down, turning it in his fingers.

"When I was seven," he said, "I asked my grandmother why our figures have no faces. And instead of answering, she sang me the forbidden verse. Once. Only once. Then she held my hands over the fire — not to burn me, just close enough to hurt — and told me that if I ever repeated it aloud, the Ashen Order would come, and they wouldn't burn the village. They'd burn the *people*, one at a time, until someone confessed to teaching it. So I kept it. Sixty years." His thumb traced the smooth blank clay. "You want to know why this village has no name? Because names attract questions. Questions attract grey cloaks. We cut off everything that could be asked about, and called it peace."

"Maren."

"I'm getting there." He set the figure down. "The forbidden verse isn't about the war. It's about the women who carried the map out. The couriers. There were twelve of them, in the last days — twelve keepers of the Hollow's memory who scattered into the mountains so the truth wouldn't die with the city. The Order caught ten. The verse is a counting rhyme. A warning to children: if a stranger asks to shelter, look at her face first."

"Why her face?"

"Because," Maren said, and his voice dropped as if the walls themselves might inform on him, "the verse describes the ones who got away. It was written so the clans would recognize them. Protect them. And the Order learned the verse too, eventually, which is why we stopped singing it — because a protection became a hunting description, and singing it was painting targets."

He closed his eyes. And then, in a voice gone thin and careful, he sang.

*Two went east where the snow lies deep,*
*two went south where the rivers sleep,*
*one went down and one went under,*
*and one walks still — and she wears thunder*
*in her eyes and ash upon her brow,*
*a scar like a river on her cheekbone now,*
*grey going white at the left temple's crown,*
*and she'll die on a road before she'll drown.*

The last note faded into the fire's crackle. Ada discovered she was gripping her own knees hard enough to ache.

"A scar," she said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through the shrine wall. "Like a river."

"On the left cheekbone," Maren said quietly. "Yes."

The dying woman's face rose in Ada's memory with terrible clarity — clearer than it had been in the moment, the way death sharpens things retroactively. The snow in her hair. The blood on her lips. And the scar: a pale, branching line down her left cheek, forked like a delta, like water finding its way downhill.

*A scar like a river on her cheekbone now.*

"She had grey at her temples," Ada whispered. "White, really. At the left side. I remember thinking it was odd, that the white was only on one side."

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised. He had known, Ada realized. He had known since the moment he saw the map's seal, maybe since the moment he saw her face. All those circuits of the frozen yard had been him deciding how much to say.

"The verse counts twelve," he said. "Ten taken. Two escaped east. One went down — into the Underfell, we always assumed, back to the Hollow. And *one walks still.*" He met her eyes. "That verse is three hundred years old, keeper. But the woman it describes — the scar, the temple, the road — the woman you found dying on the smugglers' road matched it exactly. Every detail."

"Coincidence," Ada said, and hated how thin it sounded. "Scars are common. Grey hair is—"

"Every detail," Maren repeated. "And she carried the Hollow chart, sealed with the courier's mark, and she found *you* — the daughter of the Thornfell gatekeeper, the one family in these mountains that has kept a Hollow post for nine generations — and she put it in your hands and died rather than let the Order take it back." He shook his head. "I'm an old man and a coward, and even I can do that arithmetic."

* * *

Ada walked back across the village alone, and the snow began again, fat slow flakes that landed on her shoulders and stayed.

She tried to think about the arithmetic. She tried to stay in the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was safe — probabilities, timelines, the logistics of a three-hundred-year-old verse matching a dead woman's face. If she stayed in the arithmetic, she could function.

But grief doesn't wait for proof. Grief is faster than proof. Grief had already arrived, had already unpacked its bags, was already sitting in her chest like a guest who intended to stay, and all the evidence could do was knock politely at the door and wait to be let in.

Because here was the thing she hadn't let herself think, not once, not since the pass:

Her mother had died when Ada was six. That was the story. Fever, the winter after her father's death, a grave marker her uncle had placed beside her father's at the edge of the Thornfell yard. Ada had visited it every year. She had pulled weeds from it. She had talked to it, some years, in the worst of her loneliness — told it about the gate, about Bren, about the small victories of a small life.

And the woman on the road had been — what age? Forty-five? Fifty? Old enough. The right age. And she had looked at Ada — Ada had replayed it a hundred times, the dying woman's eyes fixing on her face with something that wasn't confusion, wasn't the random focus of a stranger — and she had said:

*You already know why.*

Not *take this.* Not *run.* Not *help.* *You already know why.* As if the map were a letter addressed years in advance. As if Ada's hands were the destination the whole time.

And there was one more thing. One more thing she had filed away and not examined, because examining it hurt, and she was good at not hurting, she had built a whole life out of not hurting:

When the woman pressed the map into her hands, her fingers had been cold, yes, dying-cold — but before that, for one second, they had adjusted. Turned. Shifted their grip the way you shift a child's hand on a spoon, correcting, familiar. The grip of someone who had held those small hands before.

Ada stopped in the middle of the empty lane. Snow gathered in her hair. Somewhere behind her, the shrine fire burned, and somewhere ahead the guest hut held a wounded smuggler and a map that was starting to feel less like an inheritance and more like a message.

"If you're her mother," she said aloud, to no one, to the snow, to a dead woman on a road two valleys away, "then you let me believe you were dead for twenty years. You let me weed your grave."

The snow didn't answer. The snow never answered. That had been the whole point of her life — a gate that stays shut, a silence you can live inside.

But the verse kept turning in her head, patient as water.

*One walks still — and she wears thunder in her eyes.*

Thunder in her eyes. The dying woman's eyes had been dark, fever-bright, fixed on Ada with an intensity that had frightened her at the time and that she had attributed to death. But it hadn't been death. Death doesn't look at anyone like that.

Mothers do.

"No," she said, quieter, and started walking again, faster. "No. I need proof. I need—" 

She needed what? A letter? A confession? The dead didn't leave confessions. The dead left maps, and verses, and scars like rivers, and daughters who spent twenty years talking to an empty grave.

At the guest hut door, she paused with her hand on the flap. Through the hide she could hear Bren shifting in fever, murmuring fragments — numbers, distances, the grammar of roads she was only beginning to learn. Inside was the last person in the world she trusted, and she trusted him perhaps sixty percent, and tomorrow that number might matter enormously.

Because if the woman was her mother — *when* it was proven, not if, grief had already signed the papers — then everything changed. The map wasn't a burden passed to a stranger. It was a mother's work, finished by her daughter's hands. And the Order hadn't been hunting a courier.

They'd been hunting the last of the twelve. For twenty years. While Ada pulled weeds.

She pushed through the flap into the hut's smoky warmth, and Bren opened his eyes — glassy, feverish, but tracking her — and croaked:

"You've been crying."

"I haven't."

"You have." He tried to sit up, winced, settled. "What did the old man tell you?"

Ada looked at the map, wrapped in her coat at the foot of his pallet. At the seal. At the place where, underneath everything, she was now almost certain, an answer was waiting.

"He told me a children's rhyme," she said. "And I think my mother wrote it."
