## 9. Ambush at the Split Stone

I'd been telling myself for three days that the feeling in my gut was just the weather.

It wasn't the weather. I knew what weather felt like in these mountains — the pressure drop behind the ears, the way the birds go quiet and stupid before a storm. This was different. This was the old feeling, the one from my running days, the one that had kept me alive through nine years of smuggling everything from salt to secrets across the Thornfell range: the sense of being watched by someone patient enough to wait.

"You're doing it again," Ada said.

"Doing what."

"Walking like you're about to apologize to the ground."

I glanced back at her. She had the map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, under her coat, and she carried it the way she carried everything — carefully, like it might break, or like she might. Three days on Bren's secret paths and she still walked like a gatekeeper, heel-to-toe, deliberate, as if the mountain owed her an honest surface. It didn't. That was rather the point of secret paths.

"I'm not apologizing," I said. "I'm listening."

"To what?"

"To nothing. Which is worse." I crouched at the edge of the scree field below us and let my fingers rest on the stone. Cold. Damp with last night's melt, but no new water. "When the mountain goes quiet, it's usually because something's eating something else. When it goes quiet *and stays* quiet—" I stood, brushed off my hands. "That's when it's holding its breath for you."

Ada looked out over the valley we were about to cross. From up here you could see the whole sweep of it: grey rock, white patches of stubborn snow, and down in the middle distance, standing alone against the sky like a broken tooth, the Split Stone.

Every smuggler on this side of the range knew the Split Stone. It was a landmark older than any of the trade roads — a granite monolith sheared clean down its middle by some ancient violence nobody remembered, two halves leaning apart just far enough for a man to walk between them. The clans said the mountain god had cracked it open looking for something hidden inside. Smugglers said it was a meeting point, a place where paths crossed and deals got made, because anyone approaching from any direction could be seen for half a mile. You couldn't be ambushed at the Split Stone.

Which was exactly why I didn't like it.

"There's another way," I said. "West ridge. Adds six hours, maybe eight if the snowpack's gone soft."

"We don't have six hours." Ada said it flatly, without heat, and that was the worst part. She'd learned to count time the way the Order counted it — in how much ground their riders could cover. "You told me yourself. They swept the north pass yesterday. Today they'll sweep the valleys. We cross here or we're caught in the open tonight."

"I know what I told you."

"Then why are you arguing with yourself?"

Because the gut feeling was getting louder, and I couldn't put a shape to it, and I hated saying things out loud that I couldn't defend. I looked at the Split Stone again. Two halves of one rock, leaning apart. A gap between them just wide enough for a man.

Just narrow enough that a man walking through it couldn't turn around.

"Fine," I said. "We cross. But we do it my way. Single file, ten paces apart, and when I stop, you stop so fast you bite your tongue. Agreed?"

She nodded once. Gatekeeper's nod. Efficient. I was starting to read her the way I read weather, which should have worried me more than it did.

* * *

The descent took most of the morning. The path I chose wasn't a path — it was a seam, a line of loose shale and goat-trail switchbacks that dropped through the scree toward the valley floor, invisible from above unless you knew where the mountain had folded. I went first, testing each foothold, calling the bad ones back over my shoulder. Ada took instruction well. Too well, maybe; she moved like she'd been doing this all her life instead of three days, and I caught myself wondering what else about her was like that. What else came too naturally.

Her mother had walked these same mountains. That thought arrived uninvited, the way it had been arriving since the village, since the elder had gone pale at the map's seal. A woman who knew the Hollow's lullabies. A woman who died on a smuggler's road within a day's walk of where her daughter happened to be keeping a gate. Coincidence was a currency I'd traded in for years, and I knew counterfeit when I held it.

"Bren." Her voice, low, from behind. "You stopped."

I hadn't noticed stopping. That was the thing about the gut feeling — eventually it made your body decide things before your head agreed. I stood on the last shelf of rock above the valley floor, fifty yards of open ground between us and the tree line, and beyond the trees, the meadow, and beyond the meadow, rising out of it like a monument to every deal ever whispered in its shadow, the Split Stone.

And there was smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Not campfire smoke. This was thin and blue and wrong — the kind of smoke that comes off wet cloth drying over coals, the kind soldiers make when they want warmth but not light. Someone was in the lee of the western half of the Stone, tucked into the blind spot, the one place the landmark's famous sightlines couldn't reach.

"How many?" Ada breathed. She'd seen it too. Of course she had. She'd spent years watching a pass; she knew what smoke meant better than I did.

"One fire," I murmured. "Could be one man. Could be twenty sitting cold around one fire." I eased down onto my stomach at the shelf's edge and she copied me, close enough that I could smell pine resin and road dust in her hair. Through the trees, the meadow lay bright and empty and lying about itself. "There'll be watchers on the high side. There always are when someone camps in the blind spot. It means they're expecting company from the east."

"From us."

"From whoever uses the eastern paths. Which is me, and now you." I rolled onto my back and stared at the washed-out sky. My heart was doing something complicated. Nine years since I'd last felt it do that particular thing, and I'd hoped never to feel it again. "All right. New plan."

"We could go around—"

"No. If they've got the Stone covered, they've got scouts out, and scouts mean a perimeter, and a perimeter means going around puts us in the open longer, not shorter." I turned my head to look at her. Her face was pale but steady, and there was a set to her jaw I recognized from mirrors — the look of someone deciding to be brave before they feel it. "We go through. Fast, loud, and straight at them."

"That's your plan? Loud?"

"Loud is a weapon, Ada. Everyone out here is listening for quiet. Quiet means prey. Loud means—" I shrugged. "Means something they have to think about. And men who think are men who hesitate, and hesitation is the only friend we've got today."

She studied me for a long moment. "You've done this before."

"A long time ago. For people who paid better than you." I pushed myself up. "Stay behind me. Ten paces. When I run, you run. When I say *down*, you get small and stay small until I come back for you. Nod if you understand."

She nodded. Then, quietly: "Bren. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. Take the map and go. Don't stop for me. Promise me."

Everything in me wanted to say yes, because it was the tactically correct answer and because lying to her had gotten easier than I liked. Instead I heard myself say: "No."

Her eyes flashed. "Bren—"

"I don't make promises I intend to break, and I'm not leaving you on a battlefield to keep your hands clean. So don't ask me again, because the answer's the same every time, and we're wasting breath." I checked the knife at my belt — good steel, honed that morning out of habit — and the short blade at the small of my back, and tried not to think about how long it had been since either of them had tasted anything but rope and canvas. "Ten paces. Go on my mark."

I gave her thirty seconds of silence, then said, "Now," and ran.

* * *

The meadow was longer than it looked. Meadows always are. The grass came up past our knees in places, silvered with old snowmelt, dragging at our legs like hands, and I ran through it with my lungs burning and my ears stretched wide open, cataloguing sounds: our own footfalls, wind in the tree line behind us, and ahead — ahead, nothing. No challenge shouted. No horn. The silence of the campfire smoke pressing down on the whole valley like a held breath.

Fifty yards. Thirty. The Split Stone swelled in front of us, its two halves towering, the gap between them a blade of shadow cutting into the rock. Twenty yards. Ten.

"Down!" I threw the word over my shoulder and hit the grass myself, rolling, coming up in a crouch with both knives out.

Nothing. The gap yawned empty. The smoke rose thin and blue from behind the western half.

Then the high side spoke.

"Down here!" — a voice from the rocks above the eastern face, and a whistle, two notes, sharp, and the meadow stopped pretending to be empty.

They came out of the tree line behind us — four of them, five, moving fast and low, grey cloaks gone brown with trail dust, crossbows not raised but *ready*, held at the carry in that disciplined way the Order trained into its people. And from the rocks above the Stone, two more, already in position, already covering the gap we'd been aiming for. Seven. Maybe eight. I counted the way you count in a nightmare, quickly and without wanting to.

"Gatekeeper Crane." The voice came from the center of the patrol, from a broad man with a commander's knot in his cloak and frost in his beard. He wasn't even breathing hard. "By order of the Ashen Order, surrender the document you carry and submit to search. Do this and no harm comes to you. Refuse—" He spread his hands, almost gentle. "Well. You know how refusals end."

I put myself between him and Ada, which was stupid, geometrically speaking — seven armed men, and I chose to be the narrowest part of the equation. But some arithmetic isn't done with numbers.

"She doesn't have anything," I called back. "She's a toll-keeper out for a walk. I'm the smuggler. Anything worth arresting, arrest me."

"Bren Aldous Vey." He said my full name slowly, tasting it, and my blood went cold in a way the mountain air couldn't explain. "Yes. We know who you are. We know your debts, your routes, your little grammar of paths. We know you were hired in the spring to watch for a courier." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it worse. "We also know you never sent word. Which makes you either a fool or a traitor, and the Order forgives fools more easily. Step aside, Vey. Last kindness I'll offer."

Behind me, Ada's breathing had gone very controlled. I knew that control. It was the sound of someone deciding.

And the gut feeling — the one I'd been carrying for three days, the one I'd blamed on weather — finally spoke clearly, and what it said was: *they knew your name before you ever met her. Someone sold you before you ever had anything to sell.*

Later. Grieve later. Move now.

"Ada," I said, barely aloud. "The gap. On my word. Don't look back."

"Bren—"

"On. My. Word."

The commander sighed, theatrical, tired. "Vey. Please."

I looked at the seven crossbows and did the only mathematics left. Crossbows have one shot in them, and reloading a windlass on the move takes eleven seconds if you're good, fifteen if you're scared. Seven bolts, one volley, and then fifteen seconds of a world where the Order's men were just men.

"NOW!"

I broke sideways, screaming, arms wide, making myself enormous, making myself the loudest and most obvious thing in the meadow — and the world snapped. Bolts hissed. One tore through my sleeve and kissed the meat of my upper arm, hot as a brand. Another buried itself in the turf where my chest had been half a heartbeat earlier. And then I was among them, inside the volley's arc, inside the polite distance where crossbows become clubs, and nine years of peace fell off me like a coat.

I don't remember deciding to kill the first one. I remember his face — young, freckled, terrified — and I remember that he'd leveled his crossbow past me, at the running figure behind me, at *her*, and I remember my hand moving faster than my mercy. The knife went in under his jaw and came out and he sat down in the grass like a tired child and didn't get up, and some door inside me that I'd nailed shut for nine years blew open, and everything behind it was very old and very good at this.

The second one swung a crossbow like a maul. I took it on the shoulder — something cracked, mine or his, didn't matter — drove my knee into his thigh and put him down with an elbow I felt in my teeth. The third had a sword, an actual sword, and he was better than me, fresh against spent, and he cut a line of fire across my ribs before I caught his wrist against my shoulder and headbutted him into the middle of next week.

Through all of it, a sound behind me: Ada, running. Running *well*. Toward the gap.

A fourth man stepped out of her path — no, not out of it, into it, angling to intercept, crossbow dropping, hand going to his belt, to the long knife there, and he drew it back to throw, and I saw the whole geometry of it in one flash: the arc, the distance, her back, the gap, the way she'd never see it coming.

There was no decision. That's the truth I'll carry, whatever's left of me to carry it. There was no decision, no bravery, no noble arithmetic. There was just my body doing the thing my body had apparently been waiting nine years to do, which was to get between a thrown knife and Ada Orsen Crane.

It took me in the side, below the ribs, deep, a punch of cold that flared instantly to fire. I heard myself make a sound I didn't recognize. And then my hand had closed on the thrower's collar and pulled him into my knee, and my knife had found him twice, and he was down, and I was down on one knee beside him in the ruined grass, pressing both palms against my own side and feeling the warmth pump out between my fingers.

Three more Order men were converging. The commander stood back, watching, sword drawn now, and his kind smile was gone, and what replaced it was almost respect.

"Enough," he said. "Vey. Enough. You're dead where you stand, you understand that. Give me the girl and the map, and I'll leave your body for the clans to bury. That's more than you've earned."

I laughed. It hurt so much I saw white, and I laughed anyway, because somewhere behind me, in the shadow-gap of the Split Stone, I could hear boots on rock — Ada climbing, climbing fast, climbing *up* — and the commander heard it too, and his head turned, and in that half-second of turned attention I did the last ugly necessary thing: I scooped a handful of scree and grit and threw it into the nearest man's eyes, and rolled, and screamed upward with everything my shredded side had left:

"ADA! THE RIDGE! DON'T WAIT FOR ME!"

Because that was the cost. That was the whole bill, itemized. You choose a side, and the choosing spends you. Nine years I'd kept my hands clean and my name cheap and my debts the worst thing about me, and in one morning in a meadow I'd killed two men — three, the elbow probably counted — and bled out a fortune into the grass, all for a woman I'd known a season and a map I couldn't read, and the terrible, blazing clarity of it was that I would pay double.

Boots thundered in the gap above. A crossbow bolt sparked off stone where her head had been. Then her voice, high and cracking and furious: "*Bren!*"

"GO!" I got my feet under me, mostly, swaying, one hand plastered to my side, the other knife still out, facing the commander and his remaining men across twenty feet of trampled grass. Blood dripped off my elbow. My vision had gone soft at the edges, dreamlike, the meadow very green and very far away. "Come on, then," I told the commander, and heard my own voice slurring, and grinned anyway. "One of you's carrying me home. Let's find out which."

He raised his sword.

And the mountain answered.

Not a horn, not a shout — a *rumor* of horns, distant, doubled, echoing off the high crags to the west, the specific braided call of clan herders signaling across a valley, except no herder alive used that pattern anymore. The elder's rhyme flickered through my feverish head, the verse about watchers in the stones. The commander's men froze, heads turning, counting echoes, trying to decide whether the mountains had grown teeth.

In the confusion, hands grabbed me from behind — strong hands, sure hands, smelling of pine resin and road dust — and Ada Orsen Crane, who I had ordered to run and who had climbed halfway up the gap and then climbed back *down*, hauled my arm across her shoulders and took my weight like she'd been carrying heavier things all her life.

"You absolute idiot," she gasped into my ear, and dragged me toward the trees.

The last thing I saw clearly was the commander's face, turning from the phantom horns to us, calculating, and choosing — wisely, for him — not to follow into a tree line where clan horns might mean anything. The last thing I felt was my blood soaking through her sleeve, warm, insistent, patient.

The last thing I thought was that I'd finally found a debt worth defaulting on everything else for.

Then the green meadow folded up like a map, and the dark took me walking.
