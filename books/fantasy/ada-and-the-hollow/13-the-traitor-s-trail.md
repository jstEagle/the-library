## 13. The Traitor's Trail

Bren had learned a long time ago that the mountains kept secrets the way rivers kept water — loosely, and always moving downhill. So when he found the third marker in two days, he didn't waste time wondering *if*. He wondered *who*.

The marker was small. A cairn of stones by the fork above the Drowned Steps, restacked wrong. Bren had built that cairn himself six years back, three stones leaning north like a drunk against a wall, because that was the joke — his paths were supposed to look like nothing, like erosion, like accident. Someone had squared it. Made it neat. Made it *legible*.

Legible was the one thing a smuggler's trail could never afford to be.

He crouched beside it in the grey morning light and didn't touch it. Behind him, fifty yards down the switchback, Ada was washing her face in a snowmelt stream, her back to him, her map wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat. Her wound from the ambush at the Split Stone had closed to an angry pink line; his own knife-slash, taken across the ribs in the same fight, had gone hot and tight under its bandage, and every breath reminded him of the price of standing between a woman and a blade.

He'd do it again. That was the problem. He'd do it again without thinking, and a man who did things without thinking was exactly the kind of man who could be played.

Bren stood up slowly, ribs complaining, and looked at the squared cairn until he'd memorized what was wrong with it. Then he walked back down the path, kicking loose a stone here, nudging scree there, undoing the legibility as best he could.

Ada looked up as he approached, water dripping off her jaw. "You've got a face on."

"Do I?"

"The one where you're doing arithmetic you don't like." She wrung out her sleeve. "What is it?"

"Nothing. Trail's soft ahead. We'll want to move before the light gets good."

She studied him for a moment longer than he liked. She'd gotten better at that — at reading him — over these weeks on the road, and he couldn't decide whether he was proud or terrified. Probably both. Everything about Ada Crane came in both.

They moved.

* * *

The trouble with suspecting a traitor was that Bren knew too well how traitors were made. He'd been one, or near enough. Three years ago, deep in debt to a lowland lender named Corvess, he'd sold a route — not this route, not to the Order, but a salt road through the western fells — to men whose coin smelled of gunpowder. He'd told himself it was different. The buyers were just merchants. The clan whose path he'd sold would never know. Nobody bled.

Then somebody bled. Two of them. And Bren had spent the years since paying it back in the only currency he had: silence, caution, and never again being the man who made a hidden thing legible.

So when he saw the squared cairn, some old rotten part of him whispered, *you know how this starts. You know exactly how this starts.* A debt. A need. A small betrayal that feels like nothing. His debts were paid — mostly — but "mostly" was a word that had gotten men killed on better trails than this.

And there was another arithmetic, worse. Whoever was marking their route had to know the paths. Not guess them — *know* them. The Drowned Steps, the Hollow Bridge, the cairn forks: these were smuggler's grammar, taught mouth to mouth, sworn on salt and blood. An Order scout might stumble onto one marker. Three, in two days, placed correctly? That took a native tongue.

There were perhaps forty people alive who spoke it fluently. Bren was one.

He spent the whole day cataloguing the others and hating himself for how short the list was, and hating himself more for the name that kept surfacing at the top of it no matter how many times he pushed it down.

*You*, said the rotten part. *It's you. You're the plausible one. You're the one with the history. If Corvess came looking — if someone found the ledger with your name still half-inked in it — what would you have done, really, in a weak moment, on a bad night, with your ribs torn open and a woman's life stacked on top of yours like a weight you never asked to carry?*

He didn't have an answer he trusted. That was the worst of it. He genuinely didn't know what he'd have done, because he'd never been tested quite like this, and untested men were all innocent until the moment they weren't.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite, a fire the size of a cupped hand, because Ada insisted on fire and Bren had stopped arguing. She sat sharpening her knife with slow, even strokes, and the sound of it filled the dark like a second heartbeat.

"You found something today," she said. Not a question.

Bren fed a twig to the flames. "The trail's been marked. Cairns restacked. Someone's reading our route back to whoever's following."

Her knife stopped. "The Order."

"Or someone selling to the Order. There's a difference, and the difference has a name and probably lives in one of the villages we've slept in."

"The elder," Ada said quietly. "Old Maren's father. The one who went pale when he saw the seal."

"He went pale," Bren agreed. "Pale can mean scared. Scared can mean guilty. Or it can mean he recognized the map and knew what would come looking for anyone carrying it, and decided to get ahead of it." He turned the twig in the fire. "Fear makes people do tidy things. Squared stones are tidy."

Ada watched him across the little fire, and he felt her attention like a hand on the back of his neck. "You've done this before," she said. "Read trails. Read people leaving them."

"I've read people leaving worse things than stones."

"What aren't you telling me?"

And there it was — the question he'd been circling for two days, delivered flat and clean, because Ada didn't have the patience for anything else. He could lie to her. He'd lied badly once already, back at the start, about recognizing the map, and she'd never fully unbent from that. A second lie wouldn't bend her. It would break something.

But the truth — the truth was a door, and doors opened outward too.

"My debts," he said finally. "Before I ran the Thornfell side, I owed money to a lender in the lowlands. Corvess. Bad man, patient man. I sold him a route once. Western fells. I told myself nobody would be hurt." He met her eyes because he owed her that much honesty, at least. "Two people were hurt. Clan folk. Because the men Corvess sold it to weren't merchants."

Ada set the knife down. "That was years ago."

"Debts don't die. They wait. And if Corvess or anyone holding his paper wanted to collect, the price would be exactly this — a courier, a map, a route. They wouldn't need me to betray you outright. They'd only need me to be slow. To miss a marker. To take the long way around a fork." He heard his own voice go flat and bitter. "I'm the obvious traitor, Ada. Anyone hunting us would see it. Sometimes I see it myself, at night, and I can't swear to you that the man in my head is lying."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

Ada picked her knife back up. Stroked it along the whetstone once, twice. Then she said, "When the patrol cornered us at the Split Stone, you stepped in front of a blade meant for me. You didn't calculate it. I saw your face. There wasn't a thought in it."

"That's what worries me," Bren said. "A man who acts without thinking is a man who can be moved without knowing it."

"No." She said it simply, the way she said most true things. "A man who steps in front of knives is a man I'll trust over my own arithmetic. But we'll watch the trail together from now on. Both of us. Every cairn, every fork. If someone's marking us, we'll see it twice as fast — and if it's ever *you* they've bought—" she looked at him levelly across the fire, "—then you'll tell me. Even if it's after. Especially if it's after. Promise me that much."

"I promise," Bren said, and discovered he meant it, and discovered that meaning it was its own kind of terror, because promises were debts too, and he'd never yet met a debt that stayed cheap.

* * *

They checked the trail together after that, and for a day and a half it held clean — and then, on the morning of the third day, at the mouth of the Kestrel Cut, Bren found the fourth marker, and this time it wasn't subtle.

Someone had tied a strip of grey cloth to the thornbush at the cut's entrance. Grey like ash. Grey like cloaks. It fluttered there in the wind, cheerful as a festival ribbon, and Bren stood looking at it with his heart going slow and heavy, because this wasn't a smuggler's signal at all. This was a banner. This was someone saying *they came through here, come quickly.*

"It's fresh," Ada said behind him. She touched the cloth. "Still damp. Hours, not days."

"Hours." Bren scanned the cut walls, the scree slopes, the treeline. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved, that was the trick of being hunted — the hunters could afford patience. "Whoever it is isn't even hiding anymore. They don't need to. They know where we're going."

"The Hollow," Ada said. "There's only one road left that leads there. Everyone knows it now. Including them." She tore the grey cloth from the bush and crushed it in her fist, and Bren watched her do it and thought: *she's angry at the cloth, but she should be angry at a person, and she doesn't know which person yet, and neither do I, and that gap is going to swallow one of us.*

Because here was the arithmetic he still hadn't finished. The villages they'd passed through: Maren's village, the ferry hamlet, the shepherd camp below the Split Stone. Forty fluent speakers of the smuggler's grammar, and he'd stood in front of a dozen of them in the last two weeks, trading, talking, letting Ada's presence be seen because she'd refused to hide and he'd been too tired — too *fond* — to insist.

Any one of them could have sold the route. Any one of them could have been leaned on, the way he'd once been leaned on, the way a patient man with a ledger could lean.

And underneath all of it, quiet as water under ice, the other thought kept flowing:

*Or it's you. Or you marked those cairns yourself, on the bad nights, half-asleep, ribs burning, some old muscle of survival flexing without asking permission. You wouldn't remember. Men like you never remember the small betrayals. That's how they stay small.*

"Bren." Ada's voice pulled him back. She was watching him with that steady grey gaze, the map a hard shape under her coat. "Whatever you're calculating — stop. We're close enough now that the only way out is through. So we go through faster than they expect, and we stop trusting roads, and we trust each other instead. That's the whole plan. Can you live with it?"

He thought about lying. He thought about the ledger with his name in it, the squared stones, the grey cloth damp in her fist.

"Yeah," he said. "I can live with it."

They went into the Kestrel Cut together, and behind them, high on the eastern rim, so far up that only a man who knew to look would ever see it, a figure in grey stood watching — and did not follow.

It didn't need to. It already knew where the road ended.
