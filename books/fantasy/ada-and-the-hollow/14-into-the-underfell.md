## 14. Into the Underfell

**Bren**

The mine-mouth looked like a wound that had never been allowed to heal.

It sat in the hillside above the drowned village of Kell's Rest, a black arch framed by rotted timbers, and the air coming out of it was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season. Cold like a cellar. Cold like something held its breath down there a long time ago and was still holding it.

Bren crouched at the treeline and watched Ada watch the entrance. She'd been doing that for ten minutes. Not hesitating, exactly. Measuring.

"You're sure about this," he said. It wasn't a question when he said it, but she answered anyway.

"No."

"Good. Honest people make worse corpses." He checked his knife out of habit, found it there where it always was, and stood. "The shafts under Thornfell flood every spring. The old workings below the waterline stay flooded year-round — that's why nobody mines them. We go through maybe two hundred paces of water at the worst stretch. Waist-deep, chest-deep if the rains came late."

"And if they came early?"

"Then we learn to breathe water." He shouldered his pack. "You can still turn around. Take the map, walk back to your gate, seal yourself in with your rituals until the Order gets bored."

"They don't get bored."

"No," he agreed. "They don't."

She started downhill without another word, and Bren followed, and that was the whole of their council of war. He'd noticed it about her over the past weeks — she didn't talk herself into things. She just decided, quietly, the way she probably inspected gate-seals, one bolt at a time, and then the decision was made and there was no unmaking it.

The village below was empty the way a cup is empty after someone leaves in a hurry. Roofs fallen in. A well with no rope. The clan folk had abandoned Kell's Rest three generations back when the mines stopped paying, and the mountains had taken the rest. Bren had run goods through here in his younger days, before he learned which debts were worth carrying.

He paused at the mine-mouth and put his hand flat against the timber. Old habit. Asking permission, in a way, though nothing down there would grant it.

"What are you doing?" Ada asked.

"My mother worked these shafts. Before the clans scattered." It wasn't something he talked about. It came out anyway, the way truths did around Ada Crane, as if she pulled them up like weeds. "He said you always knock."

"He?"

"Whoever lives in the dark." He grinned at her, because the alternative was admitting he was scared, and stepped through.

* * *

**Ada**

The dark underground was total. Then Bren struck flint and their single lamp caught, and the dark merely retreated to a polite distance.

The shaft ran straight for a while, tall enough to walk upright, the walls seamed with tool-marks older than any language Ada knew. Water ran somewhere beneath their feet — she could hear it, a constant low conversation between stone and river — and every few paces the floor was slick with a film of wet that made each step a small negotiation.

"The miners marked the walls," she said, slowing. Rows of symbols climbed the stone beside them: chevrons, circles, hands. Hundreds of hands, pressed or carved, overlapping.

"Countersigns. Depth markers. Prayers, probably." Bren didn't slow. "The Hollow is deeper than all of this. Your map says the mine-works were dug *around* it. Like a man building a fence around a grave he doesn't want to look at."

Ada touched one of the carved hands. It was smaller than her own. A child's hand, maybe, or a woman's. Her mother had walked under this mountain once. Her mother had known these marks, or made them, or —

She stopped the thought before it could become a weight she'd carry into deep water.

"How far to the flooded section?"

"Half an hour. There's a gallery where the ceiling comes down and the water goes up and they meet in the middle." Bren glanced back at her, lamp-shadow making a stranger's face of his own. "When we get there, you keep one hand on the wall and you don't lift your feet, you slide them. And you don't stop. Stopping is how people drown in six inches of water — panic, not depth."

"I'm not going to panic."

"Everyone panics. Some people just do it quietly." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on her a moment longer than the joke needed. "I've seen you lie to grey cloaks without blinking, Ada. I've seen you hold a dying woman's hand and not look away. You'll do."

From him, she had learned, that was close to a declaration of love.

They walked on. The shaft narrowed, bent, narrowed again. The air grew wetter and older. And somewhere behind them — faint, so faint she almost blamed the water — came a sound like boots on stone.

Bren heard it too. He killed the lamp without being asked.

* * *

**Bren**

Darkness like a held breath.

Bren put his mouth near Ada's ear. "How many behind us since the village?" he breathed.

"One set. Maybe two." She'd have counted. Of course she'd have counted.

One set meant a scout. Two meant a scout with friends coming. Either way it meant the elder of the nameless village had sold them true, and the Order hadn't wasted a day. Bren felt no surprise, only a dull ache where surprise used to live. He'd been sold before. Usually he'd been the one selling.

"Options," she whispered.

"Forward fast, or back and kill." He weighed it while he spoke. "Back is cleaner. Forward puts water between us and them, and water doesn't take sides."

"You said stopping is how people drown."

"Moving wrong is faster."

In the black he heard her breathe — one slow deliberate cycle, the sound of a woman filing fear somewhere it couldn't reach her hands. Then: "Forward."

He relit the lamp low, shaded under his coat, and they went. The shaft dropped in long uneven stairs cut for shorter legs than theirs, and the walls closed in until Bren's shoulders brushed both sides, and still behind them, patient as dripping, came the footsteps. Never hurrying. That was the worst of it. Hunters who hurry are worried. Hunters who don't hurry already know how this ends.

The gallery opened without warning — the ceiling lifted, the walls fell away, and ahead of them lay the water.

It filled the passage floor to within a handspan of some low stone ribs, ancient supports worn to knuckles of rock, and it stretched back into darkness farther than the lamp could argue with. Black water. Still water. Water with the whole mountain pressing down on it.

"Breathe," Bren said, mostly to himself.

He went first, sliding his feet, one hand on the guide-rope some dead miner had strung — and by some mercy of rot and time it held. The cold climbed him like a living thing. Ankle, knee, waist. At his navel the water found every seam of him and he gasped despite himself, and the gasp echoed, and the echoes came back wrong, doubled, as if the passage were repeating him to someone.

"Talk to me," Ada said behind him. Her voice was tight but level.

"There's nothing to say."

"Then say nothing loudly."

He laughed — a short ugly bark — and the laugh broke something loose in his chest, and they waded on. Chest-deep now. The pack on his shoulders wanted to float and drag him backward at once, both at once, traitorous. The lamp he held high in one fist, and its light lay along the water like a gold road leading nowhere.

Behind them, at the gallery's edge, light bloomed.

Not lamplight. Whiter. Colder. Ash-lanterns — the Order burned something pale in them, and the light they threw had no warmth in it at all.

"Don't stop," Bren said, and this time it wasn't advice.

* * *

**Ada**

The water fought her and she let it fight.

Hand on the rope. Feet sliding, never lifting. Behind her the white light grew, and voices carried strange across the water — flattened, echoing, words she couldn't parse but tone she could. Orders. Calm ones. They weren't rushing because they knew the flooded run better than she did, or thought they did, or simply knew that black water collects its debts from everyone equally.

Her fingers found a break in the rope.

Just gone — rotted through, a gap of three arm-spans where some spring flood had chewed the line off the wall. Beyond the gap the wall itself curved inward, smooth and slimy, nothing to hold.

"Bren. Rope's dead ahead."

"I know. I know." His voice had changed. Tighter. "There's a ledge. Ten steps past where the rope should end, left side, knee-height. You climb onto it and wait in the dark while I come back for the pack line. Don't—"

The splash behind them wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. It was just the sound of a man stepping off the gallery's edge into chest-deep water with a white lantern held high, and it told her everything: they weren't waiting anymore.

"Go," she said.

"Not without—"

"*Go.*"

She went. Hands on smooth stone, feet sliding blind, counting — nine, ten — and her left hand struck the lip of the ledge and she hauled herself up onto wet rock in darkness absolute, and turned with the lamp raised toward the water.

Bren had stopped. He stood hip-deep in the middle of the flooded run, facing back the way they'd come, and he'd drawn his knife, and the white light was closer now, and Ada understood with a clarity that felt like falling: he meant to hold the passage. He meant to stand in black water and let them come to him, one at a time, where a knife mattered more than numbers.

"Bren, *no* —"

"Take the lamp up the ledge. There's a step-cut, twenty paces, goes up dry. I can see it from here." He didn't turn his head. "Move, Ada."

The first grey cloak came around the last stone rib, lantern high, sword out, and the water barely rippled at his passing because he'd done this before, and Ada saw Bren settle, low and easy, a man greeting an old bad habit.

She should have run. Twenty paces of dry step-cut, safety, sense.

She picked up the largest loose stone on the ledge instead.

* * *

**Bren**

The grey cloak was young. Bren noted it the way you note weather — useful, irrelevant. Young men with swords in black water overreach; overreaching men drown easy. He gave ground slowly, drawing the boy deeper, watching the second lantern behind gather itself at the rib of stone, waiting its turn like good soldiers do.

Come on. Come on. One more step and the bottom drops—

The bottom dropped.

Not for the boy. For Bren. The shelf of rubble he'd been standing on, some old collapse dressed in silt, chose that moment to remember gravity, and the world tilted, and the cold took him whole. Black water in eyes and ears and throat. Downward. His knife-hand striking stone. The lamp — gone, a fading gold smear above him like a coin sinking in a well.

And through the roar of his own blood he heard the splash of someone else coming in fast, and a voice cracking across the water, young and suddenly uncertain: "*She's got a rock — she's actually —*"

Then impact. Then white noise, white pain, and Bren's shoulder hit mud and stone in shallows he hadn't known were there, coughing, half-blind, alive by the particular grace of shallow graves.

The fight, when his eyes cleared enough to see it, was nearly done.

Ada stood astride the ledge-edge with a rock in both hands and no technique whatsoever and absolute commitment, and the young soldier was down in the water holding his face, and the second man — older, quicker — had her wrist, and it was going to be over in the next three seconds and not in her favor.

Bren came out of the shallows like something the mine had decided to return.

He'd killed before, years ago, and sworn it off, and the oath had cost him more than he ever admitted. It took less than a breath to break. Knife-hand, low line, the old geometry rising in him like water finding its level. The older soldier turned toward the splash and that was his mistake; Bren put the blade where the neck meets the shoulder and the man folded into the black water without finishing whatever he'd begun to say.

Silence. Just dripping. Just breathing.

Ada lowered the rock. Her hands were shaking now — they hadn't shaken once during. "Are you—"

"Drowning. Otherwise fine." He spat water, retrieved his knife, and made himself look at the two shapes in the flood. One groaning, one still. "You came back for me."

"You came back for me first."

"That was tactics."

"Liar," she said, and there was something in her voice he decided, firmly, not to examine.

He fished the young one's ash-lantern off the rocks and drowned its pale fire in the flood, then took the man's sword-belt and pack, because waste offended him more than violence did. "Up the step-cut," he said. "Twenty paces, dry. And Ada — we don't stop walking until the water's out of our boots. There'll be more. The elder sold us cheap, and the Order buys in bulk."

* * *

**Ada**

The step-cut delivered them into galleries older than the mines — or rather, galleries the mines had *found*, because the walls changed. No more tool-marks. No more carved hands. Instead, surfaces of a smoothness no chisel explained, veined faintly with lines that might have been decoration or might have been script, and under Ada's palm the stone was warm.

Not mine-warm. Warm like skin.

"The Hollow's near," Bren said quietly. "My grandmother used to say the deep city kept the mountain alive. Heat of it rises, that's all." He paused. "That's what she said when she wanted me to sleep."

They made camp in a side chamber where a dry shelf ran along one wall, and Ada wrung a pint of the Underfell out of her braid and ate ship's biscuit that tasted of stone-dust and victory. Her body had begun its delayed argument with the night — the shaking, the cold that lived in the joints, the image that kept replaying whether she summoned it or not: Bren going under, the gold lamp-light dying upward through black water.

"You could have died," she said. "For a map."

"For you. The map was incidental." He said it easily, lying on his back with his hands behind his head, eyes on the warm-veined ceiling. "Besides, I've priced my life my whole career. Turns out the market was wrong about it. Feels good to finally sell under value to someone worth it."

"That's the worst confession I've ever heard."

"It's honest, though. You like honest."

She did. She liked it so much it frightened her, sitting here in the warm dark under a mile of stone with a man whose loyalty she couldn't price because he'd stopped keeping accounts. She lay down on the far end of the shelf, deliberately, leaving the geography between them exactly as it was, and closed her eyes.

Sleep came thin and shallow, and the mountain dreamed over her, and she dreamed with it: her mother's voice reading the hidden letter again, *you already know why*, and beneath the words, deeper, a sound like a great door remembering how to open.

* * *

**Bren**

He woke because the warmth changed.

Subtle — a degree, maybe less — but a smuggler's instincts were built on subtler things than temperature. The veins in the walls had brightened. Faintly. Like coals under ash deciding, after centuries, to remember fire.

And from somewhere ahead, down the long throat of the descending galleries, came a sound that was not water and not wind and not any animal he could name.

A hum. Low, vast, patient. The mountain clearing its throat.

Ada was already awake, sitting up, the map open across her knees. In its light he watched her finger trace the route and stop at a mark near the bottom — a circle within a circle — and he watched her go very still.

"What," he said.

She turned the map toward him. Beneath the circled mark, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood, her mother had written four words:

*It will know you.*

Down the passage, the hum rose — and resolved, unmistakably now, into something with structure. With rhythm. With, God help them, something almost like welcome.

And behind them, far back along the flooded way, steel rang on stone. Many boots. Not far enough.

"They found the step-cut," Bren said, standing. "Ada. Whatever it knows about you — best hope it likes you."

He kicked sand over the lamp, and together they ran toward the voice of the waking city.
