## 15. Doors That Remember Hands

The water ended where the mountain did.

We came up out of the flooded shafts at dusk — or what I took for dusk, though there was no sky down here to prove it, only a vast dark that breathed cold against my face like the inside of a closed mouth. Bren leaned on my shoulder more than he'd admit, his wound bound in strips of my shirt and the elder's bitter moss-poultice, and every step he took was a small argument with gravity that he kept losing by inches.

Then the tunnel opened, and we stopped arguing about anything at all.

A cavern. That word is too small for it. A country underground, roofed in stone so far above us that our lantern-light couldn't find its ceiling, and spread across the floor of it — a city.

I had heard lullabies about the Hollow all my life. *Sleep, little keeper, the gate holds fast; the city below is the city past.* I had thought they were metaphors, the way lullabies are, the way mothers fill silence with pretty shapes. But here it was: terraces cut into living rock, streets running in spirals like the rings of a felled tree, towers that had been grown rather than built, their surfaces smooth as river-polished bone. Everything was dark. Everything was waiting.

"It's real," Bren said. His voice came out scraped thin. "I always figured it was a story smugglers told to make the passes sound worth crossing."

"Everything's a story until you're standing in it."

"Is that wisdom? Or are you just scared?"

"Both," I said. "They travel together."

We went down into the city along a road whose stones fit together without mortar, each one bearing a mark worn nearly smooth — hands, I realized. Pressed palms, hundreds of them, thousands, overlapping like leaves. The road remembered everyone who had ever walked it. My boots made no sound on it at all, as if the stones were holding their breath.

The map had gone strange in my hands since the Underfell. The ink that had guided us through the mine-shafts now shimmered when I held it toward the dark, lines lifting off the vellum like heat-haze, pointing not forward but *down* — no. Not down. *Inward.* Toward the heart of the spiral.

Toward the gates.

I felt them before I saw them. A pressure behind my sternum, faint and rhythmic, like a second heartbeat slightly out of time with my own. Bren noticed me falter.

"Ada?"

"There." I didn't have to point. The road funneled between two towers grown close together, and filling the gap between them stood doors taller than the tollhouse at Thornfell, taller than anything men should have been able to make and certainly nothing they could have carried down here. They were not stone. They were not wood. They were some third thing, dark and faintly warm-looking, veined with channels that might have been metal or might have been frozen light.

And across their face, at the height of a standing woman's hand, was a single unmarked hollow — a palm-print shaped like an absence.

"The map says—" I began, and stopped, because the map had stopped being a map. The ink had gathered itself into the shape of a hand. My mother's hand. I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall: by its proportions, its particular crookedness. She had pressed her palm to this door once, and the map had kept the memory the way the road kept footprints.

"Your mother's key," Bren said quietly. "You said the letter called it that."

"She wrote: *the gate knows my blood.*" I looked at my own hand. "I didn't understand what she meant."

"You do now."

"I do now."

He slid down the wall to sit, which he would claim later was tactical and which we both knew was his leg giving out. "Then open it. And if something with too many teeth comes out, I want it noted that I died wounded but brave."

"Noted," I said. "Shut up."

My palm hovered over the hollow. This close, I could feel the rhythm behind my ribs again, stronger now, and I understood it wasn't my heartbeat at all. It was the door's. It had been beating down here in the dark for however many years the Hollow had slept, patient as only dead things and doors can be patient.

I thought of my mother pressing her hand here. Alive, then. Walking these roads, touching these stones, while I grew up at Thornfell believing she was a grave-marker and a grief. She had known this place. She had *belonged* to it. And she had left it to guard a map until her daughter was ready — ready for what? For this. For exactly this moment, standing in front of a door that wanted my blood the way a lock wants a tooth of iron.

I put my hand in the hollow.

For one terrible instant, nothing. Cold stone under my palm, and my own foolishness ringing in my ears, and Bren watching me with that look he wore when he was deciding whether to laugh or lie.

Then the veins lit.

Light ran up the doors like fire through dry grass — gold first, then white, then a color I don't have a name for, the color of remembering something you never learned. It poured along the channels, branching, thickening, and the pressure behind my sternum snapped into alignment, my heartbeat and the door's beating together at last, and the doors swung inward without a sound.

No groan of hinges. No grinding of ancient weight. Just a breath of air from within, warm and dustless, smelling faintly of — I searched for it — of my mother's shawl. Of the box where she'd kept her sewing things. Of a childhood I'd misremembered as fatherless and motherless both.

"Ada," Bren said, very softly. "You should see your face."

"I could say the same."

"Yeah, but mine's handsome."

* * *

Inside, the city was awake.

Not inhabited — I want to be clear about that, because for one vertiginous moment I thought it was. Lights everywhere, rising out of the streets and terraces in slow ribbons, drifting upward toward that unreachable ceiling like luminous snow falling in reverse. They weren't fires. They weren't lamps. When one drifted close enough to touch, I saw it was a kind of suspended brightness, and inside it, faint as a watermark, moved *shapes*. Figures. Hands working at some craft. A child running. Two people dancing in a kitchen.

Memories. The lights were memories, loose in the streets of the city that had made them.

"They got out," I whispered. "When the vaults sealed, some of them got out, and they've been circling ever since."

"Like moths," Bren said. "Or ghosts."

"Or people who wouldn't leave their homes." One of the drifting lights passed through my outstretched fingers and I felt it — not warmth exactly, but recognition, a brush of attention, as if the memory had glanced at me and filed me away. My skin prickled for minutes afterward.

We walked. There was no question of not walking. The city pulled us gently along its spiral roads, lighting our way whether we asked it to or not, and everywhere the same pattern repeated: marks of hands. On walls, on wells, on the lintels of doorways opening onto homes whose interiors were preserved like flies in amber — tables set for meals, beds turned down, toys on floors. The people of the Hollow had left in a hurry, or been taken in one, or —

Or erased. That was what the map was for. That was what the Order burned maps to prevent. Somewhere ahead of us lay the vaults, and in the vaults lay the truth of how a whole city stops existing while the world above goes on singing lullabies about it.

Bren stopped at a fountain — dry for centuries, its basin carved with interlocking hands — and lowered himself onto its rim with a grunt. "I need to say something, and I need you not to make it weird."

"That's never once worked."

"Then I'll say it anyway." He looked up at the drifting lights, and for once his face wasn't performing anything. No smirk, no smuggler's arithmetic. Just a man looking at beauty he hadn't stolen and didn't know how to hold. "I've spent my whole life pricing things. Routes, cargo, loyalty, my own hide. You get good at it. You get so good you stop seeing anything that doesn't have a number attached." He nodded at the city. "This doesn't have a number. I keep trying to find one and it keeps slipping. Like trying to price a song."

I sat beside him on the fountain's rim. "That's what belonging feels like. In case you were wondering. It's the thing without a number."

"I wasn't wondering."

"You were absolutely wondering."

His mouth twitched. Above us, a ribbon of light drifted past carrying the image of a woman braiding another woman's hair, both of them laughing at something outside the frame, and I watched it go with an ache in my chest that had no name yet but was already starting to feel like homesickness for people I'd never met.

Because here was the thing I couldn't say yet, the thing sitting in me like swallowed glass: some of the faces in the lights looked like mine. Not exactly. But the set of the eyes, the line of the jaw — I'd seen those proportions before, in a polished knife-blade at Thornfell when I was young enough to study my own reflection, and later, impossibly, in the face of a dying stranger on a snowy road.

This was my mother's city. Maybe farther back than her. Maybe generations of Cranes had walked these spiral roads, pressed their palms to these doors, hung their washing in courtyards now dark. The gate at Thornfell wasn't just a duty I'd inherited. It was a *sentinel post*. Someone in my line had been left above to watch the seal — and someone below had been left to carry the map — and neither of them had told the children why, because telling would have been the end of the forgetting, and the forgetting was the only thing keeping the clans safe.

Or so they'd believed. So *she'd* believed, right up until she decided I was ready to know better.

"Come on," I said, standing. "The map's still pulling."

"You should rest. *I* should definitely rest. One of us has a hole in him."

"One of us also has a mother who waited twenty years to be believed. I'm not making her wait longer because your leg is dramatic."

"My leg is *wounded*, there's a difference—"

But he got up. He always got up. That was the thing about Bren I could never price either.

* * *

The vaults announced themselves by silence.

As we climbed the final terrace — the innermost ring of the spiral, where the city's towers grew tallest and closest — the drifting memories thinned, then vanished altogether, as if even the loose recollections of the Hollow were afraid to approach what lay at its center. The light remained, but it changed character: steadier, colder, sourceless. Our footsteps, silent on the outer roads, began to echo here, each one coming back to us a half-beat late like a stranger following at a distance.

The map in my hands had gone almost entirely to light. Only the outline remained, and the outline pointed straight ahead, to a plaza at the city's heart where a single structure stood.

It was smaller than I expected. After the doors, after the cavern-country, after towers grown like trees — the vault was a low building, round, its surface the same dark third-thing as the gates, veined with dormant channels. Unmarked. No hands pressed into its face. Whatever was inside had not been left for visitors.

Around its crown, cut deep and worn shallow by time, ran a line of script I couldn't read — except that I could, the way you can sometimes read a language in dreams, the letters resolving as long as you didn't look at them directly:

*What was buried shall be witnessed.*

Bren read it over my shoulder. "Cheerful."

"It's not a warning. It's a promise."

"To whom?"

"To whoever came back." I raised my hand toward the vault's surface, and my palm — my ordinary, chapped, gatekeeper's palm — began to glow at the edges, faint gold bleeding up from the lines of my skin like ink wicking through paper. My mother's key, written in me all along. Written in every Crane since the sealing, waiting for one of us to come far enough down to use it.

Bren stared. "Does it hurt?"

"No." That was the strangest part. It felt like a handshake. Like being greeted by name after years among strangers.

Behind us, far off across the sleeping city, something echoed. Not our footsteps. Heavier. Rhythmic. Boots — many boots — descending a stair that had been silent a moment ago.

Bren's hand went to his knife. "Tell me that's the city settling."

I listened. The echoes came again, closer, disciplined, unhurried. Grey cloaks didn't hurry. They didn't have to. They knew, the way I knew, that there was only one way out of the Underfell, and that sooner or later the map would bring its bearer to the only door that mattered.

"They found the shafts," I said. "Someone told them."

"Later," Bren said grimly. "Worry about traitors later. Right now — can you open it?"

I pressed my glowing palm flat against the vault.

The channels lit. The promise-script blazed white around the crown. And the vault began, slowly, with the patience of two hundred years, to open — while down in the dark behind us, the Ashen Order came walking into the city their masters had tried to erase, and the dead lights of the Hollow rose higher around them, watching.
