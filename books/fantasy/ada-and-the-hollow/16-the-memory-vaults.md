## 16. The Memory-Vaults

The doors of the Hollow had opened for her blood, and now the city wanted to know what she'd do with it.

Ada stood at the top of a stair that spiraled down into the mountain's heart, and the stair was lit — not by torches, not by the grey-glow lichen that Bren called corpse-light, but by something older. Lines of pale gold ran along the walls like veins, and they brightened where she stepped, dimmed where she left, as if the stone itself were taking her pulse.

"It's following you," Bren said behind her. He was moving slowly, favoring the side where the knife had gone in at the Split Stone, but his eyes were everywhere — the arches, the shadows, the places a man could hide. Old habits. "The light. It's reading you."

"Or counting me," Ada said. "I haven't decided which is worse."

She started down.

The stairs went on far longer than the mountain should have allowed. She counted two hundred steps, then stopped counting, because the numbers stopped mattering and the silence started to. It wasn't empty silence. It was the silence of a room full of people holding their breath — the silence of the gate on the night before the Order's inspectors came, when the whole tollhouse seemed to lean toward the morning.

Bren's boots scraped behind her, steady, deliberate. He'd stopped talking somewhere around the hundredth step, and she was grateful. Some silences you fill. Some you honor.

At the bottom, the stair opened into a hall that stole her breath and kept it.

* * *

The vaults were not what she had imagined. She had imagined, in the fever-dream way she'd imagined everything since the snow, something like a treasury — shelves of gold, chests of documents, the dry weight of paper. What she found instead was a forest.

Columns of glass rose from the floor to a ceiling lost in darkness, each one as wide as a well and taller than the tollhouse, and inside each column moved light — not still light but *living* light, turning, folding, breaking apart and re-forming, like weather trapped in a bottle. Thousands of columns. Tens of thousands. They marched away into the dark in rows so long the farthest ones were only a suggestion, a faint gold haze at the edge of seeing.

And when Ada came close to the nearest column, the light inside it *noticed* her.

It gathered at the glass nearest her face, pressing, swirling, and — she would swear this until she died — it *waited*. The way a dog waits at a door. The way a letter waits, sealed, for the hand that will break it.

"What are they?" Bren's voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when he was genuinely out of his depth and didn't want anyone to know it.

"Memories." The word came out of her before she'd decided to say it. She put her palm flat against the glass, and the light rushed to meet it, warm as a hand. "The lullaby said the Hollow kept everything. *The city under the stone remembers what the mountain was made to forget.* I thought it was a rhyme. It's an inventory."

She stepped back. The light in the column sank, dimmed, and settled into its slow turning again. Waiting still. Patient as stone.

"They're not records," she said. "Bren, they're not *records*. They're the actual thing. The war. The way it happened, inside the heads of the people who lived it. Every one of these is a person."

Bren was silent a long moment. Then, in the flat voice he used for the worst truths: "How many?"

Ada looked down the rows, into the haze where the columns became a golden fog, and did the only arithmetic that mattered.

"All of them."

* * *

She didn't know how to choose. There was no index, no ledger, no neat arrangement by year or battle. The columns stood where they stood, and the light in them turned and turned.

So she walked, and she let the vault decide.

The first column she touched showed her a market day. That was all — a market day, decades before the war, a woman selling apples and arguing cheerfully with a boy about the price of bread. The memory was so ordinary it made Ada's throat hurt. This was what the Order wanted burned. Not weapons. Not secrets. *Apples.*

The second showed her the war's beginning — a border stone in a high meadow, soldiers on both sides of it, and a misunderstanding so small, so stupidly small, that Ada felt sick watching it unfold. A stolen goat. A name called wrong. Two young men reaching for the same moment of anger, and a spear moving faster than either of their senses.

The third—

The third showed her the ending.

She knew it was the ending because the light in the column was different. Not gold but grey-white, the color of the Order's cloaks, and the memory inside it moved with the strange, jerking quality of a thing seen through fear. She was on a ridge above the Hollow. Below her, the city burned — but not from siege-fire. The flames rose from the vaults themselves, from the columns, from the memories, and figures in grey walked the rows with torches, feeding them.

And the memory's owner — a woman, Ada realized, a woman holding a child behind her, a child with dark hair and a face she knew from her own reflection — was screaming at a grey-cloaked officer, and the words came through the memory not as sound but as meaning, direct into Ada's chest:

*You didn't win. You know you didn't win. The clans had already laid down their arms — the treaty was signed — you're not burning an enemy, you're burning a witness—*

The officer's answer came the same way, cold as well-water: *History is written by whoever survives the writing of it.*

And then the memory-owner turned and ran, down into the vaults, torchlight behind her, and pressed something into a column of glass — pressed her whole self into it, her terror and her fury and her knowledge — and the memory ended.

Ada came back to herself on her knees on the vault floor, Bren's hand hard on her shoulder, her own voice hoarse from a scream she didn't remember making.

"Ada. Ada. What did you see?"

"The treaty." She made herself say it. Made herself say it plainly, because if she dressed it up she'd never believe it later. "The clans surrendered. They'd already surrendered. There was a treaty — signed, sealed — and the Order burned the city anyway, and then they burned the memory of the treaty, and then they spent a hundred years telling everyone the war was won by right arms and righteous gods." Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake as if they belonged to a stranger. "They didn't win the war, Bren. They won the *story*. That's all they ever won."

Bren crouched beside her. He didn't touch her again. He just looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on him — not the smuggler's mask, not the road-worn wryness, but something raw and young.

"My grandmother," he said slowly, "used to sing the same lullabies the clans sing. She wasn't clan. She was lowland. And when I asked her where she learned them, she hit me." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Hit me and said never to ask again. I thought she was ashamed of something. She was *terrified* of something."

"The Order didn't just erase the clans," Ada said. "They erased everyone who remembered the erasure. Whole bloodlines. Whole *songs*." She stood. Her legs held, barely. "And they've been hunting this map for a hundred years because somewhere in here is the proof, and they know it, and they'd rather burn a city's soul than let a single treaty be remembered."

* * *

She walked the rows after that with a purpose she couldn't name, and the vault seemed to feel it. The lights brightened as she passed, reaching, and she touched some and not others, and what she saw broke her in small ways she kept track of, the way you count your own ribs.

A boy hiding in a cistern while grey cloaks walked the streets above, counting the living.

An old man teaching children the clan tongue in a cellar, whispering, *if they ask, you never heard it from me.*

A soldier of the victors' own army, standing in this same hall with a torch in his hand and tears on his face, refusing — *refusing* — and being cut down by his own officer, and his last thought, the last thing the light in his column held, was *let someone find this, let someone find this, let someone—*

Ada stood before that column a long time. The light pressed against the glass against her palm, and it was not peaceful, and it was not at rest, and she understood at last what the vaults were. Not an archive. A *plea*. Ten thousand voices, sealed in glass, all saying the same thing across a hundred years of dark:

*Remember. Someone. Please. Remember.*

"I'm here," she said out loud, to all of them, and felt foolish, and said it anyway. "I'm here."

* * *

It was Bren who found it. Or rather, it was Bren who stopped walking, three rows deep in the eastern dark, and said, in a voice gone strange:

"Ada. This one's lit different."

She went to him. He was right. Every column they'd passed had held its light in slow turning, weather-in-a-bottle, patient. This one burned. The light inside it moved fast and hard, like a struck bell still ringing, and it was the color of the gold veins in the stairwell walls — her color, the color the stone made when it read her blood.

She touched the glass.

And her mother's voice said her name.

Not through meaning this time. Not through the chest. *Out loud*, in the vault's still air, in a voice Ada had spent twenty years forgetting and had never once forgotten — a voice that said, "Ada. If you're hearing this, then you found the map, and you got past the gate, and you are more stubborn than I ever managed to teach you to be."

Ada's hand stayed on the glass. Her whole body had stopped, the way a deer stops, the way the world stops.

"I don't have much time," the voice said, and there were running footsteps behind it, and torchlight flickering across the column from inside the memory itself. "They're in the vaults. They're burning the rows. I can save one thing tonight, maybe two, and I'm choosing this — I'm choosing to leave the truth of me, because someone has to know that the keeper of Thornfell didn't just guard a gate. She guarded a *door*. And the door opens for our blood, and our blood was in this city when it burned, and everything the Order told you about why I left is a lie I let them tell because the truth would have gotten you killed."

The footsteps were closer. The torchlight brighter.

"I was going to come back for you," her mother said, and here the voice broke, and Ada's broke with it, standing in the dark with her hand on a hundred-year-old wound made of light. "I told myself every year, next year, when she's older, when the road is safer, when— I was a coward, Ada. Not about the vaults. About you. And if you're angry — be angry. Be as angry as you need. Just don't let it close the door behind you. Because the world up there is built on this lie, and the lie is *tired*, Ada. Lies get tired. It's been leaning on the clans' backs for a hundred years and it's so tired, and you — you're the one it's been waiting for."

The torchlight in the memory was at the row's end now. Voices. Grey voices, giving orders.

"One more thing." The voice was steady again, the keeper's voice, the gate-voice, and it went straight into Ada like a key into a lock. "The master record. The first column, the one the whole vault turns around — it's in the deep hall, under the central stair. Everything in here is a witness. That one is the *treaty itself*, the signing, the whole of it, seen from the table where it was signed. If the vault burns, that column is the one thing that can't be allowed to burn. And if the Order comes — and they will come, because they always come — you'll have a choice to make that I couldn't make, because I ran, and I have regretted it every single day of my life since."

The torches were close. The memory was ending. Ada pressed her hand harder against the glass, as if she could hold the voice in, as if she could keep her mother in the world a moment longer—

"Whatever you choose," her mother said, "choose it with your eyes open. That's all a keeper does, in the end. The gate stays shut or it opens. There's no third door. I love you. I have always—"

The memory cut.

The column went dark.

Not dim. *Dark*, spent, like a lamp burned to the bottom of its oil, and Ada stood in front of it with her hand on cold glass and her mother's last words unfinished in the air, and somewhere in the vault's vast dark, ten thousand other lights turned and turned and waited.

Bren didn't say anything. He just stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him, and let the silence be what it was.

Finally Ada spoke, and her voice came out low and level, and she was surprised by it, and then she wasn't, because it was the gate-voice. Her mother's voice. Hers.

"The Order will come," she said. "She said they always come. And when they do, they'll go for the deep hall." She turned from the dead column and looked down the long gold haze of the rows, toward the darkness at the vault's heart where a central stair went down to a hall she had never seen and already knew she would die or kill for. "And we're going to get there first."

Behind them, far above, at the top of the spiral stair — faint, and then less faint, and then unmistakable — came the sound of boots.
