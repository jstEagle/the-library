## 1. The Taster's Portion

The broth was trying to kill the queen.

Isandro Dunmore knew this the way other people knew rain was coming — by a certain pressure behind the eyes, a faint metallic whisper on the tongue, and three years of deliberate, methodical self-poisoning that had turned her body into something between a fortress and a ledger. She stood at the queen's left shoulder in her dove-grey servant's dress, ladle in hand, and let the silver spoon rest on her lower lip for exactly four seconds. Long enough for the tongue to speak. Short enough that no one at the table would notice she'd stopped breathing.

Bittercress, she thought. And under it — monkshood, but young, cut before the roots had finished their work. Someone had been careless. Someone had also been clever, because the bitterness was masked with honey and just enough lemon to make a lazy taster think of dessert rather than death.

She swallowed.

The court went on murmuring around her: Lord Aldery complaining about grain tariffs to anyone who would hold still long enough; the ambassador from Vell weeping quietly into his wine over some slight Isandro had no interest in learning; Queen Maren herself sitting straight-backed and serene at the head of the table, watching everything and admitting to nothing. Candles guttered in their hundred sconces. Somewhere a lute player had decided, tragically, that he was better than he was.

"Isandro." The queen's voice, low, without turning her head. "You've gone quiet."

"I'm always quiet, Your Majesty."

"Yes, but usually you're quiet *contentedly*. Tonight you're quiet like a cat watching a mousehole." Maren lifted her goblet, studied it, set it down untasted. "Well?"

"The broth is fine," Isandro said. "A little heavy on the honey. Whoever made it is fond of it themselves."

It was true, in the way all her best lies were true. She had swallowed poison perhaps forty times in her life — small doses, measured doses, doses she catalogued afterward in a little book bound in grey leather that lived beneath her floorboard and that no living soul knew existed. Bittercress first: numbness blooming across her tongue like frost, then retreating. Monkshood second: a slow cold climbing her throat, settling behind her sternum like a stone dropped into a well. Her heart stumbled once, found its rhythm again. She had trained it to. Or perhaps it had simply learned, the way a dog learns which footsteps belong to its master.

"You'll take bread and water for the rest of the evening," she added, pitching her voice for the queen alone. "And I'd have a word with whoever delivered the cream this morning."

Maren's eyes flicked down the table, toward the steward's station, then back. Something passed between them — not gratitude, exactly. Acknowledgment. Two women who understood that survival at court was a craft practiced daily, like embroidery or lying.

"See that you do," the queen said, and reached past the poisoned broth for the roast.

* * *

The Guildhall of Impossible Things did not exist.

This was its greatest structural feature. There was no sign above its door, because there was no door — only an archway between a chandler's shop and a shuttered cobbler's, in Crooklane, where the city kept the things it preferred not to look at. You walked through the archway counting your own heartbeats, and if you were meant to be there, the alley behind it was longer than the alley should have been, and warmer, and smelled of candlewax and old paper and, faintly, of rain on hot stone — a smell Isandro had never managed to place anywhere else in the world.

If you were not meant to be there, you simply walked out the other side into Crooklane and forgot you'd wondered about the archway at all. Isandro had watched it happen to a drunk once. He'd walked in whistling and walked out still whistling the same note, as though the alley were a tune he'd never started.

Tonight the guildhall was quiet in the way she loved best: lamps turned low, dust hanging gold in the light, the great reading room empty except for the smell of ink. Shelves climbed the walls in ranks, holding not books but ledgers — hundreds of them, thousands, spines labeled in a hand so old the letters had worn soft. *Oaths, Forfeited. Memories, Sold. Years, Borrowed Against. Names, Surrendered.* The Guild of Impossible Things traded in what could not be carried in a purse: the memory of a first snowfall, the keeping of a promise, the last words of a dying father, seven years of someone's grief weighed out in vials and sold to those who wanted to feel something deeply or nothing at all.

And every trade, every vial, every oath pawned or redeemed or broken, passed through the ledgers.

Every entry passed through Isandro's pen.

She hung her cloak on the peg by the reading desk — hers, the only personal thing in the room, a hook she'd claimed in her first week the way a cat claims a windowsill — and lit the lamp over the day-book. Around her wrist, hidden under her sleeve, the faint cold of the monkshood still lingered, a reminder that she had already done one impossible thing tonight and survived it. She flexed her fingers. They were steady. They were always steady. Steadiness was the whole of her reputation, and reputations, she had learned, were a kind of armor that fit closely and chafed constantly.

The evening's trades waited in the intake tray, each slip written in the scrawled hands of the guild's brokers. Isandro squared them into a neat stack, dipped her pen, and began.

*One memory, sold.* A fisherman from the Salt Gate quarter, parting with the memory of his wife's face — she'd been dead nine years and he said the remembering hurt more than the drowning had. He'd asked the broker whether it would hurt less after. The broker, honest in the way guild law demanded, had said no. It would hurt differently. He'd sold it anyway. Isandro recorded the price — two crowns and a jar of pickled eels, because the guild accepted strange currencies when the seller had nothing stranger left — and felt the familiar ache open under her ribs, the one she refused to name because naming things gave them teeth.

*One oath, pawned.* A young nobleman, third son, pledging ten years of his future loyalty to a merchant house in exchange for debts forgiven. Oaths were the guild's most delicate stock. They couldn't be held in a vial; they lived in the ledger itself, in ink mixed — so Corvane said, and Corvane never lied, he merely arranged truths — with rainwater collected from a funeral and ash from a wedding. Break such an oath and the ink itself would change. Isandro had seen it happen once, early on: an entry rewriting itself before her eyes, the letters crawling like insects, until *shall pay* became *shall never pay*, and somewhere across the city a man woke with a debt that had grown teeth and interest.

She wrote the entry clean. She always wrote them clean. Her script was famous among the three people who knew she existed — small, exact, upright, every numeral identical to its brothers. *Invisible handwriting*, Corvane called it, approvingly. *The kind no one remembers reading.*

That was the trick of Isandro Dunmore, if you wanted to know it, and almost no one did: she had made herself into a tool so precise and so quiet that the world had agreed to look through her. At court she was the taster, and tasters were furniture that happened to swallow. Here she was the pen, and pens were furniture that happened to remember. Between the two lives she moved like water through cracks, fed, clothed, sheltered, and — she admitted this only in the smallest hours of the night, staring at her ceiling — starving.

Not for food. She ate well enough, in the way of people who taste everything and enjoy nothing on principle. Starving for something with no name and no market price, something the guild's shelves did not hold, though gods knew they held nearly everything else.

She was halfway through the fourth slip — *one year of luck, borrowed against a christening, terms negotiable upon the child's majority*, which was the sort of entry that made her want to write angry marginalia and never did — when Master Corvane came down the stair.

He moved the way he always did, without sound and without hurry, a tall shape resolving out of the shelf-shadows, dark-haired, grey-eyed, wearing the expression of a man perpetually amused by a joke he had no intention of sharing. Most of the guild feared him in the comfortable, distant way one fears weather. Isandro had never quite managed the fear. It required believing he saw her as furniture, and the trouble was that he didn't. He looked *at* her, directly, whenever he spoke to her, as though she were a person, and after a lifetime of being looked through, being looked at was a drug she took care not to dose herself with too often.

"You swallowed monkshood tonight," he said, by way of greeting.

"Bittercress too. The kitchen is getting ambitious." She didn't look up from the ledger. "How did you know?"

"You rubbed your sternum twice while writing the third entry. You only do that when your heart has argued with you and lost." He drifted closer, hands clasped behind his back, and read upside-down with insulting ease. "The fisherman with the eels?"

"Two crowns and a jar. The eels are technically currency now. I've decided."

"Enter them as condiments. The auditors hate it." A pause, and then, in the tone he used when he was about to be precisely as kind as was dangerous: "Forty-one doses now, isn't it? Since you came to us."

"Thirty-nine. You missed the night of the coronation feast."

"I never miss anything, Isandro. That is my defining tragedy." He watched her finish the line, blot it, reach for the next slip. "Most people who work here eventually trade something. A memory. An oath. Everyone finds the shelf they can't stop looking at. But you—" he tapped the ledger, once, lightly, "—you only ever *write*. Three years, and not one trade of your own. Not one purchase. You keep everyone else's intangibles safe and hoard none for yourself. Do you know what the brokers call you?"

"No one calls me anything. That's the point of me."

"They call you the Untraded." He smiled, and it was not entirely unkind. "It's meant as a compliment. It may even be one."

"Well," Isandro said, dipping her pen, "I'm very expensive."

Corvane laughed — softly, genuinely, the laugh of a man surprised into honesty — and left her to it, ascending back into the shadows from which he'd come. Isandro sat a moment with the pen hovering, listening to the silence where his footsteps hadn't been, and told herself firmly that the warmth in her chest was residual monkshood and nothing else.

She worked another hour. The trades thinned as the city slept; the guild did its strangest business at dusk and dawn, when people stood at the borders of their days and were willing to bargain with them. She blotted the final entry, closed the day-book, and reached for the master ledger to carry forward the balances — the nightly ritual, the balancing of the impossible, her favorite hour in either of her lives. There was a deep satisfaction in it, in making the unmeasurable add up. Griefs in the left column, comforts in the right. Debts of honor accruing interest in the margins. Somewhere in those pages, the whole secret economy of the city's hearts, and she was the only soul alive who read it end to end.

She opened the master ledger to the current folio.

And stopped.

There was an entry she had not written.

It sat near the bottom of the page in a hand she did not know and yet knew instantly, completely, the way you know your own reflection in a dark window — a slanted, hurried, beautiful hand, pressed hard into the paper, the ink still faintly wet after what must have been days. The entry was dated three days from now. It concerned no trade, no vial, no oath. It was a single line, and it named no buyer and no seller.

It named only a date, and a word — *Oathkeeper* — and beneath that, in smaller letters, as if the writer had hesitated:

*She will not believe me until it is too late to be believed.*

Isandro sat very still. The lamp hissed. Down in her chest, her heart — veteran of thirty-nine poisons, trained and steady and loyal — stumbled anyway.

Because she knew that handwriting.

She had spent her whole life not knowing it, having been told at eight years old that her mother was dead and leaving it at that, because asking questions was a luxury for girls whose names appeared in records. But she had seen the hand once, on the single scrap of paper that had come to her with her name on it, folded inside a baby's blanket twenty-three years ago, and she had copied it secretly, obsessively, practicing it in the margins of her schoolbooks until she could reproduce it stroke for stroke.

The entry in the master ledger was written in her mother's hand.

Dated three days from now.

Isandro looked at it for a long time. Then, with the meticulousness that was the whole of her armor, she checked the lock on the folio, checked the seal on the archive room, checked the tally of keys — hers, Corvane's, and the third that no one admitted existed. All accounted for. All untouched.

The ink was still wet.

She did not touch it. She blew out the lamp instead, and sat in the dark among the ledgers of impossible things, holding her own steady hands together in her lap, and understood that the impossible had finally entered her accounts — and that it had entered under her mother's name, three days early, waiting for her to catch up.
