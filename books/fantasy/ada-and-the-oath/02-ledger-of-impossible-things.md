## 2. Ledger of Impossible Things

The guildhall smelled of tallow, old paper, and the particular sweetness of memories that had been decanted too long — a scent like honey left in the sun over something faintly rotten. Isandro had long ago stopped noticing it, the way she'd stopped noticing the taste of arsenic.

She let herself in through the coal chute at half past midnight, as she did every third night, and lit the lamp above the counting desk with the practiced economy of someone who had learned that light was a debt like any other. The guildhall proper — the grand front room where Corvane received clients behind his screen of smoked glass — was dark and dignified. The back room where she worked was neither. It was a closet of shelving and ledgers, a stool worn to the shape of her, and a window too high to see out of but just wide enough to admit the smell of the river when the wind turned wrong.

Isandro Dunmore, royal food-taster by day, keeper of accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things by night, sat down and uncapped her ink.

"Right," she said to the empty room, because talking to oneself was free and everything else here cost something. "Let's see who sold their souls this week."

* * *

The ledger opened to Tuesday's trades. She began, as always, with the small stuff.

Three vials of memory, purchased from a fishwife on Tanner's Row: one of a wedding, one of a funeral, one of — Isandro squinted at the clerk's notation — *the taste of her late husband's plum duff, retained for sentimental purposes.* She entered it under Intangibles, Culinary, Sentimental, and allowed herself a small smile. People thought the guild traded in grand things. Oaths, destinies, the names of kings. Mostly it traded in plum duff. Mostly people wanted to keep what they already had, or give away what hurt too much to hold.

That was the arithmetic of the place, and Isandro understood arithmetic the way priests understood prayer. A memory sold bought bread. An oath pawned against a future bought a winter's firewood. Everything had a price; nothing had a price you could see until it came due. She kept the books because the books were honest even when the trade wasn't, and because honesty, in her experience, was safest kept somewhere no one would ever look for it.

Next entry: an oath, pawned. A cooper from Millbridge had sworn his firstborn's name against a loan, which was exactly as grim as it sounded, except that the guild's terms were gentler than most moneylenders' — the man could redeem the oath within seven years, plus interest paid in hours of honest labor. Isandro recorded the collateral, the term, the redemption clause, and noted in the margin, in her own tight hand: *Cooper cries easily. Watch him. May need to talk him out of it before spring.*

Corvane would pretend not to read her margins. He always read her margins.

Then there was the odd lot. There was always an odd lot. This week: a woman who had sold the memory of her own reflection (Isandro wrote *vanity, reverse direction* and moved on); a soldier who had pawned the sound of his mother's voice against a bet he was almost certain to lose (*almost certain is not certain*, she noted); and a gentleman of no fixed address who had attempted to sell a memory he did not actually possess — specifically, the memory of dancing with the queen at the Midwinter Ball, which had never happened, to anyone, least of all to him.

Isandro wrote: *Rejected. Cannot sell what never was. Also, sir, the queen does not dance. Ask literally anyone.*

She was halfway through reconciling the vial inventory — forty-one memories in stock, six reserved, two expiring — when she noticed the discrepancy.

It was a small thing. That was what unsettled her about it afterward, when she had time to be properly frightened: not some dramatic blot or burning page, but a neat little column of figures that didn't sum. The intake ledger said thirty-nine memories received this week. The sales ledger said thirty-seven sold or stored. Two vials unaccounted for.

Isandro did not make errors. Errors, in her line of work, got people poisoned. She went through the week again, line by line, tongue between her teeth. Nothing. She checked the reserve shelf log. Nothing. She checked it a third time out of pure spite, and that was when she saw it.

There was an entry she hadn't written.

It sat at the bottom of Thursday's page, below her last notation, in ink that looked identical to hers and was not. She knew her own ink the way she knew her own heartbeat — guild ink, oath-ink, the stuff that remembered. This was oath-ink. But the hand —

The hand was not hers.

It was a slanted, quick hand, letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line, with a peculiar hooked descender on the g's. Isandro stared at it for a long moment, feeling the room get colder around her, because she knew that hand. She had grown up knowing that hand. It was on the flyleaf of the only book she owned. It was on the letter she kept folded inside her left boot, unread these eleven years because reading it felt like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurt. (It always still hurt.)

It was her mother's handwriting. Her mother, who had been dead — or gone, which in Isandro's private accounting amounted to the same figure — since Isandro was nine years old.

And the entry was dated three days from now.

* * *

She read it perhaps forty times. She could recite it by the twentieth.

*Received: one map, sealed. Bearer: the taster. Terms: none given. Payment deferred.*

"The taster," Isandro said aloud, to the lamp. "That's me. That's — I'm the taster."

The lamp, being a lamp, declined to comment.

Her first instinct, honed by years at court, was to look over her shoulder. The room was empty. Her second instinct was to close the ledger and walk away and never come back, and she was genuinely surprised by how strong that instinct was — strong enough to make her stand, strong enough to put her hand flat on the cover. But then she thought of the margins. Of the cooper who cried easily. Of the fishwife's plum duff. Of every small honest figure she had ever set down in this book, and how the whole edifice of the guild — strange and mercenary and occasionally cruel as it was — rested on the premise that the accounts were true.

Someone had written a lie into her ledger. Or worse: someone had written a truth three days early, and truths written early were the kind of thing Isandro had only heard about in the oldest entries, the ones in the locked cabinet, the ones even Corvane handled with gloves.

She sat back down.

"Payment deferred," she murmured. Deferred to whom? Deferred until when? And received from whom? The entry listed no seller. Every other entry in fourteen years of her meticulous bookkeeping named its parties. This one had a bearer — *the taster* — and a thing received — *one map, sealed* — and nothing else. As if whoever wrote it had known the shape of the transaction but not, or not yet, its substance.

As if the ledger were waiting for her to catch up to it.

Isandro laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, the laugh of a woman who tasted poison for a living and had just found some in her own soup. She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Think, she told herself, in the voice she used at the tasting table. Clinical. Calm. What do we know?

We know the ink is real. We know the hand is real — she had spent her childhood watching that hand write shopping lists and smugglers' tallies and, once, a birthday card with a drawing of a cat so bad it had made young Isandro cry with laughter. We know my mother has been dead eleven years. Therefore either the dead are writing in my ledgers, or someone alive writes in a dead woman's hand, or —

Or the third possibility, the one she reached for last because reaching for it felt like stepping off a dock at night: the guild's ink remembered, and sometimes what it remembered hadn't happened yet. The oldest clerks whispered about such things. Entries that arrived before their events. Trades recorded before the traders were born. They called them *forward postings*, and they called the clerks who tried to chase them down *short-lived*.

Isandro had always assumed the whispers were guild folklore, the same category as the story that Corvane had no reflection and the one about the client who'd sold his shadow and come back for it. Comfortable nonsense. Nonsense with a function: it kept junior clerks from asking questions.

She was not a junior clerk. She was the keeper of the books, self-appointed, self-taught, invisible by design — and something impossible had just strolled into her accounts without so much as wiping its feet.

"Terms: none given," she repeated. "Well. That won't do."

Because here was the thing about Isandro Dunmore, the thing nobody at court suspected and only Corvane half-knew: she could not abide an unbalanced line. A missing figure itched in her like a stone in a shoe. She had once stayed up until dawn hunting a discrepancy of four coppers, and when she found it — a clerk's transposition, harmless, trivial — she had felt a peace so profound it was nearly religious. The world, balanced, was bearable. The world, unbalanced, was a broth of unknown composition, and Isandro Dunmore had never in her life swallowed anything she couldn't name.

She turned to a fresh page. At the top she wrote, in ordinary ink, in her own unmistakable hand:

*Investigation. Opened this night. Subject: forward posting, Thursday next. Query: origin of entry; identity of bearer ("the taster"); nature of item ("one map, sealed"); counterparty ("terms: none given").*

Below it she added, after a moment's thought:

*Note: I do not currently possess any map, sealed or otherwise. If in three days I do, I should very much like to know from whom.*

She underlined that last sentence twice. Then, because the night was deep and her nerve was either very good or very bad, she did the thing she had promised herself she wouldn't.

She took her mother's letter out of her boot.

* * *

Eleven years, and the paper had gone soft as cloth at the folds. The handwriting on the outside was the same slanted runner's script: *For Isandro, when she needs it.* She had decided long ago that she would never need it. Needing it meant admitting the account would never balance — that her mother had left with debts outstanding and no forwarding address in any ledger, guild or mortal.

She unfolded it now, hands steady in the way they were steady over a suspect dish, and read it for the first time since she was nineteen and had gotten drunk enough on stolen wine to try.

Most of it she remembered. The apologies, vague and plentiful. The instruction — *never let them weigh you, little bird; be the error in their sums* — which had become, though her mother could never have known it, the entire architecture of Isandro's adult life. And then the last lines, which she had forgotten, or which had waited eleven years to be heard:

*If a day comes when you find my writing where it shouldn't be — in a book, on a wall, in your own accounts — don't run, and don't tell Corvane. Come find me instead. I left you a road. It starts at the salt gate.*

Isandro set the letter down very carefully, as though it might go off.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, the river wind turned, and the smell of low tide crept through the high window. She became aware, gradually, of her own heart going like a drum in a procession — not fast, but insistent, a rhythm with somewhere to be.

"Don't run," she said. "Don't tell Corvane." A short, disbelieving breath. "You clearly never met me, madam, or you'd know running is the one thing I'm superb at, and telling Corvane is the one thing I'll die before doing."

But even as she said it, she knew the shape of the trap her mother had left her in, whether knowingly or not. Don't run meant stay and be found. Don't tell Corvane meant face whatever came alone. And *come find me* — that was the hook of it, the barb buried deep in the sweetest bait a fatherless, motherless, friendless woman had ever been offered. Come find me. As if finding were possible. As if the dead kept addresses.

Isandro looked at the forward posting one more time. *One map, sealed. Bearer: the taster.* Three days.

She closed both ledgers, squared them, tied them with their cord, and sat for a while in the lamplight doing absolutely nothing, which for Isandro Dunmore was the loudest scream available to her.

Then she blew out the lamp, folded the letter back into her boot, and climbed out through the coal chute into the cold and honest dark, where the only thing written anywhere was the frost on the railings, and even that, she noticed, looked suspiciously like handwriting.

Three days, she thought, walking home along the wet cobbles with her collar up and her mind racing. In three days, someone was going to hand her a map. And whatever door it opened, her mother had already signed for it.

She just hoped, with a taster's instinct for doom settling cold and familiar in her stomach, that whatever came sealed inside it wouldn't need testing first.

Behind her, in the dark guildhall, the ledger lay shut and patient, holding its one impossible line the way a mouth holds a secret — and, like all the guild's ink, remembering.
