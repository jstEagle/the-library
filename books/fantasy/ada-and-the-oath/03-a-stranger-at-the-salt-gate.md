## 3. A Stranger at the Salt Gate

There were three reliable ways to learn a city's secrets, and Isandro Dunmore had tried all of them. The first was to be beautiful; this had never been an option, as she had the sort of face that witnesses described to magistrates as "a woman, I think, possibly." The second was to be rich, and her wages as royal food-taster amounted to a room, two meals, and the occasional hazard of dying, which the Crown did not consider compensable. The third — the only one that had ever worked — was to be so thoroughly unremarkable that people forgot you were in the room, which was how Isandro had come to know more about the capital than the spymaster, the archbishop, and quite possibly the king.

Tonight, the docks were teaching her a fourth way: to be in the wrong place at exactly the wrong time.

She had come to the Salt Gate district on guild business, which was to say she had come to collect a debt from a fishwife named Berta Coll, who had pawned the memory of her first husband in exchange for a winter's worth of luck and had spent three months trying to weasel out of the interest. Isandro did not personally approve of the memory trade — she had opinions about it, in fact, long and complicated opinions she kept in a locked drawer of her mind next to her opinions about her own employment — but a ledger was a ledger, and the Guild of Impossible Things did not forgive late payments any more than the tide forgave a moored boat.

Berta had paid. Grudgingly, with much muttering about vultures and bloodsuckers, but she had paid, and now Isandro was walking back along the wharf with a small purse of coin and a smaller sense of satisfaction, watching the fishing boats knock their lantern-lit heads together in the swell. The evening smelled of brine and tar and the frying onions that made the Salt Gate district, in her private opinion, the only part of the city worth smelling. Gulls argued overhead about something. A drunk was singing a song about a mermaid with what he clearly believed were the mermaid's own words.

It was, all things considered, a fine night to be nobody.

She was halfway past the salt sheds when someone grabbed her wrist.

Her training took over before her fear did. She turned with the motion, dropped her weight, and had two fingers pressed to the underside of the stranger's jaw — checking the pulse, always the pulse first, the taster's reflex — before her mind caught up with her hands.

The man was dying. There was no clinical ambiguity about it. He had a wound low on his left side, and the dark spreading across his shirt was not the slow seep of a cut but the rapid flood of something that had opened an artery or worse, and his skin had gone the particular gray-yellow that Isandro had seen on three men and one very unlucky dog. He was perhaps fifty, weathered, with rope-scarred hands and the salt-crusted boots of a working sailor, and he was looking at her with an urgency that made her stomach drop.

"You're her," he said. "The ledger-keeper's girl."

"I'm nobody's girl," Isandro said automatically. "You're bleeding out. Sit down before you fall down."

He did not sit down. He did something worse: he laughed, a wet, broken sound, and pressed something into her hands — a scroll, wax-sealed, heavy, wrapped in oilcloth that had been tied and retied and tied again, the knots of a man who had expected to be searched.

"You have to take it," he said. "It can't be found on me. It can't be found at all. But it has to *exist*, do you understand? It has to exist somewhere, and it can't be anywhere they'd look."

"I genuinely don't understand," Isandro said. "Sir. Friend. You are bleeding on a public wharf. There is a watch post forty paces that way, and if you let me shout for—"

"No watch." His grip tightened on her wrist with the last of a dying man's unreasonable strength. "No watch, no guild, no *Crown*. You hear me? No one who keeps books."

Which, Isandro thought with a lurch, eliminated rather a lot of her life.

"Who told you about me?" she asked, and hated how small her voice came out. "Who sent you?"

"No one sent me." He sagged against the salt shed wall, sliding down it, and she went down with him, kneeling on the wet boards, her good wool skirt soaking up harbor water without complaint because wool was loyal that way. "I found the ledger myself. Years ago. I found *her* ledger." His eyes were going glassy, but they fixed on hers with terrible focus, the way drowning men were said to fix on lights. "The handwriting. I'd know it anywhere. I spent twenty years looking for that handwriting."

Isandro's mouth went dry. "Whose handwriting?"

"You know whose." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had ever seen on a dock, and she had once watched Berta Coll weep over a vial. "The woman who wrote the impossible entries. You have her ink. I've seen it. You have her *hand*."

She wanted to say he was mad. She wanted to say it very badly, the way one wants to say *no* when a physician is walking toward one's bed with a certain expression. But her hands — her traitor, meticulous, cataloguing hands — had already turned the scroll over, and through the oilcloth she could feel the shape of it, the weight of good vellum, and something in her chest was doing a thing that hearts were not supposed to do.

"Take it," the man said. "Oathkeeper."

"I'm not—" she began.

"Oathkeeper," he said again, softer, like a man setting down a load he had carried too far. "She said you'd be. She said it the night she—"

He never finished. His hand went slack around her wrist, and his head tipped back against the salt shed, and the gulls went on arguing overhead, and the drunk went on singing about the mermaid, and the harbor went on knocking its boats together as if nothing at all had happened, because to the harbor nothing had. Men died on the Salt Gate docks the way fish died in the holds: routinely, unremarkably, and with a smell that lingered.

Isandro knelt there for the space of three heartbeats, holding a dead stranger's scroll and a dead stranger's secret, and did the only thing her training had ever prepared her for.

She catalogued.

Cause of death: hemorrhage, abdominal, likely a blade, angled upward — no, wait, she corrected herself, pressing two fingers into the ruined shirt with a clinical detachment that would have horrified anyone watching, the wound was angled *down and in*, which meant the attacker had been taller, or above him, or—

She stopped. She made herself stop, because she had just caught herself performing an autopsy on a man who had called her *Oathkeeper* with his last breath, and because somewhere down the wharf, boots were striking boards in the particular rhythm of men who were not drunk and not fishing.

Two sets of boots. City watch, by the cadence — the watch walked like they were owed money. And it was the misfortune of every honest citizen that a dead body on the docks was everyone's business, and the further misfortune of Isandro Dunmore in particular that she was crouched over it with blood on her skirt and a sealed parcel in her hands.

The sensible thing — the invisible thing, the thing that had kept her alive through nineteen years of tasting other people's dinners — was to put the scroll down, stand up, and become a shocked witness. *I found him like this, officers. I was walking back from my aunt's. A woman, I think, possibly.* The watch would question her for an hour, lose interest, and write her down as nobody, and by morning she would be exactly as she had always been: unrecorded, unremarkable, safe.

She did not do the sensible thing.

She would spend the rest of her life not entirely sure why. Later she would blame the handwriting, though she hadn't seen it yet. Later still she would blame the word *Oathkeeper*, which had lodged under her ribs like a fishbone. But in the moment, kneeling on the wet boards with the harbor breathing its onion-and-brine breath around her, Isandro did something she had never once done in her careful, poisoned, well-managed life.

She acted on instinct.

The scroll went down the front of her bodice, oilcloth and all, cold as a corpse against her skin. She stood, wiped her hands on her skirt — adding blood to harbor water, excellent, very inconspicuous — and walked. Not ran. Running was a confession with legs. She walked, at the pace of a woman late for nothing in particular, past the frying onions, past the mermaid song, past the watch post itself, where a young officer glanced up from his ledger and then, bless him and all his dull bureaucratic descendants, glanced back down.

She made it thirty paces past the post before her legs informed her that they had opinions about what she had just done.

She turned down the fish-gutting lane, put her hand against a wall that stank of a decade of mackerel, and breathed. In through the nose, as she'd taught herself during her first week at court, when a nervous cook had salted the king's pottage with something that was not entirely salt. Out through the mouth. Again. The scroll pressed against her sternum like a second heart, and her actual heart was doing its level best to keep up.

*You have her hand. I've seen her ink.*

Her mother had been dead — unrecorded, unfindable, *unledgered* — for as long as Isandro could remember anything. There was no body, no grave, no last letter, no locket. There was a name on a guild register that Isandro had found at nineteen and never spoken aloud since, and a gap in the ledgers the size of an entire life, and now there was a dying stranger on a dock who had spent twenty years chasing that gap and had died handing the chase to a woman he'd never met.

*She said you'd be. She said it the night she—*

The night she *what*, Isandro thought, with a desperation that frightened her more than the watch had. The night she *what*?

"Evening, miss."

Isandro's soul briefly left her body.

The speaker was a gutting-hand, one of the lane's permanent residents, an ancient woman with forearms like anchor chain and a boning knife that had outlived four husbands. She was looking at Isandro with the mild, all-seeing expression of someone who had watched every kind of trouble walk this lane and been impressed by none of it.

"You've got a dead man's blood on your hem," the old woman observed.

"I — he — I was trying to help him."

"Mm." The old woman went back to her mackerel. "Watch is asking after a woman in a gray skirt. You might want to be a woman in a brown skirt. There's a chandler two lanes over keeps secondhand clothes. Tell him Marta sent you, and don't let him charge you full price, the thief."

Isandro stared at her. "Why are you helping me?"

Marta's knife paused, mid-fillet. "Because I've been gutting fish on this lane for forty years, girl, and I know the difference between someone running *from* a thing and someone running *with* one." She nodded at Isandro's midsection, where the scroll made a faint, damning ridge beneath the bloodstained wool. "You're running *with* one. That's a different animal entirely." The knife resumed its work. "Brown skirt. Full price is robbery."

* * *

The chandler's back room was dark, smelled of tallow and hemp, and contained exactly one cracked mirror, which Isandro chose to interpret as the universe being kind. She changed out of the gray skirt — Marta had been right, the watch was asking, a boy had come through the lanes whistling the watch's three-note signal while pretending to sell eels — and into a brown skirt that fit as though it had been waiting for her, which she chose not to think about.

The scroll she examined by the light of a single candle, with the door barred and her back to the wall, because she was frightened, not stupid.

The oilcloth came away in her hands. The knots had been tied by someone who expected the tying to matter, and she untied them the way she did everything: slowly, precisely, memorizing each one in case the order turned out to mean something later.

Inside was a map.

Not a map of anywhere she knew — and Isandro knew maps, had spent idle court hours memorizing the city's every alley the way other women memorized songs. This was drawn in that same strange ink the guild used for its impossible entries, the ink that never quite dried, that seemed to shift at the edge of vision like something swimming just beneath the surface of the page. The lines were fine and sure and *familiar* — the handwriting of whoever had drawn it sat in her chest like a hand around her heart, because she had spent nineteen years wondering what her mother's handwriting looked like, and now she knew, and it looked like her own.

The map showed the city. Her city. But underneath the city — beneath the palace itself, the lines descending in careful, patient strokes, past the cellars, past the cisterns, past anything any surveyor had ever charted — there was more. Stairs. A sealed door, marked with a symbol she did not know and her body, unhelpfully, *did* — a cold recognition, a prickle along her forearms, the same feeling she got an instant before a dish was poisoned, except now the poisoned thing was her own bloodline.

And at the bottom of the descending lines, in her mother's sure, fine hand, two words:

*The Oathstone.*

Isandro sat very still in the chandler's back room and listened to the harbor breathing and thought, with the strange calm that had always come to her at the worst moments, the calm that had let her swallow arsenic with her soup and smile at the queen:

So that's what it's called.

She had carried the word *Oathkeeper* against her ribs for an hour now without understanding it. She understood the map no better — less, if anything, because maps of places that didn't exist raised questions that maps of places that did were kind enough not to. But she understood two things with total clarity, the way she understood the difference between hemlock and nightshade by smell alone.

The first was that a man had died on the Salt Gate docks to put this in her hands, and whoever had opened him up would come looking for it, and for her, and they would not be two bored watchmen asking after a gray skirt.

The second was that she could not take this to the guild. Corvane would want it — Corvane would *pay* for it, beautifully, generously, and then it would be in the ledgers, and everything that touched the ledgers eventually touched the Crown, and whatever lay at the bottom of those stairs had been hidden from the Crown for a very long time by a woman who had written in ink that didn't dry.

Which left her. Isandro Dunmore, who had spent her whole life being no one, in possession of the one object in the world that could not afford to belong to anyone.

She thought about the sensible thing. She thought about it the way one thinks about a warm bed in winter from the middle of a cold river.

Then she folded the map along its mother-made creases, tucked it back against her heart, and blew out the candle.

Outside, the gulls had gone quiet, and somewhere across the dark water, a bell she had never noticed before was ringing — slow, deliberate, three strokes, a pause, three strokes again — and every fishwife on the lane had stopped working to listen.

Isandro pulled the brown skirt straight, put her hood up, and went to find out what she was now the keeper of.
