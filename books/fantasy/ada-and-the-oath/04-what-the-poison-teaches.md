## 4. What the Poison Teaches

The morning the queen was nearly poisoned with foxglove, I was thinking about wax.

Not the seal on the map — though I had spent half the night thinking about that too, holding it to candlelight, turning it, looking for the maker's mark in its depths. No. This wax was on a wheel of cheese, and it had been cut wrong.

I noticed it from three paces away, which is the distance at which a good taster notices everything and a great taster pretends not to. The rind of a Marchmont blue should be opened in a spiral, so the knife never drags through the paste and picks up whatever lives on the blade. Someone had hacked this one open like an oyster. Either the pantry boy was new — possible; there had been a new pantry boy every month since the old one ran off with a fishmonger's daughter — or someone wanted the queen's cheese handled by as many careless hands as possible before it reached her plate.

I stood at my station behind the queen's right shoulder and let the court swirl around me. Courtiers are weather. You learn to read them without looking directly at them, the way sailors read clouds. Today the weather said: *something is coming, and everyone hopes it will strike someone else first.*

"Isandro." The queen did not turn her head. Queen Maren never turned her head for me; she spoke to me the way one speaks to a wall that has proven reliable. "You're frowning at the cheese."

"I'm admiring the cheese, Majesty."

"You are doing arithmetic at the cheese. I can tell. Your lips move when you count."

They did not move. But I liked her for believing they might, so I stepped forward, took the proffered sliver, and ate it with the ceremony the court expected: chew, pause, swallow, wait. The waiting is the part they watch. Anyone can eat. It takes a certain reputation to make forty nobles hold their breath over a woman chewing cheese.

Foxglove has a sweetness to it, if you've trained yourself past the point where sweetness means anything but a number. There was none here. Just the blue, sharp and lovely, and beneath it the faintest ghost of the cellar where it had ripened — damp stone, old oak, something mineral and deep.

*Deep,* said a voice in my head that sounded suspiciously like my own common sense. *Everything down there is deep.*

I swallowed, bowed, and pronounced the cheese fit. The breath went out of the room. Somewhere a lute resumed, embarrassed.

* * *

Here is what nobody at court understands about poison work: it is mostly reading. A taster who only knows flavors knows nothing. A taster who knows ledgers — who knows which merchant owed which lord money last quarter, whose lands border whose, which kitchen boy's sister married into the Vell family that lost the river tolls — that taster can taste a dish and hear it whisper *who* before it ever whispers *what*.

I keep two ledgers, you see. One belongs to the Guild of Impossible Things, locked in my chest under a floorboard, full of memories sold by the vial and oaths pawned against futures. The other lives entirely in my head, and it is fuller.

And now there was a third document competing for space: a square of oilskin, folded eight times, sewn — sewn, not tucked, *sewn* — into the lining of my left sleeve, because a dead man had bled out against my ribs two nights ago whispering *Oathkeeper*, and because the city watch had come around the salt gate asking after "a woman seen speaking to the deceased," and because Lady Vess's grey-eyed clerks had been seen asking after the same thing in nicer clothes.

A dead stranger had pressed a map into my hands. I had spent two nights telling myself I would burn it.

Instead I had memorized it.

This is the trick they don't teach you in the guildhalls: paper can be found on you. Paper can be taken from your corpse and read aloud in front of a tribunal. But a map carried in memory leaves no ink, no crease, no evidence except competence — and competence in a food-taster is invisible by design. So I had sat up until the third bell with the map unfolded on my knees, tracing its lines by touch more than sight, and I had committed it to the ledger in my head, page by page, and then I had burned the original in the guildhall's forge and watched the wax seal bubble and go black.

Mostly I felt relieved. Underneath the relief, quietly, the way foxglove hides under honey, I felt like a woman who had burned a letter from her mother without reading it twice.

The map showed the palace cellars. That was the first horror, and I had found it quickly. The second horror took longer, because the map was drawn in the old survey style, all compass-bearings and chain-lengths, and whoever drew it had measured from landmarks that no longer existed. The Salt Gate had moved sixty years ago. The old cistern was filled in. The bear-pit was now a rose garden that Her Majesty pretended to enjoy.

But bearings do not lie, even when their landmarks die. And when I laid the map's lines over everything I knew of the palace — and I knew the palace the way a rat knows a ship, from the inside, along routes no architect would admit to — the crossing point of the two longest measured lines came to rest somewhere I knew very well indeed.

Somewhere I had walked past a hundred times, carrying trays.

*Under the wine cellar,* I thought, watching the queen dab her mouth with linen. *Under the oldest part of the wine cellar, below the shelf where they keep the vintages too valuable to drink.*

The Oathstone. The lock on three centuries of peace between humankind and the fae who had vanished beyond the borders. Buried beneath the palace. Beneath the queen's dinner table. Beneath, if my arithmetic was honest, roughly the spot where I was standing.

I kept my face pleasant and my hands still, and inside, some small careful part of me began to scream in a very organized way.

* * *

The soup course arrived at midday, and with it, trouble wearing silk.

"The pheasant broth," announced the steward, "from His Lordship Chancellor Ode's own kitchens, as a gift to Her Majesty."

Every head at the high table performed the same small calculation at once: the Chancellor does not give gifts. The Chancellor gives invoices dressed as gifts. The queen's fingers paused on her napkin, and I saw her look at the broth the way one looks at a dog that has bitten before.

I stepped forward and took the tasting spoon.

Warmth, first. Then marrow, properly drawn, long-simmered — someone in the Chancellor's kitchens could cook, which meant someone above them couldn't, which meant politics. Then herbs: thyme, a bay leaf gone slightly stale, parsley added late. And underneath all of it, faint as a rumor—

Bitterness. Not enough to name. Not enough to accuse. Just a shadow at the bottom of the flavor, the kind of note that might be a herb stem scorched against the pot, or might be nothing, or might be the opening movement of something patient.

I held the spoonful on my tongue and counted. One. Two. Three. The bitterness didn't grow. Foxglove grows. Hemlock grows. Whatever this was either wasn't poison or was the laziest poison ever attempted.

I swallowed and nodded. "Fit, Majesty."

"You hesitated," said a voice from the lower table, mild as milk.

Lady Vess did not look up from her plate when she said it. She was a small woman, grey as her eyes, with the unremarkable face of a person you would describe for an hour and fail to draw afterward. She was the Crown's spymaster, which everyone knew, and she was very good at it, which almost no one knew, because the people who learned how good she was generally stopped being in a position to discuss it.

"I always hesitate," I said. "It's the job. Haste is how tasters die."

"And yet you hesitated *longer*." She speared a pear slice with perfect delicacy. "One might think the broth reminded you of something."

"One might think the broth reminded me of Tuesday's broth, which also had a scorched herb in it, and which I also survived." I smiled the smile I use on nervous courtiers: warm, empty, slightly dim. "My lord Chancellor's kitchens have been heavy-handed with the bay all season. Perhaps he's grieving something."

A ripple of laughter, quickly smothered — you do not laugh at the Chancellor near the queen unless the queen laughs first. The queen did not laugh. She was watching me with those pale, tired eyes that missed less than anyone gave her credit for.

Vess smiled too, and hers was worse than no smile at all. "Heavy-handed bay," she repeated softly, as though filing it. "How thorough you are, Mistress Dunmore. They say you can taste a lie in a sauce."

"They say many flattering things about cooks' assistants, my lady."

"Do they say where you were on Thursday night? Near the salt gate?"

The lute player, gods bless his oblivious heart, chose that moment to reach the difficult passage, and the room's attention slid toward him like water finding a crack. I used the cover to breathe.

"I was ill," I said. "Bad oyster. I spent the evening being thoroughly punished by the sea."

"Mm." Vess returned to her pears. "The sea does keep accounts, doesn't it. So few of us escape settling ours."

She said it lightly, the way one comments on weather. But I had spent my life learning the difference between a remark and a message, and that one had a direction and a weight, and both of them were aimed at me.

* * *

After the tables were cleared, the queen dismissed her ladies and asked — casually, staring out the window at her rose garden that had once been a bear-pit — whether I would stay a moment.

There is exactly one correct answer to that question, and it is yes.

"Your hands are shaking," she observed, still facing the glass.

"They're not, Majesty."

"No," she agreed. "But they want to. There's a difference, and I know it intimately." She turned. Up close, without the crown and the distance, she looked less like a queen and more like a woman who had been sitting in a chair for thirty years while other people decided what she would eat. Which, I reflected, was precisely what she was. "You tasted hesitation today. Twice. Once at the cheese and once at the broth. You are the most precise instrument in my household, Isandro, and instruments do not develop hesitation unless something has entered them."

I bowed my head. "Something entered the broth, Majesty."

"That is not what I asked, and we both know it." She came closer. She smelled of lavender and, faintly, of the foxglove tincture her physicians gave her for her heart — I had catalogued that sweetness years ago and said nothing, because a taster who reports the royal physician poisons her own career. "Someone tried to kill me in March. Slowly. Kindly. With such small doses that even my wolf of a taster called every dish fit. Do you remember?"

I remembered. I had tasted those doses myself, day after day, feeling the accumulation like sand filling a shoe, unable to prove intent, unwilling to cry wolf. In the end it had been the queen's own suspicion, not my palate, that caught it — she had stopped eating the honeyed figs, and the councilor sending them had grown desperate, and desperation is the loudest flavor there is.

"I remember, Majesty."

"When it happened, you looked at me the way you're looking at the window now. As if something behind the glass matters more than the room." Her voice gentled. "I am not Vess. I do not set clerks on my servants for having secrets. Everyone in this palace has secrets; it's how the walls stay standing. But I will tell you a thing I have learned about secrets, taster, and you may carry it with yours."

She waited until I looked up.

"A secret buried under your feet is heavier than one carried in your arms. Because you cannot put it down, and you cannot run, and every day it grows roots." Her eyes held mine, pale and kind and terribly awake. "Whatever you swallowed besides my broth — I hope you spit it out in time."

Then she rang for her ladies, and I was dismissed, and I walked the length of the gallery with my spine straight and my mind roaring.

* * *

That night I did not go to the guildhall. Corvane's people would be watching the approaches since the salt-gate murder — or Vess would be watching them watching — and I could not afford the walk. Instead I sat in my narrow room above the scullery with a stub of candle and did what I do best: arithmetic.

The map's long axis ran from the old Salt Gate bearing south-by-west, four hundred and twelve chains. The short axis ran off the drowned cistern due east, ninety-seven. Cross them, allow for the gate's relocation, and the intersection fell—

Under the wine cellar. Specifically, under the corner where the stair descends to the sealed sub-cellar that the stewards call "the flood vault" and never open, because the door is stone without handle or hinge, and the last steward who ordered it forced lost his position and, shortly thereafter, his mind. I had carried candles down that stair a dozen times. I had stood within ten feet of the door that guarded the peace of the world, and complained about the damp.

I laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, and I pressed my hand over my mouth.

Three centuries ago, the story said, the fae had broken faith and been exiled beyond the borders by the Oath, sealed into their banishment by a stone of binding, and the humans had built their peace on top of the victory. Every child learned it. Every ballad sang it. *The fae forswore, the fae were banished, the stone keeps them beyond the veil.*

But maps are written by victors, and victors bury things. If the stone lay *here* — beneath the throne itself, beneath the queen's table, beneath my station at her right hand — then the Oath was not a wall erected at the border. It was a lock installed in the basement. And locks, unlike walls, imply a door. And doors imply that someone, at some point, intended to open them again.

Or intended to make sure no one ever did.

The candle guttered. Down in the wine cellar, ten feet of stone and three hundred years away, the Oathstone waited under its hingeless door, and I thought of the dying man's blood soaking my sleeve, and the word he'd spent his last breath on.

*Oathkeeper.*

He hadn't said *thief*. He hadn't said *seller* or *buyer* or *destroyer*. He'd looked at me — a stranger, a dockside nobody — and given me a title. Which meant either that he was delirious, or that he knew something about me that I did not.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the palace breathe above its secret, and told myself the sensible things: burn the knowledge, forget the bearings, be invisible again, be safe.

Then, from the courtyard below, came the sound I had been dreading all evening and pretending was carriage wheels: boots. Many boots, moving in formation, stopping at the scullery stair.

And a voice, mild as milk, saying: "Mistress Dunmore's rooms. Gently, gentlemen. We wouldn't want to disturb her counting."
