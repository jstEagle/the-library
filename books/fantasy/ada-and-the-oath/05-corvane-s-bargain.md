## 5. Corvane's Bargain

The guildhall smelled of beeswax and old paper and, faintly, of the river at low tide—the way all important places smell when they have been important long enough to stop pretending otherwise.

I had been coming to the Salt Gate guildhall for six years, first as a courier's daughter with quick hands and no questions, then as a taster whose palate could identify a poison to within a hair of its lethal dose, and finally as the keeper of ledgers that officially did not exist. In all that time I had learned one rule above every other: never be interesting.

So when Guildmaster Corvane said, from the shadows of his own study, "You have it in your coat," my entire body went cold in a way no broth had ever managed.

I did not turn around immediately. That was pride, and pride is a luxury for people who are seen. Instead I finished hanging my wet cloak on the peg by the door—rain again; the city had wept for three days straight as though it knew something—and arranged my face into the mild, slightly bored expression of a bookkeeper who has been asked an ordinary question about ordinary ink.

"I have many things in my coat," I said. "A pencil stub. Half a honey cake. A receipt for eels."

"Isandro." He said my name the way other people said *please*, or *stop*. "The map."

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew of Corvane before that night, because what I knew afterward is a different country entirely.

He was the master of the Guild of Impossible Things, which is not a guild in any sense the Crown would recognize. The Crown's guilds traded in wool and salt and the labor of backs. Ours traded in memories by the vial, oaths pawned against futures, last words bottled for the grieving, courage rented by the hour to men who needed to walk into rooms they would not otherwise survive. We kept accounts of things that could not be weighed, and I kept those accounts, because I was precise, because I was invisible, and because—for reasons I told myself were practical—I had never wanted to belong anywhere enough to be recorded there.

Corvane was tall in the way of things built to be looked up at, gray at the temples, with hands that moved like he was always halfway through explaining something beautiful. He wore mourning colors without ever seeming to mourn. And he had a habit, dangerous in a man like him, of looking directly at people.

At me, specifically. Which no one did. Being unseen was my craft. He treated it like a curtain he was politely waiting for me to draw back myself.

"You searched my room," I said, turning at last. He was seated behind his desk with a fire going, which he only lit when business was meant to feel like comfort. "Or you had it done."

"I asked, and someone obliged. There's a difference, morally if not practically." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit. You're dripping on my ledger floor."

"The floor has survived worse than rain."

"It hasn't survived you looking like that." He leaned forward, elbows on the desk, and studied me with an attention that felt less like scrutiny than like being read aloud. "Three days ago a man died on the docks with your name in his mouth, and you came to work the next morning and entered forty-one trades in the evening ledgers with handwriting steadier than usual, if anything. Do you know what steadier-than-usual handwriting tells me?"

"That I'm professional."

"That you were holding yourself together with both hands and calling it professionalism." He slid something across the desk. A small glass vial, empty, stoppered with wax. "This was found under the body. It's one of ours. Memory-stock, third grade, the cheap kind dockhands sell their worst nights for. Whoever he was, he'd been to a guildhall. Possibly this one."

I sat down. Not because he invited me but because my knees had opinions.

"I don't know the man," I said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "But you know what he gave you, and I confess the not-knowing is eating me alive, which is an indignity I don't tolerate gracefully. So here is what happens now, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide I'm your enemy. Lady Vess has arrested two of our couriers this week. She burned the Southbank annex on Tuesday—with the ledgers inside, Isandro, sixty years of trades gone in an afternoon. She is hunting something, and she is hunting it through us, and whatever the dead man pressed into your hands is the shape of her hunt. If you keep it secret out of loyalty to me, I'll be flattered and probably dead. If you keep it secret out of fear, I understand that too. But I'd rather you keep it out of trust, and trust requires me to show you mine first."

He opened the drawer of his desk and took out a ledger.

Not one of ours. It was older, bound in leather gone soft as cloth, and when he set it down I felt the hairs rise along my arms, because I recognized the hand on the open page.

I had spent two years of my life transcribing my mother's entries from the archive copies. I knew her slanted letters the way you know a voice heard through a wall.

"This is hers," I said. My voice came out strange.

"It is. Your mother kept the master ledger for eleven years before you were born. She was the finest oath-scribe this guild ever produced, and then she left in the night and took nothing, and we never learned why." He turned the page gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "Until three days ago, when a dying man left a sealed map with a royal food-taster, and I began asking old questions, and an old colleague remembered that your mother once made a trade here that she never collected."

"What trade?"

"A memory," Corvane said. "Her own. Deposited, not sold. She left instructions that it be held until—" he paused, and I watched him choose honesty over kindness, or perhaps kindness shaped as honesty, "—until her daughter came asking. Which, I note, you have not done. You've been carrying that map for three days and you haven't come asking. Why?"

Because asking means admitting. Because admitting means being seen. Because everything I am is built on the principle that a woman no one notices can survive anything.

What I said was: "I didn't know there was anything to ask for."

"Liar," he said warmly. "But a polite one. Sit still a moment longer."

* * *

Here is the bargain, as he laid it out, and I give it to you exactly because I have turned it over so many times since that the edges have worn smooth.

Lady Vess would find me—soon, days perhaps. When she did, I would be alone, unrecorded, deniable, disposable. The Crown's spymaster did not torture people like me; she simply erased them, and erasure required no warrant. But the guild could protect me. Sanctuary, papers, passage, watchers at every gate. Corvane would put the whole impossible apparatus between me and Vess's net.

In exchange: my oldest memory. The face of the woman who raised me.

"Not the mother you never knew," he said quietly. "I know better than to ask for that—it isn't yours to give, and besides, I suspect it's already spoken for. I mean the other one. Marta. The cook at the Ashfall orphanage who fed you first when no one else remembered you'd been brought in. The woman whose kitchen you still measure every room against. Give me that face, and it will sit in our vault beside your mother's vial, and you will be safe."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something, the ordinary sound of ordinary life continuing.

"How long?" I asked.

"For as long as you need protection. Years, if it comes to that."

"And after?"

"After, you may buy it back. Our rates are fair." He hesitated. "Isandro, I want you to understand that I hate this offer. I've made it four times in twenty years and I have hated it every time, because it works, and things that work get reused. But I would rather offer you an ugly choice than watch Vess make a choiceless one of you."

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I will set down now because a story owes its keeper honesty: for one full minute, I considered it.

Not because of safety. Safety has never been the currency I think in. I considered it because of the arithmetic of grief. Marta died when I was nine. Every year since, her face has faded a little more, the way all faces do, and I have hoarded what remains like a miser counting coins worn thin. To surrender it—to let the guild hold what my memory could not keep whole—was tempting in the way that putting a wounded bird in a cage is tempting. It would be safe there. Safe and gone.

And underneath that, quieter, more shameful: part of me wanted to know what it would feel like to owe Corvane something. To be bound to another person by a debt that couldn't be entered in a ledger and settled in coin. Six years of double lives, and I had never once been truly known by anyone, and here was a man offering to take a piece of me and keep it, carefully, in the dark, the way you keep a letter you can't throw away.

That is not safety. That is intimacy wearing safety's coat. I was hungry enough to almost mistake them.

* * *

"Ask me why I really came tonight," I said.

Corvane tilted his head. "Why did you?"

"Because of the handwriting." I pulled the map from my coat—the wax seal cracked where I'd opened and resealed it a dozen times, the parchment soft at the folds now from handling—and laid it on his desk between us. "Not the dead man's. The entry in the evening ledger, three days ago. Trade number forty-two. I didn't enter it. It appeared. In my mother's hand. Dated three days from now."

I watched him go very still. For the first time since I'd known him, Corvane looked his age.

"Show me."

I had copied the entry onto scrap paper, because I did not dare bring the ledger itself out of the vault-room, and because some part of me hoped that away from the original ink the words would look like nonsense. They did not. *Received: one map, sealed. Bearer: the daughter. Held against the breaking.* Her slant, her flourishes, her habit of crossing her sevens twice.

Corvane read it three times. Then he did something I had never seen him do: he laughed, short and unamused, at nothing.

"Oath-ink," he said. "Entries written in oath-ink don't record what happened. They record what *will*. Your mother scribed in it her whole career—that's why her ledgers were kept separate, why the masters before me locked them away. We thought it was superstition dressed as procedure." He looked up at me, and whatever calculation had lived in his face all evening was gone, replaced by something rawer. "She wrote your future into our books, Isandro. Three days from now, per her own hand, you deliver that map somewhere, and something breaks."

"The Oath," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. The word had been sitting in my chest since the docks, since the dying man's lips shaped *Oathkeeper* against my ear. "Everyone says the fae were banished by an Oath three centuries ago. Everyone says it can't be broken. But it's in our ledgers, Corvane. Everything breakable passes through our ledgers eventually."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The fire had burned low, and the light did that thing firelight does, making a room feel like the inside of a held breath.

Then Corvane reached across the desk—not for the map, I noted, though his fingers must have ached to take it—and closed my mother's ledger, and pushed both it and the map back toward me.

"Keep them," he said. "Both. And withdraw my offer."

I blinked. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. I offered you a cage because I thought you were carrying a secret. You're carrying a *sentence*, and I won't take payment from a woman mid-sentence." He stood, and for a moment he looked tired in a way the mourning colors had always hidden. "But hear the rest, because I mean it. Whatever you decide to do in three days—deliver it, burn it, feed it to the river—you will not do it alone. Not because of the guild. The guild's protections are ash in Vess's brazier by now. Because of me."

"That's a foolish distinction."

"It's the most honest one I own." He came around the desk, and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could smell cedar and ink and, beneath it, the particular cold-metal scent of someone who handles oath-ink daily and has absorbed it into his skin. "Six years you've walked into this building and out of it, and I have watched you make yourself smaller at every doorway, and I have never once understood how the most necessary person in this guild convinced herself she was furniture. I see you, Isandro Dunmore. I have seen you since the week you arrived, when you corrected the valuation on a bottled goodbye because the seller had undervalued his own grief, and you thought no one noticed."

My throat had gone tight. "Someone should mind the numbers."

"Yes," he said softly. "And someone should mind you. Those are different offices. You've been holding both your whole life, and it's killing you by inches, and you're too proud to call it starving."

I should have stepped back. I want it recorded, even if only here, that I knew I should step back, and that I catalogued the impulse the way I catalogue toxins—identified, measured, declined to swallow. Instead I stood in the failing firelight and let myself be seen, and discovered that being seen does not kill you. It only makes everything afterward harder to survive invisibly.

"The offer's withdrawn," I said, "but the price stands anyway, doesn't it? You already know her face. Marta. You've known all along what you'd ask for, because you knew it was the one thing I couldn't refuse and the one thing I'd regret forever."

"Yes," Corvane said. No flinch, no apology. "I'm not a good man, Isandro. I'm a fair one, and the difference matters mostly to the people it fails."

"Then here's my counter-offer." I folded the map back into my coat, over my heart, where it had been living like a second heartbeat. "No memories change hands. No cages. When the three days run out, I go where the map points, and you may follow me or not, and either way you'll know the truth of it because I'll tell you myself—which is more than I've given anyone in six years, and more expensive than any vial in your vault."

He smiled then, and it transformed him entirely, the way a struck match transforms a dark room.

"Accepted," he said. "Though I warn you, I intend to follow."

"Of course you do. You're a guildmaster. Following trouble is the family business."

* * *

I was nearly at the door when he spoke again, and the warmth had gone out of his voice, replaced by something urgent and low.

"One more thing, and then you go. The man on the docks—before he died, did he say anything else? Anything at all?"

"*Oathkeeper,*" I said. "Only that."

Corvane crossed to the window and looked out at the rain, and when he answered, he was half turned away from me, and I understood that he was choosing, deliberately, to show me his face while he lied or while he confessed—I genuinely could not tell which.

"There's an old entry in your mother's master ledger," he said. "The last one she ever wrote, the night she disappeared. I've read it a hundred times and never understood it. Would you like to hear it?"

The rain filled the silence. I found I was holding my breath.

"She wrote: *The stone remembers what the Oath forgot. Send for my daughter when the taster's portion turns to poison.*"

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell began to ring—the hour, nothing more, just the hour. But standing in that doorway with my mother's sentence folded over my heart and a guildmaster's eyes on my back, I felt the ground tilt beneath the ordinary sound, because I had tasted the queen's broth that very morning, and for the first time in six years of careful, clinical, invisible work—

it had tasted wrong.
