## 6. The Queen's Hunger

There were three hundred and eleven ways to poison a queen, and Maren had made a private study of every one of them.

Not by choice. She had been a girl of nineteen when the crown settled on her head like a bird deciding to nest somewhere inconvenient, and in the twenty-two years since, she had learned that a throne was simply a chair that everyone else wanted you dead in. The council called it *the burden of sovereignty*. The council said many things. The council also poured her wine.

She had noticed it first in her hands. A fine tremor, easily blamed on age—she was forty-one, which was old enough for courtiers to begin murmuring about her health and young enough to find the murmuring insulting. Then the taste: a sweetness at the back of the throat that lingered after honeyed wine should have washed away, like a lie told so often it had begun to believe itself. Then the fatigue, deep and mineral, as though her bones were being slowly replaced with wet sand.

Arsenic, she suspected. Or something subtler. The palace apothecary would know, but the palace apothecary reported to Lord Chancellor Hesse, who smiled too readily, whose rings clicked against his goblet when he toasted her health, and who had recently begun asking—with exquisite delicacy—whether Her Majesty had given thought to the question of succession.

So Maren did what queens do when they cannot trust their physicians: she began her own education.

She kept a little book bound in grey calfskin, hidden behind a false spine in her library between a treatise on drainage and a romance so scandalous it had been banned twice. In it she recorded everything she ate, everything she felt afterward, and everything she noticed about the woman who ate it first.

The taster.

Isandro Dunmore. A small, precise person with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone counting your spoons. She had served at the royal table for six years, and in all that time Maren could not recall seeing her laugh, stumble, spill, or look directly at anyone. She swallowed poisons the way other people swallowed soup—calmly, thoroughly, with an air of quiet professional judgment—and then she would stand there for the required quarter hour while the court pretended she didn't exist.

But here was the thing Maren had noticed, the thing she had never written in the grey book because writing it down would make it real and dangerous: Isandro Dunmore never got sick.

Oh, she went pale sometimes. There was a particular evening two winters past—a pheasant in plum sauce, delicious, deadly—when the girl's lips had gone grey as old pewter and she'd gripped the back of her chair hard enough to whiten her knuckles. But she hadn't fallen over. She hadn't even asked to be excused. And the next morning she'd been at her post again, thin and neat and utterly unbothered, as though swallowing death were merely an unpleasant chore, like mending stockings.

A body built up tolerances, certainly. Tasters did. But there was tolerance, and there was whatever Isandro Dunmore had, which looked less like immunity and more like *acquaintance*. As if she and the poisons had been introduced years ago and had reached some sort of understanding.

Maren found this fascinating. Maren found this useful. Maren found, if she was honest with herself, that she thought about it rather more than was dignified.

* * *

The summons went out on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were dull and dullness provided cover. "Her Majesty requires her taster," said the footman, which meant nothing, because Her Majesty required her taster four times a day. Only Maren knew that this time she had dismissed the maids, the guards, and the ever-present shadow of Lady Vess's people from the morning chamber, and that the only witnesses to what followed would be a cold hearth, a pot of chocolate, and one very suspicious cat.

Isandro arrived at the appointed hour, curtsied to exactly the correct depth, and stood with her hands folded like a woman awaiting sentencing.

"Sit," said Maren.

A flicker of alarm crossed the girl's face—the first expression Maren had ever seen her wear. "Your Majesty, I don't—I haven't tasted anything yet this morning."

"I know. Sit anyway." Maren gestured at the chair across from her, poured the chocolate herself, and pushed the cup across the table. "It's not poisoned. I checked. Well. I watched the cook make it, and then I stood guard over it like a dragon on a very small hoard, so unless Lord Hesse has taken up disguise as a kitchen maid, we're safe."

Isandro sat. Slowly. The way one sits on ice.

"You'll forgive me," Maren said, wrapping her own hands around her cup, "if I skip the pleasantries. I've discovered I have a limited appetite for them lately. Literally." She took a sip and studied the girl over the rim. "How long have you been my taster?"

"Six years, Majesty."

"And in six years, how many times have you been ill? Truly ill. Bed-bound. Dying, even a little."

The pause was precisely one heartbeat too long. "I've been fortunate, Majesty."

"Mm." Maren set down her cup. "Fortunate. You know, I once had a hound who was 'fortunate' in exactly the same way. Ate a poisoned rat meant for him and barely blinked. Turned out his previous owner had been dosing him since he was a pup—building up his resistance, the way you'd build up a wall, stone by stone. Cruel method. Effective." She let that sit in the warm chocolate-scented silence. "Someone trained you, Isandro. Someone fed you poison in small doses until death lost its teeth where you were concerned. I'd very much like to know who."

The girl's face did something remarkable: nothing at all, magnificently. But her hands, folded in her lap, tightened on each other like two people clinging to a ledge.

"I'm sorry, Majesty. I don't understand the question."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "You understand it perfectly. That's why you're frightened. If you didn't understand it, you'd simply be confused, and confusion looks quite different—it makes people talk too much. You're talking too little." She leaned back. "Which is itself an answer, and a rather interesting one."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The cat, a vast grey creature named Disapproval, jumped onto the table and settled himself between them with the confidence of a third party to the negotiation.

Then Isandro said, quietly, "May I speak honestly, Majesty? Knowing it may cost me."

"That," said Maren, "is the only kind of speech worth having. Yes."

"I learned to survive poison because it was the only way to survive anything." The words came out level, but underneath them Maren heard something raw, like a floorboard worn through to the joists. "No one taught me out of kindness. It was—" She stopped. Started again. "It was practical. A person who can swallow what kills others is useful. Useful people are fed. Fed people live. That's the whole of my education, Majesty, and I'd thank you not to look at me with pity, because pity implies I'd have chosen differently, and I wouldn't have known how."

Maren set down her cup very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and queens' hands mustn't.

"Well," she said. "That's the most honest thing anyone has said to me in eleven years."

* * *

They drank their chocolate. Disapproval supervised. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked, the palace's eternal complaint about being asked to contain so much ambition.

"Tell me," Maren said eventually, "what you taste. Not the flavors. The rest."

Isandro tilted her head. "The rest?"

"When you take a spoonful at my table. What do you actually perceive? Because I've watched you, and you don't react the way the others did. Old Coram used to gag theatrically whenever he suspected anything, bless his mercenary heart—he wanted the court to see him working. You just... swallow. Like a well accepting a coin."

Something almost—almost—like humor moved behind the girl's eyes. "Bitterness first, usually. Metals have signatures. Arsenic is faintly sweet, which is its great treachery. Wolfsbane numbs the tongue before it stops the heart, so there's a moment of pleasant surprise, which I've always found rude. Hemlock—" she paused, choosing, "hemlock tastes of mice. Don't ask me to explain that; I couldn't. But it does."

"And last Tuesday's broth?"

Ah. There it was. Maren had wondered whether the girl would admit it.

"The broth," Isandro said slowly, "contained thallium salts. Very refined. Very expensive. Whoever procured it has access to the university laboratories, or the means to buy from someone who does." She met Maren's eyes for the first time in six years of service. "And Your Majesty already knows that, or you wouldn't be asking."

"I know," Maren said, "that for eight months now, something has been eating me from the inside, and that every physician my council recommends grows richer while I grow thinner, and that yesterday Lord Hesse proposed—toasts ringing, rings clicking—that we begin discussing the regency 'for the good of the realm.'" She laughed, short and without any joy in it. "The good of the realm. As if the realm were a sick aunt and he her devoted nephew."

"You could arrest him."

"On what evidence? My own tired blood and a taster's tongue?" Maren shook her head. "Evidence, in this palace, is a currency, and I am famously broke. No. What I need is what you have—not just the tasting, but the *knowing*. Six years of meals, catalogued in that careful head of yours. Every dish that tasted wrong. Every evening you went grey-lipped and said nothing." She paused. "Why did you say nothing, by the way? All these years. You must have known what was happening to me. To us both."

And here Isandro Dunmore did the second remarkable thing of the morning: she looked away, and her voice, when it came, was smaller than Maren had ever heard it.

"Because saying it would make me visible. And invisible people survive, Majesty. That's the first lesson and the last lesson. The moment I stood up at your table and said *this dish will kill you*, I would stop being furniture and become a witness—and witnesses get handled. Silenced. Or worse, *thanked*, publicly, and made into a symbol, and symbols are eaten faster than witnesses." Her jaw tightened. "I told myself it wasn't my war. You were going to be poisoned whether I spoke or not. My speaking would only add a corpse."

"My corpse."

"Yes, Majesty."

Maren nodded slowly. She should have been angry. Some part of her, the part raised on chivalric romances, was angry—or wanted to be. But the larger part, the part that had survived twenty-two years on a throne by understanding exactly this arithmetic, felt instead a terrible, clarifying kinship.

"We are the same, you and I," she said. "Do you see it? You swallow poison to remain useful to people who would discard you. So do I. Yours comes on a spoon; mine comes in council chambers, in marriage negotiations, in the word *Majesty*, which is itself a kind of dose—administered daily, in small amounts, until a woman forgets she was ever anything else." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired at the edges. "They keep us both alive because we're useful, and they keep us both small because we're dangerous, and they are both mistaken about which of those things matters more."

Isandro stared at her. And Maren watched—actually watched—as six years of perfect blankness cracked down the middle, not into tears, but into something more dangerous: recognition.

"You're offering me protection," Isandro said at last. Carefully. Testing the weight of it.

"I'm offering you a choice. Which is rarer and worth more." Maren turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Help me identify who is poisoning me—truly help, openly, as a witness rather than furniture—and I will give you what no one has ever given you. Standing. Papers. A name that appears in rooms you're not in, spoken by people who mean you well. In exchange, I ask only honesty, and I warn you honestly: it will make you visible. Terribly. Perhaps fatally."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you finish your shift today, taste my lunch, and go home, and I will never mention this conversation, and we will both continue our respective slow murders with dignity." Maren shrugged. "I'm not in the habit of forcing people to save me. It breeds resentment."

The girl almost smiled. Almost. It was like watching dawn consider whether to bother.

"There is a complication, Majesty."

"Of course there is. There always is. Complications are the palace's native fruit."

"I... keep records. Of what I taste. Private ones. Detailed ones." Isandro's fingers moved, briefly, as though smoothing an invisible page. "If I'm to help you properly—if I'm to trace the poisoning back through eight months of meals—I would need to consult them. And they're kept somewhere I would prefer Lady Vess's people never learn about."

Maren raised an eyebrow. "Secret ledgers. How mysterious. Where does a food-taster learn bookkeeping like that?"

Another pause. Longer. The longest yet. Maren had the distinct impression of standing at the edge of something vast and dark, hearing water move far below.

"A friend taught me," Isandro said finally. "It's a long story, and most of it isn't mine to tell. But Majesty—if you give me until tomorrow, I'll bring you what you need. Names. Dates. A pattern, if there is one. There's—" she hesitated, and Maren saw genuine trouble move through her like weather, "—there's something else I need to check first. Something in the records that shouldn't be possible. An entry that I can't account for."

"An entry?"

"In my own hand, Majesty. For a date that hasn't happened yet."

The fire chose that moment to collapse inward with a soft roar, and the shadows in the room rearranged themselves, and Maren—who had spent two decades refusing to believe in omens on principle—felt the hair rise on her arms.

"Well," said the Queen of Caelport lightly, "do hurry. At the rate this court feeds me, tomorrow may be a date we can't afford to leave unexamined."

Isandro rose, curtsied, and paused at the door.

"Majesty. The chocolate. I should have tasted it first. It's habit—I apologize."

"I told you, I guarded it myself."

"Yes." The ghost of that almost-smile returned, and this time it landed. "But no one has ever guarded anything *for* me before. I find I don't know where to put it."

And then she was gone, quick and quiet as a page turning, leaving Maren alone with the cat, the cooling chocolate, and the sudden, disorienting realization that she had just trusted someone—and that the world had failed, for once, to immediately punish her for it.

In the grey calfskin book, hidden behind the false spine, Maren wrote a single line before hiding it away:

*The taster knows. The taster is afraid of something worse than poison. Find out what.*

She underlined it twice, blew out the candle, and did not sleep well—which was, she reflected toward dawn, at least consistent.
