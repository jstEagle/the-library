## 7. Ink That Remembers

The guildhall at dawn smelled of cold tallow and older secrets. Isandro let herself in through the scullery door—the one that stuck unless you lifted it just so, a trick she'd learned in her first week and had never been thanked for learning—and stood a moment in the gray light, listening to the building settle around her like an old man rearranging himself in a chair.

She had the ledgers under her arm. She always carried them against her ribs, close as a second heartbeat, which was ridiculous, because they were books. Books could not feel her holding them.

*Probably,* she amended, and went to find a candle.

The counting room was hers by unspoken agreement. Corvane had never said so out loud; he simply stopped appearing there after the third time he'd found her already installed behind the desk with her sleeves pinned back and her tongue between her teeth. He had looked at her that evening with an expression she still hadn't catalogued—something between amusement and hunger, filed away in the drawer of things she refused to examine—and from then on the room had quietly become hers, the way a cat becomes the owner of a sunbeam.

She lit two candles, then a third for stubbornness, and set the ledgers out in their morning order: Trades, Debts, Forfeitures, and the slim black volume nobody ever touched, which she kept on the shelf above the others mostly so she could look at its spine and feel superior to it.

Tonight's work was simple enough. Three trades entered at the salt gate before midnight: a fishwife who'd sold the memory of her first husband's face—*fair price, sad transaction*, Isandro noted in the margin, because even impossible commerce deserved compassion—a guardsman who'd pawned his oath to his brother's widow against next quarter's wages, and a young man with ink-stained fingers who had traded *the sound of his mother's voice* for a vial of forgetting, and had wept while he signed, and had smiled while he left.

Isandro wrote them all down in her small, exact hand. She was good at this. It was, she suspected, the only thing besides dying slowly that she was genuinely good at: the patient transcription of what people gave away.

She dipped her pen in the guild's ink—the strange stuff, thick as syrup, dark as a beetle's back, mixed by Corvane himself from a recipe he claimed to have forgotten—and finished the last entry.

Then she sat back, and frowned.

The entry about the guardsman read differently than she remembered writing it.

Not much. A word or two. Where she had written *pawned against wages*, the ledger now said *pawned against wages — bond likely broken*. And beneath it, in ink that was somehow both wet and dry at once, a date. Next month. The fourteenth.

Isandro stared at it for a long moment, the way one stares at a spider on the ceiling: motionless, calculating distances, pretending calm.

"That's new," she told the candles.

The candles declined to comment. They were the only ones in the building with any sense.

* * *

By midmorning she had conducted three experiments, all of them unauthorized, none of them destructive, and all of them logged in her private notebook under the heading *Ink Behavior — Do Not Show Corvane Until Understood*, because showing Corvane anything before she understood it meant watching him smile that slow, knowing smile, and she had decided some months ago that she hated that smile, in the way one hates a fire in winter, which is to say she kept finding reasons to stand near it.

Experiment one: she wrote a fresh oath into the Trades ledger herself—a small one, harmless, *I swear to return this pen to the third drawer*—and watched the ink dry. Nothing happened. The entry sat there, obedient and dull, like any honest sentence.

Experiment two: she broke the oath. She put the pen in the fourth drawer instead, which felt absurdly transgressive, like jaywalking in a cathedral, and turned back to the page.

The entry had changed. *Bond broken,* it now read, in her own handwriting, though she had never written those words. The date beneath it was today's.

Her skin did something complicated. She took a breath, counted it the way she counted everything—four in, seven hold, eight out, the rhythm that kept poison from being panic—and made herself write down what she'd observed.

Experiment three was the one she should have thought twice about.

She went to the shelf, took down the slim black volume nobody touched, and opened it for the first time.

It fell open to the middle, as if it had been waiting to be opened exactly there, which was the sort of thing she was trying very hard not to notice about this building.

The pages were full. Not of trades. Of *lives*. Entries in a dozen different hands, some elegant, some crabbed, each one a name and a span of dates, the way a parish register records births and deaths—except these dates were not births and deaths. They were smaller than that. *First lie. First love. Last kindness.* Whole lives distilled to the moments that had mattered, written down by someone, or something, that had been paying attention.

And there, near the bottom of the open page, in handwriting she knew better than her own—

Because it was her own. That was the horror of it. The looping descenders, the way the *d* leaned like a tired woman against a wall. Her hand. Writing entries dated years ahead.

*Isandro Dunmore,* the ledger said. *Keeper of accounts.*

Beneath it, in her handwriting, dated three days from now:

*She opens the black book.*

She looked at the line. The line looked back. Somewhere below them, in the cellars, a pipe knocked twice, the way pipes do, and she flinched hard enough to knock over the inkwell, and spent a full minute mopping black syrup off the desk and telling herself firmly that she was a grown woman, a royal food-taster, a person who swallowed arsenic before breakfast without changing expression, and she would *not* be unnerved by stationery.

The ink, when she righted the well, had begun creeping toward the black book on its own. She put a saucer over it. It seemed satisfied with this arrangement.

"All right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Someone is writing my life. Before I live it. In my own handwriting."

She considered this.

"Which means either I go mad in the next three days and start prophesying, or—" She stopped. She didn't want to finish the sentence. Sentences, once finished, became true; she'd learned that from oaths.

But the unfinished part sat in her chest anyway, cold and clear as well water: *or someone has been keeping my ledger for me. All along. Every entry. Every trade I thought I chose.*

* * *

Corvane found her there at dusk, which was either coincidence or the thing she was no longer willing to call coincidence.

He came in the way he always did, without sound, as if the floor were pleased to have him and made no fuss about it. He was carrying two cups of the terrible spiced wine the guild served in winter, and he set one down beside her without asking, which was either courtesy or a test, and she drank it, which was either trust or a failure of judgment, and neither of them remarked on any of this, because they never did.

"You've been here since dawn," he said.

"I've been working."

"The desk has a groove worn in it," he said, "shaped exactly like your elbow. You've been gripping the edge since dawn." He tilted his head at the black book, lying closed where she'd shut it an hour ago and covered it, childishly, with a cloth. "And you opened it."

"I balance your accounts," Isandro said. "I balance *everything*. It was on a shelf. It was unbalanced."

Something moved through his face—not the smile, something before the smile, something almost startled—and then the smile arrived after all, slow as tide. "Most people who work for me learn within a week that the black book is not to be opened."

"I'm not most people. I'm the woman who tastes your wine."

"I know," he said. "That's rather the point."

She should have lied to him. She was excellent at lying; she did it daily at court with nothing but her face. But he was looking at her the way no one had ever looked at her—as if she were a text worth reading closely, as if the marginalia mattered—and the truth came out of her sideways, the way confessions do when they've been waiting too long.

"The ink rewrites itself," she said. "When oaths break. The entries change. Dates appear. And the black book has—" Her voice did something embarrassing. She steadied it with professional contempt. "The black book has my name in it. In my handwriting. Dated three days from now. It says I open the book. Which I have now done, which means either the book is wrong or the book is *early*, and I don't know which possibility I hate more."

Silence. The candles burned. Corvane set down his cup with great care, the way a man sets down a cup when his hands would prefer to be doing something else.

"Show me," he said.

She showed him. He read the page twice, and she watched him read it, and what frightened her more than the book itself was the look on him: not surprise. Recognition. Grief, maybe. The particular grief of a man confirming a bill he'd known was coming.

"How long," she asked quietly, "has the book been writing people?"

"Since before the Oath," he said. "Possibly since before writing. The guild didn't make it, Isandro. We *found* it. Or it found us. There are stories." He touched the cover without opening it, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "The old keepers believed it recorded what matters. Not what happens—what *matters*. There's a difference. Most lives never earn an entry at all."

"And mine has earned several. Dated in advance."

"Yes."

"Corvane." She made herself say it plainly. "Am I going to die in three days?"

He looked up at her then, and whatever he saw in her face—the taster's calm, the clinical stillness she wore like armor over a girl who had once waited in a doorway for a mother who never came—made him answer honestly, which she suspected cost him.

"I don't know," he said. "The book doesn't record deaths. It records openings. Thresholds. Three days from now, something begins. Or ends. The ink only knows which afterward."

"Wonderful. My life is being ghostwritten by a stationery oracle with a taste for suspense."

That startled a laugh out of him—short, real, quickly caught—and she hated how warm it made her feel, how the room got less cold, how she wanted to say something else funny just to hear it again. *This,* she thought grimly, *is why I eat poison professionally. To practice not wanting things.*

"There's one more thing," she said, because if she was going to be terrified she might as well be thorough. "The handwriting. It's mine. Exactly mine. Down to the way my *d*s lean."

"I noticed."

"So either I write it in three days—which means I'll have seen the future—or—" She stopped again. The unfinished sentence again, sitting in her chest like a stone swallowed whole.

"Or," Corvane said gently, "someone taught you to write. Someone whose hand you copied so faithfully it became yours. A mother teaches a child her letters, Isandro. And sometimes—" his voice went quiet, careful, "—sometimes the child keeps the mother's hand without ever knowing it wasn't her own."

The candle flames leaned, all three together, though there was no draft.

"My mother," Isandro said, "was a laundress who died when I was six."

"Was she?" Corvane said, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't cruel, and it undid her more thoroughly than cruelty would have. He gathered the black book, wrapped it back in its cloth, and held it a moment before offering it to her. "You're the keeper of accounts. As far as I'm concerned, you always have been. Keep this where you judge best."

She took it. It was heavier than a book should be. It had always been heavier than a book should be; she'd simply never held it long enough to admit it.

"Three days," she said.

"Three days," he agreed. At the door he paused, half-turned, firelit, insufferably composed. "For what it's worth—I've read every keeper's entry in that book for forty years. Yours is the first that reads like hope."

He left. She sat alone with the candles and the terrible wine and the book that knew her better than she knew herself, and after a while she opened her private notebook and wrote, in a hand that suddenly felt borrowed:

*Day one. The ink remembers. So, apparently, does everyone but me.*

Outside, far off toward the palace, a bell rang the hour, and beneath the city—beneath her feet, beneath the queen's table, beneath everything—something old shifted in its sleep, and turned over, and began, patiently, to wait for her.
