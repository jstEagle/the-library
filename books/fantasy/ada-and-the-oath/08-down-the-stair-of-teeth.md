## 8. Down the Stair of Teeth

There is a particular hour in a palace when it belongs to no one.

Not to the servants, who have gone to their pallets; not to the guards, who belong to the watch-bells and the wine they are not supposed to drink; not even to the ghosts, who by all accounts keep courtly hours. In the palace of Vellamare that hour falls somewhere between the second bell after midnight and the first grey suggestion of dawn, and in three years of tasting for the Crown I had never once been awake to see it.

Tonight I was awake to see it because I could not sleep, and I could not sleep because the map would not stop whispering.

I had copied it twice — once faithfully, once scrambled into nonsense in case Lady Vess's people ever got their clever hands on my belongings — and burned the stranger's original in the taster's hearth, where the wax seal had spat and hissed like something alive with opinions. The copy lay against my skin now, sewn into the lining of my oldest coat, the one no one at court would ever ask about, because asking about my oldest coat would be like asking about a chair. That was the whole art of invisibility: become furniture.

But furniture does not lie awake memorizing ink.

I had the route by heart within two nights. It was not long, as maps go. A line down through the old kitchens. A turn where the wine cellars forked toward a bricked-up arch everyone called the Plague Door and nobody questioned, because questioning doors is not a servant's trade. Then a descent — the map showed stairs, drawn in that same unsettling hand as everything else, each step rendered as a small sharp triangle, so that the staircase looked less like architecture than like a jaw.

The stair of teeth, I thought, lying in the dark of my narrow room above the taster's quarters. Someone had named it already. Or the map was naming it now, which was worse.

I told myself I would wait. I told myself a sensible taster — a *surviving* taster — waits until she understands what she is walking toward. I told myself this for four nights, while I tasted the queen's morning broth (almond, honey, a thread of henbane so diluted it was practically an apology) and smiled at Lady Vess across the council table and went to bed and lay staring at the ceiling while the triangles of that stair marched behind my eyes like an army of very small, very patient sharks.

On the fifth night, the queen dismissed her household early, pressed my hand in both of hers — she has hands like cold silk, Queen Maren, and a way of looking at you as though you might be a person — and said, "You seem tired, child. Whatever keeps you, I hope it loosens its grip soon."

And I thought: *it will not loosen. It is under the floor I am standing on.*

So that is how I came to be moving through the sleeping palace at the dead hour, carrying a shuttered lantern, a taster's knife, and a flask of the antidote compound I brew for myself every week — the one that lets me swallow poison in doses small enough to build a tolerance instead of a coffin. Old habits. If I died tonight, I intended to die well-stocked.

* * *

The old kitchens were exactly as disappointing as abandoned rooms usually are: dust, dead ovens, a colony of mice who regarded me with the weary tolerance of long-term tenants. But the map's first mark was true. Behind the flour store, where the wall met the floor, a hatch sat flush with the flagstones, its iron ring worn smooth by hands long gone.

It opened without a sound. That was the first wrong thing. Everything else in that kitchen groaned or crumbled or squeaked. The hatch alone moved like it had been oiled yesterday, by someone expecting me.

I stood over it for a long moment, lantern held high, listening to my own heart conduct its indignant percussion.

"You are a food-taster," I reminded myself quietly. "You have eaten things that would kill a horse. You have smiled at men who ordered those things served. You can go down some stairs."

The voice in my head that sounds like my mother — the borrowed memory of her, the one I'd built from nothing since I never knew her — did not answer. She never did. But I felt her absence the way you feel a missing tooth, and I went down anyway, because I have learned that the ache of not-knowing is worse than any poison I've ever swallowed.

The passage beyond the hatch sloped gently downward, walls of dressed stone older than anything above. The palace of Vellamare was three hundred years old; these walls were older than that, and I knew it the way you know a face in a crowd — not by evidence but by recognition. The stones were fitted without mortar, tight as teeth, and carved along their edges with a script I did not know and yet could almost read, the way you can almost remember a song from before you were born.

My lantern flame leaned toward the wall. Not flickering — *leaning*, steadily, like a plant toward sun.

"Well," I said to it. "That's new."

* * *

The wine cellar forked where the map promised. To the left, the working cellars, barrels racked in patient rows, smelling of oak and honest rot. To the right, the bricked arch the servants called the Plague Door — except it wasn't bricked anymore.

The bricks lay stacked neatly beside the archway. Mortar dust swept into a tidy pile. Someone had unbricked this door recently, carefully, and left no tool behind.

Every instinct I owned stood up and voted to leave. I outvoted them, which is either courage or a character flaw; in my experience the two are difficult to tell apart from the inside.

Beyond the arch the air changed. Colder, but not damp — dry as paper, dry as a book shut for centuries. And there was a smell underneath the stone-smell, faint as a ghost of a ghost: something green. Rain on leaves. A season that had no business existing forty feet beneath a marble palace.

The passage narrowed. The fitted stones closed overhead until I walked with my shoulders hunched, and the carved script thickened along the walls until it stopped looking like decoration and started looking like argument — lines of it crowding and overlapping, phrases cut deep and then half-effaced and cut again, as if the walls themselves had been having a quarrel for three hundred years and had not finished.

I ran my fingers over one band of it. The stone was warm.

Stone forty feet underground, in a cellar nobody visited, was warm as a living cheek.

I took my hand back and wiped it on my coat and did not touch the walls again.

* * *

The stair began without announcement. One moment the passage floor; the next, steps descending into dark, each one cut in that sharp triangular profile I now recognized with a lurch — the map had not been drawing a metaphor. It had been drawing a portrait.

I counted them, because counting is what I do when terror arrives; numbers are my antidote. Thirty-one steps. At thirty-one the stair ended at a landing no wider than a coffin turned upright, and before it stood the door.

I had imagined every kind of door on the way down. Iron, perhaps, or bronze gone green, or wood black as bog-oak with hinges like knucklebones. What stood there was none of these. It was stone — the same seamless fitted stone as the walls — but where a door should be there was only a seam, thin as a hair, running floor to ceiling in the shape of a doorway. No handle. No keyhole. No hinge.

Only, at the height of a human heart, a shallow basin carved into the stone. Cup-shaped. The size of two cupped hands.

I knew what it was for before I knew that I knew. That is the only way I can describe it. Knowledge arrived in me the way taste arrives — not reasoned, simply *present*, on the tongue of the mind.

"It wants blood," I said aloud, and my voice fell flat in the dry dark, swallowed instantly, as if the corridor ate sound the way I ate poisons: in small doses, keeping it useful, keeping it invisible.

I set down the lantern. I drew the taster's knife. And then I made the mistake of hesitating, because hesitation is where honesty lives, and honesty asked me the question I had been outrunning for five nights:

*Why you?*

A dying stranger on the Salt Gate docks, bleeding out among the fish-reeking crates, and of every soul in Vellamare he presses the map into *my* hands and whispers *Oathkeeper* like a man passing a debt he cannot carry. A ledger entry in my dead mother's handwriting, dated three days from now. A door beneath the palace that opens, if the shape of that basin meant anything, to blood.

To whose blood?

Mine, said the knowledge again, calm as arithmetic. Mine, and mine specifically, and not because of any antidote I brewed or any tolerance I had built dose by careful dose over three years of quiet self-poisoning.

Because I had never built a tolerance at all.

I stood very still on the coffin-width landing while the thought finished arriving, the way vertigo finishes arriving when you finally look down.

Three years I had believed myself a craftsman of survival. Careful Isandro, methodical Isandro, who catalogued toxins with clinical calm and dosed herself nightly like a woman tuning an instrument. But the broth that should have blistered my throat hadn't. The arsenic-laced marchpane I'd tasted for the king's cousin two winters past — the batch that killed the cousin's spaniel before dessert was cleared — had passed through me like rain through a sieve. I had told myself my doses were small enough, my discipline strong enough, my body obedient enough.

But discipline does not explain the spaniel.

Immunity like mine isn't trained. It's inherited. It runs in blood like color runs in eyes, and there is only one lineage in any story I've ever heard — only one kind of blood in all the guild's strange ledgers, in all the queen's old histories, in all the whispers traded across the guildhall counter in vials and vows — that poison cannot touch.

Fae blood.

The word did not land like thunder. It landed like a key turning in a lock I had lived inside my whole life without knowing the door existed. All at once the room of myself was bigger than I'd thought, and colder, and full of corridors I had never mapped.

My mother. Whoever she had been — the woman whose handwriting dated entries three days into the future, the woman whose face I had sold to no one and kept in no vial because I had never possessed it — she had carried this blood. Had given it to me. Had maybe, in whatever last desperate night the stolen memory-vial had shown me, smuggled a child out of *this place*, down *these stairs*, past *this door* —

— and the door had let her pass, because the door opens to fae blood, and here I stood, her daughter, holding a knife over a basin shaped for exactly that.

"She didn't run far enough," I whispered. "Or she ran exactly far enough, and it just took the world thirty years to catch up."

The stone did not answer. But the warmth of it seemed, very slightly, to increase. Like a hand waiting.

* * *

Here is a thing about me that the ledgers do not record, because I record the ledgers: I am afraid of nearly everything, and I have made a profession of swallowing fear in small doses too. That is all courage is. I am fairly sure of it. You take the terror a drop at a time until your blood learns it, and then one day you find yourself standing in front of a door that wants your blood, holding a knife, and your hand does not shake.

Much.

"Right," I told the door, conversationally, because talking keeps the fear diluted. "You open for fae blood. I appear to have fae blood, which is news to me and will be extremely inconvenient later, I'm sure. So let's understand each other. I'm coming through, I'm having a look, and I am *not* agreeing to anything. No oaths. No bargains. I know how your kind trades."

Somewhere below — below the door, below the landing, down in whatever waited at the bottom of the world — something stirred. Not a sound, exactly. More a change in pressure, the way air shifts before a storm, or before someone in another room says your name.

I opened my palm over the basin. The knife's edge found the pad of my thumb, that soft spot every taster knows, the place we nick ourselves deliberately to test our own blood against unknown compounds — old habit, old craft, the small daily tax of my profession.

I cut. One clean line. Red welled up, ordinary red, human-looking red, and dripped into the stone cup.

For a heartbeat, nothing. The blood sat there, absurd, a tiny red coin in grey stone, and I felt the fool I had apparently been all my life settle over me like a coat —

Then the blood sank. Not ran, not pooled — *sank*, into stone that had no pores, vanishing the way a memory vanishes into a vial of guild-ink, and the seam of the door lit from within.

Light ran along the hairline crack the way dawn runs along a mountain ridge — slow, gold-green, the color of that impossible leaf-and-rain smell — and the script on the surrounding walls flared briefly into legibility. I caught one line, one phrase, cut deepest of all and effaced least, in that language I shouldn't have known and read anyway:

*WE WERE PROMISED A KEEPER OF OUR OWN BLOOD.*

The door swung inward without weight, without sound, onto darkness that breathed.

Warm air rose up the stair of teeth and touched my face, and it smelled of rain on leaves, of summer, of a country I had never seen and somehow grieved for, and from the depths below came a sound I will hear until I die: a great slow exhalation, like something enormous turning over in a sleep three centuries long, beginning to wake.

And threaded through it, faint as a voice through water, I heard singing.

I knew the song.

I had never heard it in my life, and I knew every note of it, the way I knew the shape of a mother I'd never met — and the last thing I understood, standing on the threshold with my thumb still bleeding, was that whatever waited down there had been waiting not for a map, not for a stranger, not even for an Oathkeeper.

It had been waiting for me. Since before I was born. Since the Oath was forged. Since a woman with my blood ran up these stairs with a child in her arms and a promise in her mouth.

I picked up the lantern.

I went down to meet my inheritance.
