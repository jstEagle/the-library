## 9. Vess Closes the Net

The ledgers burned beautifully.

That was the first thing Lady Vess noticed, and the last thing she would ever admit to noticing: that there was something almost tender in the way old oath-ink caught flame. It did not burn like ordinary ink. Ordinary ink surrendered. Oath-ink *argued* — it flared green at the margins, hissed like a kettle left too long on a hot plate, and for one uncanny moment in each page's dying, the words seemed to lift off the paper and hang in the air, legible, furious, and then gone.

Vess stood in the guildhall's counting room with her hands clasped behind her back and watched forty years of impossible commerce curl into ash.

"Shall we save any of them, my lady?" asked Sergeant Halloway, who was a large, honest man with the unfortunate habit of asking questions he already regretted.

"Burn them all."

"All of them? Even the—" He squinted at the ledger nearest him, its brass clasp still cool enough to touch. "Even this one, my lady. It appears to be an account of... dreams. Sold by the pound."

"Dreams are the most dangerous entries of all," Vess said. "A memory sold can be bought back. An oath pawned can be redeemed. But a dream, once traded, is gone from the dreamer forever, and no one — not the buyer, not the seller, not the Crown — knows what was lost. Burn it."

Halloway fed it to the fire with the reverence of a man burying a small animal. Vess appreciated him for that. She had chosen her people carefully over twenty years as spymaster: not the clever ones, never the clever ones, but the decent ones, the ones whose loyalty was a form of stubbornness rather than ambition. Clever men wanted things. Decent men only wanted to be told they'd done well.

She had been a clever woman once. It had cost her everything she had ever loved, and she had spent two decades making certain the lesson stayed learned.

* * *

The arrests had gone smoothly, which troubled her more than chaos would have.

Chaos meant surprise. Smoothness meant the guild had expected her — or worse, had *permitted* her. When Vess's agents had come through the salt-stained doors of the Guild of Impossible Things at dawn, they had found the clerks already awake, already dressed, sitting in neat rows in the trading hall like children waiting for a schoolmaster. Not one of them had run. Not one had reached for the ledgers. A young woman with ink-stained fingers had even offered the arresting officers tea.

"Tea," Halloway repeated now, shaking his head as though the word were evidence of witchcraft. "They made us tea, my lady. While we were arresting them."

"What did you do with it?"

"Dumped it out, my lady. Obviously." He paused. "It smelled very good, though."

Vess allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Where is the guildmaster?"

"That's the thing, my lady. He isn't here. Corvane. The clerks say he keeps his own hours. They say—" Halloway shifted uncomfortably, the way men did when repeating things they suspected would make them sound foolish. "They say he comes and goes like weather. One clerk said he'd worked here eleven years and met him twice. Another said he'd met him every day for eleven years. Both of them swore they were telling the truth."

"They probably were. That's how he works." Vess turned from the fire. "Find me the taster."

"The taster, my lady?"

"The royal food-taster. Isandro Dunmore." She said the name carefully, the way one sets down a full cup on a crowded table. "She was seen entering this hall three nights ago. After the poisoning scare at the high table. Before the stranger died at the Salt Gate."

"You think the taster has the map."

"I think," Vess said, "that within the space of four days, a stranger bled to death on the docks whispering a word that has not been spoken aloud in three hundred years; a ledger entry appeared in a dead woman's handwriting dated three days in the future; and the quietest, most invisible creature at court began visiting a guild that officially does not exist. I do not believe in coincidence, Sergeant. Coincidence is what the guilty call pattern when they hope no one is looking."

Halloway nodded slowly. "And do you think she's guilty?"

Vess considered the question longer than she needed to, because the honest answer was complicated in ways she did not intend to share with anyone, least of all a decent man holding a poker.

"I think she's *involved*," she said at last. "Guilty is a word for trials. Find her. Gently. If she runs, follow. If she fights, don't hurt her. And if anyone else asks after her — anyone at all — I want to know within the hour."

"And if Corvane asks after her?"

Vess looked at the fire. Somewhere in the ash, a half-burned line of text was still glowing faintly green, refusing to die cleanly. *Terms of the Old Peace,* it might have said. Or *Price.* Or a name.

"Then I want to know before he finishes asking," she said.

* * *

They took the guildhall's back rooms next, and it was there, among the vials and the vaults, that Vess found the thing she had been dreading for twenty years without ever once letting herself name it.

The memory vault sat beneath the trading floor: a cellar lined with cedar shelves, thousands upon thousands of little glass vials, each stoppered with wax, each labeled in a clerk's careful hand. Memories, bought and sold and pawned. Vess walked the aisles slowly, trailing one gloved finger along the shelf edges, reading labels the way other women read love letters.

*A wedding, whole and warm, 40 crowns.*
*The smell of my mother's kitchen, 12.*
*One summer, aged nine, unsold.*

Unsold. There were always unsold memories, the inventory no one wanted: griefs too heavy, joys too small, the vast gray middle of a life that no buyer would pay for. The guild kept them anyway. Someone had to. That was the part of the Impossible Things that Vess had never managed to hate, and she resented it for that.

"My lady?" Halloway stood in the doorway, awkward under the low beams. "Agents have finished the upper floors. We've seized the correspondence, the trade-books, and... there's a room up top that none of the clerks will enter. They won't say why. One of them started crying when we asked."

"Leave that room alone. Seal it. No one goes in without me." Some rooms were load-bearing, in the architecture of fear. Break the wrong wall and the whole building came down on your head. "Have you found anything about a map? Wax-sealed. Old parchment."

"No, my lady. But—" Halloway hesitated, then held out a single vial. "This was set apart from the others. On its own shelf, behind glass. The label's odd."

Vess took it. The vial was unremarkable — same glass, same wax — but the label stopped her breath.

It was written in oath-ink. And it was dated *tomorrow.*

*Final night. The forging. What was promised, and to whom.*

She knew that handwriting. She had spent three hundred years' worth of nights trying to forget it, and she would have known it blind, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

Maren's hand. The ledger-keeper's hand. The woman who had written the original terms of the Oath in blood and ink and then vanished from every record in the kingdom, leaving behind a daughter who didn't know she was a daughter, and a peace that was really a cage, and a debt that Vess had spent her whole adult life pretending wasn't hers.

"My lady?" Halloway said again. "You've gone quite pale."

"It's warm down here," Vess said. "That's all." She slipped the vial into her coat, against her heart, where it felt less like an object than a small cold bird. "Finish the inventory. Nothing leaves this cellar except under seal. And Sergeant — if a vial goes missing, I want to know which one. Not just that one was taken. *Which one.*"

"Yes, my lady."

He went. Vess remained alone among the shelves, in the smell of cedar and old wax, and let herself remember — briefly, clinically, the way she permitted herself all dangerous things — the night she had broken the Oath's terms.

* * *

She had been young. That was the excuse everyone offered her afterward, and the one she refused most fiercely, because youth was not an excuse, it was merely a season, and seasons pass, and what you plant in them keeps growing.

Three hundred years ago — no. Twenty-three years ago, though it felt geological. She had been a junior agent in the Crown's service, clever and hungry, exactly the kind of officer she now refused to hire. The Oath was a story by then, a bedtime tale, a clause in lawbooks nobody read. The fae were gone. The stone was legend. And Vess had found, in a confiscated merchant's trunk, a fragment of the true terms — and discovered that the Oath bound both sides, and that the binding could be *tested*, probed, leaned upon, if one knew where the seams were.

She had told herself she was doing it for the Crown. She had even believed it, mostly. But the truth — the truth she kept in the locked room behind her eyes, the one no clerk would enter, the one she had sealed herself — was that she had done it for a person. For a name. For a debt the Oath itself had created, a life it had taken as surety, a soul it held in escrow somewhere beneath the palace, in the dark, in the keeping of a stone.

She had pried at the seam. Just once. Just a little.

And the Oath had noticed.

She still bore the mark of that noticing: a thin white scar across her left palm, shaped like a sentence, too fine to read. Every winter it ached. Every time she lied to the queen — and she lied to the queen weekly, professionally, out of duty — it ached a little harder, as though the Oath kept books of its own and hers was overdue.

What she had learned in that one terrible experiment had shaped everything since: the stone was real. It was beneath the palace. It was listening. And someone — something — inside it had spoken to her, in a voice like her own name being read out at a funeral, and had said: *Not yet. Not you. But soon, someone will come with the right blood, and then the account settles.*

She had spent twenty years preparing for that someone. Building the net. Closing it inch by inch, patiently, so that when the daughter of the ledger-keeper finally came stumbling toward the truth, Vess would be standing between her and the stone with every door locked and every road watched.

For the Crown. Of course. For the Crown, and for the peace, and for the safety of a kingdom that slept soundly only because it did not know what was buried under its bed.

And — she admitted it now, alone, in the dark of the memory vault, where no one could hear and no ledger could record — for the person in the stone. For the chance, however slim, however damnable, that when the account settled, there might be something left to take back. Something the Oath owed *her.*

Grief makes excellent servants and terrible masters. Vess had made hers a servant long ago. But servants steal, in the end. Servants always steal.

* * *

By evening the guildhall was ash and silence, and Vess stood in the trading hall watching the last of the fire settle into orange coals, and thought about the taster.

Isandro Dunmore. She had studied the file until she could recite it: orphaned, raised by a foster-mother now conveniently dead; recruited into the kitchens at fourteen; appointed taster at nineteen for her remarkable constitution. Remarkable. Yes. Vess had read between those lines years ago, though she had never written the conclusion down — some conclusions were too dangerous to exist on paper. A girl immune to poison not by training but by *blood*. Fae blood, thinned by a generation, hidden in plain sight at the royal table, tasting treasons for a living while the palace sat atop the very lock her mother had forged.

And now a map. A dying stranger. A guildmaster who moved like weather. Entries appearing in ledgers dated days ahead, written in a dead woman's hand.

The net was closing. Vess had built it well. And yet, standing in the ruin of the Impossible Things, she felt less like a hunter than like a woman tightening a knot around her own wrist.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape: if the girl reached the stone, the account settled — and Vess might reclaim what the Oath had taken. But if the girl destroyed the stone, the Oath broke, the exiles returned, and everything Vess had guarded for twenty years came apart like wet paper. And if Vess took the girl first, quietly, before the stone could be reached—

Then nothing settled at all. Then the debt stayed open forever, and the voice in the dark stayed silent, and Vess grew old guarding a lock for a prisoner who might already be beyond saving.

There was no move that won. There was only the move that lost least.

"Halloway," she said, without turning. "Has there been any report on the taster?"

"None, my lady. She wasn't home. Neighbors say she keeps odd hours." He cleared his throat. "Though there's... one other thing. A witness at the Salt Gate. Dockside. Says the dying stranger didn't just press something into the taster's hands. Says he *kissed her knuckles* first. Like swearing fealty. Like handing over a charge."

Vess closed her eyes. In the coals, the last green flare of oath-ink rose and died.

"A charge," she repeated softly. "Yes. That's what I was afraid of."

Somewhere across the city, a girl with the right blood was carrying a map beneath the palace's feet, and the stone was waiting, and tomorrow's date was already written in a dead woman's hand.

Vess put on her gloves, covering the scar, and went to hunt the only person in the capital who mattered.

"Double the watch on the palace cellars," she told Halloway, sweeping past. "And Sergeant — if anyone asks why, tell them the queen has ordered an inspection of the wine stores."

"And if the queen asks why *we're* telling people she ordered it?"

Vess smiled, thin as the scar on her palm.

"Then tell them the spymaster lied," she said, "and pray she never finds out you said so."
