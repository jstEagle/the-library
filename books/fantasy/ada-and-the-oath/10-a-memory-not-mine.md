## 10. A Memory Not Mine

The vial had been sitting in my pocket for three days, and it was starting to feel like a stone I'd swallowed.

Not a large stone. A pebble. The kind you carry in your shoe without quite deciding to, until one morning you realize you've been limping and calling it your natural gait. That was the vial — small enough to hide in my palm, heavy enough to change how I walked through the world.

I had stolen it from the guildhall's vault on an ordinary Tuesday, which is the best day for theft, because nobody suspects a Tuesday of anything.

Technically, of course, I hadn't stolen it at all. I am the keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things' ledgers, and in the ledgers there existed a column for *unclaimed goods* — memories pawned and never redeemed, oaths abandoned by their makers, griefs left on the shelf like coats after a fire. Unclaimed goods reverted to the guild after seven years. And this particular vial — catalogued under the number 4471, with a provenance line that read only *the ledger-keeper's own* — had been gathering dust since before I could walk.

It was mine by every law of accounting I knew. It simply wasn't mine by any law of Corvane's that mattered, which was the problem, because Corvane's laws were the only ones enforced below the Salt Gate.

So I did what any sensible woman does with contraband. I took it home, hid it inside the hollow handle of my tasting knife, and then spent three days being so scrupulously honest in all other matters that even I began to find myself tedious.

On the fourth night, alone in my narrow room above the tannery row, with rain writing its long gray sentences down the window, I took out the knife, unscrewed the handle, and held the vial up to the candlelight.

Memory, in tradeable form, looks like nothing much. A thimble of liquid the color of late afternoon — amber going gold going dark at the bottom, like tea steeped too long by someone who forgot about it because they were crying. The guild sold these by the vial to people who wanted to remember being loved, or wanted to forget it, depending on the day and the price.

I had never taken one. In nine years of keeping accounts of other people's memories — first kiss, last argument, the smell of a house that burned — I had never once tasted the merchandise. Ledger-keepers don't sample the stock. It's the first rule of honest bookkeeping, and I had clung to that rule the way drowning men cling to things that float, without examining whether the thing would hold.

But this vial wasn't stock. This vial was the only thing in the world my mother had ever given me, and she hadn't even known she was giving it. She had pawned it — pawned her final night, the last hours of her life, pressed into glass and sealed with wax — and she had never come back for it.

Seven years. Then the guild's. Then mine.

"You're stalling," I told myself, and my reflection in the window agreed with such promptness that I decided we'd talked enough.

I pulled the wax seal with my thumbnail. The scent rose up out of the little bottle — rain on hot stone, woodsmoke, and underneath it something sweeter and stranger, like the air after lightning. Fae-scent. I'd never smelled it before, but some part of me recognized it anyway, the way you recognize a relative's laugh in a crowd of strangers.

I drank.

* * *

The world went sideways and then it went backward, and then I was standing in a room I had never seen but knew instantly, the way you know your own hands.

Stone walls. Candlelight. A table of black oak scarred by centuries of knives and quills, and around it — people. No. Around it, *sides*. On one side, humans in the gray robes of the old Concordance, their faces tight with exhaustion and fear. On the other side, beings I had no name for and every name for: tall figures whose skin held light the way water holds moon, whose eyes were the green-gold of new leaves, whose presence made the candles lean toward them like flowers toward sun.

The fae. The vanished fae, three hundred years gone, and here they stood in a cellar beneath — I understood this with sudden, sick certainty — beneath *this palace*, beneath the very floors where I spent my days swallowing poison for my bread.

And at the head of the table, quill in hand, ink to her elbows, sat my mother.

She was younger than I had ever imagined anyone's mother could be — younger than I was now. Dark hair escaping its braid in furious wisps. Ink on her cheekbone where she'd pushed it back with a stained hand. She wore the same plain wool I wore, the same mended cuffs, and she was arguing with a fae lord twice her height as though he were a merchant shortchanging her at market.

"The terms are written," she said, and her voice went through me like a hook through silk. My voice. Almost exactly my voice, pitched lower, worn harder. "Three centuries of exile, sealed in blood and stone. If I'm to be the one who writes it into permanence, then I will write it *accurately*, Lord Serevan, and accurately means the clause about return."

A tall fae at the table's end — silver-haired, patient as winter, with eyes that made my chest ache with recognition I couldn't place — inclined his head. "The clause stands. When the last debt is paid, the door opens."

"When the last debt is paid," my mother repeated, and wrote it, and the scratch of her quill sounded louder than it should have, louder than the rain, louder than anything, as though the room itself were listening and would remember.

*I'm remembering it,* I realized, with a vertigo that nearly unseated me. *This isn't a vision. This is her memory. I am standing inside the last night of her life.*

Because I knew what came next. Everyone in the city knew the shape of the story if not the truth of it: the Oath was forged, the fae departed, the stone was buried, and the ledger-keeper died within the year. Plague, the histories said. Fever.

The memory disagreed.

* * *

The scene shifted the way dreams shift — one blink and the candles had burned down, the human negotiators were gone, and my mother stood alone at the black oak table with a sleeping bundle in her arms.

A baby. Small, red-faced, furious at existence in the way of all newborns. Wrapped in a shawl I recognized with a jolt — I still owned that shawl, still slept with it folded under my pillow on bad nights, and had always told myself it was a habit, not a relic.

"There you are," my mother whispered to the bundle, and her voice cracked open something in my chest that I had kept boarded shut for twenty-six years. "There you are, my impossible girl."

Me. The bundle was me.

She swayed with me in the candlelight, humming something tuneless, and I stood three feet away watching my mother hold me, and I understood at last why the guild forbade ledger-keepers from sampling the stock. Because this wasn't information. Information can be filed. This was a hand reaching out of three hundred years ago and closing around my throat, gentle as a collar, gentle as a necklace.

"I have to do it tonight," she told me — told the baby-me, told the empty room, told perhaps the vial itself, knowing somehow that someday someone would drink this hour. "You understand that. You're the only one who will understand, because you'll grow up just like me, stubborn as a tax collector and twice as hard to reason with."

She laughed, wet-eyed, and set me down in a basket lined with the shawl.

Then she turned to the doorway, where the silver-haired fae stood waiting — Serevan, though I didn't yet know his name; I only knew that looking at him felt like looking at a word I'd always been able to read but never spoken aloud.

"It has to be blood," he said quietly. "The stone takes a binding in blood or it takes nothing. That was the price of making it, and you know what flows in the child."

"I know." My mother's jaw set in a way I see in my own reflection every morning. "Which is why the binding will be mine, and hers will buy her freedom instead. Write it however the stone demands, Serevan, but hear the shape of it: my blood seals the exile. Her blood — when she comes, if she comes, *when* she comes, because she's my daughter and she'll come poking at locked doors the way I did — her blood opens them again. One key. One heir. The stone doesn't get to keep both of us."

"And the guild?" Serevan asked. "Corvane's predecessors will want the ledger entry."

"Then they'll get one." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen, and it was my smile, worn thin. "Let them record that the ledger-keeper died owing everything. Let them think I skipped out on my debts. Debts are just love wearing its work clothes, and I am paying all of mine forward."

Serevan crossed the room and knelt — a lord of the fae, kneeling to a tired woman in mended wool — and took her hands. "You know what they will say of you. The histories. The songs, if there are songs."

"They'll say I broke the peace." She looked down at the basket, at me. "They'll say I doomed us all. Let them. Songs are just gossip that learned to rhyme. What matters is what's true: both sides started that war, and both sides signed this cage, and someday — not now, maybe not for generations — someone has to have the courage to admit a cage is a cage, even when everyone inside agreed to build it."

She lifted me out of the basket one last time. Held me against her heart, where I could hear — where the memory let me hear, across the whole impossible distance — the steady drumbeat of her living.

"I promise you," she said, and I couldn't tell anymore whether she spoke to the infant or to the woman drinking this memory decades later, "I promise you the world will try to file you away. Taster. Servant. Nobody. They'll put you in their columns and their categories and their careful little boxes, because that's what the world does — it keeps books on everyone. But you are not an entry, my love. You are the hand that holds the pen. Remember that, when the time comes. Whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten you with — you are not an entry."

The candle guttered. Serevan opened a door that hadn't been there, a door of shadow and green-gold light, and my mother carried me toward it, and the memory began to fray at the edges like cloth rubbed too long —

— and the last thing I saw, the very last thing, seared itself into me deeper than any poison I'd ever swallowed:

She kissed my forehead and said, "When you break it — and you will break it, I know you, I *know* you — don't do it for the dead. Do it for the living. Even the ones who don't deserve it. *Especially* those."

* * *

I came back to my body kneeling on the floorboards, cheek wet, the empty vial rolling gently against my knee.

Rain still on the window. Candle burned to a stub. Perhaps an hour gone, perhaps a lifetime; my knees voted for lifetime.

I stayed down there a while. There's no dignity in grief and I've never seen the point of pretending otherwise, so I knelt on the cold boards of a rented room above a tannery and cried for a mother I'd met once, inside a bottle, and lost again before I learned her favorite song.

But here is the thing about grief that nobody tells you while you're in it, because you wouldn't believe them: it burns clean, eventually. Underneath all that smoke there's a shape left standing, and if you're lucky — if you're stubborn as a tax collector and twice as hard to reason with — the shape is a purpose.

My mother had paid her debts forward. All of them. Her blood to seal the cage, her name to the scandal, her final night to a glass vial and a daughter she'd never meet. She had spent herself completely, and the world's response had been to misfile her as a plague victim and forget her entirely.

Well. I kept the ledgers now. Misfiling was rather my specialty to correct.

I got up off the floor, washed my face in the basin, and sat down at my little desk with the ledger I'd brought home hidden inside a bread loaf — a trick I recommend, as no one has ever searched a stale loaf with any conviction. I turned to a fresh page. I dipped my pen in ordinary ink, oath-ink being far beyond my budget and my intentions both.

And I wrote, in my own hand, for the first time in nine years, an entry that was not a record of someone else's trade:

*Item: one truth, recovered. The Oathstone beneath the palace is not merely a lock. It is a promise — mutual, binding, and dated. The fae did not flee. They waited. And the daughter of the ledger-keeper owes them an answer three hundred years overdue.*

I read it back. My hand shook only a little.

Then, because I am still who I am, I added a second line in smaller script:

*Note: verify clause regarding "last debt." Determine what remains unpaid. Determine who benefits from its remaining unpaid.*

Because that was the question, wasn't it? The door opens when the last debt is paid. Three centuries, and the door hadn't opened. Either the debt was enormous, or someone was keeping the books deliberately unbalanced — and I knew of at least one spymaster with excellent reasons to prefer the cage stay locked, and at least one guildmaster who traded in impossibilities and might find a locked door full of waiting fae to be the most valuable inventory in the world.

Below me, in the street, a cart rattled past. Above the rattle — faint, but my ears had been trained by years of listening for the wrong ingredient in a soup — footsteps stopped outside my door. Paused. Moved on.

I closed the ledger and sat very still in the dark, my mother's voice in my ear and her warning in my blood:

*Whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten you with — you are not an entry.*

Someone had been outside my door. Someone knew I had the vial, or the map, or both. And tomorrow, when I walked into that palace to taste the queen's breakfast, I would walk in knowing what lay beneath its foundations, and who had bled to bury it there, and what my own veins were for.

I blew out the candle.

In the dark, I found I was smiling — a small, fierce, inherited smile — and I said aloud to the empty room, to the rain, to the woman in the vial:

"Don't worry, Mother. I'm coming to settle the accounts."
