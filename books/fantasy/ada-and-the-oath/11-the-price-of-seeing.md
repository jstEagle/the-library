## 11. The Price of Seeing

The rain had followed me out of the palace district like a debt collector, patient and inevitable, and by the time I reached the Salt Market it had soaked through my coat to the map beneath. I could feel the wax seal against my ribs, warm as a second heartbeat. Foolish, to carry it on my body. Foolisher still to carry it anywhere else.

I told myself I was being careful. I had taken three different routes from the kitchens, doubled back through the tannery lane where the stink would cover any scent a hound might follow, and paused twice in doorways to watch the street behind me. This was the discipline of twenty years as a taster: never assume the dish is clean because it smells clean.

But Vess's agents did not smell like poison. They smelled like nothing at all, which should have been my first warning.

The market was emptying under the rain—fishwives hauling their tarps, a boy chasing a runaway cabbage with the grim determination of someone who would not be blamed for its loss. I was halfway across the cobbles, counting exits the way other people count coins, when the first man stepped out of the alley beside the chandler's shop.

He was ordinary in the way that only trained men can be: brown coat, brown hair, a face you would forget before he finished speaking. The second and third came from behind me, unhurried, closing the triangle the way water closes over a dropped stone.

"Lady Vess requests a conversation," the first one said.

"Then she should have sent an invitation," I said. "I answer those."

"She said you'd be difficult." He almost smiled. "She said to tell you she's sorry about it."

I had already catalogued them, because cataloguing is what keeps the hands steady. Three men, swords sheathed but hands near hilts. The one behind me favored his left leg—old injury, slower on a pivot. The alley to my right ran between the chandler's and the cooper's, narrow, ending at the fish-gutters' stairs down to the harbor. Wet stone. Slippery. Better than standing still.

"I'm a food-taster," I said. "I have nothing Lady Vess wants."

"Everyone has something," said the man with the bad leg, behind me. "That's rather her whole philosophy."

They moved together, which meant they'd done this before, which meant talking was finished. I went for the alley.

I got four steps. Then the world turned sideways.

Not a blow—I never saw the blow. There was simply a moment when the cobbles were above me and the rain was falling into my eyes, and a boot pressed down on the map through my coat, pinning the paper against my heart like a hand claiming it. The first agent crouched, drawing a short knife—not for killing, I understood dimly, for persuading—and said, "Now then. Let's see what's sewn into—"

And then there was a sound like a door opening in the middle of the air.

I have heard Corvane arrive before. It is always like that: not footsteps, but an arrival, as though the space he intends to occupy has agreed to receive him. One moment the alley mouth was empty; the next he stood in it, rain streaming off his dark coat, and the guildmaster's calm was on him like armor polished too well to hide the dents.

"Gentlemen," he said pleasantly. "You're standing on guild property."

The agent with the knife straightened slowly. "This is Crown business."

"This is *my* street," Corvane said, "which makes everything on it guild business, including the lady, including the knife, and—" he tilted his head, considering the boot still planted on my chest, "—including that boot, which I'll thank you to remove before I start charging rent."

What happened next happened quickly and badly for all of us. The man behind me—the one with the leg—did not wait for negotiations. He drew steel, and Corvane moved to meet him, and the third agent came around the side with the flat economy of a man who had killed people in alleys for a living and saw no reason this should be different.

I should have stayed down. Every instinct trained into me said stay down, be small, be invisible, let larger forces sort themselves out. But the third agent's blade was not going for Corvane's guard. It was going low, quick, angled past his defense toward the space where I lay—

Corvane saw it. I will swear until whatever end comes for me that he saw it, and chose not to step back.

The blade took him under the ribs instead of through my throat.

* * *

Afterward, I could never reconstruct the fight properly. Memory is a dishonest ledger; it records what it wants. What I retain is fragments: the bad-legged man going down hard against the cooper's wall; the knifeman fleeing with Corvane's cane-hook hooked through his collar and tearing free of the fabric; the rain; and Corvane on his knees in the gutter with his hand pressed to his side and blood coming up between his fingers, black in the lamplight, laughing softly at something.

"Why," I said, "are you laughing?"

"Because," he said, "you're doing the thing with your face. The ledger face. You're balancing me."

I was. I had his wrist in both hands, reading his pulse the way I read a suspect broth—counting, measuring, assessing how much time the dish had left. His pulse was fast and thin. Too fast. Too thin.

"Pressure," I said, mostly to myself. "You need pressure and stitches and you need them somewhere that isn't a gutter." I hauled his arm across my shoulders. He was heavier than he looked, or perhaps I was weaker than I pretended; both seemed true.

"There's a surgeon on Tallow Street—"

"Tallow Street is where Vess's men will regroup." I got us moving, one staggering step at a time. "There's a room behind the salt warehouse. Guild storeroom. You told me once no one watches it because no one remembers it."

"Did I?" His voice was going soft and dreamy at the edges, which is a very bad sign in a man losing blood. "I don't remember saying that."

"Then it's fortunate one of us does."

* * *

The storeroom smelled of brine and old rope and, faintly, of the guildhall's strange ink—a ghost of it, soaked into the walls over years. I got him onto a pallet of sacking, slit his shirt open with the small knife I keep in my sleeve because a taster who cannot cut cheese is a taster who starves, and looked at the wound.

It was ugly. Not mortal-ugly, not yet, but the kind of ugly that becomes mortal if left to argue with itself. The blade had gone in below the ribs, deep enough to worry about things I preferred not to name. Blood welled slow and dark.

I had thread. I had needle. I had spirits of wine, because a woman who poisons herself daily keeps antidotes the way other women keep ribbons. What I did not have was confidence, and so I did the thing I do instead of confidence: I worked.

"You know," Corvane said, as I cleaned the wound and he made a sound like a man politely declining to scream, "most people say thank you."

"For bleeding on my city?"

"For noticing." His eyes found mine, and even gray with pain they had that quality that unsettles me most about him: he looked *at* me. Not through, not past, not at the useful surface. At the whole inconvenient architecture underneath. "Most people look at a taster and see a spoon with legs. Vess looks at you and sees a map. I look at you and—" he exhaled as I pressed the cloth harder, "—and apparently get stabbed for it."

"Don't joke."

"It's either joke or think about the hole in me, and I've seen your ledgers, Isandro. I know how you feel about holes in accounts."

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet and startled, and I realized with distant horror that I was crying while I threaded the needle. I did not let him see my face while I stitched. A taster learns to keep her expressions off her hands; I kept them off my voice too, and we passed perhaps ten minutes in the rhythm of the work—his breathing, my counting, the rain on the roof like a clerk tapping a pen.

When it was done—when the wound was closed and bound and he was pale but stable, propped against the wall watching me burn the bloody cloth in the brazier—he spoke again, and his voice had lost the joking.

"The bargain stands," he said. "You know it does. Protection. The guild's whole weight between you and Vess. Safe passage, safe name, safe life. All of it yours for one memory. The oldest one. Her face."

The woman who raised me. The cook with flour on her forearms whose name I had never learned to say without my chest tightening. The one person in my childhood who had looked at me the way Corvane did—as though I were a person and not a function.

"I know the terms," I said.

"And yet you hesitate. Interesting. You hesitate over nothing else. You swallowed hemlock broth last spring without blinking."

"Hemlock doesn't take anything away. Hemlock just stops me." I fed the fire another strip of cloth and watched it curl. "A memory taken isn't stopped. It's deleted. And I've been reading ledgers long enough to know that deletions leave gaps, and gaps get filled with lies."

"Not always lies. Sometimes peace."

"Sometimes," I admitted. "And sometimes the gap is where a person used to be."

He was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere outside, the harbor bell counted the hour, patient as ever, indifferent to whether anyone lived to hear it.

"Isandro," he said, and my name in his mouth sounded like a different name, one I might have chosen for myself, "why did you come to the guildhall every night for six years? You balance books that no one audits. You could have kept the ledgers anywhere. Why there?"

"Because the ink doesn't lie," I said, which was the easy answer, and we both knew it.

"And the true one?"

I looked at him—at this impossible man who traded in other people's intangibles and paid for my life with his own blood without being asked—and the truth came up in me the way blood comes from a wound: unbidden, unwelcome, unstoppable.

"Because you see me," I said. "Because in six years you are the only person who has ever looked at me and seen anything worth looking at. Because when you walk into a room I stop cataloguing exits. Do you understand what that means? For twenty years I have walked into every room already knowing how I'd leave it. With you I forget. That is either love or catastrophic negligence, and I have checked my own pulse often enough to know the difference between fear and this."

His breath caught. Actually caught—an audible, human, unguarded sound, from the man who arrived like doors opening.

"So yes," I said, and my voice shook and I let it, because some entries must be written in honest ink, "I love you. I am telling you this now, while you are stitched shut and unarmed and unable to leverage it, because I want it on the record. I love you. And the answer is still no."

Something moved through his face—grief and wonder wrestling for the same ground.

"No," he repeated.

"No. I won't sell her face. Not for safety, not for you, not for the guild. If Vess takes me tomorrow, she takes me whole, with every memory intact, and I will die knowing the color of flour on warm skin. Some things aren't currency, Corvane. She wasn't a payment. Neither is this." I touched my own temple. "Whatever I am, whoever wrote me into these ledgers before I lived—I will meet that page with everything still in my head. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* the parts that hurt."

He studied me for a long time. Outside, the rain began to slacken, and the harbor bell rang the half-hour, and neither of us moved.

"You know what the terrible thing is?" he said finally, and there was a smile in it, tired and real. "I'm not even disappointed. I think I offered the bargain hoping you'd refuse. A man in my trade learns to distrust anything given freely—but you, you refuse to give at all unless it's freely given. You may be the only honest trader I've ever met."

"Flattery will cost you extra."

"Everything costs extra with you." He shifted, winced, and gripped my hand—his fingers cold, his grip stubborn. "Then hear my counter-offer, freely given, no price attached. The guild protects you anyway. Not as a purchase. As a—" he paused, and for the first time since I'd known him, the guildmaster of the Impossible Things seemed genuinely uncertain of his vocabulary, "—as a choice. Mine. Made tonight, in a salt warehouse, by a man who has just discovered that some entries can't be balanced, only carried."

"That's a terrible bargain."

"It's not a bargain at all. That's rather the point."

I held his cold hand and felt the pulse beneath it, steadying now, finding its rhythm, and thought about maps sewn into coats and mothers written in future ink and queens poisoned gently at their own tables. Everything in my life was a trade except this, this one unpriced thing sitting between us in the brazier-light.

"Stay here tonight," I said. "Sleep. I'll watch."

"You don't have to—"

"I'm a taster, Corvane. Watching is the entire profession."

He slept, eventually, the sleep of the honestly wounded, and I sat by the brazier with the map warm against my ribs and his pulse counting time beside me, and I let myself—for exactly one hour, no longer—imagine a life in which I belonged to no ledger at all.

Which was, of course, precisely when I noticed the ink.

It was on my fingers. Faint, black-blue, the guild's own impossible ink—though I hadn't touched a ledger since morning. I turned my hand in the firelight, and the marks shifted, rearranged themselves, and resolved into a single line of writing that curled around my thumb like a ring:

*The daughter returns to the stone. Bring the map. Bring the blood.*

Written in handwriting I knew better than my own.

My mother's.

And dated—fresh ink, still glistening—tomorrow.
