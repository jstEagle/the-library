## 12. Halfway to Exile

The queen's seal was warm against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my coat where a sensible woman might keep a love letter or a stolen spoon. It was neither. It was a small disc of lead, no bigger than a coin, stamped with Maren's private mark—the crescent and the key—and it had come to me folded inside a napkin at the morning tasting, tucked beneath a honeyed fig like a bribe from a child.

*The west road,* the napkin said, in her careful, schoolroom hand. *Trust the seal, not the bearer.*

I had read it twice, eaten the fig—it was clean, I checked, I always check—and then walked out of the palace through the servants' gate with my heart beating so loudly I was certain the whole courtyard could hear it. Nobody looked at me. That was the gift of being a taster: you learn to be furniture that swallows.

By noon I was past the salt gate, past the fish stalls, past the last of the city's crooked rooftops, and on the west road with nothing but a coat full of treason, three days' bread, and the particular lightness of a woman who has finally done something irreversible.

It should have felt like terror. It felt, mostly, like hunger.

* * *

Let me be honest about what fleeing looks like when you have spent your whole life being invisible. It looks like walking. It looks like keeping your shoulders down and your eyes soft and your face arranged into the expression of someone who is going somewhere boring for reasons that are nobody's business. I had practiced that face over ten years of royal tables. Now it was carrying me out of the capital one unremarkable mile at a time.

The map rode against my skin, sewn flat between wool and linen in eight neat rows of stitches I had put in myself by candlelight, biting the thread with my teeth. I had considered burning it. I had stood at my little hearth with the wax-sealed thing in my hands and thought, *one match and every problem ends.* But you cannot burn a debt. You can only pass it along or pay it, and I had learned enough from the ledgers to know which kind of person I intended to be.

So I stitched it into my coat like a second spine and left before dawn, and now the road unspooled ahead of me—dusty, ordinary, mercifully empty.

I ate walking. I counted my coins twice and then a third time, because counting is what I do instead of panicking. Eleven coppers, two silvers, and a gold piece I had been saving against a future so vague I had never once described it aloud. The future had a shape now, though not the one I'd imagined. The future was a stone under a palace and a word whispered by a dying man, and eleven coppers would not buy me out of it.

Near evening I passed a shrine at the crossroads—one of the old ones, pre-Oath, its carved figure worn faceless by weather and piety. Travelers had left offerings: bread gone hard, a ribbon, a child's wooden bird. I stopped, because stopping felt dangerous and therefore necessary.

"You wouldn't know anything about oaths," I told the faceless saint. "You predate them."

The saint said nothing, being stone, but the wind moved through the hedgerow behind it in a way that sounded almost amused. I left half my bread anyway. Old habits. A taster learns early that food given freely is the only food you can trust.

* * *

I learned how wanted I was on the second day, at a toll bridge over the Greywater.

There were notices nailed to the tollhouse post, four of them, layered like feathers. The topmost bore the Crown's mark and Lady Vess's personal sigil—a heron swallowing a serpent, which even as heraldry went struck me as optimistic. Beneath it, my own face looked back at me, rendered in ink by someone who had never seen me and had clearly asked a frightened kitchen boy for a description.

*WANTED FOR TREASON AND THEFT OF CROWN SECRETS. ISANDRO DUNMORE, FOOD-TASTER TO THE QUEEN. ANSWERS ALSO TO NO NAME IN PARTICULAR. REWARD: FIFTY GOLD PIECES, OR PARDON FOR ONE (1) MINOR CRIME.*

Fifty gold pieces. I made nine a year. There was something almost flattering in learning my market value, and something funnier still in the pardon-for-one-minor-crime clause, as though Vess imagined the kingdom's petty thieves lining up to trade their debts for my head. I suppose some would. Debt makes people creative.

But it was the third notice that stopped my breath.

It was printed on grey paper—not Crown paper, guild paper—and it carried the Guild of Impossible Things' mark: the open hand holding nothing. The text was short, and it was written in the old formal cadence we used only for the gravest entries.

*LET IT BE KNOWN. THE LEDGER-KEEPER ISANDRO DUNMORE HAS TAKEN WHAT WAS ENTRUSTED AND REFUSED ALL BARGAIN. SHE IS BRANDED OATHBREAKER. NO MEMBER SHALL TRADE WITH HER, SHELTER HER, SPEAK HER NAME, OR WRITE IT. HER ACCOUNTS ARE CLOSED.*

Closed accounts. In guild terms, that was a death sentence of a very specific kind: not the ending of a life, but the ending of a record. A person whose accounts are closed becomes, in the guild's eyes, a rumor. Untraceable. Uninsurable. Unsavable.

Corvane had signed it himself. His name sat at the bottom in that flowing hand I knew better than any handwriting in the world, having balanced his books for six years. And beneath the signature, small, almost an afterthought, a single line in different ink:

*This grieves me more than you can ledger.*

I stood at the toll post reading that line until the toll-keeper coughed pointedly, and I paid my copper and crossed the bridge, and only once I was safely around the first bend did I let myself laugh—which came out closer to a sob, so I did both, briefly, standing in a ditch like a madwoman.

Because here was the thing I understood, holding the memory of that grey paper: Corvane did not want me caught. If he had, he would have sent men, not paper. He had branded me oathbreaker because branding me was the only protection he could offer. Vess's agents would hunt a traitor with fifty gold on her head. But they would hesitate at a guild-branded oathbreaker, because everyone in the capital knew the guild settled its own debts, and interfering with a guild collection was how you ended up owing things you couldn't pronounce.

He had closed my accounts to open my road. It was the most Corvane gesture imaginable: a kindness shaped exactly like a wound.

And still. Still, somewhere behind my sternum, the words *her accounts are closed* sat like a swallowed stone of their own. Six years of ledgers, six years of being the one person who knew where everything the guild owned actually was—and now I was nowhere in the books at all. I had spent my life trying to stay off records. I had never expected it to hurt this much to succeed.

* * *

On the third night the rain found me, and so did the inn.

I want to say I resisted the inn. I had silver; the inn had walls; resistance lost quickly. It was called the Drowned Hen, which is the sort of name that tells you everything about the establishment before you're through the door, and it was warm, and it smelled of onion and tallow, and after three days of eating bread in ditches I would have sold a small memory for stew.

I took a corner table with my back to the wall, hood up, and ordered stew and whatever the ale was, and I performed my quiet ritual before touching either: the small pause, the attention, the taster's listening. Onion, leek, barley, salt, a ghost of thyme. Ale, hops, water from a well that had tasted iron recently. Nothing else. Clean. I ate like a woman who had been starving, which I had, in every sense available.

The common room filled around me. Drovers, a peddler, two sisters arguing cheerfully about a horse. Ordinary people living ordinary lives, none of them branded, all of them recorded somewhere in somebody's book—parish rolls, tax lists, the innkeeper's chalkboard of debts. I watched them with the strange tenderness of the exiled. They had no idea what a luxury it was, being written down.

Then the door opened and the room changed temperature.

Two men in travel-stained cloaks, but cloaks don't fool anyone who has spent a decade reading courtiers. They moved like Vess's people moved: weight forward, eyes doing the room in sweeps, hands loose near belts. One of them carried a leather satchel, and from the way he carried it—carefully, like a sleeping animal—I would have bet my gold piece it held papers. Notices. Perhaps a newer likeness of my face, drawn by someone who had actually seen me.

They took a table near the fire. They ordered ale. They did not drink it.

I finished my stew at the pace of a woman with nowhere to be, which is to say slowly, while my mind ran its cold arithmetic. Back door: through the kitchen, likely bolted, and kitchens have ears. Front door: past their table. Stairs: rooms above, windows, drop to the yard—but I had seen the yard, and the yard had a wall, and the wall had a gap where a gate had once been, because I check exits the way other people check the weather.

The taster's discipline is patience. But there is another discipline underneath it, older, the one my body knew before my training: when the broth is poisoned, you do not sip politely and hope. You set down the spoon.

I set down the spoon.

I rose, unhurried, and walked toward the kitchen as if I belonged there—as if I were perhaps the cook's cousin, late for my shift—and the nearer man's eyes tracked me the way a cat tracks a bird that hasn't yet earned the pounce. I gave him the face. The boring face. The *going somewhere dull for dull reasons* face. Furniture that walks.

In the kitchen, a large woman was beating dough with the rhythm of long marriage. "Out," she said, without looking up.

"Back door's bolted," I said. "May I? I'll owe you."

That got her attention. She looked at me—at the hood, the mud, the eyes—and something in her face shifted, the way faces do when they recognize a particular kind of tired. "Owe me what?"

"A true account. Someday, to someone."

She snorted. Then she unbolted the door, because cooks understand debts better than bankers do. "Yard wall's got a gap, north corner. Mind the dog."

"There's a dog?"

"There's always a dog."

There was. I minded him. We came to an arrangement involving the heel of my bread, and then I was over the wall and into the wet dark beyond, running along hedgerows with rain in my teeth and my heart hammering out a rhythm I recognized: not fear, exactly. Purpose. Fear with somewhere to go.

Behind me, faint through the rain, the Drowned Hen's door banged open and a voice shouted something about a woman in a grey coat. I pulled the coat tighter and kept moving. Let them chase a description. Descriptions had never caught me yet.

* * *

I walked through the rest of that night and most of the next day, and somewhere on a long empty stretch where the road climbed away from the river and the land opened into moor, I finally let myself stop and think about the queen.

*Trust the seal, not the bearer,* she had written. Which meant she knew her household leaked. Which meant she had chosen her messenger knowing it might be read, and had written a message that would survive being read. Which meant Queen Maren—who tasted nothing herself, who swallowed her council's slow poison at every meal because refusing it would tell them she knew—was playing the same game I was, from inside a smaller cage.

I touched the lead disc through my coat. Crescent and key. A lock, and the means of opening it, worn together on one seal. Whoever had designed that mark had understood something essential: that a secret kept is just a prison with good manners.

Three centuries ago, someone had forged an Oath and buried its lock beneath a palace, and the fae had gone into exile, and the peace had held the way a held breath holds. Everyone said the Oath saved us. The dying man had spent his last breath saying otherwise—or saying something, at least, that had cost him everything to carry. And my mother's handwriting waited in a ledger dated three days from now, and I was afraid of what it would say, and more afraid that I already knew.

I crested the hill as the sun went down, and the world opened below me: moorland rolling west in waves of purple and rust, a road thin as thread, and far off, at the edge of sight, a smudge of darker green that could only be the border marshes. The marshes. Where the fae road began. Where, if half the stories were true, things waited that had been waiting a very long time.

My feet ached. My coat was soaked. My accounts were closed, my face was worth fifty gold, and the only institution in the world that had ever known my name had disowned me in grey ink.

And I stood on that hill in the wind and discovered—to my considerable surprise—that I was not lonely.

Or rather: I was lonely, thoroughly, the way a bone is broken. But the loneliness had changed composition. All my life it had been the loneliness of the hidden, the ache of being unseen in plain sight, of cataloging toxins at a table where no one knew my favorite color. This was different. This was the loneliness of the visible—the specific solitude of a woman walking toward something on purpose, alone because the something required it.

One kind of loneliness shrinks you. The other one, I was beginning to suspect, is just the space your resolve takes up.

"Well," I said to the moor, to the wind, to the faceless saint and the dog and the dead stranger and the queen in her gilded cage and Corvane with his grieving ink. "Halfway."

The moor declined to comment. But far off in the marsh-smudge, something answered—a light, brief and green, kindling and fading like a held breath released. Once. Twice. As if something out there had been watching the road for three hundred years, and had just seen me coming.

I pulled my coat tight around the map, around the seal, around my own stubborn unwritten heart, and started down the hill.

The dark was falling, and I walked into it like an invitation.
