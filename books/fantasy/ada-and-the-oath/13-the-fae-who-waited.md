## 13. The Fae Who Waited

The marshes began where the road gave up pretending.

Isandro felt the change in the ground before she saw it—hard packed clay going soft, then spongy, then treacherously kind, the way a lie is kind right up until it isn't. Three days out of the city, her boots had surrendered any claim to dignity. Her coat, with the map sewn flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, was stiff with dried mud and one unfortunate incident involving a goose that had opinions about trespassers.

"You are not a spy," she told herself for the hundredth time, in the tone she'd once used to catalog toxins. *Bitterroot, mild. Foxglove, moderate. Goose, severe.* "You are a woman taking a long walk for her health."

Her health was, frankly, terrible. She'd slept in ditches. She'd eaten things she refused to name even in the privacy of her own skull. The queen's seal sewn into her collar had gotten her bread and a hayloft at two farmhouses, and a very long stare at a third, and every mile north the air had grown stranger—greener, wetter, older, as though the land itself remembered something the people on it had been made to forget.

The outer road. Serevan's road, though she didn't know that yet. All she knew was what the map whispered when she let herself read it at night by no light at all, because her eyes had long ago learned to work in the dark the way other people's learned to flinch: *past the last milestone, past the drowned church, where the reeds grow in a ring.*

She found the drowned church at noon. It was exactly as advertised—a roofless stone shell sitting in a foot of black water, its bell tower leaning like a drunkard listening for a call that had stopped coming centuries ago. And around it, growing in a perfect circle through the shallows, the reeds stood taller than a man, green-gold and utterly still.

No wind. She noticed that. There had been wind all morning, worrying her hair, shoving the reeds on the far bank into whispering crowds. Here, inside the ring, the air simply *stopped*, the way a room stops when someone important enters it.

Isandro stood at the edge of the water and did what she always did when facing something unknown: she took stock.

*One taster, half-starved, mud-caked, armed with a paring knife and an encyclopedic knowledge of which poisons kill quickly and which merely ruin your week. Pursued by the Crown's spymaster, disowned by the only family she had—the guild—and carrying a map that three separate parties would kill her for. Assets: an immunity to poison that was never trained but inherited, fae blood she still didn't fully believe in, and a queen's seal. Liabilities: everything else.*

"Well," she said to the drowned church. "I've been invited to worse."

She hadn't, actually. But the lie steadied her, and she stepped into the ring of reeds.

The water was cold and clear and far deeper than it looked. Halfway across, her boot found a stone that shifted, and she went down with a splash that seemed to echo in three directions at once, and when she surfaced, spluttering, hair plastered to her face—

Someone was holding out a hand.

She hadn't heard him approach. That was the first impossibility. The second was his eyes, which caught the noon light and gave none of it back, silver as coins at the bottom of a well. He was tall and thin and dressed in gray-green layers that might have been clothing or might have been very committed lichen, and his face was the kind of face that made her ledger-keeping mind reach for categories and find the drawer empty. Not young. Not old. *Kept*, somehow, the way a letter is kept—pressed flat and preserved against a time when it would matter again.

"You," he said, in a voice like wind through winter branches, "are late."

Isandro, still in the water, still holding her paring knife in a grip that would have embarrassed a child, said: "I'm sorry?"

"Three hundred years." He said it without accusation, the way one notes the weather. "I was told to wait for the daughter of the ledger-keeper. I did the arithmetic. I assumed I had miscounted the years around year forty. Then around year ninety. After that I stopped counting and simply waited, because counting was making me unpleasant company." He looked her over, mud and all, with the frank assessment of someone examining a delivery. "You have her hands."

"I don't—whose hands?"

"The ledger-keeper's." He crouched at the water's edge, unhurried, and offered his hand again, properly this time. "Serevan. Warden of the outer road. Keeper of the last mile, the first mile, and the mile in between, depending on which side one is coming from. You are Isandro Dunmore, you have the map sewn into your coat—yes, I can see the shape of it, you sew like a cook, all practical stitches and no apology—and you are standing in consecrated water, which is why the leeches are giving you a wide berth. They know better. Come out before you catch a chill. It would be deeply anticlimactic."

She took his hand. It was cool and dry and stronger than it looked, and he pulled her out of the water as if she weighed nothing at all, which was flattering and slightly insulting.

"You know my name," she said.

"I know a great many things. I've had time." He produced, from somewhere in the lichen-layers, a cloth, and handed it to her with the gravity of a man presenting a ceremonial sword. "Dry your face. You have a smudge of marsh on your chin that makes you look like a very earnest raccoon."

She dried her face. It was that or laugh, and laughing felt dangerous, like a stitch coming undone.

* * *

He made tea.

This was, Isandro decided later, the moment the marshes stopped being a place she was fleeing *through* and became a place she had arrived *at*. Serevan's shelter was half a mile on, tucked into the roots of a willow so old its trunk had rooms—a hollowed space with a hearth, a shelf of jars, two stools, and a kettle that had to be older than the kingdom. He moved through the small space with the economy of someone who had arranged it once, perfectly, and then had three hundred years to enjoy the arrangement.

"You said you were told to wait," she said, wrapping her hands around the cup he gave her. It was the first warm thing she'd held since leaving the city, and she had to be careful, because part of her wanted to weep into it. "Told by whom?"

"By your mother." He said it simply, the way he seemed to say everything, and then watched her the way one watches a fire catch. "Aelin Dunmore. Ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. The last person to walk this road in either direction, until you."

The tea nearly went over. Isandro set it down with the exaggerated care of the very drunk or the very shattered. "My mother came here. My mother was *here*?"

"The night the Oath was forged. She came carrying a child." His silver eyes were gentle, which she hadn't expected gentleness from, and somehow that undid her more than any blade could have. "You."

"I was a baby."

"You were extremely loud, as I recall. You had opinions about the marsh. You expressed them at length." A pause, and something flickered across his kept face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile had been kept pressed flat for three centuries, waiting. "I told her the road was watched. She told me the road was the *point*. She said, *one day my daughter will come this way, because she is my daughter, and we do not leave roads unwalked.*" He turned his cup in his long fingers. "I said I would wait. Fae oaths are not like yours. When we give our word, it is not a promise. It is a shape we pour ourselves into. I have been this shape for three hundred years."

Isandro looked at him—at the stillness of him, the patience worn into him like water wears stone—and felt something crack open in her chest that she had spent a lifetime pretending wasn't there.

All her life she had been *tolerated*. The guild had used her neat hands and her invisible habits. The court had used her tongue and her stomach. Corvane had seen her, yes—Corvane had *seen* her, and she was still not sure whether that had been a gift or the most elegant trap ever baited—but even Corvane had wanted something. Everyone had wanted something. She had moved through the world like a coin passing hand to hand, valuable precisely because she belonged to no one.

And here, in a hollow willow in the border marshes, sat a stranger who had spent three centuries keeping a lamp lit for her.

"You didn't know me," she said. Her voice came out rough. "You couldn't have. I was an infant. I could have grown up to be anyone. I could have been cruel. I could have sold the map to the highest bidder and never come north at all."

"You could have." He refilled her cup without asking, and the steam smelled of honey and something greener, something that made the ache behind her eyes ease. "But Aelin told me three things about you, and I have checked each one against the evidence. She said you would be careful. You crossed my reed ring at noon, in full light, after scouting the water. Careful. She said you would be stubborn—you have been walking for three days on feet I can hear complaining from here, and you have not once considered turning back. Stubborn." He held up a third long finger. "And she said that underneath all the careful stubbornness, you would be starving. Not for food." His eyes met hers, and there was no cruelty in it, only an awful, accurate kindness. "For a place at a table where you were the reason for the meal."

Isandro drank her tea and said nothing, because if she opened her mouth she was going to cry, and she had not survived the royal court by crying in front of strangers.

She survived it by cataloging. So she cataloged: the way he moved without sound even when he chose to make noise. The way the light in the shelter bent slightly toward him, like a crowd leaning in. The way her own blood, her strange inherited blood, sat quieter in her veins here than it ever had in the city—recognized, she realized. *At home.*

"The road," she said, when she trusted her voice. "You said you kept it. Kept it from what?"

"From everything that isn't you." He set down his cup. "The marshes are the border of the old exile. What the Oath caged, it caged behind a door, and this road is the door's last hinge. For three centuries nothing has crossed that I did not permit. Crown scouts came, twice. I turned them around. Guild runners came, four times, carrying questions about your mother. I turned them around too, though one of them I made forget his own name for a week, which I regret, because he seemed otherwise pleasant." He tilted his head. "And now you come, carrying the map to the stone, with the Crown's hounds three days behind you and closing."

Her stomach dropped. "You know about Vess."

"I know about *weather*, and I know when a storm is walking toward me wearing a coat." He rose, and the shelter seemed to rise with him, the willow roots creaking like the ribs of something waking. "Lady Vess does not want the map, Isandro. I have watched her kind for three hundred years from the wrong side of a door. She wants what the map opens. And she is not three days behind you." His silver eyes went to the still, listening air outside. "She is one."

* * *

They walked the outer road as the light went long and gold.

Serevan moved like a rumor through the reeds, and Isandro found that if she matched her breathing to his pace, her aching feet nearly stopped mattering. He talked as they went—not urgently, but steadily, the way a man pours out a store he has kept too long—and every word he said rearranged something in her.

He showed her the milestone with no number, worn blank by centuries of rain. He showed her the ford where the water ran backward at dusk, "a small vanity of the old magic, it never hurt anyone." He showed her a hollow oak where, he said, letters had been left for the exiled for three hundred years—most of them from children, most of them apologies for things the children hadn't done, because children understand debt better than their parents do.

And at the road's edge, where the marsh gave way at last to rising ground and the first true trees of the deep border, he stopped.

"Here is what I know," he said, "and here is why I waited. Your mother did not come to me the night of the Oath as a fugitive. She came as a *signatory*. She was there when it was forged, Isandro. She held the pen—or the fae equivalent, which is a thorn, and hurts considerably more. The terms of the peace were written in part by a human woman who then spent the rest of her life making certain the terms were kept. And the last thing she asked of me was not to protect the stone." He turned to face her fully, and the setting light caught his eyes and made them burn. "It was to protect her daughter's *choice*. She said, *they will all of them tell my child what the stone is for. The Crown will say weapon. The guild will say treasure. Even the fae will say key. Tell her, when she comes, that it may be all of those things, and that she is allowed to want none of them.*"

The wind moved at last through the ring of reeds behind them, a long soft sigh, like an exhale held for three hundred years.

Isandro stood very still. Somewhere behind her, south, a storm with a coat was walking through the night. Somewhere ahead, the stone waited with its voices, its promises, its terrible generous weight. And here, on a road that ended in nothing but trees and dusk and one fae who had kept his word longer than kingdoms last, she felt—for the first time in her careful, invisible, cataloged life—not tolerated. Not used. Not traded.

*Expected.*

"I'm hungry," she said, which was not at all what she meant, and was exactly what she meant.

Serevan's pressed-flat smile finally unfolded, and it was worth the wait. "Then you will eat," he said. "And then you will sleep. And in the morning—" He paused. His head turned, sharp and sudden, toward the south, toward the dark, and every easy line of him went taut as wire.

"What is it?" Isandro whispered.

"Lanterns," he said quietly. "On the road behind us. More than one. Moving fast." He looked back at her, and the gentleness in his face had hardened into something old and bright and dangerous. "It seems, daughter of the ledger-keeper, that your storm has found the door. Come. There is a ford I have been saving for three hundred years, and I believe tonight it earns its keep."
