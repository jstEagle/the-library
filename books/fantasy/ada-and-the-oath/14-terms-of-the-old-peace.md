## 14. Terms of the Old Peace

Serevan had kept vigil over the outer road for three hundred and eleven years, and in all that time he had learned precisely two things: that patience is a muscle, and that humans cannot walk through a marsh without complaining about it.

The girl — Isandro, though she had not yet told him why she carried her name like a borrowed coat — sat on a hummock of peat with her boots off and her map spread across her knees, frowning at it as though it had insulted her cooking.

"The road bends east here," she said. "Your marsh doesn't."

"My marsh," Serevan said mildly, "does exactly as it likes. It has never once consulted me. We have an understanding." He settled beside her on a root that had learned, over the centuries, to be comfortable for him specifically. "You are reading it like a human map. Fold it. Close your eyes. Ask it where you want to go."

"That's not how maps work."

"No," he agreed. "That's how *our* maps work. Yours are very good at telling you where things were. Ours tell you where things are willing to be found." He plucked the wax-sealed parchment from her hands before she could protest, folded it once, twice, until it was small enough to hide in a closed fist, and pressed it back into her palm. "Now sleep on it. The stone has waited three centuries. It can wait one more night, and so can you."

She did not sleep, of course. He watched her pretend to, lying rigid as a drawn bowstring with her eyes shut and her mind galloping. Humans did that. They thought rest was a thing you could schedule between catastrophes.

He let her. There would be time enough for truth tomorrow, and truth, unlike patience, was not a muscle. It was a wound. You did not open it casually.

* * *

In the morning she asked him the question he had been waiting three centuries to answer, and she asked it the way she seemed to ask everything — sideways, while doing something else, in this case chewing a strip of dried fish with the grim determination of a woman who had tasted better poisons.

"You said you were waiting for me. The daughter of the ledger-keeper." She did not look at him. "Waiting for what, exactly? What am I supposed to do when I get there?"

"Ah," said Serevan.

"'Ah' is not an answer."

"'Ah' is the sound a man makes when he has rehearsed an answer for three hundred years and finds, upon arriving at it, that every rehearsal was a coward's version." He turned a reed over in his long grey fingers. "Very well. Sit properly. This is a history, and histories deserve posture."

She sat properly. She even stopped chewing. He found himself absurdly fond of her already, which was dangerous, and told himself so, and ignored himself, which was also a fae tradition.

"Do you know what they teach in your cities about the Oath?" he began. "I collect versions. It is a hobby. In the capital they teach that the fae were banished for our pride — that we broke the world with our meddling and your wise kings sealed us out of mercy. In the northern holds they teach that we abandoned humanity out of contempt. In the fishing villages they don't teach anything at all, but they leave out bowls of cream on certain nights, just in case, which I have always thought showed better sense than either version."

"And the true version?"

"The true version begins with a war that both sides started." He said it plainly, because there was no kind way to say it. "Not a war of aggression. A war of grief. Your people had begun to forget us — not gradually, the way forgetting usually works, but all at once, in whole villages, whole bloodlines. Names wiped clean overnight. And we knew why, though we did not yet know how to stop it, and being fae, we did the worst possible thing."

"Which was?"

"We blamed you." He smiled, without much humor in it. "We assumed theft. Someone was stealing our names out of your heads, and surely it was deliberate, surely it was your kings hoarding memory like grain. So we took things back. Small things at first. A harvest remembered wrongly. A bride who woke unable to recall her own wedding. And your kings, quite reasonably from where they stood, concluded the fae had declared war on the human mind itself, and they retaliated in the only currency they had."

"Iron," Isandro said quietly.

"Iron, and worse than iron. They burned the groves where we kept our dead. And we —" his voice caught on the old edge of it, three centuries dull but never gone — "we answered in kind. For six years, girl. Six years of two peoples teaching each other that the other was a disease. Children on both sides grew up fluent in hatred before they could write their own names."

* * *

He rose, because the story sat badly in a sitting body, and walked to the edge of the pool where the water lay brown and patient. Isandro followed. She was learning marsh manners faster than most.

"The ones who ended it were not kings," he said. "They were bookkeepers."

Her head came up sharply.

"Bookkeepers, ledger-keepers, oath-minders — call them what you like. On your side, a woman named Maren of the Quill — no relation to your queen, or perhaps every relation; crowns repeat themselves like bad poetry. On ours, my sister, Ysolde, who kept the tally of everything the war had cost us, and whose tally grew so heavy she could no longer lift it alone." He looked at Isandro then, directly, and saw her understand arrive a half-step ahead of his words, the way understanding does with clever people. "Your mother's line, taster. Ledger-keepers on both sides of the border. Did you truly think your guild invented its trade?"

"The Guild of Impossible Things is human. Two hundred years old, maybe—"

"It is a candle burning in a window left open by a house that burned down. Yes. Continue being surprised; it becomes you." He let the gentleness land, because the next part deserved soft hands. "Ysolde and Maren met seven times in secret along this road. Seven meetings, each one recorded in a shared ledger written in ink made from oath and ash. And what they wrote, in the end, was not a treaty. Treaties are promises between enemies, and promises rot. They wrote a cage."

"A cage," Isandro repeated.

"The Oathstone is not a wall, whatever your scholars imagine. Walls keep people out. The stone keeps everyone *in* — the fae behind it, yes, exiled to the far side of the veil, but also—" he tapped his own chest, once, "—this. Every fae who crossed into exile left something behind in the stone. Our wars. Our grievances. The whole hot archive of why we fought. It holds them still. That is why the peace has held for three hundred years without a single garrison, without a single watchtower. Not because humans guard the border. Because the border guards itself, and what it guards against is *us*, or rather, the parts of us that wanted the war."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. The marsh filled the silence with its small industrious sounds — water moving, insects negotiating, a heron somewhere conducting its affairs.

"And if the stone breaks," she said at last, carefully, "the archive opens."

"If the stone breaks, everything comes home." He spread his hands. "Our griefs. Our angers. Three centuries of them, aged like wine in a sealed cask. Perhaps the fae who return will be wiser for the separation. Perhaps they will step through the veil weeping with relief and ask only for cream bowls and forgiveness. Or perhaps they will come through with six years of war still fresh in their mouths and three hundred years of grievance fermenting on top of it, and find a world of humans who have spent those same three centuries telling stories about monsters." He shrugged, a gesture he had learned from watching cranes. "I genuinely do not know. Neither did Ysolde. That is why she built a lock instead of a door."

"But a lock is also a key."

"*Everything* that locks is also a key, if you are brave enough or foolish enough. That is the entire moral architecture of the world, as far as I can tell." He crouched by the pool and stirred the water with one finger, and images rose in it unbidden — he did not summon them; the marsh simply remembered things, the way old houses do. "Look. I will show you what the stone looks like from our side."

* * *

In the brown water: a chamber beneath a palace, and in it a stone no larger than a well-cap, veined with light that moved like slow gold blood. Around it, faint as frost on glass, shapes pressed — faces, hundreds of faces, some screaming, some singing, some simply waiting with the terrible patience of the archived.

And among them, a face that made Isandro make a small wounded sound and reach toward the water as though she could touch it.

"You see her too," Serevan said quietly.

"She's in the stone." Isandro's voice had gone thin. "My mother is *in the stone*."

"No." He caught her wrist — gently; fae learn gentleness or they learn nothing. "Look again. She is not among the bound. She stands apart. She is the keeper's echo — the last hand that wrote the Oath leaves a shadow in the ink, always. Ysolde's shadow stands there too, on the far side. Two bookkeepers, keeping their books, forever." He released her wrist. "It is not imprisonment, what your mother does in there. It is more like... standing watch. Which, I can tell you from experience, is its own kind of sentence, and its own kind of love."

"Then if I break the stone—"

"Her shadow goes free with the rest. As does mine sister's. As does everything else." He met her eyes. "Including the war."

* * *

They walked after that, because walking was easier than thinking, and the marsh permitted both. It was nearly dusk when Isandro stopped on the causeway and asked the question underneath all her other questions.

"You've had three centuries to think about it. Three hundred years standing on this road." She hugged her arms against the evening damp. "Should I break it?"

Serevan considered giving her the honest non-answer, the one he had polished across three hundred years of herons and rain: *that is not mine to decide.* But she had bled for the question, someone else's blood and soon perhaps her own, and cowards' versions were for cowards.

"When Ysolde went into exile," he said, "she told me the truth about the stone, and then she told me the truth about myself. 'You will stand on this road,' she said, 'and you will want to open it, because you will miss us. Do not confuse missing with wisdom.' And I have obeyed her for three hundred years, and it has been the correct choice every single day, and every single day it has also been a small death." He looked west, where the veil shimmered at the horizon like heat above cold ground. "But you are not opening it because you miss anyone. You are carrying a dead man's map and a living mother's handwriting and a spymaster's knives at your back, and you are asking whether a cage built by two grieving women should stand forever merely because they were right *enough*."

"Yes," Isandro said. "That."

"Then here is my answer, daughter of the ledger-keeper, and mark that I give it freely, which among my people makes it binding: a lock that requires a key is a confession that the lockers knew they might be wrong. Ysolde built the stone so it *could* be broken. Not easily — nothing worth breaking is easy — but possible. If she had believed the cage eternally just, she would have built a wall." He smiled, and this time the humor reached his eyes. "So the question was never whether the stone may fall. The question is whether you are the one to catch it."

Before she could answer — and he was glad, afterward, that she never got the chance — the marsh went quiet.

Not peaceful-quiet. *Attending*-quiet. Every heron lifting at once, every insect holding its breath, the water going mirror-still in a way water never does honestly.

Serevan turned toward the eastern causeway, and his old soldier's heart, which he had assumed had retired three centuries ago, picked up its sword.

"They're here," he said. "Both of them. The spymaster rides hard, and the guildmaster walks soft, and neither knows the other follows." He glanced at Isandro, standing pale and straight-backed on the causeway with a dead man's map in her coat and a living mother's face still rippling on the black water behind her. "You have until moonrise, taster, to decide what you're carrying. After that, everyone arrives at once, and decisions stop being yours."

He was already moving toward the eastern reeds when he heard her say, very quietly, to no one — or possibly to the water, or possibly to the shadow standing watch inside a stone beneath a palace —

"I know what I owe."

Serevan did not look back. Looking back, his sister had taught him, was how bookkeepers lost count.
