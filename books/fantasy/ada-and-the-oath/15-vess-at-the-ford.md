## 15. Vess at the Ford

The rain had followed Lady Vess for three days, which she considered a personal insult.

She had spent thirty years cultivating an air of inevitability—black gloves, blacker carriage, a face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a portrait that had outlived its painter. Inevitability did not ride through marsh mud with rain dripping off her nose. And yet here she was, ankle-deep in a ford that smelled of rot and iron, watching three figures pick their way along the far bank like pilgrims who had misplaced their shrine.

"Your Excellency," said Captain Dree, hunched under his oilcloth, "we could take them at the water's edge. Six men against three."

"We could," Vess agreed. "And the fae one would drown two of your six before you touched him, and the guildsman would vanish into the reeds with the map, and I would be explaining to His Majesty why his spymaster lost a national treasure to a swamp." She adjusted her glove. "No. We wait until they cross. Water makes people honest. It's very hard to run in mud while carrying secrets."

Dree looked at her with the particular wariness of a man who had served her long enough to know she was never merely making conversation. "And if they won't bargain?"

"Everyone bargains, Captain. The only question is the currency." She watched the tall figure on the far bank—the girl, Dunmore, sleeves rolled, skirts hitched up with a complete disregard for dignity that Vess found almost refreshing. The taster moved like a woman who had spent her whole life being unseen and had recently discovered how much she disliked it. "Some pay in coin. Some pay in blood. The expensive ones pay in grief."

* * *

Isandro saw them first—or rather, heard them: the wet slap of boots on stone, the creak of leather, the small metallic music of six soldiers trying to be quiet and failing. She stopped mid-ford, water pulling at her calves, and turned.

Lady Vess stood on the near bank beneath a broad grey sky, hands folded, as serene as a woman waiting for tea to steep.

"You're out of breath, Spymaster," Isandro called across the water. "Chasing girls through marshes is beneath your office."

"Everything is beneath my office. That is what makes it my office." Vess's voice carried easily over the ford, dry as old paper. "Come out of the water, child. You'll catch your death, and I've gone to considerable trouble to prevent exactly that."

Corvane shifted beside Isandro, and she felt him go still in the way he did when he was calculating odds—not of survival, but of price. Serevan had simply stopped moving altogether, the way the fae did, becoming one more grey thing in a grey landscape.

"The map," Vess said. "That's all I want. Give it to me, walk back to the capital, resume tasting soup, and no one will ever say the word *traitor* in your hearing again. I'll even have the charges against the guildhall staff quietly drowned. They'll wake up free men with excellent stories."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take it from your cold hands, and I grieve at your funeral, sincerely, because I have always liked you, Isandro Dunmore, more than I have ever liked anyone I've been ordered to destroy."

Isandro waded forward another step. "You burned half the ledgers. You arrested a boy of sixteen for shelving books."

"I burned ledgers full of other people's sins, yes. It's called mercy where I come from." Something flickered behind Vess's composure then—a hairline crack in the porcelain—and Isandro, connoisseur of hidden things, saw it. "You don't understand what the stone is. None of you do. You think it's a weapon, or a key, or some grand historical artifact. Let me tell you what it actually is."

* * *

Vess had rehearsed this speech for thirty years, in bathhouses and confessionals and the long grey hours before dawn, and now that the moment had come, the words came out wrong—too plain, too human, stripped of all her careful architecture.

"It holds the dead," she said. "Not their souls—that's nursery theology. It holds what they were. Every oath sworn over it, every promise, every name given to it in binding—it kept them. Three hundred years ago, when the Oath was sealed, everything sworn became part of the stone. Including the people who were bound by it." Her voice stayed level. Her hands did not; she pressed them flat against each other inside her gloves. "There is a man in that stone. He swore himself to the peace before it was finished. He gave the Oath his name, his years, his—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "He was my husband. He has been in that stone since before I was born, and he is not dead, whatever the priests say. He is *kept*. And I am going to open the lock and take him back."

The rain filled the silence.

Isandro stood hip-deep in the ford, and Vess watched the girl's face do something complicated—pity and horror and recognition arriving all at once, like guests at a bad dinner party.

"That's why you broke the Oath's terms once before," Isandro said quietly. "Chapter nine of your own private ledger."

Vess laughed, short and sharp. "You've been reading my file. How flattering. Yes. Twenty-two years ago I tried to reach the chamber alone. I got as far as the stair. The door wouldn't open for me—I lacked the proper ancestry—and so I did the next best thing. I took a memory from the stone's outer seal. One vial. A single stolen hour of his voice." Her jaw tightened. "I listened to it every night for eleven years, and then the Crown confiscated it, and I have been a very efficient instrument of the state ever since, because efficiency is what remains when you remove the reason for anything."

* * *

Isandro felt Corvane's hand brush hers—warning, or comfort, or both—and stepped closer to the bank, close enough now to see the fine cracks in Lady Vess's armor, the exhaustion underneath the black gloves, the woman who had spent three decades being a knife and remembered, dimly, having once been a hand.

"You want me to give you the map," Isandro said, "so you can break the Oath and pull a man out of a rock."

"A man out of a *memory of a rock*, technically, but yes."

"And what happens to everyone else in there? The hundreds bound into it? The exiles on the other side of it?"

"They stay put. I'm not a monster, girl. I'd reseal it after."

"With what? The door opens to fae blood. Mine, apparently. Do you intend to keep me as a keyring?"

Vess's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—the small, terrible honesty of a woman too tired to lie well. "I intended to keep you alive and comfortable for as long as that took. I confess the plan had gaps."

"There's a gap the size of a kingdom in it." Isandro waded another step. "You're asking me to hand you the one thing standing between a broken peace and a war that killed thousands, because you're lonely."

"*Lonely.*" Vess said the word like it was a bone she'd choked on. "Child, I have read every letter he ever wrote. I have memorized the grain of his handwriting. I have spent thirty years being the most feared woman in this kingdom, and do you know what I want? What I have wanted every single night?" Her voice dropped, and the rain seemed to lean in to hear it. "I want someone to say my name the way he used to. Not 'Excellency.' Not 'my lady.' My *name*. And he is forty feet under a palace, kept whole in a stone, and you are telling me I should let him stay there because the alternative is *inconvenient*."

"Yes," Isandro said gently. "That is approximately what I'm telling you."

* * *

For a moment Vess genuinely considered having her archers loose their arrows, and the fact that she considered it told her precisely how far she had fallen. Instead she did the thing she despised most: she bargained honestly.

"Dunmore." She came down to the water's edge, boots sinking into the mud, close enough now that they might have spoken in a drawing room. "You have a mother in that stone too. Or near it. The ledger-keeper who forged the Oath, who smuggled a child out, who died owing debts to the fae. You've seen her final night—you stole that vial yourself, don't look surprised, I know about the vial. You know what she promised. You know what she *was*." She let that land. "Give me the map, help me open the chamber, and I will use what influence remains to me to find her fate in the stone. Her name. Her last words. Whatever the Oath kept of her. You could have your mother back—not alive, I can't do that—but *real*. Whole. Herself, instead of fragments in a stranger's vial."

She watched the blow land. Watched the girl's breath catch, watched her hands tighten in the water, watched the exact moment temptation arrived and sat down at the table like an old friend.

It was, Vess reflected, the oldest trade in the world. Grief for grief. A mother for a husband. Two women on opposite banks of a river, each holding the other's wound like currency.

"Well?" she said softly. "You of all people understand ledgers, Isandro. Here is mine: your mother's truth for the map. Fair weight, fair measure. Name your objection."

"My objection," Isandro said, and her voice shook only a little, "is that you're offering me my mother the way the council offers the queen her supper. Slowly. Kindly. Poisoned." She lifted her chin. "My mother died keeping a promise. If I trade her to buy your husband out of a stone, I unmake the only thing she ever asked of anyone. I'd have her words and lose her meaning. That's not resurrection, Lady Vess. That's taxidermy."

Vess held her gaze for a long moment. Rain ran down between them, off noses and chins and the edges of two very different kinds of armor.

"You sound like her," Vess said at last, and there was something almost tender in it, almost broken. "Did you know that? The ledger-keeper. I met her once, at the sealing. She argued with the fae for three days straight, and she was right, and they sealed it anyway." She drew a slow breath. "I have wanted to be wrong about this for thirty years, child. Convince me."

Behind Isandro, Serevan finally moved, wading forward through the shallows with the unhurried grace of his kind, and when he spoke, his voice was the sound the marsh made at dusk.

"Then let me show her what she asks for, spymaster. Let me show you both what the stone keeps—and what it costs to open it. Because I was there at the sealing too. And I remember your husband's face better than you do."

Vess went very still.

The rain kept falling. The ford waited, brown and patient, between them.

And Isandro Dunmore, waist-deep in cold water with a map sewn into her coat and a choice rising in her throat like a swallowed coal, realized that the bargain had just changed shape entirely—and that none of them, not even the spymaster, was holding quite the same cards they'd walked in with.
