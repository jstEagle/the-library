## 16. What Isandro Owes

The marshes at dawn smelled of green things rotting into better things, and Isandro had learned—over three days of sleeping on Serevan's floor of woven reeds—that this was not an insult but a compliment. The bog was a kitchen that never stopped cooking. Everything in it was becoming something else.

She sat on the flat stone outside his hut with the ledger open across her knees, and she did not open it for a long while.

It was a coward's way of reading, and she knew it. She had been a coward about ledgers her whole life in small, forgivable ways: balancing a column twice because the first time frightened her, copying a trade three times before committing ink to the fair book. But this morning's hesitation was of a different vintage. This was the hesitation of a woman who already suspected what the page would say and wanted, just for one more hour, to be wrong.

"You're doing the thing," Serevan said from the doorway.

He stood with two clay cups steaming in his long hands, his silver hair unbound and full of marsh mist, looking exactly as he had looked every morning for three hundred years, which was to say: patient as geology.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at a book as though it might apologize." He handed her a cup. "In my experience, books rarely apologize. They only repeat themselves."

"Three centuries of waiting for me," she said, "and you've developed a sense of humor. That must have been exhausting."

"It came free with the waiting." He settled beside her on the stone, careful of the ledger, careful of her, in that way of his that made her feel less like a fugitive and more like a guest at some enormous, slow feast. "You don't have to read it."

"Yes, I do."

"You could burn it. You've considered it. I've watched you consider it for two days. You look at it the way you look at suspect broth."

That startled a laugh out of her, brief and rusty. "And what do I decide about suspect broth?"

"You taste it anyway. Because if you don't know what's in it, it owns you." He sipped his tea. "I'm told that's how you've survived the palace all these years. Knowing is your armor."

"Knowing is my *job*," she said. "Armor would have been useful somewhere around the docks, when the stranger bled on my boots."

But she set down the cup, and she opened the ledger.

* * *

The book had changed again overnight. She could tell without touching the pages—the way one can tell a room has been entered by someone else, a shift in the air, a wrongness too subtle to name. Oath-ink did not sit still. It breathed. Entries she had copied out in her own neat hand years ago had migrated, reworded themselves, grown annotations in scripts she didn't recognize. One entry, a memory sold by a cooper's widow in exchange for her husband's last winter, now bore a marginal note in a looping hand: *paid in full; the grief was worth more than she knew.*

Isandro turned past it. Past the oaths pawned against futures. Past the vials. Past Corvane's elegant, terrible accounting, in which everything a person was could be weighed, priced, and transferred—and in which, she noticed with a dull ache, there was still no entry anywhere for what Corvane himself had paid when he took the blade meant for her. Some debts, apparently, were kept off the books even by the guildmaster who invented the books.

Then she reached the page.

She had seen it before, of course. Three days ago, in the guildhall, in another life: an entry in a hand she knew better than her own, because she had spent her childhood forging it. Her mother's hand. Dated three days forward—an impossibility then, a horror now, because three days had passed and the date had arrived and the ink had not fled or faded. It had simply waited, the way debts wait.

Now she read it slowly, aloud, because some things needed the indignity of air:

*"Isandro Dunmore, taster, keeper of this ledger, daughter of Maerin Dunmore, keeper before her. Debt outstanding: one life, borrowed at birth against the breaking of the Oath. Payment due: tomorrow, at moonrise, beneath the palace. Paid forward by the mother, accepted by the daughter. Entered in the hand of M. Dunmore, who writes this knowing her daughter will read it, and asks her forgiveness, and knows also that she will not need it."*

Isandro read it twice more. Then a third time, hunting for a loophole the way she'd once hunted for arsenic in a consignment of figs—methodically, hopefully, professionally.

There was no loophole. Her mother had been many things—a smuggler, a traitor by the Crown's reckoning, a woman who had carried a fae child out of a burning treaty hall—but she had never been sloppy with an entry.

"She wrote my death notice," Isandro said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Before I could walk. She wrote it in *my own ledger*, in the book I'd keep, in the hand I'd copy. She signed it like a receipt."

Serevan said nothing for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was very gentle. "Do you know what we called such entries, before the exile? In the old tongue there isn't a word for a death notice written in advance. There's only a word for a gift given early."

"That's a generous translation."

"I'm a generous translator. It's one of the few professions left to me." He turned to face her fully, and his eyes held that quality she had begun to trust more than any oath: they changed when he lied, and they weren't changing now. "Your mother caged herself into that stone's terms so that a child could walk out of the treaty hall alive. The debt was never a punishment, Isandro. It was a price, and prices are how love keeps its promises when words aren't enough."

"And tomorrow the price comes due."

"At moonrise. Beneath the palace. Which is, I grant you, a theatrical choice of venue. Your people never could resist a dramatic address."

She almost smiled. Then she looked down at the entry again—at the last line, the one asking forgiveness—and something in her chest went quiet and wide, like a held breath finally released.

Because here was the thing she had understood sometime in the night, lying awake under Serevan's roof of reeds, listening to the marsh breathe: she wasn't angry. She had expected to be angry. She had spent three days bracing for rage the way she braced for poison, cataloguing its symptoms, preparing the antidote of indignation. But the rage hadn't come. What had come instead was recognition.

Her whole life, Isandro had believed she was invisible by skill. That the poisons she swallowed daily, the small calibrated deaths, had made her a ghost—unseen, unrecorded, safe. But the ledger said otherwise. The ledger said she had been *seen* from the beginning. Seen so completely that her mother had known, thirty years ago, exactly which daughter would grow up to keep impossible accounts and break impossible seals. Seen so clearly that her death had been entered in advance—not as a sentence, but as a signature on a contract her mother had made on her behalf, trusting her to honor it.

Nobody had ever trusted Isandro Dunmore with anything except other people's food.

"They'll all be there," she said. "Vess. Corvane. The queen, if she keeps her word about the hour. Every person who wants the stone wants it tomorrow, because tomorrow is when it can be taken—or broken. My mother didn't choose the date. The date chose itself. She just... wrote it down first."

"Does that frighten you?"

She considered the question honestly, the way she considered everything, tasting it before swallowing.

"Yes," she said. "But it's a clean fear. Do you know what I mean? Court fear is dirty—it's fear of being caught, being wrong, being *noticed*. This is different. This is the fear you feel standing at a door you've decided to walk through." She closed the ledger gently, as one closes a letter from the dead. "I spent my whole life making myself small enough to survive. Tomorrow I have to be exactly the right size. It's oddly restful."

* * *

They spent the day the way soldiers eat before battle: carefully, and well.

Serevan cooked, because of course he did; three centuries of guarding a road will teach a man to cook or drive him mad, and he had chosen cookery with the same stubbornness he chose everything. He made a marsh-fish stew with wild garlic and the last of the sorrel, and Isandro ate two bowls and pronounced it better than anything she'd tasted at the royal table, which was true, and which she said loudly enough that a passing heron seemed offended.

"You're stalling," she accused him, over the third cup of tea.

"I am *savoring*. There's a difference. Stalling is fear wearing patience's coat. Savoring is knowing the meal ends and eating it anyway." He refilled her cup. "Besides, you're the one who taught me the distinction. 'If you don't know what's in it, it owns you.' I've decided it applies to afternoons as well as broth."

She laughed, and then, because the afternoon was ending and honesty was cheaper now than it had ever been, she said: "When I go back—if I come back—I want to open a kitchen. Something small. A real one. Where nobody trades anything, and nobody tastes anything for anybody else, and the ledger on the counter is just... what people owe each other, which is nothing, which is everything."

Serevan went very still.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing." But his eyes had gone bright and strange, the way they did around prophecies. "Only that I have waited three hundred years for the daughter of the ledger-keeper, and no one thought to mention she'd be a cook. The fae would have surrendered decades ago if they'd known the terms would improve this much."

* * *

Moonrise was four hours away and the palace was a day's ride. She would leave within the hour, sewn map against her heart, the queen's secret seal warm in her pocket, Serevan walking the outer road with her as far as the ford. But first there was one more thing, and she made herself do it sitting down, on the flat stone, with the last light going amber over the water.

She took out the scrap of paper she'd been carrying since the guildhall—the single line she'd cut from a burned page, salvaged from Vess's fire by a clerk who owed Corvane a favor and Isandro nothing at all. It was in her mother's hand. It was not part of any entry. It was, as far as Isandro could tell, simply a note her mother had written to herself, thirty years ago, in the margin of a page about the forging of the Oath:

*If she reads this: the debt is not a cage. A cage is something done to you. A debt is something you carry because you agreed, and agreeing is the freest act there is. I chose this for both of us. Forgive me the choosing. Keep the choosing.*

Isandro read it until the letters blurred, then folded it away.

"Some debts are paid forward," she said quietly, to the marsh, to her mother, to whoever kept the accounts of such things. "Not escaped. Fine. I'm paying."

She rose, brushed the reed-fluff from her coat, and checked her kit with a taster's thoroughness: knife, flint, the vial of antidotes, the seal, the map. Everything accounted for. Everything owed.

At the edge of the causeway she paused and looked back once at the hut, the stone, the steam still rising from the abandoned cups—and felt, for the first time in her life, not that she was leaving safety behind, but that she was carrying it with her.

"Come along, then," she said to the road ahead. "We're expected."

Far off across the darkening water, beneath the hills where the palace slept above its buried stone, the first moon cleared the ridgeline—climbing, patient as a creditor, toward tomorrow.
