## 17. Return to the Palace

The road into Vellamere smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, and Isandro Dunmore walked it in daylight, which was, by any honest accounting, the strangest thing she had done in her life.

She had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being overlooked. She knew precisely how to stand so that guards' eyes slid off her like rain off oiled leather. She knew how to walk at the pace of a servant with somewhere dull to be. She had once stood three feet from Lady Vess herself while the spymaster interrogated a kitchen boy, and Vess had looked directly through her twice.

Now she walked down the King's Road with her chin up and her coat unbuttoned, letting everyone see her face, and it felt like walking naked through a market square.

*They'll have your description by now,* she reminded herself. *Vess declared you traitor on every gate from here to the coast. The guild has branded you oathbreaker. Corvane—*

She stopped that thought before it could finish, because thinking about Corvane made her walk slower, and slow walkers got noticed.

The map was sewn into the lining of her coat, flat against her ribs, where it had ridden for six days like a second heartbeat. The queen's seal—the one Maren had pressed into her palm in the dark of the royal kitchens, back when Isandro had still been merely suspicious rather than openly hunted—was tucked inside her left boot. And beneath all of it, in her blood, in the marrow the taster's trade had never managed to explain, was the truth she'd carried since the Stair of Teeth: *the door opens to fae blood. My blood.*

Three centuries of peace, buried under the palace floor. And a council feeding its queen poison by the thimbleful.

"Name and business," said the guard at the Salt Gate, and Isandro realized with a small, hysterical jolt that he wasn't looking for her at all. He was looking at her papers, bored, already half-turned toward his companion's joke.

"I'm Isandro Dunmore," she said clearly. "Royal food-taster. I'm expected."

He looked up then. He looked at her properly, the way no one had looked at her in years, and she watched the recognition crawl across his face—the wanted notices were pinned to the guardhouse wall not ten feet behind him—and she watched something else happen too, something she hadn't planned for: hesitation. Not fear. Hesitation. As if some part of him had heard the name *Dunmore* and associated it with the only person in the palace who had ever bothered to learn the kitchen staff's names, who had once quietly warned a scullery maid that the shellfish delivery was off, who tasted first and asked questions never.

"You're expected," he repeated.

"By Her Majesty," Isandro said. "Ask the under-steward if you doubt me. Ask anyone in the kitchens." She let a beat pass. "Or you can arrest me, and explain to the Queen's Own why you delayed her taster on the day she requested me."

It was a bluff stacked on a bluff stacked on a rumor, and it worked, because most power was. The guard waved her through with the expression of a man deciding that whatever happened next would be someone else's problem.

Isandro walked into the capital that had taught her invisibility, and made herself unmistakable, one deliberate step at a time.

* * *

The palace kitchens received her the way a tide receives a stone: completely, without comment.

Marta the pastry cook saw her come in the scullery door, went white, went pinker, and then simply pointed her toward the corner where the tasting spoons lived, as though Isandro had been away a fortnight rather than hunted across half a kingdom. Nobody shouted. Nobody ran for the watch. In the kitchens, Isandro understood suddenly, she had never been invisible at all—she had been *known*, quietly and thoroughly, the way you know a fixture that keeps the room working.

"You're supposed to be dead," Marta whispered, pressing a heel of bread into her hand as she passed. "Or worse. Vess's people came through asking after you."

"I'm neither," Isandro said. "I'm worse. I'm right."

That earned her a strangled laugh from two of the turnspit boys, quickly smothered.

"Is she well?" Isandro asked, and didn't have to say who. "The Queen."

Marta's face did something complicated. "She eats less every week. Says nothing tastes of anything anymore. Lord Cassel's man brings her a special tonic now, morning and night. From the council's own apothecary." A pause, heavy as a dropped pot. "Since when does a queen need a tonic to eat her supper?"

Since always, Isandro thought, if the council wants her pliable. She had catalogued the toxins in that broth weeks ago—foxglove in fractions, valerian, a slow iron-chelate that starved the blood of vigor by degrees so gentle no physician would ever call it poisoning. She had swallowed her portion and said nothing, because saying nothing was the whole architecture of her life.

Well. That architecture was coming down today, beam by beam, and she intended to swing the hammer herself.

"Where is she?"

"The rose gallery, after midday bell. Alone—she sends her ladies out. Says the roses are the only things that don't watch her." Marta gripped Isandro's sleeve. "Dunmore. Whatever you're about to do—"

"Something reckless," Isandro said. "But not stupid. There's a difference. It's a narrow difference," she admitted, "but it exists."

* * *

The rose gallery smelled of late summer and cut stems, and Queen Maren sat among her flowers like a woman attending her own funeral—spine straight, hands folded, eyes fixed on nothing.

She heard Isandro's footsteps and did not turn.

"They told me you were a traitor," the queen said. "Lady Vess came herself, very grave, very sorry. She said you'd stolen guild property and fled the city." Now she turned, and Isandro's chest tightened at what the weeks had done to her—the hollows under the cheekbones, the faint tremor in the hands folded over the rose-printed ledger of her lap. Poisoned women aged in fast-forward. "I told her I found that difficult to believe. She asked me why. I said because you're the only person in this palace who has never lied to me about anything, including the food."

"Your Majesty—"

"Sit down, Isandro." It was not quite an order. It was the voice of a woman who had learned to make requests sound like courtesies so that no one would notice she was never permitted to give orders at all. "You've come to tell me something. I can see it in how you're standing. You stand differently when you're about to be brave. You did it the day you told me the pheasant was off."

Isandro sat. Then, because there was no gentle way to hold a blade, she held it plainly:

"You're being poisoned, Majesty. Slowly. By your own council."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere below, a fountain ticked; a bee blundered against glass and buzzed off again.

"I know," Queen Maren said.

Isandro blinked. Of all the responses she had rehearsed against, this was not one.

"Not the specifics," the queen continued, in the tone of a scholar correcting a student gently. "Not the names, though I have my suspicions, and I see yours match mine. But a woman knows her own body, taster. I have felt myself dimming for two years, like a lamp turned down a hair at a time. I told myself it was age. Then I told myself it was grief. Then I stopped telling myself stories." Her mouth curved, dry as old paper. "You want to know the worst of it? Some mornings I was grateful. When the fog thickens enough, you stop feeling afraid, and I have been afraid for a very long time."

"That's the valerian," Isandro said quietly. "And the foxglove keeps your heart too tired to argue. It's elegant work, Majesty. Whoever compounds it has real talent."

"Lord Cassel's apothecary trained in Corvei. So did Cassel." The queen looked at her roses. "Why do you think I sent you that seal, Isandro? Why do you think I let you taste my food for eleven years and never once asked what you were doing with your evenings? A caged woman learns to keep birds. Even little brown ones. Especially little brown ones." She turned those faded, fierce eyes on Isandro fully. "Tell me the rest. All of it. I have an hour before my ladies return, and I find I am done being managed."

So Isandro told her. Not everything—she kept the map's location and her own blood out of it, some debts being paid in private—but she told her about the ledgers, about the stranger dying at the Salt Gate with the word *Oathkeeper* on his lips, about the stair beneath the palace and the door that answered to fae blood. She watched the queen absorb each piece the way she herself absorbed poison: in measured doses, without flinching.

When she finished, Maren was quiet a long moment.

"Three centuries," she said at last. "My ancestors sealed a people underground and called it peace, and my council drugs me so I won't ask questions, and the spymaster burns books to keep the questions unaskable." She laughed—a short, genuine, terrible sound. "And they call *you* the traitor."

"Majesty. What I intend to do next will make the word true beyond their wildest hopes."

"Good," said the Queen of Vellamere. "I have waited thirty years for someone to be honestly guilty of something in this palace." She rose, and though her hand trembled, her voice did not. "One hour. The cellar stair is watched by Vess's people since your escape—I can't clear them without signaling her. But the under-steward owes me a debt older than her loyalty, and the wine cellars flood on schedule when I say the word. You'll have your hour of darkness and confusion, Isandro Dunmore, and I will sit in this gallery and be visibly, verifiably drugged and harmless while you do whatever it is you're going to do down there."

Isandro knelt—not as a subject, but because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

"Why?" she asked. "You don't know what I mean to do. I might shatter three hundred years of peace. I might start a war."

Queen Maren looked down at her, and for a moment the fog lifted entirely, and Isandro saw the woman the poison had been fighting all these years: sharp, lonely, furious, kind.

"Because someone has to open a door in this palace eventually," the queen said, "and every soul here is too busy guarding them. Go. The bell rings in twenty minutes. And Isandro—" a ghost of the dry smile, "—do try not to die. You're the only honest company I have."

* * *

The under-steward's flood came exactly on time.

Isandro heard it before she saw it—a groan in the pipes, a rush of water finding old channels—and then the torches along the cellar galleries guttered as panicked guards abandoned their posts to save the vintage stores, shouting about the queen's claret, and the dark beneath the palace belonged briefly to no one.

She moved through it on memory and the map's geometry, past the wine racks and the salt stores, down to where the stones changed underfoot from palace masonry to something older, fitted without mortar, humming faintly against her palm like a sleeping animal. The Stair of Teeth opened ahead of her, and at its foot the door waited—smooth, seamless, patient as it had been for three hundred years.

She pressed her palm against it, and let her blood remember what it was.

The door opened.

Beyond it, faint as starlight through deep water, something vast and old and *listening* stirred in the dark—and Isandro Dunmore, traitor, oathbreaker, taster of poisons, keeper of impossible accounts, stepped through to meet it, as somewhere far above, the midday bell began to ring.
