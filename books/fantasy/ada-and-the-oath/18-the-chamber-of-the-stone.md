## 18. The Chamber of the Stone

The stair did not end so much as surrender.

One moment Isandro was descending—the last of the teeth-stone steps worn smooth under her boots—and the next the walls simply gave up trying to contain her, peeling back into a cavern so vast that her lantern's light went out like a polite guest and never came back.

She stood at the edge of a darkness that had weight.

"Isandro." Corvane's voice came from behind and above, breathless in a way she had never heard from him. Corvane, who negotiated with death itself and always seemed to be winning. "Wait."

"You're supposed to be watching the stair," she said, not turning around. Her voice echoed strangely, as if the cavern were tasting it.

"I was. Then I decided the stair could watch itself." His footsteps caught up with hers, quick and uneven on the ancient floor. In the faint grey glow that seeped from somewhere ahead—she couldn't say where—his face was pale and young and terrified, and she found she loved him more for the terror than she ever had for all his composure. "Serevan is holding the door. Vess is—"

"Coming. I know. She's been coming my whole life; she simply didn't know it until now."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. You taught me the difference costs extra."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped him, strangled by fear. He reached for her hand and she let him take it, and his fingers were cold, and hers were not, and she thought: *the poison teaches you this too—that your body can be a hearth even when everything else is winter.*

Ahead, the grey glow strengthened. Not lamplight. Not fae-light either, though it had that quality of light that seemed to remember being something else. It pulsed, slow and patient, like a heart that had been beating for three hundred years and saw no reason to hurry now.

The Oathstone.

It sat in the center of the chamber on a plinth of fused glass—sand melted by something that was not fire—and it was smaller than she had imagined. That was the first obscenity of it. All this dread, all these ledgers and lies and lives bent around a single secret, and the stone was no bigger than a loaf of bread. A rough grey thing, unremarkable, the kind of rock a child would pocket from a riverbank and treasure for exactly one afternoon.

Except that it *breathed.*

The glow rose and fell with each pulse, and with each pulse Isandro felt something press gently against the inside of her skull, the way a cat presses its head against a hand it has decided to trust.

"Oh," said Corvane softly. "Oh, it knows you."

"It knew my mother first," Isandro said. "I'm only the second edition."

She let go of his hand and walked toward it alone. Some distances are meant to be crossed solo, like some poisons are meant to be swallowed without flinching. She had spent her whole life learning which was which.

* * *

Ten feet away, the voices began.

At first she thought it was the echo of her own heartbeat played back wrong. Then she thought it was wind through the stair behind them, though there was no wind down here, there had never been wind down here, the air was old and still as a held breath.

Then she heard her own name, spoken in a voice she did not know, and understood.

They were inside the stone. All of them. Every voice that had ever sworn an oath upon it, every promise made in its presence across three centuries, every bargain whispered over it by human and fae alike—they had soaked into it the way ink soaks into paper, and now they were speaking, all at once, all together, a congregation of the bound.

"—swear by root and rain—" said someone, in a language that predated the city above.

"—my firstborn's firstborn, till the ice comes home—"

"—I never meant it, I never meant any of it, please, I was frightened—"

That last voice broke her a little. A woman, or had been. Frightened, bargaining, swearing something she regretted before the words finished leaving her mouth, and the stone had taken her anyway, had kept her, had *held* her, the way a ledger holds a debt long after both debtor and creditor are dust.

"They're alive in there," Corvane breathed beside her. He had come closer despite everything. "Not metaphorically. Actually. Serevan told me, but I thought—" He pressed a hand to his mouth. "I thought it was poetry."

"Serevan doesn't waste words on poetry," Isandro said. "He wastes them on accuracy. It's worse."

And then, beneath the chorus, beneath the layered centuries of vow and plea, another voice rose. One voice, threading through the rest like a silver wire through rope.

A voice singing.

A lullaby. Off-key, unhurried, the kind of song sung by someone who cares nothing for the tune and everything for the listener. Isandro knew it. She had known it her whole life without knowing she knew it—it lived in the same place as her immunity to nightshade and her instinct for arsenic, some inheritance deeper than memory, planted before she could record receiving it.

Her mother's voice.

"Mama," Isandro said, and the word came out of her small and cracked, a child's word, a word she had not permitted herself in thirty years because there had been no one to aim it at.

The singing stopped.

The whole chamber stopped—the pulse of the stone, the murmur of the bound voices, even, it seemed, the slow respiration of the dark itself. Everything hung, listening.

Then, gently, the way one speaks to a bird one does not wish to startle:

*"Hello, little ledger-keeper."*

Isandro's knees nearly went. Corvane caught her elbow, and she gripped him, and stood, because falling would mean leaving, and she was not leaving. Not now. Not when—

"You know me," she whispered.

*"I wrote you,"* said the voice, warm as bread, sad as rain. *"Every entry. I'm sorry about chapter nine. And fourteen. And most of sixteen. Your mother has a heavy hand with foreshadowing."*

"The entry dated three days from now. The one in your handwriting." Isandro's throat was tight. "That was you. That's why the ink knew me. That's why the ledgers kept writing me before I lived me—you've been—"

*"Keeping your accounts current,"* her mother said, and there was apology in it, and love, and underneath both something older and harder: duty. *"Someone had to. The stone takes a keeper's blood to open. It takes a keeper's name to close. I paid the first price so you'd never have to stand where you're standing. The universe, as usual, billed me later."*

"What does it want?" Isandro asked. "What does it keep offering? Because it's offering. I can feel it. It's been offering since the stairs."

And oh, it *was.* Now that she listened for it, the offer was everywhere, honeyed and enormous, poured directly into the marrow of her. *Keep me whole,* the stone murmured, in ten thousand voices at once, in her mother's voice, in her own. *Keep me whole and have everything. Every memory ever sold, returned to its seller. Every oath pawned, redeemed. Your mother, whole and warm and singing, standing in a kitchen with flour on her hands. Your name written nowhere but where you choose. Peace, little taster. Perfect peace. You need only do what keepers have always done: nothing. Stand guard. Swallow the key. Keep the lock locked.*

"It's offering me her," Isandro said aloud. Her voice shook. "It's offering me everyone. Everything back. If I just—keep it."

"Yes," said a new voice, from the mouth of the stair.

Lady Vess stood at the top of the last steps, breathing hard, one hand pressed to her side where—Isandro saw with a strange detachment—a dark stain was spreading. Behind her, filling the doorway, Serevan stood very still, very tall, his marsh-green eyes fixed on the stone with an expression Isandro had never seen on him: grief, ancient and banked, the grief of a man looking at the cage he'd been born outside of.

"She's right," Vess said, and her voice, usually cold as a well, was ragged. "Keep it whole, girl. Keep it whole and take the bargain. Take your mother. Take mine, while you're at it—she's in there too, did you know that? Everyone who swore the Oath's terms. Everyone who *broke* them." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Three hundred years I've wanted to hear her voice again. Three hundred years I've told myself it was worth it. The peace. The quiet. The *order.*" She took a step down. "And then a food-taster with poison in her blood walks into my city carrying a map, and I remember what wanting feels like, and I hate her for it."

"Vess," Corvane said carefully, "you're bleeding."

"I'm aware."

"You should sit down."

"I should have sat down thirty years ago and I didn't, and here we all are." Another step. Her eyes never left the stone. "Don't break it, Isandro. Whatever Serevan told you out there in the marshes, whatever pretty speech he gave you about freedom—freedom is a word people use when they haven't watched a war. I have. I helped end one. The stone is the ending. Break it and you don't free the fae. You free *everything.* Every grudge. Every debt. Every voice in that stone that swore vengeance along with its vows." Her hand left her wound, red-palmed, and reached toward the glow. "Keep it whole. Take the bargain. It's the only mercy in this room."

*Take it,* the stone sang, in ten thousand tongues. *Take her. Take them all. Only keep me. Only keep me. Only—*

And beneath the chorus, small and clear and off-key, her mother began to sing again.

Isandro closed her eyes.

She was a taster. It was the one thing she had ever truly been, the one craft she owned outright, and every craft has its first principle. Hers was simple: *before you swallow anything, know what it wants from you.*

Poison wants nothing. Poison is honest; it kills you and asks no questions afterward.

Power is different. Power always wants. And the stone—she opened herself to it fully now, the way she opened her tongue to a suspect broth, cataloguing, weighing, naming—wanted *to continue.* That was all. Beneath the honey and the harmony, beneath the gift-wrapped mother and the redeemed debts, the Oathstone wanted one thing with the whole of its ancient patience: to go on existing, binding, keeping, holding. It would pay any price, grant any wish, tell any beautiful lie, so long as the lock stayed locked and the ledger stayed balanced and nothing, *nothing*, ever changed.

It was, she realized, the loneliest thing she had ever tasted. Lonelier than the palace. Lonelier than invisibility. Three hundred years of holding everyone's promises because no one had ever promised anything *to it.*

"I know what you are," Isandro said quietly, to the stone, to the voices, to her mother. "You're not evil. You're just afraid. And I have spent my entire life being useful to things that were afraid, and I am *done.*"

*"Little one,"* her mother said, and the love in it nearly undid her. *"Listen to me. There is a way to keep some of it. The stone will honor a partial bargain if the keeper asks precisely. Ask to keep the peace and release the people. Ask cleverly, ask like a guildsman, and maybe—maybe—no one pays. Maybe the ledger balances. Maybe—"*

"No," Isandro said.

*"Isandro—"*

"No. You taught me arithmetic, Mama, wherever you are, whatever you are—I learned it from your handwriting before I ever learned it from a tutor. Nothing balances. Something always goes in the minus column. For three hundred years the minus column has been *them*—a whole people, caged, so that we could sleep soundly." She opened her eyes. The glow filled them, gold-grey, pulsing. "I'm not asking cleverly. I'm not haggling. I'm a taster, and I'm telling you what's in the dish: everything, and it's poisoned, and I refuse the portion."

Corvane made a small sound beside her—not protest. Agreement. She felt it more than heard it, the way you feel a door open behind you in a dark room.

Vess lunged.

* * *

Later, Isandro would never be able to say who moved first—only that the world became very fast and very simple. Vess coming down the last stretch of floor with a knife she must have carried for thirty years waiting for exactly this. Serevan moving like weather, vast and inevitable, catching the spymaster's arm. Corvane stepping between Isandro and the blade's arc, taking the edge along his forearm because he was Corvane and he would take a cut rather than let one reach her, *of course* he would, he'd been doing it since the guildhall, since the first night he'd looked at her across a ledger and *seen* her.

"Stay behind me," he gritted.

"No," Isandro said. "Stay *beside* me. That's the whole point of you."

And she walked past all of them—past Vess snarling in Serevan's grip, past Corvane clutching his bleeding arm and laughing despite himself, past the stair where torchlight from above was beginning to spill and multiply, the queen's hour burning down—took the Oathstone in both hands, and held it against her heart.

It was warm. Of course it was warm. It was full of voices.

*"Keeper,"* it said, in every voice at once, in hers, in her mother's, in Vess's dead beloved's, pleading now, truly pleading. *"Keeper, please. Keep me whole. Keep me whole and I will give you—"*

"I know what you'll give me," Isandro said. "You already showed me the menu. But here's what *I* give *you,* stone. Here's my oath, freely made, the last one anyone will ever swear on you:"

She drew the small knife from her boot—the taster's knife, the one she'd used to pare fruit for queens and slice cheese in a guildhall kitchen—and opened her palm, and let her blood, the blood that no poison had ever touched because it had never once been merely human, fall onto the stone.

"You're released."

The stone screamed.

Or perhaps it sang—there was no difference, at that volume, at that depth—and the light that burst from it was not gold or grey but every color that had ever been forgotten, and the chamber filled with voices rising, thousands of them, tens of thousands, oaths unraveling thread by thread, promises dissolving back into the free air that had birthed them, and through it all, cutting clear as a bell through the roar, her mother's voice, laughing now, weeping now, saying words Isandro felt rather than heard, words like *brave* and *mine* and *at last*—

The stone grew light in her hands. Lighter. Light as ash. Light as a name being erased.

And Isandro Dunmore, royal food-taster, keeper of impossible ledgers, daughter of no one's records and her own, felt the first line of herself begin, gently, like snow off a roof, to fall away.
