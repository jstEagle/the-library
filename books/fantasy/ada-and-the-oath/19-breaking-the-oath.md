## 19. Breaking the Oath

### Vess

Lady Vess had spent thirty years learning that every door in the palace had a price, and she had paid them all. But the door at the bottom of the Stair of Teeth opened for no coin, no rank, no threat. It had opened for a food-taster with dirt under her fingernails and fae blood in her veins, and Vess would never forgive the universe for that particular joke.

She came down the stair now with six of her best knives and two of her worst regrets.

The torchlight bent strangely here, the way light does in deep water. The walls sweated. Somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something *sang* — a chord made of voices, hundreds of them, thousands, all pressed together like flowers in a book.

*Three hundred years,* Vess thought. *Three hundred years I have kept the terms of a peace I helped break once already.* She was younger then. Everyone was younger then, including the woman whose name she still could not say aloud without her chest doing something inconvenient.

"Stay at the top," she told her agents. "If I don't come up within the hour, burn the stair."

"And if the girl comes up?" asked Ferren, her steadiest hand.

Vess adjusted her glove. "Then arrest her gently. She's earned gentleness. Most people haven't."

She did not say what she intended to do about the stone. Some intentions were too heavy to carry in front of witnesses.

She descended.

* * *

### Isandro

The chamber at the bottom of the world was round, and it was full of light that came from nowhere, and it was full of voices that came from everywhere.

Isandro stood before the Oathstone and understood, finally, why her whole life had felt like reading someone else's book over their shoulder. The stone rose from the floor like a tooth from a jaw — grey, veined with silver, taller than two men — and every inch of it was *occupied*. Voices pressed against its surface the way faces press against a window in winter. She could not make out words. She didn't need to. She knew what a crowd sounded like when it had been waiting a very long time.

"Hello," she said, because her mother had raised her to be polite to everyone, apparently including geological formations.

The singing shifted. It noticed her.

And out of the mass of it, one voice rose — thin, worn smooth by centuries, but shaped exactly like the voice from the stolen vial, the one that had sung a child to sleep on the worst night of both their lives.

"*Isandro.*"

Her knees nearly went. She put a hand on the stone to steady herself, and the stone was warm, blood-warm, and beneath her palm she felt something turn toward her touch like a face toward sunlight.

"I'm here," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle, which was undignified, and she decided not to care. "I'm sorry it took so long. I had to learn to read first. And taste poison. It's been a whole thing."

The voice laughed. It was a small sound, rusted, but it was unmistakably laughter, and Isandro laughed too, wetly, alone in a room at the bottom of the world, laughing with a piece of rock.

Behind her, boots on the stair. Many boots. One set of them she knew by rhythm — Corvane walked like a man who had never once hurried and never once been late.

"They're coming," she told the stone.

"*I know,*" said her mother's voice. "*Whatever you choose, little ledger-keeper — choose it with your eyes open. That is all any of us ever asked.*"

"That's a terrible thing to ask," Isandro said. "Eyes open is how you get hurt."

"*Yes,*" said the voice, gentle as banked coals. "*That is rather the point of living.*"

* * *

### Corvane

Corvane entered the chamber the way he entered everything: last, unhurried, and already knowing more than anyone found comfortable.

He took in the scene with a single sweep — Isandro's hand flat on the stone, her shoulders shaking; the silver veins pulsing brighter than any report had described; and behind him, descending into the torchlight with her gloves buttoned to the wrist, Lady Vess, who looked less like a spymaster than like a woman arriving late to her own funeral.

"You brought knives," Corvane observed mildly.

"You brought *her*," Vess said.

"She brought herself. I've stopped pretending otherwise. It's very freeing. You should try it." He moved to stand beside Isandro — not between her and the stone, which would have been presumptuous, and not between her and Vess, which would have been useless, but beside her, where a person stands when they mean it.

"The guild's position," he said, "is that the stone should be delivered intact to—"

"Corvane." Isandro didn't look at him. Her hand stayed on the stone. "Don't. Not tonight. Don't give me the speech."

He paused. In three hundred years of guild ledgers, there was an entry for almost everything, and somewhere in his chest there was even an entry for this: the moment a man hears his own rehearsed words and realizes they are ash.

"The guild's position," he said again, slower, "was formed without full information. As of tonight, the Guild of Impossible Things takes no position whatsoever." He drew off one glove, finger by finger. "I am simply a man standing next to a woman, and I find I have no interest in being anywhere else."

Vess stared at him. "You'd burn three centuries of the guild for a taster."

"I'd burn considerably more," said Corvane, "for someone who saw me before I was anything worth seeing. You wouldn't understand. Your grief has always pointed backward. Hers points forward. It's a better compass."

Isandro's breath caught. She still didn't look at him, but her fingers curled slightly against the stone, and Corvane counted that as the finest entry in any ledger he had ever kept.

* * *

### Vess

There was a version of events, Vess reflected, drawing her knife, in which she simply waited. Let the girl deliberate. Let the fae sing. Let history take its slow sweet time.

But Vess had waited once before, three hundred years ago, on a night of oaths and fire, and waiting had cost her everything she had ever loved. She had learned the lesson thoroughly: *patience is just grief wearing a sensible coat.*

"No positions," she said. "How convenient for you both. The stone stays whole. That is not negotiable, and I will tell you why, since you've all been so generous with your confessions tonight."

The silver veins pulsed. The singing swelled, uneasy.

"There was a woman," Vess said, and her voice did not waver, because she had practiced this in mirrors until wavering became impossible. "Before the Oath. Before my title. She was bound into that stone with the rest of them — not because she started the war, not because she deserved it, but because the binding didn't check names, only *blood*, and hers ran wrong in the right way. I have served the Crown faithfully for three hundred years on the understanding that someday, somehow, that stone would open, and I would get back the one hour we lost." The knife turned in her grip. "So no, taster. I will not let you shatter it on a theory. I will not let you trade my hour for your philosophy."

"You'd keep ten thousand people caged," Isandro said quietly, "to buy back one."

"Yes," said Vess. "I have never pretended to be good. Only effective."

She attacked.

* * *

### Isandro

Later — in whatever life came after, in whatever kitchen, in whatever unwritten year — Isandro would remember the fight mostly as noise and cold and the strange clarity of terror. Vess fought like ink spreads: everywhere at once, from angles that hadn't existed a moment prior. Corvane met her with a duelist's economy, all deflection and dry commentary ("Left! No, your other left, Lady Vess, honestly—"), buying seconds the way he bought everything, at a fair price and without haggling.

Isandro didn't fight. She turned back to the stone.

"How do I open you?" she demanded. "Tell me. There's a key, isn't there? There's always a key, I balance the books, I know how keys work—"

"*Blood,*" said her mother's voice, and now there were other voices rising under it, agreeing, urgent, a congregation leaning forward. "*Fae blood. It was sealed in blood; it answers to blood. But hear me, daughter — the seal drinks deep. Whatever opens it will be taken as payment. That is the shape of the lock.*"

"Taken how? My memory? My years?"

"*Your name.*"

Somewhere behind her, steel rang and Corvane swore with uncharacteristic feeling. Isandro pressed her forehead to the warm stone.

"My name," she repeated. Everything she was, filed away. Every record. Every ledger line. The guild's books, the palace rolls, the watch lists, the tax rolls, the baptismal registers of a city that had never once noticed her alive. Gone. Not dead — *unwritten*. A person cannot be mourned if no one remembers she existed.

She thought of her mother's handwriting, dated three days ahead, all those weeks ago. *So that's what the entry was.* Not a prophecy. An invoice.

"It's a fair price," she said.

"*It is an outrageous price,*" said her mother.

"I've read enough ledgers to know the difference between fair and outrageous, and it's mostly a matter of who's paying." She laughed, shaky. "Besides. You want to know a secret? I've spent my whole life trying to be invisible. Turns out I was just practicing."

Steel rang closer. Someone cried out — Corvane, she thought, and her heart lurched sideways — and Isandro made her decision the way she did everything: quickly, precisely, and with no one watching.

Except, for once, everyone was watching.

* * *

### Corvane

He saw her draw the little taster's knife — the one she used for paring fruit at the royal table, the most harmless blade in the palace — and drag it across her palm, and he understood everything in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

"No—" he said, and it came out of him raw, unpriced, the first honest sound he had made in a century.

Vess took the opening. Her blade caught him across the ribs, a hot bright line, and he folded around it and went down on one knee and did not care, because Isandro had turned to look at him — at *him*, blood on her hand, light pouring up through the cracks in the world — and she was smiling.

"Corvane," she said. "I love you. I should have said it ages ago, I've had it catalogued for weeks, I just couldn't find the right column for it." She pressed her bleeding palm to the stone. "I refuse your bargain, by the way. You can't have my oldest memory. I'm keeping all of them. I'm spending something better."

"Don't," he said. Blood in his teeth. "Isandro. *Don't.*"

"Some debts," she said, "are paid forward."

The stone drank.

* * *

### Vess

Light took the chamber.

Not fire-light, not torch-light — light like the inside of a struck bell, silver and total, and through it Vess heard the sound she had waited three hundred years to hear: the Oath, breaking. It did not shatter like glass. It *unraveled*, thread by thread, oath by oath, three centuries of binding coming loose all at once, and the voices in the stone rose from a chord into a chorus into a *choir*, and the choir became a crowd, and the crowd became a people.

They poured past her. She felt them go — a wind of presence, of wings-that-were-not-wings, of the vanished becoming merely departed. One of them paused. She felt attention rest on her, old and amused and unbearably kind, and a voice that she knew, that she had loved, that she had buried in a stone instead of a grave, said:

"*You kept the terms. Foolish, faithful woman. Thank you.*"

And then it was gone, upward, outward, home.

Vess stood in the emptying light with her knife arm hanging slack and understood that she had lost everything, and that everything, it turned out, had weighed nothing at all.

On the floor, Corvane bled. At the center of the room, where the stone had been, there was only a girl — kneeling, whole, breathing, her palm healed to a pale seam.

But when Vess tried to say her name — *taster, girl, Isandro* — the name slid off her mind like water off wax. She reached for the memory of the interrogation, the map, the docks, the broth, and found... smooth paper. Blank entries. A shape where a person had been, and nothing written in it.

"What did you do," Vess whispered. "What did you *do*?"

Isandro smiled at her — tired, triumphant, already half-unreal, like a figure seen through closing mist.

"I balanced the books," she said. "Someone had to."

* * *

### Corvane

He held her while the last of the light drained out of the chamber, and she held him back, and both of them were crying, and neither of them was keeping score.

"I can't remember your surname," he said desperately. "Isandro — Isandro *what*? Tell me your surname. Tell me your mother's name. Give me something to write down, I'll write it in my own blood if I have to, I'll start a new ledger, a private one, oath-free—"

"You won't remember the ledger either," she said gently. "Ink that remembers, Corvane. Ink that forgets, too. That was always the deal."

"Then I'll remember without ink."

She cupped his bloody face in her hands. "Then remember *this*," she said, and kissed him, long and thorough and entirely off the books, and when she pulled away she was fading at the edges like breath off glass, smiling, going.

"Find me anyway," she said. "You're the guildmaster of impossible things. Make this one official."

And the chamber was empty, and the stair was dark, and Corvane knelt alone at the bottom of the world holding a memory that no ledger would ever confirm, whispering her name over and over like a man counting coins that kept vanishing — except one, except always one, the one that glowed.

Above him, far above, dawn was coming to a city that would never know what had been freed beneath it.

He climbed.

* * *

Far away, in the border marshes, the rain began — soft, warm, tasting of nothing but itself — and fell on a sleeping woman whom no book in the world contained, while a fae exile stood watch over her and smiled at the sky, because for the first time in three hundred years, the road out of exile was open, and the daughter of the ledger-keeper had paid the toll.
