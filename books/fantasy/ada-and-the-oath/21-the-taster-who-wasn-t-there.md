## 21. The Taster Who Wasn't There

The first thing I tasted was rain.

Not the way a taster tastes—no cataloguing, no ledger of bitterness and alkaloid and the faint almond ghost of cyanide. Just rain, falling on my open mouth, each drop arriving like a small bright stranger at a door I had finally unlocked.

I opened my eyes to a sky the color of pewter and reeds bending over me like curious old men.

"You drool when you sleep," said Serevan, from somewhere above me. "Three centuries I waited for the daughter of the ledger-keeper, and she drools."

My voice came out as a croak. "You waited three centuries for that observation?"

"I waited three centuries for many things. Patience gives one time to collect observations." He leaned into my field of view—a long face, silver-grey eyes with pupils like slivers of moon, hair the color of river fog. He looked exactly as he had in the chamber beneath the palace, which is to say he looked exactly as he had three hundred years ago, and there was something both comforting and deeply unfair about that. "How do you feel?"

I took inventory, because inventory was what I knew. Limbs: present. Bones: aching as though someone had used me as a ledger and written across every page. Throat: raw. Heart: beating, which surprised me more than anything else on the list.

"Like a broth that was left too long over the fire," I said. "But not poisoned. For the first time in eleven years, nothing in me is poisoned."

"Small mercies."

"Large ones," I corrected, and then, because it needed saying aloud to be believed: "The stone broke. I broke it. And I'm—" I pressed a hand flat against my own chest, feeling the ridiculous stubborn drumming under my ribs. "I should be dead. The ledger said so. My mother's handwriting said so. Dated tomorrow, it said, and tomorrow was yesterday."

Serevan sat down beside me in the mud without any apparent concern for his robes, which told me how far the fae had already come from their exile. Three centuries of standing at attention, and now he sat in muck like a heron that had given up on dignity.

"The ledger was written in oath-ink," he said. "Everything written in oath-ink was bound to the stone. When you shattered it—" he made a small gesture, fingers opening like a bird released, "—every entry unbound itself. Every debt cancelled. Every name erased."

"Including mine."

"Including yours." He said it gently, but gently did not soften it. "Isandro Dunmore does not exist. Not in the guild ledgers, not in the Crown's rolls, not in the parish books or the tasters' registry or the memory-vials Corvane kept locked beneath the guildhall. You were written so thoroughly, child, that unwriting you took all of you with it. Every record. Every trace."

I lay back in the reeds and considered this.

Here is what I had expected to feel, lying in a marsh with no name: terror. Invisibility had been my craft for eleven years—I had made myself a shadow at the royal table, a footnote in the palace accounts, a woman whose whole genius was being forgettable. But that invisibility had always been a performance held together by other people's records. Remove the records, and I had assumed there would be nothing underneath. A taster who isn't in the registry isn't a taster. A guild-keeper whose entries are ash isn't a keeper. Strip the ink away and what remains?

What remained was rain on my face and an ache in every bone and a fae watching me with the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech for three hundred years and forgotten all of it.

"What remains," I said slowly, "is me."

"That," said Serevan, "was rather the point."

* * *

He carried me—not carried, exactly; he walked and I leaned on him and pretended otherwise—to a hut built of woven reeds and driftwood at the edge of the marsh, where the water went silver at dusk and the air smelled of peat and wild mint. Inside there was a hearth, a kettle, a bed of dried rushes under a wool blanket that must have been traded from some human village, because no fae had woven anything so lumpy and beloved-looking.

"There's soup," Serevan said. "I made it myself. I should warn you that I have been guarding a road for three hundred years and my culinary education is limited to what travelers dropped."

I tasted it the old way first—out of habit, the spoon hovering, the tongue cataloguing. Leek. Salt. Something rootlike and bitter that might have been ambition. No nightshade, no arsenic, no powdered grief.

Then I put down the taster entirely and simply ate.

It was, objectively, terrible soup. It was also the best thing I had ever eaten, and I started laughing somewhere around the third spoonful, and could not stop, and Serevan watched me laugh with the wary expression of a man wondering whether he has nursed a madwoman back to health.

"Forgive me," I managed. "It's just—I spent eleven years tasting for a queen. Eleven years of the finest food in the kingdom, and I never once *tasted* any of it. It was all chemistry. Almond means cyanide, bitter means hemlock, sweet might mean anything. And now your awful soup tastes like—" I gestured helplessly. "Like being alive. Everything does. The rain did. The air does. Is this what it's like? Has it always been like this for people?"

"For most people," Serevan said, "it is like this, and they complain about the weather."

"I intend to complain about the weather," I said solemnly. "I've earned it."

* * *

The days that followed taught me the shape of my new life, which was mostly the shape of waiting for my body to forgive me.

The destruction of the Oathstone had spent something out of me beyond blood. My hands shook for three days. My fae blood—the inheritance I had never known I carried, the reason poisons slid off me like rain off wax—had burned through me like a fever breaking, and what was left was thinner, quieter, human in its exhaustion. Serevan brewed teas from marsh plants whose names he refused to translate ("If I tell you it's called widow's-lament you'll taste the name instead of the leaf, and names ruin everything"), and I drank them and slept and woke and slept again.

On the fourth day I walked to the edge of the water and stood there while the rain came down, and I let it fall on my open palms, and I tasted it properly, drop by drop, the way I had once catalogued toxins. This water had fallen from clouds that had crossed the mountains. It carried iron from the peaks and salt blown up from the distant sea and the green breath of the marsh itself. It was the finest vintage I had ever tasted, and no one had paid for it, and no one had risked anything to bring it to my lips, and I stood in the rain like a fool and wept, and the rain didn't mind.

That was the day I understood what had actually happened to me.

Everyone had assumed—and I had assumed—that the stone would take my life as payment. Vess had bet her grief on it. Corvane had feared it. Even I, walking back into the palace with my mother's death notice folded against my heart, had made my peace with dying. But the ledger had never asked for my life. It had asked for my *name*—the recorded thing, the written self, the sum of every entry ever made about me. And when I shattered the stone, the debt came due in the only currency oath-ink recognized.

So Isandro Dunmore was gone. Dead on paper, which in the world of ledgers is the only death that counts. The guild's books no longer held her. The Crown's registry no longer held her. Somewhere in the capital, Lady Vess sat in a cell of her own surrendering, and if she spoke my name aloud, the name would mean nothing even to her—a word with no referent, a key to a door that had never existed.

And yet here I was, drinking terrible tea in a marsh, hungry, hurting, *here*.

"They think I'm dead," I said to Serevan that evening. We were sitting outside while the light went gold and then violet over the water. "Vess, the queen, Corvane—they'll have found the ledgers blank where I was. They'll hold a funeral, probably. A small one. I was never important enough for a large one." I smiled at the thought, and the smile surprised me by being genuine. "Do you know, I think I'd attend. I'd bring flowers. I'd stand at the back, invisible as ever, and I'd be the only mourner who knows how it ends."

"You find this funny."

"I find this *free*." I turned my cup in my hands. "All my life, other people's books decided what I was. The guild wrote me as an asset. The Crown wrote me as an instrument. My own mother—" my voice caught, and I let it catch; I was done swallowing things, "—my mother wrote my death before I'd lived my life, because that was the price she had to pay, and she paid it forward the only way she could. Everyone who ever loved me loved me through ink. And now the ink is gone, and what's left is—" I spread my hands. "This. A woman in a marsh who likes rain and hates leeks."

"You liked the leek soup."

"I was being polite. Three hundred years of isolation, Serevan, you've forgotten how politeness works."

Something moved through his face then—something old and careful. "And what will you call yourself?" he asked. "If not Isandro Dunmore. A person needs a name. Not for ledgers. For introductions. For friends."

I looked out at the water, where the last light lay like spilled honey, and I thought about names. My mother's name had been written in a ledger dated three days past its own writing. The queen's name was a seal. Vess's name was a weapon she had turned on herself at last. Names, in my experience, were things other people gave you and then used to find you.

"I'll choose one," I said. "When I know who I am without the poison and the ledgers and the fear. A name should be earned, don't you think? Like a title. Or a scar."

Serevan nodded slowly. "The fae believe a true name is a door. That's why we guard ours. Perhaps you're the first human to understand that instinct from the inside."

"Perhaps I'm the first human to lose everything written about her and survive it." I bumped his shoulder with mine, carefully, because he was ancient and dignified and I enjoyed scandalizing him. "Someone should write a book about it."

"No one could," he said dryly. "There's no record of you."

* * *

Weeks passed. My strength returned the way tide returns—imperceptibly, then all at once. I learned the marsh: which reeds hid good eels, which pools were safe, which paths flooded at moonrise. I learned Serevan's stories, the long slow history of the fae before the Oath, and I noticed that he told them differently now—looser, sadder, with the ending changed. *And then we were freed,* the stories ended now. *A girl with ledger-keeper's hands broke the lock.*

Travelers began to pass along the outer road. With the fae unbound, the borderlands had changed; the old fear was gone, replaced by something more tentative and more interesting—curiosity. Human traders came through the marsh road again, and sometimes fae walked beside them, and both kinds stopped at our hut because Serevan would not turn anyone away and because I had started cooking.

I cooked the way I tasted now—generously, experimentally, with occasional disasters. I made a fish stew that made a fae woman cry, though whether from joy or the amount of pepper remained disputed. I baked bread that humans called almost edible and fae called a miracle, which tells you everything about three centuries of exile rations. And every traveler who ate at our fire gave me something—not coin, though some insisted—but a story, a song, a seed, a button, a name freely offered and freely taken.

I kept a book. A plain one, bought from a peddler, bound in undyed leather. Not a ledger—ledgers record debts, and I was finished with debts. This book recorded gifts. *Marta of the Ford, two onions and a song about drowned sailors.* *Fen-of-the-Reeds, a carved wooden heron, in thanks for the stew.* *An unnamed child, a dandelion, crushed, but given with great sincerity.*

No one appeared in it who hadn't chosen to. No one was written who hadn't agreed to be written. It was, I thought, the first honest book I had ever kept.

Some nights I thought about them—the ones left behind in the world of ink. The queen, who had granted me one hour beneath the palace and deserved to know how it was spent. Vess, in her cell, learning at last to grieve without bargaining. And Corvane.

Corvane, who had taken a blade meant for me. Who had heard me say *I love you* and answered with his blood instead of words, because words were his trade and he knew better than anyone what they cost. Who had offered me protection for the price of my oldest memory, and whom I had refused, and who had chosen me anyway—over the guild, over the ledgers, over three hundred years of careful neutrality.

Did he look for me? I wondered, on nights when the fire burned low. Did he search the surviving ledgers, page after page, knowing what he would find—or rather, what he wouldn't? Did he understand that the absence of my name was not the absence of me?

I hoped he did. I hoped it with an ache that lived just under my ribs, next to the ache of healing, and I did not try to cure it. Some hungers, I had learned, were not meant to be fed. They were meant to be followed.

One evening in late autumn, Serevan came in from checking the road markers with an odd expression—odd for him, meaning his eyebrows had moved.

"A traveler," he said. "Human. Alone. On foot, which is strange this late in the season. He's asking—" and here his eyebrows climbed further, "—he's asking after a kitchen. He says he was told, and I quote, 'that a woman at the edge of the marsh road feeds everyone, keeps a book of names freely given, and makes bread that is nearly edible.' He seems very certain the bread detail is accurate."

My heart did something complicated.

"He described you," Serevan continued, watching me with those moon-slit eyes. "Dark hair going grey early. A scar on the left palm from a vial broken in childhood. Eyes that look at people the way you used to look at soup—as though deciding whether they're safe to swallow." He paused. "He did not give a name. He said—" and something in Serevan's voice gentled, the way it did when he spoke of long waitings, "—he said, 'She'll know me. Tell her I brought nothing to trade. Tell her I'm done trading.'"

I set down the wooden heron I had been carving—it was terrible; I was teaching myself patience, not skill—and I stood, and my hands were shaking, and it wasn't the stone's fault this time.

Through the open door, down the path where the reeds bent gold in the evening light, a figure was walking toward us through the marsh. Unhurried. Certain. Carrying nothing but himself, and following nothing but the one map no ledger had ever held.

I wiped my hands on my apron. I checked the bread. Then I stepped out onto the path to meet him.
