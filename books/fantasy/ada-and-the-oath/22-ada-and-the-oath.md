## 22. Ada and the Oath

The kitchen smelled of bread and rain.

Ada had learned, over seven years, that this was the correct way for a kitchen to smell — bread first, because bread was a promise you could keep, and rain second, because rain was a promise the world kept whether you liked it or not. She stood at her counter with flour to her elbows and a wooden spoon in one hand, tasting the stew she'd set to simmer at dawn.

Nettle. Wild garlic. A whisper of bog-myrtle that Serevan had sworn was "merely seasoning" and was, in fact, a mild soporific useful for calming travelers who arrived at her door with swords still drawn. She'd diluted it. Mostly.

"Too much myrtle," she said to the empty room.

The room did not answer, but the kettle on the iron stove gave a small approving whistle, which was as close to conversation as her mornings usually got before the road woke up.

She had built the kitchen herself, in the way that a person builds something when they have nothing left to lose and everything left to choose: badly at first, then stubbornly, then well. It sat at the edge of the fae road where the marsh gave way to meadow, close enough to the old border stones that on certain evenings the light came through the windows slightly wrong — gold where it should have been gray, warm where the season said it shouldn't be. Travelers of both kinds found their way here. Humans came for the stew and stayed for the safety. The fae came for neither, exactly. They came because word had passed along the road, in the way words pass among people who live three hundred years and therefore never need to hurry: *there is a kitchen where no one asks what you are, and the keeper does not write anything down.*

That last part was the important part. That last part was the whole point.

Ada wiped her hands and took down the book.

It lived on a high shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was the only book in the world she allowed herself to keep. Not a ledger. She had been very precise with herself about that distinction, in the early years, when the distinction was all that stood between her and screaming. A ledger recorded what was owed. This book recorded only what was given.

She opened it to a fresh page and wrote, in a hand that had grown rounder and less careful over the years:

*Brannoc, ferryman's son, paid for his supper with a song about a drowned city. The song was terrible. I gave him extra bread anyway.*

*Two sisters of the reed-folk, names given freely: Ilsabet and Mourn. They asked me to remember them "in case the river forgets." Rivers forget everything. That is their whole character.*

*A human child, Tamsin, age six, who traded me three smooth stones for a honey bun and then tried to buy the stones back. I let her. Fair is fair.*

She blew on the ink — ordinary ink, bought from a peddler, made from oak galls and vinegar and nothing else, ink that remembered nothing and rewrote nothing and would stay exactly where she put it until the paper crumbled — and closed the book, and felt the small daily satisfaction of a woman whose accounts balanced without costing anyone anything.

Outside, the marsh frogs were tuning up for evening. Somewhere down the road, hooves.

* * *

Corvane had been finding her for seven years, and every time he arrived it was by a different impossible route, and every time she asked how he'd done it he gave a different evasive answer, and she had long ago stopped believing any of them.

The first year, he'd claimed he followed rumors of an excellent cook. The second, that a migratory bird owed him a favor. The third year he'd simply appeared at her table mid-meal, muddy to the knees, holding a bouquet of marsh flowers that were technically illegal to pick under two separate treaties, and when she'd demanded the truth he'd looked at her across the candlelight with those gray eyes that had once read her like a ledger and said:

"I don't follow roads, Ada. I follow the feeling of you."

Which was, she had decided, either the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to her or the most unsettling, and after considerable deliberation she had concluded it was both, and married him anyway.

Well. Not married, precisely. The fae road had opinions about marriage — it considered the institution charming but redundant, like a fence around a garden both parties already tended — so they had exchanged oaths instead, spoken ones, unwritten ones, oaths that existed nowhere in ink and therefore could never be broken by ink. *I will find you,* he had sworn. *I will always be finding you.* And she had sworn back the only oath that had ever come easily to her tongue: *I will leave the lamp lit.*

Seven years of lamps. Seven years of hoofbeats resolving into his gray mare, into his coat smelling of road dust and guildhall cedar, into the particular way he paused in her doorway as if asking permission to exist in a place where someone was glad of him.

Tonight the hoofbeats came early. Ada set out a second bowl without looking up from her stew.

He came through the door with marsh mud to the thigh and a cut on his cheekbone that he would claim was nothing and she would treat anyway, and he stopped in the doorway, and did the thing he always did — the pause, the breath, the quiet astonishment of a man who had spent centuries trading in memories learning that the best ones couldn't be bottled.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm memorizing," he said. "There's a difference."

"There is not, and sit down before the myrtle wears off and your legs figure out how far you've ridden."

He sat. He ate. He told her about the road — a caravan of stone-fae heading south for the winter gathering, a dispute between two hedge-witches over a stolen recipe that had escalated into actual theft of each other's shadows, a rumor that the queen in the capital had commissioned a new kind of record-keeping, something called a *book of gratitude*, kept voluntarily, updated by choice, its entries deletable at will. Corvane reported this last item with the expression of a man watching history perform a very slow, very polite apology.

"They're calling it reform," he said. "Vess calls it anarchy. Vess is, incidentally, writing the introduction."

Ada nearly dropped her spoon. "Lady Vess? Writing the foreword to a *voluntary ledger*?"

"People change." He shrugged. "Slowly. With enormous resistance and a great deal of dignity. Like glaciers. Or cats."

"What changed her?"

"You did." He said it simply, the way he said true things — no flourish, no price attached. "You showed her a woman could erase herself from every record in the world and still exist. Still cook. Still be *found*. She's spent seven years trying to work out how that's possible, and somewhere in the working-out, she became almost a person."

Ada thought about that. About Vess, cold and precise, hunting her across half a kingdom for a map; about Vess at the ford, offering grief for grief like a merchant weighing coins; about the letter that had arrived two winters ago, unsigned, containing only a pressed sprig of palace rosemary and a single line in a spymaster's hand: *Some debts are paid forward. I am trying to learn how.* She had planted the rosemary. It was doing very well by the south window.

"The world is strange," Ada said.

"The world is recovering," Corvane corrected. "There's a difference. Staring again."

"Eating again. There's a difference."

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and the lamp was trimmed and the marsh had gone soft and silver with mist, they sat on the kitchen step with their shoulders touching, which was how they did most things — separately and together, like two trees that had grown leaning.

"Serevan came by today," Ada said.

"Mm. How is our ancient gatekeeper?"

"Insufferable. He brought fish. He inspected my herb garden and pronounced it 'adequate for a mortal,' which from Serevan is practically a knighthood. He asked about you."

"And you told him?"

"That you'd be here by moonrise. He waited. We drank nettle wine and he told me the story again."

Corvane smiled into the dark. "The founding of the kitchen?"

"The whole of it. He never gets it right, though. In his version I'm braver than I was."

"In mine too."

"In yours I'm *exactly* as brave as I was, which is worse flattery."

He laughed — that low, unguarded laugh she had spent years earning, one coin at a time, back when everything between them had cost something and she had refused, in the end, to pay any of it. He reached over and took her hand, flour and all, and turned it palm-up, and traced the old scar there with one finger. The scar from the taster's blade. The first cut she'd ever given herself, at fourteen, alone in a palace washroom, teaching her body to survive what her job required.

"It doesn't hurt anymore," she said. "In case you were going to ask."

"I wasn't going to ask." He pressed his lips to the scar. "I was going to say thank you to it. For keeping you alive long enough to get here."

They sat with that. The mist moved through the meadow grass. Down the fae road, far off, lights were moving — travelers, both kinds, walking side by side now as if it had always been so, as if three centuries of exile had been only a long night and everyone had finally agreed to stop pretending the morning hadn't come.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The ledgers. The guild. Being the man who knew everything because he'd written it all down."

Corvane considered this with the seriousness he gave all her questions, which was one of the thousand reasons.

"I miss the certainty," he admitted. "Knowing where everything was. Who owed whom. It's very restful, being a ledger-keeper. Nothing is ever lost; it's only filed." He turned her hand over, closed her fingers around something small. "But I spent four hundred years filing other people's lives, Ada, and I never once wrote down a single thing I actually wanted. Then a woman poisoned herself into invisibility and walked into my guildhall carrying a map that should have ended the world, and she looked at my books and said—" he dropped his voice into a fair imitation of her old court-flat tone, "*'Who told you this was living?'*"

"I don't sound like that."

"You sounded exactly like that. It was devastating. I've never recovered." He nudged her closed fist. "Open it."

Inside her palm lay a ring. Simple iron, unadorned — fae iron, the old kind, the honest kind, metal that could not hold an enchantment and therefore could not lie. On its inner curve, freshly engraved in a hand she knew better than her own, were two words.

Not a name. Names could be lost; she had proven that at great personal cost. These were older than names.

*Chosen. Keeping.*

"For the book," Corvane said quietly. "Not the guild's. Not the queen's. Not even Serevan's. Yours. The one you keep yourself." His thumb brushed her knuckles. "Every story needs a keeper, love. I've just decided to be kept."

Ada looked at the ring for a long moment. Then she got up, went inside, took down the oilcloth-wrapped book, opened it to the final page, and wrote — in ordinary ink, in her own round hand, in an entry no power in heaven or under the hill could rewrite because no power had ever touched it:

*Corvane. Given freely. Found me by following nothing but love, which turns out to be the only road that goes everywhere.*

She signed it *Ada*, and beneath it, smaller, in the hand of a royal food-taster who had once catalogued poisons with clinical calm and starved quietly for something she wouldn't name: *formerly Isandro Dunmore, who tasted everything and belonged to no one, and is at last home.*

Then she put the book back on its shelf, next to the rosemary and the lamplight and the smell of tomorrow's bread, and went back out to the step, and to him, and to the road full of travelers who knew that at its edge there was a kitchen where the stew was good, the myrtle was mostly harmless, and the keeper wrote down only what was given —

— and gave away everything else for free.
