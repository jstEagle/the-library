## 1. Charms for a City Without Weather

The rain-charms never worked, and Cyrus sold them anyway.

That was the joke of it, the small bitter joke that kept them company through the gray mornings of Vell, arranging the little glass vials on the felt of their market stall like an apothecary laying out remedies. Blue glass, mostly, with a few green ones for variety, each stoppered with wax and filled with a solution that was nothing more sinister than river water, salt, and a drop of blue dye. Inside each vial, if a buyer held it up to the light, a thread of silver foil drifted and turned, slow as a falling feather. People liked to believe it was a captured cloud. Cyrus let them believe it. Belief was the only ingredient in the charms that did anything at all.

*Rain-charms for the dry season,* the hand-lettered sign said. *Fair weather guaranteed.*

The guarantee was true, in its way. Vell was a city without weather — not without rain exactly, though the rain here came thin and apologetic, more mist than memory — but without *weather* in the way Cyrus understood the word. No storms rolled over Vell. No thunderheads built their violet cathedrals above the rooftops. The sky lay over the city like a lid, like a hand pressed flat, and nothing in it ever broke. The Wardens liked it that way. The Gray Wardens, who patrolled the streets in their ash-colored coats and their gloves that never came off, who had a word for what Cyrus was and that word was *arsonist of the sky.*

Five years, and Cyrus had learned to sell the weather instead of calling it.

"Morning, Marchetti."

Cyrus looked up from the vials. Old Sefa stood at the edge of the stall, leaning on her broom as she did every morning, sweeping the cobbles in front of her own shop out of habit rather than necessity, because the cobbles of Vell stayed clean the way everything in Vell stayed orderly — through the tireless attention of people who had been taught that disorder was a kind of crime.

"Sefa." Cyrus smiled the smile they kept for mornings, easy and slightly tired, the smile of a person with nothing hidden anywhere in them. "You're up before the sun again."

"The sun's lazy in this city. Someone has to shame it." Sefa squinted at the vials. "Still selling your bottled rain?"

"Best rain in Vell. The only rain in Vell."

"Hmph." The old woman's face did the thing it always did, the small folding of suspicion and affection together, like paper creased and creased again until it went soft. "My knees say a storm's coming. My knees have said a storm's coming for five years, and this city has never once obliged them."

"Your knees are liars," Cyrus said lightly, and felt the lie of it like a stone in their own mouth. Because Sefa's knees were right, in a way she couldn't know. There was a storm in Vell. It had been standing very still for five years, wrapped in a gray coat, selling blue glass, and it was very tired.

Sefa shuffled off. Cyrus watched her go and let the smile fall off their face the way a coat falls from a shoulder, and for a moment they stood in the early market light among their worthless charms and felt the loneliness arrive, punctual as the tide.

That was the trick of safety, Cyrus had learned. Safety was loneliness wearing a respectable coat. Every day that no one looked at them twice was a day no one saw them. Every morning that began and ended without incident was a morning spent being no one, and no one, Cyrus had found, could not be loved, because there was nothing left of them to love. They had a stall. They had a room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and rope. They had a name that was not their name and a history that was three sentences long and rehearsed. *Marchetti. From upriver. Parents dead of fever. No trade but this.* It fit in the mouth like a smooth stone, and Cyrus had swallowed it so many times it no longer scraped going down.

It was safe. They told themselves it was safe the way a drowning man tells himself the water is fine.

* * *

Midmorning, the market filled.

Cyrus sold charms. They were good at it, which was its own small shame — good at the patter, the patter being the only magic left to them. They could read a customer in three seconds flat. The anxious ones wanted the green vials, because green looked medicinal. The romantic ones wanted blue, because blue looked like a promise. The practical ones, the farmers and carters who came in from the dry hills around Vell with dust in the seams of their faces, wanted to believe anything at all, and Cyrus sold them the belief gently, with a little ceremony, holding the vial up to the light so the silver thread turned in its water like a slow feather falling.

"See how it hangs in the air? That's a cloud's heart. Clouds keep their hearts in glass, when they're caught proper. You set this on your windowsill of a night, and by morning the sky remembers what it's for."

None of it was true. All of it was kind. The farmers went away holding their little vials like lanterns, and if the sky over Vell never obliged them, well — the sky over Vell never obliged anyone, and there was a strange democracy in that.

It was near noon when the woman came with the child.

Cyrus saw them before they reached the stall — a thin, gray-faced woman in a dyer's apron, carrying a boy of perhaps five, the boy's head lolling against her shoulder, his breath coming in the quick shallow sawtooth of fever. The market parted around them the way it always parted around sickness, politely and too fast. The woman's eyes swept the stalls — the fruit, the tinware, the charms — and snagged on Cyrus's sign, on the word *rain*, and Cyrus watched hope arrive on her face and hated themselves for what they were about to do, which was nothing, which was sell her blue glass and a patter and send her to the herb-wife like everyone else.

But the boy lifted his head.

He looked at Cyrus. Not at the vials — at *Cyrus*, at the place where a storm was standing very still inside a gray coat, and his fever-bright eyes went wide, and he said, in the cracked little voice of the very ill, "You're the rain."

The words went through Cyrus like a hook through the gill of a fish.

The mother had reached the stall now. "They said you sell weather. Please. The fever's been four days. The herb-wife's tinctures aren't touching it. I thought — the cool of rain, the sound of it — he sleeps better when there's rain, and there's never any rain—"

"There's never any rain in Vell," Cyrus said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "I sell charms, goodwife. Keepsakes. Nothing more."

"Please."

The boy was still looking at Cyrus. There was a shine of sweat on his forehead and his lips were cracked and he was looking at Cyrus with the absolute, unblinking faith of the very young and the very sick, and Cyrus felt something move inside them that they had kept still for five years, the great slow weight of it turning over like a whale surfacing, and they thought: *no.* They thought: *the Wardens, the gloves, the ash-colored coats.* They thought: *you are no one, and no one is safe.*

They thought: *he called you the rain.*

"Charms won't break a fever," Cyrus said quietly. "But I'll tell you what might. There's a well behind the tannery lane — the old one, the deep one, the one they covered over. The water there stays cold all summer. Soak a cloth in it and lay it across his brow, and sing to him while you do it. Any song. The singing matters more than the cloth."

"That's it? That's your charm?"

"That's the only charm in Vell that works," Cyrus said, and the woman's face crumpled, and she turned to go, and the boy over his mother's shoulder kept his eyes on Cyrus the whole way, fever-bright and certain, until the crowd took them.

Cyrus stood among their vials and found their hands were shaking.

Because here was the thing, the thing they never let themselves think past the first thought, the thought they kept in the same locked room as the name they no longer used: Cyrus could have done it. Could have reached into that gray lid of a sky and torn it open in a breath. Could have called a rain down on this one small fevered head, a rain the size of a blanket, the size of a kindness, cool and clean and singing. Five years ago they had called storms that drowned harbors. Five years ago the sky had known their name and come when it was asked. And now a child was burning four streets away and Cyrus stood behind a stall of blue glass and lied to his mother about wells.

*Safe,* they told themselves, the way you press on a bruise to feel how much it hurts. *You are safe.*

The morning curdled from there.

* * *

It happened in the way these things always happened, which was quickly and at the worst possible moment.

The boy came back.

Not the mother — the boy alone, wobbling on his fever legs down the tannery lane behind the market, trailing his mother's shouting behind him like a dropped scarf. Cyrus saw him coming and felt the day tilt. He stood in front of the stall, swaying, and held up one small hand, and in it was a copper bit — one copper, all he had, the whole treasury of a five-year-old's pockets.

"For the rain," he said. "I have money."

"Where's your mother?"

"Asleep. I want the rain. You're the rain." He said it without drama, without the wheedle of a child begging, with the flat certainty of someone reporting a fact of the world: *the sun is yellow, water is wet, you are the rain.* And then his knees went, and Cyrus came around the stall fast and caught him before he struck the cobbles, and the boy's forehead burned against their wrist like a coal wrapped in skin.

And Cyrus — tired, frightened, five years careful — did the thing they had sworn on everything left of themselves not to do.

They glanced up the lane. Empty. The market's noise a comfortable distance away. The gray lid of the sky overhead, unbroken, unwatched.

They cupped the boy's burning head in both hands, and they reached — gently, so gently, with the very tips of whatever they were — up into the lid of the sky, and they *tapped on it.*

It was nothing. It was less than nothing. It was a whisper of what they had been, a single drop drawn out of a sky a hundred miles away, a thread of cool wet air pulled down the lane like a ribbon through a needle's eye. The boy sighed. The fever did not break, but it eased, the way a fist eases when someone holds it, and a fine mist beaded on his eyelashes, and he smiled in his half-sleep and said, "Told you," and Cyrus stood in the tannery lane holding a fevered child and a stolen drop of weather and felt, for three whole seconds, like a person again.

Then the shadow fell across the lane.

Gray. Ash-colored. The particular quality of shade that a Warden's coat cast, which was somehow heavier than other shade, as though the coat drank the light on principle.

Cyrus did not turn around. Turning around was a confession. They stood very still, the boy in their arms, the mist already gone, the sky already sealed and innocent, and behind them a voice said — not unkindly, which was the worst of it — "Good afternoon."

Wardens always sounded reasonable. That was the horror of them. They were not monsters in the stories' sense; they were clerks of the impossible, patient as tide charts, and their voices never rose because nothing a storm-caller did could surprise them anymore.

"You're holding a child," the voice observed.

"He fell. Fever. I was carrying him to his mother." Every word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Is that so." A pause, the length of a page turning. "The air in this lane is wet."

"The tanneries boil water all day. The lane's always damp."

Another pause. Cyrus could hear the Warden breathing behind them, slow and even, a person who had never once in their life needed to hurry. And Cyrus stood with the boy's heat against their chest and understood with total clarity that the next thirty seconds would decide whether they lived the rest of their life as Marchetti, seller of blue glass, or died in a Warden cellar being asked questions in a reasonable voice that never rose.

Then the mother's voice came tearing down the lane — "*Tomas! Tomas, where — oh, thank the saints—*" — and the moment broke like a crust. Cyrus turned, surrendering the boy into his mother's arms with the practiced clumsiness of a bystander, a helpful stranger, and met, over the woman's shoulder, the eyes of the Gray Warden.

They were ordinary eyes. Brown, tired, human. That was the other horror of the Wardens: they were people. This one was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the deep patience of a fisherman. He looked at Cyrus for a long moment. He looked at the boy. He looked at the damp cobbles of the lane, where the mist had already dried to nothing, leaving not one witness but a hundred — the lane itself, the wet stone, the air.

"Charms," the Warden said at last, nodding at the stall. "They work, do they?"

"Fair weather guaranteed," Cyrus said.

Something moved at the corner of the Warden's mouth. Not a smile. The memory of one, maybe, buried under five years of the same gray coat. "Keep your weather off the sky, Marchetti." He said the name the way everyone in Vell said it, lightly, carelessly, a name that had never belonged to anyone. "The sky in this city is closed."

He walked on, ash-gray, unhurried, and turned the corner, and was gone.

Cyrus stood in the lane with their heart going like a trapped bird and their hands, when they looked down at them, perfectly steady — steady the way things are steady after the earthquake, when the shaking happens somewhere underneath where no one can see. The mother was thanking them. The boy was asleep against her shoulder, breathing easier, one drop of stolen weather cooling in his blood. And Cyrus heard themselves say the right things, the *glad to help, the saints keep him,* and smiled the morning smile, and all the while a single thought rang in them like a struck bell:

*He knew the lane was wet. He knew, and he walked on.*

That was worse than being caught. Being caught was a door slamming. This was a door left open — and a Warden on the other side of it, waiting, patient as tide, for the day Cyrus walked through.

That night Cyrus did not light the lamp in the room above the chandler's shop. They sat in the dark with the tallow smell and the rope smell and the three-sentence history, and they looked at their hands in the last gray light, the hands that had tapped on the lid of the sky, and they thought: *five years. Five years of blue glass and smooth stones in the mouth, and one child, one copper bit, one whisper of rain, and it's all thread now. It's unraveling. I felt it unravel.*

They should leave Vell. They had known that for years, the way you know a tooth is bad. But leaving meant roads, and roads meant records, and records meant the Wardens' long gray ledger of every soul in the province, and there was nowhere in the world to run to anyway, because Cyrus had run from the only country that had ever fit them, and that country was gone. Not conquered. Not renamed. *Gone* — struck from the maps, struck from the treaties, struck from the mouths of everyone who had ever lived there, so that even the word of it dissolved on the tongue like salt in rain. Cyrus had woken one morning five years ago in a foreign inn with a name and no nation, and had spent every morning since pretending the shape of that absence was not the exact shape of themselves.

They slept badly, as always. They dreamed, as always, of a coastline they could not picture and a door they could not remember, and woke before dawn with salt — actual salt, a fine white crust — on their lips, though the nearest sea was three hundred miles away and the room above the chandler's shop had no window facing any water at all.

They told themselves it was the damp. They told themselves the lane, the tanneries, the boiling water.

They went down to the market and set out the blue glass vials on the felt, one by one, in the dark before the sun, and waited for the day to begin.

It was at dusk, when the market was emptying and the light went the color of old pewter, that the stranger came.

Cyrus saw him at first as just another late customer — a big man, coat collar up, moving wrong, moving like a man whose body was a borrowed thing he was no longer sure how to operate. He crossed the emptying square in a crooked line, past the shuttering stalls, past Sefa's dark window, straight at Cyrus's little stall of worthless charms, and Cyrus looked up to give the patter, the *fair weather guaranteed, the best rain in Vell* — and saw the blood.

It was everywhere, already, soaking the stranger's left side black in the pewter light, leaving a slick on the cobbles behind him like the trail of something dragged from the sea. His eyes found Cyrus's and fixed there with the terrible focus of the dying, and his mouth worked, and what came out was not *help* and was not *guard me* and was not any word Cyrus had rehearsed an answer to in five careful years.

It was: "You're the one who called storms."

And he fell against the stall, and the vials of blue glass rang together like small cold bells, and the last light went out of the sky over Vell, which had never once in five years broken, and the dying man's hand — salt-crusted, shaking, leaving red on the felt — pressed something into Cyrus's hands and closed their fingers over it, hard, with the last of his strength.

A map. Salt-stiff, folded, heavy as a stone.

Behind him, at the far end of the square, gray coats were coming — two of them, three, unhurried, patient as tide — and the stranger looked at Cyrus with eyes already going out, and spent his final breath on four words that Cyrus would carry, though they did not yet know it, all the rest of the way to the edge of the world:

"The Door remembers," he whispered. "The Door *remembers*—"
