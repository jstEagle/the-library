## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

The room above the tannery cost four coppers a night and smelled like it was worth three. Cyrus paid anyway, because the window faced the canal and the canal meant water, and water meant—Cyrus pressed the thought flat before it could become a feeling. Water meant nothing. In Vell, water was only water.

They locked the door, wedged a chair beneath the knob out of habit rather than hope—the lock was a joke, the chair more so—and sat on the bed with the dead man's map spread across their knees.

It had stopped bleeding salt.

That was the first wrongness, and it took Cyrus a long moment to name it. All evening, walking fast through streets that kept trying to remember they were streets, the map had wept a fine white brine from every fold, dampening their palms, stinging a split in their thumb they didn't remember getting. Now, under the room's single guttering lamp, the crust had dried to a pale rime, like frost on a spring window. Like something exhaling at last.

The paper itself was not paper. Cyrus tilted it toward the light and saw the grain of it: pressed kelp-fiber, gray-green, faintly sweet when they dared bring it near their nose. Ostravan make. They knew that the way you know your mother's voice—not with the mind but somewhere lower, in the ribs.

*Don't,* they told themselves. *Don't start.*

They started.

* * *

The map showed a country.

That should not have been remarkable. Maps showed countries; that was their whole occupation. But this one showed roads that Cyrus knew for a fact did not exist, drawn with the confidence of a hand that had walked them. A high road along a ridge called the Kestrel's Spine, marked with a shrine at its shoulder. A river road following a river labeled *Vessa* in a looping script that made Cyrus's throat tighten absurdly, because their grandmother had written her spice labels in exactly that hand. A bridge at a town named Solenne Ford, and the bridge was annotated in tiny letters: *toll is a story. tell a good one.*

Cyrus had crossed the Vessa once, as a child, on the way to a cousin's wedding. There had been no toll and no story, only wind off the water and their father's coat around their shoulders. The memory arrived whole and unbidden, smelling of wet wool and fennel, and it hurt the way cold water hurts—a bright shock, then a deep ache spreading outward.

Five years. Five years since Ostrava had been taken out of the world. Not conquered. Not destroyed, exactly. *Erased.* The word everyone used, though no one could say what it meant, because the moment you tried to explain it your own understanding slid sideways like a fish. Borders redrawn. Records burned or rewritten or—this was the part that made scholars drink—never written at all, so that old atlases now showed open sea where the coast had been, and the people who had sailed that coast remembered smooth water and nothing else.

Everyone except the exiles. And there were so few of those left that Cyrus had stopped looking for others years ago. The Wardens said there had never been any such country, and the terrible genius of it was that most days, most hours, Cyrus could almost believe them. Memory of home became a thing you carried quietly, like a stolen coat, checking over your shoulder to see who'd noticed the fit.

And here was a dead man's map, drawn in kelp-fiber and salt, showing the Kestrel's Spine as if the mountain still stood watch over anything at all.

"You came from there," Cyrus said aloud, to the stranger who wasn't in the room. Their voice sounded strange in the tannery-smelling dark. "You came from home."

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

* * *

Wren had been flickering in and out of Cyrus's head all day, and Cyrus had been pretending otherwise, because pretending otherwise was how you stayed sane in Vell.

It had started at dusk, at the moment the man died. Not a haunting in any sense Cyrus had been taught to fear—no cold, no voices from walls, none of the theater the scare-stories promised. It was smaller and worse than that. It was *remembering things that hadn't happened to them.* The taste of pipe smoke when no one smoked. A woman's laugh, low and delighted, attached to no face. The sensation of hands chapped from rope-work, though Cyrus's own hands were soft from charm-craft. Little intrusions, arriving at the edge of vision like weather.

Now, sitting with the map, Cyrus let one come all the way in for the first time.

It was like stepping into a warm bath that someone else had already been sitting in. Suddenly they were lying on their back on a deck—*his* deck, Wren's deck, the *Kettlewife*, a coastal trader, small and stubborn—and above him hung a sky the color of hammered tin, and he was thinking, with a calm that frightened him, *I'm going to die tonight, and the worst part is I always knew.*

Cyrus wrenched out of it, gasping, the lamp flame doubled in their watering eyes.

"Stop," they whispered. "I don't—I didn't know you. Stop *doing* that."

But even as they said it, they understood, with the sick clarity that comes after the fall rather than before, why the man had found them. Of all the stalls in all the markets in Vell, he had collapsed against theirs. He had pressed this map into their hands and spent his last breath on two words: *the Door remembers.* He hadn't chosen Cyrus at random. He had known—or guessed, or been told by whatever passes for fate in a drowned country—that the person selling rain-charms that never worked was a storm-caller in hiding. That underneath the false name and the gray clothes and the careful nothing-expression, Cyrus Marchetti was the last person alive who could call lightning down out of a clear sky.

He had known, and he had died anyway, and now he was leaking into their skull like bilgewater into a hull.

"All right," Cyrus said to the empty room, because arguing with a ghost alone in a rented room was a step too far even for them. "All right. Show me the rest."

* * *

The rest was the coastline.

Cyrus turned the map over expecting shipping routes or tide tables and found instead the sea, drawn in Wren's sure hand—and along the northern edge, where every atlas in Vell showed unbroken blue water, the map showed land. A coast. Bays and harbors, a peninsula like a hooked finger, a lighthouse marked with a tiny sketched flame. Names ran along it in that looping script: *Carrowgate. The Nine Sisters. Port Amalie. Saltmere.*

Saltmere.

Cyrus set the map down on the bed very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

They had been born forty miles inland of Saltmere, in a hill town whose name they had not spoken aloud in five years because speaking it felt like shouting in a house where mourners sat. But Saltmere—they had been to Saltmere. Everyone went to Saltmere eventually; it was where the salt came from, the famous gray-gold salt that Ostrava traded for half its wealth, harvested from pans that glittered like spilled jewelry at low tide. Their father had bought his yearly stock there. Ada—no, not Ada, that was tomorrow's problem, that was a girl at the market who tasted lies, put her away—their father had haggled with the pan-masters while Cyrus, twelve years old, had run the wooden walkways between the evaporation ponds and eaten candied ginger until they were sick.

The ponds were gone. Every atlas agreed: open sea. The Wardens agreed: there had never been any pans, any town, any trade. And yet here was Wren's ink, patient and precise, drawing the hooked curve of the harbor breakwater as if he were tracing it from memory through the paper.

Cyrus touched the little sketched lighthouse flame and felt, distinctly, the ghost flinch.

*Careful,* came the sensation-not-word, arriving behind their eyes like a headache made of someone else's grief. *That one's mine. My wife kept it lit.*

"Oh," Cyrus said softly. "Oh, you're not just from there. You're—"

From the war years. From the end. From the five years Cyrus had missed, the five years between their flight and the erasure, which they had spent telling themselves were probably fine, probably quiet, probably the country had gotten by without its prodigal storm-caller. Wren's memories said otherwise. Wren's memories were a locked room and Cyrus had just seen light under the door, and everything behind it was smoke.

They didn't look. Not yet. They weren't strong enough, and the map wasn't finished with them.

* * *

Because here was the thing about the map that finally broke them, the thing they circled back to again and again through the long hours while the lamp burned down and the canal outside went quiet: the map refused to show the way in.

Every other feature was rendered with loving exactitude. Roads, rivers, shrines, the coast, the lighthouse, notes in the margins in two hands—one Wren's, confident and slightly rushed, and an older hand beneath, faded, as if the map had been copied from something older still. But the interior—the heart of the country, the highlands, the capital whose name Cyrus would not say—was blank. Not empty. *Blank.* A clean white space with a single mark at its center: a small circle, and inside the circle, drawn so lightly you'd miss it if you weren't crying already, a door.

Two panels. A seam of white stone. Nothing else.

Around the circle, in Wren's hand, curved a line of text. Cyrus read it eleven times. It did not get easier.

*There is no road to the Door. The Door is what remains when every road is forgotten. Bring the map to the water and follow what does not fade.*

Below that, smaller, almost apologetic:

*I'm sorry. I couldn't draw the last part. It wouldn't stay drawn.*

Cyrus laughed. It came out cracked and awful, one bark in the dark room, and they clapped a hand over their mouth like a child. Because that was it, wasn't it? That was the whole obscene joke of the erasure. You couldn't map your way home. Home was the one place the cartography failed, because the country had been unwritten so thoroughly that even ink forgot it. Wren had drawn everything else from love and memory, and the center—the reason for all of it—was a hole shaped like a door.

"The Door remembers," Cyrus repeated. His dying words. *The Door remembers.* As if the Door were the last thing left holding the country's name, a keel beneath a capsized ship, keeping it from drifting entirely out of the world.

And the map wanted water. *Bring the map to the water.* Vell was built on canals; water was everywhere and belonged to no one. But the map meant something else, surely. The sea. The erased coast. Which meant leaving Vell, which meant checkpoints and Wardens and questions, which meant—

Which meant everything Cyrus had spent five years not doing.

They got up and stood at the window because sitting still had become unbearable. Below, the canal lay black and oily, reflecting a few lamps and no stars; Vell's sky was a ceiling of chimney-smoke and civic order. Somewhere a Warden patrol's boots rang on cobbles, unhurried, certain of their city. Cyrus watched their reflection ghosted on the glass—thin face, cropped hair, eyes that had once, people said, gone silver when the storms came near. No silver now. Just a tired liar in a stolen coat.

"I ran," they said to the reflection. It was the first time in five years they'd said it plainly, even alone, even to glass. "They came for the storm-callers and I ran, and everyone who stayed—" The sentence hit the wall of everything Wren's locked-room memories held, and Cyrus turned away from it. "Everyone who stayed is a blank space on a map."

Grief, when it finally came, came like the tide. That was the only way to describe it, and Cyrus had grown up beside tides and knew their treachery: not a wave but a rising, patient, getting into everything, lifting loose objects and setting them gently down somewhere you'd never find them again. It started in the hands—the map trembling, or their hands, indistinguishable—and moved up the arms and into the chest until breathing was a labor. Their father's coat. Their grandmother's spice labels. The Vessa in flood, brown and muscular, carrying blossom-branches out to sea every spring. The candied ginger. The wedding, whose cousin's it had been they could no longer recall, and that small loss undid them more than the large ones, because it proved the erasure worked from the inside too. Even in the exiles, even in the ones who escaped, the country was slowly dissolving, year by year, name by name. Another decade and Cyrus would be a person with an inexplicable ache and a box of unidentifiable keepsakes.

Unless.

Unless a dying smuggler had carried a door across the sea in his chest for five years. Unless the Door remembered what everyone else had been made to forget.

Cyrus wiped their face with the heel of their hand, took up the map, folded it along its old creases—and stopped. On the innermost fold, hidden until now, was one more annotation, small enough to be a stain. Ink, not salt. Fresh. Written after Wren's death? No—written in a hurry, before it, the letters wobbling:

*If you are reading this, you are the one who called storms. Forgive me for what I did to you. Find the salt-girl. She will taste whether you mean it.*

Cyrus read it twice. The room seemed to tilt.

*The salt-girl.* There was exactly one salt-seller's daughter in the Lowmarket, a sharp-eyed girl who worked her father's stall and had looked at Cyrus yesterday—had looked *through* them—with an expression Cyrus had filed away as suspicion and now understood, with a fresh roll of dread, might have been recognition.

Outside, the boots of the Warden patrol paused below the window. Paused, and did not move on.

Cyrus blew out the lamp and stood in the dark holding a dead man's map against their chest, listening to the tide of their own grief go out, and out, and out.
