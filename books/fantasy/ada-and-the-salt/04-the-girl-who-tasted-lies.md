## 4. The Girl Who Tasted Lies

Ada Voss had been tasting lies since she was six years old, though it took her until she was nine to understand that other people couldn't.

It began with her father's customers. He would send her out to the front of the stall with the scales while he haggled in the back, and the buyers would lean over the salt bins and say things like *this is the finest white salt in Vell, I can tell* when they had no intention of buying anything at all. Those words came to her on the air like a flavor—thin, metallic, a little like licking a spoon that had been left too long in tea. A lie tasted like old copper and something faintly sweet underneath, the way rotting fruit is sweet. Her father's honest customers tasted of nothing at all. Just air. Just bread and river water.

She learned quickly not to say anything about it. In Vell, a girl who could taste lies was a curiosity; a girl who *said so* was a problem. So Ada kept her tongue behind her teeth and her gift folded up small inside her chest, and by nineteen she could stand in the fish market with a smile on her face while three separate merchants lied to her about the freshness of their catch, and no one ever knew.

Her father knew. Of course he did. He'd never asked how she always seemed to know which bargemen were honest and which ones watered their cargo or skimmed the tolls. He simply arranged his routes around what she told him, and never once called it magic, because calling it magic would have made it a crime, and neither of them could afford for it to be a crime. It wasn't magic, he said. It was just Ada. Like her mother's knack for knowing weather before the clouds formed. Like her grandmother's habit of humming songs nobody had taught her, in a language nobody in Vell spoke.

The Vossses were careful people. Careful the way you're careful walking on ice that you know, in your bones, is thinner than it looks.

Which was why, on the morning the stranger with the storm-grey eyes set up a charm stall two rows down from the salt stall, Ada's first instinct was to walk over and taste them.

* * *

The stranger had been in the Lowmarket for four days now. Ada had watched them from between the hanging bundles of dried kelp: a narrow-shouldered figure in a coat too big for them, selling little woven charms of reed and colored thread—rain-charms, luck-knots, blessings against damp. Useless things. Everyone in Vell knew charms didn't work here. That was rather the point of Vell. The city sat on the river like a fat grey cat on a warm hearth, smug and dry and utterly without wonder, and its people bought charms the way they bought painted fans—as decoration, as sentiment, as a small harmless lie they told themselves with their purses.

But this stranger's charms weren't harmless lies. Ada had drifted past the stall twice, close enough to brush the display table, and both times the air above the reed-weavings had *hummed*. Not a sound. A taste. Like the first breath of air off a summer storm rolling in over far hills—the green-metallic tang of rain still hours away and miles off. Real magic, banked down low, coiled tight as a fist inside a glove. The stranger was selling working charms in a city where working charms got you a cell beneath the Wardenhouse, and they were doing it with the flat, bored expression of someone selling turnips.

And they were doing it badly, Ada thought. Or desperately. Because every third customer who leaned over the table got a flicker of that storm-taste, and any one of them might be a Warden's informant. The Gray Wardens paid in silver for whispers. Half the market ran on whisper-money.

On the fourth day, the fevered child came.

Ada saw it happen because she happened to be restocking the coarse-salt bin at the end of her row, which gave her a clean line of sight through the gap between the rope-seller's awning poles. The child was maybe five, wrapped in a blanket despite the mild morning, carried by a woman with the hollow-eyed look of someone who hadn't slept in a week. Fever-season had come early to the river districts. Three children on Ada's own street had it. The physickers sold willow-bark tincture at prices that made widows weep.

The woman stopped at the charm stall. Ada saw her say something—pleading, from the shape of her shoulders—and saw the stranger go very still. Then the stranger glanced left, glanced right, and did something with their hands that was too quick and too deliberate to be nothing.

The air changed.

Ada felt it across twenty feet of crowded market like a door opening onto a different world: cool, wet, vast, the taste of a thunderhead building over open water. For one impossible heartbeat the whole Lowmarket smelled of coming rain, and the fevered child stirred in her mother's arms and sighed—a deep, easy sigh, the sigh of a body remembering how to rest.

Then the stranger closed their hands and the taste snapped shut, and Ada stood with her hand buried in a bin of salt and her heart hammering against her ribs like a bird against a window.

Because she had tasted that exact flavor before. Once. Five years ago, on a night she had never been able to explain, when the sky over Vell had gone strange and heavy and the river had run backward for the space of eleven minutes, and everyone in the city had woken the next morning having quietly agreed to remember nothing. But Ada remembered. Ada remembered the taste of that night the way you remember the taste of milk from childhood—beneath everything, under all other flavors, waiting.

The stranger had been there. The stranger had *made* that night. And now they were standing in the middle of the Lowmarket doing magic like a man striking matches in a powder store.

Down the row, two figures in grey had stopped at the mouth of the market lane.

* * *

Ada moved without deciding to move. Later she would try to reconstruct why—patriotism for a country she'd never seen, pity for a feverish child, the simple animal solidarity of one hidden thing recognizing another—but in the moment there was no reason at all. There was just the Wardens' grey coats moving into the crowd, and her feet carrying her sideways through the gap between stalls, and her voice, pitched bright and carrying, saying:

"There you are! I've been looking everywhere."

The stranger turned. Up close, their face was younger than their eyes—young bones, old gaze, skin sallow with weeks of bad sleep. Their hands, tucked behind the charm table, were trembling.

"You've been—" they started.

"The knotwork," Ada said loudly. "For my father's barge. You promised me the big one, the blue-and-silver, remember? For the prow." She planted herself at the corner of the charm table like a fence post, squarely between the stranger and the lane, and smiled the wide, guileless smile that had gotten her out of trouble since she was eight years old. Behind her, she heard the grey coats slow. Hesitate. A Warden's voice, low: *Charm-seller? No—salt merchant's daughter. Move on.*

The footsteps receded. The market noise closed back over the lane like water over a dropped stone.

The stranger let out a breath that seemed to come from the bottom of their boots. "I don't know who you are," they said quietly, "but thank you." And then, because people are fools even when they're grateful, they added: "My name's Corren. I'm just passing through."

The lie landed on the air between them like a coin dropped in a quiet church.

Copper. Old, worn copper, and that sick undertone of sweetness. Ada flinched before she could stop herself—an involuntary twitch of the nose, the way you flinch at a bad smell—and the stranger caught it. She watched them catch it. Watched their eyes narrow, watched the calculation start behind the storm-grey.

"You're lying," Ada said. Not loud. Just true.

"I don't know what you—"

"Your name isn't Corren." She kept her voice level, conversational, the voice of a girl discussing the price of kelp. "You flinched when the Wardens looked at you, but not the way an honest person flinches. An honest person flinches at the coat. You flinched at being *seen*. And you taste wrong." She tapped the side of her nose. "Like a penny left in rainwater. That's what lying tastes like, if you're wondering. Yours has been sitting in your mouth so long it's gone green at the edges."

The stranger had gone very pale. Their hand crept toward the table's edge—not toward her, she noted, toward the map-shaped bulge in the satchel at their hip, the one they'd been guarding since the day they arrived like a dog guards a bone.

"What are you?" they whispered.

"Ada Voss. Salt-seller's daughter." She shrugged. "And before you ask—no, it's not magic. Magic's illegal, and I'm very law-abiding." The irony sat on her tongue alongside the fading copper of their lie, and she almost laughed. Almost. "Here's what's going to happen. You're going to fold up your little stall, and you're going to walk with me to the river steps, and you're going to tell me the truth, because I have spent four days watching you play with fire in a city made of tinder, and either you tell me what you are or I decide you're worth more to me as a whisper to the Wardens than as a mystery."

"That's blackmail."

"It's commerce," Ada said. "I'm a merchant's daughter. We call things what they are."

Something shifted in the stranger's face then—something that might have been exhaustion, or relief, or the particular surrender of a person who has been running alone for so long that being threatened feels almost like being held. They began folding the charm table with quick, practiced motions.

"My name," they said, as the last reed-weaving went into the basket, "is not Corren."

"No," Ada agreed. "It isn't."

"And I'll tell you the rest on the water. Not before. Walls have ears in this city. Some of them are wearing grey."

* * *

Her father's barge, the *Patient Meridian*, lay moored at the Tanners' Steps with her hold half-full of coarse salt and her decks piled with net-mending that would never get mended. Papa was up on the roof of the wheelhouse re-caulking a seam, and he took one look at Ada's face and one look at the stranger behind her and climbed down without asking a single question, which was one of the thousand unspoken kindnesses between them.

They sat in the stern, among the coils of rope. The river slid past, brown and patient. Somewhere upstream a bell buoy clanked its dull note.

"Talk," Ada said.

The stranger looked out at the water for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was low enough that only the river and Ada could hear it.

"I told you a lie because I've been telling it so long I don't know how to stop. My name is Cyrus Marchetti." The words came out stiff, like items being unpacked after years in storage. "I was born in Ostrava."

Ada went cold all over, and not from the river wind.

There was no such place. Every child in Vell learned the litany early: *Vell, and Casterhale, and the Fenmark principalities, and the Salt Coast beyond.* No Ostrava. She'd checked, once, at fourteen, in the map-room of the Guildhall where her father delivered salt to the clerks—checked every atlas, every chart, every ledger of ports and tolls. There was a blank stretch of coastline east of the Salt Coast where the ink simply stopped, where the cartographers' sea-monsters swam in empty water, and when she'd asked a clerk about it the man had looked at her with mild, honest puzzlement and said, *What do you mean, what's there? Open ocean, girl. Nothing's ever been there.*

Nothing's ever been there. Said with no copper on his tongue at all. That was the part that had frightened her—the clerk believed it. The whole world believed it, the way you believe the sun rises.

"Ostrava doesn't exist," she said.

"No," Cyrus agreed bitterly. "It doesn't. That's rather the problem." And then they told her—haltingly, in pieces, watching her face the whole time for the flicker of copper that would mean she thought they were mad. About the country erased five years ago. About the Gray Wardens and the ban. About being a storm-caller in exile, hiding in the one city where their hands were tied behind their backs. About a dying man who had collapsed into their stall three nights ago and pressed a salt-crusted map into their hands and died whispering *the Door remembers.*

Ada listened, and tasted nothing but truth—truth like clean cold well-water, truth with weight and mineral in it—and the cold in her chest turned to something else. Something that ached.

"Show me the map."

Cyrus hesitated, then drew it from the satchel. Unrolled across their knees, it was a ruin of salt-stain and faded ink, and yet the lines beneath the damage were fine and sure, drawn by a master hand. Roads that matched no road Ada had ever seen. A coastline that filled in the blank water east of the Salt Coast like a key fitting a lock. And at the center, circled in ink gone brown with age, a mark shaped like a doorway, labeled in a language that made Ada's grandmother's hummed songs rise unbidden in her memory.

"This is why I need to leave Vell," Cyrus said. "Quietly. Before the Wardens finish noticing me. I need passage downriver, past the checkpoints, to the coast." They looked up, and their storm-grey eyes were naked with something that Ada recognized, because she wore it herself every day of her life: the loneliness of carrying a secret too large for one chest. "I have almost nothing to pay you with. I know what I'm asking."

Ada looked at the map. At the doorway-mark. At the blank water that was not blank, that had never been blank, that had been stolen out from under the world along with a whole country and everyone in it—including, perhaps, the source of her grandmother's songs, including whatever it was in Ada's blood that tasted lies on the wind.

Her father had always said the Vosses came from somewhere else, generations back. Had never said where. Had gone quiet, with copper-thin non-answers, whenever she pressed.

"I'll take you," Ada said.

Cyrus blinked. "Just like that?"

"Not just like that." She held up one finger. "Passage on my father's barge costs money, and you haven't any. But I'll take it in trade. Your price is the truth—all of it, the whole way downriver. Every night at supper, you answer one question, fully, with no copper in it. Who you are. What you were. What happened to Ostrava. What the Door is." She folded her arms. "I've spent my whole life tasting other people's lies and pretending I don't. I want to spend one voyage hearing something true."

For a long moment Cyrus Marchetti said nothing. The bell buoy clanked. A heron lifted off the pilings and beat away downstream, toward the coast, toward the blank water.

"You drive a hard bargain, salt-seller's daughter," Cyrus said at last, and there was—faintly, cautiously, like light under a door—something that was almost a smile. "One question a night."

"One truth a night. There's a difference, and you'll learn it."

Papa's boots thumped on the deck above, and his voice came down the wheelhouse stair, casual as anything: "Ada, love, if your new friend's staying for supper, I'd best haul up the good barrel." Which was Papa's way of saying *I heard everything and I've already decided*, and Ada felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for four days.

Cyrus stood, awkward in their stolen coat, and offered a hand that still trembled slightly at the fingertips. "Then I suppose I'm your passenger."

Ada shook it. The stranger's palm was cool and rough, and the air around them still carried, faint as memory, the green-metallic ghost of coming storms.

"Passenger," she said. "We sail with tomorrow's tide. And Cyrus Marchetti—" she watched them flinch at their own name spoken aloud, watched them glance instinctively at the grey skyline of Vell, "—the first question's free, and I'm asking it tonight: what does the Door remember?"

Cyrus's face went still as deep water.

"That," they said quietly, "is exactly what I intend to find out."
