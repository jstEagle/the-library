## 5. Downriver, Against Every Law

The barge was called the *Patient Meridian*, which Cyrus thought was either a joke or a prayer, and by the third morning on the water they had decided it was both.

She was a salt-hauler of the old design—flat-bottomed, broad as a barn door, built to sit in shallow channels and wait out weather rather than fight it. Her hull was stained white to the waterline with years of dried brine, and her hold smelled of the cargo she no longer carried: salt, salt, and more salt, worked so deep into the timber that even the rain that fell on her deck seemed to land softer, less inclined to soak in.

Ada's father, Oskar Vey, stood at the tiller from dawn until well past dusk, a barrel-chested man with forearms like mooring rope and a face that gave away nothing except when he looked at his daughter, and then it gave away everything. He had agreed to carry them downriver with a single grunt that might have meant *yes* or might have meant *I have not heard you*, and he had not spoken a full sentence since. But he had moved their departure up two days without being asked, and he had stowed Bramble's map-case beneath the floorboards of the cabin where no casual search would find it, and Cyrus had begun to understand that in Vell, among people who had learned to live under the Wardens' gray shadow, love was mostly a language of logistics.

"You'll want to stay below when we pass the weir," Ada said, crouching beside Cyrus at the rail on the third morning. She was mending a net, her fingers quick and sure, her eyes on the water ahead. "There's a checkpoint at Tollgate Weir. Wardens check every vessel going downstream."

"Every vessel?"

"Every one." She bit the thread clean. "They look for runaways, mostly. Debt-fleers. Deserters. People with the wrong faces." A pause, delicate as the thread itself. "People who lie about their names."

Cyrus kept their gaze on the river. The Senn was wide here, slow and brown-green, carrying the autumn leaves of three provinces toward a sea none of the maps could quite agree on. Mist lay along the banks in long white ribbons. It was beautiful, and it was watched, and Cyrus had not yet learned how to hold those two facts in the same hand.

"I don't lie about my name," they said quietly.

"No." Ada's voice was light, almost conversational, and that was what made it dangerous. "You just decline to tell the truth about it. There's a difference. I can taste it."

* * *

The taste of a lie, she had explained the first night, was like copper and rot together—a bright metallic edge wrapped around something spoiled. Most lies were faint, a smudge on the air. Some were thick enough to choke on. Cyrus's name, when they'd given it as *Marchetti* back at the stall in Saltmarket Row, had tasted to her like a whole drowned ship: metal and ruin and something vast moving underneath.

"You're not lying exactly," she'd said, watching them across the small lamp-lit space of the cabin while her father slept. "It's more like... you've buried the true thing so deep under the false one that even you have to dig for it. That's rare. That's expensive. Nobody does that for fun."

Cyrus had said nothing. Wren, flickering at the edge of their memory like a candle in a draft, had said nothing either. Ghosts, it turned out, were poor company for confessions.

Now, on the third morning, the weir came into view an hour after dawn—a long stone lip across the river where the water dropped six feet into churning white, and beside it, on the near bank, the checkpoint: a timber palisade, a chain stretched across the descending channel, and four figures in gray standing in the flat grey light like nails driven into the day.

Oskar brought the barge in slow, letting the current do the work, and called down through the open hatch, "Below. Both of you. Now."

The hold was dark and close and smelled overwhelmingly of salt, which Cyrus was beginning to suspect was the point. They crouched between stacked empty barrels with Ada pressed warm against their side, listening through the planking as the barge kissed the lock wall and boots thumped aboard.

"Papers," said a voice above. Young. Bored. The most dangerous kind of Warden—the kind whose attention you couldn't earn because he hadn't decided anything mattered yet.

"Salt haulage permit, toll receipt, crew manifest." Oskar's voice was gravel poured slow. "One crew. Me."

"Manifest says one crew. I'm counting footprints on your deck, old man."

A silence. In it, Cyrus became aware of their own heartbeat, loud as oars.

"Deck's wet," Oskar said. "Rain last night. Rain makes prints."

Another silence, longer. Then, above, a second voice—older, drier: "Vey. You've run this stretch eleven years. Never once had a stowaway."

"Never once had a reason. Check my hold if you like. It's empty as my purse."

Boots crossed the deck overhead. The hatch began to slide.

Ada's hand found Cyrus's wrist in the dark and gripped hard—not pulling them anywhere, just holding on, the way you hold on to a rail in a storm. Her breath was very controlled. Cyrus realized, with a strange floating clarity, that she was tasting the air through the floorboards, reading the men above by the flavor of their intentions.

"He's going to open it," she breathed. "But he doesn't want to. He wants his breakfast. He wants—" her brow furrowed, chasing the taste, "—he wants to be somewhere else. He's afraid of the river. Why is he afraid of the river?"

The hatch slid fully open. Grey light fell in a blade across the barrels, across their joined hands, and stopped an inch short of Cyrus's boot.

The young Warden looked down into the dark for a long moment. His face, half-seen, was unremarkable and unhappy. Somewhere behind his eyes, Cyrus knew, there was a list of things magic did to people, and a list of things happened to people who let magic pass, and the two lists were the same list.

Then, upstream, the mist parted—and the river made a sound.

It was not a large sound. A low groan, deep in the water, like a door in a house settling. But every head on the barge turned toward it, and the young Warden straightened as if scalded, and the older one upstream said something sharp that Cyrus couldn't catch, and then the boots were retreating, the hatch was sliding shut, and Oskar's voice rumbled overhead: "Chain's dropping. Clear the lock. Move along, move along."

The barge shuddered, sank with the falling water, and passed through.

Only when the checkpoint was a smudge of gray behind them did Ada release Cyrus's wrist. Her fingers had left marks.

"What was that?" Cyrus asked. "In the water?"

"I don't know." She was looking at the river with an expression Cyrus had never seen on her before—not fear exactly, but recognition's first cousin. "But it came from under us. From below the channel. Like something big turning over in its sleep." She glanced sideways. "And the Warden ran from it. They all did. Like they'd been taught to."

Cyrus said nothing, but in the back of their memory, Wren laughed—a short, cracked, disbelieving laugh, the laugh of a man hearing an old song from a country that no longer existed.

*That,* the ghost said, *was a waypoint waking up.*

* * *

Wren had grown stronger every mile since they'd boarded the barge, and Cyrus had a theory about why, and the theory frightened them.

In Vell, in the city of stone and law, the dead cartographer had been barely there—a pressure behind the eyes, a voice like wind through a keyhole, visible only as a shimmer, as heat-haze shaped like a tired man. But the river ran west and south, away from Vell, into country that was older and less certain of itself, and with every mile Wren sharpened. By the second evening he could sit on the deck rail—a translucent man in a coat that had been fine once, before it was soaked in blood and salt. By the third morning he could pick things up, if the things were small and no one was looking too hard. He spent an hour that morning trying to turn the pages of Bramble's atlas and failing, and swearing with a fluency that suggested he'd been a sailor or a soldier before he'd been a mapper.

"It's the stones," he said now, pacing the deck in the way ghosts pace, which is to say his feet made no sound and left no shadow but his restlessness was real enough to lean against. "Feel that?" He crouched and laid his palm flat on the deck planking, though Cyrus knew he wasn't feeling the wood. He was feeling what was under it. "Under the riverbed. Under the silt. Granite, dressed and mortared. That's Ostravan work—that coursing pattern, long-short-long. We built our foundations to sing, Marchetti. Low notes, deep in the ground. The whole country hummed if you knew how to listen."

"The country isn't there," Cyrus said. "Bramble says—"

"Bramble says it's been *unmade*. Unwritten. Scrubbed from the memory of the world." Wren's translucent face did something complicated. "He's right. And it's still humming anyway. Because some things are written deeper than memory." He looked up, and his eyes—gray, tired, alive with a guilt he wore like a second coat—fixed on Cyrus. "And every mile we come, I remember more. Do you understand what that means? The erasure is thinner out here. Or older. Or *tired*."

From the stern, where Oskar stood at the tiller pretending not to listen and doing it badly, came a single dry word: "Ghost-talk."

"You can hear him?" Cyrus asked, startled.

"I can hear arguing," Oskar said. "Been hearing it since Tollgate. Man argues like my late wife." A pause. "No offense meant to your dead."

"None taken," said Wren, delighted. "I like him."

Ada emerged from the cabin with bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled onions, and the four of them—or the three of them and whatever Wren counted as—ate in the weak sun while the banks slid past: reed beds, cattle standing knee-deep in flood meadows, a shrine to some river-saint whose name had worn off the stone. Ada talked. That was the thing about Ada; she filled silence the way her father's barge filled the channel, completely and without apology. She told them about the year the Senn flooded and her family lived on the roof for nine days; about the taste of her mother's cooking, which she described synesthetically, as a chord; about the exact flavor of every lie her father had ever told her (only four, she said proudly, and two of them were about the size of fish).

And Cyrus, who had spent five years perfecting the art of being forgettable, of being a smudge in a doorway, of being nobody worth a second glance—Cyrus sat in the sun on a salt barge and listened, and felt something in their chest loosen by a single degree. It was terrifying. It was like unclenching a fist they'd forgotten they were making.

"Don't," Wren said quietly, beside them, for no one else's ears. When Cyrus looked over, the ghost's expression had lost its delight. "Don't get comfortable. Comfortable people stop running."

"They're not running *for* me," Cyrus said. "Ada has her reasons. Oskar has his price. This is commerce."

"And when the Wardens make the price higher than the cargo? When Rehn's people put a bounty on this barge and its crew?" Wren shook his head slowly. "I watched people be brave for five years, Marchetti. Bravery is a loan. Sooner or later the interest comes due, and it's always paid by someone who didn't sign for it."

But Ada caught Cyrus's eye across the deck just then—caught it and held it, and in it was neither commerce nor a loan, only the frank, unsentimental curiosity of someone who had decided to know you whether you liked it or not—and Cyrus found that Wren's warning slid off them like rain off waxed canvas.

* * *

The first waypoint revealed itself at dusk on the fourth day, and it revealed itself the way secrets do: all at once, and only to those already looking.

They had tied up for the night in a cut-off oxbow, a slack green loop of water the bargemen used when they wanted to avoid the night traffic of the main channel. On the bank stood the ruins of a mill—or what everyone on the river called a mill, though no record existed of any mill having been built there, and no tax had ever been assessed on it, and the oldest maps showed only blank vellum at that bend of the Senn. Its walls were granite, dressed in long-short-long courses, and moss had eaten the mortar until the stones seemed to grow from the earth rather than sit upon it.

Bramble would have loved it. Bramble was still two days' travel downstream, in the prison at Corve Landing, and the plan—to be finalized tomorrow, at the landing—was to extract him. Cyrus tried not to think about that yet.

Wren stood on the bank, and for the first time since his death he cast a shadow.

It was faint, a gray smudge in the last light, but it was there, thrown long across the ruined threshold, and Cyrus stared at it with their heart going strange in their chest. The ghost had stopped pacing. He had his hand raised toward the mill's eastern wall, toward a place where the moss grew in a pattern too regular to be moss, and his lips were moving.

"Come here," he said. "Both of you. Come see what I remember."

Ada came. Oskar stayed with the barge, arms folded, watching the dark water.

On the wall, beneath the moss, the stone was carved. Not words—worse than words. A diagram: concentric rings around a central seam, and the seam was a vertical line, and the line was split like—

"Like a door," Ada whispered. Her fingers hovered over the carving without touching. "Like the drawing on your map."

"The first waypoint," Wren said. His voice had gone rough. "This is a marker. One of seven, strung along the old road from the coast to the Door. Pilgrims followed them. *We* followed them, the ones who built the—" He stopped. Swallowed a sentence whole. "The carvings wake when you get close. Look—it's warm."

It was. The stone under Cyrus's palm held a heat nothing else in the autumn dusk possessed, a heat like a body recently left, like bread, like a hand. And as they stood there, the carved rings began—faintly, grudgingly, the way an old joint moves—to glow. Not light exactly. A paling of the dark, as if the stone remembered luminescence the way Bramble said the world had forgotten Ostrava: reluctantly, and in fragments.

And the river behind them groaned again, that same deep settling note, and this time Cyrus felt it through the soles of their feet, and understood that the sound was coming from under the water, from dressed granite singing its long-short-long song, and that the song was getting louder the farther west they went.

"It knows us," Ada said softly. She was still not touching the stone, but her whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward sun. "The marker. It—" She frowned, and touched her own tongue, a gesture Cyrus had learned to read. "It tastes honest. Stone shouldn't taste like anything. This tastes like—" she searched, "—like my mother's kitchen. Like something I lost."

"That's what it does," Wren said. "That's what it *did*, before. It reminded you." He turned to Cyrus, and the ghost-light in him guttered, and for a moment he looked less like a man and more like the wound of one. "Seven markers, Marchetti. This is the first. At each one, I'll remember more of what I did. And at each one, the road will remember *you* a little better too. That's how it works. The road home has a toll, and the toll is truth." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Cyrus had ever seen. "I hope you've been practicing yours."

Downstream, far off, a bell began to ring—three strokes, a pause, three strokes. Ada's head came up sharply.

"That's Corve Landing's alarm," she said. "They only ring it for fires and escapes." She looked at Cyrus, and the warmth of the last four days was suddenly a fragile thing indeed, a lantern in weather. "Corve Landing. Where your cartographer is locked up."

The bell rang again. Three strokes, a pause, three. And under it, thin and rising on the evening wind, another sound: horns. Many horns, and moving fast, and coming from the south—from the direction of the main channel, the direction of the Gray Wardens, the direction of everything they had rowed so carefully away from.

Oskar was already casting off the mooring lines.

"Well," he said, in the longest sentence Cyrus had yet heard from him, "either that's for us, or the gods are crueler than advertised. Get below, girl. And you—" he jabbed a thick finger at the space where Wren stood, though he could not have seen him, though he must have felt him, "—whoever you are, start remembering faster."

The *Patient Meridian* slipped her lines and slid out into the dark oxbow, running without lights, against every law, toward the ringing bells.
