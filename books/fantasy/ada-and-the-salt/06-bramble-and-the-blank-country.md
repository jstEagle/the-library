## 6. Bramble and the Blank Country

The cell smelled of ink and mildew, which was, in Bramble's considered opinion, the only honest thing left in Vell.

He had been in it for three years, four months, and eleven days—not that he counted. He counted everything; that was rather the point of him. The cell was seven feet by nine, its walls furred with damp, and he had mapped every inch of it: the hairline crack running from the ceiling's northeast corner down to the floorboards like a river on an old chart, the seam where two stones met at a slightly imperfect right angle, the way the light from the barred window traveled a slow arc across the floor each day, a sun-line he had inked onto the wall with charcoal until the wardens scraped it off and he did it again. They could take his instruments. They could not take the habit of him.

They had taken his instruments, of course. That was the crime.

Not treason, whatever the warrant said. Not sedition, though the tribunal had used the word *sedition* with great relish, as if it were a spice they'd been saving for a special dish. What Aldous Bramble—formerly Royal Cartographer to a crown that no longer admitted to having existed—had actually done was simpler and far worse. He had drawn a map of a country that everyone agreed was not there.

*Ostrava.*

Even now, thinking the name felt like pressing a tongue into a socket where a tooth had been. There was a shape to the absence. Other people walked past it without noticing, the way one walks past a missing picture on a wall without ever wondering what hung there. But Bramble had spent thirty years reading coastlines the way priests read scripture, and he knew when a page had been torn out. The atlases all showed open sea east of the Vellish coast—a clean, confident blue, untroubled by so much as a shoal mark. The trade ledgers showed shipping lanes that bent around nothing in particular, wide courteous arcs, as though every captain for fifty years had simply preferred the long way round. And the people—the people would smile at you, warm and blank, and say *there's never been anything out there, Master Bramble,* in voices as smooth as poured milk.

But the arcs in the shipping lanes were too wide. That was what had undone him. A cartographer notices. The sea does not lie; only charts do, and only recently. In the oldest atlas in the royal vault—one he had been permitted to consult precisely because no one thought it mattered anymore—he had found a fragment. Half a coastline, ink faded to the color of weak tea, labeled in a hand four centuries dead: *OSTRAVA, THE SALT REACH.* And beneath it, in the same hand, a note that had kept him awake for a week: *Here the maps forget themselves. Survey thrice. Trust the stones.*

So he had surveyed. Thrice. He had chartered a fishing boat under pretense of studying gull populations, sailed east into the confident blue, and found—nothing. No coastline. No islands. Open water where the old fragment promised cliffs and harbors and a country the size of a small kingdom. And yet the water itself was wrong: too shallow by half, according to his lead lines, shallow as a bay, shallow as a place where land had been *folded away* rather than drowned. He had sounded the depths for six days and drawn what he found, and when he came home and presented the map to the Royal Geographic Society, he had made the mistake of titling it honestly.

*Restoration of the Ostravan Coastline, Provisional.*

He remembered the silence in the hall. He remembered the president of the Society looking at the map the way a man looks at a spider in his soup. And he remembered—this was the part that still turned his stomach, three years on—that the president's eyes had gone briefly, terribly *afraid*, before the blankness closed over them like water over a dropped stone. Not anger. Fear. As if Bramble had shown him something the man had once known and begged the gods to forget.

The Gray Wardens had come for him within the hour. His instruments were confiscated. His maps were burned in the courtyard of the Society while the fellows watched with expressions of polite, vacant approval, and one young apprentice had wept without knowing why, wiping her cheeks and apologizing for the weather getting in her eyes.

Bramble had laughed at the trial. It had not helped his sentence. When the magistrate asked if he understood that Ostrava had never existed, Bramble had said, "Then you may hang me for mapping a fiction, but I should very much like to know who burned the fiction's name out of your mouth, sir, because it wasn't me." They had given him ten years and a small damp room, and he had spent the years doing what he always did: surveying. The crack in the wall. The arc of the light. The precise dimensions of forgetting.

And then, on a gray morning in the month the gulls went silent, someone opened his door who was not a warden.

* * *

There were three of them, and they came in like weather.

The first was a girl, sixteen perhaps, salt-seller's daughter written all over her chapped hands and practical boots, though her eyes moved through the cell the way his own eyes moved through a landscape—cataloguing exits, measuring distances. The second was a broad, worried-looking man who stayed near the door and looked like a man pretending very hard to be hauling salt. The third—

The third stopped Bramble's heart in his chest, or would have, if his heart had any dignity left.

A tall figure in a stolen coat, hood back, face pale as paper, and standing *wrong*—standing the way a person stands when they are listening to a voice no one else can hear. And behind them, faint as breath on cold glass, flickered another presence: a shimmer at the edge of vision, a suggestion of shoulders and a wry tilted head, there and not there.

Magic. In Vell. In a Warden-patrolled prison, magic, worn as openly as a scarf.

"You're either mad," Bramble said, "or this is an extremely elaborate execution."

"Neither," said the tall one. Their voice was rough, careful, the voice of someone who had practiced saying ordinary things. "We need a cartographer. We need *you*. You mapped the coast after the erasure."

The word went through him like a sounding line striking bottom. *Erasure.* Not "the nonexistent country." Not "your delusion." Erasure—as in something done. Something with a before and an after.

"Who told you that word?" he whispered.

The girl glanced at the tall one. "Can we do this here? The warden rounds come every quarter hour. We timed it."

"We timed it," the broad man confirmed miserably. "I want it noted that I objected to all of this."

"Noted," said the girl. She crouched in front of Bramble's cot, and up close he saw she carried herself like someone much older wearing a younger body badly, or perhaps exactly right. "Mister Bramble. My name's Ada. This is my father. This is—" she hesitated, "—Cyrus. And Cyrus is being followed by a ghost named Wren, who died carrying a map to a door we think is in the country you got imprisoned for finding. Are you following all of this?"

"No," Bramble said honestly. "Continue."

From the tall one's shoulder, the air shivered, and a voice came—thin, doubled, like sound heard through both a wall and an open window. "Hello, Aldous. You won't remember me. Wren Callas. I sat second row in your lecture on tidal cartography, the year before the Society expelled you. You drew a coastline from memory that you'd never surveyed, and when they asked how, you said the coast had *told* you." A pause, and something like a smile in the voice. "I believed you then. I believe you now."

Bramble's hands had begun to shake. He pressed them flat against his knees, an old habit, steadying the instrument of himself. "You're dead," he said.

"Recently."

"And you're asking me to break out of a Warden prison to sail toward a country that has been—" he made himself say it, made himself hold the word in his mouth like a hot coal, "—erased. To find a door. Based on a dead man's map."

"Yes," said Cyrus.

"It's the worst plan I've ever heard," Bramble said. "It's magnificent. What's the map?"

Ada produced it from inside her coat with the reverence of a relic-bearer. Salt-crusted leather, stained white at the folds, and when Bramble took it—when his fingers touched it—he made a sound he hadn't made since childhood, some small involuntary noise of recognition, because he knew this vellum. Knew the weight of it, the tooth of it, the particular way it held ink. Survey-grade. Royal issue. There had been maybe forty sheets of this stock in the whole kingdom, reserved for the Crown's own surveyors.

And the map itself—

He spread it across his knees and forgot to breathe.

Roads. Roads he knew, roads he had walked, rendered in a sure fine hand—but wrong, all wrong, shifted eastward into the empty blue, running along coastlines that existed nowhere in any living atlas. Harbors marked with soundings. Towns named in a script that made his eyes sting: *Sallowmere. Ternharrow. The Nine Stairs.* Names that surfaced in him like drowned bodies rising, names he had not known he knew until he saw them written and felt the terrible click of recognition, the lock turning on a door in himself he'd sealed without remembering sealing it.

"There's a waypoint here," Ada said, tapping a mark near the coast. "Wren says the first one. But we need someone who can read the route, because the land between here and there—" she hesitated, glancing at Cyrus, "—does things. To memory. We've seen the start of it already."

"The land eats travelers," the ghost said softly. "I told them. They didn't quite believe me."

"I believe you," Bramble said, and his voice came out strange, thin, reverent. Because he did. Six days of impossible soundings, and a lifetime of watching intelligent people smile their milk-smiles, and a wall covered in charcoal sun-lines scraped away twice by men who didn't know why they were scraping—all of it, all of it, suddenly arranged itself into a single coherent chart, and the chart was horrifying.

He looked up at them. "Do you understand what was done?" he asked. "Truly? Not hidden. Not sunk. Not warded behind mists that a clever hero might part. Do you understand?"

"Tell us," Cyrus said.

Bramble rose, joints cracking, and went to the wall—to the corner where, beneath three layers of scraped-away charcoal, his oldest survey survived, the one even the wardens had stopped bothering with. He wet his thumb and rubbed. Faint lines woke under the grime: the cell, yes, but also, radiating outward, a diagram he had drawn in the dark months of his first year, when the silence in his head had grown unbearable and he had needed to put the shape of the absence somewhere he could see it.

"This is Vell," he said, touching one point. "This is the coast. And this—" his finger traced the wide courteous blue, "—is where every map, every ledger, every mind in this country agrees there is nothing. Now look at the shipping lanes. Look at the trade roads. They don't avoid a hazard. Hazards get marked; sailors are warned about hazards. These routes bend around a *blank*, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. Every road, every record, every memory bends the same way. Do you know how much agreement that takes? How much *force*?"

No one spoke. The ghost had gone very still, which Bramble suspected was a considerable achievement for a ghost.

"A country isn't erased by hiding it," he said quietly. "Hiding leaves seams. Someone remembers a song, a grandmother's recipe, a border stone in a farmer's field. Hiding leaks. This doesn't leak. This is—" he searched for the word and hated the only one that fit, "—this is *unmaking*. Someone reached into the memory of the world itself and pulled the country out by the roots. Not just our records. The world's. The sea forgot its own depth. The roads forgot where they went. People who were born there—" his voice caught, and he was furious at it, furious, because he had sworn not to weep in front of strangers, "—people born there walk around with a hole shaped like home and call it indigestion. I have watched scholars stand in front of proof and slide off it like rain off oilcloth. It isn't denial. Denial knows what it's refusing. This is *absence*, wearing a person's face."

Ada's father made a low troubled sound. "That's not possible," he said, without conviction, in the voice of a man reciting a prayer he no longer believed in.

"No," Bramble agreed. "It isn't. And it happened anyway."

He turned back to them, and here was the thing he had waited three years to say, rehearsed against the wall in charcoal, the vindication he had imagined delivering to the Society, to the tribunal, to the smug president with his afraid-then-blank eyes:

"I was right. I want that understood. Everything I said, everything they burned—I was *right*, and I will die knowing the shape of the truth if nothing else—"

And then the triumph curdled in his mouth, mid-word, because he saw their faces. The girl's steady, frightened eyes. The tall one's exhausted resolve. The ghost's sorrow like frost on glass. And he understood, all at once, what being right purchased him: not restoration, not apology, not his instruments returned and his name restored. Just a seat on the worst voyage ever attempted, toward a country that had been devoured by something with the power to eat *memory itself*—something that had, presumably, eaten it once and could eat it again, and might not appreciate guests poking at the bones.

Vindication, he discovered, tasted exactly like dread. Like salt and iron. Like standing on a headland you've finally proven exists, while the tide comes in behind you faster than any tide should.

"Well," he said, and heard himself laugh, a dry cracked sound with no humor in it whatsoever. "Well. You'll need soundings, tide tables, and someone who can navigate by stars that may or may not be where they ought to be. None of which I can do from in here."

"How do we get you out?" Ada asked.

Bramble looked at the door, at the corridor beyond where boots rang on stone in their patient quarter-hour rhythm, and then at the shimmer that was Wren, and last at Cyrus—who was watching him with an expression he recognized from his own mirror, the look of a person carrying something enormous and pretending it was luggage.

"I assume," Bramble said, "you have a catastrophically bad idea. You seem the type."

"He does," said the ghost, and there was definitely a smile in it now.

"Then," said Aldous Bramble, disgraced Royal Cartographer, the man who had mapped the blank and been right, reaching for the coat Ada held out to him with hands that would not entirely stop shaking, "let us proceed. But I warn you all—if we reach this Door of yours, I am drawing it. Whatever it costs. Some things must be *charted*, or they happen again."

Above the prison, through the high barred window, the gulls had gone silent, and the sky over the eastern sea was the color of paper waiting to be written on—and far out past the harbor bar, where every chart said there was nothing, the water lay shallow as a held breath, keeping, for now, its folded secret.
