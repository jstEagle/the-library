## 7. The First Forgetting

The river forgot them first.

Cyrus noticed it on the third morning past the last Warden checkpoint, when the barge slid into a stretch of water so still it looked poured rather than fallen. The current simply stopped. Not slowed—stopped, as if the river had reached the edge of its own remembering and declined to go further. Ada's father poled them through it in silence, his jaw set, sweat running down into his collar despite the cold, and when they emerged on the far side and the current caught them again with a gentle lurch, no one spoke for a long while.

Cyrus sat at the bow with the map spread across their knees, watching the ink behave strangely. Roads that had run straight the day before now curved. A bridge marked at Kiln's Crossing had migrated two miles downstream overnight, and the little cartographer's note beside it—"sound footing, toll one copper"—had faded to a ghost of itself, legible only if Cyrus tilted the paper against the light.

*It's not the map,* Wren's voice murmured from somewhere behind Cyrus's eyes, that familiar pressure like a second heartbeat. *Maps don't change. The country does.*

"Then we're following a country that won't hold still," Cyrus said aloud, and immediately regretted it, because Ada looked up from coiling rope and her father's pole hesitated mid-stroke.

"You talk to yourself," Ada observed. She said it without judgment, the way she seemed to say most things—all salt and directness, fourteen years old and sharper than any blade on the barge.

"I do," Cyrus agreed. It was easier than explaining the dead man living in their memory, the way he flickered at the edges of their vision like heat-shimmer, stronger here than he had ever been in Vell. Here, among stones that predated the erasure, Wren was almost solid. Sometimes Cyrus could see the outline of him standing at the rail—a lean man with river-gray eyes and a habit of looking at horizons like they owed him money.

Here, too, the land had begun to do what Bramble had warned them it would do.

They'd collected Bramble from his cell in the Cartographers' Gaol six days ago, a disgraced royal mapmaker with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like dry paper tearing. He'd spent three years imprisoned for the crime of mapping a country that officially did not exist, and he had come aboard the barge clutching his confiscated atlases to his chest like rescued children. Now he stood at the stern most mornings, taking bearings with instruments that gave him different answers each time, and each morning his face grew a little grayer.

"The boundary region," he'd called it, the night before, tapping the place where Ostrava should have been—the blank space on every map, the absence shaped like a wound. "We're crossing into it now. Everything past this line is contested territory. Not politically." He'd laughed his terrible laugh. "Contested by reality."

* * *

It began small.

At noon they stopped to eat hard bread and pickled fish, and Ada's father, Tomas, told one of his endless stories about Ada's mother—how she'd once talked a customs officer out of seizing their entire cargo by praising his hat for twenty unbroken minutes. Ada laughed in the right places. But afterward she came and sat beside Cyrus at the bow, hugging her knees, and was quiet in a way that didn't suit her.

"Cyrus." Her voice was careful, the way a person's voice is careful when they're walking on ice. "My mother. What did she look like?"

Cyrus turned. "You're asking me?"

"She died when I was six." Ada's chin was up, defiant, but her hands were white-knuckled around her own wrists. "I know her voice. I know she smelled like brine and cardamom. I know she could name every fish in the estuary. But her face—" Ada's breath hitched. "I closed my eyes just now, trying to picture her telling off that customs officer, and there's nothing. There's a shape where her face should be. Like someone pressed their thumb into wet clay."

Cyrus's stomach dropped. They thought of the erasure, of a whole country scraped out of the world's memory—and of how erasure, like water, must find its level. Must spread.

"It might be nothing," Cyrus said, and tasted the lie on their own tongue. "Grief does strange things."

"Grief doesn't take *selectively*." Ada stood, brushing bread crumbs from her skirt with sharp, angry motions. "I've grieved her my whole life. Her face stayed put through all of it. Something took it, and it took it today, on this stretch of river, and don't you dare tell me otherwise because I can taste lies, Cyrus Marchetti, and yours taste like burnt sugar."

She stalked aft to her father. Cyrus watched her go, watched her study Tomas's face with an intensity that made the old man frown and ask what was wrong, watched her shake her head and say *nothing, Papa, nothing*, and the lie hung in the air between them, sweet and terrible.

*It's starting,* Wren said quietly. He had come to stand—or to seem to stand—at the rail beside Cyrus, close enough that if Cyrus squinted, the light bent around him into something almost like shoulders. *This is what the boundary does. It doesn't kill you. It eats you a bite at a time. Small things first. Faces. Words. Then names. Then—*

"Then everything," Cyrus finished.

*Then everything.* Wren's gaze, if a ghost's gaze could be said to fall anywhere, rested on Ada. *I told you the price before you left Vell. I told you the road would cost.*

"You told me the Door would cost. You didn't tell me the *road* would start collecting early."

Wren said nothing to that. In five years of carrying his memory—in however many months it had been since his death pressed a salt-crusted map into Cyrus's bleeding hands—Cyrus had learned that Wren's silences were load-bearing. He went quiet exactly when the truth got heavy.

* * *

Bramble lost his mother tongue at dusk.

They had tied up for the night in the lee of a ruined watchtower—one of the old Ostravan border-stones, though none of them had known that's what it was until Wren stirred in Cyrus's memory like a dog catching a scent and said, *there, that's a mile-marker from the Salt Road, I used to pass it every autumn*. The stone was black basalt, older than the moss on it, carved with letters worn nearly smooth. Bramble had spent an hour of fading daylight copying those letters into his journal, tracing them reverently, whispering to himself.

"It's Old Ostravan," he'd said, wonder cracking his voice. "Do you understand? This is a *primary source*. This is a written language that supposedly never existed, sitting right here in the mud, and I am the first man in five years to—" He'd stopped. Blinked. Looked down at his own notes.

"Bramble?" Ada asked.

He held up the journal page. The letters were still there, neat and precise in his draftsman's hand. But his face had gone slack with confusion, the particular confusion of a man reading his native language and finding it suddenly foreign.

"What is this?" he said. His accent had changed—thinner, flatter, the vowels of the capital instead of the rolling burr of the northern provinces he'd grown up in. "What language—I copied it, obviously, I copy things, it's what I *do*, but I can't—" He looked up at them, and there was real fear in his eyes now, the fear of a scholar whose mind had been his fortress. "Why can't I read it? I could read it an hour ago. I could read it my *whole life*. My grandmother sang in it. She sang—" He stopped again, groping. "There was a song. About the sea. I knew every word."

He didn't know any words. They could all see it. The song was gone, and the language with it, scooped out of him clean as marrow from a bone.

Tomas built the fire higher. Ada sat beside Bramble and put her hand on his arm and didn't say anything, because there was nothing to say, and Cyrus stood apart with the wind coming off the blank water and felt the fear settle into their bones like cold working into a joint.

Because here was the thing Cyrus hadn't said aloud, couldn't say aloud: the same hour Bramble lost his language, Cyrus had reached for the memory of their mother's kitchen—the smell of rain on hot slate, the sound of her humming—and found it intact. Perfectly intact. Suspiciously intact. As if the land had reached for Cyrus too and found nothing it wanted. As if Cyrus were already partly forgotten, already mostly hollow, and there was nothing left worth eating.

Five years in Vell, where magic was outlawed and storm-callers were hunted and Cyrus Marchetti had buried their true self so deep even they sometimes couldn't find it. Five years of being no one, carefully, deliberately. The erasure had taken Ostrava from the world. Perhaps it had already taken most of Cyrus.

Perhaps that was why the land let them pass untouched while it picked at everyone else like gulls at a catch.

*You're thinking something grim,* Wren observed. He had drifted closer. Ghosts, Cyrus was learning, gravitated toward the living when the dark got deep.

"I'm thinking the land knows what I am."

*The land has always known what you are.* Wren's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. Gentle meant pity, and pity from a dead man who had hidden something—that specific something, the betrayal Cyrus could feel coiled in him like wire, always almost spoken—was unbearable. *That's why the map chose you. That's why I chose you.*

"You keep saying that. 'Chose.' Like it was a kindness."

*It was the only thing I had left to give.*

"That's not an answer, Wren."

*No,* he agreed, and faded toward the stern, and Cyrus was left alone with the firelight and the black stone and the sound of Bramble, very quietly, trying and failing to remember his grandmother's song.

* * *

They traveled two more days into the boundary region, and the forgetting walked with them like a fourth passenger.

It came in tides. That was the worst part—the rhythm of it, the way it swelled and receded. In the mornings things were mostly whole: faces, names, the order of the world. By afternoon the tide came in. Ada forgot which pocket she kept her knife in, a thing she'd known since she was seven. Tomas steered past a bend in the river he'd navigated forty times and felt, by his own shaken admission, like he was steering through a stranger's dream. Once, for ten full minutes, none of them could remember why they were on the river at all—they sat in the boat looking at each other with pleasant, empty smiles until the tide went out and horror crashed back in, all four of them speaking at once, checking each other's names like men counting fingers after a fall.

Cyrus started keeping a ledger. It seemed the only sane response: write everything down, anchor it in ink, make the outside world hold what the inside world couldn't. Every evening Cyrus recorded the day—names, faces, events, the route, the waypoints Wren guided them toward. And every evening Cyrus asked the others to read it back, to speak their own histories aloud, to rehearse themselves.

"Say your mother's name," Cyrus told Ada on the second evening, as the fire burned low.

"Maren Salt-Worker, of the Lower Estuary, born under a red moon, married Tomas when they were both seventeen, died of lung-rot when I was six." Ada recited it flatly, like scripture. Then, quieter: "It doesn't help. I can say the words and the words are just—words. Knowing her name isn't knowing her."

"No. But it's a thread. You follow threads back."

"To what? What's at the other end?" Ada hugged her knees. Firelight moved on her face. "What if we walk all this way and open this stupid Door and get Ostrava back, and I still can't remember my mother's face? What if the land ate it and it's just *gone*, swallowed into whatever hole swallowed a whole country, and no door anywhere gives it back?"

Cyrus had no answer. So instead they said the truest thing they had: "Then I'll remember her for you. Whatever you lose, tell me, and I'll carry it. That's what the ledger's for. It's not just mine. It's ours."

Ada looked at them for a long moment. Fourteen years old, salt-seller's daughter, half-key or half-lock or whatever she was becoming, and her eyes in the firelight were old as the black stone on the bank.

"You'd forget too, eventually," she said. "Wouldn't you. If the tide comes for you like it comes for us."

"Maybe."

"But you'd carry ours anyway. Right up until you dropped them."

"Yes."

Ada nodded slowly, and something in her shoulders loosened—not trust, exactly, not yet, but the decision to move in trust's direction. "All right," she said. "Then here's the first one. My mother's face. I'm giving it to you to hold, even though you never saw it, even though neither of us knows what it looks like anymore. Hold the *space* of it. Don't let the hole fill in with anything else."

"I will," Cyrus said, and wrote it in the ledger by firelight: *Ada's mother's face — held for Ada — do not let the hole fill in.*

From the stern, faintly, Bramble had begun to hum. No words—those were gone—but a melody, stubborn and half-remembered, rising and falling like the sea it was sung about. His grandmother's song, surviving as pure music after the language had been eaten out from under it. Ada listened. Tomas listened. Even the river seemed to listen, running quiet beneath the hull.

And Cyrus sat with the ledger on their knees and understood, finally, the true shape of the journey ahead. Not Warden patrols. Not the ambushes and checkpoints they'd slipped past like smoke. The real enemy was this: a country-sized hunger, patient as erosion, that fed on everything they loved a mouthful at a time. They could outrun soldiers. They could not outrun forgetting. All they could do was carry each other's losses, pile them onto the barge like cargo, and pray the boat held.

*One more thing,* Wren said, so suddenly that Cyrus flinched. He had come back. He stood at the rail looking north, toward the dark line of hills where the river narrowed, and his voice had gone strange—taut, urgent, afraid in a way Cyrus had never heard from him. Not the fear of dying; he'd done that already. The fear of a man approaching a debt.

*Cyrus. Tomorrow, past those hills, there's a ferry crossing. Old Salt Road. And at the crossing—*

He stopped. The wire-coiled secret in him pulled tight enough that Cyrus felt it in their own chest, a sympathetic ache, like pain shared through a wall.

*At the crossing,* Wren said carefully, *you'll want to be awake before dawn. Trust me. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever you see, whoever finds you—remember that I chose you, and that choosing you was the last honest thing I ever did.*

"Wren," Cyrus said slowly, "what's at the crossing?"

But the ghost was already fading, thinning into the dark above the water, and the last thing Cyrus saw of him was his face turned away—ashamed, the way a man looks away when he knows the bill is coming due.

North, past the black hills, the river ran on toward a crossing none of them could see yet.

Something was waiting there.
