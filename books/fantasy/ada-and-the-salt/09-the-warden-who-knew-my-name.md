## 9. The Warden Who Knew My Name

The ferry crossing at Hollowmere was the kind of place that existed only because two roads had nowhere else to go. A rope strung between leaning pilings, a flat-bottomed barge that groaned like an old man kneeling, and a gray stretch of water too wide to swim and too slow to freeze. On the far bank, a Warden checkpoint squatted in the mist like a toad—two lanterns, a toll table, and, this morning, a figure in slate-gray standing with her hands clasped behind her back as if she had been waiting for them since before dawn.

Cyrus saw her from the near shore and felt the old cold slide down their spine.

*Warden.*

Not one of the patrol-kind, the young ones who checked papers and kicked over market stalls looking for charms. This one stood the way stone stands. Her coat was darker than the others', cut close, silver braid at the collar gone dull with weather. She did not pace. She did not stamp against the cold. She simply watched the water, and Cyrus understood, with the certainty of a man who has spent five years learning which silhouettes mean death, that she was watching for *them*.

"Captain," Bramble said quietly. He had seen her too. His hands, always restless around his maps, went still. "That's Rehn. Ilsa Rehn. They use her portrait in the recruitment broadsheets." He swallowed. "She doesn't miss."

Ada's father, Oskar, spat thoughtfully over the side of the barge. "Then we don't cross today."

"We crossed yesterday's checkpoints because they didn't know to look," Ada said. Her voice was low, practical, but Cyrus could taste the fear coming off her—not literally, not the way she tasted lies, but in the way her jaw set, the way her fingers found the salt pouch at her belt and worried it like a rosary. "This one knows to look."

Wren stirred in the back of Cyrus's skull, a pressure like a headache forming behind the eyes. *She's from the ferry garrison at Vell's north gate,* the ghost said, and his voice had that strange doubled quality it took on when he was frightened—which was absurd, because what could frighten a dead man? *I know her face. I knew her face before I died. Cyrus, I knew her face before—*

"Before what?" Cyrus murmured under their breath.

But Wren had gone silent again, the way he did whenever the erasure pressed too hard on whatever remained of him. Five years of memory, worn down to channels and coastlines. Sometimes Cyrus wondered if the ghost remembered anything whole anymore, or only fragments, sharp as broken glass: a door, a betrayal, a name he couldn't say.

They waited through the morning in the reeds downstream, watching traffic cross. Salt carts. A funeral party. Two pilgrims who were turned back for lack of papers. The Captain never moved except to turn pilgrims back herself, and once—this Cyrus noted with a hunter's attention—to take a cup of tea from the ferryman without ever letting her gaze leave the far bank. As though the far bank were the thing that mattered. As though something might come out of the mist there that she needed to see.

"She's not checking travelers," Ada said at midday, when they'd eaten cold bread and Oskar had dozed against a willow. "She's waiting for someone specific."

"She's waiting for us," Bramble said. "Word came down after the storm." He glanced at Cyrus, and there was no accusation in it, only the weary arithmetic of a man who had done the same sum himself. "You called lightning out of a clear sky over the Shallows. Wardens three provinces away felt that. There'll be descriptions now. A storm-caller traveling with a salt-seller's daughter and a disgraced mapmaker. We're not travelers anymore. We're a *story*, and stories travel faster than barges."

Cyrus said nothing. The vow sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. Five years without calling so much as a breeze to cool their neck. Five years of watching rain fall on Vell and feeling it fall *through* them, indifferent, unclaimed. And then Ada in the water, the flats rising black and hungry, and their hands had moved before their pride could stop them—and the sky had answered the way it always answered, joyfully, terribly, like a dog that has waited its whole life at a door.

They would do it again. That was the worst part. Given the same moment, they would tear the vow open a second time, and a third, and every time after, and each time the sky would remember them a little more loudly.

"We could go around," Oskar offered without conviction. Everyone knew there was no around. The Hollowmere crossing was the last bridge over the last deep river before the boundary country began—the country that forgot. Beyond it, the maps went soft and the roads stopped agreeing with themselves. If they meant to reach the Salt Door, they crossed here, under the Captain's lanterns, or they did not cross at all.

It was Ada who found the way in, because it was always Ada. She had been studying the checkpoint through Bramble's spyglass for an hour, and now she lowered it slowly, frowning.

"There's something wrong with her," she said.

"Everything about her is wrong with us," Bramble muttered.

"No. I mean—" Ada handed the glass to Cyrus. "Look at her hands."

Cyrus raised the spyglass. The Captain stood at the toll table, checking a wool merchant's manifest, and her movements were precise, economical, correct. But her left hand—her left hand kept moving. While her right hand worked, her left had drifted up to her collarbone and was pressing there, absently, rhythmically, the way a person presses a bruise they've forgotten the origin of. Pressing, and then rubbing the pad of her thumb across her knuckles, over and over, as though trying to recall a texture.

And on her wrist, half-hidden by her cuff, Cyrus saw the glint of something small and white.

A salt charm. Old style. Ostravan.

The world tilted very slightly, the way a barge tilts when a passenger shifts their weight without meaning to.

"I know that charm," Cyrus heard themselves say. Their voice sounded far away. "My grandmother tied ones like it. You braid the cord left-over-right and seal the knot with a drop of seawater so it never fully dries. It's a *homecoming* charm. You wear it so you can find your way back to—" They stopped.

Back to a country that no longer existed. Back to a coastline scrubbed from every atlas, a language unspoken, a people who woke one morning five years ago with a hole in their hearts shaped like home and no memory of what had carved it.

*Oh,* thought Cyrus. *Oh, no. Oh, you poor woman.*

Because Cyrus remembered. That was the curse of being the exile who fled rather than the exile who stayed—Cyrus remembered everything. The harbor at Solenne with its green-lit water. The storm-bells ringing in triplets. Their mother's kitchen smelling of brine and bread. Every street of every city of a nation that the rest of the world had been made to forget. Cyrus carried Ostrava entire inside their skull, alone, a library of one, and had spent five years pretending the weight of it was just grief.

But this woman on the far bank—this woman had been made to forget. And some part of her, some animal part below the surgery of the erasure, was still pressing at the scar.

"We're going to cross," Cyrus said.

Bramble stared. "Have you been drinking river water?"

"She's not going to arrest us."

"You've met Wardens, yes? Professionally? They have one setting, and it's *arrest*?"

"She's wearing a homecoming charm she doesn't understand." Cyrus lowered the spyglass. Their heart was hammering, and not entirely from fear. There was something else underneath, something terrible and tender, the feeling of recognizing a stranger's face in a foreign city and knowing, without knowing how, that they were from the same drowned place. "Ada. When we get close. Tell me what her words taste like."

Ada looked at Cyrus for a long moment. She was the only one among them who could hear lies on the air, taste falsehood the way other people tasted smoke—and Cyrus had learned to trust that gift more than any blade. Whatever she saw in Cyrus's face made her nod slowly.

"All right," she said. "But if she puts us in irons, I'm telling my father it was your idea."

"It was absolutely your idea," Oskar said, waking. "I heard it. I want that recorded."

* * *

The crossing took twenty minutes and lasted a year.

Oskar poled them out into the current with the unhurried competence of a man hauling salt, because that was what he was doing, officially, and the manifest said so, and the manifest had survived four checkpoints already by being exactly boring enough. Ada sat amidships with her hands folded. Bramble had buried himself under the canvas with the salt sacks, on the theory that a disgraced royal cartographer was the second-most-recognizable fugitive aboard. And Cyrus stood at the bow, exposed, hatless, letting the mist bead in their hair, watching the gray figure resolve into a person.

Up close, Warden-Captain Ilsa Rehn was younger than her reputation. Lines at the corners of her mouth, yes, and a scar through one eyebrow, but beneath the slate-gray authority there was a face that might once have laughed easily. Her eyes were pale hazel, and they fixed on Cyrus the moment the barge touched the pilings, and they did not soften, and they did not waver.

"Manifest," she said.

Oskar handed it over. She read it without reading it. Her gaze never left Cyrus.

"Salt haulage, southbound. You're not southbound cargo."

"We're delivering to the boundary depots," Oskar said, easy as breathing. The lie was smooth enough that even Ada didn't flinch, which meant Oskar had found some thread of technical truth to wrap it in. "Contract work. The depots pay double for men willing to run the gray water."

"The depots are abandoned."

"They pay double for that too."

Something flickered at the corner of the Captain's mouth. Not quite amusement. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow professional performing a role. She stepped onto the barge, boots ringing on the deck, and walked past Oskar, past Ada, and stopped in front of Cyrus.

Up close, Cyrus could see the salt charm clearly now. The cord was frayed. The knot had been sealed with seawater long ago and had dried anyway, years ago, and someone—Rehn herself, probably, in some hour she didn't let herself examine—had retied it badly, left-over-*left*, the wrong way, the way a person mends a thing whose rules they've forgotten.

"Your papers," Rehn said.

Cyrus handed them over. Forged in Vell, good paper, good seals. Rehn read them properly this time, and Cyrus watched her read, and watched the exact moment her eyes caught on the name and something behind them stuttered.

*Cyrus Marchetti.* The false name. The safe name, the invented one, chosen five years ago from a list of names that belonged to no one.

Rehn's thumb moved. Once, twice, across her knuckles. Pressing the bruise.

"This name," she said quietly. "Where did you get it?"

"A priest in Vell suggested it," Cyrus said. "Said it suited me."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I'm answering."

Ada made a small sound. Cyrus glanced at her. She was staring at the Captain with an expression Cyrus had never seen on her before—not fear, not calculation, but a kind of horrified compassion, the look of someone tasting something vast and rotten in the air and understanding that the rot was grief.

"Captain," Ada said softly. "That's not your real name either, is it? Rehn."

The silence that followed had weight. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out and was not answered.

"You're under suspicion of unlicensed thaumaturgy," Rehn said, and her voice had gone flat, recited, a litany holding her upright. "Storm-calling. You'll come with me to the garrison at—"

"At where?" Cyrus asked gently. "What garrison? What town is this? Look at the signpost, Captain. Go on. Look at it."

There was a signpost at the landing, old and bleached, and everyone had walked past it without reading it because the letters refused to hold. That was the boundary country beginning its work early, or perhaps it was something older than the boundary, something leaking backward along the roads toward the Door. Rehn looked. Cyrus watched her look. Watched her stare at the signpost for four full seconds, five, six, her lips moving, her brow furrowing, her hand drifting up to press that spot below her collarbone.

"I know this crossing," she said. The flatness cracked, just slightly. "I've stood here before. Before my commission. I stood—" Her thumb stopped moving. "Why do I know this crossing?"

"Because you grew up forty miles east of here," Cyrus said. "In a town called Solenne, on a harbor where the water glows green at night. There was a bell tower with nine bells, and they rang in triplets when storms came, and every child in Solenne could tell you which triplet meant *come inside* and which meant *bring the boats up the sand*." Their voice was steady. Inside, they were shaking apart. "You wore a homecoming charm braided left-over-right, sealed with seawater. Someone gave it to you. I'd guess your mother. I'd guess you can't remember her face, but you remember her hands tying it, because the erasure took the pictures first and left the hands."

Rehn had gone very still. The kind of stillness that precedes either violence or collapse.

"You're lying," she said. "This is a trick. Storm-callers lie the way fish swim."

"Ask her," Cyrus said, nodding at Ada. "She can taste it. Ask her if I'm lying."

And Rehn—God help her, and God help them all—did. She turned to the salt-seller's daughter standing on a salt barge, and she asked, in a voice stripped of all command, all rank, all five years of slate-gray certainty: "Is he lying?"

"He isn't," Ada said. "Not one word. Captain—I'm sorry. Not one word of it tastes false."

*He.* Cyrus let it pass. Let her hear whatever she needed to hear.

Rehn's hand closed around the salt charm at her wrist, and she held it the way drowning people hold driftwood, and Cyrus watched five years of enforced forgetting collide with five minutes of unbearable remembering. Her breath came short. Her eyes had gone bright and unfocused, staring through Cyrus at something forty miles east and five years gone—a harbor, a bell tower, hands tying a knot.

"There was a girl," she whispered. "On the harbor wall. She used to call the wind to dry our nets, just a little, just enough, and her mother scolded her, and we all pretended not to see because the fish came in fat those seasons. She had—" Rehn's voice failed. Restarted. "I can't see her face. I can't see her *face*. Why can't I see her face?"

Because the girl had been Cyrus. Because the erasure had taken Cyrus out of every mind in Ostrava the day Cyrus fled, the price of survival, the wound that walking away had cut into everyone who had ever known them. Because Ilsa Rehn had stood on the harbor wall of Solenne and loved—or feared, or simply knew—a storm-caller whose name had been paid as the toll of exile, and all that remained to her was the shape of the absence.

"You knew me," Cyrus said. "Before. That's why you couldn't stop watching the far bank. That's why you've been pressing that charm for three days. Some part of you has been waiting for me to come back through this crossing since the day I left, and neither of us knew it until right now."

The lanterns swung in the wind off the water. Behind them, Oskar had quietly begun untying the mooring line, and Bramble's head had emerged from under the canvas like a wary turtle's, taking in the scene with the expression of a man revising a map in real time.

Rehn's hand dropped to her hip. To the irons hanging there. Cyrus did not move. Ada did not move. The whole world narrowed to the space between the Captain's hand and the chains, and the space between the Captain's heart and a country that had been stolen out of it.

"If I arrest you," Rehn said hoarsely, "I go back to a garrison in a town whose name won't stay written, wearing a charm I can't explain, and I forget this conversation by morning. Won't I? It leaks. It all leaks. I've *felt* it leaking for years and I told myself it was fatigue." She laughed, one broken note. "Five years I've served the thing that hollowed me. Do you know what that makes me?"

"Fellow exile," Cyrus said. "Same as me. Same as all of us. We just took different roads out of the same burning house."

For a long moment, Warden-Captain Ilsa Rehn stood on the deck of a salt barge with her hand on her irons and her mother's knot biting into her wrist, and chose.

She stepped back. Her hand left the irons. When she spoke again, the command had returned to her voice, but it was a mask now, and all four of them knew it, and she knew they knew.

"Manifest's in order," she said loudly, for the benefit of the ferryman and the mist and whatever reports might be read later. "Cargo's salt. Move along. And you—" her pale eyes found Cyrus's, and in them was a message no report would ever carry— "*keep your head down, storm-caller.*"

The barge pushed off. The mist took the landing, the lanterns, the gray coat, the woman standing in it with her fist closed around a dried-out charm, watching them go the way people watch the last ship leaving a drowning coast.

None of them spoke until the far bank had dissolved entirely.

"She let us go," Bramble said finally, wonderingly. "The Rehn. She just—let us go."

"She's coming after us," Ada said. Cyrus looked at her. "I tasted it. Under the orders, under the mask. When she said *move along*—underneath that, she was saying something else. Something true." Ada's hands had gone still on her salt pouch. "She said, *wait for me at the Door.*"

In the back of Cyrus's skull, Wren stirred at last, and his voice, when it came, was thin and strange and afraid.

*I know why she seemed familiar,* the ghost said. *Cyrus—I need to tell you what I did. Before we reach the Door. Because she isn't the only one who forgot what she was.*

*And mine is worse.*
