## 10. Salt on the Tongue

Ada had always known the taste of a lie.

It was not a flavor exactly—more like weather in the mouth, a change of pressure behind the teeth. Truth tasted clean, like well water or the first bite of bread. Lies came in varieties her father had never believed when she tried to describe them: the sour green tang of small cowardice, the copper bite of cruelty, and worst of all the sweet ones, the lies told out of love, which coated the tongue like honey gone to wax.

She had tasted Cyrus Marchetti's lie the first hour she met them.

*"Marchetti,"* they'd said, and the name had sat wrong on the air between them, sweet as candied figs, thick enough to chew. Ada had said nothing then. She'd been raised on the docks of Vell's salt quarter, where a girl who announced what she could taste would quickly find herself friendless, feared, or worse—useful, which was its own kind of terrible. So she'd held the sweetness on her tongue like a stone under her tongue and watched the storm-caller with new eyes.

But that had been weeks ago, before the river, before Bramble and his maps that bled at the edges, before Wren's ghost began to flicker in Cyrus's memory like a lamp running low on oil. Before the First Forgetting, when Ada had looked at her own father across the barge's deck and—for one horrible, endless moment—not known his face.

That was the thing that had finally done it. Not the danger, not the Wardens, not the way the land ahead of them grew blanker every day. It was standing on her father's barge and feeling, for the space of three heartbeats, that he was a stranger with kind eyes.

She could not keep carrying this alone anymore. The secret had grown too heavy, and the road too strange, and if the land was going to eat pieces of her, she wanted—she *needed*—someone else to know what those pieces were worth.

* * *

She waited for evening, because some things were easier said in the dark. They had made camp in the lee of a ruined tollhouse, one of the last structures marked on any map, though Bramble swore the ink around it had begun to fade since they'd passed it going the other direction. A fire burned low. Her father snored gently beneath the barge's spare sail, dragged up the bank for shelter. Bramble sat cross-legged with his atlas open on his knees, redrawing by firelight a coastline that refused to hold still.

Cyrus sat apart, as always, sharpening a knife they almost never used, watching the dark beyond the firelight the way sailors watch for weather.

Ada wrapped her arms around her knees and looked into the flames until they blurred.

"I need to tell you something," she said. "And I need you to let me finish before you say anything. Either of you."

Bramble looked up, quill hovering. Cyrus went still in that particular way of theirs—the stillness of someone who has spent five years learning how not to be noticed—and turned toward her fully.

"You already know I can taste lies," Ada said. "I told you that much, back on the river, when you bought your passage with the truth about your name." She paused. "Which is a lie, by the way. Has been from the start. Marchetti. Sweet as syrup. I've been chewing it for weeks."

Cyrus's face did something complicated—shame and relief tangled together, neither winning. "I know," they said quietly. "I've been waiting for you to say it."

"Well. That's the small part." Ada made herself breathe. The fire popped; somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through grass that might not exist tomorrow. "Here's the large part. My mother didn't die birthing me in Vell. My parents weren't even from Vell. My father will tell you we're from upriver, salt merchants for six generations, and that's true the way a coat is true—it covers something." She glanced toward the sleeping shape under the sail. "He doesn't know I know. Or maybe he does and we've just agreed, all these years, never to look directly at it."

"Ada," Bramble said carefully, "what are you telling us?"

"My mother went into labor on the road," Ada said. "Not near any town. Near a door."

The word dropped into the firelight and seemed to change the weight of the air.

"She never told my father where they were, exactly. Only that it was white stone, and that the sea was on the wrong side of them, and that she was bleeding before the baby ever came. She died on the threshold. And I—" Ada's voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway, the way you push through a door that sticks. "I was born there. On it. Half of me on one side and half of me on the other, according to the story my father tells when he's drunk enough to tell any of it. He says the midwife—a woman who appeared out of nowhere and vanished after, who he swears wasn't real—cut the cord and said, *'She'll taste everything and be tasted by everything,'* and then she was gone."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"And you can taste lies," Bramble said slowly. His quill had stopped moving entirely. "Because you were born—"

"On the threshold of whatever that door is," Ada finished. "Yes. I didn't understand it, growing up. I thought everyone tasted lies a little, the way everyone tastes salt in the sea air, and most people just ignored it. It wasn't until I was nine and a man came through the market selling shares in a ship that didn't exist, and I spat at his feet in front of forty people, that I learned otherwise." She smiled without any humor in it. "My father moved us to a different district that winter."

Cyrus had not spoken. They were looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—or rather, with an expression she could read too many ways at once, hope and horror fighting for the same ground.

"Say it," Ada said. "Whatever you're thinking. Say it out loud so I don't have to carry it alone."

"The Salt Door," Cyrus said. The words came out rough, like stones pulled from a riverbed. "Wren's map leads to a place called the Salt Door. White stone. A seam in a cliff. We're following it right now." They swallowed. "You were born there, Ada. You were born *on our destination.*"

"Half-key, half-lock," Bramble murmured. He said it absently, the way scholars say things, reaching for a phrase the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood. Then his head snapped up and the color drained out of his face. "No. No, wait. I've read the old survey fragments—the ones they burned me for collecting. Threshold-born. There are stories, folk stories mostly, dismissed as superstition, but the phrasing recurs across three centuries and four languages: *the door opens to its own blood.* Doors like that aren't passages, they're— they're locks with opinions. They choose. And the only key a threshold recognizes is itself."

"What are you saying?" Ada asked, though the cold spreading through her chest already knew.

Bramble looked at her with an apology already forming in his eyes, and that was the worst part, worse than anything he could actually say. "I'm saying that if the Salt Door needs opening—if it can be opened at all—it may not open to force, or to storm-calling, or to any craft I know. It may open to you. To your blood, spilled on the stone that first heard you."

* * *

Afterward, Ada walked down to the waterline alone.

It wasn't that she'd fled, exactly. She'd simply stood up, and no one had stopped her, and the dark had been waiting there like an old friend with bad news. Behind her the fire crackled and Bramble's voice rose and fell in low, urgent conversation with Cyrus—she caught fragments, *threshold-born*, *the survey fragments*, *we can't ask this of her*—and she was grateful they were talking about her instead of to her. Sometimes that was easier.

The water lapped at the shingle. Out past the shallows, the sea did the thing it had been doing more and more often as they traveled east: it forgot itself. Waves rolled in without rhythm, without pattern, arriving at the shore like strangers unsure why they'd come. Once, Ada could have sworn she saw a wave hesitate halfway up the beach, hang there trembling, and slide back as if embarrassed.

She crouched and touched the water. Cold. Real. For now.

*Half-key, half-lock.*

Her whole life, the tasting had been a burden she'd learned to carry like a hunchback carries a spine—you build your posture around it, you make jokes, you get on with things. It had cost her friends. It had cost her suitors, two of whom had run when she told them, one of whom had stayed and then spent years asking her, casually, constantly, whether his business partners were honest, until she'd left him too. It had made her father protective in a way that felt like being kept in a jar.

But it had always been *hers*. A private affliction. A private gift. Something that belonged to her the way her crooked left ring finger belonged to her, from the fall off the mast when she was seven.

Now Bramble sat by the fire turning it into a key. Now Cyrus looked at her like she was the answer to a question they'd been dying since before she was born. Now the road ahead bent toward a door of white stone, and everyone in the little circle of firelight was very carefully not saying the obvious thing:

That doors do not open themselves. That keys exist to be used. That a girl born half-in and half-out might be the only person alive who could end the erasure of an entire country—and that ending it might require the blood that made her what she was.

Ada picked up a flat stone and skipped it across the forgetful water. One skip. Two. The third throw sank without a ripple, as if the sea had declined to remember it.

"They'll want to protect me," she said aloud, to no one. "That's the trouble with found family. They think love means deciding things for you."

The dark didn't answer. But far out, where the horizon should have been, there was a faint light—not dawn, not a ship, nothing so explicable. Just a pale gleam against the black, steady as a held breath, in a place where no light had any business being.

Ada stared at it for a long time. White stone gleaming. A seam in the world.

*Come home,* something seemed to say, in a voice like her mother's might have been, if her mother had had a voice instead of a grave on a threshold.

"No," Ada said softly. "You come to me. And when you do, we'll negotiate."

* * *

When she returned to the fire, both of them stood up too fast, guilty as children.

"I'm not made of glass," Ada said. "Sit down. Both of you. And listen, because I'm only going to say this once."

They sat. Bramble closed his atlas. Cyrus's knife-hand had gone white-knuckled around the handle.

"First: yes, I heard everything. Second: no, you don't get to decide whether I go to this Door. I decided that the day I signed onto this barge-ride-turned-suicide-mission, back when I thought the worst thing ahead of us was Wardens." She held up a hand before either could speak. "Third—and this is the part you're both dancing around, so I'll dance around it too, but only once—we don't actually know that opening it costs me anything. Bramble's folk stories say *blood*, not *life*. A cut palm is blood. Every salt-seller on the Vell docks has cut palms. So before anyone starts composing noble speeches about sparing me, let's find out what we're actually dealing with."

"It could be worse than a cut," Cyrus said quietly. "Everything else on this road has been worse than it looked."

"Then we'll meet it when it arrives." Ada fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Fourth thing. The last thing. And this one isn't negotiable at all."

She looked at each of them in turn—Bramble with his ink-stained fingers and his maps that lied to him nightly, Cyrus with their stolen coat and their borrowed name and their grief worn like a second skin.

"If it turns out the Door wants more than blood—if it wants what Bramble's afraid it wants—then you take me home. To Vell. You turn this barge around and you take me home, and you live with the country staying lost, because I am not trading myself for Ostrava. I never met Ostrava. I won't die for a place that never held me." Her voice cracked on the last words, and she let it. "What I *will* do is walk up to that door and find out what it asks. Because I have spent my whole life tasting other people's truths, and I am owed one of my own."

The fire settled. Under the sail, her father turned over in his sleep and murmured something that might have been her name, or might have been the name of a woman dead twenty-two years, or might have been a word from a language the land had already eaten.

Cyrus reached across the fire and took her hand. Their palm was calloused and warm, and for once, holding it, Ada tasted nothing at all—no sweetness, no sourness, no pressure behind the teeth. Just skin, and honesty, and the salt smell of the sea forgetting itself.

"We find out what it asks," Cyrus said. "Together. And nobody pays anything tonight."

"Nobody pays anything tonight," Bramble echoed, like a vow.

Ada nodded and squeezed Cyrus's hand once and let go, and that should have been the end of it, the camp settling into watch rotations and sleep.

But as she lay back against her pack and stared up at stars that seemed thinner here, fewer, as if the sky too were losing count—she saw Cyrus rise silently, take Wren's map from inside their coat, and carry it to the firelight. She saw them spread it open. She saw Bramble lean over it, and she saw both their faces change.

"Cyrus?" she whispered.

Cyrus didn't look up. Their voice, when it came, was very quiet, and very afraid.

"The map's changed. There's a mark on the Door now." A pause. "It's shaped like a handprint. And it's fresh."
