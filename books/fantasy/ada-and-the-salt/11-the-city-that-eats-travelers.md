## 11. The City That Eats Travelers

The town appeared the way a bruise appears—slowly, and only after you'd already walked into it.

Cyrus saw it first as a smudge on the horizon where the hills folded down toward a river they had no name for, because Bramble's map refused to give it one. The cartographer had been staring at that stretch of parchment for an hour, turning it toward the light and away again, muttering.

"It's not blank," he said at last. "That's what worries me. Blank I could work with. This is *reluctant*. The ink knows something's here and doesn't want to say."

Ada stood at the rail of the beached barge with her arms crossed against the cold, tasting the wind. She did that now more than she used to—a small, unconscious flick of the tongue against her teeth, like a woman checking for blood in her mouth. "It tastes like a door left open in winter," she said. "Like somewhere people meant to stay warm and didn't."

Wren stirred behind Cyrus's eyes, a pressure like weather coming. *Meridian,* the ghost said, and the word arrived with the texture of memory that wasn't Cyrus's own: lamplight on wet cobbles, a bell tower with a cracked bell, salt bread torn and shared. *If it still stands. If it still remembers standing.*

They left the barge hidden in the reeds and walked in as the light went long and amber, and by the time the first rooftops resolved out of the haze, Cyrus understood why the map had hesitated.

Meridian was a town built of almosts.

The houses were real—stone foundations, timber frames, smoke rising from chimneys—but they were real the way a word is real when you can't quite recall its meaning. Some walls were sharp-edged and certain; others seemed to soften if you looked at them too directly, their window-glass going cloudy, their doors losing their handles as though the idea of *door* was wearing thin. A streetlamp burned at the corner of a lane that ended in nothing but grass, faithfully lighting a road that no longer led anywhere. Laundry hung between two buildings, shirts and sheets snapping in the wind, though Cyrus could not have said who had hung it or when.

And the people. The people were everywhere, and every one of them was half-finished.

A woman swept her step with a broom whose bristles faded to translucence halfway down the handle. Two men argued cheerfully over a game of stones outside a tavern, and one of them had no face to speak of—features present but uncommitted, like a sketch an artist had abandoned mid-line. Children ran past chasing a hoop, laughing, and one little girl turned to wave at the strangers, and her wave was warm and whole and her eyes were the color of water.

"They're not ghosts," Ada whispered. Her hand had found Cyrus's sleeve without either of them deciding it should. "Ghosts taste like endings. These taste like—" she searched, tongue pressed to her teeth, "—like a sentence someone stopped writing halfway through."

*The erasure spat them out,* Wren said, and his voice was rougher than usual, scraped raw. *When Ostrava was pulled out of the world, everything attached to it came loose. People who were traveling. People who were trading across the border at the wrong moment. Whole families visiting cousins. The country vanished and took its name with it, but these ones—the land couldn't hold them and the world wouldn't claim them. So they fell here, into the seam.*

"Can they be fixed?" Bramble asked quietly. He had his journal out and was drawing without seeming to decide to, his pencil moving fast, capturing the fading facades before they could shift. "Is there a way back for them?"

*Only one,* Wren said, and said nothing else, and the silence he left behind was heavy enough that all three of them felt it and none of them named it.

An old man approached them before they'd gone twenty paces into the town proper. He walked with a cane that flickered—one moment dark oak, the next a pale wood neither of them recognized—and his coat bore buttons from three different uniforms, as though his past had been assembled from whatever scraps survived the forgetting. He looked them over with eyes that were startlingly, painfully clear.

"Travelers," he said, and the word came out reverent, the way other men said *miracle*. "Real travelers. You'll stay the night. You must. We so rarely—" He stopped. His brow furrowed, and Cyrus watched him reach for something and find the space where it had been. "We so rarely... there was a word. For what we rarely get."

"Visitors," Ada said gently.

"*Visitors.*" He smiled, and the smile was so grateful it hurt to look at. "Yes. Visitors. Come. The Lantern House has beds, or it had beds this morning. It usually does."

*Careful,* Wren murmured. *Meridian is kind, but kindness isn't safety. The Wardens know about this place. They call it a quarantine. They call these people contamination.*

Cyrus kept their hood up and their hands in their pockets, where the map lay folded against their palm, salt-crusted and patient. They had learned, in five years of hiding, to walk like a man with nothing interesting about him. It was the one charm that had never failed.

* * *

The Lantern House was an inn that could not entirely agree with itself. Its common room was warm and low-beamed and smelled of stew; its windows looked out onto a garden that existed only between dusk and dawn, full of flowers no botanist had ever named because they belonged to a country that no longer existed. The innkeeper was a broad, motherly woman named Sefa—or she introduced herself as Sefa, then paused, then said, "Or possibly Marette. I had another name once. It comes back some evenings." Nobody in the room found this strange. That was the most frightening thing about Meridian, Cyrus decided. Not the fading walls. The fact that everyone had made peace with being unfinished.

They ate stew. It was good stew, thick with root vegetables, and Cyrus ate it too fast because it was the first hot food in two days, and then sat back and let the room wash over them.

Halfway through the evening, a fiddler struck up a tune, and the whole common room began to sing.

Cyrus froze with the spoon halfway to their mouth.

The song was in Ostravan.

Not the flat, formal Ostravan of the few surviving documents Bramble had shown them—not the careful reconstructed syllables of scholars handling a dead language like broken glass. This was living Ostravan, sung badly and joyfully, slurred at the edges, the words worn smooth by ten thousand repetitions in ten thousand kitchens. A harvest song. A song about rain coming late and coming anyway, about granaries and stubbornness and love that outlasted bad seasons.

Cyrus knew it.

That was the terrible thing. Cyrus knew it. The melody rose in them like water finding its level, and their lips moved around words they had sworn they'd forgotten, and their chest ached with a homesickness so vast and specific it felt less like grief than like geography.

"I don't understand," Ada said softly, watching them. "You told us you barely remembered anything. You told us you left when you were small."

"I did leave when I was small." Cyrus's voice came out strange. Distant. "I don't remember learning this song."

*But your body remembers,* Wren said, very quietly. *Names can be changed, Cyrus. Songs live deeper than names.*

Around them, the half-people sang on. The faceless man sang. The sweeping woman had come in from her step and sang, broom propped in the corner like a guest. And Cyrus sat among them with their hood shadowing their face and let themselves, just for the length of one verse, pretend that this was ordinary—that they had simply come home after a long day, that the stew would keep, that tomorrow held nothing worse than weather.

Then the verse ended, and an old woman at the next table leaned over and said, in accented but fluent Vellish:

"You sing like someone from the Salt Coast. The old Salt Coast, before the maps got shy about it."

Cyrus's blood went cold. "I'm from upriver," they said. "Near Vell."

The old woman looked at them for a long moment. Her eyes were milky at the edges, going the way of the town's walls, but what remained of her gaze was sharp as a fishhook. Then she smiled, patted Cyrus's hand with fingers that briefly turned transparent at the tips, and said, "Of course you are, dear. Everyone here is from somewhere near." And she returned to her stew.

But her hand had lingered a half-second too long, and Cyrus had seen, in that half-second, the look of a person recognizing something they weren't supposed to recognize.

*She tasted you,* Wren said grimly. *Not the way Ada does. But the erasure left marks on everyone it touched, and marks know each other.*

* * *

They took rooms above the common room—two small chambers with beds that were solid enough, windows that faced the impossible garden. Ada and Bramble took one; Cyrus took the other, and lay awake listening to the house settle, which it did audibly, like a large animal rearranging itself in sleep.

Sometime past midnight, unable to bear the ceiling, Cyrus dressed and went down.

The common room was empty except for embers and one figure: the innkeeper, Sefa-or-Marette, sitting alone at a table with a cup of something steaming, watching the fire with the patience of someone who had watched many fires in many uncertain years.

"You couldn't sleep either," she said. Not a question. "Sit. The tea is chamomile, or it thinks it is."

Cyrus sat. For a while neither spoke. The fire ticked. Outside, faintly, the garden rustled with flowers that had no right to exist.

"My grandmother came from the coast," the innkeeper said at last, to the fire rather than to Cyrus. "Before. I remember her hands. I remember she smelled of salt and tar, and she hummed that song you were singing tonight, and she called me by a name that wasn't Sefa." She turned. Her face was one of the whole ones—firm, lined, certain—but her eyes had begun to go soft at the rims, like paper left in damp. "I've spent thirty years trying to hold on to what's left of her. Every morning I wake up and check. Hands. Salt. Tar. The humming. Some mornings the humming is gone and I have to sit very still until it comes back." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "You're holding on to something too. I can always tell. People who are losing things grip differently than people who are hiding them."

Cyrus said nothing. The silence stretched, and the fire popped, and finally the innkeeper sighed and rose and wished them a good night, pausing at the stairs to add, "Whatever you're carrying through my town—carry it kindly. We've lost enough already."

Cyrus stayed by the fire a long time after she'd gone.

*I lied to her,* they thought, and the thought had teeth. *I've lied to all of them. Ada bought my lie with truth and I gave her a false coin. Marchetti. I told her my name was Marchetti and she tasted it and believed it, because I've believed it so long it's cured into something solid.*

*Has it?* Wren asked, from the quiet place behind their eyes.

Cyrus didn't answer. Because the truth was that the name had been feeling thinner lately—thinner the way Meridian's walls were thin, the way the fiddler's song had risen up out of them unbidden. Five years ago, bleeding and hunted, they had chosen a dead man's name off a gravestone in Vell and sewn it over themselves like a coat. And coats fit, given time. Coats become skin, given enough years.

Didn't they?

*There's something you're not telling me,* Cyrus said to the ghost. *You've been quieter since we crossed into the boundary lands. Quieter since Rehn. You look at me sometimes like a man reading a letter he wrote drunk.*

Wren was silent for so long that Cyrus thought he might not answer at all. When he did, his voice was gentle in a way that frightened them more than any threat had.

*Tomorrow,* he said. *Ask me tomorrow. Tonight, sleep. Meridian takes enough from travelers without our help.*

* * *

Morning in Meridian was a small violence.

Cyrus woke to shouting and came down to find the street transformed. The lane that had ended in grass now ran on for fifty yards before dissolving—and the buildings along it had changed faces overnight, shutters moved, doors repainted, one entire house replaced by a well that hadn't been there before. The sweeping woman stood in the middle of the road, staring at her own home, which now had a different roofline, and saying, over and over, very calmly, "This is nearly right. This is nearly my house. This is nearly—"

Bramble was already in the street with his journal open, gray-faced. "It redraws itself," he said when he saw Cyrus. "The whole town. Every dawn it tries to rebuild what it used to be and gets a little further wrong. Give it a year, maybe two, and there won't be a Meridian at all. Just a shape where one insisted on standing." He looked up, and his eyes were wet with something that wasn't quite tears. "Do you understand what this means? This is what the whole country looks like from inside. This is Ostrava, Cyrus. Every town, every village, trying every morning to be itself and failing. We're walking through the corpse of a nation and the corpse doesn't know it's dead."

Ada stood at the edge of the square, very still, one hand pressed flat over her mouth. When Cyrus reached her, she lowered it slowly.

"The children," she said. "The little girl who waved yesterday. I asked her mother her name this morning." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Her mother couldn't remember it. She called her 'little one' and smiled, and the girl answered to it, because what else can you do? What else can any of them do?" She turned to Cyrus, and her eyes were furious and terrified in equal measure. "We have to move faster. Whatever the Door costs—it can't cost more than *this*. Nothing costs more than this."

Cyrus put a hand on her shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing true enough to say.

They provisioned as best they could—bread, dried fish, a jar of the innkeeper's honey that she refused payment for—and made ready to walk out the western side of town, where Wren said the old coast road began, or had begun, or dreamed it had begun. Bramble marked their route in pencil, lightly, the way one touches a wound.

And it was as they passed through the market square—half-stalls, half-emptiness, vendors selling goods that flickered between existence and suggestion—that Cyrus heard their name.

Not *Marchetti.*

Their other name.

"Cyrion."

Two syllables, spoken soft and wondering, in the accent of the Salt Coast, in a voice Cyrus had not heard in fifteen years and had never once managed to forget.

They stopped as though struck. The market noise receded. The morning went narrow and bright at the edges, like looking down a well.

A woman stood by a stall of fading fruit. She was older than the last time Cyrus had seen her—of course she was, fifteen years, of course—but the crooked set of her shoulders was the same, and the scar splitting her left eyebrow, and the way she pushed her sleeves up twice even after they stayed. Her face had begun to go the way of the town's, softened at the jaw, uncertain at the eyes. But she was looking straight at Cyrus, and her expression was the expression of a drowning woman who has just felt bottom under her feet.

"Cyrion," she said again, louder now, pushing through the crowd, and the name rang in Cyrus's skull like a cracked bell. "Cyrion Ashvael. Oh—oh, merciful tides, it *is* you. You got out. You actually got out. Your mother sent you ahead on the fishing boat and we all thought—" Her voice broke. She caught Cyrus's arms with hands that flickered between solid and translucent, gripping hard anyway. "Don't you know me? It's Yeva. Yeva Corane. We shelled mussels together, we stole brandy from my father, you called a storm off the point the summer you were twelve and scared the whole village half to death—"

"Marchetti," Cyrus heard themselves say. Their voice sounded like it came from underwater, from very far away, from the bottom of a different life. "My name is Marchetti. I'm from upriver. Near Vell."

Yeva stared at them. And slowly, horribly, Cyrus watched the recognition drain out of her face—not because she was forgetting, but because *Cyrus was making her forget*, pouring the lie over her like sand over a fire, and her grip loosened, and her eyes went vague, and she said, politely, to a stranger:

"Forgive me. You reminded me of someone. Someone I..." She frowned, reaching. Losing. "...someone I used to know."

She drifted back toward her stall of fading fruit. Cyrus stood in the middle of the market square with their heart slamming and their hands shaking and the taste of their own true name still burning on the air like a struck match.

*Cyrion Ashvael,* Wren whispered, and there was no gentleness in it now, only a sorrow five years deep. *So you do remember.*

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, a figure in a gray coat had stopped at the mouth of the square—had stopped, and gone very still, and was watching the storm-caller who had just been called by name.

Warden-Captain Ilsa Rehn drew her cloak tighter, touched the empty place inside her where a country used to be, and stepped forward into the crowd.
