## 12. Rehn's Bargain

The rain found Ilsa Rehn two miles out of Meridian, and she let it.

She had ordered her squad to make camp under the limestone overhang a half-league back—Corporal Vess complaining about her boots, young Tammen whittling at nothing with the nervous energy of a boy who'd never bled—and then she'd said she was going to walk the perimeter. No one questioned it. Captains walked perimeters. It was the kind of lie that hid inside the truth like a worm inside an apple, and Rehn had been swallowing those for five years without ever quite tasting them.

Now she stood in the open road with water running off the brim of her gray hood and down the back of her collar, and she watched the lights of Meridian gutter in the distance like a city being slowly snuffed out by someone patient.

She should have taken them at the ferry crossing. That was the thought that kept circling, circling, a hawk over a dead thing. She'd had them. All four of them—the storm-caller with his stolen coat and his hands that shook when he thought no one was looking; the salt-girl with her sharp chin and sharper tongue; the disgraced cartographer whose face she'd known from wanted circulars even before he'd introduced himself as Bramble; and whatever the fifth presence was, the cold spot in the air that made her teeth ache. She'd had them alone on a riverbank with six soldiers at her back and every legal authority in Vell behind her blade.

And she had looked at Cyrus Marchetti—at the way he stood between the girl and the Wardens like a door swinging shut—and something in her chest had opened like a crack in ice, and what came out of her mouth was not *seize them* but *go*.

*Go,* she had said. To fugitives. To a man carrying a map to a Door that did not exist, out of a country that had never existed, which was of course why she could recite its name so easily: Ostrava. Ostrava, Ostrava, the word that lived behind her sternum like a second heartbeat, the word that appeared in no ledger, no atlas, no oath she had ever sworn, and yet was more real to her than her own mother's face—which, come to think of it, she could not entirely summon anymore either. Her mother had gray eyes. Or brown. There had been a kitchen that smelled of dill, or perhaps that was someone else's kitchen, borrowed memory, a room she'd wandered into in a dream and mistaken for home.

Her mother had sung while she worked. That much was true. The song had words in a language Rehn did not speak, and when she tried to hold the melody in her mind it slipped through her fingers like water through a net, and she was left with only the shape of the singing, the warmth of it, the certainty that once upon a time she had been loved in a language she could no longer pronounce.

The rain thickened. Somewhere behind her, in the camp under the overhang, Tammen laughed at something Vess said, and the sound carried thin and strange across the wet dark, and Rehn felt the distance between herself and her soldiers widen by years instead of yards.

She had reported the ferry crossing as *pursuit continuing south along the old towpath, quarry evaded in fog.* A clean lie. Vell rewarded clean lies the way other cities rewarded honest work. In eleven years of service she had risen from street patrol to Warden-Captain on a foundation of reports exactly like this one—precise, colorless, and hollow as a drum. She had believed, once, that the Gray Wardens were the wall that kept the flood out. Magic was a fire, the Commandant liked to say, and fire does not care what it burns. Someone had to stand in the rain with a bucket.

But standing in the rain now, bucketless, she understood that she had never been keeping the flood out. She had been part of it.

Because here was the thing she had told no one, the thing she could barely tell herself except in the smallest hours of the night, when the barracks was quiet and her own breathing sounded foreign: she remembered Ostrava. Not all of it. Fragments. Salt crusts on a windowsill. A bell tower with a crooked green spire. Her father's hands—she was sure they were her father's—knotting a fishing net, the knuckles scarred white. And underneath all of it, like bedrock under topsoil, the knowledge that these fragments were *hers*, that they had not drifted into her head from some stranger's life, that somewhere there had been a country with her name written in it, and that country had been taken away not by war, not by plague, not by any catastrophe she could fight with a sword, but by something quieter and far more terrible. By forgetting itself. By an erasure so complete that the erasers had erased their own reasons, and now the whole machinery of Vell ground on day after day enforcing a ban against magic in a city where magic had never been, guarding a border that led nowhere, hunting smugglers of charms that didn't work—all of them, every Warden, every clerk, every magistrate, dutifully maintaining a hole in the world and calling the hole peace.

She had felt it first as a child, she realized. Before she'd ever put on the gray coat. There had been a map in her schoolroom—a proper one, inked on calfskin, the coast drawn in confident blue—and she had stared at the place where her heart said a country should be and seen only ocean, smooth and unbroken, and she had raised her hand and asked the schoolmistress, *what's out there?* And the woman had smiled and said *ocean, child, nothing but ocean,* and the whole class had nodded, and little Ilsa had sat back down with a splinter of wrongness lodged under her ribs that had never, in thirty years, worked its way out.

The storm-caller had a word for that splinter. He'd said it at the ferry, in that low ruined voice of his, while the fog came off the water: *Ostrava.*

He'd said it like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was on her.

* * *

Rehn found them the next evening, though *found* was perhaps too strong a word. They were not hiding well. The salt-girl—Ada—had argued with the cartographer loudly enough to startle crows out of a hedgerow half a mile before Rehn reached it, and the storm-caller's fire, when she finally crested the ridge above their camp, burned in a little hollow with the cheerful confidence of people who had stopped believing anyone was following them.

Foolish. Endearing. She hated how endearing.

She watched them for a long moment from the cover of a wind-bent hawthorn. The girl was gesturing at the map, which lay weighted with stones on a flat rock, and the cartographer was shaking his head, and Marchetti sat slightly apart with his arms around his knees, staring into the fire with the expression of a man listening to a voice no one else could hear. The ghost. Rehn had felt it at the ferry—that wrongness in the air, that cold spot—and she felt it now too, faintly, like a draft from a door left open in winter. The dead man rode with them. She had read the reports on the stranger who'd died in the market district; she had signed one of them herself. Wren, the file said. No surname. Cause of death: wounds consistent with a long fall. Possessions: none recovered.

None recovered. Except the map. Except the coat on Marchetti's shoulders, which Rehn now recognized with a small cold shock—it had been described in the incident report, salt-stained, patched at the elbow. She had catalogued a dead man's belongings and then watched him walk out of the fog wearing them, and she had said *go.*

The girl tasted the air suddenly—Rehn saw her go still, nostrils flaring, head turning like a deer's—and Rehn decided the moment for lurking was over. She stepped out of the hawthorn, hands visible, sword still sheathed, and walked down into their firelight like a woman coming to borrow salt.

Three of them were on their feet before her third step. The cartographer snatched up the map. Marchetti rose slower, and it was his eyes she watched, because his eyes were the weather report: she saw the pressure building behind them, saw the wind begin to move in the grass around the hollow, saw the girl's hand close on his sleeve—*don't*—and saw him master it, barely, the way a man masters a rearing horse.

"Warden-Captain." His voice was flat. "Come to finish what the ferry started?"

"The ferry," Rehn said, "never started anything. You know that."

"I know you had six soldiers and we're all still breathing. I know that wasn't mercy."

"No," she agreed. "It wasn't."

She stopped at the edge of the firelight, close enough to feel its heat on her wet coat, far enough that the distance said what her hands were saying: *no blades, no bindings.* The girl was watching her with an expression Rehn couldn't parse—suspicion braided with something almost like pity—and the cartographer had gone very pale, because he knew her face too, everyone in the cartographers' guild had known her face after the Ostrava affair, the Captain who'd burned the maps. Well. The maps that officially had never existed, burned on the orders of officials who officially had never given them.

"You're following us," Marchetti said. "You've been following us since the crossing. Don't insult us both by denying it."

"I'm not denying it." Rehn pulled off her hood. Rain dripped from her cropped hair, and she was abruptly, absurdly aware of how she must look—half-drowned, sleepless, gray coat plastered to her shoulders like a second skin. Like what she was. "I'm asking to stop."

The wind died. Just like that, the pressure went out of the air, and Marchetti frowned as if she'd spoken in a language he'd only half learned. "Stop?"

"Following. Hunting. Whatever the Commandant's paperwork calls it this season." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it was, how much steadier than she felt. "I want to come with you."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, the ghost's cold spot shifted, and Rehn felt it settle near her shoulder like a bird landing, and she did not flinch, though it took everything she had.

"That's a trick," the cartographer said hoarsely. "That has to be a trick. You're Rehn. You burned my life's work in the courtyard of the Guildhall and called it sanitation."

"I did." She didn't look away from him. "And I have regretted it every day since, which will mean nothing to you, and shouldn't. But I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to take me to the Door."

"Why?" The girl's voice, sharp as a filleting knife. Ada had stepped forward, chin up, and Rehn saw now what Marchetti saw in her—the kind of courage that isn't loud, just immovable. "Why would a Warden-Captain want the Door opened? Your whole life is the ban. Your whole *city* is the ban."

And there it was. The question she had rehearsed on the road for two days, walking the perimeter of her own soul, and every rehearsal had collapsed, because there was no clean answer, no report-shaped answer, only the truth, which was ragged and wet and smelled of rain.

So she gave them the truth.

"My mother sang in a language I can't speak," she said. "There was a kitchen with dill in it. My father mended nets—I'm nearly certain he mended nets, though I've spent eleven years telling myself he was a cooper, because coopers are respectable and Vell respects coopers. There was a bell tower with a crooked green spire, and I climbed it when I was nine, and I could see the sea from the top, and the sea was—" Her voice caught. She pushed through it the way she'd push through a door. "The sea was *ours*. Do you understand? I remember a country. I remember it in pieces, like a plate broken and glued back together by someone with trembling hands, and every piece is real, and the whole of it appears on no map drawn in the last five years, and I have spent my entire career enforcing the fiction that the hole where it used to be is just—ocean. Nothing but ocean." She laughed, short and bitter. "I asked my schoolmistress about it when I was a child. Before the erasure. She told me it was ocean then, too. Isn't that something? The lie was already in place before the crime. We were raised inside the alibi."

Marchetti had gone very still. "You're from Ostrava."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway, because saying it aloud in front of witnesses made it real in a way ten thousand midnight repetitions never had. "I'm from Ostrava. And I forgot, and then I half-remembered, and the half-remembering is worse than either. Do you know what it's like, Captain Marchetti—"

"Don't call me that."

"—to spend your life holding a wall together around an empty room? Every arrest, every report, every charm confiscated from some frightened grandmother—they're bricks. I've been laying bricks around the space where my home was, and calling it duty." She finally let herself look directly at him, and she let him see everything, because a hunter asking to be saved had no right to concealment. "You said *Ostrava* at the ferry and it went through me like a spear. I let you go because I couldn't do anything else. I've been disobeying orders since the moment I heard that word, and there's no road back from that. If I return to Vell, I hang. If I stay here, I freeze. So I'm done choosing between two deaths. I choose the Door."

The girl was tasting the air again. Rehn watched her do it—watched her lips part slightly, watched her eyes narrow—and braced herself, because if there was one person in this company who could weigh a confession on her tongue, it was the salt-seller's daughter.

"Well?" the cartographer demanded. "Ada. Is she lying?"

Ada was quiet a long moment. The firelight moved on her face. When she spoke, her voice had lost its knife-edge and gained something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "No. She's not lying. She believes every word." A pause. "That's the frightening part. People who believe their own lies taste clean. But she's not lying at all. She's—" The girl hesitated, frowning, as if reading a flavor she had no name for. "She's grieving. She tastes like grief. Old grief. Five years old."

"Five years," Rehn said quietly. "Yes. That sounds about right."

Marchetti crouched and fed a stick to the fire, and for a while the only sound was the wet hiss of it catching. Rehn waited. She had learned patience in interrogations; she spent it now on the other side of the table, and found it cost considerably more.

"It's a trap," the cartographer said at last, but without conviction, the way a man says a thing because silence frightens him. "Wardens don't defect. It's not done. It'll be a test—she leads us into an ambush, reports our route—"

"If I wanted an ambush," Rehn said, "you'd already be in irons. I've had three days and a full squad. I chose instead to stand in the rain and tell four strangers about my mother's singing." She rubbed her eyes. "Bramble, was it? I burned your maps. I can't give them back. But I can tell you that the easternmost one—the one they accused you of inventing—was accurate. I checked it against coastal surveys before the burning. The coastline you drew exists. Draw it again. I'll help you. I have the survey memorized; it's the last useful thing my career taught me."

Bramble stared at her as if she'd offered him the moon on a string.

"And me?" Marchetti said. Still crouched, still watching her across the fire, and Rehn understood that of everyone here, he was the most dangerous judge—because he knew what it was to wear a false name, to live a constructed life over a buried self. He would recognize the seams in hers. "What do I get out of trusting you, Captain? Because from where I sit, you're the reason I live in Vell at all. You're the wall. You're the reason storm-callers sell rain-charms that don't work and watch children burn with fever."

"Yes," Rehn said. "I am. And I won't ask your forgiveness either. I'll ask for something smaller and harder: use me. I know every checkpoint between here and the coast. I know which garrisons are understrength, which captains are lazy, which roads the Commandant's new directives haven't reached. Reinforcements are coming—I signed the requisition myself, before the ferry, when I still believed in what I was signing. Within a week this region will be crawling with Wardens who are not me, and they will not stand in the rain making speeches. They will take the girl, and they will take the map, and they will take you, and they will burn whatever's left." She let that land. "You need someone who knows the hunt from the inside. I am offering to be that someone. Not your friend. Not your absolution. Your guide through the machine I helped build."

"And the price," Marchetti said. "Everyone's price. Yours?"

"To see it." Her voice cracked on the word and she let it. "To stand in front of the Salt Door and watch a country come back. To hear that bell tower ring again with the crooked spire against the sky. And if the Door takes something from me in exchange—memory, years, whatever it demands—then it can take it. I have spent five years as a hole wearing a uniform. Let the hole be filled or let it be finished. Either way, I'm done being empty on Vell's behalf."

The wind moved through the hawthorn behind her, and the fire leaned, and in the leaning light she saw the ghost.

Not clearly. Never clearly. But the cold spot beside her gathered, for a heartbeat, into something like a shape—tall, narrow, one shoulder lower than the other, the posture of a man who had spent his life stooping over charts—and she felt it regard her with an attention like frost forming. Then it thinned and was gone, and Marchetti's eyes flicked to the empty air and back to her, and something passed between them that needed no words: *he heard. He's deciding.*

"He says you're telling the truth," Marchetti said slowly, "but that truth isn't the same as safe. He says—" a pause, listening to a voice the rest of them could only feel, and his face did something complicated, grief and irritation tangled together, "—he says the Door doesn't bargain. It takes what it takes. He says if you come, you come knowing that."

"I've read the reports on what happened to the last people who sought the Door," Rehn said. "One of them is standing next to you wearing his coat. I know what it costs. I'm not asking for a guarantee. I'm a soldier; guarantees are for merchants."

Ada looked at Marchetti. Bramble looked at Marchetti. Even the darkness seemed to look at him. And Rehn stood in the firelight with her hands empty and her sword sheathed and her whole dishonored, half-remembered life spread out on the ground between them like goods at a market stall, waiting to see if anyone would buy.

Marchetti rose. He was taller than she'd registered, and tired in a way that had gone past exhaustion into landscape. He studied her for a long moment, and then he said, quietly:

"If you slow us down, I leave you. If you betray us, the storm finds you before your soldiers do. And if we reach the Door and it wants a life—" his jaw tightened, "—then you wait outside with the rest of them, because no one else dies for my country. Those are my terms."

"They're generous terms," Rehn said. "I accept."

He extended his hand. She took it. His grip was calloused and warm and trembled very slightly, and hers did not, and she thought: *this is the first honest thing I've done in eleven years,* and the thought terrified her more than the ambush she was walking them all toward.

Behind her, far to the north, a signal flare went up from the direction of the reinforced camps—green, then white, then green again. The hunt's new rhythm. The reinforcements, moving early.

Marchetti turned to look at it too, and his face in the firelight was grim. "How long do we have?"

Rehn calculated, and hated the answer. "Two days," she said. "Maybe less. We should break camp now."

* * *

They walked through the night, and Rehn led them down a drover's track that no Warden patrol bothered with because no map showed it—which was, she reflected, the whole story of her life in a single sentence. The girl fell into step beside her somewhere past midnight, and for a long time neither spoke, and then Ada said, without preamble:

"She liked fennel. Your mother. In the song, I mean—I can't explain it, but the memory tastes like fennel and rain on warm stone."

Rehn stopped walking. The column moved on ahead of her, torches bobbing, and she stood alone in the dark with her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door.

"How could you possibly—"

"I don't know," Ada said simply. "I taste things. That's all." She shrugged, awkward, and thrust out a small cloth bundle. "Here. Salt. For the road. My father says everyone traveling east should carry salt, and my father's usually right about salt."

Rehn took it. The crystals caught the distant torchlight and threw it back in tiny fierce stars, and she closed her fist around them, and they bit into her palm like the truth: sharp, preserving, impossible to swallow quickly.

Ahead, Marchetti's silhouette paused on the ridgeline, outlined against the last of the signal-fires' glow, and turned to check on them—and Rehn saw, in that instant, the exact shape of the road ahead, and how short it was, and how bright it burned.
