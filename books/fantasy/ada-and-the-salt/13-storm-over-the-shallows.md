## 13. Storm Over the Shallows

The tidal flats began where the road gave up pretending to be a road.

Cyrus felt them before they saw them—a change in the air's weight, a thinness, as though the whole world had been stretched over a drum. The mud stretched out gray and gleaming to a horizon cut by a single pale line: the sea, or what had been the sea when Ostrava still had coasts. Channels of brackish water threaded the flats like veins in an old hand. Here and there, wooden stakes rose from the mire, all that remained of fish-weirs that no one alive remembered setting. The land did not forget here so much as it hesitated, holding its breath between tides.

"Rehn said three days' crossing," Bramble said, consulting a map that had redrawn itself twice since morning. He said it the way he'd been saying everything lately—quietly, with one eye on the sky. "She said the flats are the last place they can't track us. No roads, no stones, nothing old enough to remember."

"She also said," Ada muttered, hauling a satchel of salt higher on her shoulder, "that the tide comes in fast enough to outrun a horse."

Her father's barge was two days behind them, hauled up and hidden in a reed-choked inlet with Wren's map wrapped in oilcloth beneath its floorboards. They carried what mattered on their backs now: salt, water, the fragments of Bramble's self-erasing charts, and the growing, unspoken knowledge of what waited at the end of them.

Cyrus walked at the front because Cyrus always walked at the front, and because walking first meant not having to watch the others' faces when the forgetting came for someone. Five days past Meridian, Ada had stopped being able to recall the color of her mother's dress. She hadn't cried about it. She'd just gone very still, the way she went still when she tasted a lie, and then asked Cyrus to describe rain over a harbor—any harbor—and listened as though the description were a thing she could keep.

Cyrus had described Vell's harbor. It was a lie, technically. Ada hadn't flinched. Some lies were mercy; her gift, whatever else it was, seemed to know the difference.

The light was going strange by midday—not darkening, but *thinning*, the way candlelight thins when the wick drowns. Cyrus noticed it the way they noticed everything now, with a nerve that had been raw for five years: the particular pressure behind the eyes that meant weather gathering somewhere out of sight.

Storms followed Cyrus. That had always been true, even in Vell, even charmless and sworn to silence. The sky knew them the way a dog knows the hand that fed it once, long ago.

"We should make the far channel by dusk," Bramble said. "There's high ground past it. Old foundations—I mapped them once, before—" He didn't finish. Before the erasure. Before his king's men burned his life's work and put him in a cell for insisting that a country could not simply stop having existed.

Ada stopped walking.

Cyrus turned. She stood ankle-deep in a rivulet of seawater, head cocked, nostrils flared slightly—the posture she took when tasting the air.

"What is it?" Cyrus asked, though they already knew. The pressure behind their eyes had sharpened into something with edges.

"Metal," Ada said. "Oiled metal and wet wool. And under it—" her jaw tightened— "a lie. A big one. Coming from the north."

Bramble swore in a language none of them spoke anymore, including him.

They ran.

It was not a decision so much as a reflex shared among hunted things. The flats gave them nothing to hide behind—no trees, no stone, only the low ridges of dried mud crusted white with salt, knee-high at best. Cyrus drove them toward the largest of these, a long spine of ground maybe four feet above the surrounding mire, pocked with the collapsed mouths of old burrows. Crabs scattered from their footfalls, small and quick and utterly indifferent to the affairs of people.

Cyrus counted heartbeats while they crouched. Sixty. Ninety. The thin light thickened again, ordinary, forgiving.

Then the wardens came over the northern ridge of the world.

There were nine of them, mounted, moving in the loose crescent formation Rehn used—had used, when she'd been theirs, however briefly. Gray cloaks that drank the daylight. Lances tipped not with steel but with null-glass, the ugly crystal that ate magic the way sand eats water. At this distance their faces were smudges, but Cyrus didn't need faces. They'd been running from those silhouettes since Vell. Since the fevered child and the near-miss in the alley. Since a dying man bled out across their stall and made them inherit a country.

"They've seen us," Bramble said flatly.

"They've seen us," Cyrus agreed.

The riders fanned out along the ridge, unhurried. That was the worst part—the patience of it. They didn't charge. One of them raised a horn, and the sound that came out of it wasn't sound at all but a pressure, a deadening hum that rolled across the flats and made Cyrus's teeth ache and the salt-crusted mud shiver into powder. Null-horn. It wouldn't hurt them. It would simply announce, to every listening stone and remembering wind within ten miles, exactly where they were.

And it would do something worse. Cyrus felt it immediately—a dampening, like a blanket thrown over a fire. The storm-pressure behind their eyes guttered.

"They're going to walk us down," Bramble said. "Run us until the tide turns, then take whoever's left." His hands were shaking around his map case. "I've seen them do it. In the capital, to the last of the—"

"Don't," Ada said softly.

He stopped. There were things none of them said aloud on the flats. The list grew daily.

The riders began to descend.

* * *

Later—much later, in the telling, in the years after—Cyrus would try to remember the exact moment the vow broke, and never quite find it. There was no clean instant, no crack of a snapped bone. There was only a series of small surrenders, each one reasonable, each one defensible, stacking up like sandbags against a flood that came anyway.

The first surrender was standing up when they should have stayed down.

"The ridge won't hold," Cyrus said. "When they reach the mud, they'll spread along it and we'll be rats on a wall. We have to run for the far channel. Now, while they're still in the soft ground. Horses bog past the second weir-stakes—look, see how they're circling? They know it too. They'll come the long way around the north spit."

"That's half a mile of open mud," Bramble said.

"It's half a mile they have to cross slowly, and we cross it fast. Lighter. Go."

They went. And the mud took its toll in seconds, sucking at every stride, and the horses came anyway—not through the soft ground but along it, impossibly, the animals wearing wide paddle-shoes of woven gray leather, and Cyrus understood that these were not the patrol riders from the ferry crossing. These were equipped. Prepared. Someone had told them where the quarry would run.

*Rehn*, Cyrus thought, and hated how much of the thought was hope. *Please let it be Rehn.*

It was not Rehn.

The second surrender was picking up the child.

There was no child. That was the terrible arithmetic of it—there was no child, and Cyrus picked her up anyway. She materialized out of the mists that had begun crawling off the sea, a girl of perhaps seven, barefoot, salt-white dust to her knees, standing alone in the path between the fugitives and the riders as though she'd grown there out of the mud. Her face was smooth as an egg. Not blank—smooth. The way a page is smooth before anything is written on it.

"The erasure spat her out," Bramble breathed. "Like Meridian. Like the half-remembered ones—"

"She's real," Ada said. "I can taste her. She's *real*." And she moved toward the girl, because of course she did, because Ada's whole soul was a door that opened outward.

The riders changed formation. Two of them peeled off, driving straight for the child—not, Cyrus understood with a cold drop of the stomach, to harm her. To use her. A hostage of convenience, a body to hold, a lever. The Gray Wardens did not deal in cruelty for its own sake; they dealt in efficiency, which was crueler.

Cyrus got there first. Scooped the girl up—one arm under her, startlingly light, light as driftwood—and ran, and the mud grabbed their boots and the horses closed and the null-horn sounded again and the pressure behind Cyrus's eyes became pain, became a white-hot wire drawn through the skull.

The third surrender was stopping.

There was nowhere left to go. The far channel glittered ahead, forty yards of deep water, and the riders had angled to cut them off from it, and Bramble had fallen behind, and Ada stood with her arms half-raised between the lances and the rest of them, doing nothing, able to do nothing, because she was a girl who tasted lies in a world that had outlawed everything else she might have been.

"Put me down," the child said. Her voice was the voice of the tide going out. "You know what you are. Put me down and let go of the other thing instead."

Cyrus looked at her smooth face and felt the vow—not as words, not as the memory of the night they'd sworn it, bleeding and nameless in a Vell alley, but as a shape, a scar, a door held shut with their whole body for five years.

*I will never call again. I will be no one. I will be safe.*

Safe. The word curdled. What had safety ever bought them? A stall of charms that didn't work. A city that tolerated them the way a house tolerates a draft. Loneliness dressed up as prudence, worn so long it had begun to fit.

The lead rider raised his lance. Null-glass caught the drowned light and threw back nothing at all.

"No," Cyrus said—to the rider, to the child, to the scar, to the five years. "No. I'm done being safe."

* * *

What happened next was not summoning. People thought storm-calling was summoning, that you reached up and pulled lightning down like fruit from a branch. It wasn't. It was remembering. It was opening some cellar-door inside yourself that you'd nailed shut, and letting out the thing that had been waiting in the dark, patient, enormous, loving you the whole time.

Cyrus opened the door.

The sea answered first. Far out beyond the flats, the pale line of water darkened, bruised, stood up. The mist that had crawled ashore reversed itself and fled inland like a startled animal. Above the northern ridge—above the riders—the sky, which had been thin and white and empty all day, developed a texture. Then a weight. Then a color that had no business existing in this drained and faded country: a green-black bruise of cloud boiling up out of nothing, swallowing the horizon in the time it took Bramble to say a word that was mostly a vowel.

The pressure behind Cyrus's eyes released all at once, and what flooded in behind it was—

*Exhilaration.* That was the shame of it, the part Cyrus would carry like a stone. After five years of drought, the first taste of the storm was joy, pure and total, joy like falling, joy like drowning in something that loved you. Power moved down their arms and out through their palms and it felt like coming home to a house that had burned down, familiar and impossible and grieving all at once.

The thunder arrived before the lightning, rolling across the flats hard enough to stagger the horses. The riders' formation broke—not in panic; wardens didn't panic—but in necessity, wheeling their animals about as the first fat drops hit the mud like thrown stones.

"*Down!*" Cyrus roared, and shoved Ada and the child flat against the salt ridge, and brought both hands up.

The lightning came when they called it. Of course it came. It had been waiting five years too.

It struck the mud flats in a line, four strikes in a heartbeat, stitching earth to sky, and the world went white and then black and then white again, and the smell of ozone and boiled salt filled everything. When Cyrus's vision cleared, the lead rider's lance lay shattered in a trench of fused glass. Three horses screamed and bolted, paddle-shoes flying. The riders themselves had been thrown clear—Cyrus had aimed to throw, not to kill, and the aiming had cost them, had pulled the strikes wide, had left six of nine still mounted and furious.

But they were disordered. They were off the line. And the tide—

The tide was turning.

Cyrus felt it the way they felt everything now, through the storm that lived in them: the great slow muscle of the sea flexing beyond the flats, water beginning its long crawl back into the channels, filling the veins, drowning the mud. In an hour the flats would be sea. In twenty minutes, the paths the riders needed would be soup.

"Go!" Cyrus's voice came out doubled, layered with thunder, and it frightened them to hear it. "Channel—now—all of you—GO!"

They went. Bramble hauling Ada, Ada hauling the child, who weighed nothing and stared back at Cyrus with her smooth egg-face and, just before the mist took her, smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has seen a card you didn't know you were holding.

Cyrus turned last, and faced the riders one more time across the rising water, and let the storm show through their skin.

Lightning crawled along their forearms in slow blue threads. Rain fell upward around them for a moment, pulled into the spinning column of pressure that was their body's new center. The lead rider, mud-splattered, lanceless, drew something from his cloak—not a weapon. A whistle. Bone, old, carved with a spiral.

He blew it, and the sound cut through the storm like a knife through silk, and every hair on Cyrus's body stood up, because the whistle was playing a melody. An old one. A nursery tune from a country that didn't exist, the kind of song mothers sang in kitchens that had been erased from the world.

The rider lowered the whistle. Through the hammering rain, Cyrus couldn't see his face. But they heard his voice, pitched to carry across the water:

*"Storm-caller. She said you'd come home eventually."*

*She.* Not Rehn. Someone else. Someone who knew songs and sent riders with paddle-shoes and bone whistles, someone waiting at the end of the flats.

Then the tide came in like a wall, and the riders withdrew, and Cyrus turned and ran for the channel with the storm still screaming out of them, chasing their family across the last of the mud, exhilarated and hollowed and undone, knowing that the moment the lightning left their hands, they had already lost everything they'd spent five years building—and knowing, too, with a certainty that felt like the sea itself, that they would do it again.
