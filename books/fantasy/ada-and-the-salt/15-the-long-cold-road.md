## 15. The Long Cold Road

Bramble had spent thirty years believing that a map was a promise. You drew the world as it was, and the world, in its grudging way, kept faith with you. Rivers stayed where rivers were. Mountains held their lines. Even in wartime, even in famine, the land was honest to anyone who took the trouble to look.

The Unmapped Reach broke that faith on the second night, and by the fourth he had stopped sleeping with his maps under his blanket because he could not bear to feel them changing against his chest like living things.

He sat now at the mouth of their shallow cave, the fire banked low—Cyrus insisted on low fires, always, since the storm—and worked by the light of a stub of tallow candle that Ada had traded three pins for in the last village that still remembered it existed. Before him lay the night's work: six sheets of his finest vellum, weighted at the corners with stones, and every one of them wrong.

The road they had followed all day—a good road, gravel and old paving stone, running north along a ridge of black hills—appeared on none of them. Instead the ink had crawled overnight into new shapes: a river where there was no river, a town called *Vessamy* that Bramble had never heard of and could not find when he walked the ridge at dawn, and, on the easternmost sheet, a coastline.

A coastline. Four hundred miles inland from any sea that had ever been charted, and there it was, drawn in his own hand in ink he did not remember spilling: a long, patient curve of shore, and along it, small and precise, the words *the Salt Door is near*.

"You're doing it again," Ada said behind him. "The face."

"What face?"

"The one where you look like your maps insulted your mother."

Bramble snorted despite himself. "They have. Repeatedly." He tapped the coastline with one blunt finger. "I didn't draw this. I want that understood. I checked my pens. I checked my hands for ink. This—" he turned the sheet so the firelight caught it, "—this drew itself while I slept, using me as the pen."

Ada crouched beside him, salt-seller's daughter to the bone, her braid coming loose and her eyes too old for her nineteen years. She studied the coast the way she studied everything: closely, suspiciously, as if it might lie to her.

"It's pretty," she said at last.

"It's *impossible*."

"Most true things are." She reached out and touched the paper, and Bramble saw her flinch—not at the map, but at something in herself. Her fingers hovered over her own temple for a moment, pressing, the way a person presses a bruise to check if it's still there.

"Ada?"

"I'm fine." But she said it the way people say fine when they are counting what's missing.

Behind them, deeper in the cave, Cyrus sat awake as they always did now, back against the stone, Wren's ghost a faint silver smudge in the air over their shoulder—the dead man more solid here than he'd been anywhere else in Ostrava's ghost-country, as if the Reach fed him too. Rehn stood watch at the cave mouth, Gray Warden coat swapped for oiled canvas, her hand never far from her baton out of habit rather than intent. Five of them, then. A cartographer, a liar-taster, an exile, a ghost, and a turncoat hunter, walking into a country that ate memory like tide eats sand.

Bramble looked down at his maps again and felt the old dread settle into him like cold water into boots.

In the King's service—he had been in the King's service once; that life felt like a book he'd read by someone else—he had mapped the Reach from its southern edge and been told, politely, firmly, by men with soft voices and hard eyes, that the interior was not to be surveyed. *Nothing there worth ink*, they'd said. He had believed it was politics. Now he understood it had been mercy, or cowardice, or both. The Reach was not empty. The Reach was *hungry*. It was the stomach of the erasure, the place where everything Ostrava had been went to be digested, and they were walking down its throat.

"Show me tomorrow's route," Ada said, settling beside him with the ease of someone who had decided, somewhere on this long cold road, that Bramble was hers to look after. He hadn't agreed to this. It had happened anyway, the way weather happens.

He unrolled the southern sheet. "We follow the ridge until the standing stones—Wren says they were a market once, before." The silver smudge drifted closer, and Wren's voice came thin as wind through a keyhole: "*Before the forgetting, they sold cloth and horses and honey.*" Bramble nodded along; he'd learned to translate the ghost. "Then down into the valley, two days, maybe three if the snow comes early. There should be a bridge at—" He stopped.

There should be a bridge at Corren's Ford. He knew this because he had drawn it. He looked at the map, and the map showed no ford, no bridge, no river, only blank vellum with a single line of his handwriting crawling across it like something surfacing through ice:

*who is Corren?*

"I don't know who Corren is," Bramble said quietly.

"You wrote the name yesterday," Ada said. "You said we'd camp at his ford."

"I know what I said." His voice came out sharper than he meant. He made himself breathe. "That's the thing about this place, girl. It doesn't just take memories. It takes the *words* we keep them in. Yesterday I knew Corren. Knew him well enough to name a crossing after him. Today the name is a stranger wearing my handwriting." He smoothed the vellum with both palms, as if he could press the knowledge back in. "Every night I redraw what I can salvage. Every morning some of it's gone and something else has grown in its place. I'm not mapping the Reach anymore. I'm keeping a diary of what it's eaten."

Ada was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice carefully light: "Has it started on us yet? Properly?"

Bramble didn't answer, which was an answer.

Because it had. They all knew it had. Two nights ago Ada had woken screaming about a fire—a house burning, a woman's voice calling her name—and by morning she could not say whose house or whose voice, only that the ash-smell of it had lived in her nose all day. And this morning at breakfast she had called Bramble by a name that wasn't his, some other name, *Bertram* or *Bartholomew*, and laughed at herself, and Bramble had laughed too, and neither of them had said what both of them were thinking: *that's how it starts. First the names go. Then the faces.*

*

The snow came early, as he'd feared.

It found them in the valley the next afternoon, fat slow flakes falling out of a sky the color of dishwater, and by dusk the world had gone soft and white and utterly featureless. Bramble marched at the front with his compass, except the compass had begun lying three days ago—needle swinging lazily toward whatever direction felt most like home—and so really he marched at the front with a piece of decorated brass and his own stubbornness, which was the only instrument that had never failed him.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen standing stone, one of the old market-stones, half-buried. Its carvings had mostly worn away, but Bramble ran his mitten over what remained and felt the grooves of letters. Not any alphabet he knew. Ostravan, probably. The country's true writing, the kind that had been scraped off monuments and burned out of books five years ago, surviving here only because the Reach forgot to forget it.

"Cyrus." Ada's voice, from the fire. "Can I ask you something strange?"

Bramble pretended to be absorbed in his maps. He was not. He heard everything.

"You remember things for people, don't you?" Ada went on. "Storm-callers. Wren said it once—that you all trained your memories like muscles, because your magic lives in what you hold. Storms, names, faces. You're a keeper."

A pause. When Cyrus spoke, their voice was rough, careful. "Something like that."

"So I want to make a trade." Ada's voice did something then—it steadied itself the way a rope steadies when you lean your whole weight on it. "The Reach is taking my childhood. Bit by bit. Last week I lost the smell of our kitchen. Two days ago I lost the game my father used to play, tapping coins on the rail of the barge, and I know there was a game because he keeps almost doing it with his fingers when he thinks I'm not watching, and I can't—I can't remember the rules, Cyrus. This morning I tried to picture my mother's face and I got her shawl instead. Just the shawl. Blue, with fraying edges. That's all that's left of her right now, a shawl with no one inside it."

Nobody spoke. The snow ticked softly against the stone.

"So here's the trade," Ada said. "Every night, from now on, I'll tell you things. Small things. My mother's face. The coin game. What our street smelled like in August. And you hold them. You're better built for holding than I am—you've got that great storm-proof skull—and when we come out the other side of this, if I've lost them, you give them back. Word for word. That's the price of my blood at your Door, if it comes to that. That's what I'm charging."

Bramble looked up despite himself. Cyrus sat very still, firelight moving on their face, and Bramble—who had spent a lifetime reading terrain, who could see a false valley or a dishonest hillside at fifty paces—saw the exile's composure crack down the middle like river ice.

"Ada," Cyrus said. "What you're asking—"

"Is nothing. It's nothing compared to what you're going to pay." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "Don't argue. I taste lies, remember? Lie to me and I'll know, and then I'll be angry *and* forgetting, and nobody wants that."

A sound escaped Cyrus then—half laugh, half wound—and Bramble looked back down at his maps and blinked hard at the vellum, telling himself the candle smoke was in his eyes.

"All right," Cyrus said at last. "All right. Tonight. Start with the coin game."

And Ada began.

She started badly, groping for it—"he'd use a copper and a silver, no wait, two coppers and the silver was the prize"—and Cyrus listened with their whole body, leaning forward, eyes closed, and Bramble understood that he was watching something older than magic: the oldest human technology there is, one person carrying another person's past so it doesn't drown. Cyrus murmured the details back, shaping them, ordering them, and Ada would cry out *"yes, that's it, that's exactly it"* and the lost thing would flicker back into being between them, alive in two heads instead of one.

When she got to her mother's face, she faltered. "I can't—I keep getting the shawl—"

"Describe the shawl," Cyrus said gently. "Start there. We'll build outward."

"Blue. Frayed at the left cuff because she worried it with her thumb when she was thinking. There's a burn mark near the hem, from the kettle, from the winter I was seven, and she wore it anyway because—" Ada's breath caught. "Because Papa gave it to her the year they married and she said a gift shouldn't be hidden just because it's scarred."

"There," Cyrus whispered. "She's coming back. Keep going."

Bramble bent over his maps and let the two of them have their ritual, and he did the only thing he knew how to do: he drew it. All of it. The coin game, the blue shawl, the burn mark, the street in August. If the Reach wanted to eat Ada's childhood, it would have to eat it twice—once out of her head, and once out of his vellum, and Bramble Voss had spent thirty years learning how to make ink argue back.

By the time the fire burned down, the snow had buried the world ankle-deep and silent, and Ada was asleep with her head nearly touching Cyrus's shoulder, and Cyrus sat unmoving, guarding the warmth of her like a coal cupped in bare hands.

Bramble worked by candle stub. On a fresh sheet, in his smallest, firmest hand, he began a new map—not of roads, not of ridges, but of a girl: her mother's face reconstructed from a shawl, the rules of a game played with coins on a barge rail, the smell of a kitchen. At the top, where a proper map bore its title, he wrote:

*Ada. To be returned intact.*

Then he paused, quill hovering, and added beneath it, smaller:

*And one more, when she asks. Whatever she asks. Hold it.*

He did not yet know what she would ask him to carry. He only knew, with the certainty of a man who had watched his own mother tongue dissolve on his tongue for one terrible hour in the first Forgetting, that everyone on this expedition was going to lose something, and that the losing had already begun, and that the only weapon any of them had was each other.

He rolled the maps, tied them, tucked them inside his coat against his heart, and slept.

*

He woke before dawn to voices, low and urgent, and thought for one merciful moment that it was only the wind arguing with the stone.

But Rehn was on her feet at the cave mouth, staring south the way a deer stares, and Wren's ghost had gone bright as a struck match, and Cyrus was already up with frost in their hair, saying quietly, "How many?"

"Twelve," Rehn said. "Maybe more. Lanterns on the ridge line. They've found our trail from the flats." She turned, and in the pre-dawn grey her face was stripped of everything but the truth. "That's not a patrol. That's a cordon. Someone in Meridian talked after all, and Command doesn't send twelve Wardens across the Reach for smugglers." Her jaw tightened. "They send them for the Door."

Bramble clutched his maps to his chest and looked out at the falling snow, where far off, tiny and patient, lanterns were descending through the dark like a string of cold stars—

—and beside him, Ada pressed her palm flat against the standing stone, against the worn Ostravan letters, and said, in a voice he had never heard from her, steady and strange and certain:

"They're not coming for the Door."
