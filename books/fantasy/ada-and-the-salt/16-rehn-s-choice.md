## 16. Rehn's Choice

The reinforcements came up the coast road at dusk, forty strong, their gray cloaks drinking the last of the light.

Ilsa Rehn stood on the seawall above the ferry crossing and counted them twice, because the first count had felt like a lie. Forty. She had requested twelve. Someone in the Provisional Command had looked at her reports—the storm over the tidal flats, the lightning that had split the sky open like a seam—and decided that a storm-caller loose in the borderlands was not a matter for twelve Wardens.

They were right, of course. That was the worst of it. They were right, and she was the one who had told them so.

"Captain." Sergeant Voss—no relation to anyone, a flat-faced man with a voice like gravel poured slowly—drew up beside her at the wall. "Orders?"

She watched the column halt in the market square below, boots striking cobbles in unison, the sound carrying across the water like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else. Torches bloomed one by one. Somewhere a dog began to bark and was silenced by a look she couldn't see but could imagine.

"They'll want to move tonight," she said. "The storm spooked them. Command doesn't forgive weather."

"And us?"

"We hold the crossing until they've scouted the far bank." She heard her own voice do what it always did: flatten itself into something official, something that could be repeated back without shame. "Then we follow."

Voss nodded and went down to the square, and Rehn stayed at the wall alone, her hands resting on cold stone, and let herself think the thought she had been refusing for six days.

*You know where they're going.*

Because she did. She had known since the ferry crossing, since she'd stood close enough to Cyrus Marchetti to see the salt-crusted map's edge poking from their coat like a wound that wouldn't close. She had let them go once with a warning dressed as a threat. Then again at the flats, when her own crossbow bolt had gone wide—not wide by accident, though she'd told herself it was, told herself the wind took it, and hated how easily the lie fit. And now here she was, holding the crossing for people who would never thank her, guarding a road against soldiers whose orders she had written the draft of herself.

There was a hole in her where a country should have been. She had lived with it for five years the way you live with a tooth gone bad: carefully, on one side of the mouth, never biting down. A homeland she couldn't name. A mother tongue that surfaced sometimes in dreams as syllables without meaning. An ache shaped exactly like a coastline.

And then Marchetti had said *Ostrava*, and the ache had turned over in its sleep, and Rehn had understood that everything the Gray Wardens stood for—the ban, the erasure, the tidy gray world with its tidy gray records—was built directly on top of the thing she had lost. She wasn't enforcing order. She was standing guard over her own amputation.

* * *

The command tent went up in the square an hour after full dark, and Captain-Commander Dain Orliss received her inside it with the courtesy he extended to all useful instruments.

Orliss was not a cruel man, which made him worse than a cruel one. He had a scholar's face and a patient voice, and he believed in the erasure the way other men believed in arithmetic: completely, without passion, because the sums came out right. Five years ago he had signed the protocols. He probably still kept the pen.

"Captain Rehn." He gestured her to a camp stool and did not offer tea, because tea was for peers. "Your pursuit has been thorough. I'll say that in my report."

"Thank you, Commander."

"The storm-caller. Marchetti." He unfolded a map on the field table—her map, she noticed; her own annotations copied out in a clerk's careful hand—and weighted its corners with brass objects she didn't look at too closely. "You wrote in your second report that they seemed reluctant. Hesitant to use their art even under pressure. You characterized this as fear."

"I did."

"Do you still characterize it so?"

Rehn kept her face still. Outside, the torches hissed in the sea wind. "Fear is the most common reason for restraint, Commander."

"Mm." He studied her with eyes the color of dishwater, mild and bottomless. "Command's reading is different. We believe the storm-caller isn't fleeing toward safety. We believe they're fleeing toward something. The tidal storm was defensive, yes—but storms that precise require intent, and intent requires destination." His finger traced the coast eastward, past the ferry, past the last marked town, into the blank stretch of parchment where cartographers gave up and drew waves. "There's nothing out there. Officially. But nothing is precisely where anomalies cluster, wouldn't you say?"

"You're speculating about erased territory, Commander. There are protocols against—"

"There are protocols against *naming* it," Orliss said pleasantly. "Speculation is my privilege. Yours is obedience." He straightened. "At dawn we advance along the coast. If the fugitives crossed at your ferry, you will have bought us their trail. If they haven't crossed yet—" he smiled, small and reasonable, "—then you will have them boxed between your position and ours, and this ends tomorrow, cleanly. Either way, Captain, you've done your country a service."

*My country,* Rehn thought, and the words rang hollow as a dropped pot. *Which one.*

She saluted. She walked out of the tent into the dark, and the sea wind hit her face, and it smelled of salt and kelp and something older underneath, something that made the back of her throat tighten with a homesickness for a place she could not remember well enough to miss properly.

That was when she made the decision. Not with thunder, not with any grand turning of the heart—just a quiet click, deep inside, like a lock accepting a key it had been waiting years for.

* * *

She spent the evening being scrupulously correct.

She inspected the pickets. She checked the ferry's mooring lines herself and ordered the barge chain greased against rust. She corrected a young Warden's stance at the eastern post and told him he held his spear like a broom, and he flushed and thanked her, because she was Captain Rehn and Captain Rehn was fair and hard and had never once been accused of favoritism in eleven years of service. She built the night's routine around herself like a wall of small correct things, and behind that wall, in the room of her own skull, she laid out the shape of her treason with a cartographer's precision.

It could not be violence. Forty soldiers, and even if she were willing to kill the ones under her command—which some cold ledger-line in her was not—killing them would only summon more. It had to be time. Time was the one thing Orliss hadn't posted guards over.

The tide table hung in the ferry house. She read it three times. The spring tide would turn at moonrise and run hard until the small hours, and the channel current in those hours ran at better than six knots, fast enough that no sane pilot would put a loaded barge across. Fast enough that no sane pursuer would either.

Which meant the fugitives, if they had any sense left, were already across or would cross on the morning slack. And Orliss meant to advance at dawn.

So the equation was simple: give them the night. Take the dawn away from Orliss. Make the delay look like weather, or prudence, or sabotage by persons unknown—anything but her.

She almost laughed, standing there in the ferry house with the tide table's grease-spotted paper under her lamp. Eleven years of flawless service, and her great strategic insight was that she knew exactly how to fail.

* * *

Midnight found her walking the eastern picket line alone, as captains did, and finding the sentry asleep.

He was young—younger than the ban, certainly younger than the erasure—a boy named Pell with down on his cheeks and his crossbow leaning against the pilings where it could neither be reached nor fired. He slept sitting up with his chin on his chest, dreaming whatever boys dreamed who had never seen a country die.

Rehn stood over him for a long moment. This was the moment, she supposed, where a loyal officer shook him awake and put him on report. This was the moment where the last eleven years reached out and took her by the collar.

Instead she knelt, lifted the crossbow, and carried it forty paces down the line to the old drainage culvert beneath the seawall—an iron-barred mouth that maintenance schedules had promised to clear for two years and no one had ever bothered to check. The bars were rusted through at the base. She set her boot against them, braced, and pulled, and the whole rotted grate came away with a groan she muffled against her cloak.

An opening. Small, hidden, leading down to the shingle below the wall. Not enough for forty soldiers to slip through—but enough for one captain to go where she pleased, later, if it came to that. Enough for a door. She was learning, lately, to think in doors.

She stowed the grate in the culvert's shadow and walked back to Pell, and stood over him again, and this time she woke him—with a boot to the sole, sharp and proper.

"On your feet, soldier. You're on report. Asleep at post within sight of the enemy."

Pell scrambled up, white-faced, fumbling for a weapon that wasn't there. "Captain—I—"

"Report to Sergeant Voss at first light. Tell him exactly what happened. All of it." She held his gaze and saw the misery there, the boy's whole world tilting, and felt the strange tenderness of a woman burning her own house down who nonetheless cannot bear to singe the curtains. "Everyone sleeps at post once, Pell. What matters is whether you lie about it after. Don't lie about it."

"No, Captain. Thank you, Captain." Confused gratitude. He'd remember her kindly, maybe. One person in the gray machine who would.

She walked the rest of the line and found nothing else wrong, because she had already taken the only thing that mattered, which was the hour before dawn—the hour when officers sleep deepest and sentries trust the pattern of their rounds. At the third watch change she told Voss she wanted the dawn advance preceded by a full reconnaissance of the far bank, standard caution given the fugitives' demonstrated cunning, and Voss agreed with the relief of a man being handed more procedure instead of less. Procedure was armor. She wrapped him in it.

By then the tide had turned. She stood on the seawall and listened to the channel running hard and black in the dark, six knots of cold water grinding seaward, and she thought: *if they're coming, they're coming now, and every minute I keep this camp sleeping is a minute they spend getting free.*

The moon went behind cloud. The camp breathed. And Rehn committed her final act of the night's war, the smallest and largest one: she went to her tent, sat at her field desk, and by lamplight she wrote the report that would end her.

Not a confession. Nothing so useful to them. Just a request—one page, addressed to Orliss, requesting that the dawn advance be delayed four hours pending verification of a reported sighting on the southern road. A sighting she had invented. A thread of nothing, tied to nothing, but plausible enough that a careful man like Orliss would send riders to check it rather than march blind, and careful men lost hours to their care.

She signed it. *Ilsa Rehn, Captain, Gray Wardens.* Her hand did not shake. Her hand had stopped shaking somewhere around the culvert, and what had replaced the shaking was worse and better: a stillness, a clarity, the feeling of a woman finally standing in the right place even if the place was a cliff edge.

Loyalty, she thought, blowing out the lamp. Everyone assumed loyalty was a thing you gave upward—to rank, to command, to the gray uniform and the gray law. She had given it upward for eleven years and it had landed nowhere, sunk into the erasure like coins into deep water. But loyalty downward, sideways, outward—to a storm-caller she'd hunted, to a salt-girl who tasted lies, to a disgraced mapmaker, to a dead man's ghost, to a country that existed now only in the negative space of every record in the realm—that loyalty had weight. That loyalty had a face. Several faces. One of them had looked at her across a ferry deck and said, *you forgot too, didn't you,* and it had been the kindest thing anyone had said to her in five years.

* * *

Dawn came up bruise-colored, and with it came the thing she had not planned for.

She was at the seawall when the rider came in from the south road at a gallop—one of the scouts she'd sent out at midnight to lend her invented sighting a body—and he was waving, and shouting before he reached the square.

"Captain! Smoke—there's a fire on the south road, a beacon, someone's lit a signal fire in the old watchtower ruins—"

A real sighting. By chance, by spite, by the same tide of luck that had carried her this far, something actual burned on the southern road, and Orliss would take it as confirmation, and the careful man would commit riders—perhaps half his force—to chasing smoke while the true trail lay cold and eastward with the morning tide.

Rehn closed her eyes for one breath and thanked whatever forgotten god of drowned countries had decided, today, to be on her side.

Orliss emerged from his tent already buckled, already commanding, and the camp broke into motion around her—horses, orders, the boy Pell pale and upright at his post having told Voss everything and been placed, to her private satisfaction, on latrine duty rather than the lash. Voss appeared at her elbow with the dispatch case.

"Commander wants you to lead the southern detachment, Captain. Your sighting, your ground."

"Of course," Rehn said.

She took the dispatch. She mounted. And as the column formed, she performed the last calculation of her old life: the southern detachment would ride hard, find a burnt tower and no one in it, and lose the day. Orliss would review the night's logs by noon. He would find the tide tables annotated in her hand, the picket schedule shifted, the culvert grate missing, and a captain's signature on a fiction. He would understand it all perfectly, because Orliss was a careful man, and understanding was the cruelest thing he did.

Eleven years, ended by breakfast. Possibly her life ended with it—desertion in the face of the enemy, aiding a storm-caller; the protocols were not vague. She found that she could hold both futures in her mind without flinching, the way you hold a hot iron: painfully, briefly, and then you simply get on with what the iron is for.

She wheeled her horse south with the detachment, rode a quarter mile at proper military pace, and then, at the bend where the road dipped out of sight of the camp, she reined in.

"Sergeant Halvard," she called to the detachment's second. "Ride back and inform the Commander that Captain Rehn has determined the southern road requires a wider sweep. We'll range as far as the ridge line. Do not wait for us."

Halvard saluted and spurred away, glad to be rid of the errand. And Rehn turned her horse off the road entirely, down through the gorse and the shingle grass, toward the coast path that ran east—the path that led, past the ferry, past the last maps, toward the blank place where a Door waited in a cliff that existed only when unwatched.

Behind her, faint in the morning haze, she heard Orliss's camp discovering, one logbook at a time, exactly what she had done. Ahead of her, the sea wind came off the water carrying salt, and the taste of it sat on her tongue like a word she'd known as a child.

She was done hunting them. She was, if the road allowed it, going to catch up.

Rehn bent low over her horse's neck and rode east into the unmapped morning, a traitor at last, and lighter than she had been in five years.
