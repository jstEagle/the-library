## 18. Every Hand Raised Against the Tide

### Ada

The Door did not look like a door.

It looked like a seam of white stone running up the cliff face, a crack in the world too straight to be natural, too clean to be ruined. It existed the way a thought exists at the edge of sleep—if you turned to look at it directly, it thinned, went gray, became only weathered rock and lichen. Ada had learned the trick of it in the last hour: you had to look at it sideways, the way you looked at your own grief. Softly. Out of the corner of the eye.

Behind them, down the shingle beach and across the tidal flats, the Wardens were coming. She could hear the boots—hundreds of them, a sound like a slow drum, like a heart deciding.

"Ada." Cyrus stood beside her, salt wind pulling at their coat—the stolen coat, the one that had carried them all this way. Their voice was very quiet. "You don't have to do this."

"Yes, I do," Ada said. "I'm the half of it. Half-key, half-lock. Wren said."

*Wren said,* and Wren stood a little apart from them all, more solid now than he had ever been in five hundred miles of haunting, his ghost-body flickering like a candle in a draft. He would not look at her. He had not looked at her since he'd told them the price.

*One soul, freely given. Forgotten entirely. Not killed—forgotten. Unmade from the memory of the world, the way Ostrava was unmade, so that the Door has something to trade. A name for a name. A country for a person.*

Ada unslung her satchel with hands that did not shake, which surprised her. She had expected to shake. She had spent her whole life shaking at small things—her father's silences, the taste of lies on market air, the way people looked through her when they learned what she could do. But now, with the tide coming in and the drums of boots coming closer, her hands were steady as a scale.

She drew the knife she used for cutting salt blocks.

"Wait—" Bramble started forward, maps falling from under his arm like molting feathers.

She pressed her palm flat against the white seam and drew the blade across it.

The pain was bright and simple, the simplest thing in the world. Blood welled in the line of the cut, and instead of running down her wrist it ran *up*, along her fingers, into the seam of the Door as if the stone were drinking. The white deepened to pearl. Somewhere inside the cliff, something vast exhaled.

And the Door began, very slowly, to open.

"No," Cyrus said. It was not a shout. It was worse than a shout—it was the voice of someone watching a thing happen that they had already decided to stop, and were too late to stop, and were going to stop anyway. "No. Ada. Step away from it."

"It's open," she whispered. The seam was widening, a finger's width of darkness that wasn't darkness—more like *elsewhere*, like the smell of rain before rain. "Cyrus, it's open. It's working."

"Ada." Her father's voice, from down the beach. He had stayed with the barge, had refused to come this last mile, and now here he was anyway, boots soaked to the knee, running across the shingle like a man half his age. "Ada, girl, what have you—"

The drums of boots crested the headland.

### Cyrus

They came down the cliff path in gray lines, forty of them at least, spears leveled, and at their front a palanquin of gray silk that Cyrus recognized with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear. Warden-Captain Ilsa Rehn stepped out of it before it had fully set down. Her uniform was torn at the shoulder. There was a bruise along her jaw that had not been there at the ferry crossing, weeks and a lifetime ago.

She had fought her own soldiers to get here first. Cyrus understood that the way you understand a thing in a storm—completely, without time to examine it.

"Stand away from the Door," Rehn called, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word *Door*, and Cyrus saw that she was crying without seeming to know it. "All of you. I'm ordering you as—"

"As what?" Bramble shouted back, absurd, magnificent, a disgraced cartographer in a coat two sizes too big shaking his fist at forty armed Wardens. "As the law of a country that doesn't exist? Look at your own maps, Captain! Look at the blank space on every chart you've ever owned and *tell me what's written there!*"

The Wardens wavered. Cyrus saw it ripple down their lines—the small, terrible confusion of people who had been given orders their whole lives and had never once been asked what the orders were *for*.

And in that wavering, in that crack of light, Cyrus did the arithmetic they had been doing since Wren first spoke the price aloud.

*One soul, freely given. Forgotten entirely.*

The Door was open. Ada's blood was in the stone. And every second they stood here arguing, the Wardens closed, and the tide came in, and the chance drowned.

Cyrus stepped forward, between Ada and the advancing line, and raised their hands, and said the words they had not said in five years, the words that had cost them everything and were about to cost them the rest:

"Then let it be me."

### Bramble

"No," said Bramble.

He said it the way he said everything—too loudly, with too many words behind it, a man who had spent twenty years being told he talked too much and had decided, somewhere in a prison cell with contraband maps hidden in the binding of a psalter, that he would talk until they silenced him for good.

"No, absolutely not, sit down, all of you, this is not a decision for the person with the *most* to lose to make unilaterally—"

"Bramble." Cyrus's voice was gentle, and that was the worst part. Gentle. As if he were a child. As if his life were a smaller thing than theirs. "You have your mother tongue back. You have your name cleared waiting for you in a restored Ostrava. You could map the whole country again, every road, every—"

"Every road I drew from *memory* because no one else would!" His voice broke and he hated it and he kept going. "Do you know what I did, Cyrus? I sat in a cell for five years and I drew Ostrava from memory. Every street of Callowmere. Every bend of the Salt River. My mother's kitchen. I drew my mother's *kitchen* four hundred times because I was afraid I'd lose it, and do you know what I learned?" He was crying now, furiously, wiping his face with a hand inky to the wrist. "I learned that a memory you draw is a memory you can give away. I have Ostrava in my head. All of it. I'm the most complete copy of that country left alive. It should be me. It *should be me*, and you will not stand there and tell me my maps matter more than—"

"Than a country?" Cyrus said.

"Than *you*," Bramble said, and the word came out of him like a stone from a sling, and he could not take it back, and he did not want to.

The tide was coming in. The Wardens were fifty paces off and slowing, because Rehn had raised a hand and her soldiers, God help them, were actually stopping, actually listening to the argument of five madmen on a beach at the edge of the world.

And Bramble saw Cyrus look at him—really look, the way you look at a person when you are memorizing them—and he thought: *no, don't you dare, don't you dare say goodbye with your eyes, I forbid it—*

### Rehn

Ilsa Rehn had given orders for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once stood in front of a door and wanted it to open.

She stood now on the shingle with her hand raised and her soldiers halted behind her, and she watched the salt-seller's daughter bleed into the stone, and the disgraced cartographer weep and shout, and the ghost—she could see it now, now that they were this close to the Door, now that the hole in her memory had edges she could feel with her tongue—she could see the dead man standing in the surf like a man waiting for a boat.

And she watched Cyrus Marchetti—*Cyrus*, whose name she had carried in her mouth for five years without knowing why it ached—step toward the opening Door and offer themselves up like a coin laid on a dead man's eyes.

"Marchetti." Her voice carried down the beach. "Stand down. That's an order."

Cyrus turned. The wind off the sea pulled at them, and for a moment they were not an exile or a criminal or a storm-caller; they were just a person, tired, salt-burned, standing in front of the last door out of a country that had been stolen from both of them.

"You came from there too," Cyrus said. Not a question.

"I don't—" Rehn's hand went to her chest, to the ache, the old ache, the one shaped like a word she couldn't pronounce. "I don't remember. I've never remembered. There's just this—" She made a fist. "This *hole*. And you're all so ready to jump into it."

"That's exactly why it should be me," Cyrus said. "I know what's in the hole. You don't. I'm not losing anything, Captain. I'm just giving back what was never mine to keep."

"That's a lie," said Ada.

Everyone turned. The girl was still standing at the Door, her cut hand pressed to the stone, blood threading up into the pearl. She was crying, finally, finally, and her voice came out steady as a tide table.

"That's a lie, Cyrus Marchetti. I'd taste it if it weren't. You're losing *everything*. You're losing us. And you're standing there telling the Warden it's nothing so she'll let you do it, and it's the biggest lie you've ever told, and it *hurts*, it tastes like—like the sea after a storm, like everything at once—" She pressed her forehead against the stone. "So don't you dare. Don't you dare decide for all of us. We didn't come this far to be *protected*."

### Wren

Wren had died at this door once already. He remembered it the way ghosts remember everything: perfectly, and too late.

He had stood where Ada stood, with his own blood in the stone, and he had believed—because the architects of the erasure had designed it to be believed—that the Door would take his life and be done. He had not known, until the blade was in his hand and the stone was drinking, that the price was not death. Death would have been mercy. The price was *unmaking*. He had felt himself come apart not into darkness but into *nothing*, into the specific nothing of a man no one remembers, and he had screamed into it, and no one had heard, because the first thing the Door took was the hearing.

He would not let it take one of them. Not the girl who had carried his map like a relic. Not the cartographer who had kept his country alive in ink and obsession. Not the Warden whose face he almost recognized. Not the child who called storms.

"Listen to me," Wren said, and his voice came out of him with all the force of the grave behind it, and the wind stopped, and the tide seemed to pause, and every living person on that beach turned to look at a dead man shining like a lamp in the surf. "I built this trap. I know how it springs. It doesn't take the willing—it takes the *guilty*. It takes the one whose name is already a debt." He looked at Cyrus, and made himself say it, the thing he had hidden for five hundred miles and two lifetimes. "I helped write the erasure, Cyrus. I designed the Door. And I designed it so that the one who opens it in bad faith is the one it claims. My name is the debt. It was always going to be mine. Let me finish what I started."

"You repented," Ada whispered.

"Repentance isn't a currency," Wren said. "I checked. I've had five years of being dead to check."

He turned to the Door. The seam of pearl light had widened to the width of a shoulder. Through it, impossibly, he could smell woodsmoke and bread and the Salt River at dawn.

He raised his hand—

### Ada

"—and the tide hit us all at once."

That was how Ada would remember it afterward, in the weeks when she could still remember anything: that the sea itself rose up the beach, a wall of gray water taller than the cliff, because the Wardens had brought a storm-caller of their own, or because the Door was pulling, or because the world was ending and the ocean wanted a front-row seat.

The water came down like a judgment. Ada heard her father shout her name. She heard Bramble scream something about *the maps, save the maps*. She heard Rehn roaring orders at her own soldiers—*fall back, FALL BACK*—and she heard, underneath everything, under the roar of the wave and the drums of the assault and the ringing in her own cut hand, one voice, clear as a bell across water:

"Ada. *Ada.* Whatever happens—whatever happens next—remember that we argued. Remember that we all fought to be the one. That's the whole story. That's the only thing worth carrying out of here."

And Ada, with her blood in the Door and the sea coming down and the Wardens breaking like gray toys on the shingle, turned her back on all of it—on the wave, on the soldiers, on the whole armed weight of a country that had erased her friends' home—pressed both hands to the pearl-white stone, and *pushed*.

The Door swung wide.

And through it, past the salt and the spray and the shouting, Ada saw the first light of a country that did not exist yet—saw rooftops, and bells, and a river the color of hammered tin—and she understood, with the terrible clarity of the moment before a wound, that the Door was open and the price was not yet paid and every soul on this beach was still standing in the balance, waiting to see which name the world would choose to forget.

She turned to find Cyrus.

Cyrus was already walking toward the light.
