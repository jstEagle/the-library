## 20. Ostrava Remembers

The light came out of the Door like a tide.

Ada felt it before she saw it—a warmth against her closed eyelids, a pressure in her chest, as though the whole coast had drawn one enormous breath and was holding it. She was on her knees on the white stone, her cut palm pressed flat to the seam, blood running thin and bright between her fingers, and the salt of her own body was singing. That was the only word for it. Singing. A note too low to hear and too large to ignore, rising up through the cliff, through the shingle, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her.

Behind her, someone was shouting. She thought it might be Rehn. It didn't matter. Nothing mattered except keeping her hand where it was, because the Door had asked, and she had answered, and now the answering had to be finished.

*You are half-key,* her mother had said once, in a kitchen that no longer existed, in a country that no longer existed. *Half-key, half-lock, little salt-girl. Remember that when they come for you.*

She hadn't understood then. She understood now. The lock was turning.

The light broke over them.

It wasn't like sunlight. Sunlight falls; this rose. It came up out of the ground itself, out of the sea, out of the seams between stones, a gold-white flood that did not blind but *revealed*, and Ada watched it roll across the tidal flats where the Wardens had been advancing with their gray banners and their iron-tipped pikes—and stop them. Not violently. The light simply passed through the soldiers the way dawn passes through fog, and when it cleared, the men and women of the Gray Wardens were standing very still, looking at their own hands, at each other, at the horizon behind them, with expressions Ada would spend the rest of her life trying to describe. Recognition. Grief. The look of people waking from a long sleep to find they have been walking in the wrong direction for five years.

One of them dropped his pike. Then another. Then all of them, a ringing clatter of iron on stone that sounded, to Ada's salt-strung senses, like bells.

Because bells were starting. Far off—impossibly far off, across water that should have been empty—the sound came rolling: church bells, harbor bells, the great cracked bell of a cathedral Ada had never seen but suddenly, unmistakably, knew. Her knees nearly gave. She knew that bell. She had heard it every market-day of her childhood, before her father had loaded the barge and taken her south, before the maps went blank, before—

Before.

"Ostrava," she said aloud, and the name did not taste like a lie. It tasted like bread and brine and woodsmoke. It tasted like home saying its own name for the first time in five years.

The coastline was moving. She could see it happening, could see the sea withdrawing from shores that were reasserting themselves, headlands shouldering up out of the shallows, a harbor town assembling itself out of mist and memory half a league down the coast—rooftops first, then chimneys, then the masts of fishing boats that had been anchored in nothing for half a decade. On the cliff road below them, a milestone was surfacing through the turf like something being born, and when the light touched it, letters bloomed across its face:

OSTRAVA — 3 LEAGUES
WELCOME HOME

Ada started to laugh, or cry, or both, and turned to share it with—

With Cyrus.

The space where Cyrus had been standing was empty.

Not empty the way a room is empty when someone steps out of it. Empty the way a socket is empty when a tooth has been pulled. There was a shape in the air, a Cyrus-shaped absence, and Ada looked at it and felt her mind slide off it like rain off glass. She frowned. Someone had been there. Someone important. The others were there—Bramble was on his knees with his arms wrapped around himself, weeping openly, whispering coordinates under his breath like prayers; Rehn stood rigid, her Warden's coat hanging open, staring at the reborn city with her jaw clenched hard enough to crack teeth—but there was a gap among them, a cold spot, and when Ada tried to look directly at it her thoughts went smooth and blank as unmarked paper.

"Cyrus?" she said.

The name came out of her mouth and hung there, and she waited to feel what it meant, and felt—nothing. Nothing at all. Just an ache behind her sternum, deep and specific, like a bruise on a bone. Like grief with no object. Like reaching for a handrail in the dark and finding air.

Bramble lifted his head. "Who?"

"Cyrus." She said it again, firmer, tasting it. Salt-seller's daughter; she knew flavors the way other people knew faces. And the name had a flavor—it must have, everything did—but it was fading even as she held it, going thin and mineral and strange, like water that had been boiled too long. "Cyrus Marchetti. The storm-caller. They were right here. They—" Her voice caught. On what, she couldn't say. "They stepped forward. I remember them stepping forward. Toward the Door."

"You don't," Bramble said gently. He got to his feet, wiping his face with the heel of his hand, smearing tears and dust into a cartographer's mud. "Ada. Look at me. Who called the storm at the Shallows?"

"Someone did," Ada said slowly. The memory was there and not there, a fish glimpsed under ice. "There was a storm. It saved us. It was—" Enormous. Beautiful. Terrifying. Called by someone who had sworn never to call again. "I can't see their face."

"No," Bramble said. "None of us can. None of us ever will." He looked at the Door—at the seam of white stone, which was already dimming, already becoming merely rock, its work finished—and his voice cracked. "That was the price. One soul, forgotten entirely, to anchor the restoration. Everyone remembered. Except the one who paid."

* * *

They found the map first.

It lay on the white stone where the light had been, folded small, stained dark along one edge—not with blood, Ada realized, though her palm was still bleeding. With salt. A crust of it, fine and white, as if the map had wept. She picked it up because her hands needed to do something, and the moment her fingers closed on it the ache in her chest flared so sharply she gasped.

"What is it?" Rehn asked.

"I don't know." Ada stared down at the map without unfolding it. "I just—I think it matters. I think it's the most important thing I've ever held, and I don't know why."

"That's how it will be," Bramble said quietly. He had stopped crying. Cartographers, Ada was learning, grieved the way they worked: precisely, and in private, and forever. "For all of us. There will be holes. You'll walk past a doorway and ache. You'll hear a laugh in a crowd and turn around for no reason you can name. The country got its memory back, but the payment doesn't come back. It's gone where payments go." He swallowed. "We won't even know what we're missing. That's the cruelty of it. We'll just miss."

Rehn said nothing. She was looking at the sea, at the new-old harbor town, at the smoke beginning to rise from chimneys that had been mist an hour ago. Her hand kept going to her collar, to the gray braid she'd worn for fifteen years, and pulling away again. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough. "My orders were to arrest you all. My orders were written by people who erased a nation and called it peace." She tore the braid free. It fell to the stone, gray thread on white rock, and the wind took it. "I'm going home. I suggest you do the same."

"It's not home if we can't remember who brought us here," Ada said.

No one answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

* * *

Wren came at dusk.

By then they had made camp in the lee of the cliff, because none of them could bear to leave yet, and because Bramble kept insisting—half-laughing, half-weeping—that he needed to survey the restored coast while the light lasted, that he was the first cartographer in five years to map a country coming back into existence, and that if he died tomorrow it would have been worth it. Ada sat apart, turning the salt-stained map over and over in her hands, refusing to unfold it. Some part of her understood, in the wordless way she understood lies, that unfolding it would hurt. That whatever was written on it was written in a hand she used to know.

The ghost came up out of the ground the way the light had—rising, not falling. One moment the shingle was empty; the next, Wren stood there, and for the first time since the Low Tide, since the bleeding stranger collapsing into Cyrus's stall in Vell, Wren was *whole*. Solid enough to cast a shadow in the last of the sun. Their wounds were gone. Their face was calm. They looked younger than Ada remembered, or perhaps she had simply never seen them at rest.

"You're staying," she said.

"For a moment." Wren smiled. It was a good smile. It was also the smile of someone standing at a departure gate. "The Door let me finish my work. Now it's letting me finish my rest. But I wanted—" They paused. Ghosts, apparently, could still find things difficult to say. "I wanted to tell you what happened. Because you'll carry the hole, and holes fester when they're unexplained."

"Tell me," Ada said.

"The price was freely given. No one forced it. No one tricked it out of anyone." Wren's eyes went briefly, unbearably, to the place where the Door had been. "They understood before any of us did. Before I confessed what I'd done. Before Bramble explained the mechanics. They stood in front of that seam of stone and they *understood*, and they chose, and the choosing was the bravest thing I have seen in two lifetimes—one of them lived, and one of them hasn't ended yet." The smile wavered. "I designed the erasure, Ada. I helped build the machine that ate a country, because I believed it would end a war. And then I spent five years dying by inches, carrying a map toward the only thing that could undo it, looking for a storm-caller strong enough to pay the toll. I found one. I don't deserve the forgiveness, but the Door gave it anyway. Forgiveness, it turns out, is just another kind of tide. It comes whether you've earned it or not."

"Was it worth it?" Ada asked. "What they gave? For all this?" She gestured—at the harbor lights kindling down the coast, at the bells still faintly ringing, at the stars coming out over a country that existed again.

Wren considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "Ask the child in Meridian whose house stopped fading at dawn. Ask the ferryman who remembers his wife's name. Ask Rehn, when she finds her mother's grave and can read the inscription." They knelt, so that their eyes were level with hers, and the last light went through them like light through sea-glass. "But don't ask whether it was fair. It wasn't. Nothing about this was fair. Fairness drowned years ago, and what floated instead was love, and love is never fair—it's just *given*. That's the whole of it. Given, or withheld."

"Will I forget you too?" Ada asked. "When you go?"

"No." Wren reached out—not quite touching her cheek, but close, close enough that she felt warmth like a hand. "You're the lock, salt-girl. Locks remember what passes through them. You'll keep more than the rest. Not everything. But more." They stood. The dusk was deepening around them, and Wren was growing translucent at the edges, unraveling into the blue hour like sugar into warm water. "Take care of the map. Take care of each other. And when you meet a stranger someday—a stranger with weather in their bones and no name they can recall—be kind. Be kinder than kindness requires. You'll understand why someday. Or you won't, and you'll be kind anyway, which is better."

"Wren—"

But they were gone. Not dissolved, exactly. *Released.* The way a held breath is released. The shingle where they'd stood was ordinary stone, and the wind came off the new-old sea smelling of kelp and woodsmoke and home, and Ada sat alone with a map she couldn't open and an ache she couldn't explain, and above her the first stars of Ostrava's first remembered night in five years came out one by one, like names being read from a list.

* * *

They went down to the city in the morning.

The road was three leagues of miracle. Bramble walked it with his journal open, sketching furiously, laughing and cursing in equal measure as landmarks surfaced around them—an orchard here, a shrine there, a stone bridge assembling itself arch by arch beneath their feet as they crossed it, so that Ada had the vertiginous sense of walking slightly behind reality, of arriving places a heartbeat after they finished existing. Rehn walked apart, silent, wearing her Warden coat with the insignia torn away, and twice Ada saw her stop dead at some roadside marker or leaning gatepost and stand there trembling, drinking in evidence of a childhood she'd forgotten she'd had.

And the city—Ostrava's capital, whose name Ada's tongue remembered before her mind did, shaping itself around *Serevan* like water finding its old channel—was awake. Bells everywhere. People in the streets weeping and embracing strangers who were not strangers. Doors thrown open. Tables dragged out onto cobbles with bread and salt and wine set upon them, because the oldest custom of the oldest country on the coast was this: no one comes home unfed. An old woman pressed a heel of bread into Ada's hands and kissed her forehead and said, "Welcome back, child," and Ada stood in the middle of the square holding bread she hadn't earned, in a city she'd been born in and exiled from and led a war party home to, and cried until Bramble put his arm around her shoulders.

Her father was there.

She found him at the harbor, on the barge, exactly where some part of her had known he would be—he'd felt the change the way she had, she realized, the salt in his blood calling him north for days before any of them understood why. He was talking to a harbormaster who had spent five years as a rumor, and when he saw her he dropped everything and crossed the quay at a run, and held her so tightly she could feel his heart hammering, and neither of them said anything for a long time, because there was too much to say and all of it was unnecessary.

"You went north," he said finally, into her hair. "I dreamed you went north. I dreamed there was a door."

"There was a door, Papa."

"And now?"

"Now it's open." She pulled back and looked at him—at the lines around his eyes, at the salt crusted in his beard, at the face she had almost lost once to forgetting and would never lose again. "Now everything's open."

He fed her. Of course he fed her. Fish stew on the barge deck, eaten with the whole ragged company—Bramble already drunk on new coastlines, Rehn sitting stiffly at the rail until Ada's father silently put a bowl in her hands and she ate it like a penance—and the sun went down on Serevan harbor gold and forgiving, and the bells rang vespers in towers that had stood empty and full of mist for five years, and it was, everyone agreed later, the finest night any of them had ever lived.

Ada lay awake in the hold afterward, in her old bunk, under her old blanket, and did not sleep.

The map lay beside her. She had still not unfolded it. But in the dark, with the lantern out, she found herself tracing its edges through the cloth of her pack, learning its shape the way you learn the shape of a key you keep meaning to use. And the ache was there again, deeper now, settled in for the long haul, and she lay with her hand pressed flat against her sternum and tried, one more time, to aim it at something.

A stall in a foreign city. Rain-charms that didn't work. A fevered child. A stranger bleeding out at dusk. A barge slipping past checkpoints. A girl who forgot her father's face and a man who lost his mother tongue. A storm over the Shallows, enormous and beautiful, called by—

By whom?

The question opened under her like a hole in the floor, and she lay very still at its edge, breathing, feeling the salt-stained map beside her pulse faintly warm, like something alive, like something waiting.

Outside, far off across the water, thunder rolled in off the new coast—soft, distant, harmless, the first weather Ostrava had owned in five years. Nobody had called it. Weather was simply allowed again.

Ada listened to it, and ached, and did not know for whom.

But in the morning, she would unfold the map. In the morning, she would follow it wherever it led, because locks remember what passes through them, and something in her—something older than her grief, deeper than her forgetting—was already certain of one thing, certain the way she was certain of the taste of truth:

Somewhere out there was a person with weather in their bones and no name they could recall.

And they were coming home.
