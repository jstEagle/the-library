## 22. Ada and the Salt

*There is a word I cannot say.*

It sits in me like a stone at the bottom of a well. I feel its weight when the water stirs. I do not know what it is called, only that it is mine — that I carried it a long way, farther than any road on any map, and set it down somewhere I cannot find again.

The barge rocks. Dawn comes up the river the color of pewter and rose.

Ada is laughing.

I hold to that. When the stone turns over in my chest, when the water rises, I hold to the sound of her laughing at her father's terrible joke about the customs man at Vell who weighed his own boots twice because he didn't trust the scale or himself. Her laugh is not a small thing. It has edges. It has salt in it.

"Storm-boy," she says, "you're staring."

"I'm watching the light," I say, which is true, and also not all of the truth, and she tastes the difference — I see her taste it, the little flick of her tongue against her teeth, the way she does — but she lets it go. She has learned which of my untruths are doors I am not ready to open. Or perhaps they are doors that open onto nothing. Perhaps there is nothing behind them but smooth white stone.

Her father calls from the tiller. The barge *Salt-Luck* leans into the current, and the morning opens like a hand.

* * *

*Fragment: water.*

I dream in pieces now. That is the shape of me — a shattered thing that has decided to be a mosaic instead of ruins.

In the dream there is a cliff made of white stone, except it isn't a cliff, it's a seam, a place where the world was stitched shut by someone with enormous hands and no patience for beauty. There is wind. There is always wind in the dreams, and it tastes of iron and rain and something older than both.

Someone is speaking a name into the stone.

I wake before I hear it. Every time. My body will not let me hear it, the way a tongue will not let you bite itself — some last mercy of the flesh, or some last cruelty. I lie in the narrow bunk under the salt barrels and listen to the river slap the hull and think: *that name was important. That name was the most important thing anyone ever said, and I was there, and I cannot—*

And then dawn, and Ada laughing, and the stone settling back into the dark water at the bottom of me.

Bramble says forgetting is a country too. He said it once over cards, not looking at anyone, the way he says true things. "You can be born in it," he said. "You can have borders you can't cross back over. The only difference is nobody's drawn the maps." He had ink on his fingers even then, even off-duty, even playing cards on a salt barge with a girl who could taste lies and a stranger with no past. Some men are their trade all the way down.

He is drawing new maps now. Real ones. He showed me one last night by lamplight — the coast of Ostrava, whole again, its harbors named, its rivers running where rivers run. He'd written across the top in his careful cartographer's hand: *Ostrava, restored.* And beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *surveyed from memory, imperfectly, by those who stayed.*

"You're in this map," he told me, tapping a mark near the delta. "See? The barge. Right here."

"How can I be in a map of a country I've never seen?"

He looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that measure everything, and something moved behind them — grief, maybe, or guilt, or the particular sorrow of a man who knows exactly how much a map can leave out.

"Everyone's in this map," he said finally. "That's rather the point of it."

* * *

*Fragment: the storm.*

Here is what I know about storms, though no one taught me here, though I have no right to know it:

They are not violence. Everyone thinks they are violence, but they are grief. A storm is the sky remembering too much at once. All that held rain, all that held lightning, all that pressure and longing, and then the letting-go, which looks like destruction from underneath but is only honesty, finally, at altitude.

I know this the way I know Ada's laugh. Not from learning. From having been it.

Sometimes, standing at the rail while her father works the shallows, I raise my hand without meaning to. The clouds answer. Just a little — a thickening, a green-gray bruise on the horizon, a pressure change that makes the gulls go quiet. And Ada appears beside me, always, as if she can smell it, and puts her hand on my wrist and brings it down, and says nothing, and we watch the sky settle back into blue like a dog lying down.

"Why do you stop me?" I asked her once. "Nothing happens. It's just weather."

"It's never just weather with you," she said. And then, softer, looking out at the water: "Some things should come back slow. If they come back at all."

I did not understand her. I understood her completely. Both of these were true, the way so many things in me are two true things pressed together with the seam showing.

* * *

*Fragment: a door.*

White stone. A seam in a cliff that exists only when you don't look straight at it — Bramble proved this with mirrors and mathematics and nearly put his eye out with a spyglass, and laughed afterward, actually laughed, the first time any of us heard him do it, because the cliff had winked at him and he had caught it.

Blood on white stone. Someone's blood. Small brown hands — no. Pale hands. Hands I know. Hands that—

The dream breaks there, every time, the way a wave breaks, and I surface gasping in the bunk with salt — actual salt, grains of it — on my lips, though there is no sea within a day's walk and no reason for my mouth to taste like the end of anything.

Ada says I talk in my sleep. She won't tell me what I say. "It's not fair," I told her. "You know my dreams better than I do."

"Yes," she said simply. "But they're yours. You should get to meet them in your own time."

She is wise in a way that makes me ache. She is twenty years old and carries a knife and a ledger and an entire country's worth of other people's secrets, and she has decided that patience is a form of love, and I am the beneficiary of a philosophy I did nothing to earn.

Or did I? That's the question that walks beside me all day, every day, barefoot and patient as a gull. *What did I do to earn this?* Because they look at me — all of them, Ada and Bramble and old Tomas at the tiller, even Warden-Captain Rehn when she rides down to the quay in her gray coat and pretends to inspect cargo — they look at me with a warmth that has history in it. You can tell fresh warmth from old warmth. Fresh warmth is curious. Old warmth is *familiar*, and familiarity has scars.

They knew me. Before I washed up on the shingle below the lighthouse with no name and no past and salt crusted in my hair, they knew me, and whatever I was to them, it was enough that they took me in without asking for receipts.

Once I asked Ada directly. We were mending nets, our knees touching on the deck, the evening going gold around us.

"Did you know me? Before?"

Her hands went still on the netting needle. Stillness, in Ada, is louder than shouting.

"No," she said.

The air tasted of nothing. No lie.

But I have watched her taste lies long enough to know that *no* is not the only flavor a mouth can hide. There is also the flavor of a truth with a piece bitten out of it. And that is what hung between us over the nets, sweet and hollow, like fruit with a wasp inside.

* * *

*Fragment: Wren.*

I do not know anyone named Wren.

I know this the way you know a room you've never entered: someone lived here. There are marks on the walls where furniture stood. In my memory — in the flooded lower floors of my memory, the parts I shouldn't be able to access and do — there is a person shaped like laughter and regret, quick-moving, bird-boned, always half-turned away as if about to fly or flee. They stand at the edge of every dream I have. They never come closer. Sometimes they seem to be trying to speak, and the wind takes it, and the wind is always taking things in my dreams, and I think: *even in my own head, I am being robbed.*

But last week, on the night the bells rang in Harborside for the founding of the new survey office — Bramble's office, his name carved over the door, vindication in limestone — I dreamed the whole dream through for the first time. No waking. No mercy.

A cliff of white stone. Wind like the end of the world rehearsing. A woman with gray in her black hair holding back soldiers with nothing but her body and her rank, screaming at them that this was sanctuary, that this was *law*, that they would have to go through her — Rehn, it was Rehn, I know her stride, I know the set of her shoulders, I have seen her on the quay a dozen times and dreamed of her a hundred.

And at the seam in the stone, a figure. Bird-boned. Translucent as breath on glass. Smiling at me with an expression I can only call *forgiven*, though I don't know what they did, or what I did, or why forgiveness should look like that — like sunlight through a body that is already leaving.

Their mouth moves. The wind takes it. But this time, at the very last, at the very edge of waking, one word gets through:

*"Home."*

I woke with tears drying on my face and did not know whose they were.

* * *

Dawn again. Always dawn; my days begin at the beginning, over and over, as if the world is being kind to me, or practicing.

The barge slides down the estuary toward the open coast, where Ostrava — real Ostrava, charted Ostrava, Ostrava with consulates and customs houses and children learning its name in schools that did not exist five years ago — spreads along the shore like a sleeper turning over. Bells everywhere this morning. Some festival. Ada says it's the Day of Return, whatever that means; she says it with that bitten-fruit flavor again, and I have stopped asking, because some questions are doors, and I have learned to stand in front of doors without forcing them.

Tomas steers us out past the breakwater. The water changes voice — river-murmur becoming sea-speech — and the wind comes off the ocean carrying its cargo of salt, and I breathe it in, and something in me breathes back.

"Look," Ada says, and points.

On the horizon, a storm is building. A real one, far off, all purple and silver, lightning walking its long legs along the edge of the world. It is beautiful the way dangerous honest things are beautiful. Nobody flinches. Storms are allowed here now. Storms are *named* here now — Bramble told me they keep a registry, that sailors write thank-you letters to particularly helpful ones, that there is a storm called *Mercy* that has been steering ships safely into harbor for three seasons running, and no one can explain it, and no one wants to.

"Mercy's out today," Ada says, squinting. "Good. We'll have fair wind home."

*Home.* She says it easily, the way you say a thing you've said ten thousand times. But I catch it — I always catch it — the half-second hitch, the flicker of her tongue, the taste of something underneath the word. Grief, maybe. Or the memory of grief. Or the place where grief was and a garden grew over.

We stand together at the bow. Her shoulder touches mine. This is not an accident; none of her kindnesses are accidents.

"Do you ever wonder," I ask, watching Mercy walk the horizon, "who you were? Before?"

"Every day," she says.

"And does it frighten you? Not knowing?"

She considers this with the seriousness she gives everything, turning her face into the wind so her hair streams back like a banner.

"No," she says at last. "Because whoever I was, they got me here. To this boat. To these people. To you." She glances sideways, and there is the laugh gathering in her eyes even before it reaches her mouth. "Whoever they were, I owe them everything. So I try to live like a thank-you."

The stone at the bottom of my well turns over. The water rises. For one blinding moment I am nowhere, I am *everywhen* — white stone and storm-wind and a name rising in my throat like a drowned thing breaking for the surface, my name, my true name, the one I gave away, the one that cost a country and bought it back, and I open my mouth to—

To what? To say it? To give it back?

Ada's hand finds mine on the rail. Warm. Salt-grained. Certain.

"Breathe," she says quietly, not looking at me, watching the storm. "Whatever it is — it keeps. That's the whole secret of the sea, storm-boy. Everything keeps. Nothing is ever really lost. It just waits for the tide to bring it home."

The wave crests. Breaks. Recedes.

And I stay.

Whether the memories return or never do — whether I am the person who spoke a name into white stone or only the person who woke from dreaming it — I am here, on this barge, at this dawn, with this family that chose me once and chooses me still, every ordinary morning, without needing to know why.

The salt on the wind fills my mouth. It does not taste like endings. It has never once tasted like endings.

It tastes like beginning.

Ahead, the coast of Ostrava brightens, mile by mile, bell by bell, and Ada laughs at something her father shouts from the tiller, and I laugh too, and the storm called Mercy bends gently toward the shore, and the tide — patient, faithful, keeping everything — begins to turn.

* * *

That night, moored in the lee of the new harbor wall, I dream the dream one more time.

The white stone. The wind. The ghost with the smile like forgiveness. But this time the dream does not break, and the wind does not rob me, and the figure at the seam steps aside — steps *through* — and the name rises all the way up from the bottom of the well, gleaming, whole, unmistakable.

I do not wake.

Somewhere below decks, in the dark hull of the *Salt-Luck*, the salt in the barrels shifts, very softly, like a held breath being released — and far out at sea, the storm called Mercy turns, and stands still upon the water, and listens.
