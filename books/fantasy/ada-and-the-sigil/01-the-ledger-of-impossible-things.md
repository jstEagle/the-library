## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The storm in the bottle had been sulking for three days.

I could tell because the little grey cloud at its heart had gone from a soft, apologetic drizzle to something with opinions. It pressed against the glass like a cat that had decided the window was personally insulting. Every so often it spat a single fat raindrop against the inside of the flask, and I had learned — through trial, error, and one regrettable incident involving Master Corvane's second-best waistcoat — to keep my sleeves well clear of the cork.

"Item four hundred and eleven," I said, and dipped my pen. "One bottled storm, minor temperament, provenance disputed. Seller claims it was caught off the Teeth of Marrow Bay by his late grandfather. Grandfather almost certainly did not exist."

The ink sat on the page and thought about drying. Everything in the Vellum Guild's counting room took its time about things. The light came down through windows of glass so old and thick it turned the afternoon green, the way pond water is green, and the dust hung in it like it had signed a lease. Somewhere above me, the guild's senior clerks were arguing about whether a captured echo counted as perishable goods. It did not concern me. Very little concerned me, which was precisely how I liked it.

My name is Gideon Almeida, and for six years I have been the least important person in a building full of impossible things.

This is not false modesty; it is a professional strategy. In the Vellum Guild, importance is a disease. You can watch it take a man. First he gets a better chair. Then he gets opinions at council. Then one morning he doesn't come down to the counting room at all, and someone else inherits his ledger, and after a decent interval someone else inherits his rooms, and nobody says his name again except when they're pricing his effects. The guild trades in things that shouldn't exist — bottled storms, sleeping songs, keys to doors that were never built — and it has learned, over two hundred years, that people are the most perishable inventory of all.

So I keep my head down, my columns straight, and my debts paid on time. Especially the debts.

Especially *that* debt.

* * *

Let me be precise about the storm, because precision is the only prayer I own and I say it often.

It arrived on Tuesday in a crate marked FRAGILE — THIS SIDE UP — CONTAINS WEATHER, which should have been my first warning, because honest crates don't advertise. The seller was a river-boat captain with a face like a dropped pie and a bill of sale written in three different hands, none of them his. He wanted forty crowns. Corvane offered twelve. They settled at nineteen and a bottle of the guild's plum brandy, which Corvane then charged back to the guild as "acquisition expenses, miscellaneous," because Master Corvane believes that rules are like rivers: fine things, meant to be routed around.

I catalogued it that evening. Weight: four pounds, two ounces, though it gains weight when it rains, which I have noted in the margin and which has already been cited as evidence of my "overactive imagination" by a senior clerk who once spent an entire quarter trying to determine whether a haunted mirror depreciates. It does. Nobody thanks you for knowing these things.

By Thursday the storm had begun to sulk, and by Friday morning — today — it was doing this:

A crack of thunder, small as a knuckle rapped on wood, rolled around inside the glass. The little cloud went dark at its base. And every quill in the counting room stood up on end, all at once, like a field of startled grass.

"That thing's going to blow," said Tam Wicker from the next desk, without looking up. Tam is the guild's appraiser of sounds and silences, which means he gets paid to listen, which means he is the only man in Vellum Row whose work consists entirely of what everyone else does for free. "Mark me. One day, maybe two, and we'll be picking rain out of the ledgers."

"Then it'll be item four hundred and twelve," I said. "Bottled storm, former. Contents: weather, loose."

Tam snorted. Around us the counting room went on with its patient arithmetic. Old Mistress Halloway was valuing a jar of last year's summer — actual summer, the smell of cut grass and hot stone, decanted in July by a hedgewitch who owed the guild money — and she kept lifting the lid a crack and closing her eyes, and her face went young each time, and each time she put the lid back on and wrote a lower number. Down the row, a boy I didn't know well was feeding paper shavings to a salamander that lived in the stove and was technically listed as an asset, category: heat, self-generating, do not name.

Do not name. That rule appears more often in our ledgers than any other. Do not name the salamander. Do not name the echo. Do not name the thing in cellar nine, whatever it is; the entry just says *see previous custodian*, and there hasn't been a previous custodian in living memory, and the ink on that particular line looks, if you stare too long, less like writing than like a scar.

I stared at the storm instead. Safer. Storms are honest. They want exactly one thing, which is to be weather, and everything they do follows from that. Give me a sulking thunderhead over a polite colleague any day of the week. A storm will never smile at you while moving your desk into the corridor.

I finished the entry. I blotted it. I squared the ledger with the edge of the desk, because a crooked ledger is a lie you can see from across the room, and I stood, and I carried the storm to the shelf.

The shelves of the counting room run floor to ceiling along three walls, oak older than the kingdom, and they hold the guild's working stock — not the vault treasures, not the things behind triple locks and oaths, but the everyday impossible. Jars of borrowed luck. A lantern that burns regrets. Seven keys, six of which open nothing yet, filed under *pending*. I set the storm between a box of winter mornings (assorted) and a stoppered jug labeled, in faded script, *the sound of a door you can never go back through*. The storm rattled its glass once, resentfully, and settled.

"There," I told it. "Behave. If you're very good, in fifty years some apprentice will catalogue your sediment."

It declined to answer. Wise. Everything in this building that talks ends up either sold or shelved, and the shelved ones are the lucky ones.

* * *

At midday I went up to give Master Corvane the acquisition report, because that is the shape of my life: I carry things upstairs, I carry words downstairs, and in between I make sure the numbers agree with each other, which is harder than it sounds when the numbers belong to objects that disagree with themselves.

Corvane's office sits at the top of the guild house, under the roof, where the pigeons hold their parliament. It is the only warm room in the building and the only untidy one, and both facts are deliberate. Clutter makes men comfortable, and comfortable men talk, and talking men tell Corvane things. He collects information the way the counting room collects jars: carefully, patiently, and always with an eye to resale.

He was at his window when I came in, eating an apple he hadn't paid for, watching Vellum Row with the expression of a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

"Master Almeida." He didn't turn. He never turns immediately; making you wait is half his greeting. "The storm?"

"Catalogued. Item four hundred and eleven. It's temperamental but stable, provided no one shakes it, sings to it, or tells it sad stories."

"Told sad stories? Really?"

"It gained two ounces during the ballad Tam was humming yesterday. I have witnesses."

That got the smile — the real one, quick and crooked, the one he keeps for me alone, or lets me believe he keeps for me alone. Six years of practice and I still can't fully audit that smile. I've tried. There's a column in my head where I keep the evidence for and against Master Corvane, and the columns refuse to balance, and I suspect that is by design.

"And the seller?" he asked.

"Paid. Gone. Lied about his grandfather, probably lied about the bay, definitely lied about the price he told his crew."

"Good. Honest sellers ask questions." He finally turned, tossing me the apple core toward the bin; I caught it, because he'd thrown it before I'd decided to catch it, which is another of his tricks. "You know why I bought it, Gideon?"

"To fill a gap on the third shelf."

"To fill a gap in a buyer's collection. There's a man in Ilvane's court — don't ask which office, the title changes faster than the weather it owns — who wants storms. All the storms. Small ones, contained ones, the kind that fit in a cabinet beside his other griefs." Corvane sat, laced his fingers over his waistcoat, and looked at me with the warmth of a hearth and the temperature of a contract. "We sell him three this season, we're in his correspondence. His correspondence, Gideon, is worth more than the storms. People tell princes' secretaries things."

"I'll note the projected margin," I said.

"You'll note nothing. This one stays off the books until it moves." He waved a hand, easy as brushing away a fly. "Consider it a lesson. The ledger records what happened. The guild trades in what happens next. Never confuse the two."

Six years ago, that sentence would have sounded like wisdom. It still does, if I don't listen closely. Listening closely is my whole trade, and I am getting worse at doing it to him.

"Anything else?" I asked.

"No. Go and count something." And then, as I reached the door, in the tone he uses when he wants a thing remembered without seeming to want anything at all: "Gideon. Supper tonight, my table. Just us. I've a pigeon from the coast and I'd rather share it than read it."

That is the other thing about Master Corvane. He knows exactly what I was when he found me — sixteen, starving, three days off the road out of Marsh Hollow with my master dead and my apprenticeship papers burned in the same fire that took her cottage — and he knows that a hot meal and a seat at his table lands in me somewhere no wage ever reaches. He has never once mentioned the fire. He has never once mentioned her. He simply feeds me, twice a season, regular as interest, and lets the debt compound in silence.

I said yes. Of course I said yes. I always say yes, and I have stopped asking myself whether that's gratitude or training, because the answer might unbalance a column I need to keep straight.

* * *

The afternoon went the way afternoons do: slowly, and then all at once, like a dropped jar.

I reconciled the quarterly figures for the echo trade — echoes are our steadiest line; grieving families pay handsomely to keep a voice, and the guild pays nothing to get one, which Corvane calls "a favorable spread" and I call nothing at all, out loud, ever. I re-shelved the winter mornings after discovering the box had been opened, again, by person or persons unknown, and that one morning was missing, again, and that the remaining mornings smelled faintly of someone else's kitchen. I drank tea that tasted of the pot it feared. Ordinary hours. Counting-room hours. The kind of hours I once believed would be the whole of my life, and believed gratefully.

The light had gone from green to copper when the storm spoke.

Not thunder — I'd know thunder. It made a different sound, low and rolling, and every hair on my arms rose, and the quills stood up again, and this time the inkwells shivered hard enough to ring. Down the counting room, Mistress Halloway put both hands flat on her desk. The salamander went out. Not dimmed — *out*, like a held breath.

And the storm pressed itself against the glass, all of it, the whole grey weight of it, pointing.

Toward the door.

I have catalogued four hundred and eleven items of the impossible, and I have learned that they notice things first. The mirror knew the custodian was dead before anyone told it. The seven keys hum when a lock they'll open comes within a mile. Impossible things are early to every catastrophe; it's the rest of us who arrive on schedule.

So when a bottled storm forgot its sulking and strained toward the guild's front door like a dog hearing its name called from very far away — I did what any careful, invisible man would do.

I picked up my pen. I wrote, in the margin of the day-book, in my smallest hand: *411 agitated, cause unknown.* Then I watched the door.

The knock, when it came, was wrong in every particular. Guild knocks have grammar. Merchants knock briskly, twice. Clerks knock in the rhythm of the office bell. The Quiet Brothers, when they come to collect a debt, do not knock at all, and everyone pretends not to notice.

This knock was slow. Three strikes, spaced like footsteps, like something that had walked a long way and wasn't entirely sure the walking was finished. And underneath it — I would swear this on any ledger you like — underneath it, the knocking had a sound beneath the sound, a second knock slightly out of time with the first, the way a word heard underwater is still a word but no longer quite the same one.

The counting room went still. Even the storm held its breath, pressed to the glass, waiting.

Mistress Halloway looked at me. Tam looked at me. Everyone looked at me, because looking at me is safe; I'm furniture that writes.

"Someone get the door, then," said Mistress Halloway, to no one in particular, and everyone found urgent business with their desks.

I set down my pen. I straightened the day-book so its edges matched the desk. And I went down the long green-lit room toward the door, past four hundred and eleven impossible things, every one of which was silent now, every one of which was listening —

— and opened it.
