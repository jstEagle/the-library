## 2. A Child Made of Words

The knock came at the ninth bell, which was wrong in three ways.

First, the Vellum Guild did not receive visitors at the ninth bell. We received them at the second bell, when the clerks were fresh and the ink still behaved, or at the sixth, when the day's trade had settled into that drowsy honesty that makes people confess things they'd otherwise charge for. Second, nobody knocked at the guild door. They rang the bell-pull, a brass affair shaped like a coiled serpent whose tongue you were meant to grasp — a design choice Master Corvane insisted was "a lesson in trust." Third, and most damning: I had catalogued every sound this building made. It is an occupational hazard of ledger-keeping that you come to know a place the way a physician knows a body, and I knew the Vellum Guild's knocks the way I knew my own pulse. This one was small. Hesitant. Struck with the flat of a palm rather than a knuckle.

I set down my pen mid-entry — *one bottled storm, minor, temperamental, sold to Lady Vashti of the Amber Coast, delivery by barge, do not shake* — and went to answer it, because everyone else in the building had developed a sudden and profound interest in their own ledgers.

That is how it works here. The Vellum Guild trades in impossible things, and impossible things have a way of arriving at doors, and the universal instinct of the guild's staff upon hearing such arrivals is to become very busy with paperwork until someone of lower rank deals with it. I was, at nineteen, the lowest rank in the building. I had been for six years, ever since Master Corvane fished me out of the debtor's cells and informed me that my penmanship was worth more than my freedom. He said it kindly. That's the thing about Corvane — he says everything kindly, including the parts that should make your blood run cold.

I unbarred the door, and there she was.

A girl. Eight years old, perhaps nine, though it was hard to say under the mud. She was caked in it from scalp to shoe — grey river-mud, dried in plates like the scales of some unfortunate fish — and she stood on our threshold as if she had walked out of the ground itself and simply forgotten to stop. Her eyes were the color of rainwater held up to the light. She looked at me the way I imagine a cat looks at a locked cabinet: not with fear, but with an assessment of whether the lock was worth her time.

"Can I help you?" I asked, because apparently my training extended even to the absurd.

She answered.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is all I have, and because what happened next rearranged my understanding of the world the way her words — I would learn — rearranged objects.

She spoke, and I did not understand a single syllable. That much was ordinary; the guild employs speakers of eleven languages and I have a working ear for four of them, and this was none of them. It wasn't the cadence of any tongue I knew. It didn't flow so much as *land* — each word arriving with a weight, a finality, like stones dropped into deep water. But that wasn't the strange part either. Scribes hear strange languages the way sailors hear strange weather.

The strange part was the shelf behind me.

We keep a rack of odds-and-ends by the entrance — mislabeled inventory awaiting re-cataloguing, a habit I instituted myself because the alternative was chaos. On it sat a brass compass that pointed at whoever was lying nearest (don't ask), a jar of preserved sighs, a candle that burned downward, and a small tin whistle carved from bone that played whatever song the whistler most missed.

When the girl spoke her second word, the compass rotated in its place. Not fell. Not slid. *Rotated*, deliberate as a hand turning a page, until its needle faced her.

Her third word, and the candle flame bent sideways, burning horizontally toward her like a hound straining at a leash.

Her fourth word, and the bone whistle lifted an inch off the shelf, hung there trembling, and played three notes of a lullaby before I snatched it out of the air and shoved it in my pocket with hands that had gone suddenly, embarrassingly clumsy.

The girl watched me do this with an expression of mild scientific interest, as if I were the phenomenon.

"Right," I said, to no one, to the mud, to the gods I don't believe in but who seem to enjoy watching me work. "Right."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the Vellum Guild: we are not merchants. Merchants sell things people want. We are something older and less honest — we are *brokers*. When a storm gets bottled in the Sundered Straits, someone has to find the buyer who wants a hurricane in glass. When a nobleman's shadow walks off without him, someone arranges the reconciliation. When a language dies, someone salvages the last words and sells them to collectors who wear mourning like jewelry.

Everything that comes through our doors gets catalogued. Everything gets a number, a description, a valuation, and eventually a buyer. This is the whole of the law. I have written it ten thousand times in ten thousand ledgers: *all things may be owned; all things may be sold.*

And standing in our doorway, dripping river onto imported tile, was a thing that had never been catalogued before. I could feel the ledger in my head already opening to a fresh page. That's the sickness of my profession — wonder lasts exactly as long as it takes to reach for a pen.

"Wait here," I told the girl, and she waited, which surprised me more than the compass had. There was an obedience about her that didn't sit right — a child alone, mud-caked, walking leagues to arrive at a guild door, and then standing where she was told like a soldier. Like something trained. Or something following instructions only she could hear.

I fetched Master Corvane.

He came down the stairs with the unhurried grace of a man who has never once been late for anything, drying his hands on a cloth, and stopped three steps from the bottom. I watched his face do something I had never seen it do. Corvane's face is a well-managed estate; everything grows where he plants it. But for one full second, looking at the girl, the garden went to weeds. Something raw moved through it — hunger, maybe, or recognition — and then he smoothed it over so expertly that I spent the rest of the evening wondering whether I'd imagined it.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, now."

"She knocked," I offered, uselessly. "At the wrong hour. With her hand."

"I can see that, Gideon." He descended the last steps and crouched before the girl, bringing himself to her height, which is a trick he does — making himself smaller so people tell him larger things. "What's your name, child?"

She looked at him. Rainwater eyes, mud-plated skin, that unnerving patience. And then she spoke again — a longer phrase this time, five or six words, and along the entryway shelf the whole line of mislabeled inventory shivered like a struck harp. The jar of sighs fogged from the inside. The downward-burning candle guttered and relit itself upside-down, flame licking at the wax below.

Corvane did not flinch. I will grant him that forever; the man does not flinch. But his hand came up slowly and touched the shelf as if checking it for fever.

"Gideon," he said, and his voice had gone gentle in the particular way it goes gentle when he is about to ruin someone. "Fetch Brother Quill. And Sister Maren. And anyone else who claims to know a language. Quietly."

* * *

They came, the scribes and linguists, in ones and twos, pretending curiosity and radiating greed. For two hours they circled the girl where she sat in the assessment room — a bare chamber with a table, two chairs, and nothing on any surface, a precaution I insisted on after the whistle incident. They tried High Ketterish. Old Vail. The trade-pidgin of the Salt Roads. Sister Maren attempted a dialect of the deep marshes that is mostly swallowed vowels. The girl listened to each attempt with the same mild attention she gave the furniture, and replied in her own tongue, and each time she replied, something in the room moved.

A water pitcher slid two inches left. A chair turned to face her. Once — and I felt this one in my teeth — she said a short word and the lamp above the table brightened, sharply, like a held breath released.

Brother Quill, the oldest of our linguists, a man who has forgotten more dead languages than the rest of us ever learned, finally threw down his notes and pronounced judgment. "It's not a dialect. It's not derivative. It shares no root with anything in the indexed families." His voice had the shaken quality of a man whose life's map has just been declared incomplete. "Either this child invented a language in isolation, which is impossible—"

"Nothing is impossible," Corvane murmured. "Some things are merely expensive."

"—or," Quill finished, glaring, "it predates the families. All of them. Which is worse than impossible, because impossible things can at least be studied."

I stood by the wall with my ledger open, recording observations, and I will confess what I recorded and what I didn't. I wrote down the movements of objects. I wrote down the times, the durations, the sequence of her utterances. What I did not write down — what I kept behind my teeth like a stolen coin — was that when she spoke, I *felt* the words. Not heard them. Felt them, low in the sternum, the way you feel a bell through a floor. As if her language weren't passing through the air at all but through the substance of things, and everything it touched remembered being spoken to.

And once — only once, when she thought no one was attending — she looked directly at me and said a single word, soft, almost kind.

Every key in the building turned over in its lock. Every one. Doors we had barred, cabinets we had sealed, the strongroom downstairs with its triple-warded door — all of them, one soft metallic chorus, unlocking at once.

Then locking again, a heartbeat later, as if embarrassed.

Nobody else noticed. I wrote: *subject vocalizations produce localized kinetic anomalies.* I did not write: *she unlocked everything, and I think she was telling me something.*

* * *

By the eleventh bell the scribes had given up and the girl had fallen asleep sitting upright in her chair, which somehow made everything worse. Children sleep like children — sprawled, boneless, trusting. She slept like a garrison soldier: upright, arms crossed, waking instantly when the door opened. I found myself wondering who had taught her to sleep like that, and what they'd been teaching her to wake for.

Corvane dismissed the others and remained, and I remained, because I am the lowest rank and also, I suspect, because he wanted witnesses to his mercy.

"We'll hold her for assessment," he said. It was said lightly, reasonably, the tone of a man discussing a shipment delay. "Standard procedure for undocumented acquisitions. Room and board, clothing, instruction. She'll want for nothing."

"She's a child," I said. "Not an acquisition."

Corvane smiled at me, and there it was again — that flash of the raw thing beneath the managed garden. "Gideon. Six years ago I pulled you out of a cell where you were going to die for a debt of forty crowns, and I gave you a desk, a wage, and a future. Do you know why?"

"Because of my penmanship."

"Because you look at a thing and see what it *is*." He tapped the ledger under my arm. "You haven't once asked what she's worth. Everyone else in this building is doing arithmetic behind their eyes. You're writing down what actually happened. That's rare. That's valuable." He glanced at the sleeping girl, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "She is going to make us all rich, Gideon. Or she is going to make us all very sorry. Assessment will tell us which. Now — catalogue her. Properly. Tonight."

He left me alone with her.

I stood there a long moment, pen hovering over a fresh page. In the end I wrote what the guild required:

*Item 4,417. One female child, approx. eight years, condition poor (mud). Language unknown/unindexed. Vocalizations produce anomalous effects on proximate objects. Provenance unknown. Valuation: pending assessment.*

My pen stopped there. Because beneath it, in the space where the ledger demands a final column — *disposition* — I wrote, before I could stop myself, in letters far too honest for any ledger:

*Do not let them hear her speak a whole sentence.*

I don't know why I wrote it. I didn't know then what it meant. I only knew that when she had unlocked every door in the building with one soft word, what I'd felt underneath the wonder — underneath the compass turning and the candle bending and the keys singing — was dread. Cold, patient, rising dread. Because I have catalogued impossible things for six years, and I know how the guild treats them. We dust them. We value them. We find the buyer.

And somewhere in my chest, in the place where the words had landed like stones in deep water, I understood that the most dangerous sentence in that room was not anything the girl might say.

It was the one Corvane had already begun: *assessment will tell us which.*

The girl slept on, upright, fists closed. On the shelf behind her, unnoticed by anyone but me, the bone whistle played its lullaby again — three soft notes, over and over, to a child who could not understand it and a man who wished he couldn't.

I blew out the lamp, and went to find more paper.
