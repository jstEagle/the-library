## 3. What the Masters Buy

There are three locks on the door to the restricted vaults, and I know all of them, because I am the man who writes down what goes through them.

That is the joke of my position, if you can call it a position. The Vellum Guild does not trust its ledger-keepers with the contents of the vaults — that would be like trusting a scale with the gold it weighs. But it trusts us with the *record* of the contents, which is a different thing entirely, and in my experience a far more dangerous one. A thief takes an object. A record-keeper learns what the objects are *for*. And once you know what a thing is for, you start asking who wants it, and once you start asking that, you are already halfway out the door with your coat on.

I had not yet put my coat on. I want that noted, somewhere, in whatever celestial ledger keeps accounts of men like me. Three nights after Ada arrived, I was still only a man walking the corridors at an hour when no honest clerk walks them, carrying a candle he had every legitimate reason to carry, past a door he had no legitimate reason to open.

The first lock was brass and honest. It yielded to the key I keep because Master Corvane gave it to me six years ago with the words *"you'll need this more than I will,"* and I had been too grateful, then, to wonder why a master of the guild needed his own vault key less than his apprentice did.

The second lock was iron and suspicious. It yielded to patience, a bent wire from my writing kit, and the particular shame of a man who has spent years cataloguing lock mechanisms and never once thought about what that knowledge was *for*.

The third lock was neither brass nor iron. It was a strip of vellum pasted across the seam of the door, covered in dense script, and when my candlelight touched it the script stirred like something waking.

I knew what it was. Every guildsman does; we just don't call it what it is. We call it a *seal*, as though sealing were a polite word. It was a word-lock, a spoken binding keyed to a specific voice — Corvane's voice, most likely, or the Voice of Keys, old Mistress Halloway who hadn't left her rooms in a decade. You could pick a brass lock with a wire. You could not pick a word with anything but the right mouth.

But I am a ledger-keeper, and here is the trade secret of ledger-keepers: we do not defeat protections. We *bypass* them, the way water bypasses a stone, by going around where nothing is watching.

The seal was on the door. The seal was not on the loading hatch three paces down the corridor, because the loading hatch was beneath the dignity of seals — it existed for crates of tallow and cheap ink, things no one would bother to steal. I have a narrow back and a narrower conscience, and I went through the hatch the way I have gone through everything in my life: sideways, quietly, and apologizing to nobody.

The air inside the restricted vaults tasted different. That sounds like nonsense until you've stood in it. The main floors of the guild smell of paper, tallow, and the green-metallic tang of bottled weather. The restricted vaults smelled of *age* — of dust that had been dust longer than the guild had been a guild, of leather gone soft as skin, of something faintly sweet underneath it all, like fruit rotting in a far room.

I stood up straight in the dark and let my eyes adjust, and I told myself the story I had been telling myself since Ada arrived: *I am doing my job. Inventory requires verification. The masters will thank me.*

It is remarkable how much a man can accomplish under cover of a lie he tells himself. Most of the great crimes in history were committed by people who believed they were filling out paperwork.

* * *

The restricted vaults were organized, I discovered, by year. Not by type of acquisition, not by value, not by any logic of use — by year, as though the guild's deepest business were simply a diary it kept of itself. Each alcove bore a painted numeral, and the numerals went back further than I could read by candlelight without moving, and I did not want to move, because moving meant committing.

So I stood there like a coward among the shelves and listened to the building breathe.

Then I heard, very faintly, from somewhere above me — muffled by floors and stone and the ordinary kindness of distance — a sound I knew. A child's voice, saying something that was not words, or was words in a language that predated the idea of languages, and beneath my feet the shelf nearest me shivered, and a jar of preserved moths rattled against its neighbor like teeth chattering.

Ada. Two floors up, in the assessment room, awake at this hour, speaking into the dark the way children speak when they think no one is listening. And even filtered through stone, even secondhand, her voice moved the world.

They had her behind a locked door two floors up, and she was still rearranging the furniture of reality with her bedtime murmurs. What would she do, I wondered, if she ever spoke clearly? If she ever spoke *on purpose*?

I stopped telling myself I was doing my job. Lies require energy, and I wanted mine for walking.

The recent alcoves held what I expected: acquisitions logged but not yet priced. A mirror that showed the viewer's room as it would look if they had never been born in it. A compass whose needle pointed at whatever the holder most regretted. A wedding ring recovered from a drowned village, still warm. Ordinary miracles, each with its tag in Corvane's precise hand, each awaiting a buyer.

It was the older alcoves that were wrong.

The tags down there were not in Corvane's hand. They were in a hand I didn't recognize — older, spikier, the letters leaning forward like runners — and they were not tags at all, really. They were *receipts*. And the items they described were not mirrors or rings or bottled storms.

They were people.

The first one I pulled read: *Acquisition: female speaker, approx. age nine, acquired Veil's Edge market, spring. Vocal capacity: forty-one distinct sigil-forms confirmed. Sold: yes. Buyer: [entry struck through]. Price: eleven hundred crowns and one favor, nature undisclosed. Notes: subject wept for three days, then ceased speech entirely. Value diminished. Recommend earlier sale in future acquisitions.*

*Recommend earlier sale.* As though the flaw in their process were that they'd waited too long to sell a child. As though the tragedy were a pricing error.

My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that. I had always assumed that in the moment of true horror a man's hands would shake, and instead mine were perfectly steady, the way they are steady over a ledger, because some part of me had apparently decided that this too was paperwork, and paperwork must be handled cleanly.

I pulled another receipt. Then another. Then I stopped pulling them individually and simply took the whole bundle for the last thirty years and carried it to the floor, where I sat with my candle and read the guild's true history by inches.

Speakers. All of them speakers. Boys, girls, one woman of sixty who had been sold with the note *"subject claims her words are prayers; unverified."* Acquired at markets, at crossroads, from families who sold them with receipts of their own, from orphanages that charged a finder's fee. Assessed, catalogued, priced. Sold to buyers whose names had been struck through so thoroughly the vellum wore thin — struck through not once but many times, gouged, as though whoever redacted them had been angry, or frightened, or both.

And then, at the bottom of nearly every receipt, the same final line, in the same spiky hand, added later, in different ink:

*Status: lapsed.*

Not dead. Not returned. Not resold. *Lapsed* — the word we used in the ledgers for stock that ceased to exist in any way that mattered. A bottled storm that lost its storm was lapsed. A mirror that cracked was lapsed. And these children, these forty-one-sigil girls and prayer-speaking women, had been bought by buyers whose names even the guild did not write down, and then they had *lapsed*, every one of them, within a year of sale, and no one had ever asked how, or why, or what it was about the buying that used them up.

Except someone had asked. Because beneath the last receipt in the bundle — the oldest one, sixty years deep, the vellum brown as tea — there was a loose sheet in a hand I did know. Small, cramped, hurried. A note tucked behind the official records like a confession hidden in a hymnal:

*Buyer pays in advance now. Buyer pays double for whole sentences. Buyer instructs: do not teach the subjects to read. Do not let the subjects speak to each other. Do not let the subjects hear themselves. If a subject begins composing — connecting forms into sequence — terminate the acquisition immediately and report the loss. The buyer will absorb the cost.*

*Terminate the acquisition.* Kill the child, in the plain grammar of clerks. But note what the buyer feared: not escape, not theft, not even defiance. *Composition.* A speaker beginning to connect her words into sentences. A child who might, given time and privacy and the terrible creative instinct of children, begin to say something *whole*.

I sat on the cold floor of the vault with sixty years of vanished children spread around me, and I thought about Ada, two floors up, murmuring to herself in the dark because no one had taught her not to.

She composed constantly. She couldn't help it. Her words reached out and touched each other the way hands reach for hands. Every hour she spent in the guild's keeping, alone in that assessment room, talking to the walls, she was doing the one thing the buyer had ordered terminated.

And Corvane knew. Corvane had to know. He had signed the latest assessments himself; I'd watched him do it, patient and kind, praising her appetite, noting with satisfaction that she was "unusually fluent." He wasn't documenting a marvel. He was fattening inventory.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the candle to drown twice and need relighting. Long enough for my legs to go numb and complain about it when I finally rose.

I put the receipts back exactly as I'd found them — a lifetime of habit is not so easily burned — except for one. The oldest one, the woman who claimed her words were prayers. I folded it small and put it inside my shirt, against my chest, which is where I suppose I thought a person should be kept.

Then I climbed out through the tallow hatch, resealed nothing, picked up my candle, and walked back through the sleeping guild like a ghost reviewing its own haunts.

At the top of the stairs I nearly walked into Sef, the night porter, half-asleep over his cup.

"Late for a wander, Almeida."

"Inventory," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "Corvane wants the restricted stock verified before the assessment concludes."

Sef grunted, satisfied, and I understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that I had just lied fluently, easily, *well* — and that the guild had taught me this the way it taught me my letters, patiently, over years, without either of us noticing the lesson.

In my cell I lit my lamp and took out the folded receipt and smoothed it flat on my desk, next to the working ledger I kept for the day's entries. The two documents lay side by side, and I looked at them for a long time.

On the left: sixty years of children, sold and struck through and lapsed.

On the right: tomorrow's entry, waiting for my hand. *Item: one female child, designation pending. Status: held for assessment.*

Held for assessment. Held for *pricing*. And I was the man who wrote the numbers.

Here is the thing about loyalty that no one tells you while they're earning it: it doesn't crack all at once, like ice, with a sound. It cracks the way a ledger page cracks — along a fold you made yourself, years ago, pressing too hard, and you don't notice until the day you bend it wrong and the whole page comes away in your hand.

Corvane fed me when I was starving. Corvane taught me my figures when I came off the streets with dirt under my nails and a debt I couldn't count. Everything I am, I am because he looked at a thieving scrap of a boy and saw a ledger-keeper instead of a corpse. I have repaid him six years of exact, invisible, faithful work, and I would have repaid him sixty, gladly, and if he had asked me tomorrow to walk into the sea carrying his ledgers I would have asked *which tide*.

And he was going to sell a child. He was going to sell her the way he'd sell a storm in a bottle, to a buyer whose name he gouged out of his own records, and when she was used up he would write *lapsed* in his beautiful hand and close the entry, and some future apprentice would file it in an alcove by year, and that would be the whole of her.

Unless.

I am not a brave man. Let the record show. I am a careful man, a sideways man, a man who goes through hatches rather than doors. But I sat there in my cell with the receipt against my chest and I found that my fear had changed shape. It was no longer the fear of being caught. It was the fear of the entry I would write tomorrow, in my own hand, in my own ink — the entry that would make me, formally, officially, in the language of the guild, complicit.

There is a version of me that writes that entry. I know him well. He is careful and grateful and he owes everything to a kind man with a kind plan, and he writes the number, and he sleeps, and in ten years he is Corvane.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened for her. Two floors up, through stone, I could almost imagine I heard it again — that voice that was older than kingdoms, murmuring to itself, composing, connecting, saying something whole to no one.

Tomorrow, Corvane had told me, we would begin the formal translation work. He had said it warmly, with his hand on my shoulder. *"You have the ear for it, Gideon. She trusts you. It would mean everything to the guild."*

It would mean everything to the guild. Yes. I had finally read enough of the guild to believe that.

I did not sleep. Instead I did the only thing I truly know how to do. I opened a fresh page in my private book — the small one, the one no one knows exists — and at the top, in my neatest hand, I wrote:

*Item: one girl, called Ada. Words: living. Price: —*

And I left the price blank, and stared at it, and downstairs somewhere a door knocked softly, as doors do, in a building full of people who buy impossible things — and I began, very quietly, to plan.
