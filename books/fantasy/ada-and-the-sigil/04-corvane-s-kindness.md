## 4. Corvane's Kindness

### Gideon

Master Corvane sent for me at the seventh bell, which was suspicious in itself. Corvane never sent for anyone before breakfast. The man believed — and had said so, often enough to constitute doctrine — that a mind asked to work before it was fed would produce nothing but resentment and arithmetic errors.

I found him in his study on the third floor, the one with the good light and the bad decisions. He was standing at the window with his back to me, hands clasped behind him in the posture he reserved for moments when he wanted to be mistaken for a statue of some forgotten benefactor. On the desk between us sat two cups and a small pot of the expensive tea, the kind brewed from leaves that had allegedly been carried over the Spine Mountains by monks who no longer existed. I had seen that pot exactly four times in six years. Three of those times, someone had died shortly after.

"Sit down, Gideon."

I sat. The chair was soft. That was worse.

"You're wondering why you're here." He turned, smiling, and poured the tea himself rather than ringing for a servant. Corvane pouring tea was like watching a duke shovel his own coal. It was meant to mean something. "You're wondering if this is about the girl."

"It crossed my mind," I said carefully.

"Then let me put your mind at rest." He handed me a cup and settled into the chair opposite, folding his long frame into it with the ease of a man who owned every chair he'd ever sat in. "This is not about the girl. This is about you."

He said *you* the way other men said *son*, and I felt the old warmth rise in my chest like smoke, and I hated myself for feeling it, because I had spent three days in the vaults learning what happened to things this guild assessed, and warmth from Master Corvane now arrived pre-suspected, like coin from a stranger.

"Do you remember," he said, "the night you came to us?"

"Most of it."

"Most," he repeated, amused. "You were seventeen. You'd walked out of Almeida's bindery with nothing but a satchel of stolen nibs and a burn scar you wouldn't explain, and you stood in our doorway in the rain looking like a debt collection made flesh. Do you know what I saw?"

"A runaway," I said. "A liability."

"I saw a boy who had taught himself ledger-keeping from a master who beat him for reading past his station." Corvane's voice went gentle, which was its most dangerous register. "I saw someone the world had already tried to erase once, and failed. I told the council we should take you in. Vessal wanted to turn you away. Halvard wanted to sell you to the Salt College as an indentured clerk. I outvoted them both."

I knew parts of this story. I had heard fragments of it over the years, always at moments when my loyalty needed shoring up, like mortar applied to a wall during a storm. But I had never heard it all at once, laid end to end, and hearing it whole was different. Hearing it whole was like being shown the blueprint of your own cage and being told, warmly, that the bars were a gift.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked.

"Because you've been asking questions." He said it without rancor, without even much emphasis, the way a physician confirms a symptom. "The vault archivist reported you. Third restricted gallery, records of prior acquisitions. You have a gift for invisibility, Gideon, but not in my house. I built this house. I know where its floorboards creak."

My tea sat untouched in my hands. I watched the surface tremble very slightly and pretended it was the building settling.

"I wasn't prying," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You were frightened. There's a difference, and I forgive it. You read the files on the previous speakers and you thought: *this is what becomes of her.*" He leaned forward. "Let me tell you what actually becomes of her, since you clearly won't sleep until someone does."

He rose and went to the window. Below, the courtyard was waking — apprentices hauling crates, the bottled storm from my last inventory being crated for transport to some buyer whose name I was never given. Ordinary commerce. Impossible things changing hands.

"The buyers we serve are not monsters," Corvane said. "They are collectors. Scholars. Some few of them are powers, yes — princes, colleges, things older than either — but every one of them wants the same thing the guild wants: understanding. Control. The girl speaks a language that predates every kingdom on this map. Do you understand what that means? Not in the abstract. Practically. It means there is knowledge in her skull that could unmake wars or win them. Knowledge that is currently locked inside a feral child who will one day die of a fever like any other child, and take it all with her into the ground."

"So we preserve it," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended.

"We preserve it," he said, pleased. "We translate it. We make it safe. And the girl — the girl will want for nothing. She'll be fed, clothed, educated, protected. Better here than in any ditch she might have crawled into. You of all people should understand what it is to be taken in off the street by this guild and *made something of*."

There it was. Six years of warm meals and a dry bed and a name spoken with respect in certain rooms, all of it stacked neatly behind that sentence like inventory on a shelf. He wasn't wrong. That was the poison of it. Every word he said was true, and every true word pointed away from the files I'd read — the speakers sold, the buyers unnamed, the entries ending mid-sentence because the subjects themselves ended mid-sentence.

"And if translation requires... extraction?" I asked. "If her language costs her something? You've seen what happens when she speaks. Yesterday she healed my hand and nearly fainted doing it. These words aren't free, Master. Someone pays for each one."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — quick, shuttered, gone. If I hadn't been watching for it I'd have missed it entirely, and I understood then that he already knew more than he was saying, and had known longer than I had.

"All knowledge costs," he said mildly. "That has never stopped anyone worth mentioning. Finish your tea, Gideon. And—" he paused at the door, hand on the frame, not turning around, "—whatever you read in the vaults, remember who pulled you out of the rain. Loyalty isn't a debt here. It's a home. Try not to confuse the two."

He left. I sat alone with the expensive tea going cold, and I did the thing I do best, the thing that has kept me alive through every master I've ever served: I took out my mental ledger, and I entered the morning under a new column.

*Corvane's kindness,* I wrote. *Cost: unknown. Interest accruing.*

### Corvane

Gideon Almeida believes himself invisible. It is his great talent and his great flaw, and I confess I cultivated both, because a man who watches everything while believing no one watches him is the most useful instrument a house like ours can own.

He lied to me beautifully over the tea. I let him. A lie tells you where a man's treasure is buried better than any confession, and Gideon's treasure was obvious: the girl. He had looked at that mud-caked creature for perhaps two days before deciding she was his responsibility, which was foolish of him and instructive for me. Sentiment, in a ledger-keeper, is a vulnerability. In a keeper of ledgers, it is a door left unlocked.

I waited until the seventh bell rang him out of my study, then I wrote a letter.

Not a guild letter — nothing with our seal, nothing that touched our books. I wrote it on plain stock, in a plain hand, and I sealed it with wax bought from a chandler in the undercity who owed me more than money. Then I called for Tam and gave him instructions that would have sounded, to any listener, like an errand of no consequence: deliver this to the Widow Sarr at the Lamplighters' Rest, wait for a reply, tell no one, including especially yourself, what the letter contains.

Tam was not clever enough to be curious. That is why I keep him.

While he ran, I sat at my window and considered the problem the way I consider all problems: as a ledger.

*Asset:* one child, female, approximately eight years, speaking a language attested nowhere in forty centuries of guild records. Preliminary observation suggests — suggests, I emphasize, because I have learned to distrust the word *proves* — that her speech produces physical effects. Object displacement. Tissue repair. The report on the hand incident had reached me within the hour, as reports reach me, and I had read it three times. A word that closes flesh. I have bought storms in bottles and grief in jars and a knife that remembers its victims, and I have never once purchased anything so valuable as a word that heals.

*Liability:* the same asset is a child, and children are unpredictable, and this one had been found alone, which meant someone had either lost her or discarded her, and either way there existed somewhere a party with a prior claim.

*Unknown:* the buyer.

Ah, the buyer. Let me be honest with this page, whoever reads it, since I am honest nowhere else. There is a buyer. There has been a buyer since before the girl ever knocked on our door — or rather, since before our door knocked on her, which is closer to the truth of how these things arrive. A letter came to me eleven months ago from a party I am not permitted to name, offering a sum I am not permitted to record, for delivery of a speaker of the Old Tongue, intact, alive, and *complete*. Eleven months ago there was no such speaker known to exist anywhere in the fractured realm. Whoever wrote that letter knew something the guild did not. Knew she was coming. Perhaps knew where to find her.

I have been in this trade thirty years. I have learned that when a buyer knows more than you do, you have exactly two options: become indispensable to them quickly, or become invisible forever. I chose, long ago, to be indispensable. It pays better.

The girl's words, whatever they are, are being collected. Collected implies a set. A set implies completion. And completion — this is the part I did not say aloud to Gideon, the part I kept folded away like a bill of lading for cargo too dangerous to display — completion implies that when the set is finished, something happens. Buyers do not pay fortunes for incomplete sentences. They pay for endings.

So: I need a translator. Not a guild linguist — the guild's scholars are competent and loyal and therefore useless for work that must stay beneath the guild's own sightlines. I need someone brilliant, ruined, and purchasable.

Which describes precisely one person in this city.

When Tam returned near midnight, he brought back a reply in the same plain hand: three lines, no signature, and a smell of cheap gin soaked so deep into the paper it had become part of its structure.

*I heard you found one. Half payment now, half on delivery of a working grammar. And Corvane — if this is another wild goose like the Ashen Codex, I will come to your house and scream your secrets out the window to the whole Street of Scribes.*

I smiled. Vellian Sarr had been expelled from the Royal College of Languages nineteen years ago for forging a dead emperor's correspondence well enough to fool the emperor's own descendants, and had spent the years since drinking, feuding, and producing, in occasional bursts between benders, translations of a quality that made grown scholars weep with envy. She was bitter, unreliable, extortionate, and the finest living reader of dead scripts.

Perfect.

I wrote back: terms, half payment enclosed, and a single instruction — *come to the house by the servant's entrance, Tuesday, after second bell. Bring your ears. What I have for you cannot be written down.*

Because that was the difficulty, you see. We had recordings — Vessal's wax cylinders, the scratchy phonographic attempts, the scribes' desperate phonetic transcriptions full of gaps and guesses. But a language that rearranges shelves when spoken aloud does not survive transcription. Its power lives in the sounding of it, and so my translator would need to hear the girl directly.

Which meant bringing Vellian Sarr into the guild, secretly, past my colleagues, into the presence of an asset whose value I had not yet declared to the council.

Risky. Reckless, even. I turned the risk over in my mind the way Gideon turns a coin across his knuckles, and I found, as I always find, that the fear of loss was smaller than the appetite for gain. If the girl's language was what I suspected — if it was a piece of something vast, something the old world had spent oceans of blood to bury — then the guild that controlled it would not merely be rich. The guild would be *necessary*. And necessity, not gold, is the only currency that buys survival in times like these.

I sealed the reply. Outside my window the city lay dark and dripping, and somewhere in it, in a room above a tavern, a disgraced woman was about to become the second person alive to hear the Old Tongue spoken fresh.

The first, of course, was me. Three nights ago, through the assessment room door, when the girl woke screaming from a nightmare and said six words in a voice like a bell falling down a well, and every candle in the corridor guttered toward her as though bowing.

I had stood in the dark with my heart hammering like an apprentice's, and I had thought, with perfect clarity:

*It remembers us. The language remembers what we did.*

I blew out my candle and went to bed, and slept, as I always do, extremely well.

* * *

### Gideon

I did not sleep well. I spent the night doing what I do instead: counting.

Corvane's story of the rainy doorway — I checked it against my memory and found it polished, too polished, the way a seller polishes a narrative he has told many times. The details were right. The sequence was right. But the *emphasis* was new. In six years he had never once mentioned outvoting Halvard for me. Tonight he had. Tonight, of all nights, three days after I went poking through the vault files.

Kindness, timed, is a tool. I know because I use it. When Ada wouldn't eat, I didn't order her to; I ate first, slowly, making it look like the best thing in the world, until she crept over and stole from my plate. Kindness aimed is kindness weaponized. Corvane had aimed his at me tonight with a marksman's patience, and the target had been the exact place where my loyalty was cracking.

Which meant he knew it was cracking. Which meant the vault archivist's report was real, and Corvane's forgiveness was real, and both of those facts together meant only one thing: he was keeping me close because a close Gideon was more useful than a watched one.

Or — and this thought arrived around the fourth bell, unwelcome as a creditor — because a close Gideon would lead him to anything else I'd found. Anything I hadn't reported. Anyone I'd talked to.

I lay in my narrow bed above the counting room and listened to the guild breathe around me — the settling timbers, the night clerk's cough, the distant clink of Vessal working late among his cylinders. And beneath all of it, two floors down, the small sound I had learned to listen for: Ada, breathing in her sleep in the assessment room. Steady. Alive. Speaking, sometimes, in dreams — little murmurs, two words, never more, and each time she murmured I swear the lamp outside my window burned a shade steadier, as if the flame were listening too.

Two words was safe. The hedge-witch's rule — *never let them hear her speak whole* — held as long as the sentences stayed broken.

But sentences mend. That's what sentences do.

Around dawn I finally slept, and dreamed of the vault, and in the dream every file on the previous speakers opened at once, and every entry ended the same way, in the same neat hand, with the same closing line:

*Delivered complete. Buyer satisfied.*

And in the dream I understood, finally, what *complete* meant, and I woke with Ada's name in my mouth and the taste of cold tea in my throat, and downstairs, faintly, through three floors of old stone, I heard the servant's entrance open — and close again — very quietly, at an hour when no honest deliveries come.
