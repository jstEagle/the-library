## 5. The First Sigil

*Cold. Then hands. Then the word for cold, which is also the word for small.*

I woke in a room made of paper.

Not really paper — walls of shelves, and on the shelves things that hummed when I breathed near them. A jar with weather inside. A bird that was only a shadow but had a heartbeat anyway. The man-who-smells-of-ink had put a blanket over me that smelled like dust and lamp oil and someone else's life.

He thought I was asleep. He sat at his desk scratching marks into a book, and every few scratches he looked up at me to see if I'd noticed him looking.

I had noticed. I notice everything now. That is what waking feels like — everything arrives at once and none of it has names yet, except the ones I carry under my tongue like river stones.

The scar on his hand bothered me.

It bothered me the way a crooked picture bothers a wall. His left hand, across the back of it, an old burn gone silver and tight, pulling the skin between his fingers into little ridges. He touched it while he worked, the way you touch a thing you've stopped feeling but haven't stopped carrying. Thumb over the ridges. Thumb over the ridges. Again.

I knew a word for that.

I didn't know I knew it until it was already climbing my throat, warm as swallowed sunlight, pushing against my teeth like a bird that had been kept too long in a dark room.

Here is what no one tells you about words: they are not yours. You are theirs. They wait inside you folded up small, and when one unfolds, it takes some of you with it — a little warmth, a little memory, a little of the taste of your own name. You pay without counting because the paying happens before the knowing.

I said the word.

* * *

The word had a shape like water finding a crack.

It went out of me and across the room and into his hand, and where it went, the silver scar remembered being skin. I felt it happen from the inside, the way you feel rain through a roof — not seeing it, just knowing the weight of it. The ridges smoothed. The tightness loosened. Somewhere in his hand, years of hurt quietly closed their eyes.

And something in me went out with it.

I don't know how to explain what it's like to feel yourself get lighter in the wrong way. It wasn't relief. It was subtraction. A warm coin pulled out of my chest, and I could hear the space where it used to sit, ringing faintly, like a cup after the last sip.

The room tilted. The shelves leaned in close, curious. The bottled storm rattled its glass, excited, and I thought: *don't be excited, nothing good is happening,* and then the floor came up and met me very politely, the way floors do when you've disappointed them.

Before the floor got there, I saw the man's face.

He was holding his hand up in the lamplight, turning it over, turning it back. The scar was gone. Smooth skin, pale as new paper. And his face was doing several things at once — wonder, which looks like fear standing up straight; and fear, which looks like wonder lying down; and underneath both, something softer, the look people get when someone gives them a gift they don't believe they've earned.

Then he saw me falling, and all those faces fell off his face at once, and there was just a man dropping his pen and lunging, and catching me before the floor finished its sentence.

Warm arms. Ink smell. Heartbeat going fast under my ear, faster than mine, which was funny, since I was the one who'd just paid for the miracle.

"Ada." My name, in his mouth, careful, like it might break. "Ada, what did you do? What did you *do?*"

I wanted to say: *I fixed your hand.*

I wanted to say: *it cost more than you think, ask me tomorrow, if I still remember tomorrow.*

What came out instead was a sound like a wet leaf, and then darkness again, but a kinder darkness this time, the kind with a blanket in it.

* * *

When I opened my eyes again, the lamps were lower and he hadn't moved.

That's not true. He'd moved plenty — I could tell by the ink smudges now on his chin and the way three books lay open around him like dead birds — but he hadn't moved *away*. He was sitting on the floor beside my pallet with his back against the desk leg, and his healed hand was resting palm-up on his knee, and he was staring at it the way you stare at a door that has opened in a wall where there was never a door.

"You're awake," he said. "Good. Don't — here, drink this. It's terrible. Everything I make is terrible, but it's warm."

It was terrible. It tasted like a tree had been boiled. I drank all of it.

"Your hand," I said. My voice came out rusty, a hinge nobody oiled. Words in *his* language were heavy in my mouth, foreign coins, but I'd been collecting them. Listening through walls does that. "Better?"

His laugh came out crooked. "Better. Yes. Ten years of better, in about two seconds." He turned the hand over. In the lamplight the skin was unmarked, smooth, almost offended by its own perfection. "Ada, listen to me. What did it cost you? To do that."

I looked at him.

He was clever, this man-who-smells-of-ink. Cleverer than the loud ones upstairs who wore rings and said *assessment* like it was a kind word. He'd already gotten to the question the others wouldn't think to ask for weeks. Everyone would want to know *how did she do it, can she do it again, can we do it to her.* He wanted to know what it took *out*.

"I'm smaller," I said.

He frowned. "You're — what?"

I pressed my fist against my sternum, where the ringing space was. "In here. There was a — " I hunted for the words. His language didn't have the right ones; his language thought everything could be counted. "There was a warm thing. Now there's a hole shaped like it. I gave it away. It went into your hand."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere above us, footsteps crossed a ceiling, unhurried, belonging to someone whose world was still whole.

"That's not possible," Gideon said softly. Not angrily. The way you say *that's not possible* when you've watched it happen and are simply filing a complaint with the universe.

"It happened," I said. "Possible doesn't live here anymore."

He was quiet a long time. Then he held out his healed hand, palm up, an offering or a request — I couldn't tell which, and neither, I think, could he.

"Can I — may I see yours?" he asked. "Your hands. Just to look."

I let him take them. His fingers were gentle the way careful people are gentle, like everything they touch might be the last copy of something. He turned my palms over in the light. Nothing there. No mark, no scar, no price tag. That's the trick of it — the payment never shows on the outside. The ledger is kept somewhere no auditor can reach.

"They're fine," he murmured. "You look fine."

"I told you," I said. "Smaller."

Something moved behind his eyes then, something I would learn to name much later, when I knew him properly — guilt, arriving early, the way it always did with him, ahead of any crime worth feeling guilty about. He looked at my hands and then at his own perfect one and I watched him do arithmetic he hated: *she spent something of hers to fix something of mine, and I never asked her to, and I don't even know what I'm holding.*

"Never do that again," he said.

I blinked at him. "Fix you?"

"Not for me. Not for anyone. Not unless — " He stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like a man crossing ice. "Ada. There are people in this building who are going to find out what you can do. Some of them will be kind about it. The kindness will be worse than the cruelty, because it'll come with paperwork. When they ask you to speak your words for them, I need you to say no. Can you say no?"

"No," I said.

He stared at me. Then he huffed — half laugh, half despair — "Yes, good, exactly, that's the — yes."

I smiled. It hurt my face a little; smiling was another thing that cost something, these days. But it was worth the price. He was so easy to tip over, this man. All his cleverness and his careful ledgers, and one small girl saying *no* correctly knocked him sideways.

But the smile faded off my face on its own, because under the joke, under the warmth of the terrible tea, the hole in my chest was still ringing. And I had started, in the last hour, to count.

I had been full once. I knew that the way you know a dream — not the details, just the fact of it. Before the mud and the road and the guild door, I had been *full*, brimming, a whole library walking around in one small body. And now one book was missing from one shelf, and I could feel the gap the way a mouth feels a lost tooth: constantly, and worse at night.

How many words had I woken with? Hundreds. Thousands. More than kingdoms, the old dream-whispers said. More than the stars over the place I couldn't remember.

And how many did I have left?

I didn't say any of this to him. He was worried enough, and worry makes men do stupid brave things, and I liked him stupid-brave but alive. So instead I asked the other question, the one scratching at me.

"Gideon." His name sat strangely right in my mouth, like a key cut for a lock I didn't know I had. "Why did you keep looking at your scar? Before. Every night. Thumb, thumb, thumb. If you hated it so much, why did you keep touching it?"

He looked down at the hand. Flexed it once, twice, testing a freedom ten years old.

"I didn't hate it," he said finally. "It was — proof. Of the day I earned my place here. Master Corvane pulled me out of a fire, back when I was nobody, and this was the receipt." He laughed, short and hollow. "And now it's gone, and I keep waiting to feel lighter, and I don't. I just feel like I've lost an argument with myself."

I nodded slowly, because I understood that completely, though I couldn't have explained why, and the not-being-able-to-explain frightened me more than the losing did.

What was I, if my words were receipts? What was left of me when everything spendable was spent?

* * *

They came for me the next morning.

Not hunters, not yet — just two scribes with soft shoes and a wheeled chair with straps on it, and a woman with a clipboard and a smile that had been ironed flat. "Assessment," she said brightly, to Gideon, not to me. "Just routine. The masters are *very* excited about our new acquisition."

*Acquisition.* I rolled the word around. It fit me badly, like borrowed boots.

Gideon stepped into the doorway, casual as anything, a man merely leaning in a doorframe and not barricading it with his whole body. "She's feverish," he said. "Sick all night. Whatever you're assessing, you'll assess a sick child, and whatever she says will be wrong, and you'll write it down wrong, and the masters will build castles on your wrong notes. Wait two days. Get accurate data." He said *data* like a man laying down a winning card, and the clipboard-woman flinched, because accuracy was her religion and he'd just accused her of heresy.

Two days. He bought two days with a sentence, and I understood then that this quiet man fought with ledgers the way other people fought with swords, and that I had somehow ended up on the page he was defending.

But when they'd gone, he crouched down to my eye level, and the victory was gone out of his face, and what was left underneath was scared.

"Two days," he said. "Maybe less. Ada — last night, when you healed my hand. Did you choose that word? Or did it choose you?"

I thought about the bird unfolding in my throat, warm and impatient.

"It chose me," I said. "They all do. They're waiting for reasons."

"What do you mean, reasons?"

I put my fist against my chest again, against the ringing hole, and tried to make him understand with the only coins his language had given me so far.

"Every word in me is asleep," I said. "And they wake up when something near them needs them. Your hand needed. So the mending-word woke. And it took a piece of me to pay for the waking." I looked at him straight, because this part mattered most. "So don't need things loudly, Gideon. Not near me. Because I can feel them starting to wake up. All of them. And I don't think — "

My voice caught. Not from weakness. From the thing I'd only just noticed, the cold thing coiled under the warm ones, the thing I'd been refusing to look at since the floor came up to meet me.

"I don't think they all wake up *small*," I whispered. "Some of the words in me are very old, Gideon. Older than this building. Older than your kingdom. And last night, when I went under — one of them turned over in its sleep. And it *noticed me noticing it*."

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

Gideon had gone very still, the stillness of prey and scholar at once. Slowly, he reached out and took my hand — the empty one, the one with nothing left to give — and held it like a vow.

"Then we'd better figure out," he said quietly, "what else is sleeping in there. Before the masters do."

Above us, faint through the floorboards, someone began drawing bolts across the vault doors. One. Two. Three.

They were opening, not closing.

Downstairs, in the restricted dark, something was being taken out to meet me.
