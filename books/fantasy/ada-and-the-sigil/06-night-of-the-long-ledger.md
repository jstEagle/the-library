## 6. Night of the Long Ledger

There is a particular hour in a guild-house when the ledgers are honest and the people are not. It comes somewhere past midnight, after the last lamp in the masters' wing has guttered out and before the kitchen fires are stoked, when the only sound is the building settling on its old bones and the scratch of your own nib against paper. I had lived inside that hour for eleven years. It was the one part of my life that had never lied to me.

Which made it especially bitter that tonight I intended to make it lie.

I sat in the counting room with three candles lit instead of one — a small extravagance, but forgery by single candlelight leaves shadows where you don't want them — and the master inventory ledger open before me to the page where Ada now lived as an entry. *One (1) child, female, approx. eight years. Acquired: foundling, no fee. Condition: feral. Assessed value pending.*

*Assessed value pending.* Corvane's own hand had written that, four days ago, in ink so fine it looked like it was embarrassed to be there. Four days of assessment, and what had the assessment produced? A locked room at the end of the east corridor, a girl who watched me through the door's grate like I was the exhibit, and a linguist named Pellam Voss who smelled of cheap wine and expensive failure.

I dipped my nib. I did not write.

Here is the thing about falsifying guild records that nobody tells you because nobody survives to tell it: the Vellum Guild does not trade in impossible things because it likes wonder. It trades in impossible things because impossible things cannot be stolen twice. Every item in our vaults carried a provenance written into its very substance — the bottled storm knew whose shelf it sat on; the mirror that showed tomorrow sulked if you moved it without paperwork. The ledger was not a record of the guild's holdings. In a sense that had taken me years to understand and cost me sleep to accept, the ledger *was* the holdings.

So you could not simply cross out Ada and write *worthless*. The ledger would know. The ledger always knew. Master Corvane had once caught a junior clerk who'd shaved a decimal off a shipment of bottled thunder, and the way he'd done it was eerie: he hadn't checked the vault at all. He'd simply felt the ledger disagree with itself, the way you feel a room go cold.

What you needed, then, was not erasure. What you needed was a story the ledger would believe.

I turned back three pages, to the entry for the salamander eggs we'd lost two winters ago — spoiled in transit, buyer refunded, loss absorbed. Corvane's notation beside it read, in his tidy hand: *Stock degrades. This is why we catalogue quickly.*

Stock degrades. That was my story. Not that Ada was worthless — the ledger would never believe anything under this roof was worthless — but that she was *perishable*. That whatever made her valuable was bleeding out of her with every day she spent in a locked room, frightened and unfed on anything but bread and the occasional kindness I smuggled up under my coat. Feral children, I would write, lose their wildness in captivity, and their wildness was the commodity. By the time any buyer came to inspect her, she would be a mute, ordinary girl, and ordinary girls were worth less than the cost of feeding them.

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true, which is the best kind. The only problem was that every word of it was also a countdown — because if the guild believed her value was draining away, they would stop waiting patiently for buyers and start cutting her open to find out why.

I wrote anyway.

*Assessment update, third day:* my nib moved, and I made myself watch it move. *Subject exhibits progressive loss of anomalous vocalization under confinement. Utterances now non-verbal or near-verbal. Recommend reclassification from "sigil-bearing stock" to "foundling, mundane." Recommend removal from active inventory to reduce holding costs. Recommend disposition at earliest convenience.*

*Disposition at earliest convenience.* I stared at those words for a long time. They meant sell her cheap, pass her down to some provincial broker, lose her in the great gray river of human traffic that flows beneath every ledger ever kept. They meant she would vanish — which was the point — but they also meant she would vanish *alone*, into hands I could not choose, in a direction I could not follow.

That was the flaw in my plan, and I knew it going in, and I wrote it anyway, because a flawed plan executed beats a perfect plan admired. Get her off the guild's books first. Worry about geography later.

The trouble with geography arrived at my shoulder at half past two in the morning, in the form of a voice that said, "You're doing it wrong."

I did not jump. Eleven years in this house teaches you that jumping is a luxury. But my nib did skid, leaving a small comet-tail of ink across the word *disposition*, which felt appropriate.

Pellam Voss stood in the doorway of the counting room, holding a candle of his own and looking like a man who had been exhumed slightly ahead of schedule. He was the disgraced linguist Corvane had commissioned — chapter four of my ongoing education in how deep this went — a hollow-cheeked man with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had read too many things that were true. He'd been working in the scriptorium below for days now, transcribing Ada's speech from wax cylinders and memory, and he had the specific pallor of a man whose work was going too well.

"You're doing it wrong," he repeated, nodding at the ledger. "The falsification."

"I have no idea what you mean," I said, "and if I did, I'd note that announcing it in a doorway is a poor way to keep it."

"The doorway is empty and I checked." He came in anyway, uninvited, and set his candle next to mine so the three flames became four. "And you're doing it wrong because you're lying about the wrong thing. You've marked her as losing her value over time. Clever. Believable. Also fatal."

"Explain."

"Because Corvane isn't patient, Almeida. Patience is for men who think the prize will still be there tomorrow. Corvane has already seen enough of her to know the prize is *leaving* — that every word she speaks costs her something, and that the stock is depreciating in real time whether your ledger says so or not. Your entry doesn't buy her months. It buys her days. Maybe less. The moment he reads 'disposition at earliest convenience,' do you know what he'll do?" Voss smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because there was no malice in it at all — only the flat honesty of a man who had stopped being able to afford hope some years ago. "He'll agree. And then he'll convene the extraction while the asset still has assets left."

I put down the nib very carefully, because if I didn't put it down I was going to throw it.

"Then what would you suggest," I said, "since you're evidently an expert in fraud as well as dead languages?"

"I'm an expert in neither. I'm an expert in her language, which is worse for everyone involved." He pulled the stool from behind the second desk — my old master's desk, actually, though no one used it anymore — and sat, and leaned forward, and lowered his voice as though the ledger itself might be listening. Which, given what I've just told you about the ledger, perhaps it was.

"Do you know what I've been doing down in that scriptorium for six days? Corvane thinks I've been translating. I have been translating. But translation implies the two languages are strangers to each other, and hers isn't strange, Almeida. That's what has been keeping me awake. Her language is *familiar*. It's built on roots I've seen before — fragments in Etruscan survivals, in the pre-Imperial glosses, in the marginalia of books the Church burned and the Guild quietly bought. I spent twenty years of my life being laughed out of every academic hall on the coast for claiming those fragments belonged to a single coherent system. And then a mud-caked child walks into your guildhouse speaking it fluently, natively, the way you speak the tongue you learned at your mother's knee."

"What system?" I asked, though some cold part of me had already begun assembling the answer, the way you assemble a shape out of shadow before you're willing to look at it directly.

"A binding," Voss said. "A very large one. The largest anyone ever attempted, if the fragments are to be believed — and I have staked my entire ruined reputation on believing them." He turned his candle so the flame threw his face into relief, all planes and hollows. "The old texts talk about a war at the beginning of everything worth remembering. Two empires. A power that walked between them wearing whatever shape it pleased, that spoke and unmade. And the texts talk about how it ended — not with a sword, not with a wall, but with a *sentence*. A sentence spoken by — the texts disagree — and each word of that sentence was a lock, and the locks were scattered, hidden, buried in bloodlines and tongues and places, so that no one could ever gather them again and speak them aloud in order."

"And Ada—"

"Is not carrying the sentence." He shook his head slowly. "She *is* the sentence. Or she carries it the way a riverbed carries water — the words live in her, come out of her mouth whole and alive, and every one of them is a piece of the oldest lock in the world. When she healed your hand—" he saw me flinch, and nodded, unsurprised; of course he'd heard, news traveled through a guildhouse like damp through plaster "—she didn't cast a spell. She spoke a fragment of the binding, bent to a small purpose. And it took something out of her to do it, because those words were never meant to be spent. They were meant to be *kept*."

The candles had burned low enough that the wax was pooling, little white graves around each flame. I became aware that my hands were shaking, and I put them flat on the ledger, on my own fresh forgery, as though pressing the tremor into the page would transfer it somewhere useful.

"Who would want them?" I asked. "Corvane wants to sell them, yes, but sell them to *whom*? Who buys pieces of a binding?"

"Somebody who wants the lock opened," Voss said simply. "Somebody paying *dearly* — Corvane's correspondence is not subtle, Almeida, and I have transcribed enough guild letters in my disgrace to recognize the shape of a desperate buyer. The offers aren't getting smaller as the collection grows. They're getting larger. Exponentially. Whoever is on the other end of those letters believes they are close to complete, and they are willing to fund a small war to finish. And here is the part you should really take home with you—" he tapped the ledger, right on my forged line, *disposition at earliest convenience* "—a buyer like that doesn't purchase a depreciating asset and let it walk away. If your forgery works, if Corvane marks her as failing stock, the guild won't sell her to some provincial broker. They'll sell her *fast*, to the highest bidder, before the goods spoil. You won't have saved her. You'll have expedited her."

I sat with that for a while. Outside, the rain that had been threatening all evening finally committed, ticking against the shutters like a clerk counting late fees.

"So I've written her death warrant in nicer handwriting," I said.

"You've written a draft," Voss said. "Drafts can be revised. That's rather the point of you, isn't it? The ledger-keeper. The man who makes the numbers say what they must." He rose, took his candle, and paused at the doorway — and for a moment, standing there in his shabby coat with his ruined reputation and his terrible knowledge, he looked almost like a man delivering a eulogy for someone still breathing. "Reconsider what you're lying about. Don't tell the ledger she's worthless. Tell the ledger she's *dangerous*. Tell it that keeping her is a liability the guild can't insure. Corvane is greedy, but greed has a cousin called fear, and fear moves faster. Make him afraid to hold her, and he'll want her gone — truly gone, quietly, without ceremony, without extraction, without letters to eager buyers. Give her to the dark and pray the dark is kinder than the market."

"That's insane," I said. "You're suggesting I forge a *liability* onto a living child."

"I'm suggesting," Voss said, "that you have until dawn, because I am expected to deliver my first full transcription to Corvane at breakfast, and once he reads what I've found, no ledger in this house will matter to him at all." He lifted his candle in something that was either a salute or an apology. "Choose which lie buys the most time. It's the only currency you have left."

And he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor toward the scriptorium, leaving me alone with four candles burning down to nothing and a page full of my own careful treachery.

* * *

I did not revise immediately. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own skull: I sat there for the better part of an hour doing arithmetic, because that is what I do, it is the only prayer I know. And the arithmetic was ugly.

Option one: leave the forgery as written. Ada gets marked as failing stock, sold fast to the desperate buyer, and becomes a set of keys in a stranger's fist. Timeline: days. Outcome: catastrophic, but predictable.

Option two: rewrite her as dangerous. A liability. Something the guild should want rid of *quietly*. If it worked, she might be smuggled out the back door within the week — dumped, discarded, alive. If it failed — if Corvane saw through it, if the ledger rejected the lie, if fear made him cautious instead of careless — then she'd go to the extraction table sooner, with everyone watching her more closely than before. Timeline: unknown. Outcome: better best case, worse worst case.

Every honest ledger-keeper knows the rule: never trade a certain bad outcome for an uncertain one. Certainty is the whole point of bookkeeping. Certainty is why the numbers exist — so that at the end of the day, however ugly the day was, the columns balance and you know exactly where you stand.

But every honest ledger-keeper, I suspect, has one night where he looks at the columns and realizes they balance perfectly around a hole where a person used to be. And on that night, the rule stops applying.

I thought about her hands. Small, cracked with cold, always half-curled as though still holding something from wherever she'd been before the guild found her. I thought about the way she'd watched me through the grate, and how on the fourth day she had pressed one finger against the iron, and how I had been fool enough to press mine back, and how she had studied the contact with the gravity of a scholar confirming a hypothesis: *this one, maybe. This one lies differently.*

I thought about my own name, which I had not spoken aloud in this house in eleven years except to sign documents, because a runaway apprentice learns early that a name is a handle and handles get pulled.

Then I scraped my knife across the page — carefully, cleanly, lifting the wet-ink forgery away like a scab — and began again.

*Assessment update, third day. Subject's vocalizations confirmed as operative sigil-work of pre-Imperial origin. Each utterance produces material effect at escalating scale and unpredictable direction. Subject cannot control output. Containment protocols insufficient. Recommend immediate reclassification from "inventory" to "hazard." Recommend removal from premises by discreet means at earliest opportunity. Recommend NO further documentation of subject's capabilities, verbal or written, as documentation itself may constitute activation risk.*

Three lies braided together: that she was uncontrollable, that the guild couldn't hold her safely, and — the masterstroke, if it was one — that writing about her was itself dangerous. Because if there was one thing the Vellum Guild respected above profit, it was the sanctity of the document. Tell these people that the *paperwork itself* was contaminated and they would seal her file, stop asking questions, and start wanting her off the books the way you want a fire out of a library.

I signed it with my mark, pressed the guild's wax, and closed the ledger. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled, and I chose to believe it was just the house.

Dawn was maybe two hours off. At breakfast, Voss would deliver his transcription, and Corvane would read it, and whatever happened after that would happen fast. Which meant that if my new lie worked — if Corvane decided Ada was a hazard to be quietly disposed of — I would have perhaps a week, maybe less, to arrange her "discreet removal" myself. To become, in effect, the guild's own disposal mechanism, and steer it somewhere the guild would never look.

Out the back door. Into the flooded undercity. Into the dark.

I snuffed the candles one by one and sat a moment longer in the black, listening to the rain, feeling the exact shape of the line I had just crossed. There is a moment in every forgery — I imagine there is a moment in every crime — when the ink dries and the thing you did stops being a plan and becomes a fact. Facts are heavier than plans. Facts have weight, and weight has consequences, and consequences, unlike entries, cannot be scraped out with a knife.

I had spent eleven years keeping other people's impossible things catalogued and safe.

Tonight, for the first time, I had entered myself into the ledger — as a liar, a thief, and, as of breakfast, very possibly a fugitive.

Upstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard the east corridor door rattle, and a small voice begin to hum a wordless tune that made the candle smoke curl upward against the draft.

The night was nearly over. Whatever came next, it would not wait for morning.
