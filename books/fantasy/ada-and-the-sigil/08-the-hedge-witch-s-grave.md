## 8. The Hedge-Witch's Grave

The road to Maren's cottage had not improved since the last time I walked it, which was also the first time I walked it, which was the day I buried her.

Three years of rain and root had done their patient work on the path — stones shifted, brambles advanced like a slow army, and somewhere along the way a birch had fallen across the trail at exactly the height of a man's chest, as if the forest itself had decided to check papers. I lifted Ada over it. She weighed almost nothing. Feral children, I was learning, are built out of bird bones and suspicion.

She had not spoken since the ford.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only prayer I know how to say. She had not spoken one word in two days. Not to me, not to the fire, not to the dark that pressed against our little ring of light each night with the confidence of something owed money. The word she'd spent drowning the Quiet Brothers — the sigil that had pulled the river up like a sheet being snapped over a bed — had taken my name with it. *Gideon.* Gone. Filed away in whatever ledger her language kept, balanced against four men's lives, and marked paid.

She looked at me now the way she'd looked at me the first night: a stranger, tall, tired, carrying a bag too heavy for him. Except this time she let me carry her over obstacles. That was new. Or old. It's difficult to keep track of which kindnesses you've already performed when your audience resets.

"You can put me down," she said, in her ordinary voice — the flat, unlettered voice she used for things that weren't magic, the voice that sounded like any mudlark child in any city gutter. "I can walk."

"I know you can walk."

"Then why did you lift me?"

"Because the birch was rude," I said, "and someone had to show it who's in charge."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then, mercifully, she said nothing else, and we walked on through the green gloom toward the only place left in the world where anyone might tell me what to do.

Maren Ostrova had been my master for eleven years, back when I was nobody's apprentice but hers — before Corvane, before the Vellum Guild, before ledgers full of bottled weather and saints' knucklebones. She was a hedge-witch, which is a polite way of saying she practiced medicine without a license and theology without permission, and she taught me three things: how to read, how to keep accounts, and how to recognize when a kind face is doing arithmetic.

She died in autumn, alone, in the cottage at the end of this road, and I buried her under the rowan tree because she'd once said — laughing, wine-drunk, entirely serious — that if anything ever came for her, she wanted to be found by the birds first. Rowans keep off ill luck. They also keep off most sensible conversation, being trees.

I hadn't been back. Three years, and I hadn't come back. There is a particular cowardice in avoiding a grave, and I had worn mine like a guild badge.

Now here I was, needing her more than I had ever needed anyone living, bringing her the one thing she might have understood: a child made of words, hunted by the people who had raised me.

* * *

The cottage came into view at dusk, and my heart did something complicated and embarrassing.

It was smaller than memory insisted. Memory always inflates the places that hurt us; grief is a bad bookkeeper, rounding everything up. The cottage was one room and a lean-to, stone walls furred with moss, roof sagging like an old man's shoulders. The garden had gone fully to war — nettles holding the east bed, some triumphant bramble occupying the herb rows like a conquering general. But the rowan tree stood where I'd planted my grief, taller now, its berries red as spilled wax against the grey sky.

And the door was shut.

That sounds like nothing. Let me explain why it was everything. When I'd left — after the burial, after the shoveling, after the terrible arithmetic of digging a hole for the woman who taught you to count — I had closed the door. Of course I had. I was her apprentice; order was the only liturgy I knew. But doors in abandoned cottages do not stay shut. Wind argues with them. Weather wins. Three years should have left that door hanging open or hanging off.

It was shut. Firmly. As though from inside.

Ada stopped at the edge of the garden and would go no further. She stood among the nettles, bare feet deliberate on the ground, staring at the cottage with an expression I had learned to fear — the look she got when her eyes were reading something her mouth couldn't yet translate.

"What is it?" I asked.

Her lips moved. No sound. Whatever she saw, she wasn't going to spend words on it. Smart girl. Words were currency now, and she'd already learned what the exchange rates were.

I went to the door alone.

My hand shook. I noted this with professional detachment, the way I note discrepancies in a ledger — *one tremor, unexplained, carried forward* — and pushed the door open.

Inside: dust, yes. Cobwebs, certainly. A mouse had held court in the flour crock and left the evidence of a long administration. But the room was otherwise exactly as she'd kept it. Her chair by the hearth. Her table with its scarred top. The shelf of jars — dried nettle, comfrey, a suspension of willow bark, and the one jar of honey she guarded like a dragon guards gold, still sealed, still golden, absurdly intact after three years. On the wall, her bundle of dried herbs hung in their careful bundles, labeled in her crabbed hand.

And on the table, in the exact center, weighted down with her river-stone paperweight — the smooth grey one she'd claimed held the ghost of every argument she'd ever won — there was a letter.

Addressed to me.

*Gideon Almeida,* it said, in her handwriting, in ink that had not faded, on paper that had not yellowed. My name spelled correctly, which she only bothered to do when she was angry or dying.

I stood in the doorway for a long time. Long enough that Ada crept inside behind me, drawn despite herself, and stood at my elbow looking at the letter with those eyes that read the world in older alphabets.

"She wrote this," I said stupidly. "Before she died."

"Obviously," said Ada.

There was no obviously about it. But I took the stone off the paper, and the paper didn't move, didn't curl, didn't crumble, and I sat down in her chair — her chair, which still smelled faintly of woodsmoke and pennyroyal — and I unfolded my dead master's last words.

* * *

Maren wrote the way she brewed medicine: strong enough to strip paint, measured out in doses.

*If you're reading this, then either you've finally grown a spine and come home, or the world has finally gotten bad enough to send you running back. Both, probably. You were always both.*

*I don't have time to be gentle, so I'll be brief, and you'll forgive me later or you won't, which is the same thing.*

*You remember the winter you were fourteen. The fever. The night I sat up with you and talked nonsense to keep you breathing. Some of what I said was nonsense. Some of it was a spell, and you were too busy dying to notice the difference. That's the trick of hedge-craft, boy: half of it is chemistry and half of it is grammar, and the patient never needs to know which half saved him.*

*Here is what I have never told you. Before you, I had another student. A girl. Clever as a knife. She had a gift — the old gift, the speaking, the true tongue, the one they used to build the bindings back when the world was young and stupid. I taught her control. I taught her to speak small. One word at a time. Never a sentence.*

*They found her anyway. Not the guild — worse. Buyers. Men with soft hands and hard ledgers. They took her apart word by word, Gideon. Sold her voice in pieces to people who wanted pieces of the old power, and by the end she couldn't say her own name, and by the end after that there was no end, because a person whose words are all spent isn't a person anymore. Just a shape. Just inventory.*

*I burned her records. I burned everything. And then I sat in this chair — this chair, boy, the one you're sitting in, I can see you sitting in it, you always sit like you're apologizing to furniture — and I thought about what comes next. Because the binding is fraying. I can feel it in my bones the way I feel weather. The old thing underneath the world is turning over in its sleep, and when it wakes, it will need a voice to answer it, and there aren't many voices left that speak its language.*

*So I went looking. Not for a student. For a contingency.*

*And here is the part you will hate, so take a breath.*

*I chose you.*

*Not because you were clever — plenty of clever. Not because you were loyal — loyalty is just fear wearing a uniform. I chose you because you were invisible. Because you could spend eleven years in a room with powerful people and never once be seen. Because you could keep a ledger so clean that no one would ever think to ask what else the ledger-keeper noticed. I needed someone who could stand next to a child like the one who's coming and never once let the wrong ears hear her.*

*Because she IS coming, Gideon. If you're reading this, then she has already arrived, or she's on the road, and you know it in your gut the way I knew it writing this. A girl who speaks the old tongue. Words that are not words. Sigils with a pulse.*

*Listen to me now, and listen with everything, because I will only say this once and I am dying as I write it:*

*Never let the guild hear her speak whole sentences.*

*A single word is a tool. Two words are a lever. But a sentence — a complete sentence, subject and object and meaning all sewn together — a sentence is a key. The old bindings were sentences, boy. Whole grammars, spoken by whole choirs, and it took them a hundred years to finish the verse that put the thing to sleep. Every sentence she completes brings that verse closer to being sung again — or closer to being sung WRONG, which is worse, because a broken binding doesn't hold, it leaks.*

*The guild will want to catalog her. Cataloguing is how they harvest. Master Corvane — and yes, I know about Corvane, I've known about Corvane since before you were born, ask me sometime why he really keeps his ledgers double-entry — Corvane will be kind to you. He'll be kind the way a fisherman is kind to bait. He will make you feel chosen, because feeling chosen is the drug, and you've been starving for it since the day I found you.*

*You were never his, Gideon. You were mine. And more than mine — you were the plan. Eleven years I spent making you into a lock, and now here comes the key walking through the door on bare feet, and your job is not to turn. Your job is to STAY SHUT.*

*Protect her voice. Protect her silence. Teach her to speak small, the way I taught the other one, the one I failed. And when the time comes — and it will come, it's already coming, I can feel it in the ink drying — when the choice arrives between the binding and the girl, remember:*

*Bindings are just sentences. And sentences can be rewritten.*

*There's honey in the third jar. Take it. You always were too thin.*

*— M.*

*P.S. If a man named Orla ever offers you passage on her boat, take it. She owes me. Tell her the rowan finally grew.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the third read-through is where the denial usually cracks.

Ada had climbed onto the table while I read — no respect for furniture, this one — and was watching me with her head tilted, the way she watched anything that mattered. When I finished, she reached out and touched the letter, right over the signature, and her fingers left no smudge in the old ink.

"She knew me," she said quietly. Not a sigil-word. Just a child's voice, small and wondering. "Before I came. She knew I was coming."

"The whole letter is a prophecy with a postscript," I said. My voice came out rough. "Which is very like her. She never could resist getting the last word, even three years dead."

Ada looked at the rowan tree through the window, red berries burning in the last light. Then she looked at the jar of honey on the shelf. Third jar. Exactly where the letter said.

"She left instructions," Ada said. "Like a recipe."

"She left a battle plan," I corrected. "With snacks."

Something happened to her face then — something I hadn't seen since the ford. Not a smile, exactly. But the muscles around her mouth remembered what smiling was for, and considered the possibility. It was the closest thing to hope I'd witnessed in two days, and it cost nothing. Free of charge. The rarest magic I knew.

* * *

We stayed the night, because the alternative was the road, and the road had teeth.

I did the things an heir does with an inheritance: I took the honey, the good knife from above the lintel, the bundle of dried herbs labeled *for fevers* in case Ada's spending caught up with her again. I took her mortar and pestle, because I am sentimental about tools. I did not take the chair. Some things should stay where they were set down.

Then I went out to the rowan tree in the dark and did the harder thing.

I knelt in the wet grass at the foot of it and I talked to the mound beneath, the way you talk to graves when no one living can help you. I told her about the bottled storm. About the vaults. About the previous speakers, sold and vanished. About the ford, and the word Ada spent, and the hole in the world where my name used to be. I told her I'd falsified a guild ledger and run, and that I was afraid — genuinely, bone-deep afraid — not of the hunters behind us but of the road ahead, because the letter said *bindings are just sentences,* and I had begun to suspect what that meant, and I did not want to suspect it.

"You could have told me," I said to the dirt. "Eleven years. You could have told me I was a contingency plan."

The rowan creaked in the wind. Trees are terrible correspondents.

But underneath the hurt — and there was hurt, real hurt, the sting of discovering your whole life had a syllabus — underneath it was something else settling into place. Something with weight. All those years of learning to be unseen, all those hours of double-entry and clean columns and noticing everything: I had thought they were just skills. Survival tools. The equipment of an invisible man.

They weren't. They were an inheritance. She'd built me the way you build a lock — patiently, deliberately, part by part — and hidden the purpose until the key arrived. Grief, it turns out, can be reframed. Not as loss. As commission. I hadn't lost my master. I'd been *issued* by her.

"Stay shut," I told the grave. "Right. Easy."

Nothing about it would be easy. Somewhere behind us, the Quiet Brothers' replacements were moving through the dark with their silent arithmetic. Somewhere ahead, Prince Ilvane's agents were buying words by the pound. And somewhere in between, a disgraced linguist's warning echoed: *someone is paying dearly to collect them all.*

I went back inside. Ada was asleep in Maren's chair, curled small, the letter held against her chest like a talisman. In the firelight her face had lost its guard, and she looked exactly like what she was: a child, exhausted, carrying a language older than kingdoms in a skull still soft enough to dent.

I covered her with Maren's blanket. I sat across from her. I took out my own battered notebook — the private one, the one no guild auditor had ever seen — and I began, for the first time, to write down everything. The letter. The warning. The rule: *never whole sentences.* The names of the buyers. The shape of the fraying binding. Everything Maren had left me, transcribed into the only format I trusted.

A ledger. Of course a ledger. She'd trained me too well.

I was three pages in when Ada stirred, and spoke without opening her eyes, in a voice thick with sleep:

"The tree says thank you."

I froze. "The — what?"

"The rowan." She burrowed deeper into the blanket. "It says you finally came back. It says you kept the door shut all these years. It says—" her breath slowed, catching on sleep's edge— "it says *she's proud of you*, and that you argue with dead women exactly the way she argued with live ones."

She was asleep before she finished. Whether it was dream-talk or something older moving through her, I couldn't say. I chose — deliberately, professionally, the way you choose to believe a kind audit — to believe it was the tree.

Outside, far off in the direction of the lowlands, something howled. Not a wolf. Wolves announce themselves. This was quieter than quiet, a sound like a held breath traveling, and it came from the south — from the flooded undercity, from the guild's territory, from the direction of pursuit.

They'd found the falsified entry. They'd found the trail. And they were coming to the one place in the world I'd sworn never to lead them.

I closed the notebook. I woke Ada gently, and she rose without complaint, taking the honey jar in both arms like a sleeping infant, and together we stepped out past the rowan tree — past the grave of the woman who had built a lock and mailed a key — and turned north, into the hills, toward the coast.

Toward Orla.

Because Maren had left instructions, and I had finally learned to follow them.
