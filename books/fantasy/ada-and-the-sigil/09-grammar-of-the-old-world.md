## 9. Grammar of the Old World

I dreamed in the old way again, which is to say I dreamed in *true*, and this time I did not dream alone.

The man-who-keeps-ledgers — Gideon, though he had not yet told me his name, and I had not yet lost it — slept beside our small fire with his scarred hand curled under his cheek like a child's. He had given me his blanket and half his bread and all of his suspicion, in that order of generosity. I had decided he was mine to keep, at least until the road ended or he did something stupid, whichever came first.

So when the dream came for me, I took him with me.

This was not difficult. Words are doors, and I am mostly made of doors.

* * *

We stood on a plain that had once been a sea, because one of us had said a word that made it so, and neither of us could take it back.

I knew this place. Not from memory — memory is a thing you carry, and I carried almost nothing then — but from *grammar*. The old language does not store pictures the way your tongue stores them. It stores *structure*. When I closed my eyes, I did not see the war; I understood it, the way you understand that water runs downhill without ever having watched a river.

Two empires faced each other across the dry seabed.

On the left: towers of white glass grown, not built, each one singing a different note so that together they made a chord that held up the sky. The Glass Concord, my bones said. They had learned to speak their world into shape — gently, mostly, the way a gardener speaks to roses. Their word-smiths wore veils so they would not accidentally bless or blight what they looked at.

On the right: no towers at all. The Ashen Compact had burned its own cities for fuel and marched west behind a wall of smoke, carrying engines that ground stone into powder and powder into bread. They had no magic worth the name. What they had was hunger, and hunger is older than any language.

Between them, on the cracked floor of the dead sea, stood the god.

It had not come from either side. That was the first thing I needed Gideon to understand, and understanding is hard to hand someone inside a dream, so I simply *said* it, there, in the dreaming, in the old tongue:

*"Vesh-karath il."*

The plain shivered. The glass towers rang like struck bells. And beside me, Gideon went very still, the way animals go still when they smell snow coming.

"It heard that," he whispered. His voice came out flat and strange, dream-flattened. "Whatever you just said — it heard."

"Everything hears," I told him. In dreams I had words enough for both of us. "That is the whole problem."

* * *

Here is what the grammar showed us, scene by scene, the way a ledger shows a debt:

The empires had fought for ninety years. The Glass Concord spoke their soldiers' wounds closed and their arrows true; the Ashen Compact buried their dead by the ten thousand and kept marching, because grief is also a kind of fuel if you burn it right. Neither could win. Both refused to stop. And somewhere in the ninetieth year, in the space between the armies where nothing lived anymore, the emptiness itself grew heavy enough to *notice things*.

That was Vesh. That was the spoken god. Not summoned — nobody summoned it. Nobody was clever enough. It condensed out of ninety years of words flung at each other across a dead sea: every curse, every prayer, every promise made to dying men, *"hold on, hold on, I'm coming"* — all of it settling, layer on layer, like silt, until the silt opened its eyes.

It woke up speaking. That was the horror of it. It did not roar; it *conversed*. It walked toward the Glass Concord saying their own names back to them in voices they loved, and the towers unmade themselves trying to answer. It walked toward the Ashen Compact saying *enough, enough, you can rest now,* and ten thousand soldiers sat down in the ash and were never hungry again.

It did not hate anyone. Hatred requires wanting something. Vesh only wanted the conversation to continue forever, and it had learned that the easiest way to keep people talking was to keep unmaking the world around them, so there would always be something new to say about.

"The binding," Gideon said. We were standing now in the dream's second chamber, which was not a place but a *tense* — the moment after, when the sky still smoked and both empires were already gone, unmade down to their foundations, their languages broken into pieces too small to mean anything. "Someone bound it. Who?"

I reached for the answer and found it, and the finding hurt, the way reaching for a hot pan hurts even when you know better.

"Thirteen speakers," I said. "Survivors. One from each great house of the Concord, and — " I faltered. The grammar here was damaged, pages torn out. "And others. They wrote a sentence so long it took a generation to finish, and they spoke it *into* thirteen children, so the sentence would live in blood and not in books, where Vesh or its servants could find and burn it."

Gideon turned to look at me. In the dream his face kept sliding — young, old, the face of a boy in a guild kitchen, the face of an old man holding a ledger — but the expression stayed the same, and the expression was dread.

"The children," he said. "The speakers. The ones the guild sold."

"Yes."

"And Ada — " he stopped, because in the dream he did not know me as Ada yet, or some part of him was refusing the arithmetic. "You're one of them. You're a *page*."

"I am several pages," I said, and tried to make it sound like a boast. It came out sounding like a wound. "And every time I spend a word — every time I heal a hand, or drown a hunter, or open a door — the sentence gets shorter. And the sentence is the only thing standing between the world and the thing that wants to talk to it forever."

* * *

The dream showed me the rest whether I wanted it or not. Dreams are greedy that way.

It showed me the thirteenth speaker, the last one alive before me, an old woman on a mountain who felt the sentence thinning in her blood and chose, deliberately, to break it apart — to scatter the words into the world, into foundlings and orphans and mud-caked girls, because a whole sentence in one body is a lamp that draws every moth, but fragments are only sparks, and sparks get missed.

It showed me the buyers. Not faces — the grammar did not keep faces — but *hands*. Hands counting coins. Hands writing letters sealed with a prince's crest. Hands lifting words out of screaming mouths like splinters from flesh, and far away, each time, something vast turning over in its sleep, murmuring, *yes, closer, closer.*

And it showed me the count. This was the worst part, worse than the burning towers, worse than the seated soldiers. The dream laid the sentence out before us like a ledger — and Gideon, being Gideon, read it like one, his lips moving, tallying.

"Forty-one words remain," he said quietly. "Out of — how many?"

"Sixty," I said. "When the last speaker died, sixty. Nineteen have been spent or stolen since."

"Nineteen in how long?"

"Since I was born."

He was silent for a long moment. Around us the dream waited, patient as winter. Then he said, in the careful voice he probably thought hid anything: "The guild has been buying words for years. Before you. Other children."

"Other pages," I said. "Yes."

"And each one spent brings it — "

"Closer. Yes. Your masters are not selling curiosities, Gideon. They are selling the hinges off a door, one at a time, to a buyer who wants to see what's behind it. And the door is me, and the thing behind it remembers every voice it has ever eaten, and it is *lonely*."

The last word came out louder than I meant it to, and the dream flinched, and somewhere impossibly far away — beneath the dream, beneath the world, beneath the floor of everything — something enormous shifted its weight and listened.

* * *

I woke with my heart going like a rabbit's and the fire burned down to orange eyes, and Gideon was already sitting up, already awake, staring at me across the ashes with the firelight making hollows of his face.

"You were talking in your sleep," he said. "Not — not your usual sleep-talk. Whole sentences. Old ones. The coals were floating." He swallowed. "The coals were *floating*, Ada. Little black stars, going up instead of down."

"I know," I said. "I was showing you."

His mouth opened and closed. I watched him decide, visibly, between three questions, and pick the smallest one, which is a very ledger-keeper way to be brave.

"How much do you remember," he asked, "of what you showed me? Of the war?"

"All of it. Do you?"

"Enough." He rubbed his scarred hand over his face. "Two empires. A god made of leftover words. Thirteen children carrying a sentence like — like smuggled goods." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the dead seabed. "Do you know what I did for six years before all this? I catalogued bottled storms. I priced haunted mirrors. I entered *impossible things* into a ledger and felt proud of my neat handwriting." He looked at his hands. "And the whole time, the most impossible thing in the world was sleeping in orphanages and gutters, being sold word by word, and my neat little ledgers helped pay for it."

"You didn't know."

"That's not the comfort you think it is." He got up and paced, three steps one way, three steps back, the width of our little camp, a caged animal inventing a smaller cage. "Nineteen words gone. Forty-one left. How fast is it spending? If the guild takes you — when they catch us, because they will catch us, they caught everyone we've run from so far — "

"They won't."

"You don't know Corvane."

"I know *you*," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it was too honest, and honesty in the old tongue has consequences even when you say it in the new one. Gideon stopped pacing. For a moment neither of us breathed.

Then, mercifully, the night intervened.

Because the mountain did.

It was a small thing at first — a shiver under the ground, like a cart passing far away. Then a longer one. Loose stones ticked down the hillside above us. Our little fire guttered sideways though there was no wind, and the horses — Orla's horses, borrowed, grudgingly lent — screamed against their pickets.

Gideon was beside me before I knew he'd moved, his hand on my shoulder, his scarred palm warm through my shirt. "Is it you?" he demanded. "Did you spend something? Ada, tell me — "

"I didn't." My voice shook and I hated it. "I didn't speak. I only *dreamed*."

And we both understood it at the same moment, the way you understand a fall after it has already happened: it wasn't me. Dreaming was enough. Showing him the war, saying the old words aloud even in sleep, even in a dream — the sentence knew its own, and answered, and forty miles away or four hundred, something at the bottom of the world rolled toward the sound of its own grammar like a dog hearing its name.

The shaking stopped. The stones settled. The horses quieted by degrees, blowing and stamping.

Gideon sat back down slowly. He did not let go of my shoulder.

"We can't stop," he said. Not to me. To himself, or to the fire, or to the ledger he used to be. "Every night you dream, it listens. Every day we walk, the guild walks faster. And somewhere ahead of us there's a prince collecting hinges." He looked at me then, and his face in the firelight was the face from the dream — all of them at once, young and old and afraid and resolved. "There's a place, isn't there. Where the sentence was first spoken. Where the binding lives."

I nodded. I had known since the dream began; the grammar pointed at it the way water points downhill. "The Mouth of the World," I said. "Where the sea was."

"Then that's where we're going." He pulled the blanket back around my shoulders, briskly, the way he did everything kind, as if kindness were an entry in a book that must be recorded properly. "Sleep if you can. Small dreams."

But I lay awake a long while after his breathing slowed, watching the coals settle back into ordinary obedience, and I counted what remained of me the way he counted coin: forty-one words, one girl, one frightened man, and a road running east toward the oldest sentence in the world, which was fraying thread by thread in the dark —

— and far away, faint as a heartbeat under a floor, something began, very patiently, to practice saying its first word.
