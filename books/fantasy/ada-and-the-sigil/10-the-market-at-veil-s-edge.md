## 10. The Market at Veil's Edge

There is a particular smell to a market built on the edge of a kingdom's forgetting. It is the smell of wet rope, fried onions, and transactions no honest ledger would survive. Veil's Edge had all three in abundance, and I confess the onions nearly undid me.

We had been walking for six days since the undercity, six days of Ada sleeping against my shoulder and waking with no idea whose shoulder it was. Six days of me performing, badly, the role of a stranger who was not a stranger. She had begun, at least, to accept my presence the way one accepts weather — warily, with an eye toward the horizon.

"You're the ledger man," she said that morning, chewing on a heel of bread I'd bought with the last of my coin.

"I am."

"Orla says you count everything."

"Orla says a great many things for a woman we met an hour ago."

This is getting ahead of myself. Let me do what I do best and put things in their proper order.

* * *

Veil's Edge was not so much a town as a rumor with a customs problem. It sat where the old imperial road drowned in marshland, and everything the road could no longer carry — smuggled salt, forged writs, fugitive dukes, the occasional saint's finger — pooled there like silt. The market itself was held on barges lashed together across a black canal, so that every purchase required a small act of faith in other people's knots.

I needed passage south. The letter in my breast pocket — Master Elowen's last letter, the one that had reframed my grief into something with instructions — said the binding-site lay beyond the Sunken Reach, and the Sunken Reach was crossed only by people who did not ask why you were crossing it.

Which meant smugglers. Which meant, inevitably, Orla.

I saw her before she saw us, or believed I did. She was arguing with a fishmonger over the freshness of something that was, by any reasonable measure, still swimming. Red hair like a warning flag, a coat with more hidden pockets than a confessor has sins, and a cutlass worn with the casual insolence of someone who has never once needed to draw it in front of witnesses.

Ada tugged my sleeve. "Her boat has a false bottom."

"Of course it does."

"No. Under the false bottom. Another false bottom."

I looked. I could not see any bottoms at all, false or otherwise, but Ada had spent six days teaching me that her eyes saw the grammar of things — the structure beneath the surface, the load-bearing words. If she said the boat was lying about itself twice over, the boat was lying.

"Stay behind me," I said, "and speak to no one."

"Why?"

"Because the last time you spoke to someone, a bridge remembered being a bridge and tried to stand up."

"That was one time."

It had, in fact, been one time, on the third day, when a toll-keeper had grabbed her arm and she had shouted a syllable that made the toll-gate's timber remember the tree it had been. Roots had come out of the road. The toll-keeper had wept. We had run. I had written in my head, in the ledger I no longer kept but apparently could not stop keeping: *one word, one small miracle, one more piece of the prison pried loose.*

So. The smuggler.

I approached Orla the way one approaches any dangerous animal: with flattery and a visible lack of weapons. "Mistress. I'm told you're the only captain on this canal who can get cargo past the Reach."

"Depends on the cargo." She looked me over with the frank arithmetic of a woman pricing a horse. "You're cargo?"

"Passengers. Two. Quiet ones."

"Quiet's free. Everything else costs." Her eyes slid past me and found Ada, and something in her face changed — not softened, exactly. *Sharpened.* "That the little one? The one the Quiet Brothers are asking after?"

The cold went down my spine the way it always did when a stranger knew too much. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." She grinned, and it was a good grin, the kind that has carried men through storms and into worse. "Word travels, ledger man. Guild hunters came through two days back, flashing silver, asking after a man with ink-stained fingers and a girl who talks funny. I said I'd keep an eye out." She leaned in. "I'm keeping an eye out."

"You've seen them? The Brothers?"

"Saw one. Or didn't, which is the same thing. There was a gap in the crowd, walking. A person-shaped absence." She said it lightly, but her hand had found the hilt of the cutlass she'd never needed to draw. "They don't come to markets like this. Too much noise. But noise doesn't stop them. Nothing stops them. They listen for the silence under the noise, and everyone's got one."

Ada had come up beside me despite my instructions, because children and cats share a contempt for instructions. She was staring at Orla's boat with an expression I had learned to read the way farmers read clouds.

"Your boat is sad," Ada announced.

Orla blinked. "My boat is the *Gallant Wren*, and she's the fastest hull on this canal."

"She's sad because you keep her secrets in the wrong place." Ada pointed at the waterline. "The heavy things should go low and the lies should go high. You have it backwards. That's why she lists to port when she thinks no one's watching."

A silence. Somewhere behind us, the fishmonger was still arguing with his merchandise.

"That," Orla said slowly, "is either nonsense or the most useful thing anyone's said to me all year." She crouched to Ada's level, and I watched her do it — watch the smuggler's shrewdness fold itself around the child like a coat offered in the rain. "How'd you know that, little one?"

Ada looked at me. It was the first time in six days she had looked at me for permission, and something in my chest turned over like a page.

"She knows things," I said. "It's safer if we don't discuss what things."

"Safer for who?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

Orla stood. She studied me for a long moment, and then she did the thing that told me everything about her: she laughed. Not at us — at the situation, the whole rotten arrangement of the world that put a fugitive clerk and a word-child on her dock at the same hour the Quiet Brothers' silence came walking.

"Right," she said. "Here's my offer, and I'll only make it once, so pay attention. I'll take you down the Reach, past the guild checkpoints, past the prince's river patrols — yes, I know about those too, everyone at Veil's Edge knows about those, the prince's men have been buying up every speaker and sigil-scrapper for a season now — all the way to the Mouth of the World, if that's where you're going, and don't bother denying it, only fools and pilgrims go there and you're clearly not a pilgrim."

"And the price?"

Her eyes went to Ada. "A word."

The canal seemed to go quiet around us. Or perhaps that was the Brothers, listening.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"No."

"I haven't even said *which* word."

"It doesn't matter which word." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, the voice I'd used in the vaults, reading the inventory of the sold. "Her words aren't currency. They're not for sale, not for trade, not for passage, not for anything. I've seen what happens when they change hands. I've read the ledgers. There's a fortress on the northern border that isn't there anymore because someone bought a word and spent it."

Orla's grin had faded into something more honest. "I know. That's why I want one."

"That's a strange way to argue your case."

"Is it?" She crossed her arms. "I've been running this canal for eleven years. I've smuggled everything a thing can be smuggled, and I've watched the world get meaner in a way I can't put a name to. And then this child looks at my boat and tells me it's *sad*, and I feel it — the truth of it, in my chest, like she handed me something. You think I want to sell her word? I want to *keep* one. I want to hold a piece of the old world in my pocket and remember what it was like when things could still be true out loud." She shrugged, and for a moment the smuggler's armor slipped and I saw someone tired underneath, someone who had been listening to the silence under the noise for a long time. "But fine. Refuse. I'll take you anyway."

I blinked. "You will?"

"I'll take you because the Brothers are coming and because the prince's men are coming and because a boat that lists to port needs all the luck it can get, and that child is either very bad luck or very good, and I've gambled on worse odds." She turned and started down the dock. "Deck boards are wet. Don't slip. I hate fishing passengers out of the canal; they always sue."

Ada looked up at me. "Are we going with her?"

"It appears we are."

"Is she a friend?"

"She's a smuggler who tried to buy a piece of you and then changed her price to nothing. I genuinely don't know what that makes her."

"Orla," Ada decided, with the gravity of a queen conferring a title.

From the deck, Orla snorted. "The girl has the right of it. Get aboard, ledger man. And keep your ink-stained fingers off my rigging."

* * *

The *Gallant Wren* — sad, listing, and faster than any hull had a right to be — slipped away from Veil's Edge as the market lamps came on, one by one, like eyes closing. I stood at the stern and watched the town shrink to a smear of orange light on black water, and I did the thing I had sworn off doing: I took inventory.

One fugitive clerk, formerly Gideon Almeida of the Vellum Guild, currently no one at all. One child who did not remember him but had, an hour ago, looked to him for permission, which was perhaps worth more than memory and certainly hurt more. One dead master's letter, soft at the folds from rereading. One smuggler with a hidden heart under her hidden pockets. Zero coin.

And behind us, gaining, a person-shaped absence.

I told myself the Brothers had lost the trail in the marsh. I told myself the prince's patrols were a day behind and looking in the wrong river. I told myself a great many things, because that is what I do instead of praying.

Ada found me at the rail. She didn't speak — she had been rationing her words all day, I'd noticed, hoarding them like a miser, though whether from weariness or some instinct older than weariness, I couldn't say. She just stood beside me and watched the water, and after a while her hand found mine on the rail. Small. Cold. Certain.

"Ledger man," she said.

"Yes?"

"When I forgot you —" she frowned, searching for the words the way one searches for a dropped coin in dark grass — "when the river took your name. Did it hurt you?"

I thought about lying. It would have been easy. It was always easy.

"Yes," I said. "It hurt a great deal."

"Good," she said, and squeezed my hand. "Then it was worth something."

I did not ask what she meant. Some ledgers you keep; some you let the water take.

Ahead, the canal widened into the first black throat of the Sunken Reach, and drowned towers rose out of the water like questions no one had answered. Orla stood at the wheel, humming something tuneless, and called back without turning:

"Get some sleep, the pair of you. We make the narrows by dawn. And if you hear the water go quiet —"

She stopped. The humming stopped. The wind stopped.

The water had gone quiet.

Not still — *quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when someone enters it who does not breathe. Orla's hand moved to her cutlass at last, the blade she'd never needed to draw, and her voice came out low and flat:

"Ledger man. Tell me you didn't bring one of those person-shaped absences aboard my sad boat."
