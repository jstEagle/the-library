## 12. Relearning Him

The morning after the ford, Ada looked at me the way you look at a piece of furniture that has followed you home.

Not hostile. Not curious, exactly. Just — assessing. Deciding whether I was the sort of thing worth noticing or the sort of thing you stepped around on your way to something better. She sat on a fallen log at the edge of our camp, mud still dried in the creases of her neck from the river, and watched me boil water over a fire I'd built too small because I didn't trust the smoke not to carry.

"Porridge," I said.

She said nothing. Of course she said nothing. The word that would have drowned three Quiet Brothers in the shallows of the Vell had also taken my name out of her head like a coin lifted from a purse, and whatever else it had cost her, it had cost her the habit of me. Two weeks of shared road, of teaching her to whistle through a grass blade, of catching her when she slept standing up like a horse — gone. Filed away by her own voice and paid out to the river.

I handed her the bowl. She took it with both hands, the way she always had, blew on it twice, and ate.

Small mercies: some things survived the flood. The way she held a bowl. The way she checked the treeline before eating, a habit I'd never taught her and couldn't unteach. Whatever the sigil had erased, it had left the architecture standing and only taken out the furniture. Including, apparently, one particular item of furniture named Gideon Almeida.

"You saved my life," I told her, for maybe the sixth time since dawn. "You know that? Those men would have—"

She looked up at me with the flat patience of a child being lectured about weather, and went back to her porridge.

Right. Fine. We'd start there.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being forgotten: it is enormously clarifying.

When Ada knew me, I could rely on the accumulated capital of two weeks' survival — every fire I'd built, every night watch I'd taken, every time I'd put myself between her and something with teeth. That capital was spent now, drawn down to nothing in one word spoken at a river ford. What remained was just a man, a stranger, larger than her and armed and traveling with her for reasons she could not verify, following a child who could level mountains if she ever learned to finish a sentence.

From her side of the fire, I must have looked like exactly the kind of person her instincts were built to fear. A grown man who knew her name — she'd let me keep calling her Ada, thank all the patient gods, though whether that was trust or indifference I couldn't say — who spoke to her in a language she understood but hadn't chosen, who flinched whenever she opened her mouth.

Because I did flinch. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the only currency I have left. Every time she spoke — even small things now, *water*, *hungry*, words worn smooth as river stones from use — I braced. And she'd noticed. Children notice everything except their own shoelaces. She'd noticed that the big man went quiet and careful when she talked, and she'd drawn the obvious conclusion: her voice frightened him. Which meant either she was dangerous or he was guilty, and a child alone will always bet on the second.

So we traveled in a silence with a new texture. Before the ford, our silence had been companionable, two fugitives conserving breath. Now it was a negotiation neither of us had agreed to enter.

On the third day I tried a different approach.

We'd stopped at midday in the ruins of a waystation — the old imperial road north of the Vell is strung with them, roofless stone shells where couriers once changed horses and empires once pretended they'd last forever. Ada had found a corner and was doing what she did when she thought no one watched: arranging things. Pebbles, this time. Laying them out in rows and patterns with an intensity that made my skin prickle, because I'd seen ledgers arranged like that, and sigils, and the way Master Corvane laid out his knives before dinner.

I sat down a respectful distance away and said, "Do you want to hear a story?"

Her hands stopped. One pebble hovered between finger and ground.

"It's about a man," I said. "A very boring man. The most boring man in the world, probably. He keeps ledgers."

She turned. Not all the way — a quarter turn, the pebble still pinched in her fingers, but her eyes came around to me like a compass needle finding north.

"He works for a guild," I went on, "that trades in impossible things. Bottled storms. Moonlight pressed into wax. Once, a jar containing somebody's last good day — though nobody could prove it, which is the trouble with trading in feelings, the buyers always claim they've been shortchanged." I kept my voice light, the way you talk to a spooked animal, or a creditor. "And this ledger-keeper, his whole job is writing down what things are worth. Which sounds simple until you realize nothing in the building is worth anything. How do you price a storm? By its thunder or its rain?"

Ada set the pebble down. She drew her knees up and looked at me with the grave, total attention children spend so lavishly on stories and so stingily on instructions.

"This man," I said, "was nobody's first choice for anything. He was an apprentice once, to a hedge-witch who taught him that most magic is just paying attention. Then she died, and he got lost, and a guild found him and gave him a desk and a debt, and he told himself the debt was the same thing as belonging." I heard my own voice do something complicated on the word *belonging* and smoothed it flat. "Anyway. One day a knock comes at the door."

I stopped there, deliberately, the way Orla stops a story when she wants more wine.

Ada waited. Then, when it became clear I was going to be insufferable about it, she said — carefully, in her worn-smooth way — "*More.*"

"*More* isn't a story," I said. "It's a demand. But fine. The knock at the door turns out to be a girl. Mud to the eyebrows, fierce as a wet cat, speaking a language older than any kingdom. And the boring man looks at her and realizes — this is the part where it gets embarrassing — that she's the most valuable thing in a building full of impossible things, and that everyone in the building knows it, and that none of them, including him, mean to treat her like a person."

Ada's brow furrowed. She looked down at her pebbles, then back up.

"What happened to the girl?" she asked. Full words. Clear ones. My chest did something painful, because those were more words than she'd strung together since the ford, and also because I was lying to her by telling her the truth.

"That's the thing," I said. "The story isn't finished. The man and the girl ran away together, and the girl did something brave and terrible at a river, and—" I spread my hands. "After that, the storyteller loses the thread. So he's making it up as he goes. He's hoping the girl in the story will tell him how it ends."

She considered this for a long moment, the way she considered everything, with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a petition. Then she picked up a pebble, placed it deliberately at the end of her row, and looked at me.

I chose to take it as encouragement.

* * *

The story became our ritual. Every evening, a chapter of the ledger-keeper and the girl, and I learned quickly that I had made a tactical error, because Ada had opinions.

She wanted more of the guild — the bottled storm, the scribes with ink-stained cuffs, the vaults under the counting-house where the impossible things breathed in the dark. When I described the vault, she went very still, and I realized I was describing a place she had never seen but somehow recognized, the way you recognize a room from a dream, and I filed that away next to the growing pile of things about Ada that frightened me.

She wanted the girl in the story to be cleverer than the man. I obliged. It wasn't hard; in the story as in life, the girl was carrying most of the plot.

And she wanted — this surprised me — the man to be punished. Not cruelly. But whenever the ledger-keeper got away with something, whenever his cowardice or his small betrayals went unpunished, Ada would frown and push her bowl away, and I would understand that justice had been found deficient, and I would add a consequence. A fine. A scolding from the hedge-witch's ghost. Once, memorably, a goose.

"The goose again?" I protested, the fourth time it appeared. "Why is it always geese?"

"Geese know," Ada said solemnly, and I decided that some truths were not worth interrogating.

What I was doing, of course — and I knew it even as I did it, because I am many things but not stupid — was confessing. Chapter by chapter, kindness by kindness, I was telling her everything I had done and failed to do, laundered through fiction so it could get past both our guards. The chapter where the ledger-keeper marked the girl as worthless stock in the guild's books to buy her time: true. The chapter where he stood in the vault reading the records of the speakers who came before her and walked back upstairs and said nothing: true, and the goose got him for that one, and Ada laughed for the first time since the ford, a rusty startling sound like a gate opening after years shut, and I had to turn away and tend a fire that did not need tending.

Some nights I wondered if she knew. Children know more than we credit, and this child had been made partly of language itself; perhaps the story reached her in ways plain speech couldn't. Perhaps she recognized herself the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's letter. She never said. She only listened, and corrected my lapses in narrative justice, and slowly — the way water persuades stone — the distance between us began to close.

Not all the way. Let me be precise, because precision was my trade. She trusted me the way you trust a bridge you've crossed a hundred times: functionally, without thinking about it, without love. She handed me her bowl to fill. She slept with her back to my fire instead of facing the dark. When Orla caught up with us on the fifth day — she'd been shadowing us since Veil's Edge, because Orla does nothing so vulgar as leave — Ada hid behind my leg, and something in my chest turned over at that, at being the leg hidden behind, and I hated how much it mattered to me.

"Still telling her stories?" Orla asked that night, dropping onto a log with the grace of a woman who had stolen it fair and square.

"Still eavesdropping?"

"A professional habit." She produced her pipe, considered the state of her tobacco, and sighed. "You know she doesn't remember you."

"I'm aware."

"And you're aware that she might not. Ever. Sigils don't give refunds. Whatever that word at the ford bought, it was bought outright."

"I'm aware of that too, Orla. Thank you. Your tenderness is a balm."

She lit the pipe, unhurried. "I lost a sister once," she said, to the smoke rather than to me. "Not to death. To a fever that took her memories and left the rest. She woke up knowing how to knit and not knowing my face." A pause. "You learn to be known again or you learn to stop trying. Most people stop trying. It's less work."

"Which did you do?"

"She died before I had to choose." Orla said it evenly, the way she said everything that would gut another person, and I remembered why I tolerated her company despite the smuggling and the extortionate rates. "So I can't advise you. But I'll tell you what I've observed, free of charge, which should worry you." She pointed the pipe at Ada, asleep now against her pack, one hand curled around a smooth grey pebble. "That child watches you tell those stories like she's checking them against something. Like she's holding the true version up behind the one you speak and comparing."

My mouth went dry. "Stories are just stories."

"Mm," said Orla, who had intercepted a guild courier and read its dispatches and knew precisely what stories were worth, and to whom. "Keep telling them anyway. It's the only honest thing you do."

* * *

It rained on the eighth day, a proper autumn drowning, and we sheltered in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, packed in close for warmth, which Ada permitted with the resigned tolerance of a cat allowing itself to be held. Thunder walked across the hills to the west. Somewhere out there, I didn't doubt, the Quiet Brothers were regrouping, and Prince Ilvane's agents were buying words by the pound, and Master Corvane was writing letters in his beautiful hand about retrieval. The world was coming for us at its own pace. But the rain had closed the roads, and for one afternoon the world had to wait.

"Tell the one about the storm," Ada said.

I'd been saving it. I told it now: the chapter where the ledger-keeper is sent to catalogue a bottled storm, and the storm is furious at being priced, and the girl — not yet met, in the strict chronology, but stories allow themselves liberties — tells him to open the bottle. And he does, because by then she has taught him that some things refuse to be inventory, and the storm fills the guildhall and washes the masters' ledgers clean off their shelves, every entry dissolving, every debt unreadable, and for one glorious hour nobody in the building owns anything at all.

"And then?" Ada demanded, sitting bolt upright under the oak.

"Then the storm runs out," I admitted. "Storms do. And the masters come back with dry ledgers and longer faces, and the man is fined a month's wages, and the girl laughs at him for a week."

"But it was worth it."

I looked at her. Rain silvered the entrance to our hollow; her eyes were very bright in the grey light, and there was something in her face I couldn't parse — recognition, maybe, or grief for a thing she couldn't name, the way you grieve a smell from childhood.

"Yes," I said. "It was worth it."

She nodded once, satisfied, judicial, and settled back against my pack. And then, as I was banking the fire and pretending my eyes needed occupation elsewhere, she said quietly:

"In the story. The man who keeps ledgers. He gave his name away, didn't he. At the end. That's why the girl can't remember it."

The rain filled the silence. I made myself breathe.

"Why would you think that?"

"Because names are the only thing the story never says." She was looking at the fire, not at me, a child delivering a verdict with her chin tucked. "Every other word, you say. His hair, his hands, his debts, his goose. But never his name. You skip it every time, like a step missing in a stair." She pulled her blanket up. "People only skip the true parts."

I sat with that. I am, by training and temperament, a man who sits with things — entries, errors, the slow arithmetic of consequence. Outside, the rain went on erasing the road behind us, which felt apt.

"There's a version of the ending," I said finally, "where giving the name away isn't a loss. Where it's the only payment anyone will accept, because it's the one thing no thief can steal — it has to be given. And the girl gets to keep her voice, all of it, hers alone, and the name keeps the old power sleeping, and—" I stopped. "But that ending hasn't happened yet. In any version. I'm getting ahead of the manuscript."

Ada was quiet so long I thought she'd slept. Then, drowsy, half-gone:

"When it happens. Will you tell me the name?"

"If it happens," I said, "you won't need me to."

"Hm," she said, in a tone so exactly Orla's that I nearly laughed, and slid into sleep with her hand still curled around her pebble.

I sat up the rest of the watch, as was becoming my habit, and it was near dawn, grey seeping into the hollow, when I saw it: Ada's hand had opened in her sleep, and the pebble lay in her palm — and it was no longer a pebble. It was a sliver of white stone, veined with gold, and the veins had shifted in the night into lines I knew too well: the tight, interlocking script of a binding sigil, the same script I'd seen crawling across the walls of the sunken amphitheater in her dreams, the script of the door we were walking toward.

And beside our cold fire, in the ash, written in a hand steadier than any child's, were four words in the old tongue.

I copied them into my ledger before the wind could take them. I did not know what they meant. But I knew whose grammar they were — and somewhere east of us, faint as a struck bell, something answered.
