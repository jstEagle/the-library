## 14. Sanctum of Broken Tongues

The mountain had a name once. The monks had taken it.

That was Orla's theory, anyway, delivered around a mouthful of dried fish as we climbed the last switchback toward the monastery of Veth-Saral. "You don't build a house on a mountain unless you mean to own it," she said. "And you don't own a mountain like this one without taking its name first. Names are how you hold things. Ask me how I know."

"I'd rather not," I said. "You'll invoice me."

Orla grinned. She had been grinning more since the ford — the kind of grin a person wears when they've decided a thing is worth doing and want everyone to know they decided it freely, against their own financial interest, and will be mentioning that fact at intervals for the rest of their natural life. She had refused payment in Ada's words, which I would remember until I died, and she had followed us anyway, which I suspected she'd also mention until then.

Ada walked between us with her hood up and her hands in her sleeves, which was how she traveled now. Quietly. Carefully. A child carrying a language like a jug full to the brim, walking on ice.

Three weeks since the ford. Three weeks since she drowned the Quiet Brothers in a word that cost her my name. She knew me as *the man who keeps ledgers* — a story I'd invented for her, half confession, half fairy tale — and she trusted that man the way you trust weather: completely, but without any certainty he'd stay. Every kindness I did her, I did twice. Once for her, and once for the version of her who remembered everything and would someday have to learn what I'd done while she slept.

The monastery appeared the way Orla said it would: all at once, like a held breath released. We came around a shoulder of black rock and there it was — a crown of grey buildings fused to the peak above the cloud line, walls built of stone so old it had stopped being stone and become something closer to bone. No banners. No bells. Not even smoke from the chimneys, though the windows glowed warm.

"No guards," I said.

"Doesn't need them," Orla said. "Who attacks a mountain?"

"People who want what's inside mountains," I said. I was, after all, an expert on that particular crime.

We climbed the final stair — nine hundred steps, or close enough; I counted out of habit and hated myself for it — and reached a gate of ironwood bound in bronze. There was no knocker. There was no rope. There was a single bronze plate, green with age, bearing an inscription in a script I almost recognized and couldn't read, and beneath it, worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands:

**KNOCK WITH WHAT YOU HAVE LOST.**

I stood there a long moment.

"Well," Orla said. "That's either very profound or very annoying."

Ada stepped past me. She raised her small fist — and hesitated. Then, instead of knocking, she opened her hand flat against the wood and pressed her palm there, the way you'd press your ear to a door to listen.

The gate opened.

No sound. No creak, no groan, nothing. One moment it was shut and the next it stood wide, and beyond it stood three figures in undyed wool, faces calm and closed as eggs. They looked at Ada first. Then at me. Then at Orla, with what I can only describe as the expression of a librarian regarding a dog that has wandered into the rare books room.

One of them extended a hand, palm up. In it lay three small tablets of slate, each scratched with a single symbol: a crossed-out mouth.

"They're mute," I said. "All of them."

"That's the point of the place, ledger-man." Orla took her tablet, turned it over, sniffed it, and bit it, because Orla. "Mute monks preserving forbidden languages. It's the only way to keep a secret safe — put it somewhere nobody's allowed to say it. Clever. Horrible, but clever."

The monks led us through corridors where the silence wasn't absence but architecture — thick, load-bearing silence, the kind you could lean against. I saw things in those halls I have no right to describe and will describe anyway, because I am a ledger-keeper and cataloguing is a disease. Shelves of clay tablets stamped with scripts dead before the first empire. Jars sealed with wax, each labeled in a different alphabet, each holding — I don't know. Ashes? Seeds? Words, maybe, grown like mushrooms in the dark. A hall of bells with their clappers removed, hung in perfect rows, waiting for a permission that would never come.

And everywhere, monks. Working. Writing. Never speaking. When two of them needed to communicate they wrote on small slates and showed each other, and even their writing was careful, abbreviated, as if they were afraid of accidentally composing something.

An old monk — old the way driftwood is old, bleached and toughened by weather — took charge of us. His slate said his name was **Brother Anselm**, and then, when he saw me reading it, he added: **We do not use names here. That is a courtesy I extend to guests.**

"You don't use names," I repeated aloud, and felt Ada flinch beside me. I put my hand on her shoulder. "Why not?"

He wiped the slate and wrote: **A name is a hook. Everything that hunts in this world hunts by name. Here we are unhooked.**

I thought of a boy named Gideon Almeida signing his first guild contract, and of a man with no name anchoring a god, and I said nothing at all, which in that place felt like fluency.

* * *

They fed us. Barley and salt fish and bread dense enough to stop a door, eaten in a refectory where forty monks made less noise than one cat. And afterward Brother Anselm took us down — down being the operative direction, always down, stairs cut into the living rock of the mountain itself — to the place I had come to find.

The Hall of Broken Tongues.

It was a vault, circular, its ceiling lost in shadow. Around its walls ran shelves upon shelves of tablets, scrolls, wax cylinders, knotted cords, and stranger media still: a strip of human skin inked in a script that seemed to move when I didn't look directly at it; a bird's skull with a syllable carved into the cranium; a jar of water that rippled in patterns whenever anyone breathed near it. Languages preserved the way you'd preserve disease cultures. Dangerous ones. Dead ones. Dead-but-not-finished ones.

Anselm watched me take inventory and wrote on his slate: **You look like a man who has seen a ledger.**

"I keep one," I admitted. "For a guild that trades in impossible things."

**Then you understand what this room is.**

"A warehouse," I said.

He considered that. Wiped the slate. **A quarantine,** he wrote.

And then he turned to Ada, and everything changed.

He didn't speak to her — none of them spoke to anyone — but he crouched to her height, and he showed her his slate, blank, and then he set the slate down and opened both hands, empty, palms up. The gesture of a man showing he carries nothing. And Ada looked at him, and then at the shelves, and then at me, and I saw something cross her face that I hadn't seen since the ford.

Recognition. Relief. *Home*, maybe, or the shape home leaves behind.

She touched the shelf nearest her. Ran her fingers along the spines of tablets whose scripts predated every kingdom whose ruins I'd ever looted knowledge from. And softly — so softly, testing, the way you test ice — she began to read.

Not aloud. Not quite. Her lips moved. A breath of sound, less than a whisper, less than a sigh. But the mountain heard it.

I know that sounds like poetry. It isn't. The mountain *heard*. The stone under our feet shifted its attention the way a sleeping man shifts when someone says his name in the next room. The jars on the shelves rippled. The strip of skin uncurled slightly, like a finger remembering it could flex. Somewhere far below us — far deeper than any cellar, deeper than the roots of the monastery, down where the mountain's bones met whatever the mountain was standing on — something groaned.

Not an earthquake groan. A *waking* groan. The sound stone makes when it has been holding its breath for two thousand years and hears, faintly, the first word of the sentence that once put it to sleep.

Ada stopped. Her lips closed. Her eyes went enormous.

The monks moved like water finding its level. Within ten heartbeats every monk in the hall was standing perfectly still, facing inward, hands visible, heads bowed — not toward Ada, I realized. Toward the floor. Toward the deep. Listening. Forty people listening with their whole bodies, and the silence they made was so total I could hear my own blood.

The groan faded. Or settled. Or waited. It was hard to tell the difference, and I have never in my life had more trouble telling the difference.

Anselm rose. He picked up his slate, and his chalk, and his hand shook — the first unsteady thing I had seen in that entire mountain — and he wrote:

**She must not finish.**

"She knows," I said. "She's been holding it back for weeks. She's better at it than—"

**You do not understand.** He underlined it twice, hard enough to snap a fleck off the chalk. **This mountain is not built on the binding. This mountain is part of it. We are not scholars. We are stitches. The order has held this peak for nineteen generations, and our vow of silence is not discipline. It is maintenance. Every word spoken carelessly anywhere loosens what sleeps below, and this is the one place in the world where the loose thread can be gripped. You brought the thread's owner into the wound.**

I looked at Ada. She had gone white. Her hand was still resting on the shelf, and I saw her slowly, deliberately, lift it away, the way you'd lift your hand off a hot stove.

"How much did she say?" I asked. "How close was she?"

Anselm wiped his slate. Wrote. Wiped it again, as if the answer were too heavy to carry even in chalk. Finally:

**Four words. Of a sentence of eleven.**

* * *

They gave us until dawn. That was the sentence Anselm pronounced, in writing, in the guest cell they'd lent us: **Until dawn. Then the mountain closes.**

Orla took the news the way she took all news — chewing on it. "Nineteen generations of mute monks sitting on a god," she said. "And they call *me* a criminal enterprise."

"It's not a god," I said. "It's worse. Gods want worship. This just wants to finish its sentence."

"And she's the sentence." Orla glanced at the corner of the cell, where Ada sat wrapped in a blanket, watching us with the careful stillness she'd learned. Watching, I noticed, the way she'd been watching me for three weeks: kindly, patiently, the way you watch a stranger who might become something. "Ledger-man. I've been thinking about the arithmetic."

"Don't."

"Eleven words. She's got — what, seven left? After the fortress burned, after the ford?" Orla held up fingers. "Every time she spends one, the thing downstairs gets louder. Every time somebody *steals* one, same thing, except crueler. So the math says the sentence gets finished no matter what we do. The only question is whether it gets finished by a prince with an army, a guild with a warehouse, or a girl with a conscience."

"There's a fourth option," I said quietly. "Nobody finishes it. Ever. She never speaks another whole sentence as long as she lives."

The silence that followed was the monastery's own silence, borrowed.

Ada unfolded herself from the corner. She crossed the cell and took Anselm's abandoned slate off the table — the monks had left it, along with chalk, for exactly this purpose — and she wrote on it in a small, precise hand. She had been learning our letters from my ledger, back when she remembered learning anything. Her handwriting was better than mine. That fact has annoyed me ever since.

She turned the slate around.

**Is that what you want? For me?**

And beneath it, smaller, after a pause I will hear for the rest of my life:

**Because I dreamed about him again. The one below. He is not angry. He is lonely. Two thousand years is a long time to hold one breath, Gideon.**

My name. She'd written my name — the name she'd paid away at the ford, the name she didn't remember giving up — and she'd written it like it was nothing, like it was a word she'd always known, and I understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand terrible things in pairs.

First: the name hadn't been erased. It had been *spent*. Paid into the sigil at the ford, sunk into the drowning-word like a coin into a well, and the well had kept it, and now the well was giving it back, drop by drop, because the thing below wanted her to know it knew her. Wanted her to know it knew *everything* she'd spent. It was introducing itself. In her own stolen currency.

Second: it was working. Because when I read my own name in her handwriting, some traitorous chamber of my heart went warm as a hearth.

"He talks to you," I said. My voice came out wrong. "In the dreams. What does he say?"

Ada wiped the slate. Wrote:

**He says: soon.**

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, and with it came the monks.

There was no violence in it. That was somehow the worst part. Six of them arrived at our cell with lanterns and slates, and Anselm at their head, and they simply — escorted us. Down the halls, past the jarred languages and the silenced bells, up the nine hundred steps, out the ironwood gate, which closed behind us with the same soundless finality with which it had opened.

At the threshold, Anselm caught my sleeve. He wrote fast, urgent, chalk snapping:

**Where will you go?**

"The Mouth of the World," I said. "The amphitheater. Where it was bound."

His face did something complicated. Pity and horror sharing a small room. He wrote:

**Then hear the last thing we know, keeper of ledgers, and spend it well. The binding was never finished. The ones who made it died mid-sentence — that is why it holds and why it frays. Eleven words were planned. Ten were spoken. The eleventh was never found, because the eleventh word was not a word at all.**

He paused. Looked at Ada. Looked at me. And wrote the rest slowly, pressing hard, each letter a nail:

**It was a name, given freely, by someone the power had no claim over. They searched the whole world for such a person and found none, and the empires fell, and the monks came here to wait. Nineteen generations, we have waited for someone the power has no claim over.**

He set down the chalk. He bowed to Ada — fully, deeply, the bow owed to a superior officer — and then, to my astonishment, he bowed to me too, and wrote one final line before the gate swallowed him:

**Forgive us. We cannot help you. We are only the stitches, and you are carrying the needle.**

The gate closed. The mountain stood silent against the sunrise, holding its breath, and I stood on the stair with a child who had forgotten me and a smuggler who hadn't asked why my hands were shaking, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the refectory.

Ten words spoken. One missing. A name, given freely, from someone with no claim on them.

Ada tugged my sleeve. She held up her slate, and on it, in her neat schoolroom letters, she had copied out the question she'd been carrying all morning:

**Gideon — why did the old monk bow to *you*?**

Behind us, far below the cloud line, in the direction of the lowlands and the roads and the world, I could almost hear two armies beginning to move.

"We're going to need a faster route down the mountain," I said. "Orla — how do you feel about rivers?"

Orla smiled the smile of a woman about to raise her rates.
