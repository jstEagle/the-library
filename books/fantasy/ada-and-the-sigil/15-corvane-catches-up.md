## 15. Corvane Catches Up

### Gideon

We had three days of peace at the monastery's edge before Corvane found us, which in my experience is exactly how long peace lasts when you owe someone money.

I should explain. We hadn't stayed *at* the monastery — the monks had made that abundantly clear by the simple expedient of opening their gates at dawn and gesturing toward the horizon with the serene unanimity of men who have taken vows of silence and intend to enjoy them. But we'd lingered in the pilgrims' shelter a half-mile downslope, because Ada was tired, because Orla's mule was lame, and because I am, at heart, a man who believes problems can be filed away if you just don't look at them directly.

The mountain had groaned. I kept hearing it in my sleep — that long tectonic complaint, like a giant rolling over in bed. Every time I closed my eyes I saw the abbot's face as Ada's sentence half-formed in the cold air: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has heard a debt collector knock and knows the debt is his.

So when the knock came at our shelter door on the third evening — two knocks, precise, unhurried, the knock of a man who has never once had to break down a door because doors open for him — I knew.

"Ledger-keeper," said Master Corvane Almeida through the wood, "you always did keep the worst hiding places."

I looked at Orla. Orla looked at me. Ada, who had been teaching herself to whistle with limited success and no discernible joy on anyone else's part, went very still in the way she did when she felt words gathering in her chest like weather.

"I could put a knife in him," Orla murmured, "before he finished being smug."

"He'd have finished being smug years ago," I said. "He's had practice." And then, because there was no version of this where I didn't open the door, I opened the door.

Corvane stood in the blue dusk with snow in his grey hair and not one single guard behind him, which frightened me more than forty guards would have. He wore traveling clothes instead of guild robes — good ones, but traveling clothes — and he held a small wrapped parcel in both hands like an offering.

"Master Almeida," he said warmly. "You look terrible."

"You look well," I said, "for a man who sent hunters after two children."

"One child and a ledger-keeper," he corrected gently. "May I come in? It's cold, and I've walked a long way to say something I should have said years ago."

Every instinct I owned — and I'd spent seven years sharpening them into instruments fine enough to catalogue bottled storms without losing fingers — told me not to let him in. I let him in. That is the whole tragedy of Gideon Almeida in one sentence: I have always known better, and I have always done it anyway.

* * *

He sat by our fire like he belonged there. That was always his gift. When I was fourteen and starving in the undercity, having recently discovered that apprenticeship to a hedge-witch paid in knowledge rather than bread, Corvane had sat down beside me in a tavern like he belonged there, and bought me a meal, and listened to me talk about ledgers with the rapt attention of a man hearing music. By the time I understood what the Vellum Guild actually traded in, my name was in their books, and debts to the Vellum Guild are not the kind you pay off. They're the kind that pay *you*, occasionally, so you stay.

He unwrapped the parcel. Inside was bread, cheese, dried fruit, and — this was the masterstroke — a small tin of honey cakes. The kind sold outside the temple district. The kind I'd bought for Ada once, in another life, three weeks ago.

"For the girl," he said. "I remember what hunger does to children. I was one."

Ada took the tin. She looked at him with those eyes that had watched empires die in dreams, and she took the tin, because she was nine years old and honey cakes are honey cakes, and something in my chest quietly broke.

"Gideon," Corvane said, "I'm going to talk, and you're going to want to interrupt, and I'd take it as a kindness if you saved it all up for the end. Agreed?"

I said nothing, which he chose to interpret as agreement.

"Prince Ilvane has seventeen of her words," he said. "You know this. You intercepted the courier reports — yes, I know about Orla's little postal service, don't look surprised, I've known about Orla since she stole a skiff from our Veil's Edge depot at nineteen. Seventeen words. With seventeen words, Ilvane burned Fort Cael. Do you understand what that means? Not metaphorically. Literally. A fortress with four hundred men inside it, and the prince spoke a stolen syllable over the map, and the mountain came down. He did it to test the merchandise." He leaned forward, firelight moving on his face. "He wants the rest. All of them. And when he has them, he will stand at the Mouth of the World and speak a god awake, and he genuinely believes he can hold its leash, because he is twenty-six years old and has never once been told no by anyone who mattered."

"And the guild?" I said. "The guild sold him the first six words. We started this."

"The guild made a mistake," Corvane said, without flinching, which was somehow worse than flinching. "A transactional error. We believed we were selling fragments of a dead language to a collector. We did not know we were selling ammunition. That error is why I'm here, Gideon, and not the hunters. I called the hunters off."

Orla snorted from the corner. "Called off the Quiet Brothers? Those lads don't come when called. They arrive when they arrive."

"They arrived," Corvane said. "At the ford. Where they drowned." He turned back to me. "Yes, I know about that too. I know everything, Gideon, it's my job, it has always been my job. Which brings me to my offer."

Here it was. Seven years of ledgers had taught me to recognize the shape of a contract before the first clause was read aloud. I braced myself.

"Pardon," he said. "Full pardon. Your debts dissolved, your name struck from the collection rolls, your little falsifications in the Night Ledger forgiven — and they were elegant, by the way, marking her as worthless stock, I'd have been proud if I weren't the victim. In exchange: you bring her to us. Not to sell. To *teach*. The guild will study the binding under controlled conditions. We will learn to hold the old power the way the old priests held it — not wake it, not sell it, but *hold* it, so that Ilvane cannot take it. Someone must be the wall between that boy and that door, Gideon. The guild has walls. The guild has survived four kings, two plagues, and one extremely regrettable incident with a bottled storm. Let us be the wall."

It was beautiful. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left. It was a beautiful offer, delivered by a man who had fed me when I was starving, clothed me when I was freezing, taught me a trade when I had nothing, and who had crossed a frozen mountain range alone, at his age, to make it in person. For one full minute — I counted, it's what I do — I wanted to say yes so badly my teeth ached.

Then I thought about the vaults. The records of the other speakers. Nine names in the restricted files, each one catalogued in my own careful hand before I understood what I was cataloguing. Each one marked *sold*. Each one marked, later, in a different ink, *lapsed*.

"Master Corvane," I said, "what happened to the speakers?"

His face did not change. That was the answer. His face was too well-trained to change, and it didn't.

"They were assessed," he said. "Their languages were extracted. The process is... taxing. Some of them did not survive the taxation."

"Some of them," I repeated.

"Three," he said. "Of nine. And I signed off on two of those extractions personally, and I have regretted it every day since, which is precisely why I am offering you a different path now. Gideon, I am not asking you to trust the guild. I am asking you to trust *me*. I have never lied to you. Not once, in seven years. Ask Orla. Ask anyone who's dealt with me. My word is the only clean thing I own."

"That's not true," I said quietly. "Your word isn't clean. Your word is the dirtiest thing you own, because it's the thing you spend. You've spent it on me my whole adult life. 'Just one more assessment.' 'Just one more catalogue entry.' 'Just help me move this crate up from the vaults, Gideon, mind you don't read the labels.'" I stood up. My legs were shaking and I was grateful to be standing where the shaking was less visible. "You've never lied to me. You've just never once told me the whole truth while the truth still mattered. There's a difference, and the difference has a body count."

Corvane looked at me for a long moment. And then — this is the thing I will remember until I die — he nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some examination.

"There he is," he said softly. "There's the boy who caught the sigil-work on the shelf before any of my scribes did. Do you know why I keep you invisible, Gideon? Why a ledger-keeper with your eye stays a ledger-keeper? Because men who see clearly are dangerous, and I wanted you where I could see you seeing." He rose, unhurried, brushing ash from his knees. "You're right. About all of it. The guild is corrupt. I am corrupt. We are a corruption that has, on balance, done less harm than the princes and priests around us, and I can live with that arithmetic, and you cannot, and that is the entire difference between us."

"So that's it. You'll send the hunters again."

"No," he said. "I told you. They're called off. What happens next is between Ilvane's army and whatever you decide to do at the Mouth of the World — and you *will* go there, Gideon, don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. The girl is drawn to it the way water is drawn downhill. So I'll leave you with this instead of soldiers." He paused at the door, snow-light behind him. "Ilvane doesn't need all her words. He needs most of them. Count what he has. Count what she has left. Then ask yourself what happens when a man who burns fortresses for *practice* runs out of patience — and ask yourself whether the guild holding the binding would truly be worse than a throne holding it. Kinder mask or crueler mask, Gideon, they're both masks. Choose whose face you'd rather live under."

And then he was gone, into the snow, and the door closed behind him with the soft click of a ledger being balanced.

Ada was looking at me. She still had the honey cakes clutched to her chest. She didn't eat one. She just looked at me with her old, old eyes, and said, in the common tongue she was learning word by painful word, the way a child learns anything:

"He is sad."

"Yes," I said. "He is."

"Sad is not safe," she said.

No, my darling. It never is.

* * *

### Corvane

People believe that power looks like armies. I have commanded armies, such as they are — the Vellum Guild's enforcers, the Quiet Brothers, the quiet money behind three magistrates and one archbishop — and I am here to tell you that power looks like a boy opening a door he shouldn't open, because you asked him nicely.

Gideon opened the door. Of course he did. I had counted on it, the way I count on everything, and yet my hands were not steady until it swung inward, and I confess that freely, here, in the privacy of my own skull, where no auditor can reach.

Let me tell you what nobody understands about me. They think I am a merchant of impossible things, and that the impossibility is the point. It isn't. The impossible things are inventory. The point is the *ledger*. Somewhere in this mad, burning world there must exist one book in which everything is written down truly — every debt, every payment, every price exacted and price owed — or else the world is just noise, and cruelty wins by default because cruelty keeps better records than kindness. I built the Vellum Guild to be that book. I have failed, obviously. Every institution fails; that is what institutions are *for*, in the end — failing slowly enough that people mistake the failure for order. But I have failed *in writing*, and there is a dignity in that.

The girl is the last unbalanced entry in my book. Everything else, I could close out honestly. Her, I cannot. Her language is the oldest debt in the world — a binding spoken by dead priests, collateralized across centuries, and now coming due in the person of a mud-caked child who says *he is sad* about me, correctly, damn her.

I meant what I told him. Every word. That is my one discipline: I do not lie. Lying is for men who fear audit. I offered him pardon and purpose, and I would have honored both, and if the guild's methods of "holding" the binding proved imperfect — if the extraction process cost the girl more than I promised it would — well. I would have written that down too. Truly. In my own hand. There is a dignity in that.

He refused me. Good. I confess — and here I sit by my own fire, three miles downslope, writing this by lantern light like the sentimental old clerk I am — I confess I am glad he refused me. If Gideon Almeida had said yes, I would have known that seven years of careful invisibility had finally rotted him, and I would have lost the only thing in my ledger I have never been able to price.

Because here is the truth I did not tell him, the one clause I left unread, and may the auditors of hell forgive me:

Ilvane's army is already marching on the amphitheater. Has been for days. My calling off the hunters changes nothing except who arrives second. And the guild cannot hold the binding — I have read the old priests' records, the ones in the vault even my masters don't know I've read. Holding requires a willing mortal name spoken into the sigil. A living anchor. No thief can steal it; no buyer can purchase it; no guild can conscript it. It must be *given*.

Which means either the girl gives hers — and dies, or worse, empties — or someone gives theirs in her place.

I know a man who would.

I raised him to.

I did not offer Gideon that bargain tonight because he would have refused it, and I am a coward about exactly one thing, and it wears his face and keeps my ledgers.

Instead I wrote three letters by lantern light. One to the guild, resigning my mastership — they will burn it, but it will be *on record*, and there is a dignity in that. One to Prince Ilvane, telling him nothing useful and everything insulting. And one short note, unsigned, addressed to a smuggler named Orla, care of a lame mule and a pilgrims' shelter:

*The prince reaches the Mouth of the World in eleven days. The guild's remaining agents reach it in nine. Whatever the boy intends, he intends it alone, because that is how I taught him to grieve. Do not let him. Some debts are not meant to be paid alone.*

I sealed it, and I gave it to the last Quiet Brother — the one who survived the ford, the one who owes the girl his drowning-death and knows it — and I watched him run into the snow like a black thread pulled through white cloth.

Then I sat down to finish my accounts.

There is a dignity in that.

* * *

### Gideon

Orla brought me the note at dawn. Black paper, no signature, her name spelled in a hand I'd know anywhere — I have catalogued ten thousand documents and that handwriting is in the vault of my memory between my mother's recipe book and the ransom note that ended my childhood.

She read it twice, handed it to me, and said, "Well. The old spider's gone soft."

"It's a trap," I said automatically.

"Obviously it's a trap. Everything's a trap. The question is whether it's a trap for *us* or for *him*." She scratched the mule's nose. "Numbers, ledger-man. How many days?"

I counted. It's what I do. "Nine until the guild. Eleven until the prince. Six until us, if the pass holds."

Ada was standing in the doorway with the sunrise behind her, and she was speaking — softly, to herself, in the old language, practicing. And I noticed, with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, that she was shorter by a word than she'd been a month ago. Smaller around the eyes. The language was spending her, and the prince was collecting the difference.

Six days. Two armies. One child made of sentences, running out of sentence.

"We leave within the hour," I said.

Orla grinned at me, and it was not a comforting grin. "Knew you'd say that. Pack light, ledger-man. From here on, we're all carrying more than we can hold."

Behind us, far below in the valley, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain groaned again.

And Ada stopped mid-word, and turned toward the sound, and I saw her mouth shape a syllable I had never heard her use before — one that made the frost on the railing crawl sideways, one that the monks would have fled from, one that sounded, God help me, like a *greeting*.

Something down in the dark of the world had heard her practicing.

And it answered.
