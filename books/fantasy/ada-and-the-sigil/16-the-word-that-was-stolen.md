## 16. The Word That Was Stolen

Here is what nobody tells you about being stolen: it is very quiet at first.

I had thought, if it ever happened, there would be shouting. Gideon shouting my name the way he did at the ford, before I lost it — *Ada, Ada, run* — and Orla's knife out, and the horses screaming. There would be noise, because everything in the world of men was noise. The guild had been noise. The monastery had been bells and then no bells at all, which was its own kind of noise.

But the Quiet Brothers had taught me that silence could be a net, and the guild's agents had learned the same lesson from someone. They came at the hour when Orla's campfire burned down to orange memory and Gideon slept with his ledger under his head like a pillow, which was a habit of his I had decided to find funny rather than sad.

A hand over my mouth. Not rough. That was the worst part, later, when I understood it. The hand was *careful*. They had been told to bring me unmarked. Goods, not enemies.

I bit him anyway. He made a small hurt sound, polite as an apology, and then the world went soft at the edges and dark at the middle, and the last thing I saw was Gideon, asleep, dreaming whatever ledger-men dream.

* * *

I woke in a wagon.

Not a cart, not a cell — a wagon lined with wool, with a brazier glowing at one end and a woman sitting beside me doing embroidery, as if we were on a holiday and she were my aunt. She had kind eyes. I have learned since that kind eyes are the cheapest thing a person can own.

"You're awake," she said, in the trade-tongue, slow and gentle. "Good. Don't be frightened. We're taking you somewhere warm."

I knew some of the trade-tongue by then. Gideon had been teaching me on the road, patient as rain, pointing at things — *tree, river, bread, Gideon* — and I had been teaching him things back that had no points. I said, in his language, "Where is the man who keeps ledgers?"

"Somewhere else," she said, and smiled her kind smile, and went back to her embroidery, which was a pattern of small birds, and I lay there and hated her with a purity I had not known I was capable of.

The wagon traveled for a day and a night. Through the canvas I heard wheels, and once, distantly, a river, and once a voice giving orders in the clipped accent of the Vellum Guild — I knew it from Gideon's imitations, which were cruel and excellent. There were at least six of them. Maybe more. The embroidery woman never slept, or slept with her eyes open; I tried her twice and she was watching me both times.

They fed me stew. It was good stew. I want that recorded, wherever my words end up being recorded: they stole me and fed me good stew, and I ate it, because I was a child and children are always hungry, and I have stopped pretending that this makes me foolish. It makes me a child. The men who took advantage of that are the ones who should be ashamed, and they were not, because shame requires a soul and theirs had been traded years ago, probably for something small. A pension. A title. A pattern of small birds.

* * *

They took me to a house.

Not a fortress, not a tower — a house, a fine one, in a town whose name I never learned, with a garden gone wild for winter and a door that opened before we knocked. Inside there were lamps and books and a table with straps, and I understood the table immediately, the way you understand a trap you've seen in dreams.

The man waiting there was not Master Corvane. He was smaller and sweatier, with ink on his fingers to the second knuckle, and he looked at me the way Gideon looked at a mislabeled ledger entry: with the hunger of a man about to correct an error.

"Hello, Ada," he said. My name. My name in his mouth, in the trade-tongue, and it sounded like a specimen label. "I'm going to ask you to say some things for me. Just some words. It won't hurt."

Here is the thing about lying to a child: we are not stupid, we are only young. I had felt what words cost me. I had healed Gideon's hand once and slept for a day and a half. I had drowned six Quiet Brothers with a sentence and woken not knowing the name of the only person in the world who was mine. I knew the arithmetic of my own voice better than anyone alive, and this man with ink to the second knuckle was about to do sums with it.

I said nothing. I had learned silence from the best hunters in the world, and I gave it back to them now, gift-wrapped.

He was patient too. Everyone was patient with me. They had a whole guild's worth of patience and one small girl, and they thought the math was in their favor.

It was, in the end. I want to be honest, because honesty is the only thing I have left that they can't inventory. They did not torture me. They did not threaten me. They did something much more efficient.

They brought in a bird.

A wren, in a cage, ordinary and brown and furious, and the ink-fingered man held a knife over it and said, "You know how this works better than we do, Ada. Words have weight. Say nothing, and I'll find a way to make the weight land somewhere."

And I thought: he's bluffing. Words are mine. They can't take a word out of me the way you take a tooth.

I was young. I have already said that. Let it stand as my defense, and let it be insufficient.

* * *

They strapped me down anyway — not for pain, for *stillness*, the woman with the kind eyes explained, stillness helps the extraction, like keeping a jar steady while you decant it. The ink-fingered man opened a book that hummed. Not sang — hummed, the way a struck bell hums after the sound you hear is done, the way the amphitheater in my dreams hums under its green water. It was a book made of pages of my language, or pieces of it, gathered from other places, other speakers, all the speakers before me who had been sold and vanished. I could feel the pages reaching for me. Like recognizes like. My words leaned toward that book the way a child leans toward a window when someone she loves is leaving.

"No," I said, in my language, and the lamp flames all bent toward me like grass in wind, and the ink-fingered man went pale and stepped back and then — I will give him this — stepped forward again, because greed is a kind of courage, the worst kind, the kind that always shows up.

They chose the word carefully. I learned this afterward, from Orla, who learned it from a courier she robbed: they had a list. A manifest. The guild's masters had catalogued my vocabulary the way Gideon once catalogued a bottled storm — by rarity, by power, by price — and the first word they took was not the strongest. It was a test. A proof of concept, for the buyer. Something small enough to be safe, valuable enough to be worth the trouble.

They took one of my first words.

I don't know how to explain what it was, because explaining requires the word, and the word is gone. It was a word for *fire the way fire is when it is welcome* — hearth-fire, candle-fire, the fire that means someone is home. In my language there was no separate word for hearth and home and warmth; it was one word, one thing, the oldest kind of word, the kind you learn before you learn your own name. Gideon's hand had been that word when he first pulled me out of the river mud and wrapped it in cloth. The wren in its cage was that word, small and warm and furious.

The ink-fingered man put his hands on either side of my head, not touching, and read from the humming book, and I felt it happen.

It did not hurt. That is the lie everyone believes about theft of the soul: that it hurts. It does not hurt. It *unhappens*. There was a warm thing in me, a hearth-thing, a home-thing, and then there was a space where it had been, and the space was shaped exactly like it, and the space was cold, and I knew — the way you know a room is empty before you look — that the warm thing was gone and would not be coming back.

I heard it leave. Not with my ears. I heard it the way the world heard it: a syllable of the oldest grammar, spoken once, by a man's mouth, in a voice that was not his and would never fit him, and then —

Far away — so far that the hearing of it was more memory than sound — a border fortress burned.

I did not see it. I *felt* it, the way you feel a bell through a floor. Stone that had stood for three hundred years, garrison walls, a beacon tower, the whole warm machinery of a place where soldiers slept and cooked and complained and wrote letters home — all of it touched by my word for welcome-fire, and my word, torn from its meaning, did what fire does. It welcomed everything. The stone burned. The river near it boiled. Two hundred people, Orla told me later. Two hundred people, and the guild's ledger recorded it, I am certain, as *successful field deployment, one unit*.

I screamed. I want to be accurate about this too: I screamed for a long time, and the ink-fingered man did not enjoy it, and the woman with the kind eyes held my hand and told me I was safe, and I hated her so completely and so cleanly that the hatred felt almost like the word I had lost. Almost warm. Almost home.

* * *

They came back for me the second time at dawn, and that time Orla came with them.

Not *with* them. *Through* them, is more accurate. I heard it from the wagon where they'd stowed me — shouting, a horse screaming, the wet percussion sounds that a knife makes when it is being used by someone who has stopped being polite about it. The wagon door tore open and Orla's face appeared, smudged and bleeding at the hairline and grinning like a festival.

"Hello, sprout," she said. "Sorry I'm late. Traffic."

She cut the straps. My hands were too cold to work and she swore at them, at my hands, affectionately, the way she swore at everything, and wrapped them in her scarf, and carried me out of the house on her hip like I was a much smaller child than I was, and I put my face in her neck and did not cry, because the place where crying would have come from was one of the places that had been emptied.

Behind us the house was burning. Orla's work. "Symmetry," she said, when she saw me looking. "They'll understand it. Ledger-people always understand fire."

Gideon was waiting at the tree line with the horses, and his face when he saw me — I remember his face. It was the face of a man who has been doing sums all night and gotten the worst possible answer, and I remember thinking, distantly, coldly, from the cold room inside me: *he looks like that because of me. Because I was taken. Because I matter to him.*

And the thought did not warm me.

That is what I mean by hollowed. Understand me correctly: I was glad to see him. I knew I was glad the way you know the sun is up when you're blind — by the warmth on the far side of a wall. But the word that would have made gladness into a thing I could hold, a hearth-thing, a home-thing — that word was in a fortress's ash, in two hundred dead people, in a guild ledger marked *paid*, and there was no getting it back, and I knew it the way I knew my own hands.

Gideon reached for me and I let him hold me, and I felt him feel the difference in me, and I felt his heart break a little through his ribs, and some cold clear part of me took note of that too, filed it, the way a ledger-keeper files things.

*Look at that,* said the cold clear part. *He breaks, and I don't.*

* * *

We rode north for two days. Orla talked enough for all three of us, which was her way of being kind, filling the silence so it couldn't fill itself. Gideon barely spoke. He watched me the way you watch a fire that has gone out but might not be out.

On the second night, camped in the ruin of some old waystation, I finally spoke.

"The word they took," I said, in the trade-tongue, carefully, because the trade-tongue had no word for it either and I had to build the meaning out of sticks. "It was one of the first ones. Before I had my name, I had that one. It was — " and I gestured, at the fire, at the bedrolls, at Gideon and Orla and the horse, all of it, everything warm — "it was *this*. All of this was one word. And now it's a fortress that burned, and two hundred people I never met are dead because a man with ink on his hands wanted to see if I was real."

Gideon said my name. Softly. The way you say a name when you're afraid the person attached to it might be a door that's closed.

"And here is the worst part," I said, and my voice came out level, and I was almost proud of that, almost, before I understood that the levelness was the wound itself. "I can still feel the shape where it was. Like a tooth-socket. And do you know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking: they'll come back for the others. They took one to test. The buyer paid for a proof. Which means somewhere there is a list, and my whole mouth is on it, and every word I have left is a thing that can burn a city."

"Ada," Gideon said again.

"And I used to think my language was a *gift*," I said, and here the levelness finally cracked, one hairline fracture, one drop of the old me leaking through the cold. "I used to lie in the dark on the road and think: I am made of the oldest words in the world, and that makes me precious. Precious." I laughed. It was an ugly sound and I let it be ugly. "Do you know what precious means, Gideon? Precious means *expensive*. Precious means people will cross the world and kill for you and it will never once occur to them to ask if you'd rather be left alone. I am not a gift. I am a *vault*, and everyone I meet is a thief, and the only person who wasn't — "

I stopped.

I had been about to say *the only person who wasn't was you* — and I couldn't, because of the ford, because of the drowned brothers, because the name I would have said it with had been the toll I paid, and the man sitting across the fire from me was a stranger wearing a stranger's kindness, and I knew his stories about the ledger-keeper were invented, I had always known, and I loved him anyway, somewhere, behind the wall, in the cold room.

"I'm tired," I said instead.

Gideon nodded. He got up and put another branch on the fire, and I watched him do it, and I watched him be careful with the fire, more careful than a man needs to be, and I understood that he knew. That he had understood, since the moment he lifted me onto the horse, exactly which word was gone. Ledger-keepers know what's missing from a page. It's the whole trade.

Orla, meanwhile, was not looking at the fire. She was looking at the road south, the way we'd come, and her face had the expression she got when she was counting other people's money in her head. When she caught me watching she jerked her chin at the dark.

"Courier I robbed," she said, quietly, for my ears and Gideon's both. "Before I found you. He was carrying more than a manifest." She turned a coin over her knuckles — a guild mark, gold, new-struck. "There's a name on the orders. The buyer. And sprout, it's not a merchant, and it's not the guild's masters."

She flipped the coin to Gideon, who caught it without looking at it, because he was looking at me, and Orla smiled the smile she saved for very bad news.

"It's a prince," she said. "And he's not collecting your words, little vault. He's collecting a *sentence*. And I'd wager everything I've ever stolen that he already knows where the last pieces are sleeping."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far south, under green water, something I had seen only in dreams turned over in its sleep, and I felt it turn, the way you feel a bell through a floor.

I picked up a stick and fed it to the fire, carefully, the way Gideon did, and I began, in the cold room behind the wall, to make a list of my own.
