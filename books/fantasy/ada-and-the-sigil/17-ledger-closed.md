## 17. Ledger Closed

There is a particular smell to a guild ledger when it burns. I know this now the way I know the weight of my own hands: intimately, and with a certain professional regret.

It is not parchment, mostly. The Vellum Guild does not deal in vellum for its ledgers — that would be too honest, too much like admitting what we are. The pages are treated hide, cured with ash-salts and something Corvane never let me see prepared, so that ink sinks in and cannot be scraped out again. A ledger of impossible things must itself be impossible to erase. Every entry permanent. Every debt eternal.

Which is why it took me three attempts and half a flask of Orla's lamp-oil before the thing consented to catch.

* * *

We had made camp in the shell of a watchtower on the road south of the monastery, the kind of ruin that dot the fractured realm like teeth in an old jaw. The monks had turned us out at dawn with the gentle firmness of men who have spent lifetimes practicing silence and had gotten very good at saying nothing loudly. Ada had not spoken since the mountain groaned. She walked with her hands cupped in front of her like a girl carrying water she was afraid to spill, except there was nothing in her hands, and I understood — finally, horribly — that what she was carrying was her own voice, held carefully shut behind her teeth.

Orla built the fire. Orla always builds the fire; she says it's because I build them like a man apologizing to the wood.

"You've been staring at that book since noon," she said, without looking up from the spit. "Either read it or feed it to the flames. A man caught between the two looks like a donkey tied between two haystacks."

"It's a copy," I said. "The master ledger. My hand, my entries, four years of them."

"Sentimental?"

"Inventoried." I turned it over. The cover was scarred leather, stamped with the guild's sigil — a quill piercing a closed eye. "Every impossible thing that ever passed through our vaults. Bottled storms. Keys to doors that no longer exist. A jar of last words, still warm. And—" I stopped.

"And her."

"And her." Entry eleven hundred and forty-one. *One speaker, female, approx. eight years. Language unclassified. Assessed value pending.* Pending. For months that word had been a mercy. Now I knew it had only been patience.

Orla spat into the fire. "Burn it, then."

"It's the only proof of what they did. Of who bought what. If anything happens to me—"

"If anything happens to you, a book won't save her. You will." She looked up then, and her eyes were the flat grey of open water. "I've carried cargo my whole life, Almeida. Contraband, fugitives, one memorable season, a plague-god's fingerbone. You learn the difference between things you keep and things you carry. That book is luggage. The girl is cargo of a different order entirely. Burn the luggage."

I want to say I burned it immediately. I want to tell you I was decisive, that the man who fled the guild by night through flooded streets and drowned three Quiet Brothers' silence in a river came to his convictions quickly. But the truth is I sat with that ledger on my knees while the fire cracked and spat, and I read it. All of it. Or enough of it. Page after page in my own careful hand, the hand Master Hedge-Witch Sable once told me was "too neat for a boy who'd amount to trouble" — she'd meant it as a compliment, I think — recording the buying and selling of things that should never have had prices.

I found the entry for the bottled storm I'd catalogued in my first week. I found the entry for the linguist Vess, sold north after he asked one question too many about where the speakers went. I found my own name, entered the day Corvane took me off the street: *One apprentice, runaway, debts assumed. Value: loyalty.*

Corvane had written that himself. Not *value: skilled*. Not *value: promising*. Loyalty. He had purchased me the way you purchase a dog, and I had wagged for him for six years.

That was the page that decided it. Not the children. Not even Ada. My own entry, my own price, written in a hand I knew better than my mother's.

I fed the ledger to the fire a section at a time, because treated hide burns grudgingly, and I watched four years of my life curl and blacken and go up as smoke that smelled, I realized, exactly like the incense in the guild's assessment chambers. Of course it did. Same salts. Same cure. The guild had been burning its ledgers in little ways all along, every time it burned through a person.

When the spine finally caught — the spine resists longest; the binding holds even as everything else goes — Orla said quietly, "Good. Now the hard part."

* * *

Ada was sitting at the mouth of the tower, knees drawn up, watching the dark come down over the valley. She didn't turn when I sat beside her. She hadn't turned when I sat beside her in weeks. This is the arithmetic of what her sigil cost at the river ford: she gave up my name to drown the Quiet Brothers, and names, once given, do not return. She knows me now as a stranger who tells stories about a man who keeps ledgers. She has begun, cautiously, to like the stranger. She has no idea she liked him first.

"I'm going to tell you something," I said. "And afterward you may decide you don't want to travel with me anymore. Orla will take you south regardless. I've already paid her. So whatever you choose, you'll be safe. That part isn't a bargain. It's just true."

She looked at me sideways. In the firelight her eyes were the color of the old language — which is to say, I no longer knew how to describe them, because every description felt like translation, and translations were how all of this began.

"The man who keeps ledgers," I said. "The one from the stories. He isn't a story."

Her head tilted. A small furrow appeared between her brows, the same furrow she got when a word rearranged something on a shelf.

"He's me. I'm him. And I need to tell you what he did, because you deserve to hear it from someone, and there's no one else left who knows all of it."

So I told her.

I told her about the night she arrived at the guild door, mud-caked and silent-then-not-silent, speaking a tongue that made the shelves shiver. I told her how I had been the one who understood — or thought he understood — that her words were not words but workings, living sigils older than any kingdom. How I had catalogued her. Entry eleven hundred and forty-one. I said the number out loud because she deserved to hear it said aloud, the way you read charges against yourself.

I told her about the restricted vaults, the records of the other speakers, the ones sold to unnamed buyers and vanished. I told her that when I first understood she was inventory, I did not run. That is the part I made myself say slowly, because it would have been easy to skip, and skipping is how liars are made. "I stayed," I said. "For weeks. I brought you food. I sat with you. And every hour I sat with you, some quiet part of me was doing sums. What you were worth. Whether there was a version of this where I kept you safe *and* kept my place. Where I was clever enough to have both."

She was very still. Stillness, I had learned, was her language for listening.

"There was a night," I said, "when Corvane asked me for my assessment. Your assessment. And I wrote it. I wrote that your language showed signs of fragmentation, that full recovery of the original text seemed unlikely, that your value was therefore moderate rather than exceptional. I wrote it because I thought marking you worthless would protect you. And maybe it did, for a while. But I also wrote it because I was afraid, and because a frightened man tells himself whatever lets him keep his bed and his meals and his master's kind opinion." I looked at the fire, where the last corner of the ledger's spine was still glowing like a dying eye. "I planned to sell you, Ada. Not with my whole heart. But with enough of it. Enough of it is the same thing."

The wind moved through the broken windows of the tower. Somewhere below, Orla was pretending very hard to be absorbed in the cooking.

"I falsified the records after that," I went on, because the confession had its own momentum now, a sled on a slope. "I marked you worthless stock. I stole you away in the night. I've lied to hunters, drowned men, burned bridges — real ones, in Veil's Edge, Orla can tell you. Everything since has been me trying to spend my way out of a debt I opened myself. And I don't know if that makes the running a penance or just more spending. I genuinely don't know. I've had a lot of time on roads to work it out and I've concluded that I am not the person to judge it. So I'm giving it to you. Whatever you do with it is yours."

She said nothing. Of course she said nothing; she was holding her voice behind her teeth like water she was afraid to spill, and even if she weren't, she had no memory of the guild, of the vaults, of the nights I'd sat with her doing sums. I was confessing to a stranger crimes committed against a girl who no longer existed in her. It was absolution sought from the wrong person, or maybe — and this thought arrived unwelcome, cold as riverwater — maybe there was no right person left. Maybe the girl I wronged was gone the way the ledger was gone, burned down to a smell in the air.

She reached out and touched the back of my scarred hand. The one whose scars she had healed, once, with a word that cost her a memory I'll never identify. Her fingers were small and grave.

Then she spoke.

Not a sigil. Not the old language. She said, in the trade-tongue, in a child's careful accent, the syllables new to her mouth like shoes not yet broken in:

"Gideon."

My name. Not my name as she remembered it — she doesn't remember it — but my name as I had told it to her, two nights ago, in one of the invented stories about the man who keeps ledgers. I had given it to her casually, thinking it was nothing, thinking names were cheap. She had kept it. She had carried it in her cupped hands all this way along with her shuttered voice, and now she set it down between us on the cold stone like a coin offered to a beggar.

"That's right," I managed. My voice did something embarrassing. "Gideon Almeida. At your service. Formerly entry eleven hundred and forty-one's keeper. Currently unemployed."

The corner of her mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The promise of one, filed under pending.

And that was when Orla's hand closed hard on my shoulder, and she said, in the voice she used for bad news delivered fast, "Almeida. Road. Torches. A lot of them."

* * *

We put out the fire and went to the broken parapet, and I counted lights the way I had once counted inventory: methodically, hopelessly. Two dozen torches winding up the switchback road from the valley floor. Too disciplined for bandits. Too many for a guild hunting party — unless the guild had stopped hunting and started marching.

At their head rode a rider whose banner I could just make out in the torchlight, and my stomach performed a maneuver I had previously only read about in accounts of sea voyages. Not the quill-pierced-eye of the Vellum Guild. A silver tower on black. The arms of Prince Ilvane.

"They're not following us," Orla breathed. "They're not even looking for us. They're headed—" She stopped. I felt her arrive at it a heartbeat before I did, the way you feel thunder before you admit you heard the lightning.

South. Past us. Toward the coast, and the sunken amphitheater the mute monks refused to name, the place the old maps called the Mouth of the World.

"He has enough words," I said. The realization came out of me flat and calm, which is how the worst things always arrive. "He doesn't need her anymore. He needs the door."

Beside me, Ada had gone rigid. And though she held her voice shut, though she spilled nothing, the stones beneath our feet began — very softly, like a sleeper turning over — to hum.
