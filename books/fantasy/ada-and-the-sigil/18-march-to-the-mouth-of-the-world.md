## 18. March to the Mouth of the World

### Gideon

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that happens in a man's chest when he knows he is walking toward his own ending. It is not fear, exactly. Fear is a merchant's emotion — it haggles, it bargains for more time. What I felt on the third morning of our march was closer to bookkeeping. A final tally. Assets: one child, one smuggler, one mute monk who had broken his order's silence exactly once to tell us where to go. Liabilities: an army behind us, a prince ahead of us, and a language older than kingdoms sleeping in a girl's mouth.

The ledger did not balance. It never had.

We came down out of the hill country at dawn, following the Ashen River as it widened and slowed, growing sullen the way water does when it remembers what it has drowned. The Mouth of the World — that was what the monks called it, in their careful hand-signs, before Abbot Serrin pressed a map into my hands with both palms, the way you'd return something borrowed from a dead man. The map showed the river bending into a basin no cartographer had bothered to name, because nothing there was worth naming. Just a depression in the earth, half-swallowed, where two empires had once stood and then, in a single terrible season, had not.

"Is it true," Ada asked me, "that the water goes down into it and never comes back?"

I looked at her. She had changed since the ford — since the word she'd spent drowning the Quiet Brothers, since she'd turned to me afterward with polite, empty eyes and asked who I was. Three weeks of relearning. Three weeks of stories about *the man who keeps ledgers*, told by the man himself, until she laughed at the right places and trusted me again, though the trust sat differently now. Lighter. More easily carried away.

"It's true," I said. "Rivers go in. Nothing comes out."

"Good," she said, and said no more about it, which frightened me more than anything else she might have said.

Orla caught up to us on the ridge above the basin, boots muddy to the knee, spyglass already out. She was the kind of woman who made even dread look like a business transaction.

"Well," she said, lowering the glass. "Bad news and worse news."

"I've had a lot of both lately," I said. "Give me the worse first."

"The guild's coming down the north road. Corvane himself, with maybe forty agents and enough sigil-cages to pack a zoo." She handed me the glass. "And the bad news is the prince."

I put the glass to my eye. The basin spread below us like a cupped hand, green and drowned, the amphitheater's broken tiers rising from the marshwater in crescents of black stone. And on the far rim, arranged with the obscene neatness of imperial ambition, camped Ilvane's army. Banners. Siege-gear. Wagons that could only be carrying the stolen words — I didn't know how you transported a sentence in a box, but I had learned in the last months that the world kept cruder instruments than I'd ever imagined.

"They're not besieging anything," I said slowly. "They're excavating."

"They're draining," Orla corrected. "Look at the sluices."

I looked. Channels had been cut through the marshland, feeding the basin's waters away into the river below, and the water level had dropped visibly — a tide going out that would not come back in. Every hour lowered the lake around the sunken tiers. Every hour brought the amphitheater's heart closer to open air.

"He means to walk down onto the stage dry-shod," Ada said quietly.

Orla and I both looked at her. She was watching the basin with an expression I couldn't read — or rather, I could read it, and wished I couldn't. It was the expression of someone looking at a house they'd lived in before they were born.

"You remember it," I said.

"No," she said. "But it remembers me."

* * *

### Ada

Here is what they do not tell you about being a door: everyone knocks.

All my life — all eleven years of it, the ones I can count — people have wanted things from my mouth. The masters in their tall house wanted my words in little glass rooms. The Quiet Brothers wanted my silence. The man in the fine coat across the water wanted my sentences whole, so he could take them apart like a clock. Even Gideon, kind Gideon, ledger-Gideon, had once planned to sell me, and had told me so beside a fire with his head bowed like a boy at confession, and I had forgiven him, mostly, because forgiveness is cheaper than loneliness.

But the thing under the water did not want to take. That was the difference. That was what made my teeth ache and my dreams run backward.

It wanted to give back.

Every night now I dreamed in the old grammar. Not the war — I'd shown Gideon that already, the two empires unmaking each other while the spoken god walked between them wearing a crown of borrowed faces. No, the new dreams were smaller and worse. They were domestic. In them I sat in the amphitheater's lowest tier, in a seat worn smooth by ten thousand years of rain, and something vast and patient sat beside me, and we talked. It taught me words I had lost. It gave me back the name of the man who keeps ledgers — I woke with it warm in my mouth, *Gideon*, tasting of ink and smoke — and I understood, with a child's cold clarity, that it was not being generous.

It was stocking its shelves.

Everything given is owed. That was the first law of the old grammar, older than the binding itself. The thing beneath the water had been bound by a sentence, starved of speech for an age, and now here came a girl made of its own stolen alphabet, walking toward it on her own feet, carrying every fragment the prince hadn't yet pried out of her. It was not luring me. Luring was for fishermen. It was simply leaving the door open, and letting me remember why I'd want to come home.

"Gideon," I said, on the last ridge before the descent. He turned. He always turned; it was his best quality. "When this is done — whatever happens down there — don't let them use my words to build anything. Not a cage. Not a kingdom. Not even a kindness."

He was quiet for a long moment. Below us, the drained basin gleamed, and the black tiers of the amphitheater stood up out of the mud like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and been buried standing.

"What do we build instead?" he asked.

"Nothing," I said. "Some doors should just be doors."

He nodded, and wrote something in the little book he still carried, even now, even here — his habit, his comfort, his spine. I peeked. He'd written: *Asset: one door. Intention: leave it shut.* Underneath, smaller: *Cost: everything.*

That was the thing about Gideon's ledgers. They always balanced. He just hated telling you what the other column held.

### Orla

I've run cargo past navies, bribed admirals with their own confiscated brandy, and once talked a kraken-cult out of sinking my ship by convincing them their god preferred fish. I am not easily impressed by trouble.

But I will confess, privately, to no one, that when I stood on that ridge and saw the prince's banners and the guild's cages converging on the same drowned hole in the world, my first professional instinct was to calculate the fastest route in the opposite direction. My second instinct was to look at the girl.

Ada stood at the ridge's edge with the wind pulling her hair sideways, and she wasn't afraid. That was the part that decided me, gods help me. I've seen soldiers afraid, kings afraid, whole crews afraid. Fear I know how to price. But that child looked at the largest army I'd ever seen assembled in one place and wore the face of a woman reading a letter she'd written herself, long ago, and forgotten.

You don't abandon cargo like that. You don't abandon *people* like that either, which was newer territory for me, and less comfortable.

"So," I said, crouching beside Gideon in the gorse. "Forty guild agents from the north. Two thousand regulars from the east. Us." I counted on my fingers with theatrical care. "Three. Well, three and a half, counting the girl twice, since she's worth more than the rest of us combined."

"We're not trying to beat them," Gideon said. His voice had gone flat and calm, the way it did when the numbers were bad and he'd stopped pretending otherwise. "We're trying to arrive first. Or at least together."

"Together with whom?"

"With everyone. That's the horror of it, isn't it? Everyone's marching to the same door. The prince wants to open it. Corvane wants to own the lock. The monks wanted it sealed forever and failed." He rubbed his scarred hand — the healed one, the one her word had made new, back when she still knew his name. "And Ada wants—"

"Ask her," I said. "Not me."

He glanced at her silhouette against the sky. "She wants to finish it herself. Whatever finishing means. I can see it. She's been saying goodbye to us for two days."

I had seen it too. The girl had started giving things away — her flint to me, her ribbon to the river, small objects surrendered with the solemnity of a queen distributing her estate. Children do that when they've decided something adults haven't been told yet.

"Then here's my offer, ledger-keeper, and I'll only make it once." I spat, for emphasis, because sentiment should always be seasoned with spit. "I'll get you down the north slope ahead of the guild's vanguard. After that, whatever you're planning — and I know you're planning something stupid, because you have the face of a man planning something stupid — you do it without me holding your hand."

"And after?"

"After," I said, "I steal something. I haven't decided what yet. Possibly a prince's seal. Possibly his entire treasury. Possibly, if the mood takes me, his dignity." I stood, knees cracking, and offered him my hand. "Come on, then. Let's go ruin everyone's day at once."

### Corvane

Master Corvane rode at the head of his forty agents and permitted himself, for the first time in nine years, the luxury of doubt.

He had dressed carefully for the occasion — grey traveling wool, no insignia, the dress of a man attending a funeral rather than a conquest. Beside him, in a lacquered case lined with velvet, rode the recovered words: seven fragments of the oldest sentence in the world, each one humming faintly against its cage like a bee in a jar. Seven pieces of a god's prison, bought and bled out of a child. He found he could not look at the case directly. This troubled him more than the army on the far shore.

*You are doing this to save her,* he reminded himself, and it was even partly true, which was the most dangerous kind of truth. If the guild controlled the binding, no prince could weaponize it. If the guild controlled the binding, the girl could live — watched, catalogued, confined, but alive. Order over ruin. He had built his life on that trade, and he had bought a runaway apprentice's loyalty with it once, and the boy had repaid him with fire and flight, and Corvane had discovered, somewhere on the long north road, that the wound of that betrayal sat deeper than any ledger could reach.

"There," said Vess, his second, pointing down into the basin. "Movement on the north slope. Three figures."

Corvane raised his glass. The boy. The smuggler. The girl, small between them, walking down toward the water with the unhurried stride of someone going home.

"Let them descend," he said.

Vess stared. "Master?"

"If we take them on the slope, we fight the prince's outriders too, and the girl spends her words killing my people, and everything I came to prevent happens anyway." He lowered the glass. His hands, he noticed, were not entirely steady. "No. We let them lead us down. We let everyone gather at the door — prince, thief, apprentice, child. And then, Vess, we discover whether I am the kind of man who can do what must be done."

"And if you're not?"

Corvane looked at the lacquered case, at the faint golden light seeping through its seams, and thought of a boy he'd once pulled out of a snowdrift and fed and taught to keep accounts, and how the boy had grown into a man who burned ledgers.

"Then," he said quietly, "God help us all, because I won't be able to."

* * *

### Gideon

We reached the uppermost tier as the sun went down, and the world held its breath.

Below us, the drained amphitheater lay open to the sky for the first time in an age — a great black eye socket of stone, the seats descending in perfect rings toward a stage of pale silt where nothing grew and nothing decayed. The air above it bent strangely, the way air bends over a fire, except there was no heat. Only attention. The whole basin felt *watched*, and I understood suddenly why the rivers refused to flow out of it. Water, too, knows better than to turn its back.

Across the flooded causeway, torches were moving. Ilvane's engineers had thrown a bridge of pontoons across the last channel, and the prince himself was crossing it — I could see him even at this distance, gold-crowned, walking with the light, easy stride of a man arriving at his own coronation. Behind him came the wagons. Behind the wagons came two thousand soldiers who did not know what their prince intended to wake, and I felt a flicker of something almost like pity for them. They thought they were guarding an excavation. They were pallbearers who hadn't been told whose funeral it was.

And behind *us*, up the north slope, lanterns in a disciplined line. Forty points of light descending without hurry. Corvane, letting everyone gather at the door, just as I'd known he would. He had never in his life opened a negotiation without controlling the room, and this basin was about to become the most crowded room in the history of the world.

"Everyone's here," Orla murmured. "Prince, priest, pirate, pauper. All we're missing is a poet."

"You're looking at him," I said. "I keep ledgers. It's practically verse."

Ada stepped forward to the edge of the topmost tier. The wind came up out of the basin and moved around her — not past her, *around*, the way water moves around a stone — and I saw her shoulders straighten, and I saw her mouth open slightly, and I heard, very faintly, from the pale stage far below, a sound like a great bell being struck underwater.

One note. Then silence. Then, unmistakably, a voice — vast, slow, glad, and terribly patient — speaking a single word in the language older than kingdoms.

Ada translated it for us without turning around. Her voice was steady. Her hand, hanging at her side, was not, and I took it, and she let me.

"What did it say?" Orla whispered.

"It said," Ada answered, "*welcome home*."

Down on the causeway, Prince Ilvane stopped walking. Up the north slope, forty lanterns halted as one. And in the lacquered case at Master Corvane's side, seven stolen fragments of the oldest sentence in the world began, all together, to sing.
