## 19. The Sentence Completed

I have been trying, all the way down into the Mouth of the World, to remember how many words I have left.

It is not like counting coins. Coins sit still. My words move. Some of them I can feel the way you feel a tooth that has been pulled — a hollow place in the gum of my mouth where something used to live. Others are only gone entirely, and what frightens me most is that I cannot tell which is which anymore. When Gideon asks me — and he asks gently, the way he does everything now, as if he is afraid the world will break if he handles it too fast — I hold up my fingers.

Seven.

Or maybe five.

He does not correct me. He just nods, and writes it in his little book, because that is what he does with things that frighten him: he puts them in ledgers so they cannot sneak up on anyone.

The amphitheater opens beneath us like a throat.

We come down through a crack in the mountain's shoulder, Orla first with her lantern held high, then me, then Gideon with his hand on my back the whole way, steady as a wall. The steps are older than anything I have ever touched. They are not carved so much as *persuaded* out of the rock, each one worn into a shallow bowl by feet that have been dead for longer than there have been kingdoms to count their deaths. Water runs somewhere below us in the dark, and the sound it makes is almost like breathing.

Almost.

That is the thing about this place. Everything down here is *almost* something else. The dark is almost sleep. The silence is almost a held breath. And the cold that rises up from the center of the floor, where the stones curve inward toward a ring of black glass set into the earth — that cold is almost a heartbeat.

"Charming venue," Orla says, but her voice comes out smaller than she means it to. Even her jokes go quiet here, like they have to tiptoe. "Terrible acoustics for anything you'd want kept secret."

"That's rather the point," Gideon murmurs. "A binding isn't a lock. It's a sentence. And sentences need to be heard."

I know what he means better than he knows I know it. Because I am made of sentences. Or I was. Once, before the guild took its inventory of me, before Prince Ilvane's agents tore my first word out of my chest and burned a fortress with it, I was whole. I was a language walking around in a girl's body, and every word I spoke did exactly what it said, and nothing I said was ever wasted.

Now I hoard what's left like a beggar hoards crumbs, and I hate everyone who made me do it.

We reach the floor of the amphitheater. The black glass ring hums when my bare feet touch the stone near it — not a sound in my ears, but a sound in my *teeth*, in the marrow of me, a recognition. It knows what I am. It has been waiting for someone who speaks its grammar.

*Good,* says the ring, or the mountain, or the sleeping thing underneath both. *You came back.*

I never came here before in my life. But languages don't keep track of bodies. Languages keep track of themselves.

* * *

They come at dusk, which is a courtesy, or an insult, depending on how you look at it. Torches first, hundreds of them, ringing the upper tiers of the amphitheater like a crown of orange teeth. Then the soldiers, rank on rank, filling the seats where audiences once sat to watch whatever people watched in the old world — games, or trials, or gods.

And last, down the same crack we came through, walking slowly, letting everyone see him: Prince Ilvane.

He is younger than I expected. That is the worst part. In my head I had built him out of everything that had been done to me — the agents with their iron collars and their word-snares, the burning fortress, the hollow feeling after — and so I had imagined someone enormous and twisted, a villain shaped like his own crimes. Instead he is a narrow young man with careful hands and tired eyes, dressed in travel-worn white, and he looks at me across the amphitheater floor the way Gideon looks at a ledger entry he cannot make balance.

"There she is," he says softly. Not to me. To his own people, like a merchant confirming a delivery. Then, to me, directly, and his voice is kind, which is somehow worse than cruelty: "You must hate me."

"I don't know you," I say. Which is true. I hate what he has done, but hating a person requires knowing one, and everything I know about Ilvane fits in a single sentence: *he collects.*

"I know." He walks closer. The soldiers part for him. Gideon steps in front of me, and Orla's hand goes under her coat, and Ilvane stops, and smiles a small sad smile, and raises both empty hands. "I'm not going to take anything from her today. I couldn't if I wanted to. What's left in her is hers — the binding won't answer to stolen fragments alone. It needs a speaker. A living voice that consents, or at least a voice present enough to be *used*." He tilts his head. "You've felt it, haven't you? Down here. The way the stone knows your name."

"The stone doesn't know my name," I say.

"No," he agrees. "But it knows your *language*. And that, child, is the entire problem with my kingdom." He begins to walk the rim of the black glass ring, slow, lecturing, a prince giving history lessons at sword-point. "My throne sits on a prison. Every harvest, every road, every law — all of it built on the assumption that the thing beneath this floor stays asleep. For nine hundred years it has stayed asleep because a sentence was spoken over it, a sentence so complete that no one alive could finish it or unmake it. Except—" he spreads his hands toward me, gracious as a host, "—the sentence survived. In her. In her people. Word by word, generation by generation, hidden in children's mouths like seeds."

"They weren't seeds," I say. "They were *us*. Each word was a person's whole inheritance. You took them from villages. From families."

"I took them from *history*," Ilvane says, and for one moment his kindness cracks and something colder shows through, something that has been awake much longer than he has. "Do you know what happens when the binding fails, girl? Not if — when. My grandfather saw the edges of it. The two empires that built this place didn't fall. They were *unmade*. Erased so thoroughly that we don't even know their names, because the thing beneath us ate their names first. I will not rule a graveyard waiting to be dug up. If the power wakes anyway, then it wakes holding *my* leash, not no one's."

"You want to put a collar on a god," Orla calls down from the tier above, where she has been quietly positioning herself near the exit with the practical instincts of a woman who has survived many bad rooms. "Sweetheart, I've smuggled a lot of dangerous cargo, and the one rule that keeps you alive is: you don't put a collar on anything bigger than the boat."

"It's not a god," Ilvane says. "Gods can be prayed to. This is older than prayer. And it is going to wake regardless — she nearly finished a sentence in a monastery full of mute monks and the mountain itself flinched. Time is not on our side. Only control is." He turns back to me, and here is the terrible thing, the thing I will think about for the rest of my life however long that turns out to be: he kneels. A prince of the realm, kneeling on the stones of the Mouth of the World, looking up at a mud-born girl with seven words left, maybe five. "Help me say it properly. Help me bind it *with* you instead of *through* you. No more tearing. No more theft. I swear it on my crown."

Behind me, very quietly, Gideon says: "Don't."

Not to Ilvane. To me.

"He's lying," Gideon says. "Not about the danger — about the *swear*. I've kept ledgers for men like him my whole life. The debt always gets called differently than it was written."

Ilvane's eyes flick to Gideon, and there is recognition there, and something almost like regret. "The ledger-keeper. Corvane's lost boy. You should know that your masters are coming down that tunnel behind my army even now. Whatever you think you're choosing, you're standing between two buyers."

"I know," Gideon says. "I burned the ledger."

Something moves across Ilvane's face at that — surprise, and then, horribly, respect. "Then you're the only honest man here," he says. "Which is why I'm offering her the choice at all."

And that is when the torches gutter.

All of them. At once. Along the whole crown of the amphitheater, every flame bends sideways, toward the center, toward the black glass ring, as if the air itself has begun to inhale.

* * *

Here is what nobody understood — not Ilvane with his careful plans, not the guild with its vaults, not even the monks who spent their lives silent to keep from saying the wrong thing:

The binding was never waiting to be completed.

It was waiting to be *finished being heard*.

A sentence spoken over a prisoner is a door closed. But a sentence is also a shape, and shapes have edges, and for nine hundred years the thing beneath the glass has been running its tongue along those edges in the dark, learning the exact contour of its cage. It does not need new words. It needs only for the old ones to gather close enough together to make a gap — and every fragment torn from me, every word ripped out and carried away in iron collars and guild crates and princely reliquaries, has been moving toward this place like iron filings toward a magnet, whether their owners intended it or not.

I feel them arrive.

They come up through the soles of my feet. Words I spoke in another life, in another body's memory — the word that heals, the word that drowns, the word that was stolen while I screamed in a guild wagon. They rise through the stone and hang in the air around the black glass ring, visible now, each one a small burning letterform in a script older than kingdoms, drifting like embers that have forgotten which fire they belonged to.

Ilvane stands very still, watching his life's work assemble itself without his permission, and I watch him understand — really understand, perhaps for the first time — that he was never the collector. He was the courier.

"No," he says. It is the first honest thing he has said all day. "No, this is not the order of it—"

The words begin to arrange themselves.

I feel it happen inside my own mouth, though my lips stay shut. The fragments seek each other the way water seeks level, clicking together along seams I can feel in my teeth, in my bones, in the hollow places where my stolen inheritance used to live. And as they join, the amphitheater *wakes*. The worn bowls of the seats fill with shadow that is not shadow. The water below stops sounding like breathing and starts sounding like lungs.

Gideon grabs my arm. "Ada. Ada, look at me. How many words do you have left?"

I hold up my fingers. Five. I am almost sure it is five.

"Keep them," he says. "Whatever happens, whatever you hear calling you — keep them. Don't speak. Don't finish anything. Promise me."

"Promises are sentences," I say, and my voice comes out strange, doubled, as if something under the floor is learning to use my throat for practice.

The last fragments click home.

And the binding frays.

I wish I could describe it as a sound, but it is the opposite of sound — a great unraveling silence, like the world holding its breath before a sneeze, and in that silence the black glass ring goes white, then transparent, then simply *gone*, and what is beneath it is not a pit and not a creature but a *presence*, vast and patient and awake, rising through the floor of the world like dawn rising through a window.

It wears shapes.

That is the worst of it. It does not come as a monster. It comes as *memory* — two vast silences taking form above the amphitheater, taller than the tiers, taller than the mountain, and each one is shaped like a people. I see banners that no one alive remembers, streets that exist nowhere, faces in the thousands pressed into the silhouette of something that was once two empires, whole and proud and loud, before it said the wrong word to the wrong listener and was eaten down to its architecture. The shapes are beautiful. The shapes are grieving. The shapes are *hungry*, because they have been asleep for nine hundred years and the first thing they remember is that they were not always this.

The soldiers break. Of course they break. Nine hundred years of throne-room certainty evaporates in the time it takes a man to drop his torch and run. Ilvane does not run. He stands in the wash of that vast attention, white-clad and small, shouting orders in a voice that cracks, shouting *commands* at it, because he genuinely believes, even now, that the world is a ledger and everything in it can be owned.

The presence turns its attention on him the way you might turn your attention on a fly.

I feel what it intends, because I am the only thing on this floor that speaks its grammar. It is not angry. Anger would be simpler. It is *curious* — the curiosity of something deciding whether the world above it is worth tasting again, or worth erasing again, or perhaps merely worth rearranging, the way a sleeper pulls a blanket.

And then it notices me.

The whole weight of it swings around, two dead empires' worth of attention focusing down on one small girl standing on the stones with five words left, maybe three, and Gideon's hand tight around my arm, and I hear it speak — not in sound, but directly into the grammar of me, into the hollow places, the way you'd speak into a keyhole:

*Little fragment. Little leftover. You are the last of the sentence. Come and be whole.*

And oh, it is tempting. That is the trap of it, the thing I will never be able to explain to anyone who wasn't standing on this floor. It is offering to give me back everything that was taken — every stolen word, every hollow place filled, my language entire, my self entire, the girl I was before the guild opened her up like a crate. All I have to do is walk into the ring and let the last of me click into place and become the final syllable of a sentence that ends the world.

Beside me, Gideon lets go of my arm.

I turn, and he is stepping forward, past me, toward the ring and the presence and the prince shouting his useless commands, and he is rolling up his sleeve like a man preparing to write, and he says — in a perfectly ordinary voice, the voice of a ledger-keeper about to correct an entry:

"I have a better offer."

* * *

What he does next belongs to him, not to me, and I will not steal it by telling it wrong. But I will say this much, because I was the only one close enough to hear, and someone has to carry it:

He did not shout. He did not plead. He spoke quietly, in the tone he uses for balancing accounts, and the words he said were not in any language the monks preserved or the guild catalogued or the princes bought.

They were his name.

And the whole amphitheater went still to listen.
