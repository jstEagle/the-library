## 22. A New Ledger

The trouble with teaching children words is that they ask what the words mean, and then they ask why, and then they ask *but why*, and somewhere around the fourth *why* you discover that you never actually knew either — you only ever borrowed the meaning from someone who loved you enough to explain it badly.

I run a school now. That sentence still surprises me every morning when I say it to myself over the porridge pot. *Ada runs a school.* The girl who arrived at the Vellum Guild caked in mud and older than kingdoms, catalogued between a bottled storm and a jar of somebody's regrets, now spends her days teaching eleven stray children their letters in a converted smuggler's boathouse at the edge of Veil's Edge.

Orla says I should stop being surprised. Orla says a lot of things before breakfast.

"Girl," she said this morning, arms crossed on the doorframe, salt wind coming off the harbor behind her like an uninvited student, "you've burned the porridge again."

"I was thinking."

"You're always thinking. That's your whole problem. A person can think herself right out of living if she isn't careful." She took the pot off my fire without asking, because Orla has never once asked permission for anything in her life, and scraped it with the particular violence of a woman who has decided the world is salvageable but only barely. "Feed them first. Teach them after. Hungry children learn nothing but hunger."

This is the other thing about Orla: beneath the smuggling, the profanity, and the frankly criminal collection of knives she keeps about her person, there is a woman who once followed two fugitives out of pure stubbornness and never quite managed to leave. She'll deny it. She denies it beautifully, at length, usually while mending the school roof or threatening a merchant who shorted us on chalk.

I fed the children. Then I taught them.

* * *

Here is how our mornings go: we sit in a circle on the floorboards that still smell faintly of tar, and I do not teach them the way the guild taught me — no, wait. The guild never taught me anything. The guild tried to *inventory* me. Let me start again, because starting again is something I've gotten good at.

We sit in a circle, and I teach them the way Gideon would have taught: patiently, with jokes, writing each word twice so it can't be lost.

They are strays, all of them. A fisherman's orphan named Pell who bites first and apologizes later. Twin sisters fished out of the flood channels two winters ago, who spoke nothing at all for a month and now won't stop talking, which I consider the greater miracle. A boy called Mouse who came to us already able to read, having learned from tavern signs, and who corrects my handwriting with the tactlessness only the very young and the very clever can manage.

And there is Wren, who is newest, and who reminds me of me in ways that make my chest hurt. She came speaking a language nobody recognized — not old, exactly, just *hers*, a private grammar she'd built alone in whatever wilderness made her. The townsfolk wanted her assessed. Assessed. I know that word. I know what lives underneath it, wearing its coat.

So I sat with Wren for three days and did the thing no one did for me until it was nearly too late: I listened. And on the third day she said a word — a small one, a word like a pebble turned over in sunlight — and the candle flame bent toward her, just slightly, just curiously. And I didn't flinch, and I didn't reach for a ledger, and I didn't send for anyone.

I said, "That's a good one. Keep it close. And here's a trick: words you give away freely come back carrying gifts."

She looked at me the way I must have looked at Gideon, that first night by the storm-bottles, when he asked my name instead of my classification.

"What's your name?" she asked me now, in trade-language, carefully, the way you handle something breakable.

"Ada," I said. "Just Ada. Names are the most expensive things we own, so spend them wisely — but never hoard them. Hoarded names go stale."

Pell raised his hand, which he does even though we don't do hands in this school. "Teacher Ada. What happened to the man in your stories? The ledger man?"

Ah. Yes. The stories.

Every child here knows the stories. The man who kept ledgers of impossible things until he understood that some things should never be counted. The man who gave his name away to save a girl, and whose name became a lock, and whose lock became a lullaby. I tell the stories plainly, without tragedy, the way you tell a child about weather. They know how it ends. They know the amphitheater is buried under a hill of stones I placed myself, one by one, over four years, until my hands bled and Orla threatened to tie me up and do it for me.

They know the last line best, because I end every telling with it:

"And the power sleeps not in chains, but in a remembered name — and remembering is a kind of love, and love does not need to be woken."

"But what *was* his name?" Pell asks, every time. He asks every time the way children ask things they already know the answer to, because the asking itself is the comfort.

"Gideon Almeida," I say. "It's written down. Would you like to see?"

* * *

After lessons, when the children scatter to the tide-pools and their various small crimes, I keep the ledger.

One ledger. Only ever one. It sits in a box Orla liberated from a guild courier years ago — irony noted, appreciated, filed away — and inside it there is a single list, written in my careful hand, the hand Gideon taught me when my fingers were still wild things.

**Names Given Away.**

Not stolen. Not sold. Not extracted, assessed, or acquired. *Given.* Because that distinction is the whole of everything I have learned since the amphitheater, and I will write it as many times as it takes.

The first entry, in ink gone brown with age:

*Gideon Almeida — given freely, at the Mouth of the World, to bind what could not be broken. Remembered daily. Owed everything.*

Below it, others, added across the years. A hedge-witch's name I found in her final letter and spoke aloud at her grave so it wouldn't be lost — *Maren Ostwick, given back to the ground that grew her.* The names of the two dead empires, which I recite once a year on the anniversary, quietly, into the sea, because someone should remember that they were people before they were warnings. Even, after much argument with myself, a single line for Master Corvane: *Corvane of the Vellum Guild — given to his own ambition; may he rest unremembered by me and remembered accurately by history.*

Orla thinks the ledger is morbid. "You keep a book of dead men's names, girl, and call it hope."

"It's not a book of the dead," I told her once. "It's a book of debts. And debts mean someone is owed something, and owing means the story isn't finished."

She snorted. But she also built the box a shelf of its own, high above the waterline, so I choose to believe she understands more than she admits. Orla always understands more than she admits. It's practically her defining feature, right after the knives.

Some nights I take the ledger down and read the first entry aloud, just to myself, just to keep the remembering warm. The monks at the Sanctum taught me — before they expelled us, before the mountain groaned, before everything — that a binding held by memory needs tending like a fire. So I tend it. I say his name into the dark of the boathouse, and far away under a hill of patient stones, something vast and terrible and lonely turns over in its sleep, murmurs a syllable it learned from me, and settles.

That is the arrangement. That is the peace. Not chains — a conversation, ongoing, between a sleeping god and the girl who guards its nap.

Mostly it works.

* * *

Today is the anniversary. Nine years, though I've stopped counting precisely, because Gideon would have mocked me for keeping time like inventory.

The children are with Orla at the market, buying chalk and causing distractions, and I have the boathouse to myself. Rain on the roof. The ledger open on my knees. I read the entries. I add today's date beside the first name, small, in the margin, the way you mark a grave you intend to visit again.

Then I turn to the last page, because I always turn to the last page, and because the last page is where I keep the space — one blank ruled line, waiting, that I have never filled and never explained to anyone. Orla asked once what it was for. I said, "In case." She didn't ask again. Orla knows better than anyone that some blanks are prayers.

The rain thickens. The door bangs open on a gust, and I shout "We're closed, Pell, whatever you broke can wait—" but it isn't Pell.

It's Mouse, soaked through, breathless, holding something above his head like a rescued bird.

"Found it," he gasps. "Tide-pools. Under the wreck-stones. Teacher Ada, it's — it's a book, it got wet, I'm sorry, I dried it as fast as—"

He puts it in my hands.

It is not a book. It is a ledger. Guild-ruled, guild-bound, water-swollen, stamped with the faded seal of the Vellum Guild — and my heart goes cold and old and nine years younger, because I know that stamp, I know that paper, I know the weight of a thing meant to count the uncountable.

But it isn't the master ledger. I burned that. This is smaller. Personal. A working copy, lost or hidden or thrown away, carried by tides and years to a wreck-stone at the edge of a harbor town at the exact edge of the world where a girl might be keeping her own accounts.

My hands shake. I open it.

Entries in a clerk's precise hand — storms, relics, prices paid in favors — and then, near the end, in the same hand but slower, heavier, pressed deep into the page like the writer needed the words to hold:

*If found, return to G. Almeida. Or don't. Some inventories are better closed.*

And below that, on the final leaf, fresh ink — impossibly, undeniably fresh, the rain not yet dry on the cover while the letters gleam black and new — in handwriting I would know anywhere, because I taught myself to write by copying it, because I dreamed in it for years, because a name doesn't leave the world just because it's been given away:

*Entry the last: returned in full, with interest. One (1) name, well kept. One (1) girl, well grown. Requesting audit.*

*I'm outside.*

The ledger slips from my hands. Mouse catches it — quick boy, bless him — and I am already moving, past him, through the door, into the rain, down the slick boards toward the tide-line where the fog stands open like a held breath.

A figure waits at the water's edge. Thin. Rain-soaked. Turning.

And I stop, because I have rehearsed this moment in dreams for nine years and every rehearsal falls apart now, and all I can manage, stupidly, wonderfully, is the first lesson I ever learned and the last one I ever taught:

"Say your name," I call across the water. "Slowly. Twice. So it can't be lost."

The figure smiles — and begins.
