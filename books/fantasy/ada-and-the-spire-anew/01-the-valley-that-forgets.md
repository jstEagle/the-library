## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The map was finished at the hour when the valley's lamps went out one by one, and Xanthe Almeida sat alone in his attic room with ink on his fingers and a realm he had never seen spread beneath his hands.

It had taken him four years. Four years of travelers' tales gathered like fallen fruit, of merchants' gossip cross-checked against a drover's drunken certainty, of a pilgrim's half-remembered coastline corrected by the testimony of a woman who claimed to have once sold salted eels in a port whose name she'd forgotten. He had weighed every account against every other, discarding the impossible, keeping the improbable, and what remained — after all that sifting and stitching and redrawing — was Vael's Realm entire: the Ashen Mountains crawling along the north like a sleeping serpent, the river Veil unspooling south through farmland rendered in careful green, the drowned city of Osterhale marked with a small, honest question mark where the water swallowed the streets.

He had never seen any of it. No one in Vael's Hollow had, not truly. The valley kept them, and they kept to the valley, and beyond its rim the world went on without them the way weather goes on over the sea.

Xanthe blew gently across the finished map to dry the last of the ink, and the lamplight made the mountains ripple as though they breathed.

"There," he said softly, to no one. "That's all of it."

All of it except the Spire. He looked at the blank space in the northeast where every traveler's tale grew strange and contradictory — where distances refused to hold still, where roads bent, where one merchant swore there was nothing but moorland and another swore you could see it from three days' walk, a needle of black stone piercing the clouds. He had left it empty on purpose. A cartographer who filled gaps with guesses was a liar with good penmanship; his grandmother Maren had taught him that before he could write. But the emptiness bothered him tonight more than usual, sitting there like a held breath, like an eye closed that ought to be open.

He capped his inkwell. Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear the elders gathering in the meetinghouse, their voices a low murmur that rose and fell like wind in wheat. He knew without listening closely what they were saying. They were deciding what to burn this month.

* * *

The burning happened on the last night of every season, in the square outside the meetinghouse, and everyone attended because not attending was itself a kind of confession.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the crowd with his hood up, watching Elder Coram read aloud from a list. Coram was a narrow man with a voice like a hinge, and he pronounced each item with the gravity of a judge sentencing a criminal: *one charm of knotted thread, taken from the Halloway girl; three books of verse from the Tarrow household, containing references to the old names; a dried sprig of rowan, hung above a door for luck.*

Luck. Xanthe felt the word sit sourly in his chest. In Vael's Hollow even luck was contraband, because luck implied forces, and forces implied gods, and gods — the elders said, had always said, would always say — were the reason the valley existed at all. The old gods could not reach here. Magic could not follow. Whatever bargain or binding had carved the Hollow out of the world's wild flesh generations ago, it held, and the price of its holding was vigilance: nothing that smelled of the otherworld was permitted to take root. Not charms. Not prayers. Not stories told too vividly by travelers who lingered too long.

The fire took the offerings quickly. It always did; the elders soaked them in lamp oil first, so that nothing would have time to resist. Xanthe watched the Halloway girl — she was perhaps ten, and her mother's hand gripped her shoulder hard enough to whiten — watch her knotted threads curl and blacken. Her face didn't cry. It did something worse. It went carefully, deliberately blank, the way faces in the valley learned to go, smoothing themselves down like grass under a falling stone.

*This is safety,* Xanthe reminded himself, as he reminded himself every season. *This is the shape safety takes.*

But the reminder fit less well each year, like a coat cut for a smaller boy.

Afterward, walking home through lanes that smelled of woodsmoke, he passed Maren's cottage and found her on her step shelling beans into a bowl, though the light was nearly gone and she must have been working mostly by memory.

"You stayed till the end," she said, without looking up.

"I always do."

"Mm." She tipped a bean into the bowl. "Your grandfather used to stand at the back with his arms crossed, like a man auditing the accounts. You stand at the front with your hood down. Like a mourner."

"Someone should witness it."

"Witnessing isn't the same as approving." She finally looked at him, and her eyes in the dusk were pale and sharp as frost on a window. "Careful which one your face does, Xanthe. Faces are how the elders read us."

"They don't read mine."

"No," Maren agreed, returning to her beans. "Yours is the one face they never think to check. The Almeida boy who draws maps of places he'll never go. Harmless as rain." Something flickered across her expression then, gone before he could name it — something almost like sorrow. "Goodnight, love. Mind the dark on the lane."

There were no dangers in the dark on the lane. There had never been any dangers anywhere in Vael's Hollow. That was the whole point of it. And yet he walked home faster than he needed to, and he could not have said why.

* * *

The stranger came with the first cold rain of autumn, stumbling up the valley road at dusk with a wound in her side and her blood diluting pink in the runoff.

Xanthe found her because he was out mapping the flood channels — a task no one else wanted and he secretly loved, since it meant walking the valley's edges alone with his satchel and his measuring cord. He heard her before he saw her: a wet, ragged sound of breathing, wrong for a person, right for a person trying very hard not to die loudly. She had collapsed against the boundary stones near the western pass, the ones worn smooth and nameless, and when he turned her over he saw she was dressed in travel-stained grey with a symbol stitched at the collar that he did not recognize and recognized instantly anyway, the way you know a word you've never been taught.

A priestess. Or she had been, wherever she came from.

Her eyes found his and fixed there with terrible effort. "Cold," she whispered. "So cold. Please—"

He should have called for the elders. That was the law of the valley, plain as the boundary stones: outsiders were brought to the meetinghouse, questioned, and sent on their way within a day, their stories confiscated like contraband charms. An outsider bleeding god-touched symbols onto valley soil was not a mercy case. She was a contamination.

Xanthe knelt in the freezing mud beside her and did none of those things.

Because she was dying — that was the thought his body understood before his mind caught up, the animal fact of it, the shallowing breath and the skin going grey around the mouth — and because some part of him that four years of maps had quietly fed, some part that had spent its whole life loving a world it could only reconstruct from other people's mouths, looked at this woman who had *come from* that world carrying it in her wounds, and could not bear for the world to lose her here, in the one place that forgot everything.

"It's all right," he heard himself say. "I have you."

And then — because the words were already moving in him, because his grandmother said grace at table in a whisper so old and worn it had lost its edges, because grief and panic will make a prayer of anyone — he bowed his head over her and said:

*Please. Let her live. Whoever listens. Please.*

He had never prayed before. Not properly, not meaning it. In the valley you did not pray; prayer was a door, and doors let things through.

Something answered.

There was no thunder, no light. What happened was smaller and vastly stranger: the rain stopped. Not slackened — *stopped*, mid-fall, every drop hanging in the air around them like beads of glass strung on invisible thread, and in the sudden hush Xanthe felt warmth pour into the priestess beneath his hands, felt it pass *through* him as though he were a channel, a riverbed for something that had been waiting a long time for anyone to ask. Color came back into her face. Her breath deepened. The wound in her side, when he dared look, had closed to a thin white seam.

Then the rain fell again, all at once, ordinary and cold, and the world resumed.

Xanthe stayed kneeling in the mud, his heart slamming, his mind scrabbling for purchase on what had just happened. Prayers weren't answered. That was the foundation the whole valley stood on — the gods couldn't reach here, magic couldn't follow, the wards held, the elders burned the charms and the valley slept safe. Every story, every lesson, every careful blank face agreed on it.

And yet.

Far off, at the rim of the valley where the mountains shouldered up against the sky, Xanthe saw — or thought he saw, or his whole life afterward insisted he had seen — the air shudder. A tremor ran along the boundary like a shiver through a struck bell, silent and vast, and for one instant the peaks seemed to blur, as if seen through heat-haze, as if the valley were a painting someone had bumped.

Then it stilled. The rain fell. Somewhere behind him, the priestess groaned and stirred, alive, impossibly alive.

Xanthe looked down at his own hands, muddy and shaking, and understood with a quiet, creeping dread that settled into his bones like the cold did: the walls of his whole world had just moved. Not fallen — moved. The way a house settles. The way a thing shifts that has stood a long time and is beginning, at last, to be tired.

He got his shoulder under the stranger's arm and lifted. She was lighter than she should have been, fever-hot despite the healed wound, and as he half-carried her toward the treeline and the hidden track up to his attic, she turned her head against his collar and murmured, half-conscious, words that slid into him like a splinter:

"The Spire... you have to... the king is still in there..."

Xanthe walked faster, eyes darting to the empty road, the grey rain, the unmoved mountains.

No one had seen. No one had heard. The valley slept on, forgetting as it always forgot.

But his hands, he noticed, had stopped shaking — and in his chest, where dread lived, something else had begun, very quietly, to wake.
