## 2. What the Stranger Carried

The stranger slept for a day and a night in Xanthe's loft, and in all that time the valley did not forget her.

That was the first wrongness. Vael's Hollow forgot things — that was its nature, its mercy, its design. A peddler's face would blur by the second bridge. A song from outside would lose its words before it reached the third house. The elders said it was the wards, smoothing the world flat so nothing sharp could catch on the valley's soft edges. But this woman's face stayed. The gash across her brow stayed. The smell of woodsmoke and something older, something like lightning about to happen, stayed in the rafters of his loft until Xanthe found himself breathing it in deliberately, the way a starving man breathes over bread.

He had moved her there because he had panicked. That was the honest shape of it. When her prayer had been answered — when the fever-light had gone out of her eyes at his clumsy muttering, *please, let her live, please* — the wards had shuddered like a struck bell, and every dog in the Hollow had begun to howl, and Xanthe had understood with animal certainty that if anyone else heard what he had heard, she would be dead by morning. So he had dragged her through his mother's old herb garden, past the drying racks where Maren's lavender hung, up the ladder to the loft where he kept his maps, and he had hidden her among the countries that did not exist.

She lay now beneath the great table where he worked, wrapped in the drop-cloth he used for ink spills, breathing slow and even. Around her, pinned and weighted and curling at the edges, lay the realm as Xanthe Almeida imagined it: the Thornmark forests rendered from a drover's drunken description; the coast drawn from a sailor who had wept describing water he would never see again; the Spire itself, far to the north and east, drawn three times and scraped away twice because no tale agreed on its height.

A map of a world he had never touched. His whole life, folded into paper.

"You're staring," the stranger said.

Xanthe startled so badly he knocked a weight off the table. "You're awake."

"I've been awake since dawn." She sat up slowly, one hand pressed to her ribs, and looked around the loft with an expression he couldn't read — not wonder, not contempt. Recognition, maybe. Grief. "Maps," she said. "Of course. You're the one who answered me."

"I didn't answer you. I don't even know what I—"

"You prayed." Her eyes were dark and very steady. "Badly. Out of rhythm, no liturgy, half of it apology. And it was answered anyway." She said it not as accusation but as fact, the way one might say *the river flooded*. "Do you know how long it's been since a prayer was answered in this valley? Do you know how long it's been since a prayer was answered *anywhere*?"

"Prayers aren't answered anywhere," Xanthe said. "That's the whole point of the valley. That's what everyone says."

"Yes." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tired and knowing. "Everyone says. Everyone says a great many things here."

She introduced herself then — Sable, priestess of an order whose name she gave and which meant nothing to him, though later he would learn it should have meant everything. She let him help her sit against the wall, and she ate the bread and honey he brought down from the shelf with the focused gratitude of someone who had gone hungry on purpose before and knew exactly how much food cost.

Then she asked to see his maps, and he showed her, and that was when the lying began — or rather, that was when he understood he had already been lying, and would have to keep doing it.

Because she asked him questions. What road led south out of the valley, and he told her the Shepherd's Stair, and she asked who maintained it, and he said the elders' wardens, and she asked whether the wardens could be bribed or deceived, and he heard himself say *no one comes looking up here, no one would know*, when the truth was that Elder Corvus walked the perimeter every third day and that Xanthe himself had been raised to report anything that smelled of magic to the pyre-wardens before it smelled of it to him.

The pyres were the other thing the valley did instead of remembering. Anything that carried the old power — charms, relics, books with prayers in them, sometimes people — went to the burning ground behind the meetinghouse. Xanthe had watched a woman's grandmother's ring go into the flames when he was nine. He had thought, even then, that the fire looked less like cleansing and more like fear.

"The gods are waking," Sable said, when the bread was gone. She said it plainly, the way you'd tell a man the levee had broken. "All of them, or enough of them. They starved for centuries — worship cut off, names unspoken, their altars pulled down or warded over. And now something has changed. Prayers are being answered again, out there." She nodded toward the walls, toward the world. "Every prayer. Instantly. Perfectly."

"That sounds like a blessing."

"It is a mouth." Her hand went to her collar, and drew out something on a cord — and Xanthe forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

It was a shard of what looked like living coal, no bigger than his thumbnail, and it glowed with a light that was not firelight, not sunlight, but something deeper and redder, like the memory of a furnace. It pulsed. Slowly. Like a heart deciding whether to keep going.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Dragon-fire. The last of it." She closed her fingers around it gently, protectively. "There was a dragon, once, who held the hunger back. Who stood between the waking god and everything it wanted to eat. She is dying now, and this—" the shard turned in her palm, and Xanthe saw, with a lurch, that its glow was fainter than it had been the night before, fainter than it had been an hour ago, "—dims a little every day. When it goes out entirely, there will be nothing between us and the thing that grants wishes."

"And what does it want?" Xanthe asked, though some part of him already knew, some part that had spent his life drawing borders and had always wondered why every border needed defending.

"To give people what they pray for," Sable said. "And to take what they are. Every miracle hollows the one who receives it. You've seen the crops, maybe, in travelers' tales — grain tall as a man, orchards that fruit in winter. And the villages that grow them? Empty-eyed. Smiling. Praying. The god doesn't want worship the way the old stories say. It wants the *worshipper*. It eats devotion until nothing is left but the appetite, and then it moves on."

The loft felt smaller. Outside, the afternoon light came slantwise through the shutters, ordinary and golden, and somewhere below Maren would be grinding herbs, and the wardens would be walking the perimeter, and the valley would be forgetting, forgetting, forgetting — and Xanthe understood suddenly that the forgetting was not peace. It was anesthesia. It was borrowed time, bought by his ancestors at a price no one alive remembered, and the bill was coming due.

"The valley won't hold," he said. Not a question.

"Not forever. Your wards are old work — older than your elders know, I think. Something is bound into this valley, something that keeps the gods out. But bindings fray. And when they fray—" she looked at him with those steady dark eyes, "—the god will not need to break in. It will only need to be invited. One answered prayer, Xanthe. Like yours last night. One crack, and it pours through."

"I didn't invite anything. I just—"

"You just meant it." She said it softly. "That's all it ever needs. Someone meaning it."

He sat down heavily on his stool. Below, faintly, he heard the front door, Maren's voice calling his name — *Xanthe? Are you up there? Warden Tamsin is asking after you, she says strangers were seen on the south road two nights past—*

His blood went cold.

"Under the table," he breathed. "Now. Please."

Sable moved without argument, folding herself into the shadow beneath the map-table, the drop-cloth drawn over her, the shard's dim glow cupped in her fist. Xanthe took the ladder down two rungs at a time and opened the door with a smile he did not feel.

Maren stood on the step, flour to her elbows, worry carved deep around her mouth. Behind her, at the gate, Warden Tamsin waited with her staff and her patient, terrible diligence.

"There you are," Maren said. "Tamsin says a traveler came through the south ford. Hurt. Did you see anything? Anyone?"

And Xanthe Almeida, who had never in his nineteen years told a lie larger than *I finished my chores*, heard himself say:

"No. Nothing. No one's come through here."

The words came out smooth and easy, and that frightened him most of all — how easily the valley's silence had taught him to speak.

Maren searched his face a moment longer than she needed to. Then she nodded, squeezed his shoulder, and went back down the path toward Tamsin, and the two women spoke in low voices at the gate, and did not look up at his shuttered windows.

When he climbed back to the loft, Sable was watching him from under the table's edge, the dragon-fire glowing faintly between her fingers like a secret being kept.

"You lied for me," she said.

"I lied for the valley," Xanthe said, and did not know yet which of them he was protecting — only that the shard in her palm had dimmed again while he watched, and that somewhere far to the north and east, taller than any of his maps dared draw it, the Spire was waiting.
