## 3. Elders and Embers

Sable woke to the smell of bread and the sound of someone trying very hard not to be heard.

She kept her eyes closed and catalogued what she knew. Attic room, low rafters, dust sweetened with dried lavender — a valley trick, she'd learned, hung in bundles over every door because the elders said it kept out ill luck. It did nothing of the kind. What it actually did was mask the scent of magic, which was why Xanthe had stuffed three of them under the door of this room before hiding her here. The boy was cleverer than he let himself believe.

The floorboard creaked again, closer.

Her hand found the shard beneath her shirt without her deciding to move. Dragon-fire, no bigger than a knucklebone, warm as a sleeping bird against her sternum. It had dimmed again in the night. She could feel the difference the way a sailor feels a wind dying — not gone, but going, and every hour it guttered the world outside grew louder with answered prayers.

The door opened.

Sable sat up in one motion, shard already in her palm, its faint ember-glow cupped behind her fingers like a stolen coal.

A woman stood in the doorway. Old, but old the way river stone is old — worn smooth rather than crumbled. Silver hair braided tight to her skull. She held a lamp in one hand and a loaf of bread in the other, and she looked at Sable the way Sable imagined a cartographer looked at an unmapped coast: measuring, unafraid, already drawing lines.

"You're awake," the woman said. "Good. I've been waiting two days for you to be awake."

"Then you know," Sable said. "About me."

"I know my grandson has been carrying food up a ladder he told me was broken." The woman set the loaf on the table by the bed. "I know the lavender under your door is fresh-cut, and fresh-cut means someone wanted to hide a smell, and there's only one smell in this valley worth hiding. And I know that thing you're holding behind your hand is the first true fire I've seen since I was younger than you are now."

Sable didn't lower her hand. "You're Maren."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — not surprise. Recognition, maybe, or grief wearing recognition's clothes. "Xanthe talks too much for a boy raised on silence."

"He talks about you the way people talk about lighthouses."

"And yet he hid you from me." Maren pulled the room's single stool closer and sat, joints cracking like green wood in a hearth. "Which tells me either he doesn't trust me, or he's protecting me. With Almeidas it's always the second one, and it's always worse."

Outside, far below, the morning bell rang. Not the chapel bell — the *other* one, the flat iron tone Sable had heard twice since arriving and never once been able to ignore. The burning bell.

Maren closed her eyes. "They've found something else."

* * *

From the attic window Sable watched the square fill.

The elders came first, seven of them in their grey wool, walking in the deliberate unhurried way of people who have decided something terrible and want everyone to see how calmly they decided it. Behind them, four young men carried a handcart, and on the handcart was a pile of things that made Sable's chest tighten: a child's wooden horse with painted eyes. A quilt stitched in patterns that were almost certainly sigils, though the maker had probably only thought them pretty. Books. So many books, their spines cracked, their pages furred with age.

And at the top of the pile, a map. An old one, ink browned to sepia, showing coastline shapes Sable knew by heart and knew to be wrong — wrong the way all maps of the outer world were wrong now, because the god's waking had bent rivers and grown forests where farms had been.

"They burn memory," she said quietly. "That's what this is. Not magic. Memory."

"Mm." Maren stood beside her at the window, arms folded. "My husband drew that map. Forty years ago, from travelers' tales, same as Xanthe does now. They let him keep it while he lived. They burned it the week after his funeral." Her voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse than if it had. "The valley forgets, priestess. That's the whole design. We forget the world, the world forgets us, and the thing in the Spire starves a little more each generation because hunger needs to be *fed attention*, and we give it none."

"You know what I carry."

"I know what it is. I don't know why you have it, and I suspect that story ends with my grandson standing somewhere he shouldn't be." Maren turned from the window. Below, the pyre was being built in the square's center, the old way — stacked careful, kindling dry, the elders' faces turned up toward the valley walls as if asking permission of the stone itself. "So tell me while they strike the flint. Why does a priestess of the outer shrines come to the one place in creation the gods can't follow, carrying the last dragon's heartfire?"

Sable told her.

She told it plainly, the way she'd been taught — the shrines going dark one by one, then lighting again with something inside them that answered prayers too readily; the god of hunger waking beneath Corvane, wearing King Aldric's crown; the last dragon, small and dying, who had pressed a shard of her own fire into Sable's hands and said, *carry this to the Spire, and find the bloodline that bound me to my promise.* She left out nothing except, perhaps, how afraid she was, though she suspected Maren counted fear the way she counted everything else.

When she finished, the pyre below was lit. Smoke rose straight up in the windless morning, and the elders began to sing, low and tuneless, the forgetting-song Sable had heard through the walls her first night — a song with no words anyone remembered, which was itself a kind of forgetting.

Maren watched the smoke for a long time.

"The binding was drawn by an Almeida," she said at last.

Sable went still. "Ember said the bloodline was—"

"I know what the dragon said. She's right. But she'll have told it gently, dragons always do, so let me tell it the way it was." Maren's hands tightened on the windowsill. "Three hundred years ago, when the hunger-god first wore a king, it was Aldric's own cartographer who found the shape of the cage. A woman named Isolde Almeida. She mapped the Spire's roots, she mapped the ley-lines of the king's own soul, and she drew the binding that locked them together — king and god, prisoner and keeper, forever. Then she led her people into this valley and asked the mountains to forget them, and the mountains, being old and tired, agreed."

The smoke below thinned. The singing stopped. In the sudden quiet, Sable could hear her own heartbeat.

"So the dragon's fire must be carried by—"

"An Almeida. Yes." Maren finally looked at her, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "I'm the last one old enough to survive the road. My grandson is the last one young enough. And I have spent seventy years making sure he would never, ever learn which of those facts mattered more."

* * *

They came for Sable at dusk.

Not violently — the valley didn't do violence, violence required passion and the valley had spent centuries sanding its passions smooth. They came the way frost comes. Elder Corwin at the door with six others behind him, and Maren downstairs answering their questions in a voice like still water, and Xanthe on the stairs between them all, white-faced, holding a satchel Sable recognized as the one he used for his finished maps.

"She cannot stay," Corwin was saying. He wasn't cruel. That was the horror of it; he sounded genuinely sorrowful, a man announcing weather. "The wards shuddered when she crossed the boundary, Maren. You felt it. Whatever she carries, it pulls at the valley like a hook in cloth. Every day she stays, the weave frays."

"The weave is already frayed," Maren said. "Or have you not noticed the prayers being answered beyond the walls? Your forgetting-song is a dam, Corwin, and the flood is coming whether or not we drown this woman's horse."

"The horse burns tonight regardless."

At the top of the stairs, Sable saw Xanthe flinch.

She descended slowly, letting them see the shard at her throat, letting them feel what they'd spent generations training themselves not to feel. Several of the elders looked away. One made a sign against evil that predated the valley and that none of them could have explained.

"I'll go," Sable said. "Willingly. I ask only provisions and a guide to the valley's edge."

"You'll have neither," Corwin said gently. "Provisions leave traces. Guides return changed. You'll walk out as you walked in, priestess, and the mountain will close behind you, and by spring we will have half-forgotten you were ever—"

"No."

Everyone turned. Xanthe had come down the stairs and was standing in the front room's lamplight, satchel on his shoulder, and Sable watched him do the hardest thing she had ever seen anyone do: speak against the current of everything he loved.

"She goes with a guide. She goes with me."

Corwin's face did something complicated. "Xanthe. Son. The valley is—"

"The valley is a cage my family built." His voice shook and he let it shake; Sable realized he'd decided that shaking it was honest, and honesty was armor. "I learned today that my grandmother's grandmother drew the binding on the Spire. The dragon's fire needs an Almeida's hand. If I stay, you'll send Maren instead, because that's what this valley does — it sends its oldest to hold its doors shut. I won't let it send her."

"Xanthe—" Maren started.

"No." He turned to his grandmother, and his eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "You burned your whole life keeping this secret so I could have a quiet one. I'm spending it instead. That's what inheritance *is*, Gran. Someone finally gets to use it."

For a moment the old woman simply looked at him. Then she crossed the room, took his face in both weathered hands, and pressed her forehead to his — a gesture so old it had no name anymore, some pre-valley blessing that the forgetting hadn't quite eaten.

"When you reach the Spire," she murmured, "the binding will know your blood. It will try to keep you. It will show you the valley, safe and warm, and offer you the door home. Don't take it. Isolde didn't, and look what it cost her — three hundred years of grandchildren she never got to meet."

"I'm meeting you," Xanthe said. "That counts."

"It counts," Maren agreed, and stepped back, and became granite again, because the elders were watching and someone in this family had to be. "Corwin. The boy is exiled with the priestess. Effective immediately. Draw up whatever paper soothes you."

"Sable of the outer shrines is exiled," Corwin intoned, "and the Almeida boy with her, until such time as—" He stopped. Looked at the satchel. At the shard glowing softly at Sable's throat. At the window, where beyond the valley walls the evening sky had begun, very faintly, to shimmer with the aurora of answered prayers. "Until such time as the world makes sense again," he finished quietly, abandoning the formula. "Go quickly. Before I remember how to be afraid for you."

* * *

They left through the eastern pass at moonrise, because the western road was watched and because Ember waited east, in the high ash-country where nothing grew tall enough to hide a dragon.

Sable walked beside Xanthe and listened to him breathe — quick at first, ragged at the switchbacks, gradually steadying as the valley lights fell away below. He didn't cry. She suspected he would later, alone, furiously, mapping the route by lantern-light to keep his hands busy. She'd known soldiers who grieved exactly that way.

At the boundary stone — a squat grey marker older than any kingdom — he stopped.

"This is where the wards end," he said. "Past this, I can't come back. Ever. The valley won't... it won't remember me well enough to let me in."

"Do you want to turn around?"

He was quiet a long moment. Below and behind them, Vael's Hollow lay in its bowl of darkness, small and lit and safe, the only home he'd ever known, already beginning to forget him.

"My grandmother gave me a message for a dead queen's ghost," he said finally. "I intend to deliver it."

He stepped past the stone, and Sable followed, and behind them the mountain air shivered once — like a held breath released, like a page turning — as the valley let them go.

Ahead, in the ash-country, something small and ancient lifted its head and smelled dragon-fire coming home.
