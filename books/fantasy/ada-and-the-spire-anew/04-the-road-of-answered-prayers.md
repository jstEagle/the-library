## 4. The Road of Answered Prayers

The valley had taught Xanthe that the world ended at the mist-line. He had known this the way he knew his own heartbeat — not as a fact learned but as a fact lived, so deep it required no thought at all. And then he had walked out through the pass with Sable limping beside him and the elders' shouts still ringing off the stone behind them, and the world had refused to end.

It had only gotten stranger.

Three days out from Vael's Hollow, they crested a ridge above a river valley that Xanthe recognized from a traveler's tale told eleven years ago by a wool merchant with a lazy eye. The merchant had described wheat fields here — modest ones, golden, ordinary. What lay below was not ordinary. The wheat stood taller than a man on horseback, each stalk thick as a child's wrist, bowing under heads so heavy they dragged the grain into the dirt. The field went on for what looked like miles, a golden sea gone wrong, and where the stalks pressed against the soil they had rooted again, growing sideways, tangling into a lattice no scythe could ever cut.

"Someone prayed for a harvest that would never fail," Sable said quietly. She stood beside him at the ridge's edge, her wounded side bound in fresh linen, her face drawn but steady. "And someone answered."

Xanthe got out his kit without thinking about it — the leather roll, the ink, the good paper Maren had slipped into his pack when she thought he wasn't looking. His hands were steadier than his heart. He began to draw.

That was the thing about mapping a place you'd never been: you had to decide what was true. A mapmaker working from rumor could fudge, could hedge, could leave blank spaces and call them honest. But Xanthe was standing *in* the world now, and the world demanded precision. So he measured the wheat against his walking staff, noted the direction of its growth — all of it leaning toward the river, as if drinking — and wrote beneath it, in his small careful hand: *Grain, monstrous. Growth unceasing. No sign of harvest.*

No sign of harvest. That was the detail that kept snagging in his mind like a burr in wool. If the wheat grew forever, who ate? Where were the farmhouses? There should have been villages stitched along the riverbank, smoke rising from chimneys, children shouting in the fields. Instead there was one structure, far downstream, half-swallowed by the grain itself — a farmhouse with wheat growing through its windows and up its chimney, golden stalks flowering from the thatch like some terrible crown.

They found the farmer on their way past. Or rather, they found what the farmer had become: an old man sitting in the doorway of his ruined home, thin as a lath, smiling.

"Blessed," he said when Sable asked if he needed help. "We are blessed. I asked for plenty, and plenty was given." His smile did not move. It sat on his face like something painted there. "I never have to stop giving thanks."

His eyes, Xanthe noticed as they drew away, were the color of old milk. Not blind. Just — emptied. As though everything inside him had been poured out to make room for the plenty.

He wrote that down too. His hand shook, and the line came out crooked, and he left it crooked because it felt dishonest to make it straight.

* * *

By the tenth day, Xanthe had filled nine sheets, and a pattern had begun to emerge from them the way landforms emerge from fog when you climb high enough.

Every miracle had a shape. Every granted prayer left a mark.

The village of Brindleford, where a well-woman had prayed for rain during a drought and now commanded every drop of water within a mile — her villagers knelt to her in the mud whenever she passed, and she could not make them stop, and she could not leave, because the rain followed her and drowned whatever ground she stood on too long. They saw her from a distance, a woman in a gray cloak standing alone in a circle of perpetual drizzle, her fields lush, her neighbors' faces turned up to her like faces turned up to weather.

The miller at Harrow's Crossing, who had prayed for his millstones never to wear — and now the stones ground day and night whether he willed it or not, grinding grain, then gravel, then the foundation stones of his own mill, and he slept in the road because the noise never stopped, and he smiled too, that same painted smile, and said *blessed, we are blessed* in a voice worn smooth as river pebbles.

The children of a hamlet whose name Xanthe never learned, all born after a collective prayer for healthy babies, all born with eyes like coins, gold and flat, watching the travelers pass in perfect silence from the windows of their schoolhouse.

"It takes something," Xanthe said one night, laying his maps out on the floor of an abandoned barn while Sable tended the small fire they allowed themselves. "Every answer. Look." He weighted the corners with stones. "The farmer gave up — I don't know. Hunger? He doesn't eat much. The rain-woman can't love anyone anymore; you can see it, the way people flinch around her. The miller can't rest. The children—" He stopped. He couldn't finish that one yet.

"The god takes worship," Sable said. She was feeding the fire shard slivers of dry bark, and it glowed in her cupped hands like a dying coal, dimmer than when they'd started, or perhaps Xanthe only imagined it. "But not the kind it wants. It wants freely given faith. So it grants wishes instead, and the granting hollows people out until all that's left is gratitude. Bought faith. Farmed faith." Her mouth twisted. "A god of hunger, farming its own food."

"You said that name before. On the road. The god."

"I said enough."

"Sable." He turned to look at her properly. Firelight moved across her face, and he saw how tired she was, how much of her strength the wound and the shard and the walking were taking. "You're asking me to walk toward a god. I've earned a name."

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the monstrous wheat of a neighboring farm, and it made a sound like whispering, like ten thousand voices saying thank you, thank you, thank you.

"Aldric," she said at last. "The king in the Spire. The binding holds him, and the binding is failing, and when it fails, the thing wearing his crown will be loose in the world with nothing left to eat but everything." She looked up. "There. Now you know as much as I do. Which is more than most people learn before they die."

"That's meant to comfort me?"

"No," Sable said. "It's meant to be true. You strike me as someone who prefers true."

He did. It was one of the few things the valley had given him that survived contact with the world outside it.

* * *

They met Ember on the fourteenth day, in a burned clearing high on the shoulder of Mount Cael, where the maps in Xanthe's head said there should have been a shrine.

The clearing was old burn — years old, decades maybe, the dead trees silvered and soft — except that at its center the ground was black and fresh, as though something had scorched it recently and repeatedly, a circle of cinders thirty feet across. In the middle of the circle lay a dragon.

Xanthe had imagined dragons his whole life. Every mapmaker had; dragons were the traditional ornament of unknown places, drawn coiled in the margins of seas no one had crossed. He had imagined vast wings and mountain-sized bodies and treasure and terror.

Ember was the size of a large dog.

She lay on her side in the ash, copper scales dull as old pennies, her wings folded tight against a body that had clearly once been larger and was now, somehow, visibly less — as though she were being erased from the edges inward. When she lifted her head, her eyes were molten gold, and ancient, and furious, and afraid.

"Well," she said. Her voice was smoke and gravel and, underneath it, something almost like laughter. "An Almeida. Here. At the end of everything." Her gaze fixed on Xanthe with terrible weight. "Come to finish the job your family started?"

Sable stepped forward, hands open. "Ember—"

"I know why you've come, little priestess. I can smell my fire. It's in your pack, guttering away day by day while you walk it toward me like a good courier." The dragon struggled upright, and Xanthe saw the effort cost her — saw the fresh scorch marks her body left on the ground, heat bleeding out of her with every movement. "So let us skip the pleasantries. Show me the shard."

Sable brought it out. Even in daylight, even at twenty paces, Xanthe felt it — a warmth against his cheek like standing near an oven door, a smell of woodsmoke and something older, something like the first fire anyone ever lit. In Sable's palms it glowed sullen orange, no bigger than a robin's egg.

Ember looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread.

"One spark," she said softly. "Out of all the fire I ever was. My brothers and sisters are ash. My hoard is scattered. My name is a story human children don't even tell correctly." She lowered her great blunt head until her snout nearly touched the shard, and her breath made it flare briefly bright, and for one moment Xanthe saw what she must have been — a wall of wing and flame filling the sky over some forgotten battle — and then the light faded and she was small again, and dying, and alone.

"What happens if we bring it to the Spire?" Xanthe asked. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "Sable says it can break the binding. Your fire, against the Almeida binding. But she won't say what it costs."

Ember laughed. It was a horrible sound, crackling and short.

"What does anything cost, cartographer? Ask your maps. You've been reading the ledger for two weeks — I watched you writing in it, back in the wheat country, before you ever knew I existed. Every prayer answered, something taken. Every miracle, a hole." Her gold eyes pinned him. "My fire is the last thing in this world the god cannot eat, because it was never given — it was *made*, forged, paid for in full. It can cut the binding. It can free your king. And it will burn away every ward, every shield, every borrowed protection that stands between an Almeida and the hungry dark. Starting with yours."

"The valley," Xanthe said. His mouth had gone dry.

"The valley. The mist. The forgetting. All of it, gone, the moment my fire touches your blood." Ember settled back onto her haunches, and her tail curled around the shard's glow like a hand closing. "Your ancestors built a cage, boy, and hid inside it, and called the hiding safety. I am offering you the key. But keys don't care what they unlock."

Wind swept the clearing. Ash rose around them, gray and weightless, and somewhere below, faint as memory, Xanthe could almost hear the wheat whispering its endless thanks.

"And if I refuse?" he asked.

Ember closed her eyes.

"Then you go home," she said, "and you live long, and safe, and small, and one morning the mist doesn't come back, and you will know — to the hour — which morning you could have chosen differently."

She opened her eyes again, and held out one clawed foot, palm upward, patient as stone.

Behind him, Sable said nothing at all.
