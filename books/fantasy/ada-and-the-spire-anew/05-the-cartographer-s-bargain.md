## 5. The Cartographer's Bargain

Ember had watched a thousand years of humans lie to themselves, and she had learned to smell the truth the way other creatures smelled fear: something sharp and mineral, like struck flint. The young cartographer carried it in him like a coal carried in cupped hands — carefully, painfully, afraid to let it gutter and more afraid to let it catch.

He was drawing again.

She watched him from her hollow in the cliffside, where she had wedged herself among stones that still remembered being warm. Once, her body had been long enough to circle this mountain twice without beating her wings. Once, her fire had been a thing that made rivers steam and kings kneel. Now she was scarcely larger than a hunting hound, her scales gone the grey of old ash, her wings folded tight against a body that had forgotten how to be magnificent. She had learned, over the centuries, to be small. It was the hardest lesson of her long life, harder than grief, harder than hunger: how to shrink without disappearing.

The boy — no, not a boy; he was twenty-some years, though he had the careful hands of someone much older — sat at the mouth of the cave with his maps spread across a flat stone. Xanthe Almeida. He drew the world he had never seen, line by patient line, from rumor and memory and the testimony of strangers, and there was something in the way he worked that made Ember's ancient heart ache. He did not draw to own the land. He drew to *know* it. In a thousand years of men with maps, she had never once seen that distinction honored.

Sable slept by the small fire they'd dared to light, the dragon-shard cradled against her breastbone like a second heart. The shard had dimmed again overnight. Ember could feel it the way a mother feels a child's fever fading — relief and terror braided together, because when the last of her fire went out, so did the last chance of anything ever being unmade.

*Ask him,* the shard seemed to whisper, in the voice of all the fire she used to be. *Ask him tonight.*

But first, the test.

* * *

She waited until full dark, until the moon had drowned itself in cloud and the only light was their little fire and the faint ember-glow of the shard beneath Sable's cloak. Then she unfolded herself from the stone hollow and came into the firelight on legs that trembled more than she wanted them to.

Xanthe looked up from his map. To his credit, he did not flinch. She had seen grown warriors wet themselves at far less than a dragon emerging from shadow, even a dying one, even one no bigger than a wolf. But Xanthe Almeida simply set down his pen and went very still, the way he did when a stranger told him something true about a place he'd only imagined.

"You're awake," he said. "I mean — you've been awake. Watching."

"Since the road," Ember said. Her voice came out like gravel rolled in smoke. "Since the village of the nine-foot wheat. You stood in the middle of that field, in grain tall as a chapel, and you didn't reach for your pen. Everyone else who sees it wants to measure it. Own it. You just looked sad."

"It was growing out of graves," Xanthe said quietly. "Underneath. I could feel them through my boots. Every stalk is somebody's answered prayer, and every prayer cost somebody else everything." He hesitated. "You knew that already. Didn't you?"

"I have known it since before your valley had a name." Ember settled herself near the fire, close enough to feel its heat on scales that had not felt warmth but her own in three hundred years. It hurt, pleasantly, like pressing on a bruise. "I am going to ask you questions, cartographer. Answer them honestly, or answer them not at all. A lie will taste worse than silence."

Xanthe glanced at Sable, sleeping. Then back. "Ask."

"What did your grandmother tell you, the night you left?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was the flint-smell again, truth rising off him like heat off summer stone. "That the Almeidas drew the binding," he said. "Not wrote it. *Drew* it. The wards around Vael's Hollow aren't prayers or spells. They're lines. Cartography. My ancestors mapped the valley into invisibility, stroke by stroke, and the ink they used—" His voice caught. "The ink was dragon-fire. Yours."

Ember closed her eyes. Even now, after everything, hearing it said aloud by an Almeida's mouth moved through her like weather.

"There were seven of us left then," she said, when she trusted her voice. "Seven dragons in all the world, and the god of hunger eating its way through the sky like rot through fruit. Your ancestors came to us with a plan: bind a dying king's soul into the Spire as an anchor, cage the god's attention there, and hide one valley from every divine eye so that something human might survive, some seed of the ordinary world. They needed fire to write the binding. Fire that would burn for centuries." She opened her eyes. "Six of us gave it willingly. The seventh refused. Do you know what happened to the seventh?"

"No," Xanthe whispered.

"She was me," Ember said. "I refused because I thought the binding was a cowardice. I thought hiding a valley while the rest of the world burned was not salvation but surrender, dressed up in noble language. So they took my fire anyway." She lifted one foreleg, turned it in the firelight, showed him the pale scar that ran from shoulder to claw, old beyond his understanding. "They did not kill me. Your ancestors were not cruel. But they took what they needed, and I have been ash walking ever since, carrying the last unspent drop of myself inside a shard I gave to a priestess because I could no longer carry it alone."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, the wilds breathed — the monstrous crops, the miracle-drunk villages, the whole groaning weight of a world where every wish was granted and every granting was a hook.

"I'm sorry," Xanthe said. And here was the thing that made Ember lean forward despite herself: he did not say it the way people usually said sorry, quickly, to be done with it. He said it slowly, like a man setting down something heavy. "I can't undo what they did. But it was mine to be sorry for. I've known since Maren told me, and I've been — I've been waiting for you to ask. Dreading it. Because I thought if you knew what my family was, you'd send me away."

"And instead?"

"Instead I've been sitting here drawing maps of places I helped blind," he said, "and trying to work up the courage to tell you first."

Ember studied him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a door opening in a house long shut.

* * *

"The test wasn't the questions," she told him, later, when Sable had woken and the three of them sat in a triangle around the fire, the shard glowing faintly between them like a captive dawn. "The test was whether you'd confess before I forced it. Most don't. Most wait to be caught, and then apologize to the catching, not the crime." She nodded at the satchel where Xanthe kept his maps. "Your ancestors drew the binding. You draw the world back open. That is not nothing, cartographer. That may be everything."

"But?" Xanthe said. There was always a *but*. He had the look of a man who had learned, in one hard week outside the valley, that every gift had a hook in it.

"But the bargain is ugly," Ember said, "and I will not dress it up. Listen."

She rose, crossed to Sable, and touched her snout to the priestess's hands. Sable drew out the shard and held it up, and in its depths Ember saw her own fire — dimmed now to a red no brighter than a dying hearth, but hers, unmistakably hers, the last of six dragons' worth of willing flame plus the stolen seventh.

"This fire can unmake the binding at the Spire's roots. It was the ink; it knows the script; it can read itself backward. One touch to the anchor-lines and King Aldric goes free." She paused. "But fire is not a tool. It is a covenant. It does not work for hands it does not trust, and it does not trust lightly. It was written by Almeida hands, and so it can only be unwritten by one."

"Then it should be Sable," Xanthe began. "Or you—"

"Sable is not an Almeida. And I am the fire's source, not its bearer — I cannot carry what I am." Ember fixed him with eyes gone pale gold, like coins left too long in a river. "It must be you, Xanthe. An Almeida's hand must carry my fire into the Spire's roots. And here is the price, and do not look away from it: the moment you take the shard into your keeping — truly keep it, accept it, make yourself its bearer — it will burn the valley's protection out of you. Forever. The wards are drawn in dragon-fire, and dragon-fire recognizes its own. You will become a line the binding cannot hold. No ward will ever shelter you again. Not Vael's Hollow. Not any sanctuary any hand ever draws. You will live the rest of your life in the open world, under the open sky, with the god's eye able to find you whenever it wishes."

Silence. Sable's breath had gone shallow. The fire popped and spat.

"That's why you asked about my grandmother," Xanthe said at last. "You weren't testing whether I'd confess. You were testing whether I understood what I come from. Whether I'd take the inheritance or refuse it."

"Inheritance is a strange word for a debt," Ember said. "But yes."

Xanthe looked at his hands — the ink-stained fingers, the callus on the middle finger where the pen rested, hands that had never held anything heavier than a compass. Ember watched him weigh it, and she saw exactly what passed through him, because she had watched ten thousand humans weigh their lives and she knew all the shapes of it: the pull toward safety, old and deep as bone; the image of Maren's face; the memory of the grave-wheat field and the graves beneath it; the knowledge that somewhere behind him lay a valley of invisible people living invisible lives, safe as seeds in a drawer, and ahead of him a king in a cage begging, perhaps, to stay caged.

"If I refuse," he said, "someone else pays. Someone always pays. That's what the answered prayers taught me — every wish lands somewhere." He looked up, and his eyes were steady, and Ember felt something in her ancient chest turn over, slow and enormous, like the earth remembering it could quake. "If I accept, I'm the one who pays. I can live with that arithmetic. I'd rather live with it than with the other."

"You understand," Ember said, "that once you take it, there is no giving it back. The fire does not return. If you die on the road, it dies with you, and Aldric stays bound until the binding rots, and the god eats the world at its leisure."

"I understand."

"If you die in the Spire, same."

"I understand."

"And if you succeed—" Ember's voice dropped low, and the old fire stirred in her throat, making her words glow faintly at their edges, "—you will spend the rest of your life unprotected, hunted by a god who remembers your family's name, in a world where every miracle has a hook in it. Knowing all this. Choosing it anyway."

Xanthe reached out and took the shard from Sable's hands.

It did not blaze. It did not roar. It simply warmed — warmed like a living thing recognizing kin — and Ember felt the last of herself flow across the space between them, felt the covenant close around the young man's fingers like a ring sliding home. For one heartbeat the cave filled with light the color of the oldest sunrises, and she saw, imprinted on the air, ghost-faint, the original binding-lines as her siblings had written them: beautiful, terrible, a cage drawn in devotion.

Then the light faded, and the shard sat quiet in Xanthe's palm, and his face had changed. Not older. *Opened.* Like a map finally annotated with the truth.

"So that's it," Sable breathed. "You're the bearer now."

"I'm the bearer now." Xanthe wrapped the shard carefully and tucked it against his chest, where it pulsed once, twice, settling into the rhythm of his heart. He looked at Ember. "What happens to you? When the fire's spent?"

Ember considered lying, found she had no appetite for it, and told him. "Ash. Real ash, this time. Chosen." She smiled, showing teeth gone yellow with age. "Every dragon dreams of burning out in one great act. Mine will be small, and quiet, and enough. Don't mourn me, cartographer. Write me into a map instead. That's the only immortality I ever wanted."

Xanthe laughed — a cracked, wet sound — and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist, and for a while none of them spoke, and the fire burned, and the night leaned close around the cave like a listener.

It was Sable who broke the silence. She had gone rigid, her head cocked toward the dark mouth of the cave, one hand pressed flat against the earth.

"Riders," she said. "Many. Coming up the pass with lanterns hooded." She looked up, and her face in the firelight was grim as scripture. "They're flying valley colors. Someone's sent the elders after us — and they'll reach Corvane before we do."

Ember felt the last of her fire shift against the young man's heart, and thought: *So it begins.*
