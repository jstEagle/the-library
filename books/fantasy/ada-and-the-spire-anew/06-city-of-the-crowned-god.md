## 6. City of the Crowned God

Sable had imagined a hundred cities in her years of wandering, but none of them had prepared her for Corvane.

It rose out of the plain like a second horizon — white walls, gold spires, and above them all, the Spire itself, so tall that its crown vanished into cloud. Even from three miles out, on the pilgrim road, she could feel it. Not see. *Feel.* A pressure behind the eyes, a sweetness in the throat, like standing too close to a hive of something that hummed.

"Stop looking at it," Ember said from the sling across Sable's chest. The dragon was no larger than a cat now, her scales gone dull as old ash, and she spoke in a voice like embers settling. "Every glance is an invitation."

"I'm not praying," Sable said.

"You're *wanting* to. That's worse."

Xanthe said nothing. He stood at the roadside with his journal open, sketching the city's silhouette with quick, certain strokes, and Sable watched him do it — watched him turn dread into lines, fear into cartography, as if naming a thing's shape were the same as disarming it. Maybe for him it was.

"The walls have seven gates," he murmured. "The pilgrim road feeds the southernmost. Every account agrees on that." He glanced up at the stream of travelers ahead of them — hundreds, thousands, a river of the faithful flowing toward Corvane with their wishes bundled in their hands like letters. "What no account agrees on is what they look like when they come back out."

"They don't come back out," Ember said. "Not as what they were."

Sable touched the shard at her throat. It had dimmed again overnight — she could tell by its weight now more than its light, the way a coal tells you it's dying by how little heat it gives. Three days, maybe four, before it went dark entirely. Before Ember went with it.

Then they joined the river of pilgrims, and the gates of Corvane swallowed them whole.

* * *

Inside the walls, the city smelled of bread and incense and something underneath both, faint and wrong — a sweetness like fruit going soft, like milk just turning. The streets were clean. That was the first horror, Sable decided later. She had expected squalor in a capital that fed on desperation; instead everything gleamed. Beggars sat on clean stones with full bellies. Cripples walked. A woman wept with joy over a well that had filled with clear water where salt had been that morning.

And every one of them had the same stillness behind the eyes.

She saw it first in a baker who pressed a loaf into Xanthe's hands without being asked. "The Crowned One provides," the man said, smiling. His smile reached his mouth but stopped there, like light against a wall. "He heard my prayer. My daughter's fever broke in a single night. Ask, friend. Ask, and be answered."

"I— thank you," Xanthe managed.

"Ask," the baker repeated, and for a moment his smile didn't move at all, and his eyes were two windows into an empty room. Then he blinked, laughed, and turned back to his oven, humming.

They walked on. Sable counted the hollow ones as they went — the baker, a guard at a fountain, a girl selling flowers who gave them away freely because the Crowned One provided and she needed nothing. One in five. One in four, nearer the palace district. Their prayers had been answered so completely that nothing of them remained to want anything else, and a person who wants nothing, Sable thought, is a person who can be poured into any shape.

"It takes the wanting," she whispered. "That's the price. It doesn't take coin or blood or years. It takes the part of you that reaches for things."

"And leaves the rest standing there, smiling," Xanthe said. His knuckles were white around his journal. "I mapped this city from forty different accounts, and not one of them mentioned—" He stopped. Swallowed. "No one wrote it down because no one who knew it could anymore."

Ember stirred against Sable's chest. "Now you understand why I am the last."

* * *

They lodged that night in a pilgrim hospice near the palace district, a long hall of straw pallets where the devout slept within sight of the Spire so they could be first in line at dawn. Sable lay awake between snoring strangers and listened to the prayers.

They rose all around her like heat off stone. *Let my son be tall. Let my shop outlast the winter. Let her love me back. Let me stop grieving. Let me stop grieving. Let me stop grieving.*

And beneath them, vast and patient, something listened. She felt it the way a deer feels a wolf's attention — not a sound, not a sight, but a change in the quality of the dark. The god did not sleep. The god *shopped*, turning each wish over, weighing which hunger to feed and what it would get in return.

Beside her, Xanthe was awake too. In the moonlight through the high windows she saw him drawing — of course he was — copying by memory the palace plans he'd studied in a burned book in Vael's Hollow, the ones the elders hadn't found fast enough.

"There," he whispered, tapping the page. "The processional way runs straight to the great doors, and the great doors are guarded by men whose prayers have already been answered. But underneath—" his pencil traced a line that looped beneath the palace's footprint, "—there was an older building here. Pre-dynastic. Water channels, cisterns, a passage the masons sealed when the Spire was raised on top of it. The plans call it the Weeping Stair."

"How does a sealed passage help us?"

"It was sealed from the palace side. From below, it's only collapsed in two places." He looked up, and in the dark his eyes were very steady. "Two places I think I can map our way through. If the measurements in the book are honest."

"And if they aren't?"

"Then we die under the most prayed-over building in the world, and honestly, Sable, given the alternative—" he nodded at the sleeping hall, at the soft mouths moving through dreams full of granted wishes, "—I'll take the cisterns."

* * *

They went down at the hour before dawn, when even the hollow guards grew drowsy, and the Weeping Stair took them the way old places take trespassers: reluctantly, dripping, alive with the sound of water that had been falling for centuries.

Sable went first with the shard held high. Its failing light made the passage swim — wet stone, root-veined ceilings, walls furred with white mineral bloom like frost that never melted. Ember rode in the sling against her heart, radiating a heat that was almost painful now, spending herself to keep them warm in the cold dark.

Xanthe came last, mapping as they moved, marking every junction, every collapse, every fork with pencil strokes that never hesitated. Twice they crawled through rubble where the ceiling had come down, and twice he found the seam of the original tunnel beyond it by reading the angle of the water stains. He never once suggested turning back.

The air changed before the passage ended. Warmer. Sweeter. That rotten-honey smell, thick enough to chew.

"We're under the throne room," Xanthe breathed. "Or close to it. The plans put the great hall directly above the old cistern."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, they heard singing. Thousands of voices, wordless, rising and falling in unison — dawn devotions, the whole court breathing together.

They climbed a maintenance shaft, hand over hand up iron rungs green with age, and came at last to a grate of corroded bronze set high in the wall of the cistern chamber. Beyond it, through a second grille and a slot meant for viewing, the great hall opened below them like a bowl of light.

Sable looked down into the throne room of King Aldric, and forgot how to breathe.

* * *

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Ten thousand candles. A floor of pale marble veined with gold, polished to mirror-brightness so that the crowd of worshippers seemed to stand upon a sky. Choirs in white along the walls. And at the far end, on a dais beneath the rooted base of the Spire itself — the stone of it plunging down through the vaulted ceiling like the trunk of a petrified tree — stood the king.

Aldric was tall, broad-shouldered, dressed simply in grey, as the accounts of his early reign described. He looked tired. He looked kind. He looked, Sable thought with a lurch, like a man she might have confessed to in another life, another road.

Then he smiled at a kneeling woman presenting a sick child, and the smile belonged to someone else.

It was a stranger's smile wearing Aldric's face the way the crown wore his head — placed there, seated there, *inhabiting* it. For half a heartbeat the king's own eyes surfaced through it, wide and drowning, and Sable saw him the way one sees a face behind frosted glass: *help me, help me, help me*. Then the god settled back into its borrowed features and the smile deepened, warm as a hearth, and the god touched the child's forehead and the child's twisted leg straightened with a sound like green wood bending, and the mother wept, and ten thousand voices sang, and the price was paid somewhere unseen, quietly, forever.

"She got exactly what she asked for," Sable whispered. Her hands had gone numb on the bronze grate. "Every prayer answered. Look at her. She'd feed him her own soul grain by grain and thank him for the meal."

"That's what we're walking into," Xanthe said softly. He wasn't looking at the king. He was looking at the Spire's roots, at the living rock descending through the hall, and Sable realized he was mapping it even now — measuring, memorizing, finding the way down. "The roots go deeper than the hall. The plans end where the binding begins. Everything below this line—" his pencil hovered, "—is blank."

Below them, the king raised his head.

Not gradually. All at once, like a hound catching scent, and the smile fell away and left the face bare and terrible in its attention. The choirs faltered. Ten thousand candles leaned sideways in a wind that did not exist.

The crowned thing looked directly up — past the worshippers, past the vaulted ceiling, past forty feet of stone — at the slotted grate where three small trespassers stood holding a dying flame.

It smiled again. And though no voice crossed the distance, Sable heard the words anyway, pressed gently against the inside of her skull like a kiss:

*I know your fire, little dragon. Come down. I have been saving room for you.*
