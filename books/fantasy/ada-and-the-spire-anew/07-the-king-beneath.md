## 7. The King Beneath

The passages beneath Corvane were older than the city above them.

Xanthe had known this the moment he'd unrolled his copies of the palace plans in Sable's rented room — plans he'd bought from a one-eyed archivist who charged him in secrets rather than coin. The oldest sheets of vellum showed corridors that no longer existed above ground, stairwells that descended past any sensible depth, and at their center, drawn in a hand that trembled even across two centuries, a shape like a root system spreading down from the Spire's foundations. The archivist had called it *the King's Root* and refused to say more. He had taken Xanthe's secret — *I am an Almeida; my family drew the binding* — and simply nodded, as if that explained everything and forgave nothing.

Now, three days later, Xanthe understood why the man's single eye had watered when he said it.

He descended by candlelight, though the candle was nearly unnecessary. The walls here were not stone. They were something between stone and wood, veined with pale channels that pulsed faintly, slowly, like the heartbeat of something enormous and very far away. Warmth came off them in waves. When Xanthe pressed his palm against the surface to steady himself on a steep stair, he felt it breathe.

*This is the binding,* he thought. *Not ink on vellum. Not wards on a valley. This is living tissue, grown around a wound.*

His ancestors had done this. His grandmother's grandmother's people, with Ember's fire and a king's consent, had wrapped a dying man in a cocoon of faith and set a god's teeth against it. Every map Xanthe had ever drawn had been, in some small way, an apology for this one — the first Almeida map, the one that charted not roads but a prison.

Sable waited above, in the forgotten chapel where the passages met the palace's underbelly, keeping watch and keeping Ember's shard hidden against her heart. She had wanted to come down with him. He had argued she should stay — someone had to hold their escape route, someone had to be ready if things went wrong — but the truth, which he hadn't said aloud, was simpler and worse: he needed to face the king alone first. An Almeida owed Aldric that much. A debt collector did not bring witnesses to an apology.

The stair ended at a door that was not a door.

It was a membrane, thin as parchment, stretched across an archway, and through it Xanthe could see light — gold and slow, like honey held up to the sun. Words were written around the arch in a script he recognized from his grandmother's forbidden books: the old binding-tongue, the language the valley elders burned books to forget. He read them without meaning to, the way you read your own name.

*HERE LIES ALDRIC, WHO GAVE HIMSELF THAT OTHERS MIGHT KEEP THEIRS. LET NO ONE WAKE HIM WHO IS NOT PREPARED TO TAKE HIS PLACE.*

Xanthe stood before it for a long time, candle guttering, heart loud in his ears.

Then he thought of the fields outside Vael's Hollow, the ones he'd mapped from travelers' tales — crops grown monstrous by granted wishes, wheat with teeth, orchards that wept sap the color of blood because some farmer had prayed for rain and received a flood instead. He thought of the villages ruled by petty miracle-workers, children made into oracles, old men crowned in borrowed glory. He thought of the hollow-eyed worshippers in Corvane's streets, kneeling in the mud before a palace that ate their prayers and gave back nothing but the *feeling* of being answered.

And he stepped through.

* * *

The chamber beyond was vast and round, and it was alive.

The walls curved inward like the inside of a throat, ribbed with those pale pulsing channels, all of them converging toward the center of the floor — and there, at the convergence, lay the king.

Aldric was not imprisoned the way Xanthe had imagined. There were no chains, no bars, no cage. Instead the man lay half-submerged in the floor itself, as if the living stone had risen around him like a tide and never receded. It covered him to the waist. His arms were free, folded over his chest almost peacefully. His hair had gone white — not aged white, but bleached, drained, the white of bone left in sun. His skin was translucent at the edges, and through it Xanthe could see the faint gold glow of the channels, running through him too, connecting him to everything.

His eyes were open.

"You're not supposed to be here," the king said. His voice was dry, rusted, the voice of a man who had not spoken to anyone in a very long time — or perhaps had spoken to nothing else. "The binding doesn't admit visitors. It admits prayers." A pause, and then something flickered across his ruined face — curiosity, fragile and human. "You didn't pray your way in. You *walked*. Who taught you the root-paths?"

"My family," Xanthe said. His voice sounded absurdly small in the vast breathing dark. "The Almeidas."

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Recognition, and grief, and — strangely — relief.

"Then you've come about the god."

"I've come to unbind you."

Aldric laughed. It was a terrible sound, thin and cracking, and it echoed strangely, doubling and redoubling until it seemed to come from the walls themselves, from the whole pulsing architecture of the prison. Then it stopped abruptly, cut off, as if something else had taken the laugh away mid-breath.

"No," the king said quietly. "You mustn't."

* * *

They talked for hours. Or perhaps minutes — time moved strangely in the roots, stretching and pooling like the golden light. Xanthe sat on the warm floor beside the king's stone tide, close enough to see the fine tremor in Aldric's hands, and listened.

"The binding holds because I hold," Aldric explained. His voice grew stronger as he spoke, as if speech itself fed him, or as if having someone to speak to reminded his body how to be a body. "The god is hunger. That's all it is — hunger wearing a crown it found somewhere. It cannot feed on its own. It needs worship the way a leech needs blood: freely offered, or at least freely *felt*. My soul sits between it and the world like a cork in a bottle. Every prayer meant for it passes through me first, and I... I drink what I can. I dilute the rest. That's the work. That has been the work for two hundred years."

"Two hundred years," Xanthe whispered. "You've been lucid for two hundred years?"

"Lucid?" Aldric smiled, and it was the saddest thing Xanthe had ever seen. "Boy, ask me my mother's name."

"Aldric—"

"Ask me." The king turned his head, and his bleached eyes fixed on Xanthe with terrible gentleness. "Go on. Ask me anything. My wife's face. The name of the horse I rode at the Battle of the Fords. Whether I liked honey in my tea."

Xanthe couldn't speak.

"I don't know," Aldric said. "I knew once. But every prayer that passes through me takes a little toll, and the god takes its tithe in memories. It eats what I am, piece by piece, and wears the pieces. That smile you'll have seen on the man upstairs — the one sitting my throne, blessing my people — that's my smile. It took it forty years ago. Sometimes I catch myself reaching for it and find only a hole." He flexed his fingers in the stone. "I am a wall being dismantled brick by brick. And when the last brick goes, there will be nothing between that thing and the world but paper."

"Then all the more reason—" Xanthe began.

"*No.*" The word cracked like a whip, and the whole chamber shuddered. The channels flared gold. Somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms of living stone, Xanthe heard a sound like a great inhale — the god, stirring, tasting the spike of the king's anger. Aldric closed his eyes and breathed slowly until the light dimmed again. "You don't understand what freeing me means. The binding isn't anchored to my body. It's anchored to my *soul*. Unbind me, and my soul departs — gladly, believe me, gladly — and the bottle opens. The god walks out wearing whatever's left of me, and it will be *hungry*, boy. Two hundred years of diluted meals. It will fall on this world like winter."

"Ember's fire can destroy it," Xanthe said. "That's what she told us. Dragon-fire unmade the first binding; it can unmake the second."

"Ember." Something tender crossed the king's face. "She still lives? Small and stubborn?" He shook his head. "Her fire can open the bottle, yes. Fire does not choose what it burns. The moment my binding breaks, the god is loose in this room with you, with her, with everyone — and dragon-fire, however bright, burns along a wick. It needs *time*. Seconds. Maybe less. Do you understand? You would need to hold the god's attention for the span of the burning. You would need it to *look at you* and keep looking while the flame does its work."

"And how do I make a god look at me?"

Aldric was silent for a long moment. The channels pulsed. Far above, faintly, through miles of stone and city, Xanthe could almost hear the murmur of ten thousand prayers rising from Corvane like smoke.

"You already know," the king said softly. "You're a cartographer. What draws the eye of anything that hunts?"

Xanthe looked down at his hands — ink-stained, calloused, the hands of a man who had spent his life drawing lines other people followed.

"Bait," he said.

"Faith," Aldric corrected. "True faith. Not the bought kind, not the desperate kind that fills my captor's belly every day. The god has plenty of that; it barely tastes anymore. But *true* faith — faith offered knowing the price, faith with nothing asked in return — that it cannot ignore. It has been starving for the real thing since the day I was bound. If you stood in this room and believed in something, wholly, while the fire burned..." He trailed off. "It would turn from me. It would turn from everything. For a few seconds, it would be yours."

"And if it works," Xanthe said slowly, working it through the way he'd work a difficult coastline, "and the fire destroys it — you're freed. You die human. You get to stop."

"If it works." Aldric's mouth twisted. "And if it fails, the last thing I see is another fool devoured in my place, and the world ends anyway, and my two hundred years of holding the line amount to a delay." He reached out — with effort, the stone resisting him — and gripped Xanthe's wrist. His hand was cold as riverbed stones. "Listen to me, Almeida. I have begged the god itself, in the years when I could still form words it could hear. I have begged it to let me break, to let me fail, because failure would at least be *mine*. No one answered. So now I beg you, the first living soul to reach me since my own children grew old and died believing I was a saint: *do not free me.* Let me finish. Let me be a wall to the end. Find another way, or find no way, but do not spend yourself buying my death with the world's."

The grip tightened. The bleached eyes searched Xanthe's face.

"Promise me."

* * *

Xanthe did not promise.

He climbed the long stairs in silence, candle long dead, guided by the pulse of the walls themselves, and his mind was a map being redrawn in real time — coastlines shifting, rivers rerouting, every safe assumption washed away. Behind him, below, a man who had given everything was begging for the right to give the rest, and calling it mercy.

But Xanthe kept returning to one thread, pulling at it the way he'd pull at a contradiction in a traveler's tale until the whole story unraveled or revealed its truth.

*A decoy of faith,* Aldric had said. Someone to hold the god's gaze while the fire burned.

And Xanthe thought of Sable — of the priestess who had crawled into his valley carrying a dying ember against her heart, who had knelt in ruined shrines and prayed to gods who never answered, and prayed anyway, year after year, with nothing bought and nothing returned. Faith with nothing asked in return.

He burst up into the forgotten chapel, breathless, and Sable rose from her knees by the altar, Ember's shard glowing faintly through her shirt, her eyes going wide at whatever she saw in his face.

"Xanthe? What did he say? Is it possible?"

"It's possible," Xanthe said. And then, hating himself, knowing already that she would not refuse, that she had never once in her life refused: "But it needs someone the god can't look away from. Someone whose faith is real."

The chapel went very quiet. Above them, muffled and vast, the Spire hummed like a struck bell.

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Then she smiled — and it was not the god's stolen smile, not the king's hollow one, but something fierce and entirely her own.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the road. I was always going to be the one who stayed behind."
