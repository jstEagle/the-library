## 8. Prayer as a Weapon

Sable had spent her whole life learning how to kneel.

Not the way courtiers knelt, all silk and calculation, nor the way the hollowed worshippers of Corvane knelt, their spines bending like reeds before a wind that came from inside them. She had learned the older way, in the burned chapels of the borderlands, in the years after her order was scattered: kneeling as a discipline of attention. Kneeling as a way of saying *I am here, and I am listening, and I will not look away.*

She knelt now on the cold stone of the Spire's antechamber, and she listened, and what she heard frightened her more than anything the waking world had shown her yet.

The god was singing.

Not with a voice. Not with words. It was a hum beneath the floor, a sweetness in the marrow of the mountain, the sound a starving man might make over a feast he intended to eat very, very slowly. Every prayer that had been answered in the last hundred years — every monstrous crop, every petty miracle, every wish granted in Corvane's gilded streets — had been a course in that feast. And the worshippers, hollow-eyed and smiling, were being eaten a spoonful at a time.

"Sable."

Xanthe's voice, low and careful, from the doorway. He had a way of speaking to her as though she were a map he was afraid of creasing. She turned. He was pale — he had been pale since they'd come up from the Spire's roots, since King Aldric had looked at him with dying eyes and begged not to be freed — but his hands were steady. They were always steady. It was one of the things she had come to love about him without ever saying it aloud, because saying it aloud felt like drawing it on a map, and drawn things could be found, and found things could be taken.

"Tell me again," he said. "Tell me what you're planning, and tell me slowly, so I can find every hole in it."

She almost smiled. "You'll find the holes anyway. You always do."

"That's why I'm asking."

* * *

They laid it out on the floor of the antechamber like a chart: Xanthe's maps, weighted with stones; the shard of dragon-fire in its iron case, glowing like a coal wrapped in a bruise; Ember curled in the corner, small as a hound now, her scales the grey of old ash, watching everything with eyes like banked furnaces.

"The binding is a knot," Sable said, tracing it in the dust with one finger. "Aldric's soul is the cord, the Spire is the hand that holds the knot, and the god is the thing the knot keeps tied down. Ember's fire can burn the knot. But a knot that's held for a hundred years doesn't just slip free — it *snaps*. And the moment it snaps, the god will have one thought, one hunger, one direction to lunge." She looked up. "It will lunge at whoever unbound it. At you, Xanthe. At the Almeida hand that carried the fire."

"Unless," Xanthe said quietly, "something else holds its attention first."

"Unless something else holds its attention." Sable drew a second line in the dust, branching from the first. "A god of hunger feeds on faith. Bought faith, forced faith, hollow faith — that's gruel to it, that's what it's been living on, thin and flavorless. But real faith—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—real faith is a feast. It can't look away from it. It's the one thing it has never been given freely. If someone prays to it, truly prays, with their whole self, while the knot burns — the god will turn to *that*. It will have to. Hunger always turns toward the richest meal in the room."

"And who," Xanthe said, though she could see in his face that he already knew, that he had known since the roots of the Spire, since Aldric's plea, "prays to a god of hunger with their whole self?"

"I do," Sable said.

The silence that followed had weight. Ember's tail scraped once against the stone, a sound like a door being drawn shut.

"No," Xanthe said.

"Xanthe—"

"No." He was on his feet now, and his hands, for the first time since she'd known him, were not steady. "You've seen what it does to its worshippers. You've walked through Corvane. Those people are *hollow*, Sable. It drinks them down to the rind. And you want to kneel in front of it and offer yourself up as a—"

"As a meal it can't finish." She kept her voice level, the way she'd learned to in the burned chapels, the way she'd learned to when her sisters died one by one and someone had to say the words over them. "It feeds on faith that's *given to it*. Bought faith, it swallows whole. But faith freely given, faith that keeps giving even as it's drunk — it can't drain that. It can only keep drinking. That's the trap, Xanthe. That's the whole trick. A god of hunger doesn't know what to do with a gift. It has never once in its existence been *given* anything. It only knows how to take. And I—" she spread her hands, and the old burn scars on her palms caught the light of the shard, "—I am the only person in this room who has ever prayed to something that didn't answer, and kept praying anyway. That's what real faith is. It's prayer without payment. It's kneeling without being fed. It's the one thing this god cannot digest."

"You could die."

"I could die crossing the street in Corvane. I could die of the wound in my side that your valley's healers barely closed. I could have died a hundred times between the borderlands and here." She rose to meet him, and she was shorter than him, and she did not step back. "But I won't *hollow*. That's the difference. That's the whole difference. The god can drink from me all night and find nothing at the bottom but more prayer. I've been emptying myself into the dark for twenty years, Xanthe. I know exactly what's down there, and it is not nothing."

Ember made a sound — a low, crackling rumble that might have been a laugh in a larger throat. "*She's right,*" the dragon said, her voice like embers settling. "*I have eaten faith, little cartographer, in the old days, when there was faith worth eating. The bought kind goes down like ash. The free kind burns going down. It would choke on her.*"

"That's not comforting," Xanthe said.

"*It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. There is a difference, though I know your valley forgot it.*"

Sable watched Xanthe close his eyes. Watched him do the thing he did — the mapping, the measuring, the turning of a terrible thing into lines and distances until it could be carried. When he opened them again, his hands were steady.

"If it goes wrong," he said, "if it starts to pull you under — you pray to me."

She blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. You pray to me. Out loud. By name." His jaw was set in the way she'd learned meant he'd already decided and was simply waiting for the world to catch up. "The valley hasn't prayed in a hundred years. The wards ate every word. But I prayed over you when you were dying on that road, Sable, and something *answered* — it cracked the wards, it started all of this. My prayers are not hollow. They're not bought. If the god turns from you, if it looks for a richer meal—" he swallowed, "—then it can look at me. At the last prayer of Vael's Hollow. At the man who's about to lose his protection forever. I'll be the richest thing in the room."

"That's not the plan," she whispered.

"That's the contingency. Every map has one. You taught me that — no, wait." A ghost of a smile. "I taught me that. You taught me to pray."

* * *

They spent the hours until dawn preparing, and it was strange, Sable thought, how much preparation for a death could feel like preparation for a wedding. Xanthe drew and redrew the route from the antechamber to the Spire's heart, marking where the stone thinned, where the god's attention pooled like standing water. Ember slept and woke and slept, hoarding her strength the way the dying hoard breath. Sable sat with the shard of dragon-fire in her lap and prayed — not to the god, not yet, but to the old quiet thing she'd always prayed to, the one that had never answered, the one she'd loved anyway.

She was mid-prayer when the wards at the valley's edge, a hundred miles behind them, screamed.

She felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in the air, a pressure like a storm front rolling through solid rock. Ember's head came up. Xanthe dropped his pen.

"What was that," he said.

And then the antechamber door, the forgotten door, the door that had been sealed since before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather — the door Xanthe had mapped from plans three hundred years dead — opened.

They came in single file, robed in the grey of Vael's Hollow, and there were twelve of them, and at their head, leaning on a staff she had carried since before Xanthe was born, was Maren Almeida.

Sable had met the old woman only once, in the terrible hour when the elders had found her hiding in the Almeida cellar — had met her and been saved by her, in a way, because it was Maren's confession that had turned the elders' wrath aside and sent Xanthe out of the valley instead of Sable alone. She remembered a small, fierce woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a hawk that had decided, reluctantly, to love something.

The woman who walked through the door now had those same eyes. But they were rimmed red with a hundred miles of hard travel, and her staff struck the stone like a gavel, and behind her the twelve elders fanned out with the grim economy of people who had done one thing for a hundred years and intended to do it once more.

"Maren." Xanthe stepped forward, and his voice broke on the name. "Grandmother. How—"

"The wards cracked when your priestess prayed on the road, boy. Did you think we wouldn't feel it? Did you think we'd *wait*?" Maren's gaze swept the chamber — the maps, the shard, the dying dragon, the kneeling woman — and Sable watched her read the whole plan off the floor the way Xanthe read a coastline. "Ember's fire. The knot. And her." The staff swung toward Sable. "You mean to be the decoy."

"I mean to hold its gaze while the king is freed," Sable said. "Yes."

"To *hold its gaze*." Maren laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the valley's cracked earth. "Child, I have read the original binding. I am the last Almeida who has read it. Do you know what the god does with attention? It *eats* it. Your plan is to stand in front of a starving god and be interesting." She turned to Xanthe, and her voice gentled in a way that was worse than the anger. "There is another way. The old way. We reinforce the binding. All of us — the twelve, and you, and the fire. We feed the knot instead of burning it. Aldric stays. The god stays caged. The valley's wards hold for another hundred years, and no one has to burn."

"Aldric *stays*," Xanthe repeated. "Grandmother, he's a man. He's lucid. He's *dying*, and he's been dying for a hundred years, and he begged us—"

"He is the *price*," Maren said. "He was always the price. That is what binding *means*, Xanthe. That is what protection has always cost, everywhere, forever — someone tied to the stake so the wolf stays fed. Your ancestors knew it. I knew it. I let you leave because I thought — I told myself you'd fail, and come home, and hate us a little, and be *safe*." Her voice cracked, and Sable saw, suddenly, the whole shape of the old woman's heart: the love in it, and the cage the love had built. "But you've led a dragon and a priestess to the god's own door, and now I have to be the one who protects you from your own courage."

"Grandmother." Xanthe's voice was very quiet. "The wards are already cracked. You said so. The valley's protection is already failing — Sable told us, the stranger told us, the whole road told us. Reinforcing the binding buys a hundred years. *Freeing him* ends it. One of those paths has an end, and one of them doesn't."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Sable, and something passed between the two women that had nothing to do with Xanthe at all — the look of one keeper of hard truths recognizing another.

"And if we refuse?" Maren said. "If the twelve and I do the old working, here, now, with or without you?"

Ember rose. It cost her visibly — her legs shook, her wings dragged — but she rose, and she unfolded herself to the full of what remained of her size, and the banked furnaces of her eyes opened wide. "*Then you will do it through me,*" she said, "*and I have eaten elders before, in the old days, when there were elders worth eating. I am dying, woman, but I am not dead, and I have been waiting a hundred years to spend the last of myself on something. Choose carefully what that something is.*"

The twelve elders went very still. Maren's knuckles whitened on her staff.

And Sable, kneeling again now, rising again now, felt the god beneath them stir — felt the hum in the stone change pitch, felt the hunger in the mountain turn its vast, blind attention upward toward the sound of a family breaking apart above its prison — and understood that whatever they chose, they would choose it in front of the thing that fed on them all.

"Maren," Sable said quietly. "Your grandson prayed over a dying stranger on a road, and the wards of a hundred years cracked. You should ask yourself what your valley's protection was *for* — if one true prayer could break it."

The old woman's eyes widened. And far below them, in the roots of the Spire, something that wore a crown in the dark began, very softly, to laugh.
