## 9. The Guttering Flame

The Spire's heart was not a room. It was a wound.

Xanthe felt that truth in his teeth before he understood it with his mind — a low, grinding ache, as though the whole tower were a molar cracked down to the root and something vast was worrying at it from within. The walls here had no mortar. They were fused bone-white stone, veined with gold that pulsed like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of centuries. The air tasted of copper and old incense and, beneath it all, of hunger — a hunger so old it had forgotten its own name and now simply *was*, the way stone is, the way night is.

Sable knelt three paces ahead of him on the spiral stair's last landing, her palms pressed flat against the floor, her lips moving without sound. The shard of dragon-fire hung at her throat in its iron cage, dimmer than an ember in wet ash. Ember herself clung to Xanthe's shoulder, small as a hawk, her scales gone the grey of cooling coals, her breath a thin whistle through ruined nostrils.

*He knows we're here,* Ember said, and her voice in his skull was smaller than it had ever been. *He has known since the valley.*

"Then we stop being clever," Xanthe said. He unrolled the last map across the landing's floor — the one he'd drawn by candlelight in Corvane's forgotten passages, the one with the Spire's roots inked in his own blood because ordinary pigment would not hold the shape of this place. "We just go."

Aldric waited below.

They descended into the root-chamber, and Xanthe understood at once why the maps had always failed here. There was nothing to *map*. Space itself had been folded like a letter, again and again, until the chamber was everywhere and nowhere, a cathedral the size of a thought. At its center stood the king.

He was bound not in chains but in light — threads of gold rising from the floor, from the walls, from the veins in the bone-stone, wrapping him from ankle to crown. He looked older than Sable had described, thinner, but his eyes — when they found Xanthe's — were clear. Terribly clear. The eyes of a man who had spent lifetimes watching himself be forgotten.

"You're an Almeida," Aldric said. Not a question. His voice was dry leaves, dry paper, a library burning slowly. "I can smell the binding-ink in your blood. It never washes out."

"Xanthe." He held up the iron-caged shard, and Ember lifted her head from his shoulder. "We've come to finish what my family started."

"No." The word came out cracked, and Aldric laughed — a terrible, rusted sound. "No, boy. Listen to me. I have had *centuries* to think, and I will tell you what your ancestors knew: the binding holds because I consent to it. Unbind me, and the thing wearing my crown walks free into a world that has spent two hundred years teaching it how much it is loved."

"It isn't love," Sable said, rising to her feet. Her voice shook, but she planted it like a staff. "What they give it in Corvane. I've seen their faces. It's fear wearing love's clothes. Bought worship. Hollow prayers."

"And you think your faith is different?" Aldric's gaze moved to her, and softened, and that softness was worse than any anger. "Child, I believed once too. That is how it caught me."

The temperature changed.

Xanthe felt it before he saw it — a warmth behind him, gentle as a hearth, familiar as the kitchen in Vael's Hollow where Maren had taught him to draw coastlines in spilled flour. He turned.

The god stood at the edge of the chamber, and it wore the shape of home.

It wore Maren's face first — her crooked smile, flour still dusting her knuckles — then Sable's, then Ember's, cycling through them like a merchant showing wares. Its crown was made of answered prayers, thousands upon thousands of them, tiny golden shapes interlocked: a child's recovered fever, rain over a dry field, a lost ring found. Every gift it had ever given, worn as ornament.

*Xanthe Almeida.* Its voice was every voice he had ever trusted. *Cartographer. You have spent your whole life drawing doors out of a prison. I am offering you one.*

The chamber shifted around him. Suddenly there was a window where no window could be, and through it: Vael's Hollow. The valley in high summer, the elders' smoke rising straight and white, the river doing exactly what the maps said it did. Safe. Invisible. Warded. His grandmother's garden, his desk, his unfinished charts.

*Walk away,* the god said gently. *Leave the fire unspent. Leave the king bound. Go home, and the wards will take you back, and no prayer will ever reach you again, and no god will ever see you. You will live and die unmapped, unmolested, unseen. Is that not everything you ever wanted?*

And here was the horror of it, the thing Xanthe would carry for the rest of his life: for three full heartbeats, he wanted to say yes.

He thought of the valley's clean, dead silence. Of prayers that fell like stones into deep water. Of growing old in a place where wonder was burned in the square and safety was just another word for forgetting. He thought of Sable's laugh on the road, rough and surprised. Of Ember's weight on his shoulder, lighter every day. Of Aldric's clear, exhausted eyes.

"No," Xanthe said. And then, because the cartographer in him refused to leave anything unlabeled: "You're not offering me home. Home doesn't bargain. You're offering me a cage with better light."

The god's borrowed faces went still.

*So be it,* it said, and the warmth left the room like a tide going out.

"Sable," Xanthe said. "Now."

She was already kneeling. She pulled the iron cage open, and the last shard of dragon-fire sat in her palm — no bigger than a seed, barely glowing, nearly spent after its long journey. She closed her eyes.

"I don't know if anyone's listening," she whispered. "I never have. But I'm here, and I'm not asking for anything for myself, and if faith means anything at all—" Her voice broke, and steadied. "*Let this be enough.*"

Her prayer rose.

Xanthe had no words for what happened next, except that the god *turned*. The whole vast attention of it — the hunger, the crown, the centuries — swung toward Sable like a compass needle finding north, because hers was the first freely given devotion it had tasted in an age, and hunger cannot look away from real food.

"Now, Ember," Xanthe breathed. "All of it."

The dragon slid from his shoulder and walked — small, grey, ancient — to the center of the chamber. She looked up at Aldric in his cage of gold, and something passed between them, some recognition older than either of their griefs.

*I was hatched the year they bound you,* Ember said. *I have carried your fire ever since. Forgive me that it took so long to bring it home.*

And Ember burned.

Not the guttering coal she had become, but everything she had ever been — every wingbeat, every hoarded treasure, every year of lonely survival — poured out in a single column of white-gold flame that filled the root-chamber like sunrise filling a well. The binding-threads caught fire and did not char; they *sang*, a chord two hundred years held, releasing note by note. Aldric threw back his head, and the gold ran off him like water off oiled cloth, and he stepped forward —

— and began, very quietly, to die.

Because that was the price no one had said aloud, though perhaps everyone had known: the binding had kept him alive. Two centuries of borrowed time came due at once, and Xanthe watched the king age a hundred years between one breath and the next, watched him sink to his knees, watched him *smile*.

"Human," Aldric rasped. "Oh — oh, it hurts. It's *wonderful*." He gripped Xanthe's wrist with fingers gone papery-thin. "The god. Don't let it turn on the girl. Whatever it offers — whatever it shows you—"

"I know," Xanthe said. "I know."

Aldric died the way a candle dies when a door opens: suddenly, gently, with the flame bending toward something beyond the room. And in the same instant, the god screamed.

It had lost its anchor. The king's soul, the tether it had gnawed at for two centuries, was gone — and its attention snapped back to the only faith left burning in the chamber, and that was Sable, kneeling in the fire-light with her hands raised, holding its gaze the way a lamb holds a wolf's, and Xanthe saw the moment it stopped being curious and started to *feed*.

Light poured out of her. Not gold — colorless, draining, the light of a lamp running out of oil. She gasped. Her spine bowed. The god leaned closer, crown glittering, mouths opening along its borrowed faces—

*SABLE.*

Xanthe didn't decide. There was no decision. He dropped to his knees beside her, seized her cold hands, and prayed.

He had never prayed in his life. The valley forbade it; the valley *was* the refusal of it. He had no technique, no liturgy, no words learned by rote. So he gave it the only thing he had, the truest thing, the thing he'd spent a lifetime learning to do:

He described her.

*"Let her live. Let her wake tomorrow and complain about my handwriting. Let her argue with me about roads. Let her see the sea she's never seen. I have mapped the whole world and none of it matters if she isn't in it — let her live, let her live, let her live—"*

It was not eloquent. It was not holy. It was a cartographer's prayer, precise and desperate and utterly, ruinously sincere — the first true prayer out of Vael's Hollow in two hundred years, offered not to the god but *past* it, straight to whatever listens when love speaks plainly.

And the god flinched.

Because it could feed on bought worship, on frightened worship, on worship hollowed out and served like gruel. But this — this unpracticed, unbought, specific and stubborn love — it could not swallow. It only made the hunger worse, and worse hunger, in the end, is its own weakness.

The colorless light stopped leaving Sable. Then, slowly, it reversed.

Ember's last ember — a single spark, clinging to the scorched stones where the dragon had stood — flickered once, twice, and went out with something almost like relief.

And the god, starved and blinded and faced with two people who loved each other more than they feared it, began — very slowly, like frost surrendering to morning — to come apart.

*

The chamber settled around them like ash. Where the god had stood there was only drifting gold dust, settling over everything, over Aldric's body, over the burnt outline of a small dragon's wings, over Sable, who lay breathing — raggedly, angrily, wonderfully alive — in Xanthe's arms.

"You *prayed*," she croaked, when she could speak. "You. The valley's own son."

"Don't tell the elders," Xanthe said, and she laughed, wet and weak, and held on to him.

Above them, far above, the Spire groaned — and somewhere in that groaning, Xanthe heard something else. Footsteps. Many of them, descending the long stair, and a voice he would have known in any darkness calling down through the dark:

"Xanthe! Xanthe Almeida, you answer me right now—"

Maren had come. And she had not come alone.
