## 10. Maps of the Living World

The god did not die screaming.

Xanthe had expected screaming. He had spent his whole life imagining the ends of things — the collapse of bridges, the burning of forests, the last breath of kings — and in every version there was noise, violence, a rending of the world's fabric. But the crowned thing that had worn Aldric's hunger like a mantle simply *thinned*. It stood in the heart of the Spire with Sable's prayer wrapped around it like a rope of light, and when Xanthe answered that prayer — clumsy, wordless, the first true thing his voice had ever offered to anything — the god turned its crown toward him and seemed, for one impossible moment, to be *listening*.

Then it came apart.

Not into ash. Into land. That was the horror and the mercy of it, and Xanthe would spend the rest of his life trying to render it honestly: the god of hunger dissolved into the very things it had starved for. Its crown became a ridge of hills west of Corvane that no map had ever shown. Its long fingers sank into the soil as rivers, silver-bright, running where rivers had run dry for a generation. Its hunger itself — the vast, aching want at its center — poured down into the roots of the world, and where it touched, things grew. Not monstrous, not wished-into-being, but *ordinary*: wheat, moss, wildflowers pushing up through the cracked flagstones of the Spire's foundations as though they had been waiting a hundred years for permission.

Ember was not there to see it. There was only a drift of fine grey scale-dust on the wind, catching the dawn light through the broken crown of the Spire, and a warmth in the stone under Xanthe's palms that he chose, deliberately, to believe was her.

He knelt there a long time. Sable knelt beside him, her hands burned and blistered from holding the god's gaze, her faith still humming in the air around her like a struck bell that would not quite fall silent. Neither of them spoke. Some maps you made with ink. This one, he made with silence.

* * *

They buried Aldric on the third day, on the hillside that had been the god's crown, beneath a tree that had not existed a week before. The king's body was old — older than it should have been, the years of his imprisonment settling into him all at once once the binding released them — but his face, in death, was finally his own. No stranger's smile. Just a tired man who had held a door shut against the dark for longer than any man should have been asked to.

Sable spoke the rites of her order, what remained of it, her voice rough. Ember's scale-dust still hung faintly in the air, and when it settled on the fresh-turned earth, the grass came up green around the grave overnight.

"He never learned my name," Xanthe said, standing at the edge of the little cemetery while the others drifted back down toward Corvane. "I talked to him for hours down in the dark, and I don't think he ever caught it."

"Maybe it didn't matter," Sable said. "Maybe what mattered was that someone was talking to him at all."

Xanthe thought about that for a long time afterward. He thought about all the prayers the valley had swallowed unanswered, all the voices his ancestors had sealed away behind wards so that no god could hear them — and no god could *hurt* them, either, which was the part Maren had never said aloud and everyone had always known. Protection and isolation wore the same face. He had drawn that border on a hundred maps without ever understanding what it was a border *of*.

Now he understood. And now the border was gone.

* * *

The wards fell quietly, too.

Maren sent word by rider — an actual rider, crossing open country, the first messenger to leave Vael's Hollow in living memory — that on the morning after the god dissolved, the elders had woken to find the valley's edge simply *open*. No shimmer. No wall of forgetting. Just the road continuing east out of the valley mouth, ordinary and terrifying, leading to everywhere.

"They're frightened," Maren said when she reached Corvane two weeks later, finding Xanthe in the palace scriptorium, surrounded by the old plans he'd used to find the forgotten passages. She looked older. She looked, also, lighter, as though some weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for bone had finally been set down. "Half of them want to seal themselves in deeper. Dig. Hide. Pretend the world outside is still the story we told them."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is me." She smiled, and it was Maren's smile, crooked and stubborn, the smile that had taught him that love and exasperation were often the same thing. "We put it to the valley. Everyone. Every soul, even the children. We've never voted on anything in our lives, Xanthe. We've never needed to. Nothing ever changed." She looked out the scriptorium window, at the city below, at the market squares where people who had once traded their devotion for miracles now traded, simply, *goods*, and argued about prices, and laughed. "We voted to open the gates. Not because we aren't afraid. Because we decided being afraid together was better than being safe apart."

Xanthe crossed the room and hugged his grandmother, hard, the way he hadn't since he was small enough to be carried.

"You lied for a stranger before you'd ever left home," Maren murmured into his hair. "I knew then. I knew the valley had already lost you. I'm just glad it lost you to something worth losing you to."

* * *

He began the new map in autumn.

It was nothing like his old work. His old maps had been beautiful lies — a realm assembled from travelers' tales and secondhand rumors, rendered in confident lines by a boy who had never seen a river he didn't have to imagine. Those maps had been escape routes in disguise: every blank space marked, every border noted, a lifetime spent charting the way *out* of a place he couldn't leave.

This map started with his own two feet.

He walked out of Corvane on a bright cold morning with Sable beside him and Maren waving from the city gate, and he drew the road as it actually was — rutted, real, lined with farms where the wished-monstrous crops were being patiently replaced with honest grain. He drew the village where the petty miracle-workers had set down their borrowed crowns; most of them, freed from the god's hunger, had turned out to be merely lonely people who had wanted to matter. Some of them apologized. One of them wept and gave them bread. Xanthe mapped the apology and the bread both, in the margins, because he was learning that a map of the living world had to hold more than geography.

He drew the hillside where Aldric slept. He drew the ridge that had been a crown. He drew the new river — the god's longest finger — and named it, after long argument with Sable, the Ember, because Sable had won the argument by refusing to argue, which was her most devastating technique.

They found dragons, or the beginning of them. In the warm country south of the Spire, in valleys that the god's dissolution had seeded with old fire, eggs that had slept for centuries were cracking open. Small dragons. Frightened, hungry, ordinary dragons — lizards becoming legends again, one generation at a time. Sable cried when she saw the first hatchling. Xanthe drew it badly, because it wouldn't hold still, and decided the bad drawing was truer than a good one would have been.

* * *

In winter they went home.

Not to hide. To show. The valley gates stood open, and the people of Vael's Hollow stood along the road to meet them — not as a crowd of the protected, but as neighbors come out to see travelers off, or welcome them back, or simply watch the horizon, which was suddenly a thing worth watching. Children ran alongside Xanthe's horse asking about the dragons. Elders who had once burned anything that smelled of magic now crowded around to see the hatchling sketches, touching the pages with careful fingers, as if paper might burn them back.

Maren walked the valley edge with him at dusk, the place where the wards had been. Grass grew there now, right up to the old boundary stones, unafraid.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Giving up the protection. Forever. You can't ever have it back. You know that."

"I know." Xanthe looked out past the stones, at the darkening country beyond — the wilds, the roads, the whole unmapped-and-now-mapping world, dangerous and alive and utterly indifferent to his safety. Somewhere out there were things that would try to kill him. Somewhere out there were gods, perhaps, waking slowly into a world that no longer fed them fear. "I spent my whole life invisible, Gran. Safe means unseen. I'd rather be seen."

Maren took his arm, and they walked back toward the lights of the valley, and neither of them said anything else, because there wasn't anything left that needed saying.

* * *

On the first page of the new atlas, above the title he lettered in his best hand — *A Map of the Living World, begun by Xanthe Almeida, who has seen it* — he drew a small cartouche, the kind the old masters reserved for dedications. Inside it he wrote four names: Ember, who gave everything. Aldric, who held the door. Sable, who prayed first. And Maren, who let him go.

Sable found him at the worktable near midnight, as she always did, and draped a blanket over his shoulders, and looked over his shoulder at the page.

"It's empty," she said, nodding at the great sheet spread beside the finished first leaf. Blank vellum, waiting. "Your masterpiece. All that space, and nothing on it yet."

"That's the point." Xanthe dipped his pen. Outside the window, far to the south, the sky glowed faintly warm — dragon-fire, the new generation learning to breathe it, lighting the dark the way the old world never had. "The old maps ended where the danger began. This one starts there."

He set the nib to the vellum and drew the first line: a road, going south, toward the glow.

And in the margin, small and true, he wrote: *Here be us.*
