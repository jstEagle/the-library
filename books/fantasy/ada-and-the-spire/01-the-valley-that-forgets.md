## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The morning came up out of the ground like breath.

Wendell Voss felt it before he saw it — the slow exhale of thawing earth rising through the soles of his boots as he knelt in the turnip rows, the smell of wet loam and last year's rot and the green insistence of things that had decided, overnight, to grow. The valley did this every spring. It forgot its winters entirely, shrugged them off like an old coat, and began again. That was why they called it the Valley That Forgets, though only three people still lived close enough to remember the name, and one of them was Wendell himself.

He pressed two fingers into the soil beside a seedling whose leaves had gone the color of weak tea.

*You're drowning,* he told it. *The meltwater's pooled under your roots. Lean east, toward the stones, where the ground drains.*

The seedling could not answer — plants never could; his gift stopped at the edge of anything with blood — but beneath him, in the dark runs and galleries of the soil, something stirred. A mole, old and half-blind, pushed up through the tunnel it kept beneath the garden beds.

*Ground's too wet on the west side,* the mole said, in the wordless way of beasts, a language that was less words than weather. *Been telling the worms for days. Nobody listens to worms.*

*I'm listening,* Wendell said, and meant it, and the mole made a sound like dry leaves shifting that was, in its way, laughter.

He moved the seedling himself, cupping its roots in his palms, transplanting it to the stony eastern edge of the bed. It was small work. It was the kind of work he had built his whole life from, stone by patient stone: small kindnesses, done quietly, witnessed by no one who mattered. In twenty-two years he had not spoken a word of magic aloud. He had never needed to. Magic, in the valley, was a rumor that could not follow you past the ridgeline — the old wards saw to that, whatever they were, laid down by whoever had lived here before anyone remembered. Inside the bowl of those hills, gifts went silent. Wendell's gift had never been a spell at all, only a listening, and listening needed no magic. So he stayed. He grew turnips. He talked to moles.

It was, he had told himself for two decades, safety.

The goats disagreed with him first.

They came down off the high pasture in a body just past midmorning, all eleven of them, moving in a tight knot with their ears pinned flat and their tails clamped low, and Wendell knew before he reached the gate that something was wrong above the tree line. Goats were gossips and cowards and liars, but they were honest about fear.

*What is it?* he asked.

*Men,* said the eldest nanny, whose name in beast-speech was a smell of crushed thyme. *On the pass road. Not hunters. Hunters walk soft. These walk loud.*

*How many?*

*Three.* She shuddered, a ripple running nose to tail. *And something else. Something dead that walks.*

Wendell stood very still in the gate with his hand on the top rail. Beyond the goats, beyond the birch line, the pass wound up between shoulders of grey rock toward the world he had spent his life not thinking about. Smoke rose there sometimes, far off, when the wind came from the south — woodsmoke, forge-smoke, once a greasy black plume he had chosen not to name. The valley forgot. He had let himself forget with it.

*Stay in the lower pasture,* he told the goats. *If men come, scatter into the scree. Don't let them pen you.*

*They won't want us,* the nanny said grimly. *Goats don't burn.*

He did not ask what she meant. He did not want to know yet what she meant.

* * *

The courier came at dusk, and he came falling more than walking.

Wendell heard him first through the pines — the arrhythmic stumble of boots, a wet dragging breath — and then the dogs were barking, both of them, the deep alarm-bark they almost never used, and then the man broke from the treeline at the edge of the dooryard and collapsed against the fence post like a coat dropped from a shoulder.

He was young. That was Wendell's first thought, absurdly: *he's barely older than me.* Royal livery, once blue, now mostly mud and blood. An arrow had gone through the meat of his thigh and someone had bound it with a strip torn from his own cloak, badly, the knot tied by shaking hands. His horse was nowhere. His eyes were somewhere much farther away than the dooryard.

"Water," the man rasped. "Please. I have to — there's a proclamation. I have to read it."

"You have to lie down," Wendell said, "or you're going to die in my turnip patch, and the turnips didn't do anything to deserve it."

Something flickered in the courier's face — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. Wendell got an arm under him and walked him inside, and the man weighed nothing at all, which frightened Wendell more than the blood did. He settled him by the hearth, cut away the ruined bandage, cleaned the wound with spirits, stitched it as gently as he knew how. The man bit down on a leather strap and made no sound except through his nose, long whistling exhales, until it was done.

"There's bread," Wendell said. "Broth, if you can wait for it."

"The proclamation." The courier's hand shook as he fumbled at his breast. "I was carrying it to the garrison at Marrow Ford. They shot me on the pass road. They shot me, and I still have to — if I don't deliver it, they'll — my family is in the capital—"

"Then give it to me," Wendell said, "and rest, and live, and collect your pay another day."

The man stared at him. Then, slowly, he laughed — a cracked, terrible sound, half sob. "Do you know what it says? Do you know why they shot me? Because I read it. Because I read it aloud at the last three villages, and I couldn't make myself read it at the fourth." His hand closed around the oilskin packet and did not open. "Because everyone who hears it either laughs, or runs, or turns in their neighbor. And I can't — I can't be the reason anymore."

Wendell crouched beside him. Outside, the light was going the color of ash, and the dogs had gone quiet in the way dogs go quiet when they are listening hard.

"Read it to me," Wendell said quietly. "I'll take it to Marrow Ford myself. You'll have delivered it. No one will ever know otherwise."

The courier looked at him for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Wendell's face seemed to decide him. He drew the packet out, broke the seal — the crown's wax, deep red, stamped with the sigil of the Spire — and unfolded the vellum with hands that would not stop trembling. He did not stand to read it. He read it sitting in the hearthlight, in a voice worn down to gravel.

*"By order of His Majesty, sovereign of the realm, keeper of the Spire: let it be known throughout the land that the time of cleansing is come. All persons possessed of unlicensed gift — of wild magic, of beast-speech, of sight, of shaping, of any art unsanctioned by the crown — shall present themselves at the nearest garrison within sixty days, there to be culled for the good and continuance of the realm. Those who conceal themselves, or who shelter the concealed, shall share their fate. There is no valley so remote. There is no ward so old. The Spire sees all. The Spire remembers all. Signed under the king's own hand."*

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a nightbird called and was answered.

"Culled," Wendell said. The word sat in his mouth like a stone.

"It doesn't say killed," the courier whispered. "Everyone asks that. It doesn't say. But the gifted who've gone to the garrisons these last months — none of them have written home. None of them have been seen again. And the Spire—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They say the Spire has started singing. At night. You can hear it across half the city. People used to say it was bells."

Wendell looked into the fire and felt, with terrible clarity, the exact shape of the life he had built: the turnip rows, the mole's dry-leaf laughter, the goats' gossip, twenty-two years of small kindnesses witnessed by no one. He had thought the valley's silence was protection. He had thought that a gift which could not work here was a gift that could not be found here.

But the proclamation did not need magic to find him. It needed only a neighbor with a grudge, a traveler with sharp eyes, a boy who talked to his dogs a little too fluently when he thought no one watched. Invisibility had always been an illusion. He had simply never been worth looking at before.

Now the whole kingdom was looking.

* * *

The animals came to him that night, all of them, and begged.

They gathered in the dooryard while the courier slept his fevered sleep inside — the dogs first, pressing warm against his knees, then the goats filing down from the pasture with their eyes reflecting the moon, then the barn cats, the old mare, the chickens muttering on their roosts, and last, padding out of the dark with her grizzled muzzle lowered, the she-wolf who had denned above the birch line for six winters and spoken with Wendell perhaps a dozen times in all that span, and never once without cause.

*Leave,* they said, all together, a chord of voices, fur and feather and claw. *Leave, leave, leave.*

"I can't leave him," Wendell said, nodding at the shuttered window behind which the courier breathed in hot, uneven pulls. "He'll die."

*Let him,* said the mare, bluntly, and the dogs growled at her, and she tossed her head. *One man against all of us. You know what I smelled on the pass road today? I smelled burned hair. I smelled it on the wind from the south, and underneath it, smoke that wasn't woodsmoke.*

*They are emptying the valleys,* the she-wolf said. Her voice was winter itself, level and cold and ancient. *My cousins in the hill country have run for a month. They say the hunters take the gifted alive. They say the ones who hide them burn with them. Little brother — you have hidden so long you believe hiding is a place. It is not. It is only a delay.*

Wendell crouched among them in the frost-rimed grass, and the old dog laid her head on his boot, and the youngest goat leaned her whole small weight against his shin, and he understood that everything he loved was standing here in the dark asking him to abandon it, because staying was the one thing that would truly lose it.

"Where would I even go?" he asked, and hated how young his voice sounded.

The she-wolf was quiet a moment. When she answered, there was something in her voice he had never heard from her before — something almost like pity.

*South,* she said. *Toward the Spire. My cousins who ran from the capital say a woman passed through the high country three days ago, walking alone, asking after a valley that forgets. Asking, little brother, after a man who speaks with beasts.*

The nightbird called again. The courier coughed behind the window, a thin, rattling sound.

"She asked for me?" Wendell said. "By name?"

*She asked for the valley,* the wolf said. *Names can be bought. Valleys cannot.* She turned her head south, toward the black ridgeline, toward the distant country where a Spire sang wrong in the dark. *She will be here tomorrow. Decide tonight whether you are found by her — or by what follows her.*

Above the birches, the stars wheeled on, indifferent, and Wendell Voss stood alone in his dooryard surrounded by every creature he had ever loved, and understood that the morning would change everything, whichever way he chose.
